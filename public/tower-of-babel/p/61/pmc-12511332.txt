An intelligent flow measurement system based on pressure drop in straight pipeline: a deep learning approach
Authors: Reza Shakarami, Mohamad Taghi Sadeghi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Flow metering is an essential industrial requirement commonly provided by differential pressure flow meters that have an obstruction device, causing huge upstream pressure at higher flow rates. Thus, the present study measures the flow velocity based on the pressure drop in the straight pipeline without any restriction device. Since the physical equations cannot be explicitly solved for the velocity calculation from pressure drop data, and the Trial-and-Error (TAE) procedure is time-consuming and unreliable, the deep learning approach is employed to determine the flow velocity from pressure drop data. Darcy friction factor and pressure drop are calculated to a wide range of flow velocity, pipe diameter, pipe relative roughness, and fluid kinematic viscosity to generate more than 27,000 theoretical data for training and testing different deep neural networks such as FFNN (Feed Forward Neural Network), CNN (Convolutional Neural Network), LSTM (Long and Short-Term Memory), and RNN (Recurrent Neural Network). Statistical analysis proves the influence of input variables and justifies their selection. Models have been evaluated via experimental data that include large pipe diameters up to 10cm and different fluids such as water and heavy oils with a kinematic viscosity of 340 m2/s. The FFNN with a 4-15-5-5-1 structure and the CNN with four layers had the best response, as their accuracy is more than 96% and 95% for all data, respectively. This study presents a new, reliable, and inexpensive flow metering system for incompressible fluids with a low computational cost, no additional mechanical parts, and no intrusive hardware.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19401-z.

Introduction

Flow rate measurement is a common and essential industrial task for the cost calculation of product export and raw material import to feed the process. Due to mass and energy balances in chemical processes, flow metering is vital to control the quality and quantity of products. Among several methods of flow metering, the differential pressure (DP) flow meter is the most common technique as it includes about 70 percent of single-phase flow meters in petrochemical industries. DP flow meters have a restriction device (orifice or venturi) causing velocity increment that has an explicit correlation with pressure drop around the device resulting in velocity calculation via pressure drop measurement. Since having no moving parts, DP flow meters are simple to operate and are long-term reliable devices. However, they cause huge upstream pressure and flow head decrement. This study measures the flow velocity based on pressure drop in the straight pipeline without any restriction device to deal with this issue. Pressure drop calculation from flow velocity in the straight pipeline is a routine problem solved by the Darcy–Wiesbach equation and Moddy diagram. However, there is no explicit physical equation to calculate flow velocity from pressure drop data for both laminar and turbulent flow regimes. On the other hand, the Trial-and-Error (TAE) procedure requires too much time for heavy calculations. Thus, in this study, the deep learning approach as a data-driven model is employed to correlate flow velocity with pressure drop data. The proposed method provides flow metering tasks with several advantages over existing methods. It is non-intrusive and has no mechanical parts to wear out.

Considering the importance of flow metering, various types of flow meters based on different principles have been developed. The pressure drop around a constriction in the pipeline, the transition time of an ultrasonic wave, the rotation speed of a rotor induced by fluid flow, the oscillation of fluid around a nonstreamlined body, and Faraday’s law of electromagnetic induction are the theoretical foundation of commercial flow meters. Since simple and long-term reliable, DP flow meters are the most common technique among these methods. DP flow meters consist of Orifice plates and venturi tubes to obstruct the flow and a set of pressure transducers to measure the pressure drop1. The flow rate passing the obstruction device is proportional to the square root of the pressure drop around the device2. In these devices, the restricted cross-sectional area of the obstruction device is constant. For rotameters, the most popular kind of variable area DP flow meter, the restricted area is variable, and the pressure drop around the float is as much as the difference between the weight of the float and the buoyancy force of the fluid. As the velocity increases through DP flow meters, the upstream pressure and risk of rupture in the pipeline and joins are increased. Therefore, they can only be used for a certain range of flow velocity, and their application is limited by the viscosity and flow rate of fluids1.

The turbine flow meter, one of the famous types of rotating machine flow meters, has an impeller that rotates proportional to the fluid velocity. When a magnet is placed in the impeller, it produces an impulse when it turns and moves toward a coil. Then the rotation velocity of the turbine is calculated by counting impulses over time. Since it has a moving part, the bearing wearout occurs during long-term use, causing a high permanent pressure drop and decreasing accuracy over time. In addition, the existence of solid particles and cavitation bubbles in the fluid flow can reduce the long-term reliability of this device2,3. Figure 1a illustrates a turbine flow meter. Another type of flow meter, the electromagnetic flow meter, consists of an excitation voltage to create a magnetic field around the pipe and measurement electrodes of an output voltage that is related to the fluid velocity, the pipe length, and the electromagnetic field density around the pipe. Figure 1b presents a schematic diagram of an electromagnetic flow meter. This device has a high accuracy for a variety of liquids since its accuracy is unaffected by the density, viscosity, pressure, and temperature of the liquid. Despite these advantages, the electromagnetic flow meter is expensive to manufacture, calibrate, and use, while it is only compatible with conductive liquids1,2. When an unstreamlined or bluff body is placed in the pipeline, Vortex shedding phenomena occur. Then, an ultrasound, thermal, or capacitance detector measures the vortices created downstream of the bluff body. This is the principle of the vortex flow meters that are almost independent of the fluid density, temperature, and pressure. This device has no moving part, is inexpensive, and operates at low cost. however, it can be downgraded by disturbance in the upstream of the flow and by vibration of the pipeline2.Fig. 1The schematic diagram of (a) a turbine flow meter and (b) a Magnetic flow meter2.

The schematic diagram of (a) a turbine flow meter and (b) a Magnetic flow meter2.

When an ultrasonic Beam is sent into a fluid flow, every discontinuity, bubble, or solid particle reflects the ultrasonic wave and changes its frequency based on the Doppler shift effect. The transition time and the frequency change of the ultrasonic wave depend on fluid Flow and reflector particle (bubble) velocity. Based on measuring these two parameters, two kinds of ultrasonic flow meters, called transmissive and Doppler Effect ultrasonic flow meters, have been developed. Since these devices can be clamped onto existing pipelines, their installation is inexpensive and safe to prevent the dangerous leakage of chemical fluids, and they are the best choice to measure corrosive and Poisonous fluids. Despite these advantages, these devices, requiring tedious calibration procedures for any flow situation, have such a relatively low accuracy that they are also used for flow detection instead of exact flow measurement. The existence of discontinuity in the fluid flow causes erroneous results for transmissive flow meters1,2. In addition, they rely on factors that change the sound velocity in the fluid Medium3. Rincon et al.4 have presented a novel method based on the horizontal flight of the ultrasonic beam parallel to the fluid path4. Several new methods for accuracy increment are described in reference5. Industrial experience shows that the accuracy of ultrasonic transmissive flow meters is always much lower than manufacturers’ claims6. Numerous techniques for accuracy improvement are presented in reference6. Due to its principle, the ultrasonic Doppler flowmeter is used for flow measurement and flow pattern detection for multiphase flow. There is a collection of ultrasonic Doppler methods for these purposes in reference7.

Deep learning, as a subfield of artificial intelligence, plays a revolutionary role in connecting theoretical science to industrial applications. In this study, first, the theoretical foundation of pressure drop calculation in the straight pipeline is reviewed to evaluate the feasibility of velocity calculation from pressure drop data. Due to the lack of explicit physical equations, TAE is the only approach to solve the physical equations and calculate flow velocity. Since the TAE procedure is time-consuming and unreliable, the deep learning approach relates the flow velocity to pressure drop, pipe dimension, and fluid property data. Thus, Darcy–Wiesbach and several Darcy friction factor correlations are solved to calculate pressure drop for various flow velocities and to gather a large data set to develop and train the deep learning models. After the pressure drop calculation, it is chosen as the model’s input, while velocity is the only model output. Therefore, deep neural networks are trained and tested with theoretical data to predict the flow velocity from the pressure drop. Next, models are evaluated against experimental data, acquired in the lab and gathered from the literature, to demonstrate the models’ applicability. Statistical analysis is also performed to demonstrate the influence of input variables on flow velocity and justify input variable selections. The deep learning approach, employed in this study, is faster and more robust than the TAE algorithm to calculate the flow velocity from pressure drop data. Heavy TAE calculations can lead to erroneous answers and involve significant computational costs. The overall workflow diagram of this study is presented in Fig. 2. This study presents an intelligent system for flow metering that has no mechanical parts, no intrusive hardware, and no expensive calibration procedure. Therefore, it is more inexpensive and reliable in comparison to existing methods.Fig. 2Workflow diagram of the present study representing all the procedures.

Theoretical background

The friction force, opposing the fluid flow, acts on the contact surface of the fluid phase and the pipe wall surface. Internal friction is based on the interaction force between molecules in the fluid phase, while external friction is based on the interaction force between fluid molecules and the pipe wall surface. This force, causing momentum and energy transfer between different layers in the fluid, converts the flow head to thermal energy and reduces the flow stream pressure. In addition, the gravitational force can alter the flow pressure head in an upward or downward straight pipeline based on the inclination degree. Therefore, there are two different kinds of pressure drop in the straight upward pipeline: frictional and gravitational pressure drop (for a downward pipeline, gravity force increases the flow stream pressure). Isaac Newton was the first man who define the viscosity property of fluids in the rule called Newton’s Law of Viscosity. After the definition of viscosity, several scientists like Hagen, Poiseuille, Darcy, and Weisbach worked on the relationship between pressure drop and flow velocity in the pipeline. Osborne Reynolds defined a new dimensionless variable, called Reynolds number, to determine a criterion for the transition of flow regime from laminar to turbulent flow. He claimed that the critical Reynolds number for the transition from laminar to turbulent flow is equal to 2300. The scientists’ efforts resulted in several correlations to relate the pressure drop and velocity in horizontal pipelines8. In this equation, the pressure drop (Δp) in the pipe with a diameter of D and a length of L is correlated to the flow velocity, presented by V. Pressure drop has the unit of length since it is divided by fluid density and gravitational acceleration. The second term on the left side of Eq. (1) is related to gravitational pressure drop, and θ is the inclination degree. For a horizontal pipeline, this term equals zero, and it is positive for a downward pipe due to a negative inclination degree (θ). The Darcy–Weisbach equation is the most accurate correlation for incompressible fluids in comparison with experimental data. Despite liquids, this equation can’t be used for compressible fluids for all fluid flow conditions9. For compressible gas flow with a low-pressure drop (in the short pipelines), having a pressure drop of less than 10% of the upstream pressure, this equation has an accurate result if the gas specific volume is calculated based on either upstream or downstream pressure. Darcy–Wiesbach correlation is applicable for higher pressure drops, less than 40% of upstream pressure, if the gas specific volume is calculated based on the average pressure between upstream and downstream pressure10. Darcy friction factor, presented by f, is a complex parameter that several researchers have tried to explore its relation to pipe and fluid flow properties, for a wide range of Reynolds numbers. It is easy to calculate the Darcy friction factor by introducing the Reynolds number into the Hagen–Poiseuille and Darcy–Weisbach correlations for laminar flow. Nikuradse was the first researcher who perform extensive experimental research on the effect of pipe uniform roughness on the friction factor for a wide range of Reynolds numbers. He claimed that three different flow regimes exist in three different Reynolds number regions. For low Reynolds numbers, the friction factor is independent of pipe roughness, while the friction factor is a function of pipe relative roughness and is independent of the Reynolds number for turbulent flow at high Reynolds numbers. He also concluded that there is a transition flow regime between laminar and turbulent flow existing in intermediate Reynolds numbers11. On the other hand, von Karman derived a semi-empirical implicit formula for smooth pipes based on Prandtle’s boundary layer theory and Nikuradse’s experimental data. This equation, called the Prandtl–Nikuradse–von Karman (PNK) equation, is presented in correlation (4).4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{\sqrt f } = 2\,\log ({\text{Re}} \sqrt f ) - 0.08\quad {\text{Re}} > 3000$$\end{document}

This equation is the most widely used formula for friction factor calculation in smooth pipes8. White and Colebrook performed a series of experiments on commercial pipes with non-uniform roughness. They concluded that for pipes with non-uniform roughness, the friction factor has a different behavior relative to Nikuradse’s experiments in the transition region12. Colebrook proposed an implicit formula for non-uniform rough pipes in the turbulent flow region that is commonly known as a fundamental equation to calculate the friction factor for commercial pipes13. Moody redrew Rouse’s chart to a more conventional form in engineering known as the Moody chart14. While several researchers confirmed the Colebrook correlation for its accuracy, it still suffers from being implicit. This equation cannot be directly solved for the friction factor and requires TAE calculation. Xiande Fang et al.16 have evaluated various explicit friction factor equations and proposed a new formula for turbulent flow in smooth and rough pipes. Based on flow velocity, pipe diameter, pipe relative roughness, and fluid kinematic viscosity, the friction factor is explicitly calculated via correlations (6), (7), and (9). Next, the pressure drop is determined based on the Darcy–Weisbach equation by knowing the friction factor. Thus, pressure drop can be directly calculated from flow velocity with high enough accuracy relative to the Colebrook equation. However, the present study aims to calculate flow velocity, as an unknown variable, from pressure drop data. Since flow velocity and Reynolds number are unidentified, friction factor cannot be calculated based on any of the explicit equations, including correlations (6) and (9). Since the friction factor is unknown, the flow velocity is indeterminable in the Darcy–Weisbach equation. On the other hand, replacing the friction factor in correlation (10) with its explicit formula in correlation (6) results in a nonlinear logarithmic equation presented in correlation (11). This complex equation cannot be explicitly solved for flow velocity, and the TAE procedure has to be performed. Furthermore, this equation can have several answers that can be misleading. All of the explicit equations for the friction factor are logarithmic and nonlinear, similar to the correlations (7) and (9). Therefore, flow velocity cannot be directly calculated from pressure drop data, even though the Colebrook equation for friction factor has been replaced by a new explicit formula. For this reason, the TAE procedure is the only approach to calculate the flow velocity from pressure drop data via solving physical equations containing the Darcy–Weisbach and Colebrook equations. While the Colebrook equation can be substituted with new explicit equations, the TAE procedure is unavoidable for solving the Darcy–Weisbach equation.

In this study, several TAE procedures are evaluated, and the most efficient method is selected to calculate the flow velocity from input data including pipe diameter, pipe relative roughness, fluid kinematic viscosity, and pressure drop. In the TAE1 algorithm presented in Fig. 3, the Darcy–Weisbach equation is rearranged to calculate flow velocity from the friction factor and pressure drop. By knowing the pressure drop as input data, first, the friction factor is assumed, and then the velocity and Reynolds number are calculated. Based on the Reynolds number, the friction factor is calculated for laminar, transient, or turbulent flow. The Colebrook equation for turbulent and transient flow regimes is solved by the TAE procedure to calculate the turbulent friction factor. After calculating the friction factor, it is introduced into the Darcy–Weisbach equation as a new assumption, and the new velocity and Reynolds number are recalculated. This procedure continues until the difference between the flow velocity of the new and the last step diminishes to an acceptable error criterion.Fig. 3The TAE1 algorithm for calculating flow velocity.

The TAE1 algorithm for calculating flow velocity.

As another alternative, named TAE2, the Colebrook equation for turbulent flow is replaced by correlation (6), as an explicit equation. Additionally, in another algorithm, named TAE3, the friction factor for all flow regimes is calculated via correlation (9). Employing explicit equations for the friction factor in TAE2 and TAE3 simplifies the algorithm and reduces the runtime. However, it can increase the relative error compared to the results of the Colebrook equation. The TAE algorithm is so accurate that its resulting error is lower than 0.01 percent for theoretical data acquired by solving physical equations to calculate the pressure drop. Despite its accuracy, this algorithm is not reliable since it diverges several times and fails to solve for the flow velocity after thousands of iterations. The most significant trouble with the TAE procedure is computational cost and run time. This algorithm has a heavy computational cost and is time-consuming. Therefore, this study employs the deep learning approach as a data-driven model to relate the flow velocity to pressure drop. The detailed results of the TAE procedures are presented in comparison with the deep learning approach in the next sections (Table 5, Figs. 17 and 18). The deep learning approach is robust and much faster than the TAE procedure, while both methods have almost the same order of accuracy. Thus, the deep learning model is a prominent alternative that is employed in this study to calculate flow velocity from pressure drop data in the straight pipeline.

Model development

The theoretical science review proves that velocity cannot be directly calculated from pressure drop data in the straight pipeline; however, it can determine variables that are effective for velocity calculation. According to correlations (2), (5), and (7), the Darcy friction factor is a function of fluid kinematic viscosity, flow velocity, and pipe relative roughness and diameter that is presented in correlation (12).12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f = {\text{F}}\,(V,D,\frac{\mu }{\rho },\frac{e}{D})$$\end{document}

In this correlation, t and fr, as the subscripts of pressure drop, denote total and friction pressure drop. Based on correlations (12) and (13), the flow velocity functionality can be presented in correlation (14), in which there are four input variables.14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$V = {\text{K}}\,(\,\left[ {\frac{\Delta p}{{\rho gL}} - \sin (\theta )} \right],D,\frac{e}{D},\frac{\mu }{\rho })$$\end{document}

Dimensionless pressure drop is not only useful to extend the models’ application for different pipe diameters and fluids, but also helpful to decrease the number of input variables by eliminating fluid density and pipe length. Other input variables are chosen due to their utilization in Darcy friction correlations and the Darcy–Weisbach equation that are the foundation of this study to generate theoretical data since their accuracy and reliability are proven for the experimental data. Four input variables, including dimensionless pressure drop, and one output variable (flow velocity), are introduced for the model of the present study. Since there is no explicit correlation to map the input variables to flow velocity, data-driven models are the only choice to accomplish this task. In this kind of model, the physical principle is not important, and the model is developed based on an accurately gathered dataset. For this reason, the correlation (7) and the Darcy–Weisbach equation are solved for a wide range of velocities, pipe diameters, kinematic viscosity, and relative roughness. For each set of input variables consisting of one flow velocity, pipe diameter, kinematic viscosity, and relative roughness, the dimensionless pressure drop is calculated. Thus, a dataset containing more than 27,000 data points and five columns is acquired in order to train and test the deep learning models. The data matrix has more than 27,000 rows for all examples and five columns for the five variables consisting of flow velocity, pipe diameter, kinematic viscosity, relative roughness, and dimensionless pressure drop. After gathering the data, four columns of the data matrix (as presented in correlation (14)) were selected for input variables, and flow velocity was chosen for the model’s output. Hence, a dataset containing input–output variables for more than 27,000 examples is acquired.

Different combinations of hydrodynamic parameters, including flow velocity, pipe diameter, pipe wall roughness, and fluid kinematic viscosity, can result in an identical pressure drop. Pressure drop is a many-to-one (non-injective) function of hydrodynamic parameters. Hence, an equal pressure drop can be produced by different combinations of hydrodynamic parameters. While the pressure drop remains constant, other hydrodynamic variables, such as pipe diameter, pipe roughness, and fluid kinematic viscosity, can vary. For example, when pressure drop remains constant, both the flow velocity and pipe diameter can diminish. Under these conditions, the pressure drop is identical; however, the pipe diameters have different values. To assess the flow velocity and system state, both pressure drop and pipe diameter must be considered. According to correlation (14), flow velocity is simultaneously a function of pressure drop, pipe diameter, pipe wall roughness, and fluid kinematic viscosity. It is impossible to determine flow velocity without considering all hydrodynamic parameters in combination. It is not helpful to solely know the pressure drop to determine flow velocity. The combination of all hydrodynamic parameters can determine the condition of the system and flow velocity. For a constant pipeline and fluid, flow velocity is a one-to-one (injective) function of pressure drop. Therefore, each pressure drop is only related to one flow velocity, and vice versa. In this case, the system’s condition changes only through flow velocity and pressure drop.

Pipe wall roughness that serves as a critical input variable, is not static in the short- and long-term usage of the pipe. Due to the aging of the pipe and the deposition of dissolved materials in the fluid, its value dynamically increases over time. Moreover, the chemical reaction between the pipe wall and fluid leads to the formation of pits and rough spots on the pipe wall surface. This phenomenon, known as corrosion, increases the pipe wall roughness over time. In addition, erosion can increase irregularities and roughness on the pipe wall. Some studies19,20 proposed a linear formula to predict pipe wall roughness increment during short-term usage of the pipe. For long-term usage, the parabolic equation exhibits more accurate results compared to experimental data21. The parameters of the correlations depend on pipe material, flow velocity, fluid type, and fraction of dissolved minerals in the fluid. It is critical to consider the pipe wall roughness as a dynamic input variable to account for its change over time. Periodical measurement of pipe wall roughness is important to update the parameters of the predictive correlations of roughness and increase their accuracy over time. Simultaneous measurement of pressure drop and flow velocity is an effective method to determine the friction factor and pipe wall roughness.

The deep learning approach, as a subfield of artificial intelligence, involves creating large and deep neural networks to map between input and output variables and to make accurate data-driven decisions. Due to its complex structure, it can extract and build features from a large dataset and then convert the features to the output variables. The deep learning method extracts (learns) the patterns and functions from large datasets to predict the output variable for a new dataset22. In the present study, a large dataset is acquired to develop a data-driven model. Since deep learning is the best choice for dealing with huge datasets, various types of machine learning and deep learning methods are tested to predict the flow velocity in straight pipelines. Machine learning, with its simple structure, can neither extract features from big data nor learn comprehensive patterns from data, so it causes the underfitting problem. In the present work, Feed Forward Neural Network (FFNN), Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), Long and Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) have been employed as deep learning methods in order to find the best model to predict the flow velocity. Two regression methods based on the Radial Basis Function (RBF) and the support vector machine (SVR) algorithm, as machine learning models, have also been tested to evaluate the simpler models’ ability in this field.

Feed forward neural network

Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), inspired from the human brain, simulate the decision process of human neurons. ANN consists of several neurons that are information-processing units.

Each neuron, introduced by McCulloch and Pitts, includes three different parts: synaptic weights, summing junction, and activation function as shown in Fig. 4a. In Fig. 4b, a multilayer neural network diagram is also presented that has two hidden layers and three neurons in the output layer. According to the training algorithm and the number of hidden layers, Feed Forward Neural Networks are sorted into different categories23. Networks with two or more hidden layers that have the backpropagation training algorithm are placed in the deep learning class (deep neural networks), while networks with one hidden layer are known as machine learning methods22. RBF networks have one hidden layer that is trained in an unsupervised manner to sort inputs into different classes, in which the likelihood of linear separability is maximum. The activation function of neurons in the hidden layer is the radial basis function (or kernel function), and the training algorithm for the output layer is the least square error. On the other hand, the SVR network has a hidden layer in that the number of units is equal to the number of support vectors. In the output layer, a hyperplane is used to separate and map the features23.Fig. 4Schematic diagram of (a) McCulloch and Pitts neuron and (b) a Feed Forward Neural Network23.

Schematic diagram of (a) McCulloch and Pitts neuron and (b) a Feed Forward Neural Network23.

Recurrent neural network (RNN)

Recurrent neural networks are classified based on the existence of a feedback loop of information into the input cells of the hidden and output layers.

These networks have a memory bank of information collected from previous input data to make better decisions for new input data. Since a recurrent branch of information helps to find a sequential and repetitive pattern in the data, this neural network type is also compatible with time-dependent sequential data. Figure 5a presents the diagram of a recurrent neural network. Recurrent information that backpropagates through different time steps is multiplied by a constant weight several times. If the weight is less than 1, the information will vanish after a few time steps, and the long-term memory of previous time steps will be removed from the historical data. For this reason, the LSTM network is designed to supply long-term memory for new time steps. By adjusting (training) the activation function in the forget, input, and output gate (Fig. 5b) of the LSTM unit, it makes the best decision to forget or input the previous data in new time steps. According to the complex structure of an LSTM unit (several activation functions and neurons), it is a network by itself22.Fig. 5Diagram of (a) a recurrent neural network20 and (b) an LSTM unit22.

Diagram of (a) a recurrent neural network20 and (b) an LSTM unit22.

Convolutional neural network (CNN)

CNNs are also used for image processing tasks. Convolution of matrix weights on a group of inputs (pixels of an image) considers their relation and processes them totally. Thus, CNNs can detect objects in images based on the convolution property. CNNs can have several matrix weights in each convolution layer to extract features. Each weight acts on the 1-D or 2-D input image and produces an output whose size depends on the stride length of the weight. The pooling layer is used to summarize the features to reduce the net size. After feature extraction layers, fully connected layers map the features to the model’s outputs22. The CNN developed in this study has a 1-D image input that has four pixels as four input variables for flow velocity prediction.

In this study, several types of neural networks are tried to utilize the deep learning approach as much as possible. RNNs and LSTMs are the best choices for learning repetitive patterns from sequential data. Their recurrent memory from previous data helps to learn the patterns of unsteady data and predict the current situation. They are the best choice for unsteady problems and time variable prediction having repetitive patterns, like unsteady flow prediction over time. For the steady data with random pattern changes, the recurrent branch can be useless, while it complicates the net structure. CNNs are commonly used for image processing problems to recognize edges and objects in pictures due to their convolutional property. They can admit input matrix pixels of images and work with large data sets due to their complex structure. However, they are not a good choice for simple problems and small data sets due to the overfitting problem. RBF and SVR can only be used for simple regression and function approximation problems. For large datasets and complex problems, their one hidden layer cannot extract features. Thus, the underfitting problem occurs. FFNN can have several layers and activation functions, and is more adaptable for various problems. However, it cannot be efficiently used for unsteady problems. In this study, a constant TAE procedure is used to find the network’s best structure. Training starts with one hidden layer and a small number of units in the layer, and then the number of units increases until the average of training and test errors increases. This procedure is used for other hidden layers until the model’s accuracy starts decreasing. After this procedure, the number of hidden units and activation functions is checked for the last time.

Overfitting, a basic problem in training ANNs, occurs when ANNs excessively learn unnecessary details of training data, including noise and fluctuations, while underlying and general patterns are missed. Overfitting leads to a small training error, whereas the prediction error is huge for new, unseen data. The increasing size and complexity of the network can result in overfitting. Long training time and a specific range of training data also cause overfitting. Controlling training, validation, and test error simultaneously is the best method to prevent overfitting. It is vital to plot training and validation loss during the training process to avoid overfitting. When validation error starts rising while training error decreases, the training process must be stopped. Moreover, the diversity of training data helps ANNs to learn general patterns to predict new test data.

Activation function is an important hyperparameter for feed-forward, recurrent, and convolutional networks. The Rectified Linear (ReLU) activation function is chosen for the hidden layer in CNN due to its widespread application in these networks. The Sigmoid (logistic) or hyperbolic tangent (Tanh) activation functions are tried for FFNN, LSTM, and CNN. Since the present study solves a regression problem, a linear activation function is the best choice for the output layer. Monitoring the training progress is helpful to make a final decision about selecting different activation functions. Learning rate, as another important network hyperparameter, is a criterion to update weights in training steps. 0.01 is set as the initial learning rate. Root Mean Square Propagation (RMSProp), an adaptive learning rate, optimizes the learning rate during training progress. Learning rate gradually diminishes during the training, since the weights approach their optimum value at the end of the training process. Monitoring the loss function during the training progress shows that the RMSProp is more effective than the Adaptive Gradient Algorithm (AdaGrad). RMSProp algorithm results in lower training time and a final value of the loss function.

The dataset acquired to train, test, and validate the networks covers a wide range of flow velocity, pipe dimension, and fluid properties. In addition, another dataset for higher pipe diameter and a new range for other input variables was gathered to test the models’ ability in a new situation. Besides the theoretical data (extracted by solving the Colebrook and Darcy-Weisbach equations), an experimental dataset has been acquired in the lab to evaluate the applicability of the models. In addition, several experimental studies (Table 4) have been surveyed to gather an experimental dataset for various fluids such as water and oil with different viscosities. Thus, models were trained and tested with theoretical data and then evaluated by several experimental datasets for different fluids and pipe dimensions (acquired by several researchers) to confirm the model’s ability.

Experimental procedure

In the experimental setup, two test sections with 2.54 cm and 1.27 cm internal diameters are installed to evaluate the relationship between pressure drop and flow velocity in different pipe diameters. Figure 6 illustrates the experimental setup developed in the present study.Fig. 6Schematic diagram of experimental setup.

Schematic diagram of experimental setup.

Two parallel small pumps pressurize and move the water through the setup loop. The set of pumps is used to prevent cavitation, which is the main source of noise for pressure drop measurement. Besides the pumping system, valves, and the sharp edges of joins can cause cavitation and create a two-phase flow that fundamentally differs from the single-phase flow. A transparent Plexiglas pipe section is installed upstream of the test section to monitor the bubble density in the flow. Cavitation in the pipeline is visually controlled by adjusting valves in the proper situation using a high-speed digital camera. In addition, four ultrasonic piezoelectric microphones are peripherally installed around the pipe for measuring the flow acoustic noise in order to accurately monitor the bubble density in the flow. The test section consists of a 1m metal pipe in which two pressure transducers are installed. The distance between two pressure transmitters is 70 cm, and four ultrasonic microphones are placed on the pipe 20 cm after the second pressure transmitter. The system requires about one minute to reach the steady flow after the system startup. In the steady state condition, the pressures read by sensors and their difference remain constant. An Arduino Uno microcontroller board converts the analog signal of the piezoelectric pressure transmitters to a digital signal that can be monitored by a computer system located in the lab. The internal audio card of the computer system receives the analog signal of the piezoelectric ultrasonic microphones and converts them to ultrasonic digital signals, based on the audio card sampling rate. The average energy of ultrasonic signals determines the density of cavitation bubbles. The interaction of the water and gas phases in the bubble flow is a main source of the acoustic signal of the flow. The oscillation, creation, and collapse of bubbles can also produce ultrasonic signals known as Acoustic Emission (AE)24,25. Another source of the flow acoustic noise is the turbulent behavior of eddies in the fluid flow26. The frequency range of signal noise emitted by turbulent flow is lower than 1 kHz27. Figure 7 presents the graph of the energy of the acoustic signal for single-phase water flow and two-phase bubble flow caused by cavitation in semi-closed valves. The existence of cavitation bubbles in the water flow can significantly increase the flow acoustic noise.Fig. 7Signal energy graph for single-phase water flow and bubble flow.

Results and discussion

In this study, two theoretical datasets covering laminar, transient, and turbulent flow regimes are gathered to train, validate, and test the deep learning models for new operational situations. Besides the deep learning approach, an efficient TAE algorithm is employed to solve the original physical equations and calculate flow velocity from pressure drop data. Large pipe dimensions are also used in industrial applications to transport high-flow rate viscous fluids. Hence, the models developed in this study must predict flow velocity for higher pipe diameters and new fluids, such as new black oils from different oil fields. Both the Hagen-Poiseuille and Colebrook equations are solved for a wide range of velocity, pipe diameter, pipe absolute roughness, and fluid kinematic viscosity to calculate the dimensionless pressure drop in the straight pipeline. After completing the data set, the dimensionless pressure drop is used as an input variable to predict the flow velocity as the model’s output. Thus, models are trained and tested for two specific variable ranges presented in Table 2. Models are tested in higher pipe dimensions as well as for fluids with higher viscosities to evaluate their generalization ability. The TAE1 algorithm has the most accurate results for theoretical data as well as for experimental data acquired from the literature. Despite their accuracy, the TAE algorithms have a huge runtime in comparison with deep neural networks, which is an unavoidable problem. Runtimes in Table 5 are calculated in an identical processor and coding environment. ANNS can import the matrix of all data points as a single variable. They process the input matrix data in one iteration. Therefore, they can calculate flow velocity for all data with a significantly smaller runtime. In contrast, flow velocity must be calculated for each data point in the TAE algorithm. Therefore, TAE requires a huge runtime for all data points. In addition, the TAE algorithm needs numerous trial-and-error calculations for each data point. Consequently, ANNs are significantly faster than TAE, as they can be employed for online flow measurements. They also need relatively smaller processors in comparison to the TAE algorithm. The other problem with the TAE algorithms is that they diverge and fail to calculate the flow velocity several times. The failed data has more than 20% relative error, causing a drawback for the TAE algorithms’ accuracy, especially for experimental data. The results in Table 6 show that there are 452 failed data points in the training dataset for the TAE1 algorithm. For these data, the TAE1 algorithm diverges and cannot reach a certain answer for velocity. Since the Colebrook equation in TAE1 is substituted with explicit equations in TAE2 and TAE3, these algorithms are more reliable and faster than TAE1. While TAE2 employs three friction factor correlations for laminar, turbulent, and transient flow, TAE3 uses a single explicit correlation to calculate the friction factor for all flow regimes. Consequently, it needs a small runtime for calculating the friction factor and flow velocity. However, its relative error is raised due to the accuracy of the explicit equation of the friction factor in comparison to TAE1 and TAE2. Therefore, the generated data by the FFNN model is completely equal to the input data, generated by solving physical equations, to train and test models. Input variables in the analysis of variance (ANOVA) test must be categorical with a limited number of levels, while the dependent output variable must be continuous. For the data generated to train and test models, velocity is a categorical variable with 39 levels (Fig. 9 and Table 2), while the pressure drop, as a continuous variable, is calculated by solving physical equations. For this reason, new theoretical data for UNIANOVA is generated in which pressure drop as an input variable is a categorical variable having 26 levels, while the flow velocity as an output variable is a continuous variable calculated by the FFNN model. The UNIANOVA table proves the impact of input variables, including the dimensionless pressure drop (Dp) and pipe relative roughness (Rr), as well as their interactions on the flow velocity based on the P-value that is much lower than 0.05. These results also demonstrate that there is a logical relation between changes (variance) in input–output variables and the models’ accuracy prediction is not random for theoretical and experimental data. Sensitivity analysis, as another statistical test, is performed to evaluate the correct selection of input variables among different possible options. This statistical test presents the most important input variables according to the relative and absolute importance of input variables.

FFNN and CNN, as deep neural networks, are trained in a wide range of fluid viscosity, pipe diameter, pipe roughness, and pressure drop in order to deal with comprehensive flow metering problems. These networks can not only predict the flow velocity in the training domain but also estimate the flow rate at higher pipe dimensions and fluid viscosities presented as test data in Table 2. These models are also tested via experimental data for water and various oils having a wide range of viscosities to confirm their applicability in flow metering. The Darcy–Weisbach equation does not apply to gases and compressible fluids. Thus, the application of trained networks for gases is not as extensive as for liquids. For gas flow with a high-pressure drop, the Darcy–Weisbach equation is not applicable since the pressure drop in gas flow causes a density change. Thus, trained networks can only be applied for low flow rates of gases that have a low-pressure drop, less than 10% of upstream pressure, due to the Darcy–Weisbach correlation limitation for compressible fluids. Darcy–Wiesbach equation can’t be applied to pressure drop calculation for long pipelines having a huge pressure drop greater than 40% of the upstream pressure. Several equations derived from the Bernoulli correlation are presented in reference9 in chapter four that can accurately predict the flow velocity for compressible gas flow through a long pipeline. These equations are the best alternative to the Darcy–Weisbach equation for adiabatic or isothermal gas flow with huge pressure drops in long pipelines. The flow rate can be directly calculated by these equations from the difference between the square of the upstream and downstream pressures.

Conclusion

Data-driven models are the best choice to relate flow velocity to input variables since the physical equations (Darcy–Weisbach and Colebrook equations) cannot be explicitly solved for the flow velocity, and the TAE procedure is time-consuming and unreliable. Deep neural networks, as a data-driven method, can completely extract features from input variables due to their complex structure. More than 27,000 theoretical data points are generated in order to train and test the deep neural networks by solving physical equations for pressure drop. Hence, the Darcy friction factor and the dimensionless pressure drop are calculated for a wide range of flow velocity, pipe diameter, pipe relative roughness, and fluid kinematic viscosity to create a theoretical data set. FFNN and CNN models have such a better response than other machine learning methods that they can completely predict flow velocity for theoretical data with more than 99% accuracy. These models also have more than 95% and 92% accuracy for experimental data acquired in the lab and revealed in the literature, respectively. Experimental data include different fluids such as water, light oil, and heavy oil with a kinematic viscosity 340 times greater than water and large pipe diameters equal to 10.16 cm. Thus, the models’ application is not limited to theoretical data, and they can predict flow velocity for high-viscosity fluids and large pipe diameters in the experimental situation. The influence of input variables on flow velocity is proved via statistical tests, including UNIANOVA and sensitivity analysis. Statistical tests demonstrate that there is a logical relation between input–output variables, and the result accuracy is not due to random chance.

Although FFNN and CNN models can accurately predict the flow velocity for theoretical data, these models have trouble with erroneous experimental data measured in the lab. This problem is related to the erroneous pressure drop measurements. Thus, the models’ accuracy completely depends on the exact pressure drop measurement and the correct calibration of pressure transmitters, since their accuracy is high enough for theoretical data. Pressure transmitters also have a constant error in the measurement that is relatively high compared to the pressure drop value for lower-pressure drop ranges. When the relative error of pressure drop, as the most important input variable, is high for small pressure drop ranges, the relative error of flow velocity is too high to bear in engineering calculations. Therefore, the relative error of flow velocity decreases with increasing pressure drop (decreasing relative error of pressure drop as input variable) for experimental data. In addition, when the flow velocity is so sensitive to pressure drop, any small error in the pressure drop measurement causes a huge error in the flow velocity. Hence, the FFNN and CNN models’ accuracy is low for large pipe diameters, smooth pipes, and fluids having low kinematic viscosity, since the flow velocity is so sensitive to pressure drop in these situations.

Despite incompressible liquids, the application of models developed in the present study is limited to compressible fluids. Theoretical data that are used for training the deep neural networks are gathered by solving the Darcy–Weisbach equation. Since this equation doesn’t have accurate results for a high-pressure drop of compressible fluid flow (greater than 40% upstream pressure), trained networks can’t accommodate the gas flow in the long pipelines. However, there are several equations to substitute for the Darcy–Weisbach equation to directly predict gas flow rate from the difference between the square of upstream and downstream pressure in long pipelines.

This study presents an intelligent flow metering system with several advantages. The method is based on pressure drop data in the straight pipeline without any additional hardware causing pressure drop. This method requires a low level of computational cost (after training models) as it can be performed on small processors. In addition, it has such a fast performance that can be employed to online monitoring of unsteady flow for control purposes. It is more reliable and inexpensive than other flow metering methods since it has no mechanical part to be worn out during the time. From the software aspect, this method uses only the theoretical data for training networks as it does not require expensive experimental setup development. It needs no additional calibration procedure. This study uses the deep learning approach as a revolutionary instrument to summarize the costs of other metering methods by presenting a new fast and reliable intelligent system (instead of the TAE algorithm) with no impact on the flow stream head and no intrusive mechanical part affected by corrosive fluids. Moreover, it utilizes the deep learning approach to shift the theoretical science to a new level of application and performance and to create a new fundamentally novel flow metering method that has several essential advantages.
