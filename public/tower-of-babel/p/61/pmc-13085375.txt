Identification and comprehensive characterization of the OVATE family protein in litchi
Authors: Lijun Geng, Xiaoxu Li, Lijie Huang, Lei Zhang, Huanling Li, Shujun Wang, Ziqin Yang, Pengfei Wang, Jiabao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFruit shape is a commercially important trait in litchi, yet the genetic mechanisms that regulate its development remain largely unresolved. OVATE Family Proteins (OFPs) are plant-specific transcriptional regulators known to play key roles in fruit morphology, organ growth, and overall plant development. In this study, we identified 14 OFP genes distributed across eight chromosomes in the litchi genome. Phylogenetic reconstruction and conserved motif analyses demonstrated that all LcOFPs contain the characteristic OVATE domain and can be grouped into three subfamilies based on motif composition. Promoter cis-element analysis further suggested that LcOFPs may respond to diverse phytohormone signals and abiotic stresses, indicating their involvement in multiple regulatory pathways. Integrated RNA-seq and RT-qPCR analyses revealed that LcOFP3, LcOFP6, LcOFP10, LcOFP13, and LcOFP14 show tissue-specific expression patterns and dynamic transcriptional changes during fruit development in two cultivars with contrasting fruit sizes. Heterologous overexpression of LcOFP3 significantly modified fruit morphology in tomato. These findings clarify the genetic mechanisms through which OFP genes regulate fruit shape in litchi and highlight promising genetic targets for future litchi breeding and improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08499-6.Keywords: Litchi, Fruit shape, OVATE family proteins, LcOFP3

Introduction

Litchi (Litchi chinensis Sonn.) is an economically important fruit crop highly regarded for its unique flavor and nutritional quality [1]. Elite cultivars like ‘Guiwei’ and ‘Feizixiao’ are renowned for their excellent quality but typically bear fruits of modest size. Nevertheless, market demand consistently favors cultivars producing larger fruits [2, 3]. Understanding the regulatory mechanisms underlying fruit morphogenesis in litchi is therefore essential for improving fruit shape, satisfying consumer demand, refining market grading standards, and enhancing processing adaptability.

Fruit shape represents a critical quality trait in horticultural crops, whose morphological diversity directly influences both commercial value and industrial applicability [4]. Variation in fruit morphology not only reflects diverse consumer preferences but also exerts a profound influence on harvesting techniques, postharvest handling, logistics, and processing applications. In tomatoes, oblong-shaped fruits are often preferred for processing due to their suitability for mechanical harvesting and paste production, whereas uniformly spherical fruits dominate the fresh-market sector owing to their visual appeal [5]. Similarly, compared to elongated or irregular morphologies, spherical litchi fruits exhibit higher cultivation stability, lower fruit-cracking incidence, and greater efficiency in postharvest handling, making them more suitable for large-scale production [6]. Moreover, their rounded and symmetrical appearance aligns with conventional consumer aesthetic expectations, enhancing their overall marketability [7]. Nevertheless, research on the regulatory networks controlling fruit shape in litchi, the functional roles of key genes, and their interactions with environmental factors remains at a preliminary stage.

In recent years, significant progress has been made in investigating the molecular genetic mechanisms regulating fruit shape [8]. Numerous genes and regulatory factors involved in this process have been identified and functionally characterized [7, 9]. Among them, Ovate Family Proteins (OFPs) have been recognized as key regulators and are increasingly investigated. Accumulating evidence indicates that OFPs play essential roles not only in pivotal developmental processes such as ovule development, vascular tissue differentiation, and fruit morphogenesis but also in the precise regulation of fruit quality through mechanisms including transcriptional repression and protein interactions [10–13]. Initially identified in tomato as a key morphological determinant, a single mutation in the OVATE gene leading to a premature stop codon was found to change fruit shape from round to pear-shaped. Overexpression of OVATE resulted in reduced sizes of floral organs and leaflets [14, 15]. In Arabidopsis, AtOFP5 was reported to function during early embryo sac development and is essential for female gametophyte formation. Most AtOFPs appeared to act as transcriptional repressors in transient expression assays using Arabidopsis protoplasts [16–18]. In rice, OsOFP2 was demonstrated to be associated with lignin biosynthesis and regulates vascular development [19, 20], whereas OsOFP19 was shown to negatively regulate brassinosteroid signaling and integrate cell division patterns, thereby influencing plant architecture including grain shape [21]. In melon, overexpression of CmOFP6-19b led to reduced fruit size, while its knockdown resulted in larger fruits [22]. In perennial fruit trees, the biological functions of MdOFPs and PpOFPs under growth, developmental, and stress conditions were analyzed by identifying the formation of homodimers or heterodimers through Y2H [23–25]. In addition, VvOFP4 has been shown to play an essential role in fruit shape determination by modulating cell shape [26, 27]. In tropical fruit trees, the interaction between MuMADS1 and MaOFP1 has been demonstrated to coregulate fruit quality [28], MiOFP22 and related genes are suggested to be involved in fruit shape formation through the regulation of cell elongation in mango [29].

In this study, we performed a comprehensive characterization of the OFP gene family in litchi, encompassing analyses of gene structure, conserved motifs, protein domains, and phylogenetic relationships. By integrating transcriptome datasets from two key stages of fruit development with qRT-PCR validation, we delineated the temporal expression patterns of LcOFP genes during fruit ontogeny. Functional verification using heterologous expression in tomato further demonstrated that LcOFP3 plays a regulatory role in determining fruit morphology. Together, these results provide a robust framework for identifying fruit-shape regulatory genes in litchi and contribute valuable molecular targets for breeding larger-fruited cultivars.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Two litchi cultivars (‘Feizixiao’ and ‘Ziniangxi’) with obvious different fruit shape were selected as plant materials. The fruit shape of ‘FZX’ litchi is elongated-heart, while ‘ZNX’ fruit appears nearly round. These two cultivars were obtained from the Litchi Field Genebank of Chinese Academy of Tropical Agricultural Sciences (CATAS) in Danzhou, Hainan, China, in2025. The first developmental stage was designated as initial fruit set (about 10 days after full bloom of female flowers, DAF), with samples then collected every 7 days as a stage until the fruits reached full maturity. Fruit tissue samples across all developmental stages were collected, including the pericarp, aril, and seeds from nine stages in ‘FZX’ and twelve stages in ‘ZNX’. Notably, aril was respectively initiated from the fourth and fifth developmental stages in ‘FZX’ and ‘ZNX’.

Identification, characterization, and phylogenetic analyses of LcOFPs

The GFF sequence file for the litchi genome ‘FZX’ and the HMM file for the Ovate domain (PF04844) were acquired from SapBase (http://www.sapindaceae.com/index.html (accessed on 21 October December 2024)) and Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/ (accessed on 21 October December 2024)), respectively [30, 31]. The litchi protein database was searched using Tbtools-Ⅱ [32], resulting in the identification of 14 candidate genes. Gene duplication events were then analyzed with DupGen_finder [33] to classify their types. Protein sequences of OFPs from Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa and Solanum lycopersicum as query sequences and subjected to blastp against the litchi protein database using TBtools-Ⅱ, with E-values set to 1 × 10− 5. Following the removal of redundant entries, a total of 14 members of the LcOFP gene family were ultimately identified. The protein sequences of OFPs from Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, and Solanum lycopersicum were retrieved from the Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR) (https://www.arabidopsis.org), the RiceOmics database (https://riceomics.hzau.edu.cn), and the Tomato Genome Database (TomAP) (http://bioinformatics.cau.edu.cn/TomAP/index.html), respectively (accessed on 25 December 2024). Sequence alignments were performed using MAFFT 11 and DNAMAN 9 [34, 35]. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was then constructed with 1000 bootstrap replicates. The conserved domain of the OFP protein was predicted using NCBI CD-search (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd (accessed on 21 October December 2024)) and Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/ (accessed on 21 October December 2024)). Furthermore, the conserved motifs were predicted using MEME (http://meme.nbcr.net/meme4-1/cgi-bin/meme.cgi (accessed on 21 October December 2024)) [36]. The upstream 2 kb sequences relative to the start codon of LcOFP genes were obtained from the litchi genome database. The cis-acting regulatory elements in these regions were identiffed using the PlantCARE database (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/ (accessed on 21 October December 2024)) and visualized with Tbtools-Ⅱ [32, 37].

RNA-Seq analysis

The raw RNA-seq data was download in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) under accession number PRJCA055661. Raw sequencing reads were processed using Fastp (v0.23.4) [38] for quality control. Clean reads were then aligned to the litchi (Litchi chinensis cv. ‘FZX’) reference genome [30] using Hisat2 (v2.2.1) [39]. The resulting SAM files were converted to BAM format with Samtools (v1.19.2) [40]. Gene expression levels were quantified using featureCounts [41].

Total RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA of each sample was extracted by Magnetic Hi-Plant RNA Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer instructions, respectively. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using 1 µg of total RNA with the HiScript Ⅱ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was carried out on an Applied Biosystems™ QuantStudio™ 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) employing the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). During real-time RT-qPCR analysis, GAGA-25 served as an internal reference for sample normalization (2 −∆∆Ct method) [42, 43]. And SlEF1α-Q-F was used as the reference gene primer for overexpression validation in tomato [44]. Specific primers for RT-qPCR were designed in Beacon Designer 8 and listed in Table S1. Amplification specificity was confirmed by melting curve analysis, which consistently showed a single sharp peak for each primer pair (Fig. S1). Each expression profile was independently validated with three biological replicates.

Vector construction and transformation

The CDS of LcOFP3 was amplified from the pulp of litchi cultivar ‘FZX’ cDNA and subsequently cloned into the pCAMBIA1301 vector driven by the cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter. The resulting pCAMBIA1301-GFP: LcOFP3 construct was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 for plant transformation. Transgenic lines were generated by infecting hypocotyls of Solanum lycopersicum ‘Micro-Tom’ tomato [45]. Transgenic plants were verified by detection of the hygromycin resistance gene. Primers for vector construction and the hygromycin resistance gene are listed in Table S2. Gene expression levels were quantified. The phenotypic traits of mature fruits, including the longitudinal diameter (the maximum length along the main fruit axis), the transverse diameter (the maximum width at the equatorial plane), and the lateral diameter (the width perpendicular to the transverse diameter within the same plane), were measured. Both transgenic and wild-type plants were cultivated under greenhouse conditions at 25 °C with long-day photoperiod (16 h light/8 h dark). Statistics for all phenotypes included three biological replicates.

Results

Identification and characterization of OFP genes in litchi

The sequences of 18 AtOFPs, 31 OsOFPs and 30 SlOFPs were used as queries to identify OFP in litchi. In total, 14 OFP genes were identified and designated as LcOFP1-LcOFP14, based on their respective chromosomal locations (Fig. 1A and Table S3). The encoded proteins vary in length from 122 (LcOFP11) to 417 (LcOFP12) amino acids, with molecular weights ranging from approximately 139,06.83 Da (LcOFP11) to 46,872.37 kDa (LcOFP12). Furthermore, their predicted isoelectric points (pI) span from 4.55 (LcOFP4) to 9.81 (LcOFP2). Notably, all LcOFP proteins show negative grand average of hydropathy scores, suggesting hydrophilic characteristics. Subcellular localization predictions indicate that among the 14 LcOFP genes, nine were localized exclusively in the nucleus, four exhibited dual localization in both the nucleus and cytoplasm, while only two were restricted to the cytoplasm (Table S3). Alignment of the OVATE domain across 14 LcOFPs was performed using MAFFT11, revealing that this domain shares a number of conserved amino acid residues. For instance, residues such as S1, P4, D7, F8, S11, M12, M15, L34, L35, N42, I50, and F55 within the OVATE domain were found to be conserved in all LcOFPs (Fig. 1B).

Phylogenetic and structural analysis of OFP genes

To explore the evolutionary relationships of OFP proteins, a maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed using 93 OFP protein sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Solanum lycopersicum, and Litchi chinensis. The OFP family was divided into three major clades, each containing members from all four species, indicating early evolutionary origin and conservation across monocots and dicots (Fig. S2). Notably, the OFP proteins from litchi consistently clustered closely with those from tomato, suggesting shared evolutionary history and potential functional similarity.

Structural characterization was performed through conserved motif and domain analysis (Fig. 2A-C). All LcOFP proteins contained the OVATE superfamily domain with LcOFP6 and LcOFP12 additionally harboring a DNA-binding 2 domain. The conserved OVATE domain is essential for protein function, including interactions with transcription factors. Between two and five motifs were identified per protein with Motif 1 and Motif 2 universally present. Distinct clade-specific motif patterns were observed: Clade I (LcOFP1, LcOFP3, LcOFP4, LcOFP5, LcOFP11, and LcOFP13) predominantly contained Motifs 1 2 and 8; Clade II (LcOFP6, LcOFP8, and LcOFP12) was characterized by Motifs 1 2 and 3; Clade III (LcOFP2, LcOFP7, LcOFP9, LcOFP10, and LcOFP14) typically featured Motifs 1 2 and 5. These specific motif combinations suggest functional divergence among OFP subfamilies. Promoter analysis identified cis-elements associated with light response phytohormones (auxin abscisic acid gibberellin) and stress responses (Fig. 2D) indicating potential regulation by both developmental and environmental signals. These findings demonstrate that evolutionary relationships correspond to distinct structural features and regulatory potential supporting functional diversification within the OFP family.

Syntenic analysis of LcOFP genes

Syntenic analysis identified specific duplication patterns in LcOFP genes. Two tandem duplication pairs (LcOFP10/LcOFP11 and LcOFP13/LcOFP14) and six segmental duplication pairs were found within the litchi genome (Fig. 3A). Six paralogous gene pairs were identified through intragenomic comparison. Cross-species analysis revealed the closest evolutionary relationships with Solanum lycopersicum followed by Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa (Fig. S3). The number of syntenic OFP gene pairs between litchi and the other species is notably lower than the number of syntenic pairs observed among the other species themselves (Fig. 3B). These suggests that a substantial number of ancestral orthologous OFP genes were lost in the litchi lineage following its divergence from these species.

Expression patterns of LcOFP genes

To investigate the role of LcOFP genes, phenotypic and transcriptional analyses were performed on two litchi cultivars (Fig. 4). Significant differences in fruit shape between ‘FZX’ and ‘ZNX’ emerged during the later stages of fruit development and became pronounced at maturity (Fig. 4A-B). The fruit shape index of ‘FZX’ is typically characterized by values ranging from 0.97 to 1.17, while that of ‘ZNX’ is generally observed to fall within the range of 0.80 to 0.99. The fruit shape index was significantly higher in ‘FZX’ compared to ‘ZNX’ (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). The expression pattern of LcOFP genes exhibited marked tissue specificity (Fig. 4D). Among the 14 LcOFP genes, LcOFP6 exhibited constitutive expression, with detectable transcript accumulation in all fruit tissues. In contrast, several LcOFP genes showed tissue-preferential expression: LcOFP3, LcOFP10, and LcOFP13 were found to be highly expressed in the aril, while LcOFP3, LcOFP13, and LcOFP14 exhibited elevated expression levels in the seed. To further elucidate the temporal patterns of LcOFP expression during fruit development, RT-qPCR was performed on selected LcOFP genes in ‘FZX’ and ‘ZNX’ (Fig. 4E). The expression of LcOFP genes was generally higher in ‘ZNX’ than in ‘FZX’ across most developmental stages. Although the magnitude of expression differences and the timing of peak expression varied among specific genes, overall transcriptional activity was consistently more pronounced in ‘ZNX’. Moreover, the expression level of LcOFP3 was consistently and significantly higher in the pericarp, aril, and seeds of ‘ZNX’ compared to ‘FZX’. Collectively, these results show that LcOFP genes are differentially expressed in litchi fruits with distinct shapes, and their spatiotemporal expression patterns are associated with fruit development and shape.

Overexpression of LcOFP3 changes fruit shape in tomato

To investigate the function of LcOFP3 in fruit shape, a 35 S::LcOFP3 binary vector was constructed for tomato transformation. Three transgenic lines (OE-1, OE-2, and OE-3) were selected for phenotypic analysis. Both the hygromycin resistance gene and the LcOFP3 transgene were successfully amplified and showed high expression levels in all three lines (Fig. 5B-C). Compared to the wild-type control, overexpression fruit exhibited an obvious change in shape, developing a more heart-like morpholog (Fig. 5A). Fruit transverse diameter, lateral diameter, and single fruit weight were significantly reduced compared to the wild type, while no significant difference was observed in longitudinal diameter (Fig. 5D-H). Consequently, this resulted in a tendency toward an increased fruit shape index (Fig. 5G), suggesting that LcOFP3 can influence fruit morphology in tomato.

Discussion

OVATE Family Proteins (OFPs) are plant-specific transcriptional regulators characterized by a highly conserved C-terminal OVATE domain (Pfam PF04844), which is considered the defining feature of this gene family. Previous studies have shown that this conserved domain plays a critical role in mediating protein–protein interactions, allowing OFPs to form homo- or heterodimers and to interact with other developmental regulators, such as TALE homeodomain proteins and TRM factors [46, 47]. Through these interactions, OFPs function mainly as transcriptional repressors and participate in the regulation of organ growth, cell elongation, hormone signaling pathways, and fruit morphology. However, genome-wide identification and comprehensive analysis of the LcOFP gene family in litchi (Litchi chinensis) had not been fully reported previously. This study aimed to characterize LcOFP genes using litchi genome data, focusing on their evolution, conserved features, and expression patterns related to fruit shape.

OFP genes have been identified and classified in an increasing number of plant species. In this study, 14 LcOFP genes were identified and classified into three clades based on motif composition (Fig. S2). The size of the OFP gene family differs widely among plant species and is largely shaped by each lineage’s history of whole-genome duplication, followed by gene retention or loss. Model crops such as rice and tomato have experienced additional lineage-specific expansion events, leading to relatively large OFP gene sets. In contrast, woody perennial fruit trees generally maintain more moderate OFP family sizes. For example, citrus and grape each contain 16 OFP members, a number similar to that observed in litchi [26, 48]. Genome evolution studies provide clear evidence that these differences reflect distinct duplication histories. Arabidopsis thaliana underwent the Brassicaceae-specific α duplication event [49]. In the monocot Oryza sativa, at least three to five whole-genome duplication events have occurred during its evolutionary history [50]. Similarly, Solanaceae species have experienced two major rounds of ancient polyploidization, including the widespread γ event (~ 130 Mya) and the Solanaceae-specific T event (~ 60–70 Mya) [51]. These repeated duplications likely contributed to the expansion of OFP genes in these lineages. By contrast, woody fruit crops such as citrus and grape share only the ancestral γ event common to core eudicots, with no strong evidence for more recent whole-genome duplications [52, 53]. Consistent with this pattern, genomic analyses of Sapindaceae fruit trees, including litchi and longan, indicate that no additional duplication events have occurred since the ancient γ event [1, 54]. This relatively stable genome history may explain why OFP family sizes remain moderate in these woody perennials. Based on the moderately sized genome of litchi (~ 470 Mb), gene duplication events involving OFP family members were analyzed. All 14 LcOFP genes participated in at least one duplication event, suggesting that duplication contributed to the expansion of this family [55]. A total of 18 duplication pairs were identified, including three tandem duplications, five whole-genome duplications, and ten dispersed duplications (Table S4). The E-values for all duplication pairs were low (ranging from 1.01e-15 to 7.61e-54), indicating high sequence similarity and reliable duplication events. Notably, LcOFP2 participated in the highest number of duplication events (four pairs), suggesting it may have played a central role in the evolutionary history of the OFP family in litchi. Despite these duplication events, only 14 OFP genes were retained in the litchi genome, indicating that the family may have undergone contraction during evolution.

Motif analysis revealed clade-specific conservation: while all LcOFPs contained the characteristic OVATE domain, distinct motif combinations were observed across clades. Clade I proteins predominantly contained Motifs 1, 2, and 8; Clade Ⅱ was characterized by Motifs 1, 2, and 3; and Clade Ⅲ frequently included Motifs 1, 2, and 5 (Fig. 2). Notably, LcOFP10 was grouped within Clade Ⅲ despite lacking the characteristic Motif 5 of this clade. Similarly, MiNFU1 was classified into Group 1 despite lacking Motif 3 in mango [56]. Phylogenetic placement based on overall sequence homology likely compensated for the absence of this single motif, particularly when other conserved regions strongly supported its evolutionary relationship with Clade Ⅲ members. Further investigation of protein-protein interactions or functional assays would help determine whether LcOFP10 maintains similar biological functions despite its distinct motif composition. These structural variations suggest functional divergence among LcOFP subfamilies.

Such functional divergence frequently originates from variations in transcriptional regulation and protein activity. OFP gene expression is known to be modulated by developmental and hormonal signals via specific cis-acting promoter elements, ultimately influencing organ morphology such as fruit shape [57]. In Arabidopsis, AtOFP1 exhibits distinct spatiotemporal expression patterns, with its promoter activity initially detected in roots and hypocotyls during early seedling stages before expanding to aerial and reproductive tissues. This pattern indicates the presence of developmentally responsive cis-regulatory modules within its promoter region. At the protein level, AtOFP1 functions as a negative regulator of cell elongation by repressing AtGA20ox1, a key gene in gibberellin biosynthesis. Consequently, AtOFP1 overexpression leads to morphological alterations including shortened siliques. This established pathway linking transcriptional control, protein function, and morphological outcome lends support to the possibility that natural variation in the cis-regulatory elements of LcOFP promoters could influence their distinct expression profiles [58]. To investigate this possibility, promoter sequences of four haplotypes (two from each cultivar) were compared for the five candidate LcOFP genes. Multiple sequence variants were identified across these promoters, including numerous polymorphisms in putative regulatory regions. These sequence differences may contribute to the divergent expression patterns observed between ‘FZX’ and ‘ZNX’ and could potentially influence fruit shape variation (Fig. S4-8). However, their exact regulatory roles during litchi fruit development remain to be functionally validated.

Consistent with the proposed role of promoter cis-elements, the expression patterns of OFP genes vary markedly across tissues and developmental stages. In mango, MiOFP5 and MiOFP22 showed consistently high expression during all three phases in round-fruited varieties, indicating potential roles in lateral cell expansion for circular fruit formation. Additionally, MiOFP11, MiOFP21, and MiOFP23 exhibited significantly elevated expression in round fruits at DAP5 and in elongated fruits at DAP7, suggesting involvement in early-stage cell elongation of round cultivars. In this study, expression analysis revealed higher transcript levels of LcOFP genes in ‘ZNX’ compared to ‘FZX’ across multiple fruit tissues and developmental stages. Given the known function of OFPs as negative growth regulators, the observed expression pattern in ‘ZNX’ aligns with its more rounded fruit shape (Fig. 4). These findings are consistent with earlier reports in other fruit species such as mango and grape [26, 29]. To further link the expression patterns with natural fruit shape variation in litchi, multiple sequence alignments were performed for five LcOFP genes (LcOFP3, LcOFP6, LcOFP10, LcOFP13, and LcOFP14) to compare CDS sequences between two haplotypes (hap1 and hap2) from ‘FZX’ and ‘ZNX’ (Fig. S9). High sequence conservation was observed overall, as indicated by extensive dark blue regions representing identical nucleotides. In contrast, polymorphic sites and sequence divergence were denoted by pink regions. Long insertion/deletion (InDel) polymorphisms were identified in LcOFP3 and LcOFP14, representing structural variations that distinguish haplotypes between the two cultivars. Additionally, multiple single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were detected across all five genes, including both synonymous and non-synonymous substitutions. Notably, within the conserved OVATE domain, SNPs were identified in LcOFP3, LcOFP6, and LcOFP14, with each containing both synonymous and non-synonymous changes. Together, these results may underlie the observed expression differences between ‘FZX’ and ‘ZNX’, and could play a role in regulating fruit shape during litchi development. Further functional studies are needed to validate these possibilities.

A study in peach demonstrated that PpOFP2 expression was significantly higher in flat-fruited varieties than in round-fruited ones, and its heterologous expression in tomato resulted in flattened fruit morphology [59]. In citrus, CitOFP19 expression was markedly elevated in round-pomelo ovaries compared to pear-shaped ovaries, yet its heterologous overexpression in tomato promoted pear-shaped ovary development [48]. In the present study, heterologous LcOFP3 overexpression significantly reduced tomato fruit size and produced heart-shaped fruits with prominent apical tips. Previous reports have described dominant‑negative effects in plants, where overexpression of one gene family member may lead to heterodimer formation with endogenous homologs, thereby interfering with their normal function. For example, in wheat, overexpression of the loss‑of‑function allele HisR enhanced resistance to Fusarium head blight by forming heterodimers with homologous proteins His‑3A and His‑3D, which disrupted the catalytic or binding activity of the complex and ultimately reduced susceptibility [60–62]. Another wheat study revealed that the susceptible allele LR67sus functions as a high‑affinity glucose transporter, whereas overexpression of the resistant allele LR67res conferred rust resistance by forming heterodimers with homologous glucose transporters, thereby decreasing glucose uptake [63]. Moreover, OFP proteins have also been shown to form dimers. Yeast two‑hybrid assays indicated that apple MdOFPs can assemble as heterodimers, and bimolecular fluorescence complementation confirmed homodimer formation for Arabidopsis AtOFP1 [23, 64]. And the interaction balance between specific SlOFPs and SlTRMs influenced their subcellular localization [65]. In addition, sequence analysis of the two litchi cultivars identified a non-synonymous G→T substitution in the conserved OVATE domain of LcOFP3 in the ‘ZNX’ haplotype. This substitution results in a tryptophan (W) to serine (S) amino acid change, replacing a large hydrophobic residue with a small polar residue [66, 67]. Such a physicochemical change may affect protein structure or interaction capacity, potentially contributing to the differences in LcOFP3 expression levels and phenotypic effects observed between the two cultivars. Another observation in this study was that exclusion of the apical tip length resulted in a significantly lower fruit shape index in overexpression lines relative to the wild type (Fig. S10), suggesting that LcOFP3 may regulate fruit shape through localized apical development rather than global growth suppression. Based on these findings, it is proposed that the LcOFP3 protein may interfere with the activity of endogenous tomato proteins. This interference could be a contributing factor to the altered fruit morphology observed in transgenic lines [68]. Future studies are recommended to investigate potential interactions between LcOFP3 and endogenous tomato proteins such as SlOFPs or SlTRMs [69].

This study provides fundamental insights into the LcOFP gene family and reveals clade-specific structural features and expression patterns associated with fruit shape variation. The unusual regulatory mechanism of LcOFP3 offers new perspectives on OFP functional diversity. However, the specific functions of individual LcOFP genes and their regulatory networks remain to be fully elucidated. Future studies should employ genetic approaches to verify LcOFP functions in litchi and explore their interactions with other regulatory factors.

Conclusions

In this study, a genome-wide identification of the OVATE family proteins (OFPs) was conducted in litchi (Litchi chinensis Sonn.), leading to the identification of 14 LcOFP genes. Phylogenetic, structural, and promoter analyses revealed that these genes are evolutionarily conserved and potentially involved in hormonal and stress responses. Expression profiling by RNA-seq and qPCR analysis further indicated their roles in fruit development and tissue-specific regulation. And overexpression of LcOFP3 was found to alter fruit shape in tomato. These findings provide a foundation for future functional studies on LcOFPs, particularly in regulating fruit morphology and quality in litchi.
