A four-year misdiagnosis of spinal Burkholderia pseudomallei infection: A case report and literature review
Authors: Gangjun You, Shuai Wang, Yuchen Hua, Jiahui Su, Yemei Yang, Benchao Shi, Shuizhong Cen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Melioidosis, traditionally an endemic disease, is increasingly reported in non-endemic regions. Its causative pathogen, Burkholderia pseudomallei, exhibits distinct characteristics from common pathogens but is prone to misdiagnosis due to clinical overlap with other infections. Despite advances in diagnostics, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has not been featured in case reports. We present a case of melioidosis, misdiagnosed for four years, where mNGS proved pivotal for definitive diagnosis. Based on our findings and literature review, we advocate for mNGS in melioidosis diagnosis. Furthermore, we identify subtle distinctions between melioidosis and tuberculosis amidst their similarities and propose integrating these features into a differential diagnostic framework.

Introduction

Melioidosis, historically endemic to South and Southeast Asia, is expanding geographically [1], [2]. Its highly variable clinical presentation and mimicry of other diseases often lead to misdiagnosis and high mortality [3]. Although treatment is prolonged and requires individualization, a clear and targeted therapeutic strategy can be formulated once the diagnosis is confirmed, which is crucial to avoid adverse outcomes caused by empirical and ineffective treatment due to misdiagnosis.

Case report

A 61-year-old retired male railway worker presented to our hospital in 2023 wheelchair-bound with recurrent back pain and paraplegia. His history began in November 2019 with severe, worsening chest and back pain and intermittent fever lasting one week. Initial evaluation at a primary hospital in Haikou included basic blood tests (routine blood work, blood biochemistry, and blood glucose testing), which revealed new-onset hyperglycemia and markedly elevated inflammatory markers. Cefoperazone/sulbactam (3.0 g, q8h) and levofloxacin (0.4 g, qd) were administered intravenously as broad-spectrum antibiotics, which achieved partial symptomatic relief that sustained for 2 weeks prior to symptom recurrence.

Symptoms recurred six months later. MRI and CT demonstrated osseous and intervertebral destruction involving the T7-T8 vertebrae and the T7/T8 intervertebral disc, including a T8 pathological fracture (Fig. 1A, B). Paravertebral lesion puncture specimens were collected for routine bacterial culture, mycobacterial culture and acid-fast staining, all of which yielded negative results. While pathogen cultures, acid-fast staining, and susceptibility tests were negative, a positive T-cell γ-interferon release assay in the setting of high local prevalence of extrapulmonary tuberculosis led to an empirical diagnosis of spinal tuberculosis (TB). A first-line anti-TB regimen consisting of isoniazid (0.3 g, qd) + rifampicin (0.45 g, qd) + ethambutol (0.75 g, qd) + pyrazinamide (1.5 g, qd) was empirically administered for nearly 3 months; however, the lesions continued to progress, necessitating T7-T8 lesion resection and screw—rod internal fixation surgery. Intraoperative lesional tissue and intervertebral disc samples were collected for microbiological testing (pathogen culture, acid-fast staining, antimicrobial susceptibility testing) and histological examination, with results consistent with those of the preoperative puncture biopsy (negative microbiological findings and non-specific inflammatory changes). Approximately 2.5 years later (December 2022), the patient developed recurrent severe back pain, bilateral lower extremity numbness and weakness (progressing to paraplegia: ASIA B), persistent hyperglycemia, and elevated inflammatory markers. Since there was no definitive diagnostic evidence for melioidosis and TB could not be ruled out, a second-line anti-TB regimen targeting presumed multidrug-resistant TB was initiated, including levofloxacin (0.5 g, qd) + amikacin (0.4 g, qd) + prothionamide (0.75 g, qd) + cycloserine (0.25 g, bid). This regimen only achieved transient partial relief, and the patient's condition deteriorated rapidly after 2 months of treatment. Enhanced thoracic spine MRI showed a T7-T8 level right paraspinal ring-enhancing lesion (suggestive of abscess), inflammatory changes in vertebrae adjacent to the surgery, spinal canal stenosis, and cord compression (Fig. 2A). CT confirmed progressive vertebral destruction (Fig. 2B). Indicating failure to control local lesions with prior therapy. Upon readmission to our hospital, the patient presented with paraplegia, as previously noted. A comprehensive systemic imaging workup (chest CT and abdominal ultrasonography) was completed pre-operatively, which identified no pulmonary or other extra-pulmonary lesions/abscesses, confirming the infection was isolated to the T7-T8 spinal segment and adjacent paraspinal soft tissues. Emergency thoracic decompression, debridement, continuous irrigation, and vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) were subsequently performed to preserve neurological function. Intraoperatively, extensive caseous necrotic tissue, characteristic of tuberculosis, compressing the cord was found. The collected tissue (comprising necrotic muscle, ligament, and bony tissue) specimens were subjected to standard bacterial culture, mycobacterial culture and fungal culture as well as mNGS. Histopathology revealed chronic inflammation with caseous necrosis and multinucleated giant cells; special stains (hexamine silver, acid-fast, periodic acid-Schiff [PAS]) were negative (Fig. 3A). Crucially, mNGS (outsourced to BGI Group, Shenzhen, China, performed on the MGI4500 platform with 15 M data volume for quality control and an average of ∼35 M clean data generated; no critical threshold was set for microbial abundance analysis [4]) identified B. pseudomallei (Fig. 3B-C). Targeted culture for B. pseudomallei was then performed: intraoperative tissue specimens were inoculated onto Ashdown’s agar and 5% sheep blood agar, incubated at 37℃ with 5% CO₂ aerobically, and typical colonies were first identified on the 3rd day of incubation. Definitive confirmation of B. pseudomallei was achieved via a combination of phenotypic identification (biochemical tests and morphological staining) and molecular identification (PCR amplification and sequencing of TTSS1 and orf2 genes). Broth microdilution susceptibility testing confirmed the strain was susceptible to ceftazidime (MIC=2 μg/mL), imipenem (MIC=1 μg/mL) and TMP-SMX (MIC=0.5/9.5 μg/mL) (Fig. 3D-F).Fig. 3Pathological and microbiological findings from initial surgery tissue. Antibiotics were switched to intravenous ceftazidime (2.0 g, q8h), and glycemic control was optimized with strict blood glucose monitoring. Neurological function improved post-op (paraplegia: ASIA C). However, VSD removal precipitated recurrent high fever and rising C-reactive protein (CRP) (Fig. 2C). Antibiotics were escalated to intravenous imipenem (1.0 g, q8h), yielding partial systemic improvement but persistent nocturnal fever prior to re-exploration surgery. Incisional swelling developed, culminating in purulent discharge. Urgent re-exploration was performed with complete removal of the screw-rod internal fixation metalwork; intraoperatively, we identified caseous necrosis within the screw tracts and extensive loculated, pus-filled collections interconnected and extending into the chest wall, with necrotic material adherent to the removed pedicle screws (Fig. 2D).

Postoperative management included adequate doses of imipenem and strict glycemic control. Fever resolved promptly. VSD was removed after 12 days. Follow-up MRI demonstrated reduced inflammatory changes at the surgical site following implant removal (Fig. 4A). Inflammatory markers gradually normalized (Fig. 4B). The incision healed normally. He was discharged to a local hospital to complete a 6-week intensive phase of intravenous imipenem (1.0 g, q8h), followed by a long-term eradication phase of oral TMP-SMX (160 mg/800 mg, q12h) for a minimum of 6 months, with regular outpatient follow-up to evaluate treatment response. Discussion

Burkholderia pseudomallei infection causes melioidosis, a disease whose symptoms often mimic other conditions under specific circumstances. The bacterium enters the body via ingestion, inhalation, or skin breaches [2]. Eradicating this intracellular pathogen is challenging, particularly in immunocompromised individuals. Consequently, when host immunity is impaired (especially neutrophil function), such as in diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or with long-term immunosuppressant use, opportunistic infection or reactivation of latent bacteria is more likely [5]. Melioidosis is broadly categorized as acute or chronic [6]. Sepsis, the most common acute presentation and a leading cause of death, often results from systemic spread of localized lesions [6]. While chronic cases account for only 10% of diagnoses, this figure is significantly underestimated due to pre-diagnosis chronicity in most recorded cases [1]. This phenomenon encompasses two key scenarios relevant to our case: (1) latent infection, where B. pseudomallei persists asymptomatically in tissues for years; and (2) misdiagnosis-induced chronicity, where progressive infection is misattributed to more common pathogens (e.g., tuberculosis) during prolonged empirical therapy. Because these protracted pre-diagnostic phases are not integrated into the recorded disease course, cases are frequently misclassified as acute at the time of confirmation, leading to underreporting of chronic melioidosis.

Despite its potential severity, melioidosis receives insufficient attention compared to traditional infectious diseases, contributing to misdiagnosis, especially in expanding endemic areas. Even in historically endemic regions like Southeast and South Asia, unfamiliarity leads to incorrect diagnoses outside central research hospitals [7]. Research consistently shows B. pseudomallei exhibits multi-organ tropism, frequently forming abscesses in the lungs, liver, and spleen, which complicates differentiation from TB [8]. Mortality from bacterial dissemination with delayed or inadequate treatment exceeds 50%, contingent on local healthcare capacity [9]. Despite macroscopic similarities between melioidosis and tuberculosis, studies indicate distinguishing pathological features such as smaller, denser caseous abscesses in melioidosis presenting as clustered microcavitary lesions with intervening near-normal tissue—consistent with our intraoperative findings [10], [11]. Microscopically, widespread neutrophil infiltration characterizes melioidotic lesions, forming a striking histopathological distinction from the lymphocyte-dominated infiltrates typical of TB [11]. Early-stage differentiation may leverage distinct subtle distinctions in histopathology, laboratory markers, pathological characteristics, and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles (Table 1), which serve as the core basis for clinical preliminary discrimination of melioidosis and tuberculosis [11], [12], [13], [14]. For resource-constrained settings where advanced diagnostic techniques (e.g., mNGS, targeted B. pseudomallei culture) are unavailable, synthesizing these discriminators into a weighted diagnostic scoring system is a hypothetical clinical strategy that could potentially guide targeted therapeutic strategies at initial presentation—this scoring system is a preliminary research idea that requires large-sample, multi-center clinical studies for validation and refinement, and no relevant published literature is currently available. Currently, traditional bacterial culture is still the gold standard and is required for diagnosis, but its sensitivity is only 60.2% [15], and negative culture results are not necessarily reliable because antibiotic treatment before sampling, different severities of infection, and mixed infections may lead to false-negative culture results [2]. However, this still requires laboratory personnel to possess sufficient knowledge of B. pseudomallei for its targeted culture. Furthermore, among various detection methods, as the pathogen was unidentified, we obtained relevant clues through mNGS. Indeed, mNGS exhibits superior overall performance in the identification of elusive pathogens. These findings are consistent with those reported by Lv H et al. (2024), who demonstrated that mNGS provides a significantly higher detection rate, sensitivity, and accuracy compared with conventional culture for spinal infection, while also enabling a more rapid diagnostic turnaround [16]. This study also found mNGS has higher diagnostic efficiency in pus samples, and our case collected mixed samples of necrotic tissue and purulent secretions intraoperatively, which may have improved the positive detection rate of B. pseudomallei. Notably, B. pseudomallei was identified by mNGS with only 6 specific sequencing reads in this case, a seemingly low abundance that warrants comprehensive contextual interpretation. The mNGS assay was performed on the MGI4500 sequencing platform with 15 M data volume as the quality control standard and an average of approximately 35 M clean data generated for subsequent analysis; for microbial abundance analysis, no critical threshold was set, and the abundance results were generated synchronously with the sequencing reads and presented directly in the final detection report. The specific nucleic acid extraction method was not disclosed by the commercial clinical diagnostic laboratory (BGI Group) as it belongs to the institution’s proprietary validated workflow for clinical microbial detection, and other core pipeline parameters were also not available for the same reason. Despite the low read abundance, this result was unequivocally validated as a true positive by subsequent targeted bacterial culture—the gold standard for melioidosis diagnosis, ruling out sequencing noise or environmental contamination as potential causes. Nevertheless, it demands high-level medical resources, which are largely unavailable in most melioidosis-endemic areas. Therefore, utilizing propensity scoring based on data obtained through simpler methods may present a feasible diagnostic approach.

Treatment requires individualized regimens due to melioidosis’ systemic involvement potential. Antibiotics are central. Summarized regimens from Thailand and Australia [3] recommend for bone/joint infections: ≥ 6 weeks of intensive-phase treatment followed by ≥ 6 months of eradication-phase therapy. Intensive phase duration and drug choice should be adjusted based on clinical response and blood culture results 1-week post-treatment [11]. Surgical drainage is advised for large localized abscesses (single or grouped), but not for visceral microabscesses [17]. According to experience, foreign bodies within lesions should be removed completely, as B. pseudomallei prosthetic infections are also documented [18]. Noticeably, addressing underlying conditions and complications is equally vital in this protracted treatment course.

This 4-year misdiagnosed case of spinal melioidosis highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by this disease when it clinically mimics tuberculosis. Standard diagnostic protocols are at risk of failure in clinical settings where medical personnel lack specific methodological expertise in identifying B. pseudomallei. For such complex refractory infectious spinal lesion cases with negative routine microbiological tests, mNGS proves to be an essential molecular diagnostic tool for definitive pathogen identification. We therefore recommend the consideration of mNGS in similar clinical presentations to avoid diagnostic delays and the subsequent adverse clinical outcomes caused by inappropriate empirical therapy.
