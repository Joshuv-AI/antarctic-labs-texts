Intratumoral plasma cells predict patient prognosis and responsiveness to neoadjuvant immunotherapy in advanced gastric cancer
Authors: Jia-Bin Wang, Chen-Yang Jiang, Yin-Hua Ye, You-Xin Gao, Qiao-Ling Zheng, Hua-You Luo, Shuan-Hu Wang, Tao Zhang, Qin-Wen Jin, Chao-Hui Zheng, Ping Li, Jian-Xian Lin, Qi-Yue Chen, Long-Long Cao, Ying-Hong Yang, Chang-Ming Huang, Jian-Wei Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLimited biomarker availability hinders the accurate prognostication of individual responses to immunotherapy in gastric cancer (GC). Mature tertiary lymphoid structures (mTLS) substantially affect immunotherapeutic efficacy in patients. However, the inherent limitations of tissue size in gastroscopic biopsies necessitate the development of a new mTLS marker-based classification strategy. An integrated analysis of multimodal data, including GC tissues collected from seven independent medical centers, single-cell transcriptomes, spatial transcriptomics, and transcriptome sequencing, revealed mTLS as a key site for somatic hypermutation and clonal selection in the infiltrating plasma cells of GC. An MZB1 positive score (MPS) was established based on the different degree of plasma cell infiltration between mTLS-positive (mTLS-Po) and mTLS-negative (mTLS-Ne) tumors characterizing MPSHigh and MPSLow statuses, respectively. MPSHigh status and combined positive score ≥ 5 indicated a favorable outcome for anti-PD-1 therapy. Conclusively, MPS offers a valuable quantitative approach for improving clinical outcomes and guiding treatment decisions in GC.Subject terms: Next-generation sequencing, Predictive markers

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), which identify and eliminate tumor cells by targeting immune checkpoints to activate the autoimmune system, have revolutionized cancer treatment1. ICI therapy offers a safe and effective treatment option for gastrointestinal tumors2. In the KEYNOTE-062 phase III trial, anti-PD-1 agents exhibited notable efficacy as monotherapies in patients with advanced gastric cancer (GC) marked by high-microsatellite instability (MSI-H)3. Furthermore, in the ATTRACTION-4 study, anti-PD-1 therapy markedly extended overall survival (OS) across patients without regard to PD-L1 expression levels4. In the CheckMate-649 and ORIENT-16 studies, patients with a PD-L1 combined positive score (CPS) ≥ 5 achieved notable objective response rates (ORR) and OS improvements following immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy regimens5,6. Therefore, immunotherapy become the first-line treatment for GC7. Generally, patients with metastatic GC are screened for PD-L1 to assess their possibility of benefitting from ICIs. However, the effects of ICIs on resectable advanced GC remain unclear.

Neoadjuvant immunotherapy has demonstrated potential in managing resectable advanced tumors. More than 100 clinical trials covering a wide range of tumor types have been conducted or are planned to explore the effectiveness of neoadjuvant ICIs therapies (monotherapy or combination therapy)8. The GERCOR NEONIPIGA phase II trial reported a pathologic complete response (pCR) following neoadjuvant anti-PD-1 treatments in almost 60% of patients with resectable mismatch repair deficient (dMMR/MSI-H) esophagogastric adenocarcinomas, suggesting that neoadjuvant therapies are not only effective but also have the potential to achieve curative outcomes9. Similarly, in a phase II rectal cancer trial, 12 participants who received anti-PD-1 drugs achieved pCR without surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy10. These findings highlight the potential of neoadjuvant immunotherapy in enhancing anti-tumor immunity and postoperative immune surveillance11. Notably, screening patients for neoadjuvant immunotherapy is crucial to optimize or complement treatment regimens.

Mature tertiary lymphoid structures (mTLS), compared with immature TLS (imTLS), more accurately predict the responsiveness of patients to ICIs12. The TLS are an organized aggregation of lymphocytes clustered on a fibroblasts network, consisting of T and B cell zones, with interactions between dendritic cells and T cells within T cell zones being particularly critical13. Furthermore, compared with TLS, mTLS are characterized by an extra germinal center, in which CD23+ dendritic cells interact with B cells to facilitate the development of memory B cells and plasma cells (PCs)14,15. PCs produce specific antibodies that bind to tumor antigens, forming complexes that CD23+ dendritic cells internalize and present to T cells effectively, thereby amplifying T cell response and synergizing with ICIs16,17. However, the limited size of gastroscopic biopsy samples poses a challenge for direct mTLS evaluation. Therefore, identifying mTLS biomarkers is crucial for enhancing anti-tumor immunity and improving clinical outcomes.

Although classifications based on MSI and tumor mutation burden (TMB) are currently used for GC immunotherapy, further improvement is pertinent to enhance their robustness. This study focused on pinpointing biomarkers for mTLS and develop a simple and reliable mTLS scoring system for patients with GC to more precisely predict their prognosis and response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy. The findings from this study are anticipated to offer practical guidance for neoadjuvant immunotherapy and assist surgeons in crafting tailored treatment strategies for patients with GC.

Results

Study design

We analyzed postoperative specimens from 435 patients in East China with progressive GC without neoadjuvant therapy and included six independent external datasets for validation (Fig. S1). As shown in Fig. 1, 1009 patients participated in the study. The clinical prognosis and molecular characteristics of patients with mTLS-Po and mTLS-Ne were comprehensively explored using tumor microenvironment and multimodal data. Finally, the MZB1 positive score (MPS) was successfully developed to predict responsiveness to neoadjuvant immunotherapy. The clinicopathological details of the patients are summarized in Tables S1 and S2.

Validation of clinical and molecular characterization of mTLS

To minimize the impact of tumor heterogeneity on TLS assessment, three distinct regions of GC tissue were randomly chosen for hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and immunohistochemical staining analyses in each case. Two experienced pathologists independently performed the evaluations. Of the four groups of patients, 574 (57.0%) had TLS, with 248 (24.6%) displaying mature TLS, characterized by CD23+ cells in germinal centers (Fig. S2). Survival analysis showed that, across four independent datasets, patients with mTLS experienced improved overall survival compared to those with imTLS or noTLS. (Fig. 2A). Additionally, time–ROC analysis confirmed that the mTLS consistently and strongly predicted prognosis in all four datasets (Fig. 2B). Compared to traditional clinical or molecular features such as age, sex, tumor differentiation, MSI, and EBV status, mTLS demonstrated higher predictive accuracy (Fig. 2C). Although mTLS had a slightly lower predictive efficacy than TNM staging, its prognostic accuracy significantly improved when used in combination, suggesting that mTLS can complement AJCC staging to effectively improve predictive efficacy. Additionally, multicenter univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses revealed that mTLS independently influenced the prognosis of patients with GC (Figs. 2D and S3). Stratified analyses, considering AJCC stage, differentiation level, MSI status, and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) status, demonstrated significantly improved prognoses for patients with mTLS compared to those without, across four independent cohorts (Fig. S4). In the subgroup analyses of stage II patients in the North China cohort and EBV-positive patients in the East China cohort, mTLS showed no statistically significant differences, which potentially owing to the smaller sample size and lower mortality.

In this study, we quantified immune cell subsets in different TLS subtypes, including CD3+, CD4+ helper, CD8+ cytotoxic, CD45RO+ activation memory, FOXP3+ regulatory T cells, and CD45+ leukocytes to elucidate their immune microenvironmental characteristics (Fig. S5A). In patients with mTLS, there was a significant elevation in the densities of CD3+, CD4+, CD8+, CD45+, and CD45RO+ lymphocytes within the tumor microenvironment, whereas imTLS and non-TLS samples showed an accumulation of FOXP3+ regulatory T cells (Fig. S5B). The numbers of CD3+, CD4+, CD8+, CD45+ and CD45RO+ lymphocytes differed significantly among the three TLS subtypes in the core of tumor region (CT) compared with those in the invasive margin region (IM). In mTLS tumors, the infiltration of these immune cells was skewed towards the CT region (Figs. 2E and S5C), resembling an inflamed phenotype. Thus, we compared the immunophenotypic compositions of different TLS subtypes. The mTLS tumors (53.85%) showed more inflamed features than the imTLS (22.60%) and noTLS (24.32%) samples, which were predominantly of the excluded and desert phenotypes (Figs. 2F and S5D). This suggests that mTLS are associated with a more aggressive T cell-driven immune reaction. Additionally, a significantly greater proportion of patients with mTLS exhibited a CPS ≥ 5 compared to those with imTLS and noTLS (Fig. 2G), further supporting the link between mTLS and immunity activation.

Owing to the significant differences in prognosis and immune microenvironment among mTLS, imTLS, and noTLS, the term mTLS-Ne will be used to refer to both imTLS and noTLS. The associations between mTLS classification and clinicopathologic features or molecular subtypes were determined using the x2 test. The results indicated that mTLS had significant correlations with AJCC stage and MSI status. Additionally, mTLS-Po tumors were associated with a lower incidence of lymph node metastases and weaker tumor infiltration (Table S3). The prevalence of MSI-H was found to be greater in mTLS-Po tumors compared to mTLS-Ne (Table S3 and Fig. S5E). This finding suggests that mTLS may reflect MSI-H characteristics to some extent. Prior research has demonstrated that MSI-H correlates with heightened immune infiltration and a higher immunotherapy response rate10,18,19, supporting the use of mTLS as a biomarker in tumor immunotherapy.

The mTLS classification also partially mirrors the spatial distribution patterns of T-cells. Effector T cells (Teffs) serve as the main agents of the tumor immune response. The distribution of Teffs within the tumor nests and stroma were assessed using CD8A and GZMB as markers to elucidate the relationship between Teffs and mTLS subtypes. In mTLS-Po tumors, pronounced infiltration of Teffs was observed, particularly within the tumor nests in the CT region, suggesting their potential anti-tumor activity (Fig. S6A, B). In contrast, the distribution of Teffs in the mTLS-Ne tumors was relatively homogeneous and lacked dense regional infiltration (Fig. S6C). The distribution patterns of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) were explored across different TLS subtypes (Fig. S7A). The mTLS-Ne tumors exhibited predominant infiltration of M2 TAMs in the CT and IM regions compared to the mTLS-Po tumors (Fig. S7B, C). In contrast, the density of M1 TAMs in mTLS-Po tumors was significantly higher in the CT region, indicating the presence of an immune-activated environment (Fig. S7B, C). Additionally, there was a trend of aggregation of CD68+INOS+ M1 TAMs in the IM region of mTLS-Po tumors, although this trend was less significant, due likely to sample size limitations. The spatial distribution of multiple TAMs in tumors was also examined. In mTLS-Po tumors, there was a tendency for M2 TAMs, marked by CD68+CD206+ and CD68+CD163+CD206+, to gather in the IM; however, there was no substantial difference in the distribution of CD68+CD163+ TAMs between the CT and IM areas (Fig. S7D). In the case of mTLS-Ne tumors, an accumulation of CD68+CD163+ TAMs was noted in the CT region, while the presence of CD68+CD206+ TAMs showed an opposite pattern (Fig. S7D).

Overall, the density of M2 TAMs was low in mTLS-Po samples; however, in mTLS-Ne tumors, M2 TAMs were clustered in the CT region, and there was an increasing trend in their distribution from the IM region to the CT region. These results suggest that mTLS-Ne tumors are immunosuppressive, whereas mTLS-Po tumors have an active anti-tumor immune response and may be more responsive to immunotherapy, which is consistent with previous studies that defined mTLS as a marker of responsiveness to immunotherapy.

Spatial co-localization of mTLS and B cells in GC

We utilized 10X Genomics’ single-cell transcriptome sequencing (scRNA-seq) and Visium spatial transcriptomics to investigate the impact of mTLS on the spatial structure of the tumor microenvironment (TME). The scRNA-seq data were subjected to quality control, and 48,893 cells were categorized into 12 cell types (myeloid cells: CD14, AIF1; mast cells: TPSAB1; endothelial: VWF, fibroblasts: LUM, DCN; pericytes: RGS5; smooth muscle: DES; epithelial: KRT8; CD4+ T cells: CD3D, CD4, IL7R; CD8+ T cells: CD3D, CD8A; B cells: CD79A; NK cells: KLRD1; proliferating cells: MKI67) (Figs. S8 and 9). A comprehensive description is provided of the cellular diversity in the tumor microenvironment of GC by revealing the pathways that are specifically enriched in differentially expressed genes across various cell types

Using the RCTD algorithm20 for deconvolution analysis, spatial transcriptome data from 24 patients with GC (13 mTLS-Po and 11 mTLS-Ne) were analyzed in combination with single-cell data (Table S4). In mTLS-Ne tumors, B and T cells appeared in reduced numbers within the tumor area and were diffusely distributed (Figs. S10 and 11). In contrast, the spatial distribution of the TME in mTLS-Po tumors exhibited a unique pattern. B and T cells, notably CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, were mainly clustered in the mTLS region, and fibroblasts were slightly more abundant in the mTLS region than in the tumor region (Figs. 3A, B, S10, and S12). This distinct spatial distribution pattern of the TME was observed in both sets of tumor samples. This study suggests a potential link between the mTLS and B cells, T cells, and fibroblasts in GC. Furthermore, by performing differential gene expression analysis of mTLS regions (regions identified by HE with multiplex immunohistochemistry [mIHC] or immunohistochemistry [IHC], Fig. 3C) and tumor regions, combined with cross-validation of five mTLS-Po tumors, incorporating genes with an AUC value > 0.75 in at least four mTLS-Po tumors, an mTLS-specific gene set was defined consisting of 22 genes, including five B cell marker genes (CD79A, MS4A1, BLK, BANK1, and BCL11A), three B cell related immunomodulatory genes (CD37, TNFRSF13C, and IKZF1), four fibroblast related cytoskeletal and migration genes (ARHGAP15, EVL, WIPF1, and CORO1A), three cell proliferation and apoptosis-related genes (LIMD2, NIBAN3, and TRAF3IP3), and seven cell signaling-related genes (CXCR4, CD53, INPP5D, LTB, RASGRP2, FCMR, and CLEC2D) (Fig. S13A, B). This specific set of genes showed high discriminatory power in the initial 5 mTLS-Po samples (AUC values of 0.91 to 0.94) and demonstrated robustness after validation in other samples (AUC values of 0.80 to 0.94) (Fig. S13C).

Owing to the predominance of B cell-related genes in a specific gene set, we focused on B cells. The findings indicated significant spatial co-localization of fibroblasts with B cells in mTLS-Po and mTLS-Ne tumors (Fig. S10). Previous studies have demonstrated that chemokines such as CXCL12 can cause directed migration of PCs in chronic inflammatory tissues, bone marrow, and lymph node medulla21–23. Therefore, RCTD were utilized to spatially re-localize B cells, including naïve B cells, memory B cells, germinal center B cells, and PCs, as well as fibroblast subpopulations, such as CXCL12+ and CXCL14+ fibroblasts, which were identified in the single-cell data (Fig. S14). All B cell types in mTLS-Po tumors were primarily located in the mTLS region. However, some scattered PCs were still present at multiple tumor sites away from the mTLS. There was clear colocalization of CXCL12+ and CXCL14+ fibroblasts, suggesting a possible role for chemokines in this process (Figs. S15 and 3D).

Somatic hypermutation and clonal selection occur in the mTLS region

The observed co-localization of B cell subtypes in the mTLS region suggests a potential B cell-adaptive immune response. In this process, B cell receptor (BCR) sequences undergo somatic hypermutation (SHM), which drives the expansion of higher-affinity clonotypes through clonal selection (CS) mechanisms. To test this hypothesis, it was necessary to probe the BCR sequences in greater depth by increasing the sample sequencing depth to 200,000 reads/spot for some samples to ensure adequate coverage of the CDR3 region for spatial BCR analysis. We captured 201 immunoglobulin (Ig) heavy and 473 light chains (Table S5). The mTLS region exhibited the greatest diversity of unique Ig light chain clonotypes (Fig. 4A). By comparing the number of mutations and the mutation rate in the V region of the Ig light chain in the two regions, it was found that the peak of mutation occurred more frequently in the tumor region, and the degree of mutation in the mTLS region was lower than that in the tumor region; however, a high degree of mutation was observed in the region surrounding the mTLS (Fig. 4A). This finding suggests that SHM occurred in the mTLS region, and PCs subsequently migrated throughout the tumor.

As bulk sequencing can capture the CDR3 region in a comprehensive and deep manner, the bulk sequencing data of 62 GC tumors (including seven paired spatial transcriptome samples) were analyzed to investigate SHM and CS in mTLS-Po tumors. Tumors were divided into high and low mTLS signature groups, based on the median expression levels of the mTLS-specific gene set. Relative to the low group, the high group exhibited a notable elevation in the level of somatic mutations in the V region of the Ig heavy/light chain (Fig. 4B). The Ig clonotypes displayed diversity, with a high proportion of Ig clonotypes having a clonal count of more than 200, pointing to a continuous and active immunological reaction in the high group samples (Fig. 4C). Pathway enrichment and gene set enrichment analysis indicated the enhancement of pathways and genes associated with immune activation, antigen presentation, immune checkpoints, and tumor killing in the high group (Fig. S16A–D). B and T cell proportions were markedly elevated in the high group compared to the low group, which was consistent with the results of the spatial transcriptome data deconvolution of mTLS-Po and mTLS-Ne tumors (Figs. S16E and S11). This finding suggests that patients with mTLS-Po tumors may benefit from immunotherapy. The mTLS-specific gene set was applied to the only available GC immunotherapy transcriptome sequencing cohort (PRJEB25780), which lacked pathological tissue data, revealing that it effectively differentiated patients who responded to immunotherapy (Fig. S16F).

To determine the relationship and location between the amplified Ig heavy-chain clonotypes, the spatial transcriptomic data was assessed for clonality. Clonality was defined as presence of the same Ig heavy-chain V-region gene family, J-region gene, and CDR3 region. Additionally, the maximum difference threshold between the CDR3 sequences within the V region was set at 15%. In total, 28 families of Ig heavy-chain clones containing BCR sequences were identified, ranging from two to nine species. A single sequence appeared up to 80 times and was scattered across 50 spots (Fig. 4D). To confirm the validity of BCR analysis of the spatial transcriptome data, BCR analysis was performed on paired bulk sequencing data (P1 samples). This analysis confirmed the presence of 10 Ig heavy-chain CDR3 sequences that co-occurred in both the spatial transcriptome and bulk sequencing data (Fig. 4D). In addition, spatial localization analysis revealed that Ig heavy chains of the same clonal family were present in both mTLS and distant tumor regions (Fig. 4E). This suggests that B cells migrate between the mTLS and tumor regions. Although the limited number of Ig clonotypes detected in the mTLS-Ne tumors (Table S5) made their analysis difficult, this finding supports the notion that mTLS are pivotal in the generation of intratumoral PCs.

Collectively, these data suggest that mTLS facilitate B cells maturation, leading to PCs selection, clonal expansion, and migration to various regions of the tumor.

PCs derived from mTLS infiltrate the tumor via fibroblasts

To investigate the mechanism of PC migration, mIHC was performed to confirm the presence of MZB1+ PCs in the mTLS region. PCs were surrounded by abundant CXCL12+ DCN+ and CXCL14+ DCN+ fibroblasts in the mTLS (Fig. 5A, B). They aggregated along the distribution of the fibroblasts and were embedded in a compact network of CXCL12+ DCN+ and CXCL14+ DCN+ fibroblasts (Fig. 5B), and through quantitative analysis it was discovered that PCs were closely positioned to both fibroblast types, exhibiting mean distances of 21.9 µm and 22.2 µm across 10 samples, respectively (Fig. 5C). These findings confirm the colocalization of PCs with CXCL12+ and CXCL14+ fibroblasts at the transcriptional level (Fig. 3D) and support the notion that mTLS-derived PCs spread intratumorally with the assistance of the fibroblast network.

MPS forecasts the effectiveness of neoadjuvant immunotherapy in GC

Given the observation that PC-derived mTLS in mTLS-Po tumors infiltrates tumor regions, we posited that these tumors harbor a higher count of PCs compared to mTLS-Ne samples. Consequently, we propose that PCs abundance could serve as a biomarker to discriminate between gastroscopic biopsy sample types, thereby facilitating the prediction of neoadjuvant immunotherapy efficacy. To test this hypothesis, we developed MPS based on the staining density of the PC marker gene, MZB1, in GC (Fig. S14B). The East China cohort was used as the training cohort, and three outlying cohorts were used for validation purposes. By setting 47.7 as the optimal threshold through the F1 score, the validation cohort confirmed that MPS, as determined by IHC, effectively distinguished between mTLS-Po and mTLS-Ne tumors (Figs. 6A, B and S17).

In clinical practice, patients with GC receiving neoadjuvant therapy can only be assessed using gastroscopic biopsy tissues. Our previous study demonstrated that the accuracy of immunohistochemical scoring is comparable to that of postoperative samples when the area of gastroscopic biopsy samples exceeds 0.16 mm224. Based on this assessment, MPS was evaluated in 59 patients undergoing neoadjuvant anti-PD-1 therapy. Of these, 24 were MPSHigh and 35 were MPSLow (Figs. 6C and S18A). Following neoadjuvant immunotherapy, MPSHigh patients exhibited a greater proportion of tumor regression compared to MPSLow patients (tumor regression grade, TRG 0–1 vs 2–3: MPSHigh = 62.5%, MPSLow = 8.6%; P < 0.001; Fig. 6D, E). Imaging results showed four complete responses (CR) and 14 partial responses (PR) in MPSHigh patients, resulting in an ORR of 75%, which markedly surpassing the 20% ORR observed in MPSLow patients (Figs. 6F and S18B). Kaplan–Meier survival curves showed that MPSLow patients exhibited a higher postoperative recurrence rate than MPSHigh patients, with a significantly shorter recurrence-free survival (RFS). In contrast, the statistical difference in RFS between patients with CPS ≥ 5 and CPS < 5 was not significant (Fig. 6G). Additionally, MPSHigh patients who underwent neoadjuvant immunotherapy exhibited decreased ypT, ypN, and ypTNM stages (Table S6).

To control for the interference of combining immunotherapy and chemotherapy in the neoadjuvant immunotherapy cohort with the outcome assessment, 70 patients were included who underwent only neoadjuvant chemotherapy as a control group (Fig. S16A and Table S7). There were no significant differences in ypT, ypN, and ypTNM staging in the neoadjuvant chemotherapy-only cohort of MPSHigh compared with MPSLow patients (Table S8). When comparing MPSHigh patients in the neoadjuvant immunotherapy and neoadjuvant chemotherapy-only cohorts, the neoadjuvant immunotherapy cohort showed higher ORR, more pronounced tumor regression, reduced rates of postoperative recurrence, and lower postoperative stage (ORR: neoadjuvant immunotherapy cohort = 75%; neoadjuvant chemotherapy = 20%; Table S9). Although the sample size limited the statistical significance, the observed trend suggests that MPS may exclusively forecast the response to anti-PD-1 therapy, rather than chemotherapy. This result also implies that MPS is potentially generalizable and equally applicable to combination therapies involving anti-PD-1 treatments.

Previous research has shown that patients harboring MSI-H or EBV-positive tumors exhibit greater responsiveness to immunotherapy; nevertheless, patients without MSI-H or EBV-positive patients can also derive advantages from immunotherapy25. The data suggests that MPS can identify MSS/MSI-L (ORR: MPSHigh = 75.0%, MPSLow = 18.2%) and EBV-negative (ORR: MPSHigh = 77.3%, MPSLow = 15.2%) patients who benefit from immunotherapy (Fig. S18C). Thus, MPS may provide guidance for treatment selection in MSS/MSI-L and EBV-negative patients. In conclusion, MPS offers a new treatment option for patients with MSI and EBV subtypes. This approach is expected to serve as an alternative strategy for treating these subtypes. However, the neoadjuvant immunotherapy cohort included only six patients with MSI-H (10.2%) and four EBV-positive patients (6.8%), constraining further investigative efforts.

Currently, the presence of CPS ≥ 5 and inflamed phenotypes is recognized as biomarkers that can be responsive to ICI therapy26–28. Therefore, the predictive value of MPS, CPS, and inflammatory phenotypes were assessed for ICI therapy. The findings indicate that MPS consistently outperformed CPS and inflamed phenotypes in efficacy, irrespective of the application of RECIST or TRG criteria (Figs. 6H and S18D). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses confirmed a robust association between MPS and outcomes following neoadjuvant immunotherapy (Figs. 6I and S18E). The discriminatory power of MPS in combination with CPS was further explored. Patients were divided into four groups through a combined evaluation of MPS and CPS. Type A patients (MPSHigh, CPS ≥ 5) had the highest ORR (92.3%) with a percentage of 76.9% achieving TRG 0–1 and were identified as the group most likely to benefit from neoadjuvant immunotherapy. In contrast, type D patients (MPSLow, CPS < 5) had an ORR of only 14.3% and a percentage of TRG 0–1 of only 5.3%, making them unsuitable for neoadjuvant immunotherapy (Figs. 6J and S18F). The survival analysis revealed that the combination of MPS and CPS significantly predicted the long-term outcomes for patients undergoing neoadjuvant immunotherapy. (Fig. S18G). Furthermore, it was discovered that 50% of patients with CPS of ≥ 5, displaying the inflamed phenotype, did not respond to immunotherapy and showed lower MPS scores (Fig. 6K).

Together, these findings imply that MPS serves as a reliable standalone predictor for the effectiveness of neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Additionally, the combined assessment of MPS and CPS may further enhance the accuracy of identifying patients who would benefit from immunotherapy.

Discussion

This study, based on data from seven distinct medical institutions, showed significant differences in prognosis and TME between mTLS-Po and mTLS-Ne. In mTLS-Po tumors, a significant influx of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells was observed in the tumor region, particularly the high density of CD8+GZMB+ Teffs in the tumor nests in the CT region, reflecting their potential advantage in tumor-targeted migration29. Furthermore, a significant presence of M1 TAMs (CD68+INOS+) was detected in the tumor region, complementing the highly inflammatory microenvironment defined by a high abundance of Teff cells and the associated positive prognosis. Together, these results highlight the critical role of a preactivated anti-tumor immune microenvironment for achieving an immunotherapeutic response30–32. In contrast to the increase in Treg abundance in the inflammatory TME described in the literature33, this study found a trend towards a decrease in Treg abundance in the CT region of mTLS-Po tumors, suggesting that the immune milieu of mTLS-Po may be less immunosuppressive. Regarding immune checkpoints33, this study’s results are consistent with previous findings that immune checkpoints are highly expressed in mTLS-Po tumors. This phenomenon may reflect an immune-homeostatic regulatory mechanism present within the tumor, not only as a result of being propelled by neoplastic cells34. Hence, immunotherapy facilitates tumor eradication by altering the equilibrium within the tumor milieu.

Using the integrated analysis of multimodal data such as mIHC, scRNA-seq, spatial transcriptomics, and RNA-seq, it was also confirmed that mTLS serve as a site of SHM and CS tumor-infiltrating PCs and that mTLS-derived PCs can migrate to the tumor region under the guidance of fibroblasts (Fig. 7). Accordingly, we constructed MPS applicable to GC tissues based on differences in the abundance of PC infiltration and categorized patients with GC into two immune statuses, MPSHigh and MPSLow, corresponding to mTLS-Po and mTLS-Ne, respectively. Although MPS reflects the abundance of plasma cells and shows high concordance with histologically validated mTLS regions, we recognize that its use is primarily a transcriptional and immunohistochemical proxy. The definitive identification of mTLS should still rely on structural features such as organized lymphoid architecture and high endothelial venules (HEVs). Thus, MPS serves as a surrogate readout that complements, rather than replaces, structural confirmation. Notably, based on single-cell transcriptomic profiling, tumor-infiltrating plasma cells were classified into IgA- and IgG-producing subtypes. Subsequent immunohistochemical validation confirmed that IgG was the predominant immunoglobulin subtype enriched in both mTLS_Po and MPS^High samples, suggesting a key role of IgG-producing plasma cells in the mTLS-associated anti-tumor immune response (Fig. S19, Table S12). Several clinical trials have demonstrated that neoadjuvant immunotherapy significantly improves RFS and OS in patients with resectable tumors. However, PD-L1 expression, MSI, EBV status, and TMB are known biomarkers with insufficient robustness for forecasting treatment response to ICIs25,35. To date, biomarkers for forecasting the outcomes of neoadjuvant immunotherapy, especially in GC, remain insufficiently identified.

mTLS play a crucial role in SHM and CS of tumor-infiltrating PCs in GC.

PCs derived from mTLS can migrate to the tumor region under the guidance of fibroblasts.

MZB1, also known as the marginal zone and B1 cell-specific protein, has previously been recognized as a biomarker for high CD8+ T cell recruitment in patients with pancreatic cancer undergoing neoadjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy36, and is also involved in mediating IgA secretion to inhibit the progression and worsening of colorectal cancer37. High MZB1 expression is also strongly associated with poor prognosis in adverse outcomes in individuals with estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer38. This study demonstrates the predictive significance of MZB1 for the response to neoadjuvant immunotherapy in GC and establishes that MPS is superior to two recognized biomarkers, PD-L1 and CD8, in predicting outcomes of preoperative immunotherapy. Additionally, MPS accurately identified the beneficiaries of patients with MSS/MSI-L and EBV-negative. Furthermore, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses demonstrated that MPS, in conjunction with PD-L1, could enhance the precision in predicting patients likely to respond to neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Patients with MPSHigh and CPS ≥ 5 were found to have the greatest potential for deriving benefit from neoadjuvant immunotherapy. In contrast, patients with MPSLow and a CPS < 5 exhibit a low response rate to neoadjuvant immunotherapy, are susceptible to postoperative relapse, and may not be appropriate candidates for neoadjuvant immunotherapy because of potential immunotherapy-related adverse events.

Tumors with high PD-L1 expression and an inflamed phenotype are generally deemed responsive to ICIs treatment26–28. However, some patients remain unresponsive to treatment. These patients tended to have low MPS, which was equivalent to a low abundance of PC infiltration. Tumor-infiltrating PCs generate antibodies specific to tumor antigens, initiating antibody-mediated cytotoxic effects that lead to the destruction of tumor cells. Also, antibodies bound to tumor cells may activate the immune response through C1q attachment, triggering the complement cascade, and engaging Fcγ receptors on NK cells and macrophages39,40. A limited quantity of neoplastic cells recognized for killing can release sufficient amounts of tumor antigens to amplify T-cell responses and lower antigenic thresholds, resulting in better therapeutic responses to ICIs41,42. PCs could be pivotal in orchestrating the cellular and humoral responses of adaptive immunity, resulting in an effective immune surveillance mechanism that not only helps shrink tumors, but also remodels the tumor microenvironment and promotes a durable immune response. However, it has not been confirmed whether mTLS-derived PCs in GC contribute to the generation of anti-tumor antibodies. Further studies are required to test this hypothesis.

MPS has a significant advantage because of its wide applicability, being applicable to not only postoperative pathological samples but also preoperative gastroscopy biopsies, providing favorable conditions for clinical translation. In cases where gastroscopy-derived biopsy tissue confirms a gastric cancer (GC) diagnosis, subsequent immunohistochemical analysis for MZB1 on the same biopsy specimens can obtain MPS, which can guide neoadjuvant therapeutic strategies without the need for additional invasive testing. Although MPS shows promise for the treatment of GC, this investigation is subject to certain constraints. Initially, the study utilized retrospective data, potentially leading to selection bias in the cohort. Furthermore, the cohort undergoing neoadjuvant immunotherapy was relatively small.

In addition, we performed an exploratory analysis based on available Lauren classification data and found no statistically significant association between TLS status and Lauren subtype distribution (p = 0.1416; see Supplementary Table S11). This result is consistent with previous studies reporting no consistent correlation between TLS presence and Lauren classification43. While Lauren classification remains an established histopathological parameter with prognostic relevance in gastric cancer, our findings suggest that its association with TLS-related immune features may be limited. Nevertheless, further investigations in larger and more comprehensive cohorts are warranted to clarify the interplay between histological subtypes and TLS-associated immune landscapes, and to support the clinical application of MPS.

Methods

Patients selection for four cohorts

This study enrolled postoperative gastric cancer tissue samples from 1009 patients across seven distinct medical centers in various Chinese regions. The specimens were preserved in formalin and encased in paraffin. Among these, 435 patients were from Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (FMUUH, Fuzhou, China), who received D2 radical gastrectomy during 2012 to 2015 were studied to evaluate the prognostic significance of mTLS. A discovery cohort of 194 tissue samples was randomly selected to elucidate the characteristics of the tumor microenvironment. Additionally, the study encompassed 574 patients from six additional institutions who underwent D2 radical gastrectomy from September 2008 to March 2016. These comprised 86 patients from Liaoning Cancer Hospital & Institute (LCH, Shenyang, China); 91 from the Bethune First Affiliated Hospital of Jilin University (JUBFAH, Changchun, China); 90 from the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical College (BMCFAH, Bengbu, China); 161 from the First Affiliated Hospital of the University of Science and Technology of China (USTCFAH, Hefei, China); 54 from the Guangxi Medical University Affiliated Tumor Hospital (GMUATH, Nanning, China); and 92 from the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (KMUFAH, Kunming, China). Data collected from seven distinct medical institutions were consolidated into four cohorts according to their geographical origins. Tables S1 and S2 provide the clinicopathological details of the gastric cancer patients included in our cohorts.

The eligibility criteria for this study were: (1) histopathologically confirmed advanced gastric cancer; (2) absence of other malignancies or distant metastases; (3) accessible follow-up information and clinicopathologic data; and (4) assessment of TNM staging in postoperative gastric cancer samples according to the 2010 International Union Against Cancer guidelines. Criteria for exclusion included: (1) mortality occurring within one month after surgery; (2) receipt of preoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy. All patients were administered fluorouracil-based chemotherapy as a standard protocol. All procedures performed in studies involving human subjects were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients selection for neoadjuvant therapy cohorts

To evaluate mTLS’s predictive capacity for neoadjuvant immunotherapy outcomes, an additional cohort of 198 patients with locally advanced gastric cancer, who underwent neoadjuvant treatment between March 2019 and November 2021 at FMUUH, was analyzed. Of these, 69 were not considered due to insufficient tumor regions or absence of biopsy specimens, resulting in the inclusion of 129 patients, whose baseline characteristics is detailed in Table S7. Every patient underwent treatment with a regimen incorporating fluorouracil and paclitaxel. Fifty-nine patients receiving camrelizumab (anti-PD-1 therapy) were categorized under the neoadjuvant immunotherapy cohort, whereas the other 70, who were treated exclusively with chemotherapy, were placed in the neoadjuvant chemotherapy group. The efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy was independently evaluated by two professional radiologists following the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST 1.1) guidelines, with final outcomes confirmed after cross-review. Postoperative pathological tissues were assessed based on the Becker criteria to ascertain the tumor regression grade (TRG). Pathologic complete response (pCR) was characterized by the complete absence of viable tumor cells in the tumor bed and lymph nodes following neoadjuvant treatment. This research project received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Union Hospital affiliated with Fujian Medical University, approval number 2023QH047. Ethical approval was granted by the ethics committees of all seven medical centers involved, with informed consent obtained from all patients prior to tissue collection.

Collection of clinical prognostic information

Patient prognostic data were acquired through a meticulously executed follow-up regimen—carried out by professionally trained physicians employing a variety of methods tailored to individual circumstances, including outpatient consultations, written communications, telephonic engagement, electronic mail, and domiciliary visits. Our center’s follow-up schedule mandated clinical evaluations at approximately three-month intervals during the initial year post-surgery, with subsequent biannual assessments in the second year post-operation. Survivors were entered into a regular follow-up program, extending for a minimum duration of three years. Overall survival time was delineated as the period from the date of surgical intervention to either the date of last known contact or the patient’s demise. For patients experiencing recurrence, disease-free survival was defined as the interval from the date of surgery to the first occurrence of recurrence; for patients without recurrence, it spanned from the surgical date to the final follow-up interaction. This follow-up protocol was uniformly implemented by clinical practitioners across the seven participating centers.

Immunohistochemistry and evaluation

For immunohistochemical (IHC) evaluation, tissue sections, prepared at 4 µm thickness from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded samples, were subjected to deparaffinization with xylene and sequentially rehydrated using a series of ethanol dilutions. Antigen recovery was facilitated by heating in a citrate buffer (pH 6.0) under high pressure for 3 min. To quench endogenous peroxidase activity, sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min at ambient temperature. Non-specific interactions were minimized by treating the sections with 5% normal goat serum for one hour.

Primary antibodies were administered to the tissue samples and subjected to an overnight incubation at 4 °C. Details on the use of primary antibodies can be found in Table S10. Following a rinse with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the samples were then exposed to horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-linked secondary antibodies for a duration of 30 minutes at ambient temperature. Visualization of the antigen-antibody complexes was achieved using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as a chromogen substrate, followed by counterstaining with Harris’s hematoxylin.

The specificity of the immunostaining was validated by omitting the primary antibody in negative control sections. Sections known to express the target antigen were included as positive controls in each batch of staining. Images were captured using the Motic EasyScan system (Motic, Xiamen, China) and Nikon E200 microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Microsatellite instability (MSI) status was determined utilizing a panel of four mismatch repair proteins: MSH2, MSH6, MLH1, and PMS2. Staining assessment criteria, as delineated in Fig. S5E, required positive expression localized to the cell nucleus. If all four proteins displayed positive expression within the tumor sample, the sample was classified as having proficient mismatch repair (pMMR), indicative of MSI-L or Microsatellite Stability (MSS). Conversely, deficiency in any single protein denoted mismatch repair deficiency (dMMR), correlating with MSI-H. The Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) status was ascertained via in situ hybridization using an EBER probe (ISH-6021, ZSGB-BIO, China; Fig. S5E).

Quantitative assessments of immune cell markers and MZB1 Positive Score (MPS) were derived by calculating the density of positive cells within a unit field of view. Experienced pathologists randomly selected five 200× magnification fields at both the invasive margin (IM) and core of tumor (CT). Using Image Pro Plus software and manual counting, the number of positive cells per field was documented. Cell density for each field was determined by the formula: positive cell count/field area, with the overall density being the mean of the five fields. Manual counts were assisted by the ‘Measurement’ labeling feature in Image Pro Plus software. The densities obtained by software and manual counting were compared; if the discrepancy was within 5%, the average of the two was used. If the difference exceeded this threshold, another pathologist performed a recount, and the average of the two closest values was taken. These evaluations were conducted blind to any clinical data. When the F1 score reaches the maximum, the corresponding cut-off value is taken as the cut-off value to distinguish between MPSHigh and MPSLow. F1 score = 2 * (Precision * Recall)/(Precision + Recall). Precision = True Positive/(True Positive + False Positive), Recall = True Positive/(True Positive + False Negative).

Patient phenotypes—characterized as inflamed, excluded, or desert—were categorized through CD8+ immunohistochemistry on tissue sections and criteria established in prior research. PD-L1 expression was computed using the Combined Positive Score (CPS), where CPS = sum of PD-L1 positive cell counts/total tumor cell count * 100. PD-L1 positive cells included PD-L1 positive tumor cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages. All assessments were independently conducted by two senior pathologists for the entire sample set.

Multiplex immunohistochemical and evaluation

In the investigation of cellular phenotypes and microenvironmental interactions within formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections, a sophisticated multiplex IHC staining protocol was employed utilizing the PANO 7-plex IHC kit, in strict accordance with the provided manufacturer’s guidelines.

Initially, tissue sections underwent antigen retrieval via microwave-mediated heat induction to expose antigenic sites, enhancing antibody accessibility and binding efficiency. Subsequent to antigen retrieval, sections were incubated with a meticulously selected panel of primary antibodies (Table S10). Following primary antibody incubation, sections were exposed to secondary antibodies conjugated with HRP, setting the stage for the subsequent tyramide signal amplification (TSA) step. The TSA process, leveraging the catalytic activity of HRP, resulted in the deposition of fluorophores in the vicinity of the enzyme, markedly amplifying the signal associated with each antigen. This step is pivotal for enhancing the detection sensitivity of low-abundance proteins. Between each staining cycle, microwave heat repair was judiciously applied to prevent cross-reactivity and ensure the specificity of subsequent antibody applications. Nuclear counterstaining was achieved using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), providing a clear demarcation of cell nuclei and facilitating the contextual interpretation of antigen localization within the cellular architecture.

For the acquisition of high-resolution multispectral images, an Olympus VS200 MTL system was employed, capturing detailed spatial distribution of the stained antigens. The images were subsequently analyzed with the InForm Image Analysis Software (PerkinElmer, USA) to extract quantitative data from designated regions of interest (ROIs). InForm software exhibits the capability to accurately identify and enumerate positive cells within ROIs by setting appropriate thresholds, thereby calculating the density and proportion of target cells present. Furthermore, the software supports automated discrimination between tumoral nests (panCK-positive) and stromal compartments (panCK-negative), enabling the collection of quantitative data from various distinct ROIs.

In order to quantitatively analyze the spatial distribution of fibroblasts and plasma cells within tissue specimens, we employed the HALO Image Analysis Software (Indica Labs). Initially, collected fluorescent images were imported into the HALO platform, where necessary pre-processing steps, including contrast enhancement and noise reduction, were implemented to optimize the visualization of cellular markers. Subsequently, the software’s automated cell recognition capabilities were harnessed, utilizing the specific fluorescence tags associated with fibroblasts and plasma cells to identify and categorize each cell type accurately. Following identification, the HALO software’s integrated spatial analysis tools were utilized to compute the mean spatial separation between fibroblasts and plasma cells. This analysis was predicated on calculating the Euclidean distance from the centroid of each fibroblast to the nearest plasma cell. Collating the distance measurements for all fibroblast-plasma cell pairs facilitated the determination of the average interspatial cellular distance.

Selection of samples for spatial transcriptome sequencing

TLS are aggregates consisting of CD3+ T lymphocytes and CD20+ B lymphocytes. Only TLS located interstitially or at the invasive margin (within 1 mm of tumor cells) and comprising over 100 cells were considered in the analysis. TLS were deemed mature upon the identification of CD23+ cells. Isolated CD23+ cells, considered indicative of TLS maturity, required dendritic morphology (i.e., the cytoplasm exhibiting dendritic extensions); otherwise, TLS were categorized as immature.

We included 370 patients with GC who underwent D2 gastrectomy at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital between September 2022 and February 2023. After excluding 120 patients who received neoadjuvant therapy, the TLS status of samples from the remaining 250 patients was independently identified by two senior pathologists. Spatial transcriptome sequencing was conducted by Wancheng Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). RNA was isolated from 5 μm sections employing the Qiagen RNeasy FFPE Kit, and only samples with a DV200 (>200 bp RNA fragments) ratio exceeding 50% proceeded to the next experimental phase. Sections were mounted on Sigma–Aldrich Poly Prep slides and allowed to dry overnight before incubation at 60 °C for 2 h. Deparaffinization carried out in accordance with the “Visium CytAssist Spatial Gene Expression for FFPE-Deparaffinization, Decrosslinking, Immunofluorescence Staining & Imaging Protocol” (10× Genomics, CG000519 Rev B). This was followed by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and scanning using a Leica Aperio Versa 8 scanner. After de-crosslinking the H&E-stained sections, full-length human transcriptome probes were introduced to the tissue and crosslinked with target genes. Subsequently, slices were subjected to RNase treatment and permeabilization using the Visium CytAssist system. Finally, the probes were hybridized with spatially barcoded oligonucleotides in the capture areas, creating a spatial transcriptomic library.

Transcriptome sequencing

This study encompassed a cohort of 62 patients who underwent D2 radical gastrectomy for gastric cancer at the Union Hospital affiliated with Fujian Medical University between 2017 and 2021. Transcriptome sequencing was conducted by three distinct facilities: 29 cases were sequenced by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), 26 cases were processed by KangCe Bio-tech Inc. (Wuhan, China), and the remaining 7 cases were processed by Wancheng Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). RNA extraction from frozen tumor specimens was performed utilizing the RNeasy Kit. Transcriptome sequencing was carried out on an Illumina HiSeq platform using the TruSeq SR Cluster Kit v3-cBot-Hs (Illumina, CA, USA). Subsequent to sequencing, inter-batch variability was mitigated using the ComBat-seq algorithm, resulting in an integrated FMUUH_RNA-Seq dataset analyzed collectively from data acquired by the three companies.

Acquisition of transcriptome sequencing data for immunotherapy cohort

Transcriptomic data from a gastric cancer immunotherapy cohort (PRJEB25780) were collected, and PB-16-043, PB-16-047, PB-16-048, PB-16-049, PB-16-051, PB-16-052, PB-16-055, PB-16-056, and PB-16-057, and PB-16-066 have been omitted due to HLA mismatches or low coverage of protein-coding genes (<10 M reads).

Sequencing data preprocessing

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) reads were aligned using CellRanger (version 6.0.2) with default parameters. Spatial transcriptomic reads were mapped using SpaceRanger (version 1.3.2) with default settings, incorporating the ‘-reorient-images’ option for automated image calibration. Both alignment processes utilized the human hg38 reference genome.

Single-cell and spatial transcriptome sequencing data processing

For the integration and analysis of scRNA-seq samples, Seurat (version 4.3.0) was utilized. The exclusion criteria for quality control were as follows: (1) a gene count lower than 200 or exceeding 4000; (2) a count number below 500 or above 10000; (3) mitochondrial gene expression above 25%; and (4) identification as doublets by DoubletFinder (version 2.0.3) using default settings. The NormalizeData function was applied for normalization to mitigate sequencing depth effects. Dimensionality reduction was conducted on the top 2000 highly variable genes shared across the majority of samples using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). PCA adjustment was performed using Harmony (version 0.1.1), with samples serving as covariates. An SNN graph encompassing the top 30 principal components was constructed via the FindNeighbors function. Subsequent clustering of cells was achieved using the Louvain algorithm, thereby identifying the representative primary cell types. For the identification of subcellular types, similar steps were followed, including the identification of highly variable genes, dimensionality reduction, batch effect elimination via Harmony, and identifying clusters within the constrained clustering derived from the comprehensive analysis. Marker genes for determining the primary cell types were identified using the COSG function within COSG (version 0.9.0).

The filtered expression matrix obtained through SpaceRanger (version 1.3.2) served as the preliminary data for spatial transcriptomics analysis conducted using Seurat (version 4.3.0). Spots exhibiting fewer than 100 detected genes were excluded. Normalization of individual count matrices was performed employing the Sctransform method.

Cell trajectory analysis

Cell trajectory analysis was executed using Slingshot (version 2.6.0) to examine differentiation among various cell subpopulations, and the results were projected onto UMAP representations. Additionally, CytoTRACE was employed as a method to assess differentiation potential, estimating this potential based on the gene sets included. Both analyses were conducted utilizing the recommended default parameters. No new codes were used in this study; details are described in the “Methodology” section.

The mTLS-specific gene set

Continuous tissue sections were stained for CD4, CD8, CD20, and CD23 to identify spots associated with mTLS. These spots were then manually delineated as mTLS regions using Loupe software (10X Genomics). Differential expression analysis between mTLS regions and tumor regions was performed using the FindMarkers function for five mTLS-Po tumors. Genes that fulfilled the subsequent criteria were chosen for further analysis: a minimum twofold change in expression, an adjusted p-value less than 0.05, and at least a 0.5% differential in expression across various regions. The leave-one-out cross-validation method was utilized for the selected genes, identifying 22 genes that exhibited an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of no less than 0.75 in at least four mTLS-Po tumors. For each spot, a mature TLS signature score was derived by calculating the geometric mean of these 22 genes.

Cellular localization of spatial transcriptome data

A reference matrix was generated using our single-cell atlas generated above, and the cell type composition of each spot was calculated using the full mode of RCTD (v2.2.1) with the recommended default parameters.

Analysis of the B-cell receptor repertoire

BCR repertoire analysis was conducted on both the transcriptome and spatial transcriptome datasets using MIXCR. Given the limited sequencing depth inherent to spatial transcriptomics, the sampling depth was augmented to 200,000 reads per spot to ensure comprehensive coverage of BCR fragments, facilitating a thorough evaluation of the entire CDR3 region within the Ig light- and heavy-chain sequences. MIXCR was used to delineate the clonal subtypes of Ig light- and heavy-chain sequences within the samples. Barcodes provided by 10× Genomics were instrumental in determining the spatial localization of these clonotypes.

Analysis of differential expression and functional enrichment

Analysis of gene expression differences was performed using the DESeq2 package in R, which utilizes a negative binomial distribution model. Genes presenting an adjusted p-value (corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg approach) below 0.05 were recognized as differentially expressed. To explore overrepresented biological functions and pathways, an enrichment analysis of these genes was carried out through the Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene sets for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were sourced from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) to ensure thorough representation of well-established biological states and pathways. The enrichment score (ES) was computed for each gene set to measure its overrepresentation within the ordered gene list. The significance of the ES was assessed through an empirical phenotype-based permutation test, setting a nominal p-value cutoff of 0.05. Additionally, the False Discovery Rate (FDR) was calculated to adjust for the multiple testing scenario, considering gene sets with an FDR below 25% as significantly enriched.

Deconvolution analysis of transcriptomic sequencing data

The BayesPrism method was utilized to perform deconvolutional analysis of transcriptome sequencing data, using gastric cancer single-cell RNA-sequencing profiles as a reference frame for cell-type-specific expression profiles. The quality-controlled transcriptome sequencing data was then entered into the BayesPrism tool along with the reference data to confidently infer the cellular composition of the patients. The recommended default parameters are used for all of the characteristics mentioned above.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were executed using R (version 4.2.2). For continuous variables that did not follow a normal distribution in two groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed, while the Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized for analyses involving multiple groups. Comparison of categorical variables related to clinicopathological features was performed using either the Chi-squared (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the context. Overall survival (OS) estimation was performed via Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, with the log-rank test applied for assessment. Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to ascertain the relationship between clinicopathological factors and OS. Following this, variables demonstrating statistical significance in the univariate analysis (P ≤ 0.05) underwent subsequent examination via multivariate Cox regression analysis. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to determine the association between variables and the outcome of immunotherapy.
