Phylogeography and evolutionary history of Prunus tomentosa in China: guiding germplasm conservation and development
Authors: Tian Wan, Feng An, Liu-Yi Pan, Tian-Hua Zhou, Dong-Qian Shan, Juan Zhang, Yu-Liang Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrunus tomentosa is a fruit tree native to China, with a broad distribution and high conservation value from ornamental, economic, and ecological perspectives. The germplasm resource is valuable for developing new cherry cultivars due to its high adaptability to almost all soil types and climatic conditions. However, little is known about the genetic diversity, phylogeographic structure and historical events, with its abundant genetic information. Here, we clarified the phylogeography of P. tomentosa based on chloroplast DNA (cpDNA) fragments and nuclear ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) across 779 individuals from 42 populations.ResultsWe found 26 unique chloroplast haplotypes and 23 unique ITS haplotypes. Haplotype diversity was high based on cpDNA (Hd = 0.689) and ITS (Hd = 0.877) haplotypes. We observed a significant difference in the genetic differentiation based on Wright’s fixation index (cpDNA: Fst = 0.6077; ITS: Fst = 0.3447). Comparing two genetic differentiation indices, Nst and Gst, it was inferred that the P. tomentosa populations have a phylogeographical structure. Combination of network map, phylogenetic tree, population distribution, and population structure of P. tomentosa haplotype revealed two phylogeographic groups—eastern phylogeographic group (EG) and western phylogeographic group (WG). The divergence time of EG and WG occurred about 12.31 Ma and the common ancestor of P. tomentosa may have emerged during the Miocene period, and a significant correlation was observed between genetic distance and geographical distance of the populations. Signals from cpDNA and ITS data indicate that P. tomentosa has undergone recombination and population expansion. The expansion time was about 5.74 Ka based on the chloroplast data.ConclusionOur findings illuminated the genetic diversity, species divergence and phylogeographical history of P. tomentosa and provided robust genetic evidence to support that Xiaolong Mountains, Hua Mountains, and Qian Mountains might be the glacial refuges of P. tomentosa. Our findings will not only guide conservation and utilization strategies for P. tomentosa but also contribute to the development of germplasm for breeding novel cherry cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08482-1.

Background

Most cultivated fruit trees are domesticated from wild fruit tree species [1]. Compared to cultivars or species, wild fruit tree resources possess greater potential, such as abundant agronomic traits and stronger stress resistance, which can provide excellent genes for breeding superior rootstocks or cultivars [2]. Prunus tomentosa (2n = 16), a deciduous fruit tree belonging to the subgenus Cerasus within genus Prunus (family Rosaceae), is a widely distributed wild cherry species native to China [3]. The natural range encompasses the Northwest, Northeast, North, Southwest, and Southeast of China. Occurring mainly in wild and semi-wild habitats, P. tomentosa has been utilized as a food source for more than 2,000 years [4]. China harbors abundant wild germplasm resources of P. tomentosa with high genetic diversity, comprising various ecological types and retaining ancestral traits [3, 5]. And wild germplasms are the crucial resources for the exploitation and utilization of plants [6]. Additionally, P. tomentosa exhibits remarkable adaptability to almost all climatic conditions and soil types, tolerating environmental extremes such as severe cold, extreme heat and drought [3, 4]. Specifically, P. tomentosa demonstrates exceptional cold tolerance, capable of withstanding extreme temperatures as low as − 40 °C, with its underlying molecular adaptation mechanisms having been elucidated [4, 7]. Unlike most cherry fruit trees, which exhibit self-incompatibility and propagate by artificial or cross-pollination, P. tomentosa is partially self-compatible, and its fruits are resistant to rain-induced cracking and ripen synchronously [4]. These characteristics make P. tomentosa an excellent resource for creating new cultivars.

To date, research on P. tomentosa populations mainly involves genetic diversity and population structure using simple sequence repeats (SSRs) [3, 4, 8]. In our previous study [3], we found P. tomentosa established high genetic diversity, provided an initial assessment of their contemporary population structure and gene flow. It revealed a pattern of significant genetic divergence that correlates with the geographical distribution of the species. And the origin of biodiversity in mountainous regions, where P. tomentosa is predominantly distributed in China, is complex. Historical events, often accompanied by mountain uplift and climatic shifts, their influence on the phylogeographic structure remains to be further elucidated. A more in-depth phylogeographic investigation of P. tomentosa populations from their natural distribution would yield valuable insights into species’ biodiversity patterns, conservation strategies, and facilitate sustainable utilization. Historical events, geographic changes, and climatic oscillations have profoundly influenced the shaping of a population’s distribution, adaptive divergence, and spatial genetic structure [9–11]. Phylogeography is used to identify species and study the evolutionary history of species, particularly to reveal the causes of the formation of hybrids [11–13]. Furthermore, it can infer the post-glacial expansion and migration routes of species, glacial refuges, and the phylogeographic differentiation times among or within species [14, 15]. Understanding species distribution patterns and their evolutionary dynamics is fundamental for effective conservation planning [16]. Phylogeographic studies have revealed phylogeographic structures in P. serrulata [17], P. dielsiana [9], and other fruit trees, such as P. sibirica [12], Musa balbisiana [18], and Pyrus betulifolia [19]. The glacial refuge of Pyrus betulifolia might be near the Ziwuling Mountains and the Funiu Mountains [19]. Malus sylvestris in Europe experienced the processes of population contraction and distribution area differentiation during the Quaternary Ice Age, and there was an independent refuge in Europe [20]. While wild P. pseudocerasus in China [21] and wild P. avium [22] do not have a phylogeographic structure. It is speculated that the P. avium populations in Northern Europe might have been formed by the migration and dispersion of the southern populations from refuges after the Ice Age [22]. However, there are relatively few reports on the phylogeographic structure and historical events of P. tomentosa [4, 8, 23].

Chloroplast DNA (cpDNA) sequences are typically utilized in phylogeography due to their maternal inheritance characteristic and relatively independent evolution, preserving a significant degree of genetic information in the long evolutionary process without gene recombination and genetic recombination deletion [17, 24–26]. Nuclear sequences, specifically the internal transcribed spacer (ITS), provide valuable complementary information [17]. ITS sequences can trace potential hybridization events within a population, due to their universality, ease of amplification, biparental inheritance, and intragenomic uniformity [27]. Consequently, we unified cpDNA and ITS analyses for the first time to explore the phylogeography of P. tomentosa, relying on large-scale sampling (766 individuals across 42 populations). The primary goals of this research are to unveil the spatio-temporal distribution and historical dynamics to better comprehend its genetic diversity and structure and to offer conservation and utilization suggestions for the germplasm resources.

Results

Haplotype diversity and population genetic differentiation

Through sequence alignment and calibration from 779 individuals across 42 populations of P. tomentosa, the combined lengths of the cpDNA sequence and ITS sequence were 2487 bp and 615 bp, respectively. The cpDNA contains an 875 bp petN-psbM sequence, an 812 bp rps16-trnQ sequence, and an 800 bp rps4-trnT-trnL sequence. These three cpDNA sequences demonstrated high diversity (Table S3). The number of haplotypes (h) detected from the chloroplast and ITS were 36 (Fig. S2) and 57 (Fig. S3), respectively. The haplotype diversity (Hd) values were 0.689 and 0.877, respectively, and the nucleotide diversity (π) values were 0.00111 and 0.00378, respectively. The Watterson’s nucleotide polymorphism (θW) values were 0.00226 and 0.00466, respectively (Table 1 and Table S2). However, the nucleotide diversity of cpDNA is relatively low. According to the cpDNA haplotype data, the total gene diversity (Ht = 0.781) of P. tomentosa was significantly greater than the average gene diversity within populations (Hs = 0.317). As expected, both were lower than the genetic diversity of the ribosome. In terms of the ribosomal ITS haplotypes, the difference between Ht (0.921) and Hs (0.734) values is relatively small, with both exhibiting high levels of diversity (Table 1).

The analysis of genetic variation among P. tomentosa populations revealed differences in Analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) based on cpDNA and ITS (Table 2). The genetic differentiation of P. tomentosa populations primarily occurs between populations (Fst = 0.608, Va > Vb), according to the cpDNA data. Conversely, the ITS genetic variation shows a pattern opposite to that of cpDNA, with a genetic differentiation coefficient of 0.345 (Vb > Va), indicating that genetic differentiation mainly exists within populations. Therefore, based on ITS data, genetic differentiation within populations exceeds that between populations. Furthermore, it was found that the P. tomentosa populations exhibit lower levels of gene flow. However, ITS-based Nm (0.475) was significantly higher than cpDNA-based Nm (0.161) (Table 2).

Phylogeographic structure of P. tomentosa revealed by cpDNA and ITS haplotype distributions

The Nst (0.641) and Gst (0.594) values based on cpDNA were both higher than the genetic differentiation coefficient based on ITS (Nst = 0.337, Gst = 0.193). Nst was greater than Gst at both levels and statistical analysis showed a significant difference (P < 0.01) with Nst > Gst at the ITS level (Table 1). Consequently, the distribution of P. tomentosa exhibits a significant phylogeographic structure [28], with closely related haplotypes often occurring in neighboring regions. A Mantel test also revealed a significant correlation between genetic distance (GD) and geographic distance (GGD) among P. tomentosa populations at the levels of cpDNA (R2 = 0.0402, P = 0.0052 < 0.01) and ITS (R2 = 0.2458, P = 0.0001 < 0.001), indicating a very significant correlation (Fig. 1).

Phylogeography and haplotype distribution: insights from cpDNA

A total of 36 chloroplast haplotypes were identified in 42 populations, represented by H1-H36 (Fig. 2A). The most widespread haplotype, H1, was found in 23 of the populations (54.8%) spanning ten provinces or autonomous regions (Figs. 2 and 3A). This was followed by H2, which was found in 15 populations (35.7%) across eight provinces, including Shaanxi, Jilin, Liaoning, Beijing, Tianjin, Shandong, Shanxi, and Henan. The H4 haplotype was present in 13 populations (31.0%), distributed in seven provinces namely Shaanxi, Gansu, Jilin, Liaoning, Beijing, Hebei, and Shanxi. H8 was located in 26.2% (11/42) of the population across six provinces or autonomous regions, while H3 and H9 each appeared in 11 populations—with H3 spanning six provinces and H9 four provinces. Haplotype H11 was located in Shaanxi, Gansu, Beijing, and Shanxi provinces, H25 in Shaanxi, Shanxi, and Liaoning provinces, and H26 in Gansu and Ningxia. From the geographical distribution of haplotypes in the population (Fig. 2A), it is evident that Jilin, Liaoning, Inner Mongolia, Beijing, Hebei, and Tianjin are primarily dominated by haplotype H1. In specific, Jing-YtM and Liao-XshR have numerous haplotypes, exhibiting six haplotypes (H1, H3, H4, and H10-H12) and five haplotypes (H1-H4, and H25) respectively. Furthermore, except the Jing-YtM population which is comprised of wild species, the other three populations in Beijing were either introduced from Northeast China or cultivated. However, the haplotypes of the five populations in Xinjiang are relatively singular, with only H8 detected in Xin-YL, Xin-ShCh, and Xin-ALR. Only H8 haplotypes were found in Chuan-Aba from Sichuan province.

In addition, haplotype diversity was abundant in some provinces, with 26 unique haplotypes identified. The most abundant chloroplast haplotypes were detected in Shaanxi Province (Fig. 2A), where a total of 18 haplotypes were found, among which were ten unique haplotypes (H13-H20, H23, and H24) (Fig. S4). The second province was Gansu, where 13 haplotypes were identified, including eight unique haplotypes (H27-H32 and H36). The Gan-XlM population had the greatest haplotype abundance in Gansu Province (Fig. S4), with ten haplotypes detected, namely H8, H9, H11, and H26-32. However, Gan-ZhC mainly had the haplotype H8. Two unique haplotypes, H21 and H22, were discovered in Shanxi Province, and two unique haplotypes, namely H10 and H12, were also detected in the wild population of Jing-YtM in Beijing. The two populations in Shandong, both introduced from Northeast China, also possessed three endemic haplotypes, namely H5, H6, and H7.

Moreover, the cpDNA haplotype network, reconstructed using the median method, displays the distribution of 779 P. tomentosa individuals across different haplotype branches (Fig. 3A). Based on haplotype frequency, H1 had the highest frequency in the network and formed a haplotype group dominated by EG, totaling 16 haplotypes. Haplotypes H2 and H8 formed another group, WG, containing 20 haplotype types. We constructed phylogenetic trees of 36 haplotypes using Bayesian Inference (BI), which also divided them into two major lineages, EG and WG, with high reliability (posterior probability [PP] = 0.933) (Fig. 3B). These tree results align with the haplotype network (Fig. 3). In conjunction with the haplotype structure (Fig. 2A), EG is predominantly composed of haplotypes H1, H3, and H4, commonly found in the Northeast and North China regions, and additionally detected in individual populations in the Shaanxi North region. Notably, populations from Northeast (Liaoning, Jilin, and populations introduced from Northeast China) and North China (Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei) are dominated by H1 of the EG haplotype group. A few populations from different regions, such as Southern Shaanxi (Shaan-XX, Shaan-YC, and ShaanMC) and the Xin-TKS population from Xinjiang have also been detected with H1. In addition, the more frequent haplotypes in EG are H3 and H4. Contrarily, the higher frequency haplotypes of WG are H8, H2, H9, and H26, predominantly found in Northwest populations. H8 and H9 are mainly discovered in Xinjiang, Qinghai, and Shaanxi populations (Xin-YL, Qing-HD, Shaan-YG, etc.). H26 haplotype was detected in two populations of Ningxia Helan Mountain (Ning-HlM) and Gansu Xiaolong Mountain (Gan-XlM). Whereas H2 was found in a wild population (Yu-SMX) of Henan and an introduced population (Lu-YT_ I) of Shandong. The phylogeographic structure and phylogenetic trees based on haplotypes was further supported by the population structure analysis (Fig. 2 and FiG. S7A). Through SAMOVA, when K > 2, the increase in FCT values was not significant, and newly defined groups often consisted of single populations (Fig. 2, Fig. S6A and Fig. S7). Therefore, the grouping at K = 2 (FCT = 0.675) was retained and the grouping pattern was nearly consistent with the geographic distribution of the haplotypes (Fig. 2 and Fig. S7A). WG included 15 populations (Lu-YT_I, Xin-ShCh, Shaan-YG, Shaan-HM, Gan-XlM, Ning-HlM, Xin-YL, Qing-HD, Shaan-Y’A, Gan-ZhC, Shaan-YX, Xin-ALR, Yu-SMX and Chuan-Aba) and EG included other 27 populations (Fig. 2 and Fig. S7A).

Thus, upon combining the analysis of the network map (Fig. 3A), the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3B), and the population distribution and structure (Fig. 2A) of P. tomentosa chloroplast haplotype, it becomes evident that there are two phylogeographic groups in P. tomentosa. The first is the eastern phylogeographic group (EG), predominantly in Northeast and North China, and the second is the western phylogeographic group (WG), largely in the Northwest.

Population structure analysis based on ribosomal ITS

A total of 57 ITS haplotypes were identified in 42 P. tomentosa populations, labeled as N1 ~ N57 (Fig. S3 and Fig. 4). Haplotype N8 had the highest frequency (Fig. 4 and Fig. S5), covering 30 out of 42 populations, a rate of 71.4%. Aside from Inner Mongolia, N8 was observed in the majority of provinces. The second highest frequencies were of haplotypes N32, N1, and N9 (Fig. 4 and Fig. S5). N1 and N9 were found in 22 populations, corresponding to 52.4% of the total, while N32 was only present in eight populations (19.0%) in Shaanxi, Gansu, and Ningxia provinces. Following these are haplotypes N4, N5, N20, N21, and N12 contributing to 26.2% to 52.4% of populations and found in 11, 12, 10, 7, and 8 provinces, respectively (Fig. S5). Excluding N8, haplotypes N4, N5, N10, and N20 were detected in 10–12 provinces, followed by N1, N2, N7, N9, N11, N12, and N21, each detectable in 6–9 provinces (Fig. S5).

Similar to the chloroplast haplotype, ITS haplotype diversity was also abundant in some provinces, especially in the Northeast region. Meanwhile, there were 23 unique ITS haplotypes, and it was found that the haplotypes of populations from or introduced from the Northeast region exhibited high diversity. Liaoning and Jilin provinces had a total of 29 haplotypes, with 21 and 19 haplotypes, respectively. In addition, Liao-QM, Hebei-ChL_ I. Jing-BG, Ning-HlM, Lu-YT_ I. Xin-UC_ I. Ji-KSh, Liao-DshL, Shaan-HM, and Ji-JL are the ten populations with the most abundant ITS haplotypes (10–15). Among them, the highest count is at Liao-QM, which contains 15 ITS haplotypes. We also found that the provinces of Shaanxi, Gansu, Ningxia, and Qinghai primarily contain the N8 and N32 haplotypes, with a total of nine unique haplotypes detected.

Fifty-seven ITS haplotypes were divided into G1 and G2, constructed through the phylogenetic tree (PP > 0.5) (Fig. 5B and Fig. S5). Based on the ITS haplotype network (Fig. 5B and Fig. S5), G1 is predominantly composed of N8, featuring a total of 40 haplotypes, whereas G2 is mainly composed of N1 and N9, with a total of 17 haplotypes. After combining the geographical distribution of ITS haplotypes (Fig. 4), it is noticeable that haplotypes in the four Northwest provinces, except Xinjiang, are primarily N8 and N32 in G1. Haplotypes like N4 and N8 found in Sichuan also originate from G1 (Fig. 4C). The two principal haplotypes of the wild population from Xinjiang are N9 and N12 from G2 (Figs. 4B and 5B and A). At K = 2, the structure analysis clustered the wild populations primarily into one group, represented in purple. (Fig. S9). On the other hand, the haplotypes of the introduced population (Xin-UC_I) bear similarities with those of the populations from the Northeast (introduced region), featuring more haplotypes. Statistics reveal that the N1 haplotypes of the G2 group were the main haplotypes of Northeast China populations and introduced populations from Northeast China (Xin-UC_I, Hebei-ChL_I, Lu-T’A-I, and Lu-YT_I) followed by N8 (G1) and N9 (G1) (Fig. 4D, E). Structure analysis (Fig. S9) validated the haplotype distribution patterns observed at K values ranging from 2 to 4. In North China, except for Shanxi, the primary haplotypes are N8 and N4 from the G1 branch, followed by N9 and N12 from G2. In other provinces or autonomous regions of North China, the main haplotypes are N1, N9, and N6 from G2 (Fig. 4D). The haplotypes N5 and N8 found in Henan P. tomentosa originate from G1.

In addition, by combining network map analysis (Fig. 5A), the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5B), population distribution, and population structure (Fig. 4) of P. tomentosa’s ITS haplotype, we can obtain phylogeographic results similar to chloroplast haplotypes. Specifically, there are two major phylogeographic groups of P. tomentosa: the EG, which is primarily composed of populations from Northeast and North China, and the WG, mainly made up of populations from Northwest China, along with five populations from Sichuan, Henan, and Shanxi (Fig. 4 and Fig. S1). Through SAMOVA, when K > 2 (FCT = 0.385), the increase in FCT values was not significant, and when K > 3 newly defined groups often consisted of single populations (Fig. S6B and Fig. S9C). WG included 15 populations (Chuan-Aba, Gan-PL, Gan-ZhC, Ning-HlM, Qing-HD, Shaan-HM, Shaan-KQ, Shaan-LT, Shaan-XfD, Shaan-Y’A, Shaan-YG, Shaan-YX, Shanxi-ChZh, Shanxi-HD and Yu-SMX), while EG comprised the other 27 populations (Fig. 4, Fig. S6B and Fig. S9). At K = 3 (FCT = 0.396), a newly defined group emerged from WG, including Shaan-KQ, Shaan-LT, Shaan-XfD, Shaan-Y’A, Shaan-YG, Shaan-YX, Gan-PL, and Yu-SMX (Fig. 4 and Fig. S6B).

Differentiation time and evolutionary history of P. tomentosa

The haplotype differentiation time of P. tomentosa was inferred using a prior time of 3.50 Ma (million years Befor Present; million years before AD 1950) [29], with reference calibration points from the fossilization times of P. serrulata, P. sibirica, P. triloba, and P. domestica. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using Beast v1.5.3 [30] (Fig. 6A), revealing that P. tomentosa species share a common ancestor that emerged during the mid-Miocene period (Fig. 6A) and may have originated from the Northeast of China (Fig. 6B). Following geographical isolation events, P. tomentosa branched into two central phylogeographic groups—EG and WG—with potential other distribution areas yet unknown (Fig. 6B). The differentiation time between these two groups is estimated to be roughly 12.31 Ma (PP = 0.79), with further differentiation occurring between approximately 0.13–7.17 Ma (Fig. 6A). Concurrently, it was identified that haplotypes H5, H19, and H33 divided into the two main phylogeographic groups, but the progenitors of these three haplotypes all stemmed from the EG (Fig. 6B). The emergence time of haplotypes H5 and H9 is estimated to be around 3.83 Ma (Fig. 6A).

Simultaneously, it can be inferred that P. sibirica and P. triloba share a closer relationship with P. tomentosa. The differentiation time of P. sibirica and P. triloba is approximately 6.75 Ma (PP = 1), and their respective crown ages are 3.53 Ma (PP = 1) and 3.36 Ma (PP = 0.89) (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, the inferred times of P. sibirica, P. triloba, P. serrulata, and P. domestica lie within the fossilization time range (2.59–5.30 Ma).

Population expansion and historical dynamic analysis

According to the “four-gamete” test [31], it is inferred that the minimum number of recombination events is three based on the cpDNA sequence and six based on the nuclear ITS sequence in the historical evolution process of P. tomentosa. The cpDNA sequence shows very little recombination, which also reflects the conservatism and uniparental inheritance characteristics of the chloroplast genome. Based on the statistical analysis of Fu and Li [32] and Tajima [33], D *, F *, and Tajima D values were all negative. Fu and Li’s D * and F * values (Table 1 and Table S3) showed varying degrees of significance at the cpDNA and ITS levels. The results were very consistent with the expected distribution under the sudden expansion model and were confirmed by a single peak or insignificant bimodal mismatch distribution (Fig. 7). It indicated that there is population expansion or positive selection in P. tomentosa, and the chloroplast level was mainly manifested at the rps16-trnQ sequence interval.

(based on cpDNA data) and τITS (based on ITS data) of the mismatch distribution model were 0.685 and 2.125, respectively [35]. The g values are calculated based on 20 years [38]. Therefore, based on the T-value of cpDNA, Tcp. = 0.685/2 × (2487 × 1.50 × 10− 9 × 20) = 5.74 × 103, TITS = 2.125/2 × (615 × 2.15 × 10− 9 × 20) = 40.18 × 103. It was found that the expansion time of the P. tomentosa population occurred at 5.74 Ka (kilion-anniversary Before Present; thousand years before AD 1950) based on chloroplast data, while based on nuclear ITS data, its expansion time was approximately 40.18 Ka.

Discussion

Genetic diversity and conservation implications of P. tomentosa

Genetic assessments and monitoring are critical to species conservation and management, and genetic diversity, a product of a species’ long-term evolution, is influenced by various factors and is crucial for species survival, environmental adaptation, and evolutionary potential [39, 40]. Species with higher genetic diversity exhibit improved adaptive capacity to environmental fluctuations, facilitating range expansion and successful colonization of novel habitats [41, 42]. Conversely, a lack of genetic diversity can result in a species’ inability to adjust to complex and fluctuating environments, which could eventually lead to its extinction [41].

Petit et al. [43] synthesized data related to the population genetic diversity of multiple species and found that the average genetic diversity of angiosperms was 0.67, according to chloroplast data analysis. The genetic diversity of P. tomentosa (Hd = 0.689) (Table 1) was akin to that of angiosperms on average. However, it was higher than that of the widely dispersed P. pseudocerasus (Hd = 0.557 or 0.565) in China [21, 44], but lower than P. serrulata (Hd = 0.829) [17]. Nonetheless, the haplotype number of P. tomentosa (h = 36) considerably surpassed that of P. serrulata (h = 19) [17]. This discrepancy might be linked to the distribution range, habitat, and sample collection of the species. Most notably, the studies on Chinese cherry [21, 44] used a limited number of wild populations (9 or 10) and had low coverage, thus they might not accurately reflect the genetic diversity level of the wild cherry population in China.

Additionally, we have discovered differences in the genetic diversity of P. tomentosa species, as indicated by cpDNA, nuclear ITS and nSSR [3]. The haplotype types, haplotype diversity, and nucleotide diversity based on ITS surpass those based on chloroplast diversity indicators (Table 1). This nuclear-cytoplasmic inconsistency may arise from the biological characteristics of the P. tomentosa species, including insect pollination, outcrossing, germination of underground seeds, seed reproduction, and transmission through animals and gravity [45, 46]. Typically, the parental inheritance of nuclear substances is facilitated by the dispersion of seeds and pollen, while the parental inheritance of chloroplast heritage substances is achieved through seed dispersion; this may account for the differences in nuclear-cytoplasmic diversity [11]. Additionally, factors such as sample size, intra-species mutation rate, and population statistical history may also influence the diversity level and pattern of a species [47].

Moreover, understanding the genetic diversity of species can help propose priority population conservation strategies with simple, high genetic coverage for the P. tomentosa species [48–50]. It can also streamline resource allocation in species conservation, thereby reducing wastage and inefficiencies. This facilitates the efficient use of germplasm resources in breeding work [48, 49]. Based on chloroplast haplotype analysis, it has been found that Gan-XlM (10) has the most haplotype types. This is followed by Shaan-HM, Jing-YtM, and Shanxi-ChZh populations, all with a haplotype count of 6. Meanwhile, based on the SSR data, priority should be given to protecting populations with relatively higher genetic diversity, such as Gan-XlM from Gansu and Shaan-HM from Shaanxi [3]. These populations should be conserved and utilized as valuable breeding parent materials.

Synthesizing cpDNA and ITS data to elucidate the phylogeography of P. tomentosa

Genetic variation is crucial in deciphering the evolutionary history of a species, with the level of such variation typically reflecting the potential for evolution, thereby aiding the evaluation of other evolutionary hypotheses [51]. A species entirely devoid of genetic variation may find it difficult to adapt to perpetually evolving environmental conditions. The amount and distribution of genetic variation within and across populations of an endemic species can inform recommendations for the preservation of wild germplasm resources, such as determining the number of populations to be protected and identifying those containing distinct genotypes [51, 52]. Therefore, the presence of endemism must be considered when safeguarding species. This study uncovered nine unique chloroplast haplotypes distributed in the Shanxi Province (H21 and H22), Beijing (H10 and H12), and Shandong province (H5, H6, and H7). Specifically, two Shandong populations (Lu-YT_I and Lu-T’A_I) originated from Northeast China (Fig. 2 and Table S1). However, these three haplotypes were not found in all extant wild populations in Northeast China. Based on haplotype topology (Figs. 2A and 3A), we infer that H5 and H6 may be rare haplotypes of the P. tomentosa population from the Northeast, while H7 haplotypes may have experienced genetic variation after being introduced to Shandong. In this study, we detected 36 chloroplast haplotypes and 57 ITS haplotypes, which will facilitate and expedite the P. tomentosa breeding. Haplotype-based breeding, as a one cutting-edge technologies in the realm of crop improvement, can decrease the data complexity, circumvent the challenges associated with linkage drag and hasten the breeding cycle [53].

AMOVA analysis has further demonstrated a significant genetic differentiation in P. tomentosa populations during the evolutionary process, with an Fst of 0.608 (cpDNA; P < 0.001) or 0.345 (ITS; P < 0.001). Genetic differentiation is tied to gene flow, natural selection, and mutation. Gene flow promotes gene exchange within or between populations, consequently reducing population differentiation [54]. The gene flow of P. tomentosa is lower at the chloroplast level (Nm = 0.161) and higher at the nuclear level (Nm = 0.475). Plant gene flow primarily consists of pollen flow and seed flow [55], with the propagation of seeds and pollen playing an important role in gene flow. Generally, plants with cross-pollination have higher gene flow, achieved through the spread of pollen by insects, such as bees, or the dispersal of seeds with the help of animals [56]. Compared to most cultivated sweet cherry varieties, P. tomentosa can self-pollinate and cross-pollinate and possesses relatively strong self-compatibility. This trait could potentially impact its gene exchange. Moreover, geographical barriers, like mountains, oceans, and rivers, can separate potentially isolated regions to create micro-isolation effects, where continuous gene flow via seed or pollen dispersal [54, 57]. Seed movement is often restricted by gravity and water flow directions and while pollen flow by insects is likewise reduced across these physical divides [3, 54]. The distribution of P. tomentosa is primarily in slopes, gullies, and similar areas (Table S1), which are also the very architecture of its dispersal barriers and presents a higher challenge to gene flow [54]. This explains the low gene flow estimates will allow genetic drift and lead to the significant population differentiation [3, 54, 57]. Natural selection occurs in unique and specific environments, putting significant pressure on species evolution. This pressure has resulted in a low gene flow in the P. tomentosa population, leading to noticeable genetic differentiation.

After analyzing the network map (Figs. 2A and 5A), phylogenetic tree (Figs. 3B and 5B), population distribution, and population structure (Figs. 2 and 4) of chloroplast and ITS haplotype, the P. tomentosa populations exhibit a phylogeographic structure (Nst > Gst) and are divided into western and eastern phylogeographic groups (Table 1; Fig. 3, and Fig. 5B). At K = 2, the results of STRUCTURE and SAMOVA analyses were nearly identical (Fig. 2; Fig. S7). However, at K = 3, some discrepancies emerged between the two methods based on ITS data. SAMOVA identified an independent geographical group comprising six populations from Shaanxi (Shaan-KQ, Shaan-LT, Shaan-XfD, Shaan-Y’A, Shaan-YG, Shaan-YX) along with Gan-PL and Yu-SMX (Fig. 4; Fig. S9B). Phlogeographic structure can be better to clasify geographical barriers to dispersal and describing the effects on the geographical distribution of P. tomentosa [54]. Inaddition, we found Xinjiang populations, Chuan-Aba population from Sichuan procince and Qing-HD population from Qinghai procince exhibited limited haplotype (Fig. 2A, Fig. S4 and Fig. S5 B) and lower SSR diversity [3]. Structure analysis (Fig. 2B and Fig. S9) revealed clear differentiation between these populations and the others, particularly in the Chuan-Aba, Xin-ALR and Qing-HD populations [3]. Notably, when K = 3, the population STRUCTURE analysis of P. tomentosa based on ITS data distinctly separated the two populations (Shanxi-YCh and Shanxi-ChZh) from Shanxi from the remaining groups (Fig. S9). And we found two unique chloroplast haplotypes (H21 and H22) (Fig. 2A and Fig. S4). This finding reminds us that future exploration and utilization of P. tomentosa germplasm resources should also focus on the populations from Shanxi.

The roles of historical vicariance and population dynamics in shaping the phylogeography of P. tomentosa

Based on the molecular clock, the common ancestor of P. tomentosa appeared around mid-Miocene, with a crown age estimated at approximately 7.71 Ma in WG and 5.17 Ma in EG (Fig. 6A). The common ancestor time for other species within the subgenus Cerasus also occurred in the Miocene, such as P. serrulata at 8.84 Ma [17], and P. dielsiana at 6.28 Ma [9]. Additionally, Polystichum glaciale (6.89 Ma), alongside other species, has been predicted to have arisen during the Miocene period [58]. The differentiation of Ixiolirion tatrium and I. songaricum took place around 7 Ma [59]. During mid-Miocene, significant historical events, such as the Himalayan movement, development of the Arctic ice sheet, and global climate cooling down, took place [17, 60]. These intense topographic and climatic changes might have been the catalysts for species differentiation. Moreover, the West Qinling region, which is adjacent to the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, has a history closely tied to the continuous uplift of the latter. This suggests that the Qinling Mountains may have been influenced by the Himalayan movement, which had a significant impact on the climate. This could also be a factor in the differentiation of cherry and other species [61, 62].

Numerous studies have posited that the region stretching from the eastern Tibetan Plateau to the Pacific Ocean and from the Qinling Mts–Huai River Line (at ~ 34°N) to the tropical South (< 22°N) is not only one of the most critical forest ecosystems and biodiversity hotspots but also a crucial historical glacial refugium [9, 63]. Combining the chloroplast haplotypes, we found that locations in Qinling, such as the Xiaolong Mountains (Gansu) and Hua Mountains (Shaanxi), could be glacial refuges for P. tomentosa, boasting high genetic diversity. The same could be true for the Qian Mountains [19]. The MaxEnt model identified stable, highly suitable areas in northern China, such as Gansu and Shaanxi, under both current and future climate scenarios [23], which spatially corresponds to the mountain regions we proposed as potential refugia. Furthermore, the model projected a northward expansion of suitable habitats under future warming [23].

Additionally, this study revealed that during the evolution of P. tomentosa, varying degrees of deviation from the neutral evolution model were observed, based on both nuclear ITS and chloroplasts (Table 1 and Table S3). P. tomentosa might have experienced population expansion (Fig. 7), potentially due to the influence of natural selection [11, 47]. Due to the slow evolutionary rate and maternal inheritance of cpDNA, it can effectively trace seed-mediated gene flow and historical evolutionary events, while the moderately evolving ITS provides complementary insights. Therefore, through the integration of these markers with rapidly evolving nSSR [3], this study effectively elucidated the historical lineage differentiation and the formation process of the geographical distribution pattern of P. tomentosa populations. This approach not only validated the accuracy of the nSSR results [3], but also further confirmed the correlation between genetic differentiation and geographical distribution, ultimately revealing the existence of two major eastern and western lineages. Building upon the nSSR analysis, this study further estimated lineage divergence times, inferred the species’ origin and historical expansion events, and linked its current distribution patterns to historical geological events such as the uplift of the Himalayas. Consequently, a more comprehensive strategy for the conservation and utilization of wild P. tomentosa resources is proposed. Moreover, in phylogeographic studies on subgenus Cerasus, it was found that P. dielsiana also underwent population expansion events [9]. However, for P. serrulata, there were no population expansion events detected, either by neutral testing or mismatch distribution across different regions at the species level [17].

Conclusion

Phylogenetic and biogeographic studies on P. tomentosa have indicated a moderate level of haplotype diversity and a clear phylogeographical structure. These studies identified a total of 36 chloroplast haplotypes, most prevalent in the provinces of Shaanxi and Gansu. The most common haplotypes were H1, H2, H4, and H8. As for ITS haplotypes, 57 were detected, with the highest number in Liaoning and Jilin provinces. Haplotypes N8, N32, N1, and N9 were the most commonly observed ITS haplotypes. It is hypothesized that the common ancestor of P. tomentosa emerged during the mid-Miocene and differentiated into two major phylogeography groups—EG and WG —throughout its evolutionary process. This evolutionary lineage is believed to have been influenced by several geological and climatic changes during that period, with the differentiation of the two groups occurring approximately 12.31 Ma ago. Based on the genetic diversity and phylogeographical structure of chloroplast haplotypes in P. tomentosa, we strongly recommend protecting the populations from Xiaolong Mountain in Gansu, Huashan in Shaanxi, Yangtai Mountain in Beijing, and Qian Mountain in Liaoning.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Based on the Flora Republicae Popularis Sinicae (http://www.iplant.cn), the Plant Photo Bank of China (http://ppbc.iplant.cn/), related literature [4, 64], and field investigation, we collected a total of 779 individuals from 42 populations in Shaanxi, Gansu, Liaoning, Jilin, Shanxi, Ningxia, Qinghai, Sichuan, and other provinces across six geographical regions (Northwest, North, Northeast, Southwest, Southeast, and Central South) in China (Fig. 2, Fig. S1 Table S1). The four samples were used for chloroplast genome sequencing to screen primers (Table S2). Our survey indicated that the introduced cherry resources were primarily sourced from the Northeast region.

The sampling strategy included the following steps: (1) A minimum of 15 individuals were collected from each population; moreover, all ecotypes were collected for populations consisting of fewer than 15 trees. (2) The sampled trees were distanced by at least 100 m. (3) Between 10 and 15 fresh, healthy leaves were collected from each individual and promptly placed in a sealable bag containing an appropriate amount of dry silicone. (4) The sampling location, altitude, longitude, and all applicable parameters were recorded thoroughly.

Primer screening, PCR amplification, and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from the leaves using an enhanced cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) extraction method [65]. The sequencing, assembly, and annotation of the chloroplast whole-genome DNA of P. tomentosa were performed following the method described by Wan et al. [64].

The chloroplast genome sequences of P. tomentosa were compared using the MAFF function in Geneious Prime v2022.0.2 software [66]. Regions with high mutation points were screened and sequences measuring around 1000 bp were selected. The primer function in Geneious Prime v2022.0.2 software was used for primer design, and primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). From four populations with substantial differences (Shaan-TB_YX, Ji-JL, Chuan-Aba, and Xin-ALR), three DNA samples were randomly chosen for primer PCR amplification, sequencing, and sequence alignment. Ultimately, three cpDNA primers (Table 3) are selected that show single, clear, and highly polymorphic fragments. Furthermore, in line with existing studies, a universal primer ITS1-ITS4 is selected for the ribosomal ITS primer (Table 3) [67].

PCR amplifications were executed in a 30 µL reaction mixture consisting of 1.50 µL DNA template, 0.75 µL forward/reverse primer, 15.00 µL 2 × Rapid Taq Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, China), and 12.00 µL sterile ultra-pure water. The PCR program utilized involved an initial step at 94 ℃ for 4 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 ℃ for 30 s, annealing at 55.0 ℃–57.5 ℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72 ℃ for 30 s. This was followed by a final extension at 72 ℃ for 10 min. The PCR products obtained were analyzed using 1% (w/v) agarose gel electrophoresis. Clear, solitary products were screened in both directions by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Sequencing results were manually proofread through Geneious Prime v2022.0.2, referencing the sequencing peak map and comparison documents. Sequences with low sequencing quality at both ends were removed. Failures having overlap peaks in sequencing results were amplified at least three times to ensure multiple copies of ITS region amplified in the productions. Sequencing peaks of nucleotide sites having an overlap greater than 80% in the nuclear sequences were treated as heterozygous [68]. Finally, the proofread cpDNA fragments were assembled and one ITS sequences containing heterozygous sites were considered as a set of haplotypes for analysis.

Nucleotide diversity and genetic variation analysis

The population genetic diversity-related index was calculated for each locus using DnaSP v.6 (Rozas et al., 2017). This included the haplotype number (h), haploid diversity (Hd), nucleotide diversity index (π), and the Watterson’s value θw. Furthermore, the minimum number of separation sites (s) and recombination events (Rm) were also determined. The total genetic diversity (Ht) and average genetic diversity (Hs) within the population were analyzed using the PERMUT v1.2.1.0 software [28]. AMOVA analysis was conducted to evaluate genetic variation using the Arlequin software [69], such as the genetic differentiation coefficient Fst, based on an analysis of variance. The significance testing was conducted based on 1000 permutations, and genetic variation was categorized into three levels: inter-species (group), intra-species (group), and intra-population.

Phylogeographic structure

The software PERMUT v1.2.1.0 [28] was used to analyze the genetic differentiation coefficients Gst and Nst. While Gst infers genetic differentiation of gene flow only considering the frequency of alleles, Nst considers both allele frequency and haplotype similarity. If Nst values significantly exceed Gst values, it implies the existence of a phylogeographic structure with strong differentiation [43]. For this study, we set the mutations to 1000, treating multiple consecutive insertion or deletion sites as a single variant site.

Geographical distribution maps of the chloroplast haplotypes and ITS haplotypes of Prunus tomentosa were developed using ArcMap 10.2 software. The data for multi-year provincial administrative boundaries in China was sourced from the Resource and Environmental Science Data Platform (https://www.resdc.cn/, DOI identification: 10.12078/2023010103). Using PopART 1.7 software [70], we constructed chloroplast haplotype and ribosomal haplotype network diagrams. In these constructs, gaps were treated as single events, using Median Joining as the method of construction. Potential population structure was examined using STRUCTURE v.2.3.4 and SAMOVA 2.0 (https://cmpg.unibe.ch/software/samova2/) [3]. We performed SAMOVA with 100 simulated annealing steps and 100 independent runs for each K value (ranging from 2 to 10). The optimum number of population groups was identified for which the highest genetic differentiation between groups (FCT) was obtained.

To further assess the correlation between GD and GGD among cherry populations, we utilized MEGA v11 [71]. This software was used to calculate the average GD between populations, employing the kimura2 parameter model algorithm. This process generated a GD triangular matrix. We converted the sampling position longitude and latitude of the populations into a GGD matrix using GenAlEx v6.5 software [72]. A logarithmic transformation was applied during this conversion, generating a Log (1 + GGD) GGD triangle matrix. Lastly, using GenAlEx, we conducted Mantel testing, repeating the process 9999 times to test for a correlation between the average GD and GGD of P. tomentosa populations.

Differentiation time and ancestor distribution

Haplotype evolution trees, based on cpDNA and ITS, were constructed using Bayesian Inference of the Generous Prime v2022.0.2 [73]. The Mrbayes analysis followed these Markov chain Monte Carlo simulation settings: chain length = 110,000, sampling frequency = 200, heated chains = 4, burn-in length = 100,000, heated chain temp = 0.2, and random seed = 170. The phylogenetic tree was then visualized and refined using Interactive Tree Of Life (iTOL) v5 software [74].

The divergence time inference of haplotypes was estimated using Beast v1.5.3 software [30]. Parameter settings and file generation were first performed in the BEAuti program. “HKY model” was set for the Substitution model, “estimated” for the Base Frequency, and “variant sites” for the Site Heterogeneity model. The differentiation time estimation is based on the “uncorrelated lognormal relax clock model.” The Starting Tree was set as “randomly generated” and the Tree Prior to “Coalescent-Constant size.” MCMC settings were as follows: chain length = 200,000,000, sampling every 2000 times [75]. Due to the lack of petrification time for P. tomentosa, the Priors calibration point used four closely related Prunus species: P. serrulata, P. sibirica, P. triloba, and P. initiatia (P. domestica). P. initiatia, Damson plum, has been renamed P. domestica subs P. initiatia, as a subspecies of P. domestica (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datahub/taxonomy/114939/). Petrification periods for the four plum species occurred in the Pliocene, approximately 2.59 to 5.30 Ma [29]. Therefore, the prior time point was set to a median of 3.944 and the standard deviation to 2.

Chloroplast genome sequences of the four species were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The corresponding cpDNA sequences were extracted and compared with the haplotype sequences obtained in this study. The alignment files obtained were used to infer haplotype divergence time. All parameters were checked with Tracer v1.5 software to ensure that the Effective Sample Size (ESS) was greater than 200, indicating the result has converged. After a successful ESS test, the TreeAnnotator v1.5.3 program conducted the calculation of the consensus tree. Concerning the burn-in setting, the first 20% of the initial tree data was discarded. The final tree was visualized using FigTree v1.3.1 software and enhanced with iTOL v5 software [74]. Additionally, the ancestral area of P. tomentosa was inferred using RASP v4.0 software [76]. This data were based on the phylogenetic tree topology obtained from Beast, with the tree model created using the Bayesian Binary MCMC method [77].

Historical dynamic analysis

The statistical analysis of Tajima D [33] and Fu and Li’s D* and F* [32] was performed using Arlequin v3.5.2.2 [69, 78, 79]. It was used to determine whether P. tomentosa adhered to the neutral evolution model during the evolutionary process. The mismatch distribution of the P. tomentosa population was analyzed with DnaSP v.6 [35]. A mismatch distribution showing multiple peaks or uneven distribution would suggest that the P. tomentosa population is in a dynamic equilibrium state. The population expansion of P. tomentosa was calculated using the formula T = τ/2u and u = µ × k × g [34], where “τ” is the parameter value of the mismatch distribution model, and “µ” denotes the base substitution rate. The median value of angiosperms for cpDNA, 1.50 × 10− 9 s/s/y (substitution/site/year), was implemented for this study [36]. The average rate of ITS usage in perennial woody plants was 2.15 × 10− 9 s/s/y [37]. “k” is the fragment length (combined cpDNA length: 2487 bp). “g” is the generation time, set as 20 years, as used in the genomics study of P. armeniaca [38].
