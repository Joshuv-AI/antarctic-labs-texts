Viral hepatitis as a differential diagnosis of yellow fever suspected cases in Cameroon: Prevalence and molecular characterization
Authors: Abdou Fatawou Modiyinji, Huguette Tchetgna Simo, Aristide Mounchili-Njifon, Moise Henri Moumbeket-Yifomnjou, Lionel Franklin Djomo, Damaris Ngo Yabi, Justine Gwendolyne Odi, Gisèle Liliane Machuetum, Abanda Njei Ngu, Richard Njouom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This study presents viral hepatitis as a differential diagnosis for yellow fever.•Hepatitis A virus (HAV) is the most detected of the four hepatotropic viruses identified.•First report on the circulation of HAV-I and II genotypes in Cameroon.•HAV is more prevalent in young people, whereas hepatitis B virus is identified in adults.

Introduction

Yellow fever (YF) is a viral hemorrhagic fever caused by the YF virus (YFV) and transmitted by Aedes spp. and Haemagogus spp. mosquitoes [1]. Despite the availability of a safe and effective vaccine, YF remains a significant global health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. To control and prevent YF epidemics, the World Health Organization (WHO) has implemented routine surveillance system in endemic countries, primarily focused on the monitoring of acute febrile jaundice. However, most suspected YF cases—defined as an acute fever followed by the development of jaundice within 14 days—remain of unknown origin, suggesting the involvement of other hepatotropic pathogens. Of the approximately 2000 patients with acute febrile jaundice identified and tested each year through the YF surveillance program, over 95% test negative for antibodies and/or genetic material of YFV [2].

Viral hepatitis is a major global public health problem, affecting millions of people annually and causing significant morbidity and mortality [3]. Hepatitis A virus (HAV) and hepatitis E virus (HEV) share notable similarities [4]. Both viruses are transmitted primarily through the fecal–oral route and by ingestion of contaminated food. HEV is a zoonotic infection, whereas humans are the primary host for HAV [4]. HAV does not cause chronic infection, but persistent HEV infections have been documented over the past decade. Both viruses have genomes of approximately 7.2–7.5 kb and are positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses. HAV belongs to the genus Hepatovirus within the family Picornaviridae [5]. There are six genotypes of HAV, three (I, II, and III) that infect humans and three (IV, V, and VI) that infect simians [5]. According to the WHO, there are an estimated 1.4 million cases of hepatitis A worldwide each year, resulting in approximately 7000 deaths [4]. HEV belongs to the family Hepeviridae. Eight distinct HEV genotypes (HEV-1 to HEV-8) have been identified in the Orthohepevirus A species [5]. Each year, there are an estimated 20 million HEV infections, leading to 3.3 million symptomatic cases and around 44,000 deaths.

Hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), and hepatitis D virus (HDV) are major causes of chronic hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, resulting in high mortality and morbidity worldwide [6]. These three distinct viruses are responsible for viral hepatitis transmitted parenterally, sexually, or from mother to child [7]. HDV requires a preexisting or concurrent infection with HBV. HDV also increases the burden of HBV-related chronic liver diseases [8]. An estimated 350 million people are chronically infected with HBV and 150 million with HCV worldwide. The HBV genome consists of partially double-stranded circular DNA approximately 3.2 kb long. HBV strains are classified into nine genotypes (A to I), with some further divided into subgenotypes. HCV belongs to the Hepacivirus genus of the Flaviviridae family and is classified into six major genotypes. It is an enveloped, single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus. HDV is classified into eight genotypes (1 to 8) and several subgenotypes [9]. Genotypes 5 and 8 are the most frequent and are found in Africa [10]. About 15–20 million people chronically infected with HBV are also carriers of HDV [11].

As in other endemic countries, only 5% of suspected YF cases are actually diagnosed as YF in Cameroon each year, leaving 95% of these patients and samples without any diagnosis [2]. In neighbouring countries such as the Central African Republic (CAR) [12], the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) [13,14] and Chad [15], viral hepatitis has been shown to play an important etiologic role in YF-negative suspected cases, although with variable prevalences. To date, only HEV has been investigated in these icteric patients in Cameroon [16], which led to the identification of an HEV outbreak in 2013 in the northern region of the country and highlighted the public health importance of investigating icteric samples beyond YF. Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the prevalence and genetic diversity of five hepatotropic viruses in YF-negative suspected cases collected through the national YF surveillance system in Cameroon.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this prospective cross-sectional study, plasma and serum samples collected from suspected YF cases that tested negative for YF were simultaneously tested for viral hepatitis. A suspected YF case was defined as a patient with an acute onset of fever followed by jaundice within two weeks of the first symptoms. According to the National Yellow Fever Surveillance Program in Cameroon, samples from suspected YF patients are submitted to Centre Pasteur du Cameroun for laboratory confirmation of YF infection. Once YF testing was completed and a patient’s sample tested negative, the sample was then submitted for viral hepatitis testing. Samples with adequate residual volume and collected between September 2024 and January 2025 were analyzed in this study.

Nucleic acid extraction and viral detection by the one-step pentaplex real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction assay

Total nucleic acids were extracted from 200 µl of plasma or serum samples using a QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Courtaboeuf, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Detection of HAV, HBV, HCV, HDV, and HEV was performed with a previously published multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assay using the One Step PrimeScript™ reverse transcription-PCR (RT-PCR) kit (Takara, Dalian, China) [17]. In brief, real-time PCR reactions were performed in a 25 µl reaction mixture, including 12.5 µl of 2X One Step RT-PCR Buffer III, 0.5 µl of Taq HS (5 U/µl), 0.5 µl of PrimeScript RT Enzyme, 6.5 µl of a mixture of all primers and probes (200 nM of primers and probes for HBV, 100 nM of primers and probes for HCV, HAV, and HDV, and 400 nM of primers and probes for HEV), and 5 µl of nucleic acid template. PCR amplification was performed in the QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, USA). The cycling conditions were as follows: 42 °C for 5 minutes, a 10-second denaturation step at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 seconds and 58 °C for 40 seconds. The fluorescence signal intensities of FAM, VIC, CY5, ROX, and TAMRA were recorded after each annealing step. Results were considered positive if the threshold cycle (Ct) value was less than or equal to 38 (Ct ≤ 38). Two positive controls and one negative control for each virus tested were included in every run. The experiment was validated if at least one positive control for each virus showed a sigmoidal curve with Ct < 38, and the negative control showed no amplification.

Genotyping, sequencing, and phylogenetic analyses

All RT-PCR-positive samples were subjected to nested RT-PCR for HAV, HCV, and HEV, and nested PCR for HBV. The VP1/P2A region of the HAV genome was amplified using primers previously described [18]. RT-PCR was performed using the SuperScript™ III One-Step RT-PCR System with Platinum Taq (Invitrogen by Life Technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA). Briefly, 10 µl of total RNA was added to a reaction mixture containing 0.2 µM of each primer (HAV6 and HAV7) (Table 1), 12.3 mM MgSO4, and 200 µM deoxynucleoside triphosphates (dNTPs). Amplification started with complementary (cDNA) synthesis for 30 minutes at 50 °C, followed by initial denaturation for 2 minutes at 94 °C. This was followed by 35 PCR cycles of 15 seconds at 94 °C, 30 seconds at 60 °C, and 1 minute at 72 °C, with a final extension for 5 minutes at 72 °C. For the second step, 5 µl of RT-PCR products were used in a final volume of 50 µl containing 1.25 U of Taq polymerase (Life Technologies Corporation, USA), 1.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.2 µM of each primer (HAV8 and HAV9) (Table 1). The thermal profile consisted of 94 °C for 5 minutes, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 seconds, 55 °C for 30 seconds, and 72 °C for 1 minute 30 seconds, with a final extension of 72 °C for 10 minutes. Pre-S region of the HBV genome was amplified by nested PCR using two sets of primers as previously described, with modifications [19]. Briefly, the first PCR was performed in a final volume of 50 µl containing 1 U of Taq polymerase (Life Technologies Corporation, USA), 5 µl of 10× buffer, 2 µl of MgCl2, 2 µl of dNTPs, 2 µl of each primer (HBPr1 and HBr135) (Table 1), 10 µl of DNA, and 26 µl of RNase-free water. For the second step, 4 µl of PCR products were used in a final volume of 50 µl containing 1 U of Taq polymerase (Life Technologies Corporation, USA), 5 µl of 10× buffer, 2 µl of MgCl2, 2 µl of dNTPs, 2 µl of each primer (HBPr2 and HBr3) (Table 1), and 33 µl of RNase-free water. Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the Maximum Likelihood method and the Kimura 2-parameter model in MEGA software (Version 7).

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics including age, sex, and location, were extracted from the YF surveillance database. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS software, version 16.0. A P-value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics and prevalence of HAV, HBV, HCV, HDV and HEV

In this study, 404 samples from suspected YF cases that tested negative for YF infection were included. The majority of patients were male (64.9%; 262 of 404), with a median age of 16.7 years (range: 0–80). The samples were collected from all 10 administrative regions of Cameroon, with most originating from the Far-North region (Table 2). Among the 404 participants, viral hepatitis was diagnosed in 107 (26.5%; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 22.4–31.0%). The distribution of each virus was as follows: HAV, 70.1% (75 of 107); HBV, 23.4% (25 of 107); HCV, 3.7% (4 of 107); and HEV, 2.8% (3 of 107). No sample tested positive for HDV, and no dual viral infections were observed. Genetic diversity of viral hepatitis identified

From a total of 107 quantitative PCR (qPCR)-positive samples, 101 (73 HAV, 24 HBV, 2 HCV, and 2 HEV) were successfully amplified by nested PCR, and 97 (69 HAV, 24 HBV, 2 HCV, and 2 HEV) were successfully sequenced. As shown in Figure 1, HAV genotypes I (95.7%, 66 of 69) and II (4.3%, 3 of 69) were detected. Phylogenetic analysis of the 69 isolates indicated minimal diversity, with one subgenotype identified per genotype (subgenotype IB and subgenotype IIA) (Figure 1). These sequences clustered with reference isolates previously identified in France, Gabon, Ghana, and Cameroon. Phylogenetic analysis of the 24 HBV sequences showed that 23 belonged to HBV-E and one to HBV-A (Figure 2). These findings confirm the high prevalence of HBV-E in Cameroon. Analysis of the Core region of the two HCV isolates revealed that they belonged to HCV-4 and HCV-1 genotypes, the two predominant genotypes in Cameroon (Figure 3a). Discussion

Acute febrile jaundice is an indication that a patient could have a serious illness. For about 95% of patients identified through the YF surveillance program, the underlying cause of acute febrile jaundice remains unknown. Our analysis shows that viral hepatitis is the underlying cause of acute febrile jaundice in 26% of these YFV-negative patients in Cameroon. This is the first comprehensive study investigating the presence of all five hepatotropic viruses in YFV-negative surveillance samples in Cameroon. Only HEV has previously been investigated in these samples. A previous study from the DRC showed that 43.7% (218 of 498) of YFV-negative YF suspect cases were infected with at least one of the five investigated hepatitis viruses. The reported seroprevalences were 16.7% for HAV, 24.6% for HBV, 2.3% for HCV, and 10.4% for HEV, and 26.1% of HBV-positive patients were also infected with HDV [14]. More recently in the same country, among 1239 YFV-negative suspected YF cases, seroprevalences were 16.1%, 11.2%, 5.0%, and 3.1% for HAV, HBV, HCV and HEV, respectively [13]. Previous hepatitis studies based on the YF surveillance program have reported similar observations in two countries bordering Cameroon. In 2017, a study by Gardia and colleagues reported a seroprevalence of 19.8% for hepatitis B surface antigen (Ag), 5.6% for HCV Ag/immunoglobulin (Ig)G/IgM, 34.0% for HDV Ag, and a prevalence of 13.6% for HEV RNA among 198 YFV-negative sera randomly selected from 2177 samples collected during YF surveillance in the CAR [12]. In 2019, a study by Yandai and colleagues reported a seroprevalence of 10.6% (27 of 255) for hepatitis B surface Ag, 2% (5 of 255) for HCV total antibodies, and 34.1% (87 of 255) for IgG and/or IgM HEV among suspected YFV-negative YF cases in Chad [15]. The difference between the techniques used—enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for these previous studies and qPCR for our study—to determine prevalences makes it difficult to compare the results obtained. Nevertheless, all these studies show that viral hepatitis is responsible for a large proportion of jaundice, which is generally attributed to YFV. We believe that the use of molecular technique alone is a limitation of our study. The fact that it is a multiplex technique strongly influenced our choice of molecular testing alone to detect acute febrile illness unrelated to YF. However, the combined use of molecular biology and serology could have produced a higher prevalence rate. Indeed, in our recent study, we found an HEV seroprevalence of 22.3% using serology [22], whereas in the present study we detected only two cases of HEV infection using the molecular assay alone. We recommend that future surveillance and research studies employ a combined molecular and serological testing strategy.

African countries, including Cameroon, have some of the highest reported prevalence rates of viral hepatitis. Previous studies by our group and others have reported high prevalence of HBV, HCV, HDV, and HEV in Cameroon, with limited information on the prevalence of HAV [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. In this study, of the five viruses, four were detected by qPCR. Of these, HAV was the most prevalent. This result indicates that among hepatotropic viruses, HAV is the main pathogen associated with acute febrile jaundice in YFV-negative cases in children under 15 years of age. HAV infection is associated with poor hygiene, which could explain the high prevalence in Cameroon's three northern regions (Far-North, North, and Adamawa), which are ranked among the poorest in the country [27].

Although the HAV vaccine is available, the WHO does not recommend routine vaccination against hepatitis A in highly endemic areas. To date, Cameroon, like many other African countries, has not included systematic vaccination against hepatitis A in its Expanded Program on Immunization. However, the prevalence of this neglected infection found in this study is alarming. Providing clean water, ensuring proper wastewater disposal, and improving personal hygiene through health education, along with awareness-raising for HAV vaccination, are approaches that can help control HAV infection in the future.

In contrast to the only available study reporting the circulation of HAV genotype IA in diarrheic children in the South-West region of Cameroon [28], our study shows the presence of two other genotypes (HAV-IIA and HAV-IB), with HAV-IB predominating. Despite the large number of studies on the prevalence of genotypes worldwide, the first evidence of HAV-IIA circulation in Central Africa was only recently provided in 2020 [29]. Phylogenetic analyses revealed the circulation of HAV‐IIA strains belonging to a region‐specific lineage.

Early diagnosis of infected individuals remains challenging: nine out of every 10 persons with viral hepatitis are unaware of their status and remain undiagnosed, putting them at risk of unknowingly transmitting the virus [30]. To boost awareness and testing, existing surveillance programs, such as that of the YF program, could be utilized. Our results show that the cause of more than 70% of jaundice cases observed in patients included in our study remains unknown. It would be important in further studies to include other non-YF, non-hepatotropic viral pathogens (such as other arboviruses, leptospiroses, etc.) that are also known to cause jaundice.

One limitation of our study is that we did not examine the impact of seasonal variations on these infections, particularly those that are enterically transmitted (HAV and HEV), due to the short study period. A longer study period could provide a better understanding of the complete epidemiology of these viruses.

Conclusion

Our study shows that viral hepatitis is responsible for a significant number of febrile jaundice cases, which are often attributed to the YFV in Cameroon. These data reveal that the national YF surveillance system can serve as a valuable resource for understanding the epidemiology of viral hepatitis in our country. The prevalence of HAV in the studied population was alarmingly high, and a comprehensive study should be conducted to determine the true burden of this infection.
