Unmasking a rare contrast reaction: acute non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema after iohexol injection
Authors: Cecil Maria Jose, Naveen Gamini, Sabarish B Nair, Bharath Prasad, Gireesh Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryA woman with multiple comorbidities developed acute respiratory distress within minutes of intravenous iohexol administration for abdominal imaging. She presented with severe hypoxaemia, bilateral pulmonary crackles and diffuse opacities on chest imaging without evidence of cardiac dysfunction. Bedside echocardiography and brain natriuretic peptide levels excluded cardiogenic pulmonary oedema. A diagnosis of contrast-induced non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema was made. She required non-invasive ventilation and supportive therapy with complete resolution over 1 week. This case highlights a rare, life-threatening but reversible reaction to iodinated contrast and underscores the importance of early recognition, prompt respiratory support and readiness with resuscitation equipment during contrast administration.

Background

Iodinated contrast agents are widely used in diagnostic imaging. Modern non-ionic, low-osmolar monomers such as iohexol are generally considered safe yet adverse reactions can occur.1 Most reactions are mild, but severe complications—including nephropathy, hypersensitivity and cardiopulmonary events—are reported.1 2 Non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema (NCPE) is an extremely rare and poorly understood immediate reaction.3 Its pathophysiology is thought to involve endothelial injury and capillary leak rather than IgE-mediated hypersensitivity or cardiac dysfunction.1 4 Recognition is crucial because its presentation may mimic cardiogenic oedema or anaphylaxis, but treatment strategies differ. We describe a patient who developed severe NCPE within minutes of iohexol injection, with successful recovery following early supportive care.

Case presentation

A woman in her 60s with a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus, systemic hypertension, chronic coronary artery disease (status post stenting) and hypothyroidism presented for evaluation of vague abdominal pain. Renal function was normal (serum creatinine 0.9 mg/dL). She had no history of asthma, drug or contrast allergy or heart failure symptoms. She underwent a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the abdomen with 100 mL intravenous iohexol (Omnipaque, 350 mg I/mL) injected over 1 min. Within 5 min, she developed acute dyspnoea, chest tightness, restlessness, cyanosis and hypoxemia (SpO2 59% on room air). On examination, she was tachypneic (30 breaths/min), tachycardic (105 beats/min), hypertensive (140/80 mmHg) and in severe distress. Bilateral coarse crackles were heard without wheezes, stridor or cutaneous features of allergy. She was transferred to the emergency department on high-flow oxygen. Point-of-care echocardiography revealed diffuse B-lines with preserved left ventricular systolic function and no pericardial effusion. Chest radiograph showed extensive bilateral pulmonary infiltrates without cardiomegaly. ECG and cardiac biomarkers were normal. Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) was not elevated. Differential diagnosis

Contrast-induced anaphylaxis: ruled out due to absence of urticaria, angio-oedema, bronchospasm or hypotension.

Contrast-induced cardiogenic pulmonary oedema: excluded by normal cardiac silhouette, echocardiographic findings and normal BNP.5

Contrast-induced NCPE: favoured due to timing, clinical features and imaging findings.

Treatment

The patient was managed with non-invasive ventilation (BiPAP) and supplemental oxygen. She received intravenous furosemide initially, followed by infusion. Haemodynamic status remained stable and corticosteroids or antihistamines were not required.

Outcome and follow-up

Respiratory status improved steadily over 24 hours with reduction in pulmonary infiltrates on repeat imaging. She was weaned from non-invasive ventilation by day 3 and maintained oxygen saturation of 95% on room air by day 6. Renal and cardiac functions remained stable. She was discharged on day 8 without residual respiratory symptoms. At the 1-month follow-up, she reported no recurrence of symptoms and had resumed daily activities.

Discussion

Contrast-induced NCPE is exceedingly rare, with an incidence estimated at ~0.005%. Unlike anaphylaxis or cardiogenic oedema, it results from acute endothelial injury and capillary leak rather than allergic or cardiac mechanisms.63 Patients typically present with abrupt hypoxaemia, bilateral pulmonary opacities and preserved cardiac function immediately after contrast injection.3 7 8

Published reports describe similar cases after both low-osmolar and iso-osmolar agents.7 3 8 Fatal outcomes have been reported despite supportive therapy.8 9 Risk factors are unclear though comorbid vascular disease may predispose patients. Importantly, prior uneventful contrast exposure does not eliminate risk.3

Management is primarily supportive focusing on oxygenation and positive pressure ventilation.2 Diuretics may have limited benefit compared with cardiogenic oedema.1 Corticosteroids and antihistamines are not effective unless features of anaphylaxis overlap.4 Prompt airway management is critical.

This case reinforces the need for vigilance among emergency and radiology teams. A quick bedside echocardiogram and BNP can help differentiate NCPE from cardiogenic causes.2 Guidelines recommend that contrast studies be performed only where immediate airway and resuscitation equipment are available.2 A practical diagnostic algorithm forthe evaluation of acute respiratory distress following iodinated contrast administration is presented in (figure 4).
