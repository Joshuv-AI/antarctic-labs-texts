Rabies virus glycoprotein variants modulate neuronal nicotinic receptors in a conformation-dependent manner
Authors: Seyedeh Melika Akaberi, Sabina Yeasmin, Pooja Sapkota, Kavita Sharma, Marvin Schulte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRabies encephalitis remains nearly 100% fatal once symptoms appear, and no effective treatments exist because the molecular mechanisms underlying neuronal disruption remain poorly understood. This has prevented the development of molecular-based therapies, leaving only symptomatic management strategies. RABV-G has long been proposed to interact with muscle and, more recently, neuronal nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), but neuronal receptors have only been implicated using short peptide fragments. This study characterizes the interaction of a synthetic full-length RABV-G ectodomain with two neuronal nAChR subtypes associated with rabies-related behaviors, α4β2 and α7. We examined two forms within the predicted nicotinic binding domain: the naturally occurring 183P variant and the charge-reversal substitution 196D, and tested whether modulation depends on the glycoprotein’s conformation by conducting experiments at pH 6.5 and 7.4. At physiological pH, 183P inhibited both receptors (approximate IC₅₀(α7) = 3.6–11.5 µM; approximate IC₅₀(α4β2) = 4.2–14.1 µM), whereas 196D produced potentiation (~60% for α4β2; 35–40% for α7). Co-application restored α4β2 currents to control levels. Modulation occurred only at pH 7.4. These findings show that full-length RABV-G directly modulates neuronal nAChRs, that residue 196 determines the modulation polarity, and that the pre-fusion conformation mediates this interaction, supporting the exploration of receptor-targeted strategies for rabies encephalitis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53603-3.Keywords: Rabies virus glycoproteins, Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, Allosteric modulation, pH-dependent conformational changes, Neuro-invasion, Host–pathogen interactionsSubject terms: Biochemistry, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Untreated rabies infection produces a fatal viral encephalitis, leading to ~59,000 deaths annually, with the highest burden in regions lacking timely access to post-exposure prophylaxis1. While vaccination is highly effective when administered shortly after exposure, there are currently no approved treatments once neurological symptoms have appeared. This outcome reflects our incomplete understanding of how the rabies virus disrupts neural function in the central nervous system (CNS)2.

The rabies virus surface glycoprotein (RABV-G) is essential for mediating host cell attachment, membrane fusion, and neuronal entry3. As a class III fusion protein, RABV-G undergoes reversible, pH-dependent conformational changes due to domain-linker reconstructions; maintaining a compact, bent, receptor-binding form at neutral pH (~7.4) and an elongated fusion-active form in acidic endosomal conditions (~pH 6.5)4,5. The pre-fusion conformation, which predominates at physiological pH, mediates the initial receptor interaction, whereas endosomal acidification triggers the transition required for membrane fusion5,6. Recent cryo-EM studies of lyssavirus glycoproteins have visualized these pH-driven transitions. These structures revealed loop-to-helix rearrangements and new low-pH contacts between the C-terminal and adjacent domains that stabilize the post-fusion form. This structural shift hides receptor-binding surfaces, preventing further interaction under acidic conditions. This conformational flexibility supports viral entry and likely modulates receptor engagement within the CNS6.

Ligand-gated ion channels (LGICs), including nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), are critical mediators of fast synaptic transmission in the CNS and have emerged as potential targets for neuroinvasive viruses7–9. Early studies demonstrated that rabies virus enters neurons primarily through neuromuscular junctions, where it colocalizes with nAChRs on muscle endplates, identifying these as the initial sites of viral attachment and entry10. While muscle-type nAChRs at neuromuscular junctions mediate early infection, neuronal nAChRs are believed to contribute to the behavioral and neurophysiological manifestations of rabies7.

Prior studies have shown that peptide fragments derived from RABV-G can inhibit neuronal nAChRs, suggesting that the virus may alter neuronal excitability and signaling by targeting these receptors11. However, the ability of full-length RABV-G to modulate neuronal nAChRs remains largely uncharacterized, and no prior study has functionally assessed the effects of full-length RABV-G on neuronal nAChR subtypes using two-electrode voltage clamp (TEVC) across its distinct conformational states. Thus, the structural basis and physiological consequences of RABV-G interaction with neuronal nAChRs remain unknown. Understanding these interactions is crucial for clarifying how the rabies virus affects cholinergic signaling and neuronal activity, thereby establishing a mechanistic link to the behavioral and cognitive changes observed during infection and identifying new possibilities for receptor-based therapeutic approaches.

To better understand how RABV-G engages neuronal receptors, attention has focused on a toxin-like segment within its ectodomain (residues 175–203). This segment shares sequence and structural similarity with loop 2 of snake venom α-neurotoxins, α-conotoxins, and prototoxins, which are potent antagonists of nAChRs12,13. These toxins inhibit nAChR activity by binding to the acetylcholine-binding site, and similar receptor-interacting features have been identified within the RABV-G loop region11,14. Residues within this segment are mostly conserved among natural rabies virus isolates, forming a surface-exposed, cationic loop crucial in receptor recognition. Molecular evolutionary analyses identified residue 183 as the only site under positive selection in the RABV-G ectodomain, making it the most polymorphic position in the glycoprotein, while other residues within the neurotoxin-like loop remain highly conserved among natural rabies virus isolates11,15. Peptide binding studies demonstrated that substitutions at this site change affinity for acetylcholine-binding proteins, suggesting that residue 183 contributes to the receptor interaction interface11. In contrast, residue 196 is strongly conserved as a basic residue (arginine or lysine) across all natural and pathogenic RABV-G sequences, forming a positively charged surface group that is postulated to mimic the quaternary ammonium of acetylcholine, a feature shared with α-neurotoxins that engage nicotinic receptors12,16. Peptide studies have shown that replacement of this residue with aspartic acid (R196D) reverses the charge and converts the peptide from an inhibitory to a potentiating modulator of nAChR-mediated currents11.

Because the α4β2 and α7 nAChR subtypes are the main mediators of cholinergic signaling involved in neuronal excitability, arousal, and autonomic control, their disruption could explain the agitation, paralysis, hyperactivity, and autonomic instability characteristic of rabies infection17,18. Evidence linking nAChR inhibition to RABV-G pathogenicity suggests that restoring receptor function or preventing its inhibition could reduce disease severity11,14,16. However, previous studies have relied on short RABV-G peptides to model receptor interactions11,14. O’Brien et al. (2024) demonstrated that a synthetic peptide containing the neurotoxin-like loop of RABV-G could bind and inhibit human α7 nAChRs, supporting the hypothesis that this domain contributes to receptor targeting14. Yet, peptide-based approaches fail to reflect the structural flexibility and pH-driven transitions that define the functional behavior of the intact glycoprotein. Using the complete, properly folded ectodomain provides a more physiologically relevant framework to evaluate how structural transitions in RABV-G influence neuronal receptor modulation and disease mechanisms.

In this study, we investigated how the full-length RABV-G interacts with human α4β2 and α7 nAChRs and the relevance of pH-dependent conformations of RABV-G in mediating its functional effects. Using purified ectodomains representing a pathogenic variant (183P) and a charge-reversal mutant (R196D), we assessed subtype selectivity, pH dependence, and modulation mechanism through two-electrode voltage-clamp recordings in Xenopus laevis oocytes. These findings advance understanding of RABV-G–mediated neuronal signaling and identify receptor modulation as a potential therapeutic target in rabies neurotoxicity.

Results

Expression and purification of RABV-G proteins in COS-7 cells

Recombinant RABV-G ectodomains were expressed in COS-7 cells and purified using immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC). Optimization of the elution buffer to 250 mM imidazole at pH 6.5 produced consistent purification profiles, yielding 0.3–1 mg of purified protein per 100 mL of culture supernatant. SDS–PAGE under reducing conditions revealed a single major band migrating near 70 kDa, consistent with the predicted ~66 kDa monomeric ectodomain Fig 1a. Comparable results were obtained across five independent transfections, confirming reproducible expression and purification. Functional effect of RABV-G on α4β2 nAChRs:

To determine how full-length rabies virus glycoprotein (RABV-G) variants modulate neuronal α4β2 nAChRs, two-electrode voltage-clamp (TEVC) recordings were performed in Xenopus laevis oocytes during co-application of acetylcholine (ACh) with purified recombinant RABV-G ectodomains Fig 2. Oocytes were voltage-clamped at −60 mV in ND96 buffer (pH 7.4). For each recording, 10 µM ACh (approximately the EC₅₀ for α4β2 receptors) was co-applied with RABV-G across an 8-point logarithmic concentration range (1 nM–30 µM: 1, 3, 10, 100, 300 nM, 1, 10, and 30 µM) to assess concentration-dependent modulation. Each data point represents the response from a single oocyte (n = 4–5 per condition) obtained from ≥3 independent batches, and data are presented alongside the mean ± SEM to illustrate variability and reproducibility (Fig 2a,b,c,e,g). Individual data points are shown in the upper-right inset panels (a, c, e, g); due to similar values at some concentrations, multiple data points overlap and are not visually distinguishable.

Functional modulation of α4β2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors by RABV-G variants at physiological and acidic pH. Panels (a) and (b) show concentration–response relationships for RABV-G 183P at pH 7.4, where (a) displays mean ± SEM and (b) shows representative TEVC traces, demonstrating concentration-dependent inhibition of ACh-evoked currents. Panel (c) shows the concentration–response relationship for 183P at pH 6.5 (mean ± SEM), indicating minimal modulation across the tested range, and panel (d) shows representative TEVC traces. Panels (e) and (f) show concentration–response relationships for RABV-G 196D at pH 7.4, where (e) displays mean ± SEM and (f) shows representative TEVC traces, demonstrating biphasic modulation characterized by potentiation at lower concentrations followed by reduced responses at higher concentrations. Panels (g) and (h) show concentration–response relationships for 196D at pH 6.5 (mean ± SEM) and representative TEVC traces (h), indicating limited modulation.

Two RABV-G variants were tested: 183P, which contains a proline at position 183 found in pathogenic field strains, and 196D, which carries an aspartic acid substitution at position 196. Both residues are located within the neurotoxin-like loop of RABV-G that has been proposed to contribute to receptor interaction11,14.

Representative traces in Fig 2b illustrate the electrophysiological responses. Co-application of increasing concentrations of RABV-G 183P with a constant 10 µM ACh produced concentration-dependent inhibition of α4β2 receptor currents, reaching approximately 70% reduction at the highest concentration tested (30 µM). Peak current amplitudes decreased progressively across the concentration range (1 nM–30 µM), while activation and desensitization kinetics remained largely unchanged. Consistent with these observations, the integrated current (AUC) decreased in parallel with peak amplitude, indicating reduced receptor activation. The normalized concentration–response relationship Fig 2a yielded an estimated IC₅₀ of 4.2–14.4 µM (95% CI), consistent with moderate inhibitory potency at α4β2 receptors. Because the concentration range did not fully reach saturation at the highest concentrations tested, these values should be interpreted as approximate. The availability of purified recombinant glycoprotein limited the extension of the concentration range, as TEVC recordings with protein ectodomains require substantial material across multiple oocytes.

In contrast, RABV-G 196D exhibited a biphasic modulation of α4β2 receptor activity Fig 2e–f, which was consistent across oocytes (n = 4–5 per condition). At lower concentrations (200–300 nM), co-application with ACh enhanced current amplitudes by up to ~60% without detectable changes in response kinetics. This potentiating phase was followed by a decline in response at higher concentrations (≥3 µM), with currents returning toward baseline levels. The concentration–response relationship, therefore, reflects a bidirectional effect, with an apparent EC₅₀ of approximately 0.2 µM (range 0.1–0.3 µM) for potentiation and an apparent IC₅₀ of approximately 1.1 µM (range 0.8–1.6 µM) for the inhibitory phase. These findings indicate concentration-dependent dual modulation by the 196D variant; however, detailed mechanistic interpretation is limited under TEVC conditions. Accordingly, the present data define the direction and relative magnitude of receptor modulation but do not constitute a fully saturated pharmacological characterization.

pH-Dependent modulation of α4β2 nicotinic receptors by rabies glycoprotein variants

The effects of RABV-G 183P and RABV-G 196D on α4β2 receptors were evaluated at pH 6.5 using MES buffer to examine how extracellular acidity influences receptor modulation. At this pH, RABV-G has been reported to adopt an elongated, post-fusion conformation. Control recordings with ACh alone showed that α4β2 receptors remained functional at pH 6.5, although the overall current amplitude was reduced by approximately 10–15% compared with pH 7.4, consistent with the known pH sensitivity of neuronal nAChRs. No major changes in activation or desensitization kinetics were observed. As shown in Fig 2c, d, RABV-G 183P, tested across concentrations from 1 nM to 30 µM in the presence of constant 10 µM ACh, did not significantly alter α4β2 receptor activity, and the corresponding dose–response data did not yield a reliable IC₅₀. Similarly, RABV-G 196D produced no detectable modulatory effect over the same concentration range Fig 2g, h. At acidic pH, both variants generated currents similar in shape but smaller in amplitude than those evoked by ACh alone.

Mechanistic analysis: competitive vs. non-competitive

To determine whether RABV-G modulates α4β2 receptors through direct competition with ACh or an allosteric mechanism, ACh dose–response curves were recorded in the absence and presence of fixed concentrations of each RABV-G variant Fig 3. As a control, ACh dose–response curves were first obtained alone to define baseline ACh potency. The same recordings were then repeated with RABV-G co-applied with increasing concentrations of ACh, allowing direct comparison of potency (EC₅₀) and maximum current (Iₘₐₓ) in the presence of RABV-G vs ACh alone.

Mechanistic analysis of α4β2 receptor modulation by RABV-G variants using ACh dose–response curves. (a) RABV-G 183P (1 µM and 3 µM) reduced Iₘₐₓ by ~45 % and ~65 %, respectively, and produced a slight leftward shift in the ACh dose–response curve. EC₅₀ values were 11.4 µM (control), 8.1 µM (+1 µM RABV-G), and 9.2 µM (+3 µM RABV-G), consistent with a mixed allosteric inhibitory effect that primarily decreases receptor efficacy. (b) RABV-G 196D (300 nM and 1 µM) potentiated α4β2 receptor responses to ~135 % and ~195 % of control, respectively, and produced a modest change in ACh EC₅₀ values, shifting from 7.8 µM (ACh alone) to 5.7 µM (300 nM RABV-G) and 10 µM (1 µM RABV-G). These results indicate positive allosteric modulation that enhances receptor efficacy while maintaining overall agonist potency within a similar range.

For RABV-G 183P, co-application of fixed concentrations of RABV-G (1 µM and 3 µM) with increasing ACh concentrations (0.01–300 µM) reduced the maximal current amplitude (Iₘₐₓ) without producing a marked shift in the ACh dose–response curve. ACh concentrations were tested up to 300 µM, which represents the upper range routinely used in our TEVC recordings, as higher concentrations can distort responses through increased receptor desensitization and channel blockade. At the highest ACh concentration, Iₘₐₓ decreased by approximately 40–45% in the presence of 1 µM RABV-G and by ~65% with 3 µM RABV-G. EC₅₀ values for ACh were determined from nonlinear regression of the dose–response curves and are reported with their 95% confidence intervals: 11.4 µM (ACh alone), 8.1 µM (+1 µM RABV-G), and 9.2 µM (+3 µM RABV-G), indicating only a modest change in apparent receptor sensitivity. Importantly, even in the lower portion of the ACh concentration–response curve, no parallel rightward shift relative to control conditions was observed. These results indicate that RABV-G 183P does not behave as a competitive antagonist but instead reduces the maximal receptor response, consistent with noncompetitive or allosteric inhibition. No consistent differences were observed in desensitization rates or current kinetics. This pattern indicates a mixed allosteric inhibitory effect, suggesting that RABV-G 183P probably does not block the ACh binding site directly but acts at an allosteric site that limits receptor activation Fig 3a.

For RABV-G 196D, co-application of constant 300 nM and 1 µM RABV-G with increasing ACh concentrations (0.01–300 µM) enhanced the maximal current amplitude (Iₘₐₓ) without producing any measurable shift in the ACh dose–response curve. At the highest ACh concentration, responses increased to approximately 135 % and 195 % of control in the presence of 300 nM and 1 µM RABV-G 196D, respectively Fig 3b. EC₅₀ values (7.8, 5.7, and 10 µM) had overlapping 95 % confidence intervals, indicating no significant change in agonist potency while overall current amplitude increased. No consistent changes were observed in desensitization rate or current kinetics. This profile demonstrates positive allosteric modulation, suggesting that RABV-G 196D enhances receptor activation through an allosteric interaction that increases efficacy rather than altering ACh binding affinity Fig 3b.

ACh was applied over a concentration range of 0.01–300 µM in the absence and presence of fixed RABV-G concentrations to compare receptor potency (EC₅₀) and maximal current (Iₘₐₓ).

Reciprocal modulation by combined RABV-G variants

To test whether the inhibitory and potentiating effects of RABV-G variants influence each other, we recorded ACh-evoked currents sequentially under three conditions. Application of 10 µM ACh alone produced the expected control response. When 1 µM RABV-G 183P was co-applied with ACh, the peak current decreased by approximately 40 %, confirming its inhibitory effect Fig 4, Red trace. When 1 µM RABV-G 196D was added together with 1 µM RABV-G 183P and ACh, the current partially recovered, returning close to the control amplitude, ACh alone Fig 4. Desensitization rate and current kinetics remained unchanged. Thus, RABV-G 196D can counteract the inhibition caused by 183P, consistent with opposing allosteric actions on α4β2 receptor activity.

Traces show sequential application of 10 µM ACh alone (lower black trace), ACh + 1 µM RABV-G 183P (red trace), and ACh + 1 µM RABV-G 183P + 1 µM RABV-G 196D. Functional effect of rabies glycoprotein on α7 nAChR

We next examined the effects of full-length RABV-G ectodomain variants on homomeric α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) co-expressed with the RIC-3 chaperone protein in Xenopus laevis oocytes Fig 5. RIC-3 was included to facilitate proper folding and surface expression of functional α7 receptors, which otherwise exhibit low oocyte expression levels. Recordings were performed using TEVC at a holding potential of −60 mV in ND96 buffer (pH 7.4) containing 100 µM ACh, the approximate EC₅₀ for α7 receptors, to detect both inhibitory and potentiating effects. Baseline ACh responses were applied repeatedly until stable responses were obtained before testing RABV-G variants.

Co-application of increasing concentrations of RABV-G 183P with constant 100 µM ACh produced a concentration-dependent inhibition of α7 receptor currents. At the highest concentration of 183P (10 µM), the peak current amplitude decreased by approximately 55% compared with ACh alone, and this inhibition was consistent across 4 oocytes Fig 5a,b. The normalized dose–response curve yielded an estimated IC₅₀ of 3.6–11.5 µM (95% CI). No consistent changes in activation or desensitization kinetics were observed, indicating that modulation primarily affected response amplitude. In contrast, RABV-G 196D enhanced ACh-evoked responses across a concentration range of 0.1–10 µM, with maximal potentiation of 35–40% observed near 10 µM Fig 5e,f. These experiments were designed to assess functional modulation under co-application conditions rather than to distinguish between competitive and noncompetitive mechanisms. Together, these results demonstrate that both RABV-G variants retain activity at α7 receptors: 183P functions as a moderate inhibitor, and 196D is a modest potentiator. Compared with α4β2 receptors, where 196D increased peak currents by ~60%, the potentiation at α7 was smaller, suggesting that receptor subtype and conformational flexibility influence how rabies glycoprotein variants interact with neuronal nAChRs.

Discussion

This study extends the work of Hueffer et al. (2017), who determined that a neurotoxin-like peptide within RABV-G can modulate α4β2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs)11. Their result demonstrated that a peptide containing the equivalent of the 183P residue of the RABV-G ectodomain inhibited receptor activity, while substitution of a basic residue at a position equivalent to the 196 position with an acidic residue (D) potentiated it. O’Brien et al. later evaluated peptide interactions with α7 receptors using an attenuated RABV-G, but their work did not address α4β2, pH dependence, or the structural context of modulation14. Because short peptides lack the tertiary and quaternary constraints of the full glycoprotein11, we examined recombinant full-length ectodomains (183P and 196D) on α4β2 and α7 nAChRs under conditions that stabilize either the prefusion or post-fusion state of RABV-G corresponding to those present at pH 7.4 and 6.5, respectively.

RABV-G, 183P represents a natural pathogenic form of rabies and retains the native proline at position 183, located within the purported neurotoxin-like loop12. This residue is highly polymorphic and contributes to strain-specific differences in receptor engagement and virulence 11,15,20. In contrast, residue 196 is strongly conserved: lyssaviruses almost always retain a basic amino acid (arginine or lysine) at this site12,16,21. We observed that a single basic-to-acidic substitution reverses modulation polarity, likely because RABV-G relies on precise electrostatic contacts for nAChR interaction. Introducing aspartic acid at position 196 potentially disrupts local charge balance, alters conformation, and weakens stabilizing interactions, consistent with reduced activity; however, it is unclear precisely how this results in a potentiating effect22.

Earlier studies have shown that the rabies virus binds saturably to purified nAChRs, and that this interaction is partially reduced by α-bungarotoxin, suggesting that the viral binding site lies near or overlaps the toxin-binding region of the receptor12,23,24. Peptide mapping and pharmacological assays supported this view, but peptide fragments cannot recapitulate the behavior of the intact, pH-sensitive ectodomain. No high-resolution structure currently defines a RABV-G–nAChR interface; only available cryo-EM data capture RABV-G in complex with antibody 523-115.

The RABV-G ectodomain consists of three coordinated regions: the fusion domain (FD), which mediates membrane fusion; the pleckstrin-homology-like domain (PHD), which stabilizes the trimer; and the central domain (CD), which contains the neurotoxin-like loop involved in receptor engagement5. These regions pivot around the QC linker. At neutral or basic pH, RABV-G adopts a compact prefusion conformation competent for receptor binding. When the pH falls to ~6.5, the ectodomain transitions into the post-fusion state, with coordinated movements that expose the fusion loops. During this shift, residues such as Asp211 and His261 move closer to Glu269 and Asp266, forming stabilizing interactions that support the elongated conformation5. At higher pH, the QC interloop is highly flexible, and these residues lack defined electron density, indicating limited contact in the prefusion state. This pH-driven rearrangement also reduces the affinity of antibody 523-11 and removes the surface configuration required for nAChR engagement, explaining why receptor modulation was lost at pH 6.55,25. These structural considerations align with our functional data.

All experiments were performed in a heterologous expression system using Xenopus laevis oocytes, and therefore, the findings should be interpreted within the context of an in vitro model. While the results demonstrate that RABV-G variants can directly modulate neuronal nicotinic receptors, they do not fully recapitulate the complexity of native neuronal environments, including receptor composition, membrane context, and synaptic signaling. Accordingly, these findings are best interpreted as evidence of direct receptor modulation rather than a complete representation of in vivo physiological processes.

The pharmacological characterization in this study is limited by the concentration range tested, which did not fully reach saturation at higher concentrations. This constraint was primarily due to limited availability and yield of purified recombinant rabies glycoprotein, as well as concerns regarding protein stability and aggregation at higher concentrations, which could compromise functional interpretation. As a result, EC₅₀ and IC₅₀ values should be interpreted as approximate estimates rather than definitive parameters. Despite this limitation, the data reliably define the direction of modulation and relative potency. A more complete quantitative analysis will require extension of the concentration range in future studies, contingent upon improved protein yield and stability.

In our study, electrophysiological recordings in Xenopus laevis oocytes showed that 183P consistently inhibited α4β2 and α7 receptor currents, whereas 196D produced strong potentiation. These effects were reproducible and independent of desensitization kinetics. The interpretation of α7 nAChR modulation requires consideration of the intrinsic kinetic properties of this receptor subtype. α7 receptors exhibit rapid activation and desensitization, and under non-equilibrium TEVC conditions, responses are highly sensitive to agonist application timing, perfusion dynamics, and receptor state. These features complicate classical mechanistic analysis based on shifts in EC₅₀ and maximal response, as both peak currents and integrated responses can reflect changes in agonist affinity, channel gating, and desensitization. Accordingly, α7 receptor activity in this study was quantified using the area under the curve (AUC) under controlled co-application conditions, which provides a more robust and reproducible measure of receptor function for this subtype. Under these conditions, both inhibitory and potentiating effects were consistently observed across independent oocyte batches, supporting the reproducibility of α7 receptor modulation by RABV-G variants. However, while these results demonstrate clear functional modulation, they do not resolve the precise mechanistic basis (e.g., competitive versus allosteric) underlying these effects. Therefore, the α7 findings should be interpreted as evidence of functional modulation rather than a definitive mechanistic characterization, and higher temporal resolution approaches will be required to resolve the underlying mechanisms.

When 183P was applied with ACh, the evoked current was smaller than with ACh alone, suggesting that 183P interferes with receptor activation, either by preventing channel opening (inhibition), or by only weakly triggering it (partial agonism). In contrast, the charge reversal at residue 196 (R→D) converted this effect into potentiation, highlighting the role of local electrostatics in determining receptor coupling. Surprisingly, co-application of 196D with 183P restored α4β2 currents to the same amplitude observed with ACh alone, demonstrating that the opposing actions of the two variants directly counterbalance each other. It is not clear if they do so via binding to the same or different binding site although the charge difference suggests at least a slight rearrangement of the binding position.

The observed interactions with both the α4β2 and α7 receptors occur only at neutral to slightly basic pH, consistent with the prefusion, receptor-binding conformation, and disappear at acidic pH (~6.5), when RABV-G adopts its post-fusion form4,6. Although local pH fluctuations can occur during neuronal activity or inflammation, extracellular pH in the CNS remains predominantly neutral26,27, making this the most amenable environment for receptor modulation. While the in vivo concentration of soluble RABV-G remains unknown, multivalent virion surfaces and shedding of ectodomains28 likely increase local effective levels near synaptic membranes.

The functional selectivity we observed aligns with known neurological hallmarks of rabies encephalitis. α4β2 receptors, concentrated in the cortex, hippocampus, and thalamus, regulate excitatory–inhibitory balance and neuronal firing that control arousal and behavior29,30. Their inhibition by RABV-G may contribute to hyperexcitability, agitation, and aggression typical of encephalitic rabies. α7 receptors, abundant in cortical and limbic regions that regulate cognition and emotion, play key roles in memory, attention, and anxiet29–31; disruption of α7 signaling may cause cognitive decline and emotional instability during infection.

Future studies in neuronal systems and in vivo models will be required to determine the physiological relevance of these interactions.

Materials

Protein expression

COS-7 cells (ATCC® CRL-1651™) were used for recombinant protein expression. Plasmid constructs encoding the rabies virus glycoprotein ectodomain (residues 23–439; GenBank accession GQ406342) with C-terminal FLAG and His₆ tags were cloned into the pSF-CMV-PURO vector (Oxford Genetics). The construct representing the naturally occurring pathogenic variant containing proline at position 183 (183P) was used as the reference sequence. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed on this background to substitute arginine 196 with aspartic acid, generating the R196D mutant. E. coli DH5α (Promega) was the bacterial host for plasmid maintenance and amplification. Recombinant proteins were purified by immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) using Ni-Sepharose 6 Fast Flow resin and HisTrap HP columns (Cytiva). Supplementary Table S1 provides the amino acid sequences of the receptor-binding loop region (residues 175–203) for both variants.

Protein characterization

MES, imidazole, NaCl, and non-fat dry milk were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Invitrolon™ PVDF membranes (0.45 µm, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# LC2005), Novex™ 4–12% Tris-Glycine Mini Gels, Tris-Glycine running buffer, and pre-stained protein ladders (10–180 kDa, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 26616) were used for SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis. SuperSignal™ West Dura ECL substrate and 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for chemiluminescent and colorimetric detection, respectively.

Electrophysiology

Xenopus laevis oocytes (stages IV–V) were obtained from Xenopus1 Inc. Human α4, β2, α7, and Ric-3 cDNAs were synthesized and characterized by GeneArt, Addgene, and GenScript. The Effectene® Transfection Kit (Qiagen) was used for mammalian transfection, and the T7 mMESSAGE mMACHINE™ kit (Thermo Fisher, Cat# AM1344) for Capped cRNA synthesis. ND96 and MES buffers were freshly prepared at pH 7.4 and 6.5, respectively, for electrophysiological assays.

Methods

Design and expression of rabies virus glycoprotein mutants

Mutations at positions 183 and 196 (including the double mutant 196D/183P) were selected based on previous structure–function analyses of synthetic RGP-derived peptides (Hueffer et al., 2017) 11. The coding sequence for the mature rabies virus glycoprotein ectodomain (residues 23–439; GenBank accession GQ406342) was cloned into the mammalian expression vector pSF-CMV-PURO-COOH-3C-FLAG-His₆ (Oxford Genetics), which encodes C-terminal FLAG and His₆ tags for detection and purification. Constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α cells (Promega) using a standard CaCl₂-based chemical transformation protocol. Briefly, 50 µL of competent cells were mixed with 1–5 µL of plasmid DNA, incubated on ice for 30 min, heat-shocked at 42 °C for 30 s, and returned to ice for 2 min. Cells were recovered in 500 µL of pre-warmed SOC medium at 37 °C with shaking (225 rpm) for 1 h, then plated on LB agar containing 50 µg/mL kanamycin and incubated overnight. Individual colonies were inoculated into LB broth with kanamycin (50 µg/mL), and plasmid DNA was isolated using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen) to assess DNA yield and quality. Verified preparations were then expanded for large-scale purification using the Qiagen Plasmid Maxi Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Plasmid maps and restriction sites were analyzed using Serial Cloner 2.6.1, and correct insertion was confirmed by restriction digestion with XhoI and SphI.

COS-7 cells (ATCC® CRL-1651™), a fibroblast-like kidney cell line derived from Cercopithecus aethiops (vervet monkey), were transfected with recombinant plasmids using the Effectene Transfection Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Because the pSF-CMV-PURO vector lacks a secretion signal, an N-terminal insulin signal peptide (MALWMRLLPLLALLALWGPDPAAA) was fused upstream of the rabies virus glycoprotein ectodomain (residues 23–439) to direct secretion of the soluble protein. Transfected cells were cultured at 37 °C in 5 % CO₂ for 72 h before harvesting. The recombinant ectodomain was efficiently secreted into the culture medium and collected for downstream purification.

Protein purification and characterization

Recombinant RABV-G proteins were purified by immobilized-metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) using 1 mL HisTrap HP columns (Cytiva). Filtered COS-7 culture supernatants (50 mL) were diluted 1:5 with loading buffer (50 mM MES, 20 mM imidazole, 150 mM NaCl, pH 6.5) and loaded onto the Ni-affinity column pre-equilibrated with the same buffer. After protein binding, the column was sequentially washed with 10 column volumes (CV) of Buffer 1 (50 mM MES, 300 mM NaCl, pH 6.5) to remove nonspecific and electrostatically bound proteins, followed by 15 CV of Buffer 2 (50 mM MES, 20 mM imidazole, 150 mM NaCl, pH 6.5) to eliminate weakly associated contaminants19. Elution was performed with Buffer 3 (50 mM MES, 500 mM imidazole, 150 mM NaCl, 20 % glycerol, pH 6.5). Twenty 1 mL fractions were collected and analyzed. The predominant RABV-G peak was consistently observed in fractions 4–6, which were pooled for subsequent processing. Pooled samples were desalted using 30 kDa centrifugal filters (Amicon) with three PBS washes (4000 rpm, 20 min each). Protein concentration was estimated from absorbance at 280 nm using a Nanophotometer and the theoretical extinction coefficient for the FLAG-His–tagged RABV-G ectodomain (ε₂₈₀ = 65 320 M⁻1 cm⁻1; corresponding to 1.09 mL mg⁻1 cm⁻1 for a 60 kDa protein), calculated from amino-acid composition (ExPASy ProtParam).

SDS-PAGE and Western blotting were used to verify the purity and molecular identity of purified RABV-G. Samples were mixed with 2× Tris-Glycine SDS sample buffer and 10× NuPAGE™ reducing agent, loaded onto 4–12 % Tris-Glycine mini gels (1.0 mm, WedgeWell™ format), and electrophoresed under constant current (20 mA per gel, ~110 V, 1 h). Gels were either stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 or transferred to 0.45 µm PVDF membranes (Invitrolon™, Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a semi-dry transfer system at 40 V for 1.5 h in 1× transfer buffer. Transfer efficiency was verified using a pre-stained protein ladder (10–180 kDa). Membranes were blocked overnight at 4 °C in PBS containing 5 % non-fat dry milk, then incubated with Rabies Virus Glycoprotein Polyclonal Antibody (PA5-117507, Thermo Scientific; 1:1000 dilution) as a primary antibody and HRP-conjugated Rabbit IgG (H+L) Secondary Antibody (31460, Thermo Scientific, 1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using SuperSignal™ West Dura ECL or TMB substrate. Equal volumes of purified protein (10 µL) were loaded per lane because the experiment aimed to verify protein identity rather than quantify expression levels.

Preparation of plasmid cdna and crna synthesis

Complementary DNA (cDNA) encoding human α7 (NM_000746.4) nAChR subunit was obtained from Addgene (Cat. 62276) and subcloned into the pcDNA3.1-CHRNA7/amp vector using XhoI and EcoRI restriction sites. The chaperone protein Ric-3, which promotes proper folding and surface expression of α7 nAChRs in Xenopus oocytes, was obtained from GenScript (NM_001206671.4; Cat. OHu11533) and inserted into the pcDNA3.1+/C-(K)DYK/amp vector using BamHI. Human α4 (NM_000744.5) and β2 (NM_000748.2) nAChR subunits were synthesized by GeneArt Inc. and cloned into pcDNA3.1/Zeo(+) and pcDNA3.1/hygro vectors using HindIII/BamHI and NotI/XhoI, respectively.

Each construct was transformed into E. coli DH5α cells and cultured in LB medium containing ampicillin (final concentration: 100 µL L⁻1) for plasmid amplification. Plasmid DNA was purified using a Qiagen Maxi Prep kit and validated by restriction digestion and agarose gel electrophoresis. α4 and β2 plasmids were confirmed with EcoRI and PscI, and α7 and Ric-3 plasmids with SspI and FspI. Restriction fragments were isolated and verified by gel electrophoresis before transcription.

Purified plasmid DNA served as the template for in vitro transcription. Capped complementary RNA (cRNA) transcripts were synthesized using the T7 mMESSAGE mMACHINE™ transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. AM1344) following the manufacturer’s protocol. cRNA yield and purity were determined spectrophotometrically using a Nanophotometer P330 before microinjection into Xenopus laevis oocytes.

Oocyte preparation and cRNA injection

Stage IV–V Xenopus laevis oocytes were purchased from Xenopus1 Inc. and enzymatically defolliculated with collagenase type I (2 mg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #17100-017) in Barth’s buffer (88 mM NaCl, 1 mM KCl, 0.4 mM CaCl₂, 1 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4) supplemented with 50 mg/L gentamicin. Oocytes were gently agitated for 1 to 1.5 h at room temperature, washed, and stored overnight in fresh Barth’s buffer containing 1 mM pyruvate at 15 °C to allow membrane recovery and stabilization.

After recovery, 20–30 oocytes were injected with 46 nL of capped cRNA using a Nanoject III microinjector. For α7 nAChR expression, α7 and Ric-3 cRNA were mixed at 1200 ng/µL and 400 ng/µL, respectively, before injection. Functional α7 receptors were detected within 5–7 days. For α4β2 nAChR expression, α4 and β2 cRNA were mixed at equal concentrations (1:1 ratio; 300 ng/µL each), and characteristic α4β2-mediated currents were detected within 24 h of injection.

Receptor functionality was verified by two-electrode voltage-clamp (TEVC) recordings using acetylcholine (ACh) as the agonist. Oocytes expressing α4β2 nAChRs exhibited slow activation kinetics and biphasic desensitization, with an ACh EC₅₀ of ~2–10 µM, consistent with previous reports32–34. α7-expressing oocytes displayed rapid activation and desensitization typical of homomeric α7 channels, with an EC₅₀ near 100 µM35,36. Application of nicotine (10 µM) produced ~40–60% inhibition of ACh-evoked currents, consistent with literature-reported IC₅₀ values (1–3 µM for α4β2; 30–100 µM for α7), confirming receptor identity and expected pharmacological profile37,38. Only oocytes exhibiting these characteristic current patterns were included in subsequent analyses.

Functional assay: two-electrode voltage clamp electrophysiology

Recordings were conducted using an automated oocyte recording system equipped with a computer-controlled perfusion and drug delivery system (Gilson 231 autosampler), an OC-725C amplifier (Warner Instruments), and a Digidata 1550B interface (Molecular Devices LLC). Currents were low-pass filtered at 1 kHz using the internal filter of the OC-725C amplifier, with no additional analog or digital filtering applied. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using Clampex and Clampfit (Molecular Devices) and GraphPad Prism 939.

Oocytes were voltage-clamped at −60 mV in a low-volume (~100–250 µL) horizontal flow recording chamber continuously perfused with ND96 buffer (96 mM NaCl, 2 mM KCl, 1.8 mM CaCl₂, 1 mM MgCl₂, 5 mM HEPES, pH 7.4) at a flow rate of 5–6 mL/min. Under acidic conditions, HEPES was replaced with MES and the pH adjusted to 6.5. The chamber design enabled rapid solution exchange, allowing accurate resolution of fast receptor kinetics, particularly for α7 nAChRs.

Recombinant RABV-G proteins were prepared in PBS and diluted into the corresponding recording buffer (ND96 or MES). Samples were prepared fresh on the day of recording and co-applied with acetylcholine (ACh) to assess modulatory effects. A 5-minute washout period was used between applications to ensure full recovery and minimize cumulative desensitization.

Concentration–response experiments were performed using an 8-point logarithmic series (1 nM–30 µM). For α4β2 nAChRs, responses were quantified using peak current amplitude following stabilization of baseline ACh responses. For α7 nAChRs, responses were quantified using area under the curve (AUC) to account for rapid activation and desensitization kinetics, which limit reliable peak-based or mechanistic analysis under TEVC conditions.

For each oocyte, responses were normalized to the ACh-alone control recorded in the same cell to reduce variability due to expression differences. Concentration–response data were fitted using nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism 9. IC₅₀ and EC₅₀ values were determined from a minimum of four oocytes per condition obtained from at least three independent donor frogs. Strict inclusion criteria were applied, including stable holding currents, reproducible ACh-evoked responses, and absence of leak currents or rundown.

Data analysis

Functional TEVC recordings were analyzed using pCLAMP. For each oocyte, current amplitudes or area under the curve (AUC) values were normalized to the corresponding ACh control response to minimize variability arising from differences in receptor expression and oocyte size. Normalized currents were expressed as a percentage of the ACh-alone response (set to 100%) for the construction of dose–response and inhibition curves. AUC was used for analysis of α7 receptor responses to account for rapid activation and desensitization. This provides a robust and widely accepted measure of total receptor activation for this receptor subtype.

Concentration–response relationships were analyzed using GraphPad® Prism version 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) with nonlinear regression. Potentiating effects were fit using the built-in log(agonist) vs. response – variable slope (four-parameter) equation, and inhibitory effects were fit using the log(inhibitor) vs. response – variable slope (four-parameter) equation. For all fits, the lower response plateau was constrained to 0 to stabilize curve fitting and facilitate comparison across oocytes with variable expression levels. Unconstrained fits were also tested and produced EC₅₀/IC₅₀ values within 10% of the constrained model, confirming that fixed-baseline fitting did not bias parameter estimation. Best-fit parameters and 95% confidence intervals were determined using the profile-likelihood method.

For modulation of the α4β2 receptor by the RABV-G 196D variant, which exhibited a biphasic concentration–response profile, data were fit globally using Prism’s bell-shaped dose–response model to characterize both the potentiating and inhibitory phases.

All statistical analyses and curve fitting were performed in GraphPad Prism version 9. Results are presented as mean ± SEM from independent oocytes (n indicated in figure legends) obtained from at least three independent experimental batches. Nonlinear regressions showed good agreement with the experimental data, with R2 values exceeding 0.8.

Reproducibility

All electrophysiology experiments were independently repeated at least three times using oocytes from different frogs or separate biosensor runs, and response kinetics were consistent across independent experiments. Recombinant rabies glycoproteins were freshly produced and purified for each series of recordings. SDS-PAGE and Western blot examined every protein batch to confirm its identity, purity (typically >90%), and expected molecular weight (~66 kDa) before use.

Data were included in the final analysis only when the following quality criteria were met: stable membrane potential during voltage clamp (holding current within ±100 nA at −60 mV), reproducible ACh-evoked control responses within ±20% across replicates, no signs of leak or rundown during repeated stimulations, and protein preparations showing a single major immunoreactive band without degradation. These measures ensured that all reported results were based on reliable, well-controlled, and reproducible experiments.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that full-length RABV-G ectodomains act as reversible, pH-dependent modulators of neuronal nAChRs. A single charge change at residue 196 reverses modulation polarity, linking viral sequence variation to receptor activity and potentially to neurovirulence. Although rabies encephalitis involves multiple inflammatory and neuronal processes beyond direct receptor modulation40,41, our results provide a mechanistic basis for how RABV-G may influence cholinergic signaling during infection. These observations may help guide the identification of structural features or molecular strategies capable of counteracting receptor suppression. Further research is needed to define the binding interface, subtype specificity, and physiological relevance of these interactions to different stages of rabies pathogenesis.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, electrophysiological recordings in Xenopus laevis oocytes and heterologous mammalian systems do not fully reproduce the complexity of neuronal receptor organization in the mammalian brain. In vivo studies will be needed to determine physiological and behavioral relevance. Second, this work examined two ectodomains (183P and 196D), but additional natural or engineered variants may show distinct modulatory profiles. Third, although we demonstrated clear pH-dependent effects, the atomic details of how pH influences RABV-G conformation and receptor coupling remain unresolved and will require high-resolution structural or computational studies. Additionally, concentration–response analysis for α7 nAChRs was limited to a range that enabled reproducible and interpretable recordings under TEVC conditions; while this range was sufficient to establish the direction and magnitude of functional modulation, it may not fully define the upper asymptote of the response. Accordingly, pharmacological parameters reported here should be interpreted as approximate estimates. Fourth, our analysis focused on α4β2 and α7 nAChRs; other nicotinic or ligand-gated ion channels may also contribute to rabies pathogenesis. In addition, structural similarities across other members of the broader ligand-gated ion channel family might also enable rabies virus to interact with other family members (ex, serotonin type 3 or GABA-A receptors), thus additional research into these interactions is warranted. Finally, while 196D counteracted 183P-mediated inhibition, the translational relevance of such variants to rabies therapeutics remains uncertain and would require extensive evaluation in animal models.
