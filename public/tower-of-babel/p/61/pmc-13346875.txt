A concise and practical route to Gedatolisib via a continuous-flow Kumada coupling reaction
Authors: Jianhai Yang, Jun Li, Donghe Tian, Cai Xu, Liyan Dai, Xiaozhong Wang, Jia Guo, Weibin Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A practical three-step route to Gedatolisib, a dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitor for breast cancer, has been developed, featuring a key continuous-flow iron-catalyzed Kumada coupling. This continuous-flow setup enhances heat and mass transfer, eliminates the need for noble metal catalysts and associated removal steps. This approach avoids hazardous reagents as well as high-boiling solvents, thereby improving both safety and scalability. The 100 gram scale synthesis affords Gedatolisib in 66% overall yield (98.9% purity), featuring mild conditions and crystallization-based purification, offering lower costs and better scalability for industrial production.

The 1,3,5-triazine core has emerged as a privileged scaffold in medicinal chemistry,1 and Gedatolisib (PKI-587, PF-05212384) stands as its most clinically advanced exemplar. Gedatolisib is a dual PI3K (phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase)/mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin) inhibitor that blocks PI3K-α with an IC50 of 0.4 nM and mTOR with 1.6 nM, while the other PI3K isoforms (β, γ, δ) are inhibited in the 5–6 nM range, addressing a critical therapeutic need as PI3K pathway hyperactivation occurs in over 70% of human cancers (Fig. 1).2 Originally developed by Pfizer and currently advanced by Celcuity, the compound has demonstrated promising activity in solid tumors and has completed enrollment for the Phase III VIKTORIA-1 trial in HR+/HER2−/PIK3CA wild-type breast cancer,3 with a New Drug Application submitted to the FDA in 2025 and priority review granted in January 2026.4 The s-triazine nucleus provides high-affinity binding to the ATP pockets of both PI3K and mTOR, and the morpholine-urea side chains modulate selectivity and pharmacokinetics. These features position Gedatolisib as the flagship s-triazine anticancer agent and a template for next-generation PI3K/mTOR-targeted therapeutics.

However, the original palladium-catalyzed synthesis suffered from critical limitations that severely constrained its industrial applicability: expensive palladium catalysts (5 mol% Pd(PPh3)4), residual palladium contamination exceeding 20 ppm, a level that fails to meet regulatory standards for active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) preparation and laborious chromatographic purifications that hindered scalable production and increased manufacturing costs. The original five-step sequence from cyanuric chloride afforded the Gedatolisib in only 37% overall yield on a 45 g scale, with additional challenges including the need to prepare specialized intermediates such as 4-isocyanatobenzoate (Fig. 2, route 1).5 Mao et al. reported a breakthrough convergent synthesis eliminating noble metals entirely. The five-step route delivers Gedatolisib in 49% overall yield with 99.3% purity, employing inexpensive dicyandiamide and 4-nitrobenzonitrile as starting materials. Key innovations include controlled hydride-mediated double N-alkylation and crystallisation-based purification (Fig. 2, route 2).6

Different synthetic strategies for access to Gedatolisib.

Herein, we report a concise and practical route to Gedatolisib via a continuous-flow Kumada coupling reaction (Fig. 2, this work).7 While the Suzuki coupling offers the advantage of employing pinacol boronic esters, which are chemically stable and commercially available reagents, the iron-catalyzed Kumada coupling presented in this work provides distinct practical benefits at scale. Grignard reagents, though requiring anhydrous conditions and inert atmosphere handling, are nevertheless widely utilized in industrial processes and can be prepared cost-effectively from readily available aryl halides. More importantly, the Kumada coupling eliminates the need for expensive palladium catalysts and the associated metal scavenging steps required to meet stringent residual palladium limits for API production. Furthermore, adaptation of the Kumada coupling to continuous-flow operation, as demonstrated herein, effectively addresses the thermal sensitivity of Grignard reagents by enhancing heat and mass transfer, thereby improving operational safety and reproducibility. Thus, the iron-catalyzed continuous-flow Kumada coupling provides superior economic and scalability profiles for industrial manufacturing. This new synthetic route also avoids the use of hazardous reagents such as large quantities of sodium hydride (NaH) and toxic 2,2′-dichlorodiethyl ether, enhancing operational safety. Furthermore, it circumvents the use of high-boiling solvents like DMF in the final steps, mitigating concerns over potential genotoxic impurities. Overall, this new approach offers a higher total yield (66% over three steps), high purity (98.9%), lower raw material costs, and features milder reaction conditions. With its simpler work-up procedures and crystallization-based purification, the process is more environmentally friendly and demonstrably better suited for scale-up and industrial production.

The synthesis scheme comprises three steps: selective substitution, Kumada coupling, and nucleophilic addition. The reaction conditions for the selective disubstitution of cyanuric chloride with morpholine were systematically investigated (Table 1). Cyanuric chloride (1) (100.0 g, 0.54 mol) and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA, 153.5 g, 1.19 mol, 2.2 equiv.) were dissolved in acetone (500 mL). A solution of morpholine (94.3 g, 1.08 mol, 2.0 equiv.) in acetone (100 mL) was added dropwise to the reaction mixture over 30 min at 25 °C. After complete addition, the mixture was stirred at the same temperature for an additional 2 h. Upon completion, the mixture was cooled to 0 °C, and an aqueous HCl solution (10 wt%, 450 mL, pre-cooled to 0 °C) was added slowly. The resulting slurry was stirred at 0 °C for 1 h and then filtered. The collected precipitate was washed sequentially with water and methanol, and dried in vacuo to afford 4,4′-(6-chloro-1,3,5-triazine-2,4-diyl)dimorpholine (2) as a white solid (138.7 g, 89.9% yield) (entry 1) (detailed experimental procedures are described in SI, Section 2.1). When triethylamine (TEA) or pyridine was employed as the base instead of DIPEA, the product was obtained in reduced yields of 79.2% and 88.5%, respectively (entries 2–3). Variation of the base loading revealed that increasing the amount of DIPEA to 2.5–4.0 equivalents did not improve the yield (86.9–89.8%, entries 5–7), whereas reducing the base to 2.0 equivalents resulted in a slightly diminished yield of 86.8% (entry 4). To suppress the formation of the trisubstituted byproduct 2,4,6-trimorpholino-1,3,5-triazine, the stoichiometry of morpholine was strictly controlled. Increasing the morpholine loading to 2.1–2.15 equivalents led to decreased yields (87.2% and 82.7%) and reduced product purity (entries 8–9), indicating that 2.0 equivalents of morpholine is optimal for this transformation.

The cross-coupling of 4,4′–(6-chloro-1,3,5-triazine-2,4-diyl)dimorpholine (2) with (4-(bis(trimethylsilyl)amino)phenyl)magnesium bromide (3) was conducted under iron catalysis to prepare 4-(4,6-dimorpholino-1,3,5-triazin-2-yl)aniline (4) (Table 2). A freshly prepared solution of Grignard reagent (0.6 mol L−1 (4-(bis(trimethylsilyl)amino)phenyl)magnesium bromide (3) in THF) was utilized. To a suspension of anhydrous FeCl3 (57 mg, 0.35 mmol, 0.10 mol%, ≥98% purity) in dry THF (1000 mL) under nitrogen atmosphere was added compound 2 (100.0 g, 0.35 mol). The resulting mixture was cooled to 0 °C, and the Grignard solution (700 mL, 1.2 equiv.) was added over 1 h. The reaction mixture was gradually warmed to 25 °C and stirred for an additional 2 h. After complete consumption of the starting material, the reaction was quenched by the careful addition of water (500 mL) at 0 °C. The organic phase was collected and washed with brine (3 × 1000 mL), followed by treatment with 10% HCl (1000 mL) at room temperature for 1 h. The layers were separated, and the organic extract was concentrated under reduced pressure. Compound 4 was crystallized by the crude residue in EtOH/H2O as an off-white solid (103.2 g, 86.1% yield) (detailed experimental procedures and the synthetic access to 3 are described in SI, Section 2.2). The bis(trimethylsilyl) protecting groups were strategically incorporated in 3 to prevent undesired side reactions involving the aniline moiety under the coupling conditions, and these protecting groups were subsequently removed under acidic workup. In contrast, alternative protecting groups such as benzyloxycarbonyl (Cbz) and tert-butoxycarbonyl (Boc) either suffered from low deprotection efficiency or required overly complicated procedures. Evaluation of alternative iron sources revealed that both Fe(acac)3 (≥98% purity) and Fe(NO3)3 (≥98% purity) provided inferior results, delivering 4 in 83.2% and 80.5% isolated yields, respectively (entries 2–3). The effect of iron catalyst purity on the Kumada coupling reaction was considered. FeCl3 in 98% purity and FeCl3 in 92% purity afforded the product in 85.8% and 83.5%, respectively (entries 4–5), indicating that the iron content was the primary determinant. Catalyst loading was next examined: reducing the catalyst amount to 0.05 mol% diminished the yield to 76.8% (entry 6), whereas increasing the loading beyond 0.10 mol% (0.15–0.20 mol%) failed to enhance the efficiency (entries 7–8). Thus, 0.10 mol% FeCl3 was identified as optimal. Owing to the thermal sensitivity of aryl Grignard reagents, a slight excess of 3 proved necessary for complete conversion. Employment of exactly 1.0 equivalent of 3 resulted in a substantially lower yield of 72.2% (entry 9), while increasing the amount to 1.4 equivalents did not improve the outcome (entry 10). Consequently, 1.2 equivalents of Grignard reagent were selected for the standard procedure.

Continuous flow synthesis and microreactors offer significant advantages over traditional batch processes, including enhanced heat and mass transfer, leading to improved reaction safety and selectivity. These systems facilitate process intensification and easy scalability. Following the success of batch synthesis, continuous flow synthesis for the Kumada coupling was investigated (Fig. 3). To a suspension of anhydrous FeCl3 (57 mg, 0.35 mmol, 0.10 mol%, ≥98% purity) in dry THF (1000 mL) under nitrogen atmosphere was added compound 2 (100.0 g, 0.35 mol). The solution was pumped at a flow rate of 10.0 mL min−1 combined with the Grignard solution (700 mL, 1.2 equiv., flow rate 7.0 mL min−1) through a microreactor (ID = 1.0 mm, and volume = 60 mL) maintained at 25 °C. 500 mL Ice water was added to the mixture collected at the outlet, and the solution was allowed to stir for 1 h. The layers were separated, and the organic phase was washed with brine (3 × 1000 mL), followed by treatment with 10% HCl (1000 mL) at room temperature for 1 h. The layers were separated, and the organic extract was concentrated under reduced pressure. Compound 4 was crystallized by the crude residue in EtOH/H2O as an off-white solid (105.6 g, 88.0% yield).

The final stage of the synthetic sequence involved the coupling of aniline derivative 4 with in situ generated isocyanate 5′ to furnish the urea-containing target molecule Gedatolisib (Table 3). To a stirred solution of (4-aminophenyl)(4-(dimethylamino)piperidin-1-yl)methanone (5, 74.2 g, 0.30 mol) and triethylamine (60.7 g, 0.60 mol, 2.0 equiv.) in anhydrous THF (200 mL) was added triphosgene (BTC, 29.7 g, 0.10 mol, 0.33 equiv.) in THF (50 mL) dropwise over 30 min, maintaining the internal temperature below 5 °C. Following complete addition, the reaction mixture was stirred at 0 °C for 1 h to generate isocyanate intermediate 5′. Subsequently, a solution of compound 4 (102.7 g, 0.30 mol) in THF (200 mL) was introduced, followed by 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, 1.1 g, 9 mmol, 3 mol%). The resulting mixture was allowed to warm to ambient temperature and stirred for 3 h. The precipitated solid was collected by filtration, washed with deionized water and THF, and dried to afford crude Gedatolisib. Recrystallization from EtOAc/EtOH furnished the purified product as a white solid (159.3 g, 86.2% yield) after drying under reduced pressure (detailed experimental procedures are described in SI, Section 2.3). Replacement of TEA with DIPEA resulted in diminished efficiency, affording Gedatolisib in only 80.1% yield (entry 2). 2.0 Equivalents of base were considered to be enough for this transformation, as reduced quantities (1.2 and 1.5 equiv.) led to inferior yields of 70.1% and 77.8%, respectively (entries 3–4) and excess base (2.5 equiv.) provided no improvement (85.9%, entry 5). The stoichiometry of BTC proved critical for complete conversion to isocyanate 5′. Deviation from 0.33 equivalents, whether to 0.30 or 0.35 equivalents, resulted in slightly reduced yields of 80.8% and 84.8%, respectively (entries 6–7), confirming the necessity of precise control over this parameter. Employment of increased amount of 5 (1.1–1.3 equiv.) failed to enhance the reaction outcome significantly (86.6–86.9%, entries 8–10), indicating that 1.0 equivalent was enough for this conversion. Additionally, a catalytic amount of DMAP facilitated the nucleophilic addition between compound 4 and compound 5′. It was found that 3 mol% of DMAP were sufficient for efficient conversion. Lower loadings resulted in a slight decrease in yield, whereas higher loadings complicated the purification process without further improving the reaction outcome (entries 11–12).

Upon establishing the optimal reaction conditions, the total synthesis was carried out on a 100 gram scale starting from 100.0 g cyanuric chloride (1), and it afforded 222.0 g of Gedatolisib in 66% yield with 98.9% purity. Detailed experimental procedures are described in SI (Section 2.4).

In summary, a concise and practical synthesis of Gedatolisib has been developed with optimized reaction conditions. A pivotal advancement is the implementation of a continuous-flow iron-catalyzed Kumada coupling, which not only replaces the costly palladium-catalyzed Suzuki coupling to construct the key aniline intermediate, but also significantly enhances heat and mass transfer, improving reaction safety and reproducibility compared to conventional batch processes. This strategy eliminates the need for noble metal catalysts and associated removal steps, while avoiding hazardous reagents such as sodium hydride (NaH) and toxic 2,2′-dichlorodiethyl ether. The final steps also exclude high-boiling solvents like DMF, reducing concerns over genotoxic impurities. The new route achieves a higher total yield (66% over three steps), excellent purity (98.9%), and lower material costs under milder conditions. The continuous-flow setup facilitates process intensification and seamless scalability, and together with simplified work-up and crystallization-based purification, renders the process more environmentally friendly and well-suited for large-scale industrial production.
