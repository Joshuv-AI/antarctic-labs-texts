Navigating workforce challenges and strategies for sustainable mental health inpatient services—perspectives from Victoria’s public mental health sector
Authors: Christina Teo, Lisa Brophy, Fiona Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis article explores the critical challenges faced by the mental health workforce in Victoria, Australia’s inpatient services, and outlines strategies for workforce planning and development essential to sustaining inpatient mental health care. By analysing submissions to the Royal Commission into Victoria’s Mental Health System (RCVMHS), the article highlights the barriers and enablers identified by publicly funded mental health services. It underscores the urgent need for comprehensive reform in mental health services, emphasising that addressing workforce issues is fundamental to achieving these reforms. Without a well-supported and adequately resourced workforce, efforts to improve mental health care delivery will remain insufficient and unsustainable.MethodsA comprehensive review and analysis of eighteen submissions from designated Area Mental Health and Wellbeing Services (AMHWS) to the Royal Commission on the current condition of their inpatient units (IPUs) was conducted. These submissions served as the primary sources of data, providing detailed information on the challenges and proposed enablers affecting the delivery of mental healthcare within these inpatient facilities.ResultsThe findings identify major themes such as insufficient training, and challenges related to recruitment and retention are contributing to workforce shortages. These factors negatively impact occupational safety and contribute to staff burnout, which is further compounded by limited prospects for career advancement and an aging workforce.Potential solutions proposed by health services include designing attractive recruitment and retention strategies, the assurance of workforce safety, and the establishment of staff training and development initiatives to mitigate identified challenges. These measures necessitate a cohesive strategy, supported by comprehensive statewide workforce planning. Through the implementation of such strategies, mental health services can cultivate a resilient and enduring workforce capable of meeting the evolving needs of consumers.ConclusionThe challenges identified in this article are not unique to the state of Victoria, Australia. Rather, these issues are pervasive in IPUs across mental health systems worldwide. Recognising the nature of these pervasive challenges within acute inpatient settings is crucial for developing effective strategies and interventions to address them globally.Keywords: Mental health, Inpatient services, Workforce planning, Workforce development, Victoria, Australia.

Background

The increasing strain on global mental health systems is a pressing concern. Data from the World Health Organization [1] indicates that while one in eight individuals globally experiences a mental health condition, an average of less than 2% of national health budgets is dedicated to mental health services. This under-resourcing occurs despite mental disorders constituting a major public health issue affecting a substantial portion of the worldwide population (World Health Organization, [1]). Mental disorders represent 7% of the global burden of disease measured in disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) and 19% of all years lived with disability [2]. In Australia, the National Study of Mental Health and Wellbeing reports high prevalence rates, with over two in five Australians experiencing a mental disorder in their lifetime (Australian Bureau of Statistics, [3]). Australia’s mental health system is a shared responsibility between federal and state governments, with each state and territory delivering public mental health services through a mix of community-based and hospital-based care (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, [4]). Acute Inpatient Units (IPUs) play a pivotal role in providing essential mental health interventions to individuals in crisis ([5]; Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, [4]). Mental health inpatient services provide care for vulnerable individuals seeking treatment, care and support, whether their admission is voluntary or involuntary [6]. A significant challenge in inpatient mental health care is to create an inclusive and recovery-oriented environment conducive to consumer needs, safety, and a supportive working atmosphere for staff (Pelto-Piri et al., [7]). There is general agreement about the need for reform to meet this challenge.

These issues are not unique to Australia. Comparable jurisdictions such as the United Kingdom, Canada, and New Zealand are facing similar challenges in their inpatient mental health services. For example, the UK’s National Health Service has reported significant staffing shortfalls and increased use of temporary or agency staff across psychiatric inpatient settings (NHS England, [8]). In Canada, concerns over burnout, retention, and violence in inpatient mental health units have prompted system-wide workforce strategy reviews (Mental Health Commission of Canada, [9]). Likewise, New Zealand’s mental health inquiries have pointed to chronic under-resourcing and unsafe working conditions in inpatient environments, leading to major reform initiatives (New Zealand Government Inquiry into Mental Health and Addiction, [10]).

The effectiveness of mental health care delivery relies on the capacity and resilience of the workforce (Department of Health and Aged Care, [11]), which faces significant challenges, notably within inpatient settings. The prevalence of violence and high levels of acuity within IPUs, compounded by the use of restrictive practices such as restraint and seclusion, have detrimental implications for consumer recovery, staff well-being, and organizational dynamics [12].

Despite the initiatives outlined in Victoria’s mental health and wellbeing workforce strategy from 2021 to 2024, workforce shortages are expected to worsen due to increasing demand and uneven distribution, exacerbating existing challenges worsened by an ageing workforce (State Government of Victoria, [13]). Staff turnover and shortages delay the implementation of the reform agenda. A stable and capable workforce is needed to increase the capacity and accessibility of mental health services. Reform requires improved access to recovery-oriented services, including acute inpatient units to meet current and future demands. IPUs need innovation and reform that addresses common, major concerns about the nature and quality of care [14].

This article examines the workforce challenges faced by mental health inpatient services in Victoria, Australia, through submissions to the Royal Commission into Victoria’s Mental Health System (RCVMHS). A royal commission is an independent investigative body established by the Australian government to inquire into matters of significant public concern. It aims to address systemic deficiencies and drive transformative change (Royal Commissions, n.d. [15]). Established in February 2019, the RCVMHS was a response to long-standing concerns about the fragmentation, inaccessibility, and under-resourcing of mental health services across the state [16]. Decades of systemic underinvestment, growing demand, and the over-reliance on crisis-driven care had led to widespread public dissatisfaction and calls for structural reform. This was further amplified by advocacy from people with lived experience, clinicians, and peak bodies (State Government of Victoria, [17]).

The Commission was chaired by Penny Armytage AM, with Commissioners Professor Allan Fels AO, Professor Bernadette McSherry, and Dr Alex Cockram, bringing together expertise in justice, economics, psychiatry, and lived experience. The RCVMHS was tasked with delivering a comprehensive blueprint for systemic reform. The RCVMHS aimed to address significant systemic deficiencies in mental health care, as recognized by the Victorian Government. By analysing these submissions, this study highlights the challenges and proposed solutions for the workforce within public mental health services. The Royal Commission highlighted critical challenges in mental health settings, such as capacity imbalances, safety concerns, and gaps in therapeutic care, urging a shift towards patient-centred models and substantial investment (Department of Health, [18]). The Commission identified that acute inpatient care was operating under significant pressure, often characterised by overcrowding, unsafe environments, and high staff turnover. It found that inpatient units were often a last resort due to gaps in community-based care and that the workforce was experiencing high levels of burnout and distress (State Government of Victoria, [17]). The interim report recommended that the Victorian government fund 170 additional youth and adult acute mental health beds designed with contemporary standards and codesigned with lived experience input [19]. It also recommended assertive outreach for acute care in home or community settings as alternatives to inpatient beds.

While the Royal Commission into Victoria’s Mental Health System made 65 recommendations to reform the system, only one (Recommendation 58) specifically addressed workforce planning. The final report acknowledged general workforce challenges but did not explore in depth the unique and persistent workforce issues faced by IPUs. This study builds upon the Commission’s findings by conducting a focused thematic analysis of public submissions from designated mental health services, with the aim of identifying and synthesising the specific workforce challenges and sustainability strategies relevant to IPU settings (State Government of Victoria, [17]).

While community-based services, prevention, and early intervention are vital for a holistic mental health system, IPUs are indispensable for addressing acute crises that cannot be managed elsewhere. These units serve as critical safety nets for the most vulnerable individuals, offering intensive care during periods of severe mental distress.

By prioritizing reforms in IPUs, we can ensure that these essential services are equipped to meet current demands, support recovery-oriented care, and improve outcomes for both patients and staff. Ignoring the specific needs of IPUs risks undermining the overall effectiveness of the mental health system, as these units are often the last line of defence in preventing further deterioration of mental health conditions. Therefore, a balanced approach that strengthens IPUs alongside community services is essential for a resilient and responsive mental health care system.

Methods

The Royal Commission into Victoria’s Mental Health System undertook one of the most extensive public engagement processes in the state’s history, designed to center lived experience and ensure inclusive participation. Over 3,200 public submissions were received, including from consumers, carers, clinicians, and organisations. The Commission held 65 community consultations across metropolitan and regional areas, 12 weeks of public hearings featuring 99 witnesses, and 19 expert advisory workshops (State Government of Victoria, [17]). A comprehensive review of public submissions from designated AMHWS to the Royal Commission on the state of their IPUs was conducted as the primary source of data for this study. This document analysis was chosen to provide a detailed examination of the challenges and proposed solutions impacting mental healthcare delivery within inpatient facilities.

Document analysis involves systematically reviewing and evaluating documents to extract meaningful information and gain insights into a specific issue. By analysing these submissions, which serve as rich, first-hand accounts of those directly involved in the mental health system, this study aims to identify key themes, patterns, and trends related to workforce barriers and enablers in Victoria’s acute mental health IPUs. This approach ensures that the findings are grounded in real-world experiences and perspectives, offering a comprehensive understanding of systemic issues and potential solutions. The methods employed for data collection and analysis are outlined below:

Identification of relevant reports

The Commission issued a media release on 17 April 2019 for submissions until 15 July 2019. The call for submissions was open for 11 weeks and the Commission received 3,267 submissions. A comprehensive search was conducted to identify and select relevant reports submitted to the Royal Commission by designated mental health services in Victoria. Eighteen publicly funded designated mental health and wellbeing services in Victoria Australia provided submissions to the Royal Commission totalling 541 pages as illustrated in Table 1. The purpose of this table is not to critique or compare services based on the length or format of their submissions, but rather to demonstrate the extent and diversity of engagement across the sector. All submissions were publicly available through the official archived website of the Royal Commission. In line with its stated terms of engagement, these resources are free for public use. It is acknowledged that the quality and relevance of contributions are not determined by their length as even brief submissions may provide significant insights into the challenges and priorities of individual services.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study centres on identifying overarching patterns and trends related to barriers and enablers within an inpatient setting. However, owing to its broad scope, this article addresses only workforce-related matters among Victoria’s acute mental health IPUs. Workforce issues were chosen because they are crucial for ensuring effective and sustainable mental health care delivery, impacting all other aspects of inpatient care. All health service submission reports were from health services that had acute adult mental health IPUs. Specialist forensic, aged and child and adolescent services were excluded.

Data extraction

Pertinent data, including key findings, recommendations, and descriptions of workforce conditions with publicly funded acute mental health inpatient care, were systematically extracted from the selected submissions. The data were organized and coded for analysis via NVivo. The data extracted from the selected reports were subjected to qualitative content analysis. This process involved the following steps:

Data analysis

A general inductive approach was used in the data analysis.

i.Thematic coding:

Thematic coding:

An initial round of open coding was conducted to identify recurring themes and concepts within an inpatient setting from the submission reports. Key themes such as increasing demand, quality and safety of care, occupational violence, workforce issues, lack of trauma-informed and continuity of care were identified. The codes were reviewed, and the coding framework was then jointly developed with input from the research supervisors (LB, FS) who also assisted in refining key themes in workforce barriers and enablers related to mental health inpatient care.

ii.Categorization:

Categorization:

Codes were grouped into categories and subcategories to facilitate a structured analysis of the data. Barriers and enablers were organized into distinct categories to provide clarity and coherence in reporting the findings.

iii.Synthesis:

Synthesis:

Thematic categories were synthesized to identify overarching patterns and trends concerning barriers and enablers within an inpatient setting. Given the extensive scope of these findings, this article will specifically concentrate on constructs related to challenges and propositions for fostering an effective and sustainable workforce within acute mental health IPUs in Victoria.

Ethical considerations

All data used in this review were obtained from publicly accessible submissions and reports available through the official website of the Royal Commission into Victoria’s Mental Health System. As this study involved analysis of documents already in the public domain and did not include any personal, confidential, or identifiable information, formal ethical approval was not required. Ethical considerations related to data privacy and confidentiality were therefore not applicable in this context.

The documentation analysis identified the following key themes in relation to IPUs and workforce:

Workforce training

Most mental health services identify workforce training as a key issue. This included deficiencies in undergraduate education and limited opportunities for meaningful clinical experience prior to graduation.

The submissions highlighted that new graduates and junior staff often lack specific skills and experience relevant to the mental health sector owing to the structure of results in staff who are not ‘job ready,’ requiring substantial support and supervision from clinicians, who may themselves lack the necessary experience.

Moreover, in the field of nursing and allied health, there is a lack of specialist qualifications required for mental health work, with no regulatory requirement for these practitioners to hold specialist credentials. The absence of comprehensive training in undergraduate nursing degrees further diminishes the pool of mental health-trained clinicians and reduces the capacity of general hospital staff to address mental health needs effectively.

Since 1993 when the Nursing syllabus was changed, there has been a lack of undergraduate mental health specific training in either the enrolled or registered nursing pathway. Nurses are expected to study the general ‘comprehensive’ nursing degree, with a specialist mental health nursing post-graduate master’s degree being available. This diminishes the pool of nurses who may choose a career in mental health (South West Healthcare, [20], p. 22). Allied health undergraduate degrees are also generalist in nature and do not adequately equip new clinicians to work in clinical mental health specialist services. (South West Healthcare, [20], p. 27)

Workforce recruitment and retention

A common problem is the multifaceted challenge in recruiting and retaining a trained and competent mental health workforce. A key issue identified by services is the inadequacy of university programs in producing clinicians suitable for caring for consumers within the public mental health system. This is compounded by the growing opportunities in the Commonwealth-funded primary sector, creating competition for experienced clinicians.

Negative perceptions about working in mental health, stemming from undergraduate training, further hinder recruitment efforts, making attracting individuals with the required skill set and capabilities challenging.

The mental health sector suffers generally from negative perceptions about what it is like to work in the area. This extends back to undergraduate training for students and makes it difficult to recruit capable people with the right skill set and capabilities. (Alfred Health 02,[23], p. 10)

The mental health sector suffers generally from negative perceptions about what it is like to work in the area. This extends back to undergraduate training for students and makes it difficult to recruit capable people with the right skill set and capabilities. (Alfred Health 02,[23], p. 10)

This difficulty spans across all professional disciplines within the mental health workforce, including psychiatrists. Notably, many rural services have reported challenges with accreditation concerning local training and development programs. Persistent workforce shortages across clinical disciplines necessitate staged openings of new mental health beds owing to the insufficient availability of staff.

Additionally, the mental health services described face constraints in attracting and retaining a skilled workforce, relying heavily on overseas-trained clinicians, especially in nursing and junior medical positions. The recruitment process for specialist international medical graduates is labour-intensive, with delays in registration and immigration processes causing gaps in medical coverage. Despite these challenges, they play a crucial role in the workforce, and efforts should be made to support them through exams and assessments. Successful recruitment may be futile if retention is not addressed and several strategies aimed at enhancing workforce retention were proposed. For example:

Mechanisms to attract and retain more staff include: more training and development; limiting psychological exposure to risk and trauma; employee forums; giving managers more authority to facilitate greater problem-solving and team cohesion through shared purpose and increases in staff numbers, particularly allied health (Monash Health, [24], p. 48)

Workforce career development and progression

Health services expressed concerns to the Commission about the absence of clear career paths for individuals entering the field, noting that leadership positions historically favour those with medical and nursing backgrounds, thereby diverting experienced personnel away from direct care roles. This lack of defined career pathways, compounded by limited opportunities for research and publication, creates incentives for staff to seek prospects in more high-profile services outside the mental health sector.

With direct care not viewed or remunerated as a rewarding career path, the result is a junior, unskilled, and disempowered frontline workforce. (Alfred Health 02,[23], p. 10)

With direct care not viewed or remunerated as a rewarding career path, the result is a junior, unskilled, and disempowered frontline workforce. (Alfred Health 02,[23], p. 10)

Submissions stressed the need for improved career pathways and opportunities tailored to mental health practitioners in rural communities, where access to professional development and advancement opportunities is often constrained. Suggestions were made to enhance career development by increasing access to professional development opportunities closer to rural mental health services.

Furthermore, there was also a call for advancing leadership and management skills among nursing, allied health, and medical professionals, emphasizing the importance of multidisciplinary involvement in decision-making processes concerning both professional work and broader health policy. Engaging clinical staff in change management and equipping them with skills in this area were highlighted by South West Healthcare as essential for informing decision-making processes and driving positive changes within the mental health sector.

Workforce safety and burnout

The submissions suggest that there has been an exponential surge in demand for mental health services that has outpaced the growth of the workforce, creating a glaring disparity between the need for care and the capacity to provide it. Critical shortages of mental health professionals across disciplines were described, which were linked to difficulties in providing timely access to services and the quality and comprehensiveness of care delivered were compromised. Moreover, “burnout” was frequently referred to, with rates among the mental health workforce being described as alarmingly high, further depleting already strained personnel and compromising the continuity and efficacy of care.

Staff exposed to extreme workloads and the risk of occupational violence are traumatised and disenfranchised, which contributes to a poor work culture. The lack of available time creates an inability to participate in workforce support programs, such as training, reflective practice and professional development. There is a developing pattern of more junior staff managing the most complex high-risk situations as senior staff enter specialist consultant or managerial roles (often to escape growing work demands, deteriorating work conditions and burnout). Investment is required to support all disciplines in this area (North Western Mental Health,[22], p. 45).

Inadequate training, limited resources for professional development, and disparities in workforce distribution further compound the workforce crisis, perpetuating cycles of disadvantage and inequity in mental health care delivery. Thus, while other challenges undoubtedly warrant attention, the workforce crisis stands as the key issue, necessitating urgent and concerted efforts to ensure the sustainability, efficacy, and equity of mental health services.

The increased acuity of consumers, stemming from limited bed capacity and inadequate early intervention and community treatment, has led to what was described in the submissions as “a surge in occupational violence and aggression (OVA) within inpatient settings.”

Increasing levels of violence, especially in inpatient settings, exacerbated by poor early access to services, poor access to intensive supports, and poor infrastructure planning, are significant contributors to poor staff retention. Lived experience workforce

The document analysis revealed that St Vincent’s Hospital has prioritized input regarding the lived experience workforce in addressing workforce issues. Their submissions highlighted the emerging landscape of this workforce within mental health services, focusing on both the consumer workforce and the family/carer workforce. Carer peer support, among its various benefits, provides individuals the opportunity to share their stories with someone who comprehends their experiences firsthand.

However, despite the increasing recognition of its significance by clinical staff and management through the submissions, there is a notable lack of lived experience input in critical areas such as training, program codesign, and evaluation. These observations and recommendations underscore the need for greater inclusion of lived experience perspectives to enhance the effectiveness and sustainability of mental health services.

Services need support to train clinical staff in how to work effectively alongside peer workers, while peer workers need consistent access to training including, but not limited to, Intentional Peer Support. Services must be supported to ensure there are adequate structures in place in relation to individual peer supervision, governance/management and career advancement structures inclusive of senior roles. (St Vincent’s Hospital Melbourne, [27], p. 15)

Rural workforce

The mental health services also highlighted intricate challenges in rural mental health services around recruiting and retaining qualified professionals. Across regions, difficulties persist in attracting psychiatrists and other clinicians due to the complex health needs of rural populations. The scarcity of experienced professionals is exacerbated by factors such as distance from major cities and lack of extended family support.

The regional workforce is also impacted by professional isolation and limited access to quality professional development opportunities. The added costs of transport and travel, and the lesser availability of discretionary funds at the agency level, result in a greater risk that staff are not able to access professional networks and therefore it is extremely difficult to retain them in rural mental health services. The experience of such professional isolation may impact morale and negative community perceptions (South West Healthcare, [20], p. 23).

Inadequate workforce planning exacerbates the issue, resulting in significant shortfalls in meeting service demands. Significant incentives in the private sector often draw mental health professionals away from public practice, particularly in rural areas.

Despite placement programs and incentives, the concentration of tertiary health courses in metropolitan areas delays replacement and sometimes leads to the recruitment of less experienced clinicians in rural settings. Extensive orientation and mental health competency training for staff without clinical experience prolong understaffing, necessitating caseload reallocation and potentially compromising service provision.

Moreover, the uneven distribution of the mental health workforce, mainly in metropolitan areas, exacerbates disparities in mental health service provision, especially in rural and remote regions. These findings emphasize the urgent need for comprehensive strategies to address rural workforce shortages and ensure equitable access to mental health services across diverse geographic areas.

Ageing Workforce

Although only two mental health services explicitly identify the ageing workforce as a critical issue, the analysis indicates substantial challenges related to an ageing workforce and the impending retirement of experienced nurses. This trend is anticipated to have profound impacts, particularly considering the reduced length of stay and the high acuity levels in inpatient settings, thereby exacerbating the existing critical shortage of mental health nurses.

Furthermore, several rural and regional locations raised in their submissions the risk of their workforce retiring within 3 years of each other. Retirements have already commenced and are expected to continue over the next few years. Compounding this issue is the difficulty in attracting qualified staff to rural and regional areas, posing significant challenges for filling vacancies.

The retirements, particularly with inpatient nursing staff commenced at the end of 2018 and has and will steadily continue into the next 2–5 years. As experienced staff begin to retire, Southwest Hospital Mental Health Service will find it challenging to fill the vacancies because of the challenges of finding and attracting qualified staff to rural/regional areas. (South West Healthcare, [20], p. 27).

Discussion

This (novel) analysis of submissions to the RCVMHS identifies challenges related to the mental health workforce in acute IPUs. The themes identified in the document analysis are not unique to the State of Victoria, Australia. Rather, these issues are pervasive across mental health systems worldwide [28]. Understanding the nature of these challenges is crucial for developing effective strategies and interventions to address them globally.

Several of the potential solutions discussed were directly identified in the thematic analysis of submissions to the Royal Commission into Victoria’s Mental Health System. These insights emerged as practical responses proposed by inpatient services themselves to address workforce challenges. Other recommendations are drawn from the broader literature to complement and contextualise the themes emerging from the submissions. This distinction highlights the interplay between sector-identified needs and wider systemic reform strategies.

Workforce training and qualifications

The lack of adequate training and qualifications among mental health professionals is a widespread issue globally [29], significantly affecting the quality of care provided to consumers. This concern was echoed in our analysis of the RCVMHS submissions, where most public mental health inpatient services identified workforce training and capability as a critical challenge. Specifically, the submissions highlighted that new graduates and junior staff often enter the workforce without sufficient skills or experience tailored to the demands of acute mental health settings.

These findings reinforce broader concerns identified in the literature, including in countries such as the United Kingdom, where limited training pathways and inconsistent accreditation standards contribute to persistent capability gaps among mental health professionals [30]. Additionally, varying education standards and accreditation requirements further complicate efforts to ensure a competent and qualified workforce.

In Australia, the mental health workforce is predominantly composed of nurses. However, the current system, which relies on the Bachelor of Nursing award to produce mental health nurses, fails to encourage nurses to pursue careers in mental health nursing, making it an unlikely choice for nursing graduates [31]. Moreover, Australia is facing a crisis in the training of psychiatrists and other mental health professionals. Training opportunities for comprehensive psychiatry experiences are limited, with public adult mental health services increasingly focusing on specific clinical conditions, leaving out crucial aspects of psychiatric care. This narrow focus not only limits the exposure of trainees but also hinders the attractiveness and effectiveness of training programs, posing serious challenges for the future mental health workforce [32]. Allied health professionals usually complete generic degrees and without pathways into specialist mental health roles through advanced field education opportunities, graduate positions and the clear potential for leadership roles, are difficult to attract to public mental health service careers.

Thus, the reintroduction of specialist undergraduate and graduate programs tailored to nursing, occupational therapy, social work, and psychology students interested in mental health was suggested. This reintroduction could be incentivised through discounted fees or contractual agreements with public health institutions. The World Health Organisation [33] estimates a projected shortfall of 10 million health workers (mental health inclusive) by 2030. To address this global challenge of workforce shortages a potential solution is the development of new, shorter training pathways, which can expedite the transition from training to service delivery, thereby increasing workforce supply (Department of Health and Aged Care, [34]). Investments should also be made in staff training and development programs which are crucial for enhancing the skills and competencies of mental health professionals, ultimately improving patient care quality. These programs should prioritize evidence-based practices, trauma-informed care, and cultural competency [35].

Furthermore, initiatives such as scholarships, educational incentives, and partnerships with academic institutions can help attract and retain qualified professionals in the mental health field. Continuous professional development opportunities should also be provided to ensure that staff members stay updated on the latest evidence-based practices in mental health care [36].

Workforce recruitment and retention

Recruiting and retaining mental health professionals in IPUs in Victoria is challenging, mirroring national and international issues. Negative workplace cultures, characterized by high administrative burdens, stress, and burnout, deter entry and longevity in public mental health settings. Inadequate pay, career progression, and job stability further hinder attracting and retaining qualified staff (Department of Health and Aged Care, [34]). Similar issues are observed in the UK, exacerbated by the impacts of COVID-19 and post-Brexit impacts [37].

Workforce satisfaction and retention suffer from a lack of access to quality supervision, especially for occupational therapists, psychologists, and social workers, who struggle with generalist roles and superficial supervision [38]. Low wages, heavy workloads, limited career advancement, and insufficient support for staff well-being, contributing to burnout, exacerbate these challenges [39]. Recruitment is particularly difficult in rural and underserved areas due to competition from other sectors [40].

High turnover disrupts care, treatment, and support in IPUs, emphasizing the need to understand why clinicians leave to develop effective retention strategies [41]. Addressing these challenges requires competitive remuneration, improved working conditions, and comprehensive support programs [40]. Enhanced recruitment strategies, including outreach to underrepresented communities and partnerships with educational institutions, can diversify talent [42].

Efforts should also focus on improving the public perception of mental health professions and promoting career pathways (Department of Health, [43]). The attraction factors include a desire to help others, an interest in mental health and human behaviour, and the value of discipline-specific roles [44]. The State Government of Victoria [17] recommends targeted recruitment strategies, such as competitive salaries, career advancement opportunities, and flexible work arrangements, to retain skilled professionals in health services.

Workforce career development and progression

Limited opportunities for career development and progression were identified as significant barriers in our analysis. Several health services expressed concern about the absence of clear career pathways for individuals entering the mental health field. Specifically, they noted that leadership roles have historically favoured those from medical and nursing backgrounds, often at the expense of other disciplines. This imbalance was seen to divert experienced personnel away from direct care, contributing to frustration and workforce attrition. These findings align with international literature highlighting that a lack of structured progression pathways can negatively affect motivation, job satisfaction, and staff retention [45].

This lack of progression opportunities not only diminishes the appeal of the mental health sector but also undermines job satisfaction and retention. In particular, affected are professional groups like occupational therapists, psychologists, and social workers, who often find themselves employed in roles that underutilize their specialized skills due to the absence of consistent career structures (Department of Health and Aged Care, [34]).

Addressing these challenges necessitates a multifaceted approach, emphasizing the establishment of clear pathways, investment in training and development, promotion of interdisciplinary collaboration, and prioritization of supervision and mentorship [46]. Organizations must prioritize the creation of structured career development programs, specialized roles, and leadership positions, as well as support for further education and professional certifications [47, 48].

Tailored training opportunities can ensure that professionals are equipped to excel in their roles, enhancing job satisfaction and enabling the delivery of high-quality care [49]. Furthermore, recognizing excellence through performance incentives and career advancement opportunities fosters a culture of continuous learning and growth, incentivizing staff to contribute to organizational success [50].

Workforce safety and burnout

Workforce safety and burnout are critical challenges in mental health settings worldwide. Workplace violence, especially in mental health settings, is a significant issue that is often underreported and ignored [51]. Compared with those in other healthcare settings, mental health nurses experience higher rates of workplace aggression than those in other healthcare settings [52]. In the United States, workplace violence is prevalent in inpatient psychiatric settings, where staff frequently face violent or aggressive behaviour from consumers, leading to significant suffering [12, 53].

Occupational violence, including verbal abuse and physical assaults, has serious implications for both staff and patient outcomes [54]. High workloads, challenging situations, and inadequate support systems contribute to compassion fatigue, vicarious trauma, burnout, and stress among staff, posing significant safety risks and impacting retention rates [55–57].

Workplace violence in mental health facilities is prevalent, with verbal abuse being the most common (80%), followed by physical violence (34%) and bullying (30%). One-third of victims report psychological distress, with over half experiencing severe distress [58]. Higher violence rates are observed in wards with unclear staff roles, or a larger proportion of shifts covered by casual staff. Increased staff-to-patient ratios are correlated with more incidents of violence, underscoring the importance of staffing levels and experience [59].

Addressing these issues requires a comprehensive approach prioritizing workforce safety, high-quality patient care, and staff well-being. Essential strategies include staff training in de-escalation techniques like Safewards, conflict resolution, and self-care practices are crucial [60]. Implementing robust safety protocols, providing access to counselling services and trauma-informed care, and emphasizing areas such as risk management, training, and incident reporting are imperative [61].

Improving organizational culture, enhancing staff resilience, and implementing effective violence prevention and de-escalation strategies are crucial [62]. Ongoing training in trauma-informed care and self-care techniques, along with supportive work environments such as employee assistance programs and peer support networks, are essential for mitigating burnout and improving retention rates.

Lived experience workforce

The lived experience workforce has evolved from grassroots support and activism to the fastest-growing sector within mental health [63]. Recognized for their essential contributions, these roles are increasingly integrated into various settings to diversify supports for carers and consumers, improving outcomes (Department of Health and Aged Care, [34]).

Internationally and in Australia, the lived experience workforce has seen substantial growth, with a notable rise in designated roles within mental health services over recent decades (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, [64]; [65, 66]). In Australia, peer workers are increasingly integrated into multi and interdisciplinary teams, a critical strategy for enhancing service quality and relevance [67].

Lived experience workers form a vital component of mental health care. They offer valuable insights and empathy. (Australia Institute of Health and Welfare, [68]; Grundman, Edri & Stanger, [69]) but face unique challenges related to stigma, disclosure, and boundary management. Barriers such as stigma, discrimination, and lack of role definition often hinder meaningful participation [70, 71]. Addressing these issues requires a multifaceted approach, including de-stigmatization campaigns, peer support programs, and initiatives empowering individuals with lived experience to pursue careers in mental health (National Mental Health Commission, [72]).

A critical challenge is the lack of quality assurance in training and practice, compounded by limited access to supervision, raising concerns about service consistency ([73]; Queensland Mental Health Commission, [74]). As integration into mainstream mental health services, there is growing pressure for professionalization [75]. The establishment of a national professional organization for peer workers is proposed to protect the role authenticity and develop an effective workforce [67].

Reeves et al., [70] identify four key actions: education and training, organizational readiness, structural adjustments and resourcing, and commitment to peer integration and recovery practices. Organizations must support and train lived experience workers adequately and promote their participation in decision-making to create a supportive and inclusive environment (Department of Health and Human Services, [76]).

Rural workforce

Workforce challenges in rural and regional areas were a recurring theme in the submissions analysed for this study. Mental health services consistently highlighted the difficulty in recruiting and retaining qualified professionals, particularly psychiatrists and allied health clinicians, in regional locations. These findings reflect broader international patterns. Rural and remote regions worldwide report similar systemic barriers, including limited access to clinical supervision, fewer opportunities for professional development, and geographic and cultural isolation (WHO, 2020).

In these contexts, Indigenous mental health workers face additional cultural and structural challenges. For example, indigenous workers frequently report the tension of maintaining community connections while navigating mainstream mental health systems that may not align with their values or cultural practices [77].

In the Australian context, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Mental Health Workers are similarly affected. The need to travel off-country for employment or professional development opportunities can disrupt social and cultural responsibilities and sever vital links with family and community. Despite policy commitments, career progression and training opportunities remain predominantly metropolitan-centric, posing financial and cultural barriers to the participation and retention of Indigenous workers in the mental health system (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, [64]; Wagenaar et al., [78]).

To address rural workforce shortages, health services must employ targeted recruitment strategies, such as financial incentives, flexible work arrangements, local training programs, and telehealth technologies to extend service reach. Partnerships with community organizations and peer support networks can facilitate the establishment of sustainable mental health workforces in these regions, while collaborations between urban and rural organizations ensure equitable access to quality mental health care [79].

Despite some improvements, career progression opportunities remain predominantly metropolitan-based, posing financial and personal barriers to rural mental health workforce participation [80]. This disproportionately affects Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Mental Health Workers, who face additional challenges in travelling off-country for work, including maintaining community connections while away (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, [64]).

Ageing workforce

The ageing mental health workforce presents a challenge to maintaining and enhancing mental health services. In 2017, about 3 in 5 mental health nurses (58%) were aged 45 and over, and one-third (33%) were aged 55 and over in Australia (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [4]). Similar patterns are seen in psychiatry and psychology, where over half of psychologists and 70% of psychiatrists were aged 45 and over, with substantial portions aged 55 and over. This trend threatens the retention of highly skilled workers and complicates efforts to attract experienced professionals (Department of Health, [43]).

This ageing workforce trend is observed globally, raising concerns about skill shortages and loss of institutional knowledge [81]. While the experience of older workers is invaluable, there is a critical need to attract and retain younger professionals to ensure a sustainable workforce.

To address these challenges, health services must invest in succession planning and knowledge transfer initiatives. Mentorship programs pairing experienced staff with younger colleagues can facilitate expertise transfer and promote intergenerational learning. Flexible retirement options and incentives for delayed retirement can help retain experienced staff while creating opportunities for new talent. Additionally, continuous professional development and strategic succession planning are essential to mitigate the impact of retiring workers [82, 83].

National and statewide workforce planning National and statewide workforce planning is crucial in the contemporary socio-economic context, where factors such as population growth, demographic shifts, and rising mental health conditions emphasize the need for a coordinated and forward-thinking approach. It involves aligning educational institutions, policymakers, employers, and other stakeholders to cultivate a resilient and adaptable mental health workforce capable of addressing future challenges (Premier of Victoria, [84]).

The National Mental Health Workforce Strategy, developed by the Department of Health and Aged Care in November 2022, provides a high-level vision and roadmap for building a sustainable mental health workforce (Department of Health and Aged Care, [11]). This strategy outlines collaborative efforts between governments and the sector to attract, train, support, and retain a workforce capable of meeting the evolving demands of the mental health system.

Workforce planning should address not only mismatches and shortages through recruitment and retention but also consider diverse approaches to meet the workforce challenges (Productivity Commission, 2020). Collaboration between governments, educational institutions, and training bodies is essential to fund places, provide access to teaching and clinical placements, and transition from reactive to proactive service provision. Moreover, such planning must ensure a sufficient supply of staff across all healthcare disciplines to meet the specific needs of mental health service provisions (Department of Health and Aged Care, [34]).

Limitations

While there is a great deal to learn from this document analysis, there are some notable disadvantages that must be acknowledged. They are listed as follows:

Conducting a Victoria-focused documentation analysis, rather than a global one, introduces several limitations. First, the findings may lack generalizability beyond Victoria’s specific socio-political and economic context, limiting their applicability to regions with differing healthcare systems and workforce dynamics.

Additionally, Victoria’s unique legislative and funding structures within its mental health services may shape workforce issues and solutions in ways not replicable elsewhere, potentially overlooking innovative practices seen internationally. Moreover, a concentration on Victoria’s public mental health services may overlook the diversity of experiences and solutions present in private or non-governmental sectors, limiting the study’s scope and implications. Lastly, cultural factors specific to Victoria may influence workforce challenges and interventions differently than in other global contexts, reducing the study’s relevance to international audiences seeking similar insights in their own regions.

The use of secondary data and documentation analysis, as opposed to primary data collection through direct participant interviews, presents several limitations. Secondary data may suffer from incomplete or outdated information, failing to capture current developments or nuanced aspects of workforce challenges.

Moreover, the data may be originally collected for different purposes, leading to misalignment with research objectives. The data used in this study were derived from submissions to the RCVMHS, which invited services and stakeholders to provide input into a statewide review of the mental health system. The primary purpose of the RCVMHS was to examine the existing system its strengths, weaknesses, and challenges and to identify the root causes of systemic failures and barriers to timely, effective, and compassionate care. As such, the submissions analysed in this study were problem-focused, highlighting challenges and deficiencies rather than well-functioning aspects of inpatient units.

This approach also limits the depth of exploration into specific issues and restricts the ability to gather detailed, context-specific insights. Furthermore, secondary data analysis lacks the richness of information obtained through direct participant engagement, such as personal experiences and motivations. Biases inherent in the original data collection process may also influence the validity and reliability of findings.

However, despite these limitations, it is important to acknowledge that in the case of the RCVMHS, the extensive volume and diverse sources of secondary data provide robustness to the analysis. There were over 3,267 submissions, with more than 12,500 contributions from individuals and organizations. 99 witnesses were called to the stand at public hearings, and nearly 3,000 respondents completed the workforce survey. The breadth and depth of information collected offer a comprehensive understanding of the challenges in Victoria’s mental health system.

The submissions from the 17 publicly funded mental health services with a total of 573 pages provide a robust foundation for understanding workforce challenges in Australian inpatient settings. With a wealth of submissions, contributions, and survey responses, these data offer comprehensive insights that enhance the credibility and reliability of research outcomes, albeit within the context of Victoria’s mental health system.

This wealth of data allows for the triangulation of findings across multiple sources, enhancing the credibility and reliability of the research outcomes. Furthermore, the sheer volume of submissions and contributions reflects the active engagement and input from stakeholders directly involved in the mental health system, lending credibility to the insights derived from secondary data analysis. Therefore, while primary data collection through direct participant interviews may offer certain advantages, the extensive secondary data available from the Royal Commission provide a solid foundation for understanding and addressing workforce challenges in Australian inpatient settings.

Conclusion

Mental health workforce issues are not confined to the state of Victoria but are global challenges that affect hospitals and mental health IPUs worldwide. The RCVMHS provided the rare opportunity to seek out the collective wisdom of service providers in both describing the problems and hearing directly from them about how these challenges could be addressed. Amidst the myriad challenges confronting the mental health sector, the submissions indicated that the workforce crisis is the paramount issue in mental health IPUs in Victoria, eclipsing the other themes identified including high acuity, increasing demand, poor infrastructure, lack of trauma-informed care, and occupational violence. While each of these challenges has significant implications for mental health service delivery and outcomes in an acute inpatient setting, the workforce predicament underpins and exacerbates them all.

A strong theme that emerged from the submissions analysed in this study was the limited attention given to inpatient settings in the final recommendations of RCVMHS. Although it recommended expanding the number of inpatient beds across the state and a range of structural workforce reforms aimed at attracting, training and transitioning the staff needed for Victoria’s mental health services, developed a Mental Health and Wellbeing Workforce Strategy in 2021 which included addressing workforce supply, wellbeing and capability, as well as developing a regional and rural workforce (State Government of Victoria, 2021). While the Commission’s 65 recommendations comprehensively addressed structural reform, community mental health investment, and workforce development, many inpatient and workforce specific concerns raised by health services received comparatively less targeted response. This is notable given the inpatient unit is where the most acutely unwell consumers receive care and where workforce shortages, occupational safety concerns and sub-optimal therapeutic environments were repeatedly identified in submissions. As such, the lack of specific recommendations tailored to improving inpatient services represents, as highlighted by several submissions, a missed opportunity to strengthen a critical part of the mental health service continuum.

The challenges are complex and multifaceted, requiring comprehensive and coordinated strategies to address them effectively. By prioritizing recruitment and retention efforts, investing in staff training and development, genuinely respecting and supporting the contribution of staff in designated lived experience roles, promoting career advancement opportunities for all, and fostering a supportive work environment, organizations can build a sustainable workforce capable of delivering high-quality mental health care. Additionally, targeted initiatives tailored to the needs of rural and remote communities are crucial for ensuring equitable access to mental health services.
