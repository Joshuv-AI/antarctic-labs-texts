A network analysis of depression and anxiety symptoms among Chinese elderly with multiple chronic conditions: based on the 2017–2018 Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS)
Authors: Junqing Luo, Jinsong Deng, Wei Zhou, Leiyu Shi, Qiuli Zhu, Zixian Huang, Ruoyu Gui, Xiangang Cai, Guangli Hu, Gang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder adults with multiple chronic conditions represent a vulnerable group with health issues intricately intertwined, making them more susceptible to mental health issues such as depression and anxiety. To identify central and bridge symptoms to implement effective interventions, this study constructed a network model of depression and anxiety symptoms among older adults with MCCs, exploring the correlations among specific symptoms.MethodsThis study used the data from the 2018 Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS) to construct a network model for depression and anxiety symptoms among older people with MCCs. Depression and anxiety symptoms were assessed by using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale-10 (CESD-10) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale-7 (GAD-7). Network analysis was used to identify the central symptoms and bridge symptoms between the network of depression and anxiety symptoms. The network stability was examined by a case-dropping bootstrap procedure.ResultsNetwork analysis revealed that nodes GAD2 (Uncontrollable worry) and CESD3(Felt sadness) were the most primary symptoms and GAD1(Nervousness), CESD1 (Bother by things), CESD10 (Sleep quality), and GAD3 (Excessive worry) were bridge symptoms of the anxiety-depression network.ConclusionCentral symptoms and bridge symptoms played a critical role in the anxiety-depression symptoms network. Future research should explore intervention strategies for these symptoms to improve the mental health of older adults with MCCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04499-1.

Introduction

Currently, the psychological well-being of older adults has received significant attention, especially regarding the high prevalence of depression and anxiety among individuals suffering from multiple chronic conditions, MCCs [1].Multiple chronic conditions refer to a situation where a person suffers from two or more chronic diseases simultaneously. As China’s demographic landscape undergoes profound transformation with accelerating population aging and shifting disease patterns, an increasing proportion of older adults are afflicted with multiple chronic conditions [2]. In reality, due to the burden of illness, limitations in physical functioning, and the complexity of treatment regimens, older adults with MCCs are more likely to experience severe mental health problems, such as depression and anxiety [3]. The persistent burden of illness, the decline in independent living capacity, and concerns about disease progression contribute to a heightened vulnerability to despair among older adults. This psychological state represents a significant contributing factor to the development of depressive and anxiety disorders [4]. Studies indicate that older adults with MCCs exhibit a higher prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms compared to their healthier counterparts [5]. These emotional disorders not only diminish patients’ quality of life but may also contribute to the deterioration of physical health by compromising treatment compliance and self-management capacity, thereby exerting a profound impact on the overall well-being of elderly individuals with MCCs [6].

Many studies have examined the association between multimorbidity and the prevalence of depression and anxiety disorders [7]. Studies show that compared to healthy individuals, individuals with MCCs exhibit a significantly increased risk of developing depressive disorders [5]. Furthermore, the coexistence of multiple chronic conditions may function as a direct and effective predictive marker for identifying elderly populations at high risk for depression [8]. Many studies have also confirmed that the coexistence of multiple chronic diseases increases the risk of depression among the elderly [9, 10]. Anxiety is one of the main psychological challenges faced by elderly people with MCCs [11], often coexisting with depression [11]. Study indicates that individuals with MCCs frequently experience anxiety, which arises from factors including the fear of disease progression, decline in physical functioning, substantial treatment burdens, and financial strain [12]. The study conducted by Ivanny OY Sinaga et al. further demonstrates that disease burden is significantly associated with symptoms of anxiety and depression in older adults [13]. Furthermore, due to their restricted mobility, the complex interplay of multiple symptoms, and a general lack of mental health awareness, older adults with MCCs may face substantial barriers in accessing timely and effective healthcare services [14]. These factors collectively contribute to the decline in quality of life and the elevated mortality rates among older adults with MCCs [15]. Therefore, depression and anxiety in older adults with MCCs constitute a complex and urgent public health concern. Clarifying the interrelationship between these mental health conditions is critical for the development of effective preventive and intervention measures. For older adults with MCCs, the inherent somatic symptoms of chronic diseases, such as pain, fatigue, and sleep disturbances, may form a strongly interconnected symptom network with depression-related sadness and anxiety-related excessive worrying [16]. However, previous studies usually regarded depression or anxiety as a single “total score” or latent variable, without considering the relationship between specific depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms. Therefore, we chose to use network analysis (NA) to investigate the association between specific depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms in the elderly with MCCs.

Network analysis has recently been widely used in the field of psychosomatic disorders and serves as a tool for visualizing the relationships between complex variables, which helps to focus on the specific symptoms of mental disorders [17]. Nodes and edges are two core concepts in network analysis, representing specific symptoms and the correlation between two nodes, which constitute the network graph [18]. This method helps identify the most influential symptoms in the network by identifying central nodes that are highly connected to other nodes [19]. The symptoms that connect two mental disorders are called bridge symptoms [20]. In addition, bridge symptoms that increase the risk of symptom transfer from one disease to another were identified, providing targeted intervention points for clinical control [21].

For elderly individuals with multiple MCCs, due to the burden of physical illness, their depressive and anxiety symptoms are intertwined with physical symptoms, forming a distinctive characteristic that is markedly different from that of other populations. Therefore, we must pay close attention to the depressive and anxiety symptoms of the elderly with MCCs. However, previous studies on this topic often overlooked the specific manifestations of depression and anxiety disorders. To address this gap, this study identifies central and bridge symptoms and uses network analysis to investigate the relationship between depressive symptoms and anxious symptoms in Chinese elderly individuals with MCCs, thereby providing intervention targets for the mental health problems in this group.

Methods

Source of data and participants

This study drew upon data from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), a nationwide ageing study jointly administered by the Center for Healthy Aging and Development at Peking University and the Center for Aging and Development at Duke University. Initiated in 1998, the baseline wave randomly sampled 631 counties, county-level cities, and districts across 22 provinces, covering an aggregate population of 985 million—approximately 85.3% of China’s populace—and has since completed eight follow-up waves. CLHLS is the longest-running longitudinal survey of older adults in China and constitutes the largest scientific investigation globally that includes a substantial cohort of the oldest-old, particularly those aged 90 and above. The questionnaire comprehensively collected information on respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics, family background, economic circumstances, health status, morbidity profiles, and medical expenditures [22]. The CLHLS study received ethical approval from the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-13074), and written informed consent was obtained from each participant or their authorized representative. The survey was in accordance with the ethical standards delineated in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Participation in the CLHLS is voluntary, and respondents are free to withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences.

In this study, the elderly were defined as people over 65 years old. The inclusion criteria for this study are: (1) verified age ≥ 65, (2) suffering from two or more chronic diseases, and (3) complete data from all depression and anxiety programs. Participants who lacked the values or answers of “cannot answer”, “do not know” or “not applicable” in any of the scale items of interest were exclude. Finally, a total of 4,176 elderly people were included in this study(The complete flowchart of the selection process is shown in Figure S1.).

Measurement

Assessment of multiple chronic conditions

Participants were inquired: “Have you been diagnosed by a physician with any of the following conditions?” Accounting for the elevated prevalence and high comorbidity rates among older adults, this study incorporated 17 chronic conditions as response options, including cardiac disorders, stroke, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic pulmonary diseases (bronchitis, emphysema, asthma, pneumonia), tuberculosis, malignancies, dyslipidemia, arthritis, peptic ulcers, cholecystitis and cholelithiasis, Parkinson’s disease, rheumatism, chronic nephritis, hepatitis, dementia, and epilepsy. Respondents answered “1 = yes” or “0 = no” for each item. When two or more of these 17 chronic diseases coexist in the same individual, it is called multiple chronic conditions [23].

Assessment of depressive and anxiety symptoms

This study utilized the 10-item version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) to assess depression symptoms among older adults with MCCs [24]. The CESD-10 consists of ten items related to sleep disturbances, sadness, hopelessness, and depression affect. Its psychometric properties, including validity and reliability, have been well established within the Chinese population, making it a widely accepted tool for evaluating depression symptoms in this demographic. Scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores reflecting greater symptom severity. When their CESD-10 score was at least 10, older Chinese people showed adequate levels of sensitivity (0.85) and specificity (0.80), according to prior research [25]. So the current study employed an average CESD-10 score of at least 10 points to define depressive symptoms.

To assess anxiety symptoms in older adults with MCCs, this study employed the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7). The GAD-7 includes seven items that capture various manifestations of generalized anxiety [26]. Scores range from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. When a GAD-7 score was > 5, anxiety symptoms were identified [26]. Both the Chinese versions of the CESD-10 and GAD-7 have been rigorously validated, demonstrating strong reliability and validity. These scales are widely used in the evaluation of mental health disorders among the elderly population in China [27].

Statistical analysis

The network analysis was conducted using R (v4.3.1). Given that the CESD-10 and GAD-7 items used in this study are ordinal categorical variables based on Likert-type scales, we used a polychoric correlation matrix to handle the data and constructed a network model of depressive and anxiety symptoms using the Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM). To regularize the network estimation, we applied the Graphical LASSO algorithm, which helps minimize spurious or false positive edges [19]. We adopted the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC), using the EBICglasso algorithm with the hyperparameter γ set to 0.5 for edge selection [28].

Within these network models, 17 nodes represented distinct symptoms interconnected through edges. Edge thickness denoted the strength of associations between nodes [19], specifically with green edges indicating positive correlations and red edges signifying negative correlations between nodes. The ‘ggplot2’ package was implemented to optimize network visualization [29].

Knowing reporting standards for network analyses in cross-sectional data can improve reporting and transparency [30]. Node centrality was quantified through four metrics: strength, betweenness, closeness, and expected impact, each reflecting the significance of individual nodes within the network structure. Strength represents the sum of weighted connections of nodes and measures their importance in the network. Betweenness centrality measures the frequency at which a node appears on the shortest paths between other nodes in the network. Closeness centrality measures the average shortest path length from a symptom to all other symptoms [31]. Expected Impact (EI) refers to the total activating or inhibiting effect that a node has on other nodes in the network through all its connecting edges, reflecting the overall influence of that node in the network. We used the Expected Influence (EI) to identify the central symptoms and the Bridge Expected Influence (BEI) to identify the bridge symptoms because research has shown that it outperforms the commonly used “Strength” index when there is a negative correlation between nodes [32].

Furthermore, to ascertain network stability, the correlation stability coefficient (CS-C) was applied, representing the maximal proportion of dropouts in the original sample required to maintain a correlation of r = 0.7 between the original and subsample estimates at 95% probability. Theoretically, CS coefficients exceeding 0.25 indicate adequate network stability [19], with values surpassing 0.5 suggesting sufficient stability and values beyond 0.7 indicating high stability. Network precision, defined as edge weight accuracy, was assessed using non-parametric bootstrapping to estimate 95% confidence intervals for edge weights, with narrower intervals implying reduced volatility in the network estimates [19]. Additionally, bootstrap difference tests evaluated the stability of strength estimates and edge weights. Network structural stability was verified using the “bootnet” package (version 1.5). In the current study, 1,000 bootstrap iterations were used in all cases.

Results

Descriptive results

Thus, the prevalence of anxiety in our study is 11.5%.

The network structure of depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms

In the network model, there were 136 potential edges generated from 17 nodes, of which 94 edges (69.12%) had a non-zero-weight. The left panel of Fig. 1 shows the relationship network between depressive symptoms and anxiety symptoms among older adults with MCCs. Within the depression symptom network, the strongest association was identified between CESD1 (Bother by things) and CESD3 (Felt Sadness), followed by CESD5(Hopeful about future) and CESD7(Felt happy), as well as CESD8(Felt lonely) and CESD9(Could not get going). In the anxiety symptom network, the strongest association was detected between GAD1(Nervousness) and GAD2 (Uncontrollable worry), followed by GAD2 (Uncontrollable worry) and GAD3 (Excessive worry), along with GAD5(Restlessness) and GAD6(Irritability).

The right panel of Fig. 1 shows the ranking chart of the “EI”. EI measures t refers to the total activating or inhibiting effect that a node has on other nodes in the network through all its connecting edges, reflecting the overall influence of that node in the network. GAD2 (Uncontrollable worry) ( EI = 1.00) exhibited the highest strength centrality, followed by CESD3 (Felt Sadness) ( EI = 0.95 ), indicating that these symptoms have the strongest direct associations with other symptoms and may be the central symptoms in the network of depression and anxiety symptoms.

Bridge symptoms refer to the key node symptoms in the network of psychological symptoms that connect different symptom clusters or different disease domains. As shown in Fig. 2, among all the nodes in the network, the absolute value of the Bridge Expected Influence (BEI) of the GAD1 (nervousness) (the absolute value of BEI = 0.36) is the largest, followed by CESD1 (bothered by things) (the absolute value of BEI = 0.19), GAD3 (excessive worry) (the absolute value of BEI = 0.16) and CESD10 (sleep quality) (the absolute value of BEI = 0.15) ,indicating that these nodes play a crucial role in cross-community connections and serves as a key pathway between different symptom groups.

Network stability

Figure 3 shows the results of the case drop guidance test. The CS coefficient of centrality EI is 0.75, indicating that when 75% or less of the cases fall off, the order of the original network and the re-estimated centrality strength remains at 0.7. The CS coefficient of the BEI is 0.75, indicating that the BEI is stable enough. Fig.S2 shows the narrow guidance 95% CI of edge weights, indicating the reliable results of the network model. Fig.S3, S4 and S5 respectively present the non-parametric guided difference test results of edge weights, node expected influence and node bridge expected influence. Most edges and nodes, especially the strongest edges, central symptoms and bridge symptoms, are significantly different from other edges and nodes, indicating that the preliminary results are reliable.

Discussion

Our study aims to construct a network model of depression and anxiety symptoms in elderly people with MCCs, with the goal of identifying core symptoms and bridge symptoms to provide effective preventive and intervention measures. Our findings indicated CESD3 (Felt Sadness) and GAD2 (Uncontrollable worry) are the primary symptoms of the anxiety-depression network. Anxiety and depression are linked by the symptoms of GAD1(Nervousness), CESD1 (Bother by things), CESD10 (Sleep quality), and GAD3 (Excessive worry).

Sadness is an inherent response, encompassing cognitive process, emotional experiences, and behavioral manifestations, that individuals exhibit in the face of a bereavement [32]. Within this network analysis, CESD3 (Felt Sadness) is a central symptom within the depression-anxiety network relationship among older adults with MCCs. This might stem from their psychological responses to multiple blows such as loss of physical functions, chronic pain, and social isolation. Chronic diseases often involve loss of physical functions and decline in independent living abilities, and persistent physical pain reinforces and sustains feelings of sadness; the burden of the disease leads to restricted activities, increasing the risk of social isolation, and loneliness intensifies the feelings of sadness [33, 34]. Moreover, GAD2 (Uncontrollable worry) is another central symptom in the network structure, which refers to a cognitive process characterized by repetitive concerns or thoughts about potential negative events, dangers, or risks [35]. Compared with single chronic disease, multiple chronic conditions lead to more severe health consequences, greater economic expenditures and mental burdens [36]. Therefore, elderly people with MCCs have specific and persistent concerns about future deterioration of health and medical expenses. This concern becomes uncontrollable due to the complexity of disease management and the uncertainty of disease development, thus becoming the hub of the anxiety emotion network.

Bridge expected influence may provide guidance in the study for bridge symptoms that play an important role in the development and maintenance of psychiatric disorder comorbidity, and interventions targeting bridge symptoms may be effective in both disorders [20]. Our study identified GAD1(Nervousness), CESD1 (Bother by things), CESD10 (Sleep quality), and GAD3 (Excessive worry) as the most important bridge symptoms in this network model. Nervousness refers to an emotional and physiological state resulting from the anticipation of threats, challenges or uncertainties. In the depression-anxiety network, GAD1(Nervousness) emerged as a key bridge symptom, which is consistent with previous study. For instance, in study on Chinese elderly with disabilities, GAD1 (Nervousness) was identified as a pivotal bridge symptom in the depression-anxiety network [37]. Also, Study of Chinese older adults with hypertension have similarly highlighted GAD1(Nervousness)’s role as a bridge symptom between anxiety and depression comorbidities [38]. To our knowledge, older adults with MCCs often feel nervousness. The physical symptoms caused by multiple chronic diseases and the concerns about diseases can exacerbate their feelings of nervousness, which may eventually lead to depression, anxiety, and other psychological health issues [39]. In addition, both exploratory factor analysis studies and network analysis studies have discovered that nervousness is an important transdiagnostic symptom associated not only with anxiety but also with depression [40, 41]. Sleep quality refers to an individual’s subjective evaluation and objective measurement of their sleep experience. It encompasses how well sleep fulfills its essential functions of physical restoration, cognitive consolidation, and emotional regulation. To our knowledge, elderly people with MCCs often experience sleep problems [42]. The physical symptoms such as pain and breathing difficulties caused by multiple chronic diseases can reduce sleep quality and sleep disorders can impair the mechanisms of emotional regulation and stress response, perpetuate negative emotional cycles and potentially exacerbate or accelerate the symptoms of depression and anxiety disorders [43, 44]. Bother by things means that an individual, in their daily life, experiences excessive emotional reactions or persistent psychological burdens due to events resulting in mood swings, anxiety, or distraction. This is the psychological manifestation of elderly people with MCCs in terms of their daily disease management affairs. The elderly with MCCs need to take multiple medications every day [45], and the dosage and timing of each medication may vary. This causes some inconvenience for them to a certain extent. Besides, the treatment plans and daily management for multiple chronic diseases are complex [46], requiring high levels of planning, memory and execution skills. This is a severe challenge for the elderly whose cognitive functions may already be declining, and it is prone to cause “medication errors” or “treatment omissions”. Excessive worry refers to a persistent and excessive state of concern and anxiety. For elderly people with MCCs, their worries are specific, real and persistent: the diseases may continue to worsen, and they need long-term care and face high medical expenses [47]. In China, elderly individuals are reluctant to impose burdens on their children, which contributes to depression and anxiety [48].

In the bridge symptoms network identified in this study, GAD1(Nervousness) and CESD10 (Sleep quality), CESD1 (Bother by things) and GAD6(Irritability), and GAD3 (Excessive worry) and CESD3 (Felt Sadness) all showed the strongest connections. This result is highly consistent with the psychological characteristics of the elderly with multiple chronic diseases and is supported by relevant research evidence [49]. Among them, GAD1(Nervousness) is most closely associated with CESD10 (Sleep quality). The long-term stress caused by multiple chronic diseases keeps the elderly in a state of continuous nervousness and anxiety, activating the high arousal of the autonomic nervous system and interfering with sleep rhythms and maintenance [50]; lack of sleep further weakens the ability to regulate emotions and intensifies nervousness, forming a vicious cycle of " nervousness — sleep quality“ [51]. CESD1 (Bother by things) is strongly associated with GAD6(Irritability), and is closely related to the decline in psychological resilience and the accumulation of chronic stress in the elderly with multiple chronic conditions. The elderly are constantly troubled by various trivial matters such as fluctuations in their conditions and caregiving pressures. Continuous stress depletes cognitive resources, reduces emotional tolerance and impulse control abilities, and makes them prone to angry reactions [52]. Anger, in turn, will exacerbate inner distress and interpersonal conflicts. The strong connection between GAD3 (Excessive worry) and s CESD3 (Felt Sadness) reflects the interaction between negative cognition and depressive emotions. The health threats posed by multiple chronic diseases make the elderly prone to excessive worry about disease progression and life security [53]. This persistent negative cognition can trigger and exacerbate depressive symptoms such as sadness; sadness, in turn, reinforces negative expectations and amplifies worry, forming a vicious cycle [54]. Studies have shown that multiple chronic diseases are important risk factors for depression and anxiety in the elderly, and the co-occurrence of the two disorders is more prominent under the stress of chronic diseases [55].

The core symptoms and bridge symptoms identified in this study are of great significance for the intervention of depression and anxiety symptoms in elderly people with multiple chronic conditions. While considering these key symptoms, intervention measures should also be based on a comorbidity and cross-diagnostic perspective, considering the physiological characteristics of elderly people with multiple chronic diseases. Integrating the core techniques of CBT, BA, and MBSR [56, 57] therapies to form a simplified plan can improve the treatment compliance of elderly people with multiple chronic conditions. In addition, the participation of caregivers in collaborative care should combine psychological intervention behavior training with guidance on a healthy lifestyle for chronic diseases. These measures aim to improve the mental health outcomes and overall quality of life of this vulnerable group.

This study has two main advantages. First, we employed a standardized assessment tool - network analysis - to demonstrate the relationship network of multiple chronic disease-related depression and anxiety symptoms among the elderly individuals with MCCs, and to determine the core intervention targets. Second, the data sample derived from the CLHLS is large, representative, and stable. Although this study provides some new insights into the depression and anxiety symptoms of the elderly individuals with MCCs, several limitations remain. First, our definition of MCCs is based on the presence/absence of diseases and does not account for disease severity. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study precluded the establishment of a causal relationship between anxiety and depressive symptoms. Therefore, future longitudinal studies are needed to delve deeper into the dynamic interplay between these two conditions. Third, reliance on self-report scales introduces potential biases, including, recall inaccuracies and subjective interpretation variability. The absence of clinical diagnostic validation for anxiety and depression further limits the precision of symptom assessment.

Conclusion

This study highlights the key characteristics of the anxiety and depression symptoms network in elderly individuals with MCCs, identifies CESD3 (Sadness) and GAD2 (Uncontrollable worry) as the central symptoms, and GAD1(Nervousness), CESD1 (Bother by things), CESD10 (Sleep quality), and GAD3 (Excessive worry) as the bridge symptom. This provides a theoretical basis for targeted intervention measures to improve the mental health of elderly individuals with MCCs in the future.
