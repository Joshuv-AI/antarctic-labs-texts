Evaluation of different doses of Femoston therapy for incomplete abortion: A prospective observational trial
Authors: Wenrong Wang, Bingchen Gong, Yingying Cai, Xueyao Huang, Jian An
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare the efficacy of different doses of femoston with expectant management in patients with incomplete abortions. Patients diagnosed with incomplete abortion were included if they chose to continue medical treatment after relevant contraindications were excluded. Participants were divided into 3 groups: the femoston (1/10) and femoston (2/10) groups received different doses of femoston, and patients in the control group received expectant treatment. The success rate of complete abortion and the rate of menstrual recovery among the 3 groups were compared to evaluate the efficacy of different doses of femoston in patients with incomplete abortions. A total of 197 patients were analyzed: 73 in the femoston (1/10) group, 73 in the femoston (2/10) group, and 51 patients were followed up without treatment in the control group. The femoston group was significantly more effective than the control group P < .0001). The adjusted odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were 3.103 and 1.153 to 8.350 (P = .025). The success rate of complete abortion in the femoston (2/10) group was significantly higher than that in the femoston (1/10) group (adjusted OR: 0.403, 95% CI: 0.145–1.118, P = .081). In addition, the rate of menstrual recovery in the femoston group was significantly higher than that in the control group (P = .007), and the rate in the femoston (2/10) group was also higher than the femoston (1/10) group with statistically significant (P = .001). Femoston is effective in treating incomplete abortion, with femostons containing 2 mg estrogen being more effective. Patients with incomplete abortion are treated with femoston, and menstrual recovery time may be shortened. Femostons may be a new option for pharmacological treatment of incomplete abortions.

1. Introduction

Miscarriage is the loss of pregnancy before the baby survives outside the womb.[1] The most common complication after miscarriage or abortion is a partial loss of products of uterine conception, referred to as incomplete abortion.[2] Patients may present with vaginal bleeding or with lower abdominal pain. Untreated leaves can lead to bleeding, infections, shock, and death.[2,3] Complications of incomplete abortion considerably affect a woman physical and mental health, including sleep disturbances, social withdrawal, anger, and marital disturbance.[4] Fathers can also be affected emotionally by short-term anxiety and depression.[5] Therefore, patients with incomplete abortions should be effectively managed with safe and appropriate treatment.

Incomplete abortions must be treated with uterine evacuation, which can be achieved surgically, medically, or expectantly.[6,7] The World Health Organization and International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics recommend surgical treatment with manual vacuum aspiration or medical treatment with misoprostol when the uterus is 14 weeks or less in size.[6,8] Traditionally, surgical treatment has been the standard of care worldwide, and its safety and effectiveness have been well-documented. Management of incomplete abortion using misoprostol is an easy-to-use, feasible, and cost-effective modality, with success rates ranging from 80% to 91%, depending on the dose and method of administration.[9] Expectant management allows for products of conception to expel spontaneously without medication, with a meta-analysis demonstrating the success of expectant management from 39% to 78%.[10,11]

Medication for incomplete abortion reduces the burden on medical institutions and the need for surgical equipment and supplies, operating rooms, sterilization, and anesthesia.[12] Medication is less invasive and more acceptable than standard surgical treatment for patients. However, no drugs other than misoprostol were effective in treating incomplete abortions. In addition, medication guidelines and an international consensus on incomplete abortions are lacking. Therefore, exploring more efficient and safer medications is essential for improving the quality of life of patients with incomplete abortions.

Most studies on the medications treatment of incomplete abortion have focused on misoprostol,[13–15] while Mifepristone combined with estrogen-progesterone combination therapy has rarely reported. Mifepristone, a progestin receptor antagonist, decreases estrogen and progesterone receptors in decidua and promotes trophoblast and decidua cells degeneration.[16,17] Meanwhile, mifepristone also enhances uterine contractility and promotes cervical softening, which facilitates the intrauterine residues of conception evacuation.[18,19]

In estrogen-progesterone combination therapy, Femoston is a common choice in our hospitals. Femoston, complex packing estradiol tablets/estradiol, and dydrogesterone tablets, with estradiol at 2 doses of 1 mg and 2 mg, depending on the needs. Traditionally, femostons have been used as hormone replacement therapy to control moderate-to-severe menopausal symptoms in peri- or early postmenopausal women.[20,21] Oestradiol is responsible for proliferative changes in the endometrium. Dydrogesterone is a retro-progesterone with a molecular structure closely related to endogenous progesterone, which can differentiate the endometrium glandular and stromal cells.[22] Withdrawal of the drug causes endometrial exfoliation to play a role in drug curettage, promoting the products of conception in uterine evacuation. In this study, we investigated the efficacy of different doses of femoston for the treatment of incomplete abortion.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Population

The data of 289 patients diagnosed with incomplete abortion at the Women and Children Hospital Affiliated with Xiamen University, Fujian, China, were screened for this study from September 2022 to March 2023. Based on the initial collection of 50 patient data, the calculated sample size was 120 by Clinical Trial Sample Size Calculator. Incomplete abortion was defined as evidence of retained product of uterine conception by transvaginal ultrasonography and human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) levels above normal after induced or spontaneous abortion.[23] All patients were <12 weeks of gestation at the time of miscarriage or induced abortion. Patients with profuse vaginal bleeding, shock, signs of pelvic infections, allergy to mifepristone, estrogen, and dydrogesterone, and those who refused to undergo conservative treatment were excluded. After screening, 230 patients were included in this study. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Women and Children Hospital of Xiamen University, and informed consent was obtained from all patients included in the study. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. This study followed the ethical regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki. The confidentiality and anonymity of the data, and the voluntariness of participation were assured.

2.2. Treatment

In our hospital, patients with medical or induced abortions were recommended to return to the hospital 14 days after the abortion for follow-up examination. Participants with incomplete abortion were administered 25 mg mifepristone (twice a day) for 14 days on the day of diagnosis based on consensus and previous studies.[24–26] Then, the patients were informed of their condition by the 2 doctors in charge and informed the pros and cons of the 3 groups of conservative treatment at the first follow-up, and then were classified into 3 groups according to their willingness. Participants in the femoston (1/10) group were administered 2 tablets of femoston (1/10) (one estradiol tablet and 1 estradiol and dydrogesterone tablet) daily for 14 days with estradiol tablets containing 1 mg of estradiol and estradiol and dydrogesterone tablets containing 1 mg of estradiol and 10 mg of dydrogesterone. Participants in the femoston (2/10) group were administered 2 tablets of another dose of femoston (2/10) per day, 1 estradiol tablet (containing 2 mg of estradiol), and 1 estradiol and dydrogesterone tablet (containing 2 mg of estradiol and 10 mg of dydrogesterone) for 14 days. The control group was followed up without treatment.

The second follow-up appointment at the Women and Children Hospital of Xiamen University was scheduled for a post-recovery menstrual period or 45 days after being diagnosed with incomplete abortion for women in the 3 groups to confirm their abortion status. All women underwent transvaginal ultrasound assessment, and β-HCG levels were tested. β-HCG levels help to rule out conditions such as gestational trophoblastic disease and ectopic pregnancy. Ultrasound investigations were performed by the same team of sonographers at the time of diagnosis and follow-up. All the participants were informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time.

In this study, treatment failure (incomplete abortion) was defined as incomplete uterine evacuation (residues remained) based on ultrasound. Hysteroscopic evacuation is performed in patients who treatment failure regardless of symptoms of abdominal pain or vaginal bleeding. Treatment success (complete abortion) was defined as a clear image of the uterus without residual uterine content obtained or endometrial thickness < 15 mm on ultrasonography.[27,28] In this study, the duration of medication for incomplete abortion was long, and we selected 60 days after abortion as the node to observe menstruation recovery in the 3 groups. We distinguish between menstrual recovery or other types of bleeding by the duration of vaginal bleeding, the amount of bleeding, relative to previous menstrual flow, and its relationship to medication. Detailed medical history of the participants, including age, gravidity, parity, the days required to resume menstruation, the diameter of the residual uterine content, and whether there was a missed abortion or scarred uterus were obtained. The level of HCG in pre- and post-treatment was also collected.

2.3. Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) and IBM SPSS (version 23.0; IBM Corporation, Chicago, USA). A chi-square test was used to detect differences in clinical characteristics used as categorical variables. Furthermore, t-tests were used for continuous data, such as age and the level of HCG pre-and post-treatment. And the logistic regression method was applied for further verification and calculation of the odds ratio (OR). Unless otherwise stated, data are presented as the mean ± SD or number (%). Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. The characteristics of patients

The trial enrollment process and follow-up records are summarized in Figure 1. A total of 289 patients diagnosed with incomplete abortion were screened, of which 59 patients were excluded for various reasons. A total of 230 patients were included in this study. Twelve patients underwent surgical evacuation of the residual at the first follow-up, and 21 patients were lost to follow-up. The remaining 197 patients were treated in groups: 73 patients in the femoston (1/10) group were treated with 2 tablets of femoston (1/10), 73 patients in the femoston (2/10) group were treated with 2 tablets of femoston (2/10), and 51 patients were followed up without treatment in the control group.

3.3. Follow up and outcomes

The Femoston group (77.46%) was significantly more effective than the control group (48%) (OR: 3.724, 95% CI: 1.886–7.353, P < .0001, Table 4). The difference remained statistically significant after adjusting for factors including age, gravidity, width of the residual, the level of β-HCG before treatment, scarred uterus, missed abortion, types of abortion (adjusted OR: 3.103, 95% CI: 1.153–8.350, P = .025). The success rate of complete abortion in the femoston (2/10) group (84.51%) was significantly higher (OR: 0.437, 95% CI: 0.192–0.991, P = .035, Table 5) than in the femoston (1/10) group (70.42%). However, there was no statistical significance after adjusting for above 7 factors simultaneously (adjusted OR: 0.403, 95% CI: 0.145–1.118, P = .081). The success rates of complete abortion in the 3 groups of patients are shown in Figure 2. At 60 days after abortion, the rate of menstrual recovery in the femoston group was significantly higher than that in the control group (P = .007), and the rate in the femoston (2/10) group was also significantly higher than that in the femoston (1/10) group (P = .001) (Fig. 3).

4. Discussion

Incomplete abortion is the most common complication after abortion(induced and spontaneous), and may lead to bleeding, infection, shock, and potentially death if left untreated.[2] Thus, incomplete abortion is a significant problem for patients that should be managed effectively with appropriate procedures. The results of this study showed that the success rate was significantly higher in the femoston-treated group than that in the control group, with a higher success rate in the femoston (2/10) group. Moreover, the rate of menstrual recovery was significantly higher in the femoston group than that in the control group. Therefore, we considered that femostons might be an effective treatment for incomplete abortion.

Femoston is composed of estrogen and dydrogesterone. Estrogen can promote rapid proliferation and repair of the endometrium, and the endometrium at the adhesion of pregnancy residual tissue proliferates, facilitating the separation of residues from the uterus and making it easily discharged.[29] Progesterone can promote differentiation of the endometrium, and the combined effect of estrogen and progesterone promotes rapid proliferation and repair of the endometrium.[22,30] Withdrawal of the drug causes endometrial exfoliation to play a role in drug curettage, promoting the products of conception in uterine evacuation.

Several studies have shown satisfactory efficacy of medications for incomplete abortions, including oral, sublingual, and vaginal misoprostol or vaginal prostaglandin E1 (gemeprost).[13,14,31,32] The results of a review from the Cochrane Library showed that the success rate for treating incomplete abortion with misoprostol ranged from 80% to 81%, and that for surgery ranged from 91% to 100%.[9] The success rate of femoston (2/10) for treating incomplete abortion was 84.51% in our study, which was similar to that of other medical treatments in previous studies, such as misoprostol. In this study, the success rate was higher in the femoston (2/10) group (84.51%) than in the femoston (1/10) group (70.42%). We speculate that the reason may be that a higher dose of estrogen can promote better proliferation and repair of the endometrium and more complete endometrial exfoliation after withdrawal of the drug, which facilitates the products of conception in uterine evacuation and improves the success rate. Therefore, we may recommend selected femoston in the treatment of incomplete abortion, preferably femoston (2/10), in patients with allergies or contraindications to prostaglandins.

Femoston treatment in patients with incomplete abortion was highly effective, less invasive, and more acceptable than standard surgical treatment. However, there are also some limitations with femoston treatment compared to surgical treatment, such as longer treatment cycles, longer bleeding time. In addition, the success rate of drug treatment was affected by the diameter of the residue.[26] One study showed that estrogen and progestogen therapy after medical abortion may effectively reduce the duration of hemorrhage and promote menstrual recovery.[33] Similar results were obtained in the present study. Several studies have shown that the success rate of medication abortion correlates with the level of plasma HCG.[34,35] A similar phenomenon was observed in this study: the HCG level of patients in the control group after treatment was significantly higher than that in the femoston group, which was consistent with the higher failure rate in the control group. However, many factors can affect HCG levels, so the width of the residual, HCG levels, and symptoms should be combined in clinical practice in order to choose an appropriate treatment plan. The main advantage of this study is that it is the first to investigate the effectiveness of different doses of femoston in the treatment of incomplete abortion and positive results were obtained. There are also limitations in this study, such as the small sample size and single-center design. Further multicenter studies with larger samples are needed to provide more evidence to support our conclusions.

5. Conclusions

Femoston is effective in treating incomplete abortion, with femoston containing 2 mg estrogen being more effective. Patients with incomplete abortion are treated with femoston, and menstrual recovery time may be shortened. Femostons may be a new option for pharmacological treatment of incomplete abortions.
