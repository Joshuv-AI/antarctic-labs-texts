Forced vital capacity of healthy Iranian adults in Tehran
Authors: Hamidreza Jamaati, Maryam Akhtari, Parisa Sadat Madani, Somayeh Lookzadeh, Alireza Eslaminejad, Hooman Sharifi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global prevalence of chronic respiratory diseases is increasing, necessitating accurate lung function assessment. Forced Vital Capacity (FVC) is an important spirometric indicator of respiratory health. This study aims to determine the normal FVC values in healthy Iranian adults in Tehran, considering variables such as age, sex, environmental exposure, education, occupation, and comorbidities.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted on 732 healthy participants aged ≥ 40 years from 22 districts of Tehran. Spirometry was performed via a calibrated spirometer, and demographic data were collected via standardized questionnaires. Exclusion criteria included smoking, recent surgery, respiratory symptoms, and a history of lung disease. Data analysis was performed via SPSS, with p-values < 0.05 considered significant.ResultsThe mean FVC was 3.3 ± 0.86 L in men and 2.4 ± 0.62 L in women (p < 0.001). FVC declined with age and showed a positive correlation with height (p < 0.001) and a negative correlation with body mass index (BMI, p = 0.012). Significant variations in FVC were observed across different districts, with the highest values in District 2 (3.09 ± 0.82 L) and the lowest in District 16 (2.32 ± 0.57 L). FVC was lower in individuals with comorbidities such as heart disease, hypertension, and diabetes. It was also reduced in unemployed men and increased with higher educational attainment.ConclusionThis study provides baseline FVC values for healthy Iranian adults, highlighting the influence of demographic and environmental factors. Region-specific values are vital for accurate respiratory disease assessment and management.

Introduction

The increasing incidence of chronic respiratory diseases worldwide highlights the need for more information on global lung health [1]. Assessment of lung function is crucial in predicting outcomes when diagnosing respiratory disease. Forced vital capacity (FVC) is an important measure in lung function testing and assessment of lung health that quantifies the maximum amount of air an individual can forcibly expel after taking a deep breath. Deviations in FVC from normal values can indicate various respiratory conditions, such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [2].

FVC is primarily assessed through spirometry. The interpretation of spirometric lung function results typically relies on comparisons with reference values derived from a healthy population. Previous studies have examined variations in healthy lung function among different groups [3, 4]. The FVC is influenced by a combination of regional, environmental, and ethnic factors. Several determinants, including age, sex, and environmental elements such as air quality, significantly impact FVC levels in healthy individuals [5]. For example, people living at high altitudes generally have a higher FVC because they have lower oxygen levels than do those living at low altitudes [6]. The FVC has also been shown to be positively associated with long-term relative humidity [7]. Poor environmental conditions, particularly elevated levels of air pollution, can negatively impact respiratory health, resulting in a decline in FVC and exacerbating existing respiratory diseases [8, 9].

Older people and those with obesity tend to have reduced FVC [10], whereas regular physical activity can improve lung function [11]. In addition, socioeconomic factors may also affect lung health, highlighting the multifaceted nature of FVC as a clinical measure [12]. Additionally, ethnic differences significantly impact FVC, with Caucasian, African American, and Asian populations typically having different values for respiratory indices [3, 4]. This highlights the need for developing tailored, region-specific FVC reference values that are critical for the accurate diagnosis and management of respiratory diseases. Normal values enable healthcare providers to interpret lung function tests in the context of specific populations, particularly in countries characterized by diverse ethnic demographics and varying environmental conditions [3].

It is important to establish specific lung function reference values for each nation or ethnic group by assessing individuals without respiratory problems. In Iran, where various ethnic groups reside in different climates and altitudes, determining the normal range for spirometric measurements is particularly important. However, currently, there is a lack of reliable reference values for FVC from a representative group of healthy adults in Tehran, which limits the precision of respiratory assessments in this region. This study assessed the FVC value of healthy Iranian individuals in Tehran and evaluated changes in FVC in relation to environmental conditions, age, sex, and comorbidities. and BMI.

Methods

Study design

In this cross-sectional study, lung function test results were collected from 22 districts in Tehran. The protocol for this study has been thoroughly explained in detail previously [13]. This project included individuals over 40 years old who consented to participate. Spirometry and data collection were performed by a three-member team. Spirometry data were recorded electronically (2012 In2itive Handheld Spirometry and Spirotrac V Part. No.79000) and conducted by trained specialists. A standardized, interviewer-administered questionnaire collected basic information about the subjects’ health and lifestyle, medications, smoking status, and comorbidities. All the participants were tested free of charge.

Sampling strategy

This study employed a stratified cluster sampling method with proportional allocation within each stratum. The target population included all non-institutionalized residents aged over 40 living in Tehran. The sampling process incorporates the stratification of the population across the 22 municipal districts of Tehran. The number of clusters assigned to each district was proportional to the number of households in that district. The total number of clusters was determined based on the overall sample size, the average household size, and logistical considerations for identifying, transporting, and examining participants.

In each selected cluster, a three-person field team consisting of one male and one female interviewer and a driver visited the index household identified through random sampling in 22 districts of Tehran. Both of the interviewers were under the age of 28 and dressed in white medical overalls. After interviewing the index household, the team systematically visited 10 neighboring households in a clockwise direction. If more than one eligible individual was present in a household, the Kish method was used to select the participant. The team members introduced themselves with official identification and explained the purpose of the study to the head of the household. Data collection was scheduled for Fridays, which is the official weekend in Iran. All eligible individuals were invited to complete the questionnaire and, if applicable, undergo spirometry testing.

Reference population

Among the 1,766 subjects, 732 were included in the sample used to establish normal values for the FVC parameter (shown in Fig. 1). Individuals who were under 40 years old, did not consent to participate in the study, or met any of the following exclusion criteria were not included: a history of chest or abdominal surgery in the past three months, a heart attack in the last three months, eye surgery or injury within the last three months, any heart problems in the last three months, a resting heart rate exceeding 120 beats per minute, undergoing treatment for tuberculosis infection, being in the last three months of pregnancy, or any other reason for not participating.

Recruitment and enrollment of individuals in the study

To determine the FVC of healthy individuals, the following groups were excluded from the study: current smokers; individuals who experienced respiratory symptoms such as phlegm, cough, wheezing, and shortness of breath on most days over the past three months; and those taking medication for respiratory conditions or with a history of lung diseases such as bronchitis, emphysema, or asthma.

Questionnaires

The examination protocol included a comprehensive questionnaire that focuses on respiratory symptoms, overall health status, activity limitations, and contact with possible risk factors, including tobacco smoke, occupational hazards, and exposure to biomass. This core questionnaire is based on elements from several established sources, including the 1978 ATS/DLD Respiratory Symptom Questionnaire [14], the Respiratory Health Study [15], the CNR study [16], and the OLIN study [17]. Additionally, it incorporates the Short Form-12 to evaluate general health status [18]. Data on various respiratory symptoms, including coughing, sputum production, wheezing, and shortness of breath, were collected. Additionally, it inquired about exposure to risk factors such as smoking and occupation, as well as respiratory diagnoses such as asthma, emphysema, COPD, and chronic bronchitis. The questionnaire also addresses comorbidities, healthcare utilization, medication use, activity limitations, and general health status. Participants’ height and weight were measured by trained field staff. To ensure the highest quality control, a wall-mounted stadiometer for height measurement was used. Height and weight were recorded with participants wearing indoor clothing and without shoes. All teams were provided with the same brand of stadiometers and weight scales to maintain consistency.

Spirometry

The primary outcome of this protocol was the measurement of FVC. The methods developed for BOLD meet or exceed ATS standards regarding appropriate equipment and techniques [19]. The primary spirometry parameters for analysis, including FVC, were assessed. Each participant performed a minimum of three spirometry maneuvers. Two best FVC values were accepted if they were within 150 mL of each other, consistent with ATS/ERS repeatability criteria. To maintain quality control in this study, all teams were mandated to utilize the 2120 In2itive Vitalograph spirometer, which was selected for its accuracy, durability, portability, and convenience for storage. This device is suitable for field use, including locations without power. Moreover, the 2012 In2itive Handheld Spirometry and Spirotrac V (Part No. 79000) have received approval from the National Research Institute of Tuberculosis and Lung Diseases for meeting the established performance standards concerning measurement reliability, field applicability, and data accessibility.

Data analysis

SPSS (version 27.0) was used to analyze the spirometric parameters and demographic information collected during the study. The normality and homogeneity of the data were first assessed via the Shapiro‒Wilk and Kolmogorov‒Smirnov tests. Descriptive statistics, including the mean ± standard deviation, were calculated for both spirometric and anthropometric variables. An independent t test was conducted to evaluate the significance of differences between the means. Additionally, linear regression analysis was performed to examine the relationships and strengths of the correlations between age, sex, weight, height, BMI, and spirometric measures of ventilatory function. In this analysis, one anthropometric variable was systematically excluded via backward selection. Statistical significance was established at a p value of less than 0.05.

Relationship of FVC level with height and BMI

The relationships between FVC levels and height among participants were assessed. The results revealed that the FVC significantly increased (p < 0.001) with increasing height (Fig. 3). Additionally, the FVC was notably lower in individuals with a BMI greater than 25 than in those with a BMI less than 25 (p = 0.012; see Fig. 4). Multiple linear regression (MLR) with the backward method was used to evaluate the influence of age, sex, height, weight, and BMI on FVC. Table 4 indicates a strong negative correlation between age and FVC, whereas a strong positive correlation was observed between height and FVC.

FVC levels in the 22 districts of Tehran

Considering that the severity of air pollution varies throughout the year across the 22 districts of Tehran, we compared the FVC levels of individuals residing in different areas. Residents in District 2 presented the highest vital capacity at 3.09 ± 0.82 L, whereas those in District 16 presented the lowest vital capacity at 2.32 ± 0.57 L (see Fig. 5; Table 5). Additionally, the highest percentage of FVC was reported in District 2 at 88.05 ± 13.75%, whereas District 21 had the lowest percentage at 67.52 ± 17.41% (Table 5). We also analyzed the relationship between FVC levels and the air pollution indices published by the Meteorological Organization across the 22 districts; however, we found no significant correlation between FVC levels and air pollution in the various areas.

FVC levels based on employment and education

Considering the impact of physical activity, mobility status, and socioeconomic factors on FVC levels, this study assessed the influence of employment type and education level on FVC. Discussion

The increasing prevalence of chronic respiratory diseases worldwide highlights the need for a greater understanding of global lung health, particularly the factors that influence lung function [20]. Pulmonary function tests are essential for the early detection and management of respiratory diseases [21]. Accurate interpretation of these tests requires normal values. Various factors can affect the results obtained from these tests. Influencing factors include age, sex, weight, height, lifestyle, environmental conditions, socioeconomic status, altitude, and ethnicity [5, 22]. Establishing reference values for pulmonary function tests across various demographic groups with different living conditions and characteristics is crucial.

It has been previously demonstrated that the Caucasian GLI equation is inappropriate for interpreting pulmonary function tests in the Iranian population [23]. The lack of predictive values specific to the Iranian population may result in the misclassification of diseases. Therefore, it is crucial to establish normal spirometry values. To address this issue, we conducted a study on FVC levels in healthy, asymptomatic, nonsmoking adults in Tehran, Iran. Understanding the normal ranges of FVC can aid in the early detection of potential respiratory issues and guide public health strategies aimed at improving lung health [24].

In our study, the FVC was 3.3 ± 0.86 L in men and 2.4 ± 0.62 L in women over 40 years of age. Similarly, Aloosh et al. reported FVC levels of 4.18 ± 0.59 L in Iranian men and 2.47 ± 0.37 L in women over 50 years of age in the city of Hamedan, Iran [25]. In a study of 1499 individuals aged 18–60 years in Mazandaran, Iran, the FVC was 3.94 ± 0.94 in all participants [26]. Our results revealed that the FVC levels in men aged 60–69 years and 70–79 years were 3.04 ± 0.61 and 2.59 ± 0.69, respectively. For women in the same age groups, the FVC values were 2.03 ± 0.57 and 1.85 ± 0.37. A cohort study of the Swedish population reported FVC levels of 4.12 (4.06–4.18) for men aged 60–69 years and 3.59 (3.53–3.65) for those aged 70–79 years, whereas for women, the levels were 2.93 (2.90–2.97) and 2.51 (2.47–2.55), respectively [27]. As previously reported [28], the FVC of the population decreases rapidly with age over 40 years. The lower FVC in our study may be due to the older age of the participants, genetic factors, and environmental conditions.

In line with previous research (5), we observed that FVC levels were related to both sex and height. Although we did not find a direct correlation between weight or BMI and reduced FVC in patients, we discovered that FVC was significantly lower in individuals with a BMI greater than 25 than in those with a BMI less than 25. The influence of obesity on spirometric measurements varies across different studies. While some research indicates minimal to no effects, others may report differing results [29], while others indicate significant influences [30]. In the study by Triebner et al., obese individuals had greater declines in FVC (25 mL/year) than did those with a normal BMI (10 mL/year) [31]. Conversely, Luoto et al. reported that a BMI greater than 35 was associated with a slower decline in forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) but did not significantly affect the decline in FVC [27]. This inconsistency may be due to the different ethnic backgrounds of the populations assessed in pulmonary function tests or could stem from differences in the methodologies employed in these studies.

Traffic-related air pollution has been associated with reduced lung function and the onset of asthma in adults [32]. Although we evaluated FVC levels across various districts of Tehran, we did not find a significant correlation between these levels and the districts despite the different levels of air pollution. This finding contrasts with the results reported by Santos et al. [8], who reported a positive relationship between traffic-related air pollution and impaired lung function. The absence of correlation in our study may stem from the complex interaction of multiple environmental factors and individual susceptibility. This suggests that further research is essential to clarify these relationships, particularly concerning FVC levels in the unique ecological context of Tehran.

Our analysis revealed that FVC levels were significantly affected by education and employment status. We observed a lower FVC in unemployed individuals and those in high-risk occupations, such as firefighting, which underscores the impact of physical activity and occupational exposure on lung function. Consistent with our findings, Luzak et al. demonstrated that physically active individuals had higher spirometric values [33]. Previous studies have demonstrated that lung function parameters can be altered in high-risk occupations involving exposure to smoke, fire, or chemicals. While research indicates that short-term exposure to welding fumes and gases may affect lung function, the findings are not universally consistent [34, 35]. Our study revealed no significant correlation between FVC levels and high-risk occupations, except for firefighters, who had significantly lower FVC levels. In support of our findings, Adele et al. reported a slight decrease in pulmonary function among firefighters following exposure to house fires [36]. Additionally, we found that participants with higher education levels demonstrated better lung function, which aligns with the findings of Johannessen et al., who reported that socioeconomic factors significantly affect lung health. They reported that lower education levels and lower occupational status were associated with a greater decline in lung function in men [12].

Finally, our study revealed that individuals with comorbidities such as heart disease, hypertension, and diabetes had significantly lower FVC levels. This finding is consistent with the literature suggesting that chronic diseases can adversely affect lung function [2]. The interplay between respiratory health and comorbid conditions highlights the need for integrated healthcare approaches that address multiple health determinants.

Our study has several limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small considering Tehran’s population, and it includes a relatively high percentage of female participants. Additionally, individuals under the age of 40 were not included in the study, and the sampling method may not accurately represent the entire population. We recommend conducting a more comprehensive study on lung function that considers air pollution levels in Tehran across all age groups.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study provides vital reference values for FVC in healthy Iranian adults. These findings underscore the significant effects of factors such as sex, age, height, BMI, environmental influences, socioeconomic status, and comorbidities on lung function. These findings highlight the necessity for region-specific reference values in pulmonary function testing and the importance of considering various demographic and environmental factors when evaluating respiratory health. Future research should aim to conduct longitudinal studies to understand better how lung function changes over time and to assess the impact of interventions designed to improve respiratory health in diverse populations.
