From Recommendation to Implementation: Clinical Outcomes of a Single-Center Molecular Tumor Board
Authors: Tabea Fröhlich, Ulrich Lehmann, Tanja Reineke-Plaaß, Thomas Wirth, Sabrina Welland, Chen Du, Bernd Auber, Benjamin-Alexander Bollmann, Philipp Ivanyi, Tjoung-Won Park-Simon, Diana Steinmann, Christian Hartmann, Ariana de Oliviera Mekonnen, Anja Heutling, Andrea Schütte, Hans Heinrich Wedemeyer, Florian Heinrich Heidel, Susanne Isfort, Arndt Vogel, Anna Saborowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMolecular tumor boards (MTBs) support precision oncology by translating genomic profiling into evidence-based treatment recommendations, for example according to the European Society for Medical Oncology Scale for Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESCAT). Their clinical utility in real-world care depends on effective implementation within healthcare systems.ObjectivesTo evaluate the implementation rate of MTB recommendations, associated determinants, and clinical outcomes in a real-world single-center cohort.Patients and MethodsAt a single-center MTB, 582 consecutive cases (2020–2023) were retrospectively analyzed, with last survival follow-up in August 2025. Patient demographics, tumor characteristics, genomic alterations, ESCAT and ZPM (Zentrum für Personalisierte Medizin) evidence levels, targeted therapy recommendation and implementation rates, survival outcomes, and barriers to implementation were evaluated using descriptive and inferential statistics, as appropriate.ResultsOf 582 patients (median age 61 years, 48.5% female, 78.2% UICC stage IV), 55.6% (n = 324) received a targeted therapy recommendation, most based on ESCAT I–II evidence (59.9%). Among patients with a therapy recommendation, implementation status was unknown in 83 cases, but at least 23.5% (n = 76) received the proposed therapy. Major barriers included continuation of alternative systemic therapy (n = 89, 53.9%) and best supportive care or death before initiation (n = 54, 32.7%). Stronger levels of evidence (ESCAT per level increase: OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.08–1.39, p = 0.003; ZPM per level increase: OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.19–1.75, p < 0.001) and lower gastrointestinal cancer (OR 2.39, 95% CI 1.13–4.93, p = 0.020) were positively associated with increased likelihood of therapy implementation, while extramural molecular profiling showed a trend toward lower uptake (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.36–1.03, p = 0.062). Overall survival (OS) from the date of MTB discussion in the full cohort (n = 563) was 11.1 months (95% CI 9.7–12.8). In patients who received at least one prior therapy before MTB discussion (n = 202), OS was 20.1 months (95% CI 12.3–NA) in patients who received the MTB-recommended therapy (n = 62) versus 7.7 months (95% CI 6.5–12.5) in those who did not (n = 140; p < 0.001). Among 31 patients with paired data, PFS under MTB-guided therapy was significantly longer than in the prior line (p = 0.036), with 54.8% achieving a PFS2/PFS1 ratio ≥ 1.3.ConclusionsIn this real-world analysis, implementation of MTB recommendations was associated with improved survival outcomes. However, systemic, clinical, and logistical barriers substantially limited uptake. Earlier integration of MTBs into treatment planning and targeted strategies to address these barriers may help increase their potential benefit for patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11523-026-01231-2.

Key Points

More than half of patients discussed in a molecular tumor board received a targeted therapy recommendation, but only about one in four ultimately received the proposed treatment.Patients who were treated according to the molecular tumor board recommendation had significantly longer survival compared with those who did not receive the recommended therapy.These findings highlight both the clinical potential of precision oncology and the practical barriers that limit its implementation in routine care.

Introduction

The advent of precision oncology has fundamentally changed cancer treatment, driven by advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies that have made the comprehensive molecular characterization of tumors more accessible. These approaches allow for the identification of actionable genomic alterations across a wide range of cancer types. However, the integration of molecular data into routine cancer care remains challenging, as the clinical interpretation of increasingly complex genomic information often exceeds the scope of organ-based treatment algorithms. Molecular tumor boards have thus emerged as a central platform for the multidisciplinary interpretation of molecular findings and for guiding the selection of targeted therapies beyond approved tumor indications [6, 7, 11, 14, 22, 26].

Prospective evidence supporting precision oncology strategies has historically been limited. Platform trials such as NCI-MATCH demonstrated the feasibility of assigning molecularly matched therapies across tumor entities and provided important insights into the variability of clinical benefit across molecular subgroups but lacked a randomized comparison against standard therapy [18]. Recently, the randomized phase II ROME trial provided high-level evidence for MTB-guided treatment strategies [15]. In this multicenter study, genomically matched therapy significantly improved objective response rate and progression-free survival compared with standard of care, while overall survival was not prolonged, largely due to a high crossover rate. These findings support the clinical activity of MTB-guided therapies while highlighting the complexity of demonstrating survival benefits in this setting.

The clinical benefit of MTB-guided therapy is further supported by a growing body of real-world evidence. A systematic review by Larson et al. summarized outcomes from more than 3000 patients discussed in MTBs and reported implementation rates ranging from 11% to 39%, with several studies indicating an association between MTB-guided treatment and improved clinical outcomes [13]. Regarding OS benefit, the most comprehensive evidence comes from a meta-analysis of 34 studies including over 12,000 patients, which confirmed a significant overall survival (OS) benefit associated with MTB-guided treatment (HR 0.46) [5].

Beyond demonstrating feasibility and activity, there is increasing interest in how to assess clinical benefit in the context of MTB-guided therapies, which are frequently supported by moderate- or tumor-extrapolated evidence. Traditional end points such as overall survival are often difficult to interpret owing to treatment crossover, heterogeneity of tumor entities, and limited patient numbers. Next to others, the progression-free survival ratio (PFS2/PFS1) has been proposed as a pragmatic metric to evaluate individual clinical benefit. This concept was supported by the prospective MASTER trial conducted by the German Cancer Consortium, which reported implementation of MTB recommendations in 31.8% of patients and demonstrated clinical benefit, defined by a PFS2/PFS1 ratio greater than 1.3, in 35.7% of treated patients [9]. Together, these studies highlight the relevance of pragmatic real-world end points for evaluating the effectiveness of MTB-guided therapies.

In Germany, the first experience was reported from Freiburg, where the implementation of an MTB resulted in molecularly guided treatment in only a minority of patients. Subsequent analyses confirmed the feasibility of MTB workflows but emphasized ongoing challenges such as reimbursement constraints and clinical deterioration prior to treatment initiation [7, 8]. The largest series from Munich analyzed 1000 consecutive cases and reported recommendations in 41% yet implementation in only 17% [6]. Additional cohorts subsequently corroborated these findings [26, 29].

The range of actionable alterations has increased markedly in recent years, spanning tumor-agnostic approvals such as NTRK, RET, and BRAF inhibitors, as well as tumor-specific indications including therapies directed against, amongst others, KRAS, ERBB2, and FGFR2 [4, 17, 19, 21, 24, 25]. MTBs play a critical role in contextualizing these therapies, particularly when evidence must be extrapolated across tumor entities or applied outside approved indications.

In light of these advances, systematic real-world data are increasingly important to understand the value of off-label precision oncology in clinical practice. The present study aims to characterize the patient population presented to the MTB of the Comprehensive Cancer Center Hannover, evaluate molecular findings and associated levels of evidence, assess the rate of implementation, and analyze clinical outcomes including OS and PFS ratio as a surrogate for clinical benefit.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Cohort

This study is a retrospective, single-center analysis of cases presented at the Molecular Tumor Board (MTB) of the Center for Personalized Medicine (ZPM) within the Comprehensive Cancer Center Hannover, Hannover Medical School (MHH), Germany. Between February 2020 and December 2023, a total of 590 consecutive cases were discussed. After exclusion of patients with incomplete clinical or molecular data, 582 patients were evaluable for analysis. Retrospective follow-up was conducted by review of electronic and paper charts and, when necessary, obtained from referring physicians. The last survival follow-up was performed in August 2025.

Molecular Profiling

Molecular profiling was performed either in-house or through external laboratories. Tumor tissue was used as the standard material for molecular profiling. In a few cases, circulating free (cf) DNA and/or cfRNA preparations from peripheral blood were used as DNA/RNA source. Different targeted and comprehensive next-generation sequencing (NGS) assays were applied, including, Oncomine™ Comprehensive Cancer Assay v3, Oncomine™ Focus Panel, Archer Fusionplex Lung v2, Archer Fusionplex Pan-Solid v2, FoundationOne CDx, and an in-house DNA-based panel. For an overview of the most frequently applied sequencing panels (91.8% of all analyses, only sequencing panels used in more than four cases (n > 4) are shown), and the genes covered, see Supplementary Table 1. PD-L1 expression status was recorded as reported in the original pathology reports and analyzed as a binary variable (expressed versus not expressed). Given the use of different scoring systems (e.g., TPS, CPS, or IC score), any reported PD-L1 expression was considered positive, while cases explicitly reported as negative were classified as not expressed.

MTB Workflow

The MTB convened on a biweekly basis and was organized as an interdisciplinary board with experts in medical oncology from different subspecialties, pathology with a focus on molecular pathology, and human genetics, complemented by additional disciplines as required. Cases were submitted either by internal physicians or external partners, with clinical information and molecular results provided in advance.

Therapy recommendations were rated according to the ESMO Scale for Clinical Actionability of molecular Targets (ESCAT) [16] and the classification system used by the German National Center for Tumor Diseases (NCT/DKTK) and the Centers for Personalized Medicine (ZPM) in Baden-Württemberg [10]. Additional considerations included availability of clinical trials, drug accessibility in Germany, and patient-related factors (e.g. ECOG performance status).

Data Collection

Clinical and molecular data were extracted from the electronic medical records and MTB databases. Collected variables included patient demographics (age, sex, ECOG performance status, tumor entity, and UICC stage, previous lines of systemic therapy, detected molecular alterations (gene, variant type, potential actionability), MTB recommendations, implementation status and reasons for nonimplementation, and clinical outcomes.

Objectives

The primary objective of this retrospective real-world analysis was to characterize the patient population presented to the MTB and to evaluate the spectrum of molecular pathology findings, the levels of evidence supporting MTB recommendations, and the rate of recommendation implementation. Secondary objectives were to determine clinical and molecular determinants of implementation, to evaluate survival outcomes as exploratory indicators of clinical utility, to delineate barriers to implementation in real-world care.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were applied for patient demographics, tumor characteristics, genomic alterations, and recommendations. Associations between clinical or molecular factors and the likelihood of therapy implementation were assessed by univariate and multivariate logistic regression. Continuous and ordinal variables were modeled per unit increase; in addition, selected variables were explored using clinically meaningful categorizations. Survival was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier methods and compared with log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards models were used for multivariate survival analyses. For patients with paired data, differences in PFS before and after MTB-guided therapy were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) within RStudio (version 2025.05.0+496, Posit Software, Boston, MA, USA). Figures were edited with Inkscape (version 1.4.2; Inkscape Project).

Results

Patient Cohort and Baseline Characteristics

Between February 2020 and December 2023, 590 cases were registered for MTB discussion of which 8 cases were excluded due to formal reasons (Fig. 1). Median age was 61 years (range 12–92 years) and 48.5% of patients were female (Table 1). Most patients had advanced disease with 78.2% UICC stage IV. The most common tumor entities were biliary tract cancer (BTC) (23.8%), lung carcinoma (13.7%), pancreatic cancer (12.7%), lower gastrointestinal (GI) cancer (10.3%), and sarcoma (9.6%). Most patients presented with an ECOG performance status of 0–1 (74%) and had received one or two prior lines of systemic therapy (55.3%). The baseline characteristics were comparable across patients with or without a therapy recommendation and among those with or without therapy implementation.

Molecular Profiling and Actionable Alterations

Molecular testing was performed using a range of next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels. In 65.1% the profiling was carried out in-house, while 22.3% were analyzed at external laboratories or within clinical trials. The most commonly applied panels were the Oncomine™ Comprehensive Cancer v3 (249 cases) and the Archer Fusionsplex Lung v2 (170 cases) panels (Supplementary Table 1). In-house analyses frequently combined multiple panels tailored to the clinical question and tumor entity, frequently integrating both DNA- and RNA-based assays, such as the Oncomine™ comprehensive v3 and the Archer FusionPlex Lung v2 panel. A complete list of the analyzed genes and their respective gene coverage is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Across the full cohort, the most frequently reported altered genes and biomarkers were KRAS (n = 143), TP53 (n = 137), PD-L1 (n = 61), FGFR (FGFR2 n = 29, FGFR1/3/4 n = 29), and PIK3CA (n = 58) (Fig. 2A). These findings reflect all molecular alterations and biomarkers reported in the diagnostic work-up, irrespective of therapeutic relevance at the time of MTB discussion. Their prevalence and distribution varied substantially across tumor entities. In BTC, the predominant findings included alterations in FGFR2 (22.0%), KRAS (22.0%), and CDKN2A/B (17.4%). Lung cancers were most often characterized by TP53 (35.1%), EGFR (27.0%), and KRAS (25.7%) alterations. PD-L1 expression was reported in 41.9%. In pancreatic cancer, KRAS alterations predominated as expected (78.1%), followed by TP53 (47.9%), and CDKN2A/B (20.5%).

Figure 2B illustrates the distribution of molecular alterations and biomarkers with potential therapeutic relevance across tumor entities, based on all alterations summarized in Supplementary Table 2. This analysis reflects a retrospective assessment of potential targetability according to current knowledge and is independent of whether an alteration directly resulted in an MTB therapy recommendation at the time of discussion. Consequently, only a subset of sequenced tumors harbored alterations that were considered actionable in the clinical context of the MTB (e.g., 20 of 73 evaluable pancreatic cancers), whereas the presented distribution captures the broader spectrum of potentially targetable findings. The prevalence of alterations with potential therapeutic relevance differed markedly by tumor entity. In BTC, most potentially actionable alterations were found in FGFR2 (27.9%) and IDH1/2 (IDH1 13.2%, IDH2 11.8%). In lung cancer, 36.4% of the cases showed PD-L1 positivity, and potentially actionable EGFR alterations were detected in 31.8% and ERBB2 alterations in 13.6 %. Among the 29 lower gastrointestinal cancer cases with at least one potentially actionable alteration, ERBB2 was most frequent (24.1% with 5 cases of amplification, and 2 mutations, see Supplementary Table 2), followed by KRAS (G12C n = 4), PD-L1, and PIK3CA (13.8% respectively). In pancreatic cancer, the most frequent alterations with potential therapeutic relevance were BRAF (35.0%, including 2 cases of V600E, 4 non-V600E mutations, and 1 fusion), KRAS (15.0%, G12C n = 3), and BRCA2 (15.0%). A comprehensive overview of all detected, potentially actionable alterations in the MTB cohort is shown in Supplementary Table 2.

In 66 of 576 cases with molecular diagnostic, no genomic alteration was reported. In the remaining 510 cases, at least one alteration was detected, of which a subset formed the basis for MTB therapy recommendations, resulting in a recommendation for targeted therapy in 324 patients (Fig. 1).

Evidence Levels of Recommendations

Therapy recommendations were stratified according to both the internationally established ESCAT system and the ZPM classification used within German MTBs (Supplementary Table 3). While ESCAT ranks molecular targets primarily by the strength of clinical evidence for therapeutic efficacy, the ZPM system was developed to reflect real-world MTB decision-making by incorporating additional categories (e.g., early clinical or preclinical evidence) and differentiating between levels of maturity within the same evidence tier. This allowed each recommendation in this cohort to be assigned to both an international and a national evidence framework.

According to the ESCAT classification (Fig. 3A), 194 recommendations (59.9%) were based on high or highest levels of evidence (ESCAT I–II), while 108 recommendations (33.3%) were supported by intermediate evidence (ESCAT III). Only a minority of cases (6.2%) were classified as ESCAT IV, representing the lowest evidence levels. A comparable distribution was observed using the ZPM classification (Fig. 3B), with 194 (59.9%) recommendations falling into high (m1), and 103 (31.8%) into intermediate (m2) categories, whereas only 8 cases (2.5%) were classified as m3. Across tumor entities, high-level recommendations predominated in BTC, lower GI, and lung cancer, while pancreatic cancers and sarcomas more frequently received recommendations supported by lower evidence levels. Overall, the two classification systems showed a high degree of concordance (Fig. 3C). ESCAT I recommendations were almost exclusively assigned to ZPM m1, while ESCAT II recommendations were predominantly classified as m1 and less frequently as m2. ESCAT III recommendations mainly corresponded to ZPM m2, whereas ESCAT IV recommendations were exclusively classified as m3.

Implementation of Recommendations

Among the 324 patients with a therapy recommendation, 76 (23.5%) ultimately received the proposed therapy, while 165 (50.9%) were documented not to have received the recommended treatment. For the remaining 83 patients, information on therapy implementation was not available (Fig. 4A). The most common reasons for nonimplementation were the initiation of an alternative systemic therapy (n = 89), death before therapy initiation or transition to best supportive care (combined n = 54) (Fig. 4B).

A Bar chart and B tabular overview of the implementation of MTB-recommended therapies, grouped by tumor entity

Implementation rates varied considerably across tumor entities. In BTC, 16 of 78 patients (20.5%) received the recommended therapy, while in pancreatic cancer only 7 of 42 (16.7%) did. In contrast, lower and upper GI cancers had a higher implementation rate (23 of 34; 35.3% and 6 of 19; 31.6%). In sarcoma and gynecological cancers, approximately 20% of recommendations were implemented.

Predictors of Therapy Implementation

In univariate logistic regression, several variables were associated with the probability of implementing an MTB-recommended therapy (Fig. 5). Patients with lower GI cancers had a significantly higher probability of therapy implementation compared with other entities (OR 2.39, 95% CI 1.13–4.93, p = 0.020). Conversely, lower levels of evidence were linked with a lower implementation rate as reflected by both ESCAT (OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.72–0.93, p = 0.003) and ZPM scores (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.57–0.84, p < 0.001). External profiling was included to assess whether logistical factors, such as increased organizational complexity and potential delays, may influence therapy implementation. In this analysis, external profiling showed a nonsignificant trend towards lower implementation rates (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.33–1.12, p = 0.122).

Univariate and multivariate analysis of variables associated with the likelihood of receiving an MTB-recommended therapy. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) are shown. Continuous variables (UICC stage, number of prior therapy lines, ESCAT and ZPM scores) were modeled as ordinal variables; odds ratios reflect the effect per unit increase

The number of prior systemic therapy lines, analyzed as a continuous variable, was not significantly associated with therapy implementation (OR 1.14 per additional line, 95% CI 0.95–1.36, p = 0.157). To further explore the clinical relevance of MTB timing, a categorical analysis comparing patients presented at diagnosis (0 prior therapy lines) versus those presented after initiation of systemic therapy (≥ 1 line) was performed, showing a nonsignificant trend towards higher implementation rates in patients discussed at diagnosis (OR 0.68, 95% CI 0.34–1.42, p = 0.285, data not shown).

Variables included into the multivariate model included lung and lower GI cancers, number of previous systemic therapy lines, lower ESCAT evidence, and external profiling; ZPM evidence was excluded to reduce collinearity bias with ESCAT evidence. In this analysis, lower gastrointestinal cancers retained a trend toward higher implementation rates (OR 2.31, 95% CI 0.93–5.48, p = 0.062), while a lower ESCAT evidence level was the only independent factor associated with reduced therapy implementation (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.73–0.97, p = 0.016).

Survival Outcomes

Median overall survival (mOS) from the date of MTB discussion for the entire evaluable cohort (n = 563) was 11.1 months (95% CI 9.7–12.8; Supplementary Fig. 1) with a median follow-up of 30.3 months (95% CI 28.1–34.1, reverse Kaplan–Meier). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis from the date of MTB discussion revealed a mOS of 10.8 months (95% CI 8.5–13.3) in patients without a targeted therapy recommendation and 12.1 months (95% CI 9.7–14.4) in those with a recommendation (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.71–1.06, p = 0.175; log-rank p = 0.17) (Fig. 6A). To reduce the risk of immortal time bias, a subgroup analysis including only cases with a targeted therapy recommendation after at least one prior line of systemic therapy was performed. Of 253 patients fulfilling these criteria, 202 were evaluable for survival analyses due to availability of follow-up data and complete treatment information. Patients who received the recommended therapy achieved a mOS of 20.1 months (95% CI 12.3–not reached), compared with 7.7 months (95% CI 6.5–12.5) in those who did not (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.38–0.77, p < 0.001; log-rank p = 0.00057) (Fig. 6B). Median follow-up at cut-off in this subgroup was 29.1 months (95% CI 27.8–36.9, reverse Kaplan–Meier).

In patients with available paired data (n = 31), median progression-free survival (mPFS) under the previous systemic therapy (PFS1) was 3.6 months (IQR 2.4–5.0), compared with 4.3 months (IQR 2.4–10.6) under the MTB-recommended therapy (PFS2). The difference was statistically significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank p = 0.036). A clinical benefit was defined as a PFS2/PFS1 ratio ≥ 1.3, following the approach described by Dörner et al. [3]. The median PFS2/PFS1 ratio was 1.55 (IQR 0.69–3.15), and 17 of 31 patients (54.8%; 95% CI 36.0–72.7%) achieved a ratio ≥ 1.3, considered to reflect clinical benefit (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B).

Best overall response (BOR) under targeted therapy was progressive disease in 25 patients (29.1%), stable disease in 19 (22.1%), partial response in 12 (14.0%), and complete response in 4 (4.7%), while BOR was not available in 16 patients (18.6%). The leading reason for treatment discontinuation was progression in 34 patients (39.5%), followed by therapy change to BSC/death in 14 (16.3%) and toxicity in 9 (10.5%). In one case, targeted treatment was discontinued due to ongoing complete remission.

Discussion

In this large real-world cohort of 582 cases evaluated at a single-center MTB, more than half of the discussions (55.6%) resulted in a therapy recommendation, of which 23.5% ultimately led to receipt of the proposed therapy, while implementation status was not available for 83 patients. Among patients who received MTB-guided therapy after at least one prior systemic line, overall survival was markedly prolonged compared with nonimplementation, and more than half of evaluable patients achieved a PFS2/PFS1 ratio ≥ 1.3. Acknowledging that the ≥ 1.3 threshold represents a pragmatic and previously proposed cut-off rather than a formally validated surrogate end point, this comparison suggests potential clinical activity of MTB-guided therapies in a relevant subset of patients.

Beyond confirming previously reported implementation rates, our study adds several novel insights to the existing MTB literature. First, we systematically characterize the gap between recommendation and implementation and identify key real-world barriers. Second, by applying both ESCAT and ZPM classifications, we directly compare two complementary evidence frameworks and demonstrate their high concordance. Third, we identify independent predictors of therapy implementation, highlighting the central role of evidence level. Finally, by combining subgroup-restricted survival analyses with individual-level PFS2/PFS1 assessment, we provide a more nuanced evaluation of clinical benefit beyond conventional end points. In addition, the relatively early timing of MTB presentation in a substantial proportion of patients offers insight into the role of MTBs beyond late-line treatment decision-making.

Our recommendation and implementation rates are comparable to recently published large real-world MTB cohorts. In the Regensburg real-world cohort of 658 patients, 50% received a therapy recommendation and 30.4% ultimately underwent MTB-guided treatment [23]. Similarly, Gladstone et al. reported actionable findings in a substantial proportion of patients with advanced cancer, yet with moderate real-world implementation rates, underscoring that the gap between molecular recommendation and actual treatment delivery remains a consistent challenge across health-care systems [5].

From a conceptual perspective, these findings align with the evolving role of MTBs as outlined by Tsimberidou et al., who emphasize that structured interpretation of molecular alterations, transparent evidence grading, and multidisciplinary decision-making are essential to translate genomic data into clinical benefit [27]. By systematically applying both ESCAT and ZPM classifications, our study operationalizes this principle and demonstrates that higher evidence levels independently predict successful implementation. In this context, ESCAT and ZPM classifications showed a high degree of concordance, while also differing in their level of granularity, particularly in intermediate evidence categories. These findings underscore that actionability is not a binary molecular attribute but a graded construct influenced by evidentiary strength, drug accessibility, and patient condition.

The baseline characteristics of this cohort, including median age, ECOG performance status, number of prior therapies, and sex distribution, were comparable to previously reported MTB series [6, 7, 22, 28]. Compared with these reports, our cohort included a higher proportion of hepatobiliary tumors and lung carcinoma, likely reflecting the regional referral patterns. In addition, a substantial proportion of patients were discussed at diagnosis or during early lines of therapy, indicating a selection pattern that differs from many predominantly late-line MTB cohorts. Regarding the molecular landscape, frequent potentially actionable alterations included PD-L1 expression, high TMB, PIK3CA, and FGFR1–3 with marked differences in their prevalence and clinical relevance across tumor entities.

In the hepatobiliary cancer cohort (n = 123), the most relevant actionable alterations were FGFR2-fusions and IDH1/2-mutations, consistent with prior reports [2, 30]. MTB-guided treatments were implemented in 20.9% of patients with BTC, mostly with FGFR-inhibitors, reflecting good concordance between detected alterations and applied therapies. This implementation rate is in line with other German MTB experiences in hepatobiliary malignancies, where the expansion of molecularly guided treatment options has notably improved tractability in this entity [23]. Among the pancreatic cancer cases (n = 74), the most relevant actionable alterations were KRAS, BRCA1/2, BRAF with MTB-guided therapies implemented in 17.0%, mainly comprising PARP- and MEK-inhibitors. These findings are consistent with prior reports demonstrating the overall low number of actionable targets in pancreatic cancer, with clinical benefit restricted to small subsets [29, 30]. Notably, with the anticipated introduction of RAS-targeted drugs into routine clinical care, the therapeutic relevance of precision oncology is bound to increase in the near future in pancreatic cancer. In 60 lower GI cancer cases (mainly colorectal cancer), actionable events were largely limited to ERBB2 amplifications in the context of KRAS wild-type tumors and BRAF mutations, whereas most patients harbored nontargetable RAS/RAF pathway alterations. Here, MTB-guided therapy was implemented in 40%, consistent with a high proportion of recommendations supported by strong levels of evidence (ZPM m1A-C 61.8%, ESCAT IA-C 32.4%). This rate exceeded that reported in other MTB series [22, 28], likely reflected by the enrichment of patients with ERBB2 and BRAF alterations in this cohort. In the cohort of 80 cases with lung cancer, MTB-guided therapy was implemented in 30.6% of cases, supported by a high proportion of ESCAT I–II alterations including EGFR, BRAF V600E, RET fusions, and KRAS G12C. These findings are concordant with the recent IASLC consensus statement on molecular testing in lung cancer, which advocates comprehensive and early biomarker assessment as a prerequisite for optimal therapeutic sequencing [1]. This comparatively high implementation rate likely reflects both strong evidence levels and established diagnostic pathways in this entity.

Overall, the composition of our cohort suggests a relevant selection bias related to referral patterns and timing of MTB presentation, which should be considered when interpreting the results and comparing them with other MTB series. The tumor spectrum of our cohort included a high proportion of biliary tract cancers and lung cancers—entities in which molecular profiling is clinically relevant early in the disease course and may directly inform treatment decisions. While a substantial proportion of patients were discussed at diagnosis or after only one prior line of therapy, the majority still had advanced-stage disease, indicating that MTB presentation occurred earlier within the course of systemic treatment rather than at an early disease stage. In this context, MTB recommendations were often generated in parallel to ongoing standard-of-care therapies and may not have been intended for immediate implementation, which likely contributed to the observed gap between recommendation and treatment initiation. This is consistent with the finding that continuation of alternative systemic therapy was the most common reason for nonimplementation. Furthermore, the high representation of biliary tract cancers, an entity with a comparatively high prevalence of actionable alterations, likely contributed to the observed rate of actionable findings and may limit generalizability to tumor spectra with lower targetability.

Our findings are broadly in line with national and international MTB cohorts, which consistently demonstrated modest overall implementation rates but meaningful benefit in selected subgroups [5–7, 13, 22, 26, 28]. Compared with these reports, the present series is characterized by a relatively high representation of hepatobiliary and lung cancers, providing complementary evidence to the existing literature.

Strengths of our study include the relatively large real-world cohort with systematic follow-up, detailed annotation of both ESCAT and ZPM evidence levels, and the evaluation of survival outcomes including the PFS ratio as a pragmatic surrogate for clinical benefit. In line with current methodological discussions on MTB evaluation, complementary end points beyond overall survival may better capture the clinical impact of precision oncology strategies in heavily pretreated populations [27].

Several limitations need to be acknowledged. The retrospective single-center design and the selective composition of the cohort entail inherent biases and limit generalizability. Molecular testing was heterogeneous, with different in-house and external panels used, which may have influenced the detection of actionable alterations. In addition, the lack of data on patients who underwent molecular testing but were not referred to the MTB represents a further source of selection bias. Barriers to implementation included rapid clinical decline shortly after MTB discussion, while in some cases recommendations may still be realized later during follow-up. Delayed access for external patients often prolonged workflows. Testing strategies and timing of presentation were heterogeneous, with some cases discussed to initiate profiling and others only after results were available. These factors illustrate the heterogeneity of MTB practice and contribute to variable turnaround times.

Driven by multiple targets and emerging therapeutic approaches—such as clinical trials evaluating KRAS inhibitors across cancer types, cell-based therapies including CAR-T cells, and the rapid expansion of antibody–drug conjugates—precision oncology continues to advance. As treatment options broaden, integrating MTB-based expert recommendations into routine care becomes increasingly important to translate robust evidence into timely clinical decisions. Earlier molecular testing, harmonized workflows, and faster access for external patients can help reduce delays, while sustainable reimbursement models and wider trial availability are essential to enhance real-world impact. Initiatives such as TEAM-D aim to harmonize and compare molecularly informed therapy recommendations across institutions, underscoring the need for shared interpretative frameworks [20]. In parallel, ongoing digitalization efforts facilitate the matching of molecular profiles with appropriate therapies and clinical trial opportunities [12].

Conclusions

The present cohort demonstrates that MTB-guided therapy can provide substantial benefit for selected patients, but implementation is frequently hampered by structural limitations and lack of high-level evidence. Continued efforts toward earlier testing, standardized workflows, and improved access to targeted options will be essential to maximize the impact of precision oncology in routine care.
