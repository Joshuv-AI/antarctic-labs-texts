Maternal homicide rates among 15 United States
Authors: Melissa Bright, Alyssa Amendola, Daniel Harris, Chris Delcher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPregnancy increases the risk of homicide, particularly for women facing intimate partner violence (IPV). Limited existing data suggests significant variation by state.MethodUsing data from the National Violence Death Reporting System (NVDRS), we analyzed maternal homicide by U.S. state from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2022. We assessed the prevalence of manners of death and proportion linked to IPV.ResultsFrom 47 states during the 5-year period, 637 maternal homicides were identified, for a rate of 5.33 per 100,000 live births. Firearms accounted for 78% of deaths, and 51% were associated with IPV. Only 15 states had sufficient data for reliable state-level rate calculations. Three states had 0 reported maternal homicides. Of the remaining 12, Louisiana had the highest rate at 12.64 per 100,000 live births while New York had the lowest at 2.86 per 100,000 live births.ConclusionsThe national maternal homicide rate aligns with prior research but reveals notable state-level differences where data were available. Restrictions on rate reporting for most states highlights a major gap in surveillance, limiting efforts to address this critical public health issue. Reliable state-level data is essential for understanding and addressing the factors driving maternal homicides.

Maternal mortality has emerged as a public health priority in the United States over the past two decades. The addition of a “pregnancy checkbox” to death certificates in 2003 had a noteworthy impact on maternal mortality surveillance [1]. Prior to this, maternal mortality could only be reported as deaths with the underlying cause of death assigned to International Classification of Disease (ICD)−102 codes A34, O00–O95, and O98–O99 ( ectopic pregnancy, infection, or other medical complications directly resulting from the pregnancy or childbirth). In the medical community, these deaths are categorized as “pregnancy-related.” Beginning in 2003, pregnancy status of all decedents was required on death certificates. These changes enabled maternal mortality surveillance systems to operationalize both the diagnosis code and pregnancy status to enumerate all deaths of pregnant and postpartum women, not only those with predetermined ICD-10 codes.

This pregnancy checkbox changed the landscape of maternal mortality surveillance by identifying all deaths during pregnancy or within 1 year of pregnancy, or pregnancy-associated deaths. Surveillance systems could identify non-obstetric medical causes of maternal deaths (i.e., deaths from comorbid conditions that were exacerbated by pregnancy such as cancer) and accidental and incidental causes of death (e.g., suicide, motor vehicle crashes, homicide, overdose) [1]. The pregnancy checkbox made it easier to identify and report on non-obstetric maternal deaths which contributed to an increase in the observed absolute incidence over time.

However, national (and international) agencies responsible for maternal mortality surveillance do not attribute these proximal causes to pregnancy and therefore exclude accidental and incidental deaths from maternal mortality rate (MMR) calculations. This is problematic because it limits public health opportunities to study many maternal deaths to from a prevention perspective. In fact, social scientists have repeatedly found that pregnancy is associated with a greater risk of homicide victimization, especially for women experiencing intimate partner violence (IPV) and maternal homicide in the US frequently exceeds rates of mortality from leading obstetric causes of death [2–4]. By excluding incidental causes of maternal death from MMR calculations, the federal government severely restricts comprehensive surveillance of maternal mortality, particularly for women and girls of color. In fact, most maternal mortality review committees (MMRCs), state-based committees – present in many but not all states - that study pregnancy-related deaths, do not review incidental causes of maternal death. Like obstetric causes of death, low-income women and girls of color are disproportionately impacted by homicide during pregnancy and postpartum, dying at double to quadruple the rate of their White peers [5].

Wallace and colleagues have documented an upward adjustment to the maternal homicide rate of approximately 6.2 per 100,000 live births when misclassification of pregnancy status on death certificates is rectified. Substantial variability in mortality rates have also been observed by time period and state of residence. For example, the maternal homicide rate in Louisiana (2017) was 16.9 per 100,000 live births, which exceeded mortality from any other obstetric cause of death, including infection (4.6/100k), preeclampsia (3.1/100k), and embolism (3.1/100k)[6]. This correction increased observed rates in North Carolina in 2005–2011 (maternal homicide rate of 6.2/100k)[7], in Maryland in 1993–2008 (maternal homicide rate of 9.3/100k)[8], and in Illinois from 2002 to 2011 (5.0/100k)[9].

Understanding state-level variability in maternal homicide is important for several reasons. First, because homicide is often overlooked as a pregnancy-related causes of death, many states fail to monitor this critical health measure. Second, statistical variability can inform research inquiries into what contextual, geographic, and political factors contribute to disparities in risk and/or protective factors associated with maternal homicide. Finally, many policy-level interventions that are expected to affect maternal homicide (e.g., firearm regulations, Medicaid coverage, Maternal Mortality Review Committees) vary by state. Understanding state-level differences is important for designing such policies and measuring their efficacy.

In the current study, we use the National Violence Death Registry System (NVDRS) to examine state-level maternal homicide rates, including newly available data on cases which occurred during the peak of the coronavirus pandemic in the US.

Method

Maternal homicide

Maternal homicide deaths that occurred between January 1, 2018 and December 31, 2022 were obtained from the National Violent Death Reporting System (NVDRS) Restricted Access Database (RAD). This timeframe was selected because of limitations in data availability for previous years. The NVDRS RAD is a de-identified, multi-state, case-level data set with data from death certificates, toxicology, medical examiner/coroner (ME/C) reports, and law enforcement reports. The latter two data sources include investigative report narratives to provide more context on the circumstances related to violent deaths. Manner is determined by the ME/C and, when considered in conjunction with the narrative cause of death statements on the death certificate, is the basis for how the official underlying cause of death is coded in vital statistics data. As of December 31, 2018, all 50 states submit data to the NVDRS but there are variations in reporting strategies (i.e., sampling vs. complete population reporting).

In the NVDRS RAD, the victim’s pregnancy status is defined from the death certificate and ME/C’s investigative report. Consistent with many public health surveillance systems globally [10], the NVDRS categorizes maternal homicide cases temporally as (1) pregnant at time of death, (2) not pregnant but pregnant within 42 days of death, (3) not pregnant but pregnant 43 days to 1 year before death, or (4) pregnant not otherwise specified.

Weapon type, according to the ME/C included: firearm, sharp instrument, hanging/strangulation/suffocation, blunt instrument, personal weapon, drowning, fall, fire/burn, motor vehicle, poison, and unknown/other.

IPV-related

NVDRS abstractors reference law enforcement, ME/C report notes, and the NVDRS codebook to determine if cases are IPV-related. Per the codebook, IPV-related cases are those in which the homicide is related to documented immediate or ongoing conflict or violence between current or former intimate partners. This includes all deaths where a victim is killed by their current or former intimate partner or, for example, the former intimate partner of a current intimate partner. Intimate partner is defined as a current or former boyfriend/girl, dating partner, ongoing sexual partner, or spouse.

Birth rates

Live birth counts were obtained from the 2018–2022 National Center for Health Statistics natality files. To estimate the number of times women gave birth, as opposed to the number of births, we divided the number of twin births by two and triplet births by three and combined with singleton births.

This study was determined exempt by Pearl Institutional Review Board.

Analysis

Aggregate calculation

We excluded California and Texas because the data captured represented less than 80% of the state population. The NVDRS dataset does not provide any data about Florida because the case reporting was deemed too incomplete for inclusion. That is, too many of the cases reported were missing significant amounts of information. These three states, which account for 27.2% of the population, were excluded from the denominator of our aggregate calculation.

We included data for 47 states (and District of Columbia (DC)) to calculate the maternal homicide mortality rate (Fig. 1). However, there was considerable variability in the yearly data contributed by each state over the study period. Therefore, we selected as many years as available to capture as many cases as possible. There were 36 states (and DC) where all five years were available and 12 states with less than five years: Hawaii (2 years), Pennsylvania, Illinois, Mississippi, Tennessee, Arkansas, South Dakota, and Idaho (each 3 years), and New York, North Dakota, Wyoming, and Montana (each 4 years).

Flow chart of states selected for analysis

The aggregate maternal homicide mortality rate (deaths per 100,000 live births) was estimated using the combined total maternal homicide cases contributed each year divided by total of live births for the 47 states (and DC) with complete data.

State-level calculation

NVDRS policy requires users to (a) suppress table cells < 10 events and (b) only report rates when >19 events occur in the reporting period (zeros allowed) or if the rate has a coefficient of variation that is < 23%, based on the assumptions of a Poisson distribution [11]. Thus, we were only permitted to report state maternal homicide rates for 15 states though cases from 47 states in the aggregate calculation. Based on these guidelines, approximately 35% of the US population lives in a state with suppressed data (i.e., 1–19 deaths during the study period or incomplete data). Demographic distributions were calculated by state.

For geographic mapping of state-level maternal homicide cases, we categorized by 0 homicides, 1–9 deaths, 10–19 deaths, and 20 + deaths. For the 15 states with reportable rates (including states with a rate of 0), we categorized the rates by quintiles. Analyses were conducted using R (R version 4.3.1).

Results

Overall

For the 5-year study period and the 47 states (and DC), we identified 637 women who died of maternal homicide, for an aggregate rate of 5.33 per 100,000 live births. Of these, 355 (56%) women were coded as pregnant at the time of death, 51 (8%) were not pregnant at the time of death but were pregnant within 42 days of death, and 227 (36%) were pregnant between 43 days and 1 year of death. An additional 4 (< 1%) women were pregnant, but timing of pregnancy relative to death was unspecified.

Most (55%) maternal homicide victims were identified as non-Hispanic Black, 25% were non-Hispanic White, 18% were Hispanic, 3% were two or more races, 2% were Asian, 2% were American Indian or Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, other/unspecified, or unknown race. The average age of maternal homicide victims was 26.4 years old (SD = 6.23).

Manner of death

The most common manner of death was firearm (n = 498; 78%), followed by sharp instrument (n = 64, 10%), hanging strangulation or suffocation (n = 33, 5%), blunt instrument (n = 16, 2%), and personal weapon (n = 10, 1%). Drowning, fall, fire/burn, motor vehicle, poison, unknown and other causes each had < 10 cases. Missouri, Michigan, and Ohio had the highest proportion of maternal deaths coded as firearm related (each with 89%) and New York had the lowest proportion of firearm related (50%).

IPV-related

Overall, 51% of homicide deaths were IPV-related. The remaining 49% were either not related or unknown (grouped together in the dataset). This includes all deaths where the homicide is related to documented immediate or ongoing conflict or violence between current or former intimate partner. Though low counts limit interpretability of differences between states, New York had the lowest proportion of IPV-related cases (n = 7, 29%) and North Carolina the highest (n = 29, 59%). Manners of death for IPV-related homicides were similar to overall homicides: 75% from firearms, 11% sharp instrument, 6% hanging, strangulation or suffocation, 3% from blunt instrument, and 2% or less from all other manners. Approximately 38% of maternal homicides that were both firearm and IPV-related. This proportion was highest in Missouri (42%) and lowest in New York (13%).

By state

The total number of maternal homicides by state are reported in Table 1. Only fifteen states met minimum criteria for calculating state rates during the reporting period (Fig. 2). Three of these states (New Hampshire, Vermont, Idaho) had 0 deaths (i.e., 0 per 100,000 live births). Among the remaining 12 states, Louisiana had the highest rate at 12.64 per 100,000 live births while New York had the lowest at 2.86 per 100,000 live births (Table 2). Discussion

We found an aggregate maternal homicide rate of 5.33 per 100,000 live births, which is comparable to other recent studies in the US[4]. The US consistently ranks worse than other high income and similarly developed countries in overall rates of maternal mortality [12]. Research on maternal homicidedeaths outside of the US is also limited. According to a systematic review of maternal homicide data from the early 2000 s, the US (maternal homicide rate = 2.9) was an outlier compared to a range of other countries including Bangladesh (maternal homicide rate = 0.39), Finland (maternal homicide rate = 0.70), and the United Kingdom (maternal homicide rate = 0.97)[13]. Additionally, the US has a significantly higher percentage (61%) of firearm-related maternal homicides compared to the other countries. For example, in the UK, where most maternal homicides victims died from stab wounds (57%) or strangulation (25%), firearm-related deaths are grouped with “other” manners of death and still only comprise < 20% of maternal homicides [13]. Cross-national comparisons, however, should be interpreted with caution due to potential differences in data quality, classification practices, and surveillance systems across countries and time periods. Moreover, such differences must be contextualized within each country’s legal framework, cultural norms, and structural factors—such as the markedly greater availability of firearms in the U.S. compared to the U.K.

More than half of women in the current study were known to be killed by an intimate partner. This is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that maternal homicides were more likely to be perpetrated by an intimate partner than homicides among women of reproductive age in the general population [14].

We found a rate of firearm deaths – 78% – comparable to other recent studies but higher than studies using data before 2020 [4]. Wallace et al. found that 69.2% of maternal homicide deaths from 2018 to 2019 involved a firearm but by 2020, Wallace (2022) found that 81% of maternal homicides were firearm-related. This increased rate coincides with the beginning of the Coronavirus pandemic. Early in the pandemic (March 2020-July 2020), during the emergency period when stay-at-home orders were strict, the observed number of firearm purchases exceeded the expected number by 4.3 million and gun violence rates were 30% higher than the same period the year prior [15]. Furthermore, excess firearm purchasing was positively associated with domestic violence-related firearm injuries and death, especially during April and May 2020 [16].

This variation in state rates highlights the need for state-level monitoring to inform recommendations for improved maternal care and solutions to preventing maternal homicides. Idaho, New Hampshire and Vermont each had zero recorded maternal homicides during the study period. Even though these states’ data may represent “true zeros” we must also consider the possibility of artifacts in data recording, surveillance bias, underreporting, and systemic factors.

First, it is possible that the lack of maternal homicides are data artifacts or that these states have some differences in the way deaths are recorded or reported. However, pregnancy checkboxes were used by all three states during the study period. In addition, all three states report using a multi-source method to identify deaths of women during or within a year of pregnancy. These sources include direct report from medical examiners, death certificate checkbox, and linking death certificates of women to birth certificates. It is possible, however, that these states more often record homicide deaths as undetermined intent than other states, or that they regularly record homicide deaths of pregnant/postpartum women as undetermined.

Second, it is possible that these states have fewer pregnancies resulting in fewer opportunities for maternal homicide at the population-level. All three states are consistently among the 15 least populated US states. However, the fertility rates (i.e., births per 1,000 females of childbearing age) of these states tell a different story. Although the fertility rates of New Hampshire and Vermont are consistently among the lowest in the U.S., the fertility rate of Idaho in 2022 was the 8th highest in the U.S. and was higher than the national average. That is, relative to the state population, there are more births per year in Idaho than in 42 other states. Thus, it does not seem likely that there are fewer opportunities for maternal homicide in Idaho.

Third, it is possible that these states experience fewer maternal deaths – death of a woman during or within a year of pregnancy – from any cause. Between 2018 and 2021, Vermont had 7 maternal deaths and New Hampshire had 23 maternal deaths [17, 18]. During this same time, Idaho had 42 maternal deaths [19]. Thus, it does not seem likely that the lack of reported maternal homicides are because of fewer overall maternal deaths, at least in Idaho.

Fourth, because most maternal homicides are firearm-related, it is logical to expect states with more restrictive gun laws would experience fewer maternal homicides. Indeed, Vermont has more restrictive gun laws than most U.S. states, but New Hampshire and Idaho have among the least restrictive gun laws [20–22]. Thus, it does not seem likely that the absence of reported maternal homicides in Idaho and New Hampshire are because of reduced access to firearms.

In sum, it seems plausible that the absence of any maternal homicide cases in Vermont and New Hampshire represent “true zeros.” The absence of reported maternal homicide cases in Idaho, however, could arguably reflect an error in classification or reporting. Adding to this complexity, Idaho has some of the most restrictive abortion policies [23] and discontinued its Maternal Mortality Review Committee (MMRC), a state-level committee that conducted annual reviews of maternal deaths to identify key areas for death prevention, in 2023 [1]. These combined legislative actions suggest that the systems for improving maternal health and surveillance of maternal death in Idaho could be improved.

Limitations

Only 15 states had sufficient data for calculating maternal homicide rates. Data from the remaining states (and DC) were suppressed based on NVDRS guidelines. The two reasons typically cited for suppression are to (a) maintain confidentiality of cases when low counts could reveal identity and (b) avoid reporting on statistically unstable data. The latter reason is particularly problematic for timely reporting of maternal homicides because most states do not observe more than 2 maternal homicides per year. Thus, it would take an impractically long period of time to publish and act on quantitative information on maternal homicide in the US. Thus, we urge state and federal agencies to examine the balance that these “rules” provide [24].

Three of the four most populated states (California, Florida, Texas) were not included in analyses because either too few counties in these states provide data or data quality was too low. These states represent 27% of the US population. Other data sources (e.g., NCHS) would likely allow for examination of these states because the data come exclusively from death certificates, and do not rely on data from law enforcement. However, most other states would still not be included because of the minimum data suppression rule. In addition, only the NVDRS allows for examination of contextual factors surrounding the homicide.

Another limitation is the potential for failing to identify IPV-related homicides. Local and state law enforcement agencies differ in the information they document around homicides and thus more homicides than are flagged may be related to IPV. Relatedly, another limitation is potential for misclassification of pregnancy status. Many pregnancies are spontaneously aborted in the first 12 weeks of gestation, often without a woman knowing she is pregnant. Thus, it is unlikely that women who had experienced spontaneous or induced abortions within the year prior to their death will be identified, creating an opportunity for these cases to be misclassified as non-pregnant/non postpartum. Linking NVDRS data to other administrative databases, such as electronic medical records, could provide more definitive evidence of recent/current pregnancy as well as other contextual information.

Finally, several other limitations of the NVDRS data warrant mention. The completeness of death registration and reporting to NVDRS varies by state and year, which could influence the representativeness of maternal homicide counts. Second, although the pregnancy checkbox has been included on the U.S. Standard Certificate of Death since 2003, there is documented variability in how consistently and accurately this field is completed across states and years [25, 26]. This introduces potential misclassification of pregnancy-associated deaths. Third, deaths of undetermined intent were excluded from our primary analyses, which may result in underestimation if some maternal homicides were misclassified due to insufficient investigative information. Fourth, in the analysis of intimate partner violence (IPV)-related deaths, contextual data may be missing or incomplete, limiting our ability to identify and code relationship dynamics and precipitating circumstances [27]. Collectively, these limitations likely bias our findings toward undercounting the true burden of maternal homicide, especially those involving IPV. Therefore, the estimates presented should be interpreted as conservative.

Public health implications

We found an aggregate maternal homicide rate comparable to other studies but uniquely observed significant variation in maternal homicide rates among 15 states for which data were reportable. The inability to report on maternal homicides rates for most US states severely undercuts the capacity for surveillance for this significant public health issue. To successfully understand the contextual, geographic, and political factors that contribute to maternal homicide, we must have the ability to identify and report state-level rates.

There also must be a standardized protocol for MMRCs to review maternal homicides and determine whether the deaths should be considered pregnancy-related. The number of pregnancy-related deaths and the causes of these deaths impacts the overall maternal mortality rate in the US. Standardizing the maternal homicide review process would improve our ability to mitigate the maternal mortality crisis in the US. Although this analysis does not fully explain why homicide is a leading cause of death during pregnancy and the postpartum period, it represents a critical step in the public health approach to prevention. By identifying when and where these deaths occur, and who is most affected, descriptive analyses like this one lay the foundation for future work that can uncover the underlying causes and inform effective prevention strategies to improve safety for women and families.
