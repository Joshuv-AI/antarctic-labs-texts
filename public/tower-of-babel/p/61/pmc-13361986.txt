Effects of dexmedetomidine vs. magnesium sulfate on hemodynamics in spine surgeries: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Eman Mohamed Teema, Ahmed Oun, Bassma Raslan, Sara Helmy, Mohamed K. Elghobashy, Omima Samaha, Aiman Al-Touny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlood loss and hemodynamic instability are common challenges in spine surgery, often managed with controlled hypotension. Dexmedetomidine (DEX), a selective α2-adrenoceptor agonist, and magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄), an NMDA receptor antagonist and calcium channel blocker, are commonly used for this purpose, but their comparative efficacy and safety remain unclear. This comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis aim to critically evaluate and compare the hemodynamic effects of both drugs in spinal surgeries.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, searching PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar up to August 15, 2025. We identified randomized controlled trials comparing intravenous DEX vs. MgSO₄ in spine surgeries. Outcomes included blood loss; changes in mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP); recovery time; first time to analgesic needs; total opioid consumption; extubation time; and incidence of bradycardia and tachycardia. Effect sizes were pooled using random-effects models.ResultsAnalysis of nine RCTs involving 463 patients showed that DEX significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss (MD = − 1.447 mL; p = 0.009), SBP (MD = − 0.448 mmHg; p = 0.012), tachycardia (RR = 0.330; p = 0.004), and prolonged the time to first analgesic request (MD = 1.409 min; p = 0.004). DEX was associated with an increased risk of bradycardia (RR = 4.819; p = 0.001) and delayed MAP recovery (p = 0.030). No significant differences were observed for MAP, HR, DBP, recovery time, postoperative opioid use, extubation time, or ephedrine requirement. Several outcomes showed high heterogeneity.ConclusionsOur study showed that dexmedetomidine offers superior blood-sparing and analgesic effects compared to magnesium sulfate but carries a higher risk of bradycardia and delayed MAP recovery. Magnesium sulfate remains effective for analgesia and muscle relaxation. Larger, well-designed RCTs are required to validate these findings and guide drug selection in spine surgery. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the extremely high heterogeneity observed across several pooled outcomes, which considerably constrains the reliability and generalizability of the estimates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-04009-1.

Introduction

Spine surgery, ranging from discectomy to complex fusion procedures, often results in significant blood loss, increasing the risk of transfusions, longer hospital stays, and higher morbidity and mortality [1]. Controlled hypotension, which reduces mean arterial pressure to a set range (typically 55–65 mmHg), is commonly used to address these risks [2, 3]. This technique enhances surgical field visibility, supports precise maneuvers, and may reduce operative time and blood loss [4]. Selecting appropriate pharmacological agents for controlled hypotension is essential, as each drug has unique effects on hemodynamics and patient outcomes.

Dexmedetomidine (DEX), a highly selective α2-adrenoceptor agonist, is increasingly used in anesthesia for its sedative, analgesic, and sympatholytic effects [5]. By binding to α2-adrenergic receptors, DEX produces a dose-dependent reduction in mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate, cardiac output, and norepinephrine release [6]. In spine surgery, DEX effectively induces controlled hypotension, helping maintain target MAP and improve surgical field visibility [7, 8]. Low-dose DEX infusion can alter intraoperative hemodynamics, with an initial drop in blood pressure linked to decreased cardiac index [9]. Although DEX may reduce blood loss, likely through α-2β adrenoreceptor-mediated vasoconstriction, it also increases the risk of intraoperative hypotension and bradycardia, especially with a loading dose [10, 11]. Both mild and severe bradycardia are more common with DEX use [12].

Magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄) has emerged as a potentially beneficial agent in the perioperative management of patients, particularly during spine surgery [13]. MgSO₄ stabilizes cell membranes, enhances the activity of sodium (Na⁺) ATPase and calcium (Ca⁺⁺) ATPase enzymes, and inhibits the release of norepinephrine by blocking N-type calcium (Ca⁺⁺) pathways. This contributes to a reduction in arterial blood pressure [14]. Although some studies indicate that MgSO₄ can induce controlled hypotension comparable to other agents, a consistent hypotensive effect or significant changes in blood pressure and heart rate compared to control groups have not always been evident [15, 16]. Nonetheless, MgSO₄ is well-regarded for its analgesic properties; it can reduce postoperative pain and opioid consumption while also improving patients’ verbal responses and orientation after surgery [13, 16]. Additionally, MgSO₄ may decrease the need for muscle relaxants [16].

The comparative efficacy and safety of dexmedetomidine (DEX) and magnesium sulfate (MgSO₄) in achieving controlled hypotension and maintaining hemodynamic stability during spinal surgeries are subjects of ongoing research. Some studies suggest that DEX is generally more effective than MgSO₄ in achieving controlled hypotension and providing better conditions for the surgical field [2]. Additionally, DEX may offer improved hemodynamic stability in certain spinal surgical contexts [17]. However, it is crucial to consider the potential drawbacks of DEX, such as a longer recovery period and a higher probability of bradycardia [2]. On the other hand, while MgSO₄ aids in analgesia and reduces the need for opioids, it may require the co-administration of other hypotensive agents to reach the desired blood pressure targets [14].

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the hypotensive and hemodynamic effects of intravenous dexmedetomidine versus intravenous magnesium sulfate in patients undergoing spine surgery.

Methods

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted for studies published till August 15, 2025, using PubMed, Scopus, Google Scholar, and Web of Science databases to evaluate studies comparing the effects of dexmedetomidine versus magnesium sulfate on hemodynamics during spine surgeries. It was registered on PROSPERO with the number (CRD420251149274).

The search strategy employed the following combination of terms: ((“dexmedetomidine” OR “Medetomidine” OR “MPV 1440”) AND (“Magnesium Sulfate”) AND (“spin*” OR “spine surgery” OR “discectomy” OR “laminectomy” OR “fusion”)). The full search strategy is available in detail (Supplementary file/Appendix 1). This systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviewers and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines 2020 (Supplementary file/Appendix 2).

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria encompassed (a) comparative studies of effects of dexmedetomidine vs. magnesium sulfate on hemodynamics in spine surgeries, (b) RCTs of effects of dexmedetomidine vs. magnesium sulfate on hemodynamics in spine surgeries, and (c) adults above 18 years old.

The exclusion criteria included articles not written in English, cohort studies, review articles, case reports, letters, and conference abstracts.

Study selection

Two reviewers manually screened the studies by title and abstract, followed by another two reviewers who screened them based on the full text. An independent reviewer then verified the results to ensure accuracy and to resolve any disagreements.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data from each included study, with a third reviewer verifying the data for accuracy.

Extracted data included year of publication, country of origin, study design, sample size, patient demographics, and outcomes (intraoperative blood loss, change in mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), time to restore MAP, recovery time, extubation time, time to first analgesic request and postoperative opioid consumption).

Quality assessment

The Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomized trials (ROB2) was used to assess the risk of bias of the included studies [18]. This tool evaluates five main domains: risk of bias arising from the randomization process, missing outcome data, risk of bias in measurement of the outcome, and risk of bias in selection of the reported result. The analysis was applied using Review Manager 5.4 Software. Five domains were evaluated: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, selection of the reported result, and overall risk of bias. Some studies have raised concerns, as shown in Fig. 1.

Publication bias was not evaluated using funnel plots or Egger’s test, as our meta-analysis included only 9 studies.

Each study was assessed based on these criteria. Two independent reviewers conducted the quality assessment, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were pooled for continuous outcomes, while risk ratios (RRs) were calculated for dichotomous outcomes. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for overall effect estimates. The DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model was applied.

Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic, with p < 0.10 for the Q test indicating significant heterogeneity. According to Cochrane guidelines, heterogeneity was categorized as low (I² ≤ 25%), moderate (I² = 26–50%), and high (I² > 50%). Statistical analyses were conducted using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) software, Version 4 (Biostat, Englewood, NJ, 2022).

Additional analysis

Subgroup analysis was performed, considering outcome measurement at different time points, and a more detailed examination of the results at specific intervals. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to compare dexmedetomidine with magnesium sulfate groups.

Additionally, a Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) assessment was conducted using the online GRADE pro tool. The GRADE approach evaluates the quality of evidence and the strength of recommendations, considering factors such as the risk of bias, heterogeneity, precision of results, and consistency across studies.

Results

Study Selection

Initially, a total of 154 articles were identified. After removing 35 duplicate studies, 116 articles were screened based on their titles and abstracts to assess their eligibility. Of these, 15 articles were found to be suitable for full-text review. During the full-text screening, 6 studies were excluded for reasons including non-English language, review articles, and non-open-access studies. Ultimately, 9 articles [9, 12, 19–25] were included in this review. Figure 2 illustrates the entire study selection process.

Outcomes

Outcomes regarding intraoperative blood loss, mean change in MAP, mean change in HR, and incidence of bradycardia are shown in (Tables 3 and 4). A meta-analysis of five studies was performed to evaluate intraoperative blood loss measured in mL. The pooled analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in blood loss in the dexmedetomidine group (mean difference (MD) = − 1.447 mL; 95% CI: − 2.526 to − 0.368; p = 0.009). However, the absolute magnitude of the difference was small and may have limited clinical significance. Heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 93.55%) (Fig. 3). A subgroup analysis was conducted using the sample size per group to examine heterogeneity. Notably, in studies with fewer than 25 patients per group, heterogeneity remained high (I² = 80.16%), whereas in studies with more than 25 patients per group, heterogeneity markedly decreased (I² = 6.22%) (Figs. 4 and 5).

Four studies were included in the meta-analysis assessing the mean change in Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) measured in mmHg. The pooled effect size did not show a statistically significant difference between groups, although a trend favoring dexmedetomidine was observed (MD = − 0.606 mmHg; 95% CI: − 1.376 to 0.163; p = 0.122). Substantial heterogeneity was detected (I² = 85.43%) (Fig. 6). Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method showed that omitting the study by Kabir et al. [9] reduced heterogeneity to (I² = 62.86%) (Fig. 7).

Five studies examined the mean change in heart rate (HR), measured in beats per minute. No statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups (MD = − 0.458 beat/minute; 95% CI: − 1.558 to 0.642; p = 0.414). Heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 94.22%) (Fig. 8).

The incidence of tachycardia, assessed in three studies, showed no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). Pooled analysis revealed a significantly lower incidence in the dexmedetomidine group (risk ratio (RR) = 0.330; 95% CI: 0.156 to 0.691; p = 0.004) (Fig. supplementary).

Four studies assessed bradycardia incidence. Dexmedetomidine was associated with a significantly higher incidence of bradycardia (RR = 4.819; 95% CI: 2.614 to 8.883; p = 0.001), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. supplementary).

Two studies assessed the mean change in systolic blood pressure (SBP) measured in mmHg. The pooled effect size indicated a significant reduction in SBP in the dexmedetomidine group (MD = − 0.448 mmHg; 95% CI: − 0.797 to − 0.099; p = 0.012), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I² = 0%) (Fig. supplementary).

Three studies evaluated the mean change in diastolic blood pressure (DBP) measured in mmHg. The pooled analysis demonstrated no statistically significant difference between groups (MD = − 0.320 mmHg; 95% CI: − 0.785 to 0.145; p = 0.177), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 61.48%) (Fig. supplementary).

Three studies were included in the analysis of time to restore MAP, measured in minutes. Dexmedetomidine was associated with a significantly longer restoration time (MD = 0.336 min; 95% CI: 0.033 to 0.640; p = 0.030). Heterogeneity was high (I² = 90.53%). Sensitivity analysis revealed that excluding the study by Kabir et al. [9] reduced heterogeneity to (I² = 0) (Fig. supplementary).

Three studies assessed the time to first analgesic request, measured in minutes. Dexmedetomidine significantly prolonged this interval (MD = 1.409 min; 95% CI: 0.448 to 2.369; p = 0.004). Heterogeneity was high (I² = 93.55%). Sensitivity analysis revealed that excluding the study by Refaat et al. [23] reduced heterogeneity to (I² = 70.91%), indicating that this study was a major source of variability (Fig. supplementary).

Two studies assessed postoperative opioid consumption measured in mg. Although dexmedetomidine was associated with a reduction in consumption (effect size = − 2.162 mg; 95% CI: − 5.081 to 0.757; p = 0.147), the result was not statistically significant. Heterogeneity was high (I² = 93.97%) (Fig. supplementary).

Two studies compared the need for ephedrine as a rescue drug measured in mg. No significant difference was observed (MD = 0.945 mgs; 95% CI: 0.056 to 16.044; p = 0.969), with high heterogeneity (I² = 90.83%) (Fig. supplementary).

Two studies assessed recovery time measured in minutes. No significant difference was observed between groups (MD = 0.504 min; 95% CI: − 1.627 to 2.635; p = 0.643). Heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 93.55%) (Fig. supplementary).

Extubation time was evaluated in two studies, measured in minutes. There was a trend toward a shorter extubation time in the dexmedetomidine group, but this trend was not statistically significant (MD = − 1.251 min; 95% CI: − 2.520 to 0.019; p = 0.054), and there was a high level of heterogeneity (I² = 89.69%) (Fig. supplementary).

GRADE assessment

Table 5 presents the GRADE assessments. The certainty of evidence was low for the mean change in mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate (HR), and intraoperative blood loss. Moderate certainty was shown for the incidence of bradycardia. Downgrading was primarily due to serious inconsistency and imprecision resulting from heterogeneity and wide confidence intervals. Studies exhibited no publication bias, as publication bias could not be formally assessed because fewer than 10 studies were included; the effect size was not large, and in some cases, the results showed considerable variability.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to compare the effects of dexmedetomidine and magnesium sulfate on hemodynamics and other relevant outcomes in patients undergoing spine surgeries. Our findings reveal several significant differences and similarities between the two drugs.

Our meta-analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in intraoperative blood loss in the dexmedetomidine group compared to the magnesium sulfate group (MD = − 1.447 mL; 95% CI: − 2.526 to − 0.368; p = 0.009). Although dexmedetomidine was associated with a statistically significant reduction in intraoperative blood loss, the absolute magnitude of the pooled effect was small, which may limit its clinical relevance. Furthermore, interpretation of this outcome is constrained by the very high heterogeneity observed across studies, suggesting substantial variability in study protocols, patient populations, surgical characteristics, and perioperative management. Therefore, the generalizability and certainty of this pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have highlighted the blood-sparing effects of dexmedetomidine in various surgical settings, including spine surgery [2, 12].

It is noteworthy that our study revealed significant heterogeneity (I2 = 93.55%). Subgroup analysis based on sample size confirmed that studies with more than 25 patients per group had lower heterogeneity (I2 = 6.22%); however, the overall high heterogeneity suggested variability in study designs, patient populations, or anesthetic methods. This is consistent with the results of Wang et al. [2], who found the effect of dexmedetomidine on intraoperative blood loss in spine surgery was highly heterogeneous in their meta-analysis. However, they did note a significant reduction in blood loss only in the subgroup that received total inhalational anesthesia.

On the other hand, there is less reliable information regarding magnesium sulfate’s ability to lower intraoperative blood loss during spine surgery, despite some studies suggesting a potential benefit due to its vasodilatory and anti-inflammatory properties [6]. Our meta-analysis examined the direct effect of magnesium sulphate on blood loss compared to dexmedetomidine, rather than a placebo or other agents. The better blood-sparing effect of dexmedetomidine in this situation is further supported by the observed difference in blood loss between the two groups in our study.

Our meta-analysis of four trials evaluating the mean change in MAP revealed a trend towards dexmedetomidine (MD = − 0.606 mmHg; 95% CI: − 1.376 to 0.163; p = 0.122), but no statistically significant difference between groups. The significant heterogeneity observed (I2 = 85.43%) could explain the lack of statistical significance in our pooled analysis. Omitting the study by Kabir et al. during the sensitivity analysis significantly reduced the heterogeneity (I² = 62.86%), suggesting that this study was either an outlier or that methodological differences affected the pooled estimate [9].

Our findings regarding dexmedetomidine’s effect on MAP concur with those reported in previous meta-analyses on spine surgery. Dexmedetomidine raises the incidence of intraoperative hypotension, according to some research [2, 26], particularly when used as a loading dose. This side effect is well known due to its sympatholytic effect. On the other hand, some proposed that it can reduce stress responses following surgery, hence promoting hemodynamic stability [13]. Possible explanations for this discrepancy include variations in dosing regimens, patient comorbidities, and the type of anesthesia used, as supported by Tian et al. [27].

Our research revealed no significant difference in MAP between the groups. Magnesium sulfate can lead to a decrease in blood pressure due to its vasodilatory effects [28]. Controlled hypotension is frequently the goal of its usage in spine surgery, and the dose and concurrent anesthetic drugs can affect how it affects MAP [29].

There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups, according to our meta-analysis of five trials assessing the mean change in HR (MD = − 0.458 beats/minute; 95% CI: − 1.558 to 0.642; p = 0.414). Significant heterogeneity was noted, likewise in MAP (I² = 94.22%). This finding contrasts with the well-established pharmacological profile of dexmedetomidine, which is known to cause bradycardia because of its central sympatholytic effects [30].

The substantial heterogeneity among individual studies, which contributed to the pooled analysis’s lack of a significant difference in mean change in HR, may reflect differences in study design, dosing protocols, anesthetic techniques, and patient populations. This variability may reflect differences across studies in dexmedetomidine dosage, infusion duration, and concomitant medications that affect heart rate. Consistent with its well-documented adverse effect profile, Wang et al. [2] demonstrated that dexmedetomidine increased the incidence of intraoperative bradycardia during major spine surgery, particularly when a loading dose was used.

Our meta-analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in the incidence of tachycardia in the dexmedetomidine group (RR = 0.330; 95% CI: 0.156 to 0.691; p = 0.004), with no evidence of heterogeneity (I² = 0%). This likely reflects dexmedetomidine’s sympatholytic effects, which reduce heart rate and prevent tachycardic episodes [30]. This finding is consistent and robust across the included studies.

There was a higher incidence of bradycardia in the dexmedetomidine group (RR = 4.819; 95% CI: 2.614 to 8.883; p = 0.001), without heterogeneity (I² = 0%). This is a well-characterized and expected side effect of dexmedetomidine, attributable to its potent α2-adrenergic agonism, which reduces sympathetic tone and enhances vagal activity [26, 30]. The consistent and significant increase in bradycardia highlights the need for careful monitoring and management of heart rate when using dexmedetomidine, especially in patients predisposed to bradycardia.

Magnesium sulfate, on the other hand, is generally not associated with significant changes in heart rate at therapeutic doses, but at high doses can lead to bradycardia [28]. The lack of a significant difference in HR between the two groups in our meta-analysis, despite dexmedetomidine’s known bradycardic effect, may be attributed to other mitigating factors affecting the mean HR, as the included studies might have had a low incidence of severe bradycardia requiring intervention, which would have minimized changes in the mean HR.

Our meta-analysis showed a significant reduction in SBP in the dexmedetomidine group (MD = − 0.448 mmHg; 95% CI: − 0.797 to − 0.099; p = 0.012), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%). This outcome aligns with the known hypotensive effects of dexmedetomidine, which acts on central α2-adrenoceptors to decrease sympathetic outflow, causing vasodilation [30]. The lack of heterogeneity in this analysis suggests a consistent SBP effect across the included studies.

Conversely, for DBP, our pooled analysis of three studies demonstrated moderate heterogeneity (I² = 61.48%), but no statistically significant difference between groups was found (MD = − 0.320 mmHg; 95% CI: − 0.785 to 0.145; p = 0.177). The disparity in effect between SBP and DBP may be attributable to differences in physiological responses to the medications, patients’ preexisting hemodynamic status, and the influence of concomitant anesthetics. The overall effect on DBP may be less pronounced or more variable depending on the patient’s vascular resistance and cardiac output, despite dexmedetomidine’s predominant effect on sympathetic tone, which influences both systolic and diastolic pressures.

Our analysis revealed that dexmedetomidine was associated with a significantly longer time to restore MAP (MD = 0.336 min; 95% CI: 0.033 to 0.640; p = 0.030). This finding is critical, as prolonged hypotension can lead to adverse outcomes. The high heterogeneity (I² = 90.53%) suggests variability across studies, and the sensitivity analysis indicates that removing the Kabir study reduced heterogeneity to (I² = 0%), highlighting the influence of individual study characteristics on this outcome. This prolonged restoration time is likely a direct consequence of dexmedetomidine’s sustained hypotensive effect, due to its prolonged half-life and sustained α2-adrenergic agonism [30].

In contrast, magnesium sulfate, while also capable of inducing hypotension, has a shorter duration of action and is easier to reverse [28]. These findings underscore the need to select patients carefully and tailor dexmedetomidine dosing in spine surgery.

Our meta-analysis of two studies evaluating recovery time found no significant difference between dexmedetomidine and magnesium sulfate (MD = 0.504 min; 95% CI: − 1.627 to 2.635; p = 0.643). The very high heterogeneity (I² = 93.55%) suggests that recovery duration is influenced by multiple perioperative factors beyond the anesthetic agent itself, including surgical type and length, patient comorbidities, and anesthetic protocols. Dexmedetomidine, known for its sedative properties, can delay emergence from anesthesia [31], while magnesium sulfate may prolong recovery by enhancing neuromuscular blockade [5]. However, in spine surgeries, their effects appear comparable, with perioperative factors likely outweighing drug-specific differences.

The time to first analgesic request was significantly extended by dexmedetomidine (MD = 1.409 min; p = 0.004). Its simultaneous sedative and analgesic effects are highlighted by this action, which is consistent with its α2-adrenergic agonism [32, 33]. Sensitivity analysis confirmed a consistent trend despite the substantial heterogeneity. Dexmedetomidine was more effective in prolonging analgesia duration, which likely reduced early opioid requirements and enhanced patient comfort. Of note, magnesium sulfate also exerts analgesic effects via NMDA receptor antagonism [34].

Our pooled analysis of postoperative opioid consumption showed a non-significant reduction with dexmedetomidine (MD = − 2.162 mg; p = 0.147), with high heterogeneity (I² = 93.97%). Although both drugs are well-established for their opioid-sparing effects [32, 34, 35]. The limited number of included studies and methodological heterogeneity constrained the robustness of our conclusions. Similarly, analysis of ephedrine use as a rescue drug showed no significant difference between groups, despite dexmedetomidine’s known tendency to cause hypotension and bradycardia. This finding suggests that intraoperative management strategies may have minimized the need for vasopressor support.

A trend toward shorter emergence time with dexmedetomidine was noted across two studies reporting extubation time (MD = − 1.251 min; p = 0.054), although this finding did not achieve statistical significance and was accompanied by high heterogeneity. Dexmedetomidine’s unique ability to provide arousable sedation may facilitate a smoother extubation process, whereas magnesium sulfate may delay recovery by potentiating neuromuscular blocking agents [5, 36].

The limitations of our meta-analysis include high heterogeneity across outcomes. Although we accounted for between-study variability, heterogeneity remained extremely high (I² > 90%) for several pooled outcomes, indicating considerable inconsistency among the included studies. Such levels of heterogeneity suggest that the observed effect estimates may be strongly influenced by differences in study design, patient populations, intervention protocols, outcome definitions, and follow-up durations rather than reflecting a single underlying effect. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity; however, heterogeneity remained persistently high even after sequential exclusion of individual studies/subgroup analyses, indicating that the inconsistency was not driven by a single outlier study alone. Consequently, the pooled estimates should be interpreted with considerable caution, as their reliability, robustness, and external generalizability are substantially limited. In this context, the summary effect sizes may serve primarily as exploratory or hypothesis-generating findings rather than definitive quantitative conclusions.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that both dexmedetomidine and magnesium sulfate have distinct hemodynamic profiles and clinical benefits. Dexmedetomidine appears to be more effective in reducing blood loss and providing prolonged analgesia, but its use is associated with a higher risk of bradycardia. Conversely, magnesium sulfate is more valuable for its analgesic and muscle relaxant effects without superiority on hemodynamic stability. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as heterogeneity remained extremely high across several pooled outcomes, substantially limiting the reliability and generalizability of the estimates. Further large-scale, well-designed randomized controlled trials are needed to establish the comparative efficacy and safety of these agents in spine surgery.
