Caring as a potential existential refuge from intimate partner violence during the breastfeeding period: a reflective lifeworld study
Authors: Ida Gustafsson, Lina Palmér, Katarina Karlsson, Aleksandra Jarling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIntimate partner violence (IPV) during the breastfeeding period endangers women's health and well-being. From a caring science perspective, it may be understood as suffering, potentially reduced through caring. This study aims to describe the meaning of caring, as experienced by women exposed to IPV during the breastfeeding period.MethodsNine women with experience of IPV during the breastfeeding period participated in lifeworld interviews, analyzed phenomenologically following the methodological principles of reflective lifeworld research (RLR).ResultsCaring, as experienced by women exposed to IPV during the breastfeeding period, means a potential existential refuge, further described by the constituents: an encounter requiring mutual invitation and trust; an opportunity to rediscover oneself when reflected in another's gaze; being met with insight influences the experience of exposedness; the possibility to get a break from exposedness; and being alleviated from loneliness when confirmed.ConclusionsFor care to be caring in this context it needs to be lifeworld-led and based on insights into the existential situation of breastfeeding women exposed to IPV. Through an encounter of mutual invitation and trust, caring has the potential to be an existential refuge, enabling rest, confirmation, reflection and escape from loneliness.Keywords: Breastfeeding, intimate partner violence (IPV), caring, women, lived experience, qualitative, reflective lifeworld research (RLR), phenomenology

Introduction

Breastfeeding has been shown to be potentially existentially challenging regardless of objective difficulties (Palmér, 2015). Support, especially from one's partner, is important in overcoming breastfeeding obstacles and in being able to breastfeed according to one's wishes (Koksal et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2024). However, not all partners are supportive. Sexual and/or physical Intimate Partner Violence (IPV) is experienced by one-third of women at some point in their lives (World Health Organisation (WHO), 2021), and IPV is common during childbearing (Bowen et al., 2005; Finnbogadóttir & Dykes, 2016). This is likely an underestimation due to stigma (Bhatia & Soletti, 2019; Murvartian et al., 2023; Wright et al., 2021), fear (Murvartian et al., 2023; Wright et al., 2021), and women's tendency not to identify as IPV victims despite meeting objective criteria (Crann & Barata, 2015; Wright et al., 2021). Research on the correlation between IPV and breastfeeding has shown varying results; however, IPV seems to impose a risk of reduced breastfeeding (Mezzavilla et al., 2018; Normann et al., 2020). IPV during the breastfeeding period has been shown to manifest as being accused, devalued, neglected, controlled, opposed, forced to adapt, and punished (Gustafsson et al., 2024), and is experienced as living in an existential exile (Gustafsson et al., 2025).

IPV can be physical, psychological, sexual, economical, material, digital, or social (United Nations (UN), n.d.). On a societal level, IPV has a negative impact on sustainability and economy. At the individual level, women are at risk of physical, mental, gynaecological, and obstetrical ill-health, isolation, and economic loss. Their children also risk negative mental and developmental consequences of the IPV (World Health Organisation (WHO), 2024). In Sweden, child healthcare services are voluntary, free of charge, and responsible for tracking and supporting children's development throughout their first 5 years of life. They are responsible for identifying signs that a child may be at risk of harm and for reporting such concerns to Social Services (Socialstyrelsen [Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare], 2014). Other levels of the healthcare system that women exposed to IPV during the breastfeeding period might access also have a duty to ask about IPV experience and provide appropriate care, but the duty to report is legally binding only when children are considered at risk (HSLF-FS 2022:39, 2022).

From a caring science perspective, experiencing IPV during the breastfeeding period is a kind of suffering. Caring can potentially elevate suffering and enhance health and wellbeing. Health, in this context, is not merely the absence of illness but an existential vitality that facilitates the achievement of life goals, balance, meaning, and a sense of context in life. Caring may originate from both natural and professional carers, as it is an aspect of the interdependency of being human (Arman et al., 2015; Bergbom et al., 2022; Eriksson, 2018). Professional caring is a profession neutral (Dahlberg & Segesten, 2010) development of natural caring provided by, for instance, relatives and peer supporters (Arman, 2022). However, not all care is experienced as caring, as it can include non-caring aspects. Non-caring may arise due to misunderstandings, stress, or lack of reflection (Dahlberg & Segesten, 2010). Women exposed to IPV generally wish for individual, empathic, respectful, and insightful care. They are generally positive about being asked about IPV but do not want to be pressured to answer or take action (Hargrave et al., 2024), especially when they do not trust the carers or their ability to protect against negative disclosure effects (Eikemo et al., 2024). However, what is experienced as caring is dependent on each individual's unique lifeworld; that is, the world as it is perceived, based on the individual's former experiences, dreams, and struggles (Arman et al., 2015). This study aims to describe the meaning of caring, as experienced by women exposed to IPV during the breastfeeding period.

Materials and methods

This study used a reflective lifeworld research (RLR) approach. RLR is rooted in the ontology and epistemology of Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty, Gadamer, and Ricouer, and views phenomenology and hermeneutics as parts of a methodological continuum to investigate meaning in phenomena through lived experience. The phenomenon of this study is caring when experiencing IPV during the breastfeeding period. The RLR methodological principles—openness, pliability, and bridling—assist researchers in remaining curious and focused on the phenomenon, while still being aware of their own pre-understanding and its impact on shaping their understanding going forward (Dahlberg et al., 2008).

Participants

Nine women with lived experience of IPV during the breastfeeding period participated in this study. They were 25–45 years old, born in Sweden, and had completed their post-secondary education. All but one had left the (heterosexual) relationship in which they had experienced IPV, which lasted from less than a year to more than 10 years. No participant was still experiencing IPV, and the experience occurred between one and more than 10 years prior to the interview. Experienced IPV types, manifestations, and frequencies varied within the group. The women had breastfed 1–4 children in total during their lives, of which one to two children during the period while experiencing IPV. The opportunity to breastfeed to the extent that they wanted varied.

Data collection

Data were collected by the first author (IG) through lifeworld interviews via telephone (2) or encrypted video meetings (7) in 2022 and 2023. According to RLR methodological principles, a lifeworld interview is focused on meaning and lived experience and is characterized by curiosity and openness towards the phenomenon and bridling of the researcher's pre-understanding, so as not to rush understanding during data collection and analysis (Dahlberg et al., 2008).

Data for several studies (Gustafsson et al., 2024, 2025) were gathered during the interviews. All interviews began with the open question, “Please tell me about your experience of exposedness in your partner relationship during the breastfeeding period”. Focus was then shifted to the caring experience through the question, “Please tell me about your experience of caring and support in this situation”. The question was deliberately non-contextual, to bridle the researcher's pre-understanding and to capture the meaning of caring, regardless of its source or manifestation. “Support” was mentioned, because in some breastfeeding- and IPV contexts it is employed more frequently as a term than “caring”. In this study, support is considered as an aspect of caring.

Answers were further deepened through follow-up questions such as “What did that mean to you?” and “Can you elaborate on that?”. It became evident that participants expressed both caring and non-caring aspects and that their experiences originated both from professional carers within healthcare, professionals within other societal support agencies, and natural carers, like friends and relatives. Occasionally, caring experiences originated from self-care. This is in line with the caring science view of caring as described above. The interviews lasted between 1 and 2 hours (mean: 88 minutes) and were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim by the first author.

Data analysis

The phenomenological analysis followed the aforementioned RLR methodological principles (Dahlberg et al., 2008). Given the pliability in both phenomenon and aim, the data were deemed suitable for phenomenological analysis. The transcribed interviews were read and re-read during the familiarisation phase (IG). Meaning units relevant to the aim were then marked and related to each other to reveal patterns of meaning in the data, which then guided cluster formation (IG). The analysis was led by openness and curiosity towards the data. Pre-understanding and premature understanding of the phenomenon were bridled through repeated reflection among the authors (IG, LP, KK, AJ). The clusters of meaning gradually illuminated the essential meaning (i.e., the invariant). Simultaneously, they differentiated into constituents that articulated variations of the phenomenon, collectively constituting the essential meaning. This unfolding involved a dynamic interplay of figure and background between the constituents and the essential meaning, each alternately foregrounded. In the results, the essential meaning—caring—a potential existential refuge—is described through its constituents: an encounter requiring mutual invitation and trust; an opportunity to rediscover oneself when reflected in another's gaze; being met with insight influences the experience of exposedness; the possibility to get a break from exposedness; and being alleviated from loneliness when confirmed. The findings include quotations for exemplification from all nine participants, translated into English, and slightly refined for clarity and anonymization.

Ethical considerations

This study is the third in a four-study research project that was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board in Linköping, Sweden (ref. no. 2022−01397−01), in accordance with national legislation. The two previous studies in the project explore women's experience of the manifestations (Gustafsson et al., 2024) and meaning of IPV during the breastfeeding period (Gustafsson et al., 2025), while the final study will examine the meaning of caring in this context as experienced by carers. In accordance with the Helsinki Declaration (WMA, 2024), participants consented to participate after receiving verbal and written information about their rights, the study's purpose and approach, and data management. To avoid sending sensitive data, and due to the remote interview format, verbal informed consent was obtained from the participants and documented in writing by the interviewer (IG) with a participant-chosen pseudonym, in accordance with the ethical approval. The WHO's ethical and safety recommendations on violence against women were continuously considered throughout the study (WHO, 2001, 2016). The remote interview format reinforced security and confidentiality, as the participants never had to reveal their locations. Data containing personal identifiers were minimized, and demographic data were stored and presented without relation to the lifeworld stories. The risk that participants would experience discomfort in sharing their experiences was considered to be outweighed by the benefit of studying a subject formerly unexplored in a lifeworld theoretical sense and the possibility that the women's stories would enable more caring care. However, contact details for support organisations were provided, participants were continuously offered pauses, and the interviewer (IG) ensured that participants had no questions and did not require additional support by lingering after the interview.

Results

Essential meaning: caring—a potential existential refuge

The meaning of caring, as experienced by women exposed to IPV during the breastfeeding period, is a potential existential refuge in which well-being can be nurtured. A “refuge” is a place or a state where caring enables shelter from the chaos, loneliness, and helplessness of the IPV experience. A refuge is a shelter where one can hide and rest while having the opportunity to experience relief through sharing the IPV experience—and the responsibility for its consequences—with someone who can receive and bear the weight of the story being told. For care to be caring, it is essential that carers are knowledgeable and experienced, but it is not enough if they lack insight into how to use their competence in the individual situation. Unreflective, insensitive, and standardized care counteracts the experience of refuge and risks empowering the partner instead of the woman, thus deepening women's exposedness.

Finding existential refuge is to meet carers who perceive what is hidden and hear what is left unsaid. It is possible to make choices in one's own life that are experienced as caring, but another person's gaze is needed to reach a sustainable existential refuge. There is always a risk that another's gaze could prove diminishing and reinforce feelings of loneliness, but such a gaze also has the possibility to empower by stimulating reflection. Experiencing an existential refuge, where well-being is nurtured through shelter, rest, sharing, insight, reflection, and empowerment, provides an opportunity to begin existing on one's own terms.

An encounter requiring mutual invitation and trust

Caring means an encounter that requires mutual invitation and trust, whether it comes from a relative, friend, nurse, therapist, police officer, or social worker. Not trusting the carer's competence or values may reduce the women's propensity to invite the carer to share their IPV experience, counteracting the encounter's potential to be caring. Experiencing a trustful encounter entails gratitude, but also surprise, because the women tend to have negative expectations based on their own or other people’s earlier experiences. One woman illustrates her distrust in the carer's competence to affirm her breastfeeding choice when she is feeling attacked by her partner after choosing to breastfeed longer than he preferred:

Suppose these arguments [with the partner] were escalating, and we had to speak to someone at the child healthcare centre about, like, “What's the benefits of breastfeeding?” If we had done that when she [the child] was one-, one and a half year old, I believe there is a significant chance that they would not truly have the knowledge.

Mutual trust entails believing that the carer has the ability to receive the IPV story in an empathetic, attentive, present, individual, and curious way and experiences that the carer believes the story. Non-caring in this context means being met with prejudice and a lack of sensitivity, which is both exposing and silencing. One woman recounts her experiences of an untrustful encounter with a professional in healthcare after her ex-partner accused breastfeeding of being responsible for their child's food refusal:

When you're completely out of sorts and the child won't eat, and then someone says, “Well, maybe you should stop breastfeeding”. I understand that it wasn't meant to offend//But that support, it became a bit misdirected sometimes.

Believing that encounters based on mutual invitation and trust really exist is the primary prerequisite for finding one. For the encounter to be caring, it requires time, space, continuity, and accessibility of a safe person or place. A stressful atmosphere makes deeper conversations impossible and counteracts the experience of caring. Continuous and relational caring, where trust builds up over time through multiple encounters with carers who keep asking, demand answers, and are prepared to stay committed when the story unfolds, is affirming, but this kind of caring is sometimes difficult to find. One woman experienced the following:

It took several years before I received proper care that assured me there was nothing wrong with me. You are exposed to violence.//In my opinion, I encountered quite a lot of incompetent people [professional carers].

An opportunity to rediscover oneself when reflected in another's gaze

Caring means an opportunity to rediscover oneself when reflected in another's gaze. Reflection may be an eye-opener, visualising one's own unsatisfied need for affirmation, love, and caring. One woman experienced this when a relative offered to cut her meat when she breastfed at a family dinner:

It was actually a bit confusing. Since that kind of attention to the fact that I only had one hand free, or was always hungry or thirsty or exhausted from lack of sleep, it was never acknowledged. My husband didn't care.

Caring may provide the woman with the strength, tools, and courage she needs to stand up for her own rights and needs and regain control of her life. To rediscover oneself when reflected in the gaze of someone living in a healthy relationship is to adopt a bird's eye view of one's own life situation. One woman experienced mutual reflection with another mother as caring.

I wrote and told her things [about the partner relationship and the breastfeeding situation] because I was genuinely unsure about my experience, and she confirmed that this is completely wrong, this is really crazy.

When having lost one's voice due to IPV, and thus being incapable of articulating exposedness, caring means being offered possibilities to regain one's voice through reflection. The reclaimed voice means freedom through the possible outlet of negative emotions and experiences. Caring is an opportunity to reconcile one's own perceived separate realities, re-gain access to one's own innermost thoughts, and re-learn how to exist for one's own sake. One woman illustrates rediscovering herself when writing in her diary:

I had a place where I could exist//even if I was diminished//But at the same time, no one else knew what was written there…It would have been much better if I had dared to share this with a friend, or, with healthcare professionals.

Being reflected as strong and competent in another's gaze offers hope for the future and the strength to continue living in a chaotic breastfeeding situation. However, experiencing IPV means being extra-vulnerable and adaptive to other people's opinions. When the other's gaze is diminishing and questioning, or when the carer is unable to articulate IPV suspicions in a way that is comprehensible to the woman experiencing IPV, this can mean risking losing oneself and experiencing amplified exposedness:

The worst thing I know is when someone pities me. In a way, I am pitiful, but I find there is something very demeaning about pitying someone.

Being met with insight influences the experience of exposedness

Caring means being met with an insight into IPV's existence and manifestations during the breastfeeding period, built on relevant, up-to-date knowledge and prior experience. When knowledge and experience are lacking, IPV risks passing unrecognized in the caring encounter, exacerbating the experience of exposedness. One woman recounts her experience of both caring and non-caring at the maternity ward when the staff knew that her partner had previously been violent:

I felt they placed some of the responsibility on me—like: “We tolerate no ‘fighting’ here”//But then there was someone who was…noticeably more knowledgeable. Who was firmer in her opinion that he must not misbehave here. Then I will throw him out.

Even though the women are both understanding and forgiving when meeting carers lacking insight and experience, it does not change the fact that care becomes non-caring when carers lack insight into their own limitations regarding their knowledge and ability. Being met with insight is caring because the risk that the partner will manage to manipulate the caring encounter is reduced. One woman illustrates why such an insight is needed.

He was always holding the baby when we were there [at the child healthcare centre]. He behaved as if he was taking care of the baby around the clock. And I really just adapted. I took a step back. And he weighed and measured [the child] and did all those things.

Caring is to be met in an individual way with the insight that partner relationships are not always healthy. When insight is lacking, and caring is focused on equality of rights and responsibilities in the partner relationship instead of empowering the woman, this risks exacerbating IPV exposedness. One woman illustrates the importance of IPV insight when communicating standard advice in maternal and child healthcare.

They try to involve the father and tell you that “You don't need to meet other people after the birth'//With a controlling [partner], it's like: 'We should do as the maternity ward said, we shouldn't meet people”. How could I argue against that?//Even though I would have needed to talk to my mum.

Care fails to be caring when women's attempts to receive breastfeeding support from their partner are misinterpreted by the carers as a lack of capacity on the part of the woman. Conversely, caring means being listened to even when nothing is said or when it is said in a low voice. Care also becomes caring through insight into the informal societal structures that form rules on gender roles and acceptable breastfeeding behaviour, which creates fertile ground for IPV. One woman reflects that postnatal screening instruments focus on the mother's mental well-being.

There doesn't seem to be an instrument to catch violent men. But there are instruments to check that we aren't crazy in the head, if I may exaggerate a bit.

The possibility to get a break from exposedness

Caring also means the possibility to get a break from exposedness. A break means being offered a caring place where one can rest and feel safe. A woman who sought help because breastfeeding was not working at home describes an appointment at a breastfeeding clinic where she could finally breastfeed in peace:

I'm sitting there with a calm, reassuring midwife who's about to carry out a breastfeeding observation…and suddenly, I have a baby who's nursing contentedly.

The caring “place” offered could be a physical place, but could also mean having one's needs and opinions supported when one no longer has the strength to stand up for oneself (for instance, through IPV documentation in the patient record, in case of future legal proceedings) or being spared direct confrontations with the partner. A woman who was exhausted, and thus urged to sleep by a doctor at the maternity ward, remembers being unable to independently get a break:

One would have wished…since I was incapable at that moment of getting my partner to listen to me//that she [the doctor] could have assisted [the partner to leave the room]//For me, it would have been beneficial.

The break from exposedness could also mean finding moments of normalisation and escapism in the midst of exposedness. Writing or talking to someone outside one's circle of acquaintances, implies a “vent” where reflection is allowed space and time. One woman illustrates how a break may give rise to further break opportunities:

I don't want to talk about my [IPV] exposedness every time I meet my friends, because there I want to be recharged with pleasant things. Instead, I've been able to have those continuous appointments with them [IPV specialized counsellors].

Getting a break when experiencing IPV during the breastfeeding period also means obtaining practical assistance. Being offered temporary accommodation or financial help provides an opportunity to relax and focus on the baby and breastfeeding. Caring is to have a backup plan or a safety net, implying immunity against deepened IPV exposedness and isolation. Caring is also receiving simple and clear communication and getting a break from the sole responsibility for the child, even if the shared responsibility can sometimes be challenging because of the notion that a good mother should be able to take care of her child independently. One woman describes getting a break when a healthcare professional saw her indecisiveness about giving formula, and decided for her:

That someone stepped in and said, “Well, let's do it this way”.//Or not “Let's do it this way”, but rather “I recommend this approach”. It was very…comforting.

Being alleviated from loneliness when confirmed

Finally, caring means being alleviated from loneliness, when confirmed. Confirmation is crucial because the experience of IPV during the breastfeeding period implies loneliness, even in the presence of others. Being confirmed is to feel as if one belongs and could mean meeting women with similar experiences and thus being saved from the loneliness of comparing one's own situation to healthy partner relationships. Confirmation that the relationship is not healthy may even give the strength to end the relationship. One woman recalls feeling confirmed in her breastfeeding choices when participating in social media groups:

Regardless of what the child's father said…I stood my ground…because of what felt right inside myself and because of the knowledge I had acquired in those groups.

Carers without IPV experience may also reduce the experience of loneliness if the woman's lifeworld story is heard and trusted. When the story is not confirmed, loneliness, chaos, and distrust persist. One woman recalls an appointment at the Child Healthcare Centre:

A situation had occurred that was quite threatening and aggressive//And I brought this up with her [the child healthcare nurse]. A cry for help, and a need for validation of what I am experiencing. And she didn't do much with that information, really.

Caring also means being confirmed that neither breastfeeding nor IPV are maternal responsibilities, but rather societal responsibilities. Loneliness is reduced by knowledge; thus, easily accessible IPV information and high IPV awareness in society are considered caring. However, the experience of societal reluctance to believe that IPV exists threatens both women and children, because it provides an opportunity for partners to continue their abuse. When women are met without an understanding that breastfeeding is beneficial for the child or that visitations with an abusive father could be harmful, the partner's right to the child is perceived to be valued higher than the child's rights. Meeting government officials who refuse to choose sides and prompt the woman to forget about IPV really means being rejected instead of confirmed, because the woman is left alone with the responsibility to protect herself and her child. As one woman relates:

They said to me, “Even if he beat you, he can still be the best dad in the world”. And I'm like. Yes, he may become a good father, but the title “the best dad in the world” is probably forfeited when you beat the mother.

Being alleviated from loneliness is to be confirmed in one's motherhood. When breastfeeding against the partner's will or choosing to breastfeed outside the norm without partner support, caring could mean being confirmed in the experience of well-functioning breastfeeding, thus reducing feelings of stress, shame, sadness, failure, and incompetence. However, one woman illustrates a situation in which the carer instead confirmed and empowered the abusive partner:

He [the partner] suddenly decided to start putting the child to bed, and the child screamed in panic because, “Where is mum?”. She wasn't used to him. The father brought it up with the child healthcare nurse. And she just scoffed and said, “But that's because the mother is breastfeeding”. It was extremely problematic.

Discussion

The results show that caring when exposed to IPV during the breastfeeding period, means a potential existential refuge. Refuge can occur when a woman is met with insight in a trustful and preferably continuous encounter with a natural or professional carer. Refuge could also originate from reflective and validating “self-care” activities, such as writing in a diary or through social media engagement with women who share similar experiences. Shared responsibility and practical support from authorities, healthcare professionals, and relatives offer a break and shelter women from loneliness. To our knowledge, this study is unique in its focus on the meaning of caring in this vulnerable context. Previous research on IPV and breastfeeding, although heterogeneous in validity and design, has mainly focused IPV impact on breastfeeding initiation and duration (Nigussie et al., 2025). Studies on the experience of IPV during childbearing have mainly focused on pregnancy and rarely on breastfeeding (Baird, 2015; Engnes et al., 2012; Finnbogadóttir et al., 2014; Fogarty et al., 2019; Hooker et al., 2016).

The essential meaning of caring as a potential existential refuge underscores the significance of intersubjective relationships between women and carers in the context of IPV during the breastfeeding period. Intersubjectivity is a central concept among several twentieth-century existentialist philosophers. Husserl emphasized our capacity for empathic perception, which enables us to sense the subjectivity of the other without direct access to his or her lifeworld, enabling a shared world (Husserl, 2020). Accordingly, carers possess the potential to respond to the women's desire to share both lived experiences and responsibilities—provided they remain open, empathetic, and attuned. However, the study results also reveal that the women particularly value sharing their experiences with others who have similar experiences, underscoring the importance of establishing dedicated forums for women exposed to IPV. Further, De Beauvoir highlighted the importance of regarding the other as a subject in her own right rather than uncritically reducing her to an objective “Other” through preconceived notions based on socially constructed roles (de Beauvoir, 2024). In the context of IPV during the breastfeeding period, it is essential that carers engage in critical reflection on their own pre-understanding and cultural lenses, to prevent these from unconsciously shaping the care provided to women. Within caring science research, such reflective practice has been emphasized as vital, both during (nursing) education and caring practice (Ekebergh, 2007). In Merleau-Ponty's philosophy, the lived body is central, intersubjectivity is embodied, and the presence of the other shapes our perception of the situation (Merleau-Ponty, 2023). This is in line with the results of this study, showing that the gaze of the other has the potential to be empowering, but this also risks deepening loneliness and exposedness when the gaze is diminishing or draws attention to differences in relation to the other. This emphasizes the importance of the carer remaining attuned and empathic to the woman's unique lifeworld.

However, it is striking how frequently the study results reflect care that is non-caring and insensitive to the women's lived experience, thus risking empowering the partners instead of the women. The results indicate that, in the context of breastfeeding, it is relevant to reconsider routine advice given to all new parents, as advice that is reasonable in some situations may be harmful in others. Prior research has shown that uncaring support from social workers risks enabling post-separation violence (Kjellberg, 2024); that non–trauma-informed therapeutic counselling is unhelpful (Barnes et al., 2024); and that women hesitate to reveal their exposedness if healthcare professionals are perceived as distressed, untrustworthy, or uninviting (Wallin Lundell et al., 2018). Caring care demands sufficient time and space in the caring encounter, as well as a permissive, supportive, and nurturing care organisation (or surroundings) (Wiklund-Gustin, 2022); a potential challenge in contemporary healthcare.

The current study shows that care becomes caring when carers have knowledge and insight into IPV and provide individually focused, existentially informed, and open-minded caring free from prejudice, shaming, and outdated views of male and female parenting roles. From a caring science perspective, being present and steadfast besides the suffering other, while managing one's own feelings of inadequacy, is a fundamental principle for care to be caring (Wiklund-Gustin, 2022). A caring encounter that strengthens health and well-being is characterized by the carer inviting the cared-for to a mutual presence, where openness and pliability to the other's individual lifeworld is in focus (Dahlberg & Segesten, 2010). Our results indicate that practical help is more highly valued than good advice and that both natural and professional carers could provide such help. Support from natural carers, such as relatives, friends, or colleagues when disclosing IPV exposedness, is associated with improved psychological health (Sylaska & Edwards, 2013). Caring in the form of social support has also been shown to protect women from additional IPV (Gerino et al., 2018), and support efforts to leave the violent partner (Zapor et al., 2015). Caring has multiple and unique meanings and is both a natural part of being human and an important focus for multiple professions (Arman et al., 2015). A carer may be either a natural or professional carer, but the professional carer is expected to act in a more reflective way and assume greater responsibility in the caring encounter (Dahlberg & Segesten, 2010). When carers dare to hear what is said in a low voice or see what is hidden, he or she has opened up to the “otherness,” as described by Gadamer (Gadamer, 1997), and gains the possibility to see beyond the own pre-understanding. This attuned caring approach carries the potential to offer an existential refuge for women exposed to IPV during the breastfeeding period.

Strengths and limitations

The lifeworld interviews contributed profound, varied and meaningful data, which is a strength of a study grounded in RLR. This study also fills a knowledge gap. Nonetheless, a generalisability limitation is the small number of participants, albeit common in qualitative interview studies. The participant group was also quite homogeneous, despite measures taken to seek participants through various channels. The limited participant demographic data collected due to ethical considerations also limits generalisability. However, video-meeting and telephone interviews enabled national recruitment, and generalisation in a phenomenological sense is primarily about identifying common patterns in lived experience, with contextual awareness (Dahlberg et al., 2008). The adopted openness towards the lived experience of the meaning of caring could be seen as a limitation in relation to generalisability and comparability with other studies, but a strength in relation to validity and objectivity, as openness is a core RLR concept (Dahlberg et al., 2008). It is also an example of the researcher's efforts to bridle their pre-understanding's effects on the study results. The use of quotes to ensure authenticity is another example.

The decision to collect data for multiple studies during the interviews was ethically motivated to reduce the number of interviews on a potentially triggering subject. This approach may have compromised the quality of the interviews because of potential strain placed on the participants. However, the rich data contradicts this assumption. Interviews via telephone and video meeting software are considered study strengths, as they enhance participant anonymity. However, this choice is somewhat controversial, as, in RLR research, immediacy and corporality in the interview situation are highly valued (Dahlberg et al., 2008). Post-pandemic research has challenged this perspective by showing that although video interviews require specific considerations, they could be preferable when studying sensitive subjects, such as IPV (Thunberg & Arnell, 2022).

Conclusions

Caring when experiencing IPV during the breastfeeding period means a potential existential refuge where one can live (and breastfeed) in peace when experiencing invitation, trust, and confirmation from both natural and professional carers. Caring is to have the possibility of reflecting, resting, and escaping loneliness when being met with insight. Care risks becoming non-caring and enabling IPV when women's lifeworld and breastfeeding wishes are ignored, or when care is standardized to fit all families. For care to be caring, it needs to be continuous, individualized and allow room for the woman's unique life-world story. These insights underscore the need to equip professionals who engage with these women with both comprehensive IPV knowledge and a caring space that enables genuine listening and recognition of each woman's unique situation. Equally important is the provision of reflective space for carers to examine their own pre-understanding, to avoid unintentionally deepening the women's sense of exposedness. Given that everyone holds the potential to be a natural carer, this awareness should preferably extend to society at large.

Ida Gustafsson is a PhD student in Caring Science at the Faculty of Caring Science, Work-Life, and Social Welfare at the University of Borås, Sweden. Her PhD project focuses on the existential aspects of IPV during the breastfeeding period. Her previous research mainly explored caring during breastfeeding.

Lina Palmér is an associate professor in Caring Science at the Faculty of Caring Science, Work-Life, and Social Welfare at the University of Borås, Sweden. She leads the research group, “Existential Issues in Caring and Learning Contexts”. Consequently, her research interests focus on existential issues related to caring, health, and well-being, particularly in the context of breastfeeding, childbearing, and care of older people. Lina is dedicated to advancing the field of existential Caring Science and developing its theoretical foundations.

Katarina Karlsson is an Associate Professor in Caring Science and Dean of the Faculty of Caring Science, Work Life, and Social Welfare at the University of Borås, Sweden. Katarina's research focuses on existential issues related to caring, health, and well-being. Her areas of research and expertise encompass the experiences of young children undergoing various medical procedures, augmentative and alternative communication, and children's rights in healthcare.

Aleksandra Jarling holds a PhD in Caring Science and works as a Senior Lecturer at the Faculty of Caring Science, Work Life, and Social Welfare at the University of Borås, Sweden. Her research interests include mental health and existential care. Aleksandra's doctoral thesis highlights homecare for older people from multiple perspectives, a research area that she continues to explore and develop.
