The role of NETosis in breast cancer: mechanistic insights and biomarker potential
Authors: Fahimeh Norouzi, Pooya Eini, Safa Tahmasebi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeutrophil extracellular trap formation (NETosis), previously described as an effector host defense mechanism, is more frequently associated with cancer-driven inflammation and tumor development. In the case of breast cancer, NETosis assists with several oncogenic processes, including epithelial-mesenchymal transition, immune evasion, organotropic metastasis, angiogenesis, and resistance to therapy. The current study summarizes the evidence of the mechanistic role of NETs in breast cancer and their potential to act as a diagnostic, prognostic, or therapeutic biomarker. Like many other promising candidates for novel prognostic or diagnostic biomarkers, citrullinated histone H3 (citH3), myeloperoxidase-DNA (MPO-DNA) complexes, and circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) need further validation to be functional. However, there is some evidence suggesting clinical relevance. Like many candidate therapeutic indices, several therapies are exploring targeting NETosis, including deoxyribonuclease I (DNase I) and peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) inhibitors, as a clinical intervention. However, methodological differences across studies and a lack of standardized detection of NETs are likely impeding future findings. Future insight into the efficiency of detection methods and consistency of experimental design will be beneficial for transferring NETosis to the clinic.Keywords: Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), Breast cancer, Biomarkers, Metastasis, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality. In 2020, approximately 2.3 million new cases were diagnosed, resulting in 685,000 deaths globally [1]. Incidence varies significantly across geographic regions and socioeconomic contexts, highlighting disparities at both national and local levels [1, 2]. Despite advances in screening, early detection and treatment, which have improved survival rates, the biological heterogeneity of breast cancer remains a significant barrier to effective clinical management [2–4]. Central to breast cancer progression is the tumor microenvironment (TME), a dynamic network of cancer-associated fibroblasts, immune cells, extracellular matrix components and signaling pathways [4]. The TME not only sustains tumor growth but also drives intratumoral heterogeneity and therapeutic resistance. As highlighted by Hanahan and Weinberg (2023) in their updated hallmarks of cancer, the TME promotes cancer cell plasticity and adaptability, contributing to phenotypic diversity and resistance to conventional therapies [5]. Within the TME, neutrophils play a critical yet often overlooked role, exhibiting dual functionality in both host defense against tumor progression and tumor-promoting activities, modulated by the TME’s signaling landscape [6]. A key mechanism of neutrophil action is the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)—web-like structures of decondensed chromatin, histones and antimicrobial proteins released during NETosis, a distinct form of neutrophil cell death [6, 7]. Initially identified as an antimicrobial defense mechanism, NETosis is now recognized as a pivotal mediator in inflammation-driven pathologies including cancer [8]. In addition to cancer, NETosis is pivotal in the pathology of various inflammatory and autoimmune disorders, such as systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and severe COVID-19, as it leads to immunothrombosis, tissue injury, and the disease progression. In sepsis, NETosis is a significant cause of disseminated intravascular coagulation and organ dysfunction, pointing to the broader pathophysiological significance of defective NET formation outside cancer [9–12].

Three NETosis pathways—suicidal, vital and mitochondrial—differentially influence cellular and tissue dynamics, disrupting processes such as tumor growth, extracellular matrix remodeling, immune evasion and metastasis [7, 8, 13–18]. In breast cancer, NETs exhibit subtype-specific roles, with pronounced activity in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) compared to estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) tumors [19]. In TNBC, NET formation correlates with increased tumor size, elevated Ki-67 proliferation indices, and lymph node metastasis [20]. NETs reshape the TME by polarizing tumor-associated macrophages toward an immunosuppressive phenotype and enhancing cancer-associated fibroblast invasiveness, thereby fostering tumor progression [21].

Emerging evidence also identifies NET components—such as citrullinated histone H3 (citH3), neutrophil elastase (NE)-DNA complexes and cell-free DNA (cfDNA)—as potential biomarkers for monitoring disease progression [19–21]. Preclinical studies suggest that targeting hyperactive NETosis through interventions such as DNase I (DNase I enzymatically cleaves the phosphodiester bonds of extracellular DNA scaffolds that comprise the structural backbone of NETs [22, 23]) administration or peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) (PAD4 inhibition prevents the citrullination of histone arginine residues required for chromatin decondensation and subsequent NET extrusion [24, 25]) inhibition may exert antimetastatic effects [20]. However, translating NET research into clinical practice faces challenges, including assay standardization and specificity [21]. Innovative technologies, such as microfluidic platforms and multi-omics approaches, offer promising solutions to enhance the sensitivity, speed and accuracy of NET detection and analysis, facilitating their integration into clinical settings.

Although several reviews have focused on the role of NETs in breast cancer, our review provides a unique synthesis of the review literature by integrating the depth of the mechanism, the potential of biomarkers, and the clinical applicability of the final product into a single review. First, we present an in-depth detail of the molecular and cellular mechanisms linking NETosis to breast cancer progression, the development of a pre-metastatic niche, metastasis, and therapy-resistance by summarizing the latest experimental work in this area. Second, we include a focused review of NET-related biomarkers and assess their value for diagnosis, prognosis, and prediction, while also discussing the current limitations of standardization of assays for NET-associated biomarkers. Third, we covered the latest preclinical models, including patient-derived organoids (PDOs) and organ-on-chip platforms, as promising new models to investigate NETs, in line with the recent effort by the NIH and FDA to use human-relevant models. Finally, we assessed the therapeutic approaches to reduce NET activity, focusing on precision medicine with biomarker guidance. This comprehensive analysis allows our review to address an unmet need for a mechanistic understanding of NETs in breast cancer and the clinical adoption of NET-targeted therapies.

This narrative review elucidates the mechanistic contributions of NETosis to breast cancer pathophysiology, evaluates NETs as potential biomarkers and explores their translational potential as novel therapeutic targets.

NETosis mechanisms and molecular markers

NETs are intricate networks of decondensed chromatin decorated with antimicrobial proteins, including neutrophil elastase (NE), myeloperoxidase (MPO), and histones. Released through NETosis—a programmed cell death process distinct from apoptosis and necrosis—NETs are generated in response to diverse stimuli, such as pathogens, immune complexes, and inflammatory cytokines [7, 26]. During NETosis, activated neutrophils undergo morphological changes, including chromatin decondensation, nuclear envelope disassembly and translocation of granular enzymes (e.g., NE and MPO) into the nucleus [26]. Once sufficiently decondensed, chromatin is extruded into the extracellular space following plasma membrane rupture [26, 27]. NETosis encompasses three mechanistically distinct pathways: suicidal (lytic), vital, and mitochondrial NETosis, each with unique morphological and functional characteristics (Fig. 1). Suicidal NETosis, typically triggered by phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), occurs 4–6 h post-activation and relies on reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation via the NADPH oxidase pathway, culminating in neutrophil lysis [14]. In contrast, vital NETosis, often induced by activated platelets or microbial products, enables NET release without neutrophil death. This pathway involves histone citrullination by PAD4, vesicular chromatin transport, and NET extrusion, with the neutrophil remaining intact [7, 28]. Mitochondrial NETosis, a non-canonical pathway, involves the extrusion of mitochondrial DNA rather than nuclear DNA, triggered by mitochondrial calcium ionophores that induce rapid ROS production and mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening [29, 30]. External stimuli like calcium ionophores (e.g., ionomycin), complement factor C5a, and some autoantibodies can activate mitochondrial NETosis, especially in the context of an autoimmune disease or inflammation. In the presence of any of these stimuli, mitochondrial ROS generation and mitochondrial permeability transition occur, rapidly releasing oxidized mitochondrial DNA often without plasma membrane disruption [29, 31–34]. Citrullinated histone H3 (citH3), generated via PAD4-mediated conversion of arginine to citrulline, is a highly specific and physiologically relevant marker [35, 36]. MPO, translocated to the nucleus during NETosis, contributes to chromatin decondensation through its enzymatic activity but may also cause collateral tissue damage [37, 38]. Similarly, NE translocates to the nucleus, cleaving histones to facilitate chromatin relaxation. Notably, NET formation is impaired in the absence or inhibition of NE, underscoring its essential role [39, 40]. Collectively, citH3, MPO, and NE serve as key molecular markers of NETosis, linking physiological defense mechanisms to pathological inflammation, including in cancer.

The three main modes of NETosis: suicidal, vital, and mitochondrial, each providing different functions in immune responses. Suicidal NETosis is studied by three hallmarks: chromatin decondensation, rupture of the nuclear envelope, plasma membrane lysis leaving wounds of neutrophil death, and release of nuclear DNA in a NET. Vital NETosis releases NETs through vesicles, and it retains the viability of the cell robotically, but neutrophils are left anucleated. Mitochondrial NETosis selectively expels mtDNA and maintains nuclear integrity while also leaving the cell depleted of mitochondria. The mechanistic philosophies of NETosis represent the functional heterogeneity of the neutrophil's roles in defense of the host while also illustrating how improperly regulated NETosis could support cancer growth or contribute to tissue damage

NETs in breast cancer: mechanisms of progression, metastasis and therapy resistance

NETs have a complex role in the progression of breast cancer by regulating epithelial plasticity, inflammation, immune evasion, metastasis, angiogenesis, and resistance to therapy. NETs were initially recognized as an innate immune response against pathogens, but NETs have now been shown to be involved in many pathophysiological processes in cancer.For instance, exposure of Michigan Cancer Foundation-7 (MCF7) breast cancer cells to NETs downregulates epithelial marker E-cadherin while upregulating mesenchymal markers N-cadherin, fibronectin, and vimentin, enhancing metastatic potential [16, 41–43]. In the context of inflammation and host immune response, NETs drive chronic inflammation by activating nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling, which induces interleukin-8 (IL-8) expression, creating a positive feedback loop that sustains an inflammatory microenvironment [42, 43]. NETs capture circulating tumor cells (CTCs) by interaction with β1-integrin to protect from immune clearance as they enter circulation and use similar mechanisms to enhance CTC extravasation. Extermination of NETs with DNase I in murine models disrupts CTC adhesion and decreases metastasis [44, 45]. NET formation also facilitates organ-specific metastasis. They promote brain metastases by compromising the blood–brain barrier and modulating osteoclast and osteoblast activity to facilitate bone colonization [46]. In addition to supporting local tumor progression, NETs also promote metastasis using organ-specific mechanisms. In the case of brain metastasis, NET-associated proteases, such as NE and MMP-9, can degrade tight junction proteins, including claudin-5 and occludin, which would increase blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability and tumor infiltration [47, 48]. Within the bone microenvironment, NETs also alter bone remodeling in favor of osteoclast differentiation and activity, thus creating an osteolytic niche for tumor colonization [49]. NETs create lungs and liver pre-metastatic niches by remodelling the extracellular matrix, increasing tumor cell adhesion, and modulating immune cell recruitment, thus supporting metastasis seeding [44, 50].

Beyond metastasis, NETs reshape the TME to promote angiogenesis and immune suppression. NETs release pro-angiogenic factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A), angiopoietin-1 and -2 (ANGPT1/2), and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), which stimulate endothelial cell proliferation and vascular remodeling [41, 51]. NETs impair anti-tumor immunity by polarizing monocytes into immunosuppressive macrophages, inhibiting dendritic cell function, and degrading T-cell signaling factors via neutrophil elastase (NE) and arginase-1. Additionally, NETs upregulate immune checkpoint molecules, such as programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1), on tumor and immune cells, facilitating immune evasion [16]. NETs hinder anti-tumor immunity by polarizing monocytes into immunosuppressive macrophages, inhibiting dendritic cell function, and degrading T cell signaling factors using NE and arginase-1 [52, 53]. Furthermore, NETs upregulate checkpoint molecules like PD-L1 in tumor and immune cells [52, 54]. PD-L1 is a transmembrane protein that binds programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) on activated T cells, providing inhibitory signals that suppress cytotoxic T cell proliferation, cytokine production, and survival. PD-L1 overexpression in cancer provides tumor cells a mechanism of evading immune surveillance that functionally exhausts tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. This checkpoint pathway is a primary target for immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies), which have clinical benefits in several solid tumors, including triple-negative breast cancer. Induction of PD-L1 by NETs is central to creating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that inhibits anti-tumor immune responses and limits immunotherapy efficacy [55–57]. NETs also contribute to resistance to therapy. Chemotherapeutic agents like doxorubicin and cisplatin induce IL-1β secretion. IL-1β is one of the initiators of NET formation and activates TGF-β signaling, which is connected with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) as well as chemoresistance [41, 58]. NETs also inhibit the infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and the cytotoxic potential of natural killer (NK) cells to limit immunotherapy effectiveness [42, 43, 59]. This is the product of the interaction of many cytokines, chemokines, and their receptors, as this effect was mediated through NF-κB and toll-like receptor signaling through damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [41–43]. NETs also promote cancer-associated thrombosis through activation of platelets, thrombin generation, and fibrin deposition [60–62]. With their multifactorial effects, NETs provide a clear therapeutic target. Preclinical studies demonstrate that DNase I and PAD4 inhibitors reverse NET-mediated resistance and reduce metastasis [16]. NETs further act as a structural scaffold in the TME, facilitating TAN-tumor cell interactions to promote the required adhesion, invasion, and metastasis. Tumor cells can adhere to NETs through integrin α5β1 and the CCDC25 receptor, and blocking these interactions reduces adhesion, indicating therapeutic benefits of blocking [63]. NET-derived DNA binds to CCDC25 on tumor cells, activating integrin-linked kinase (ILK), β-parvin, RAC1, and CDC42 signaling, which drives cytoskeletal rearrangements to enhance tumor cell motility and invasion [65].

The role of NETosis in the premetastatic niche

NETosis significantly contributes to the formation of premetastatic niches (PMNs) in breast cancer by remodeling the ECM and modulating the immune landscape of distant organs, facilitating metastasis [66]. Through structural and immunological mechanisms, NETs create a tumor-permissive environment, offering potential therapeutic targets to disrupt metastatic progression.

NETs drive ECM remodeling by releasing proteolytic enzymes, such as NE and MMP-9, which systematically degrade ECM components [67]. This degradation exposes cryptic sites, reactivating dormant tumor cells and promoting their proliferation within the metastatic cascade [66, 68]. Additionally, PAD4-mediated citrullination of ECM proteins, including fibronectin and collagen, enhances tumor cell adhesion by inducing conformational changes that facilitate colonization. These proteolytic and post-translational modifications dismantle structural barriers, creating a microenvironment conducive to metastatic growth [68]. NETs also shape the immune landscape of PMNs by recruiting and activating immunosuppressive cells. NET-associated proteases degrade thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1), diminishing its tumor-suppressor activity and fostering an immunosuppressive TME [66]. NETs trap CTCs in distant organs, aiding extravasation and colonization [16]. NET-derived DAMPs (DNA, histones) and proteases (NE, MPO) activate pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on stromal cells, inducing cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) and stellate cell activation in liver and pancreas, remodeling the ECM for a tumor-permissive TME [6, 69]. They also recruit myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) via CXCL1/CXCL2, suppressing T-cell activity and enhancing breast cancer stemness and metastasis through IL-6/STAT3 and NO/NOTCH signaling [70]. NET-derived signals reshape the extracellular matrix and reprogram the stroma and myeloid compartment of the PMN landscape to form a tumor-permissive PMN. Notably, two cell types that are typically recruited and functionally activated in NET-rich niches are CAFs and MDSCs. CAFs are a heterogeneous population of activated fibroblasts within the tumour stroma that secrete components of the ECM, matrix-remodelling enzymes, and multiple pro-tumourigenic cytokines/chemokines (e.g., IL-6, TGF-β, CXCL12). CAFs govern ECM architecture, induce cancer-cell invasion, initiate angiogenesis, and provide local immunosuppression. In contrast, MDSCs represent a pathologically expanded, immature myeloid population that has the potency to suppress T-cell and NK-cell function and promote non-immunological, pro-metastatic events (angiogenesis, matrix remodelling, and niche conditioning) [65, 71, 72].

On a mechanistic level, NETs can activate or attract these immune and stromal cells in several synergistic ways. NET-associated DNA, histones, and proteases (NE, MPO, MMP-9) act as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that cause PRR/TLR signalling on stromal fibroblasts and stellate cells, activating them to a CAF-like, matrix-remodelling phenotype, as well as more secreted ECM ligands and chemokines that further stiffen and permissivize the niche. NETs and the tumour/host secretome elevate CXCR2 ligands (CXCL1, CXCL2, CXCL5/8) and IL-6 within the pre-metastatic milieu; these chemokines recruit CXCR2-expressing PMN-MDSCs and facilitate MDSC-mediated activation of STAT3 in cancer cells, both inhibiting anti-tumour immunity and enhancing cancer stemness and metastatic competence. In doing so, NETosis enables the establishment of a network of direct ECM signalling mediated by NETs and indirect amplification of mode of action where CAFs and MDSCs combine their activities in order to create an immune-suppressed, adhesive, and ECM-remodelled PMN that is favourable for circulating tumour cell seeding and outgrowth. NETosis contributes to organ-specific metastasis mediated by unique microenvironmental mechanisms in each target organ [19, 54, 73–75].

In the lung, one of the most frequent metastatic sites for breast cancer, chronic psychological or physiological stress induces pulmonary epithelial cells to secrete acetylcholine, which upregulates NET formation and accelerates ECM remodeling, thereby establishing a permissive premetastatic niche. NETs interact directly with tumor cell β1-integrins, enhancing adhesion to the lung parenchyma and promoting invasion into the alveolar interstitium. Additionally, NET-derived proteases degrade lung-specific basement membrane components such as laminin-332, facilitating extravasation and colonization. Lung-resident immune cells, including alveolar macrophages, further amplify this effect by secreting chemokines that recruit additional neutrophils, sustaining a cycle of NET release and ECM degradation [76–78].

In the liver, TNBC-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) upregulate CX3CL1 expression in hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells, recruiting CX3CR1+ macrophages that secrete MMP-9 to remodel the ECM. PAD4-mediated citrullination of key ECM proteins in the space of Disse increases tumor cell adhesion to hepatocytes and stellate cells, while NETs also promote the activation of hepatic stellate cells into a myofibroblastic phenotype, further stiffening the ECM and supporting metastatic cell survival. Kupffer cells interact with NET components to produce proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, which sustain an inflammatory yet tumor-permissive hepatic microenvironment [79, 80].

In the bone, NETs modulate osteoclast differentiation and resorptive activity by releasing NE, MPO, and cathepsin G, which not only degrade the bone matrix but also liberate growth factors like TGF-β stored in the bone ECM. These factors stimulate metastatic breast cancer cell proliferation and survival. NET-driven osteolytic niche formation also disrupts osteoblast function, tipping the bone remodeling balance toward bone resorption. Furthermore, NET-induced release of IL-6 and RANKL enhances osteoclastogenesis, amplifying the vicious cycle of bone destruction and metastatic colonization.

By tailoring their effects to the molecular and cellular landscape of each organ, NETs orchestrate the creation of specialized PMNs that optimize metastatic colonization efficiency.

Therapeutically, targeting NETosis to disrupt PMN formation is promising. DNase I degrades NET DNA, reducing lung metastases in murine models without affecting primary tumor growth [13, 35]. PAD4 inhibitors, such as GSK484, suppress NET formation and metastatic burden in PSGL3 breast cancer models [81]. Given DNase I’s existing clinical approval for other indications, its repurposing for breast cancer warrants further investigation [15].

Based on prior experience with murine models that express P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1) in aggressive forms of breast cancer, we found that PAd4 inhibition with GSK484 decreased NET formation, reduced metastatic burden, and was associated with enhanced survival. While GSK484 has been studied as a highly selective PAD4 inhibitor and radiosensitizer with investigation in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) models, it appears to augment irradiation-mediated inhibition on proliferation, migration, and invasion. The PAD4-mediated hypercitrullination reaction in neutrophils catalyzes the release of nuclear chromatin to form neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), and studies show that PAD4 deletion from the 4T1 breast cancer cell line decreased tumor growth and lung metastasis. Our murine models have aggressive TNBC phenotypes, and PSGL-1-mediated interactions enhance neutrophil-driven metastasis via NETs. PSGL-1 is a mucin-like selectin counterreceptor expressed on neutrophils and other leukocytes that bind P-selectin, E-selectin, and L-selectin [19, 82].

Recombinant human DNase I (dornase alfa, marketed as Pulmozyme®) is an FDA-approved treatment for cystic fibrosis and acts to enzymatically cleave extracellular DNA in mucus, lowering viscosity and promoting pulmonary function. Dornase alfa is a highly purified solution of a single recombinant human deoxyribonuclease I (rhDNAse) with selective and specific DNA cleaving activity, first approved by the FDA in 1993. The drug is inhaled as a nebulized solution. Dornase alfa catalyzes the hydrolysis of DNA to degrade the viscosity of airway secretions and promotes clearing asthma-related airway mucous plugging. Preclinical oncology and surgical oncology studies have secured and demonstrated reduced tumor-associated thrombosis and metastasis following DNase I treatment in tumor-bearing mice. Additionally, reduced metastatic spread was reported using systemic administration of DNase I in models of stress-induced metastases. The therapeutic benefits of DNase I treatment extend beyond oncology, as notable data and evidence show dornase alfa effectively reduces lung inflammation and hospital discharge time in treated patients with COVID-19 pneumonia. Clinical trials utilize nebulized dornase alfa to clear extracellular chromatin, providing a known systemic inflammatory response to NETs and improving COVID-19 pneumonia patient outcomes [83, 84].

Sustained expression systems depend on adeno-associated virus (AAV) for the adaptation of DNase I delivery systems to address the short half-life of DNase I. AAV-DNase I delivery systems, which expresses liver specific AAV-DNase I in mouse models yielded effective numbers of DNase I near liver tolerable levels which inhibit metastasis while maintaining renal protective functions in cancer-affected situations, rapid clearance of DNase from serum, and partial neutrophil inhibition post treatment in tumor tissue. The vector for AAV-mDNase I consisted of a capsid that delivered KP1 and, in an expression cassette, made a hyperactive model of murine DNase I under the regulation of liver-specific promoters, as this facilitated elevated expression and serum activity of murine DNase I for at least 8 months. In colorectal cancer models, AAV-mediated liver gene transfer of DNase I following single intravenous injection suppressed development of liver metastases compared to control, with evidence of less neutrophil infiltration and NET formation in tumor tissue, and evidence of restored local immune responses in the tumor sites by having greater CD8+ T cell percentages compared to the controls not having diminished capacity for CD4+ T cells [72, 73].

In addition to approaches that aim to block NET DNA, alternative approaches targeting (PAD4)-mediated extracellular modification (PEM) are under investigation. Some studies have investigated the potential use of CXCR2 inhibitors to inhibit IL-8-mediated NETosis and inhibit tumor growth in pre-clinical models. CXCR2 is a key neutrophil chemokine receptor that is important for regulating neutrophil behavior and potentially NETosis. Antagonism of CXCR1/2 has been shown to decrease NETosis, thrombosis, lung injury, and mortality in experimental models of sepsis. The CXCR2 antagonist SCH527123, in combination with oseltamivir, has been shown to inhibit NETosis and protect from lethal influenza infection. Multiple studies found that IL-8 (CXCL8) and CXCL1, -2, and -8 recruit neutrophils following interaction of these agents with the CXCR1 and two receptors on the neutrophil membrane. These stimuli are shown to induce NETosis in human neutrophils as well as granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells [82, 85].

Together, these treatment modalities—DNase I (conventional injections or AAV delivered), PAD4 inhibitors (such as GSK484), and potential adjunct therapies aimed at NET-inducing cytokines—represent a range of diverse mechanisms for inhibiting NET-mediated metastasis. The translational potential options for these strategies, particularly in combination with indications in immunotherapy and chemotherapy for breast cancers (especially TNBC), demonstrate a wide range of potential opportunities for targeting NETs to improve outcomes [72, 82].

NETosis in breast cancer subtypes: a subtype-specific mechanism

Luminal A and B breast cancers, classified as immunologically “cold” tumors, exhibit low tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) levels and subdued immune activation. Luminal B tumors display a more immunosuppressive TME than luminal A, with elevated regulatory T cells (Tregs) and Th17 cells, and reduced NK and Th1 cell infiltration, contributing to limited immunotherapy efficacy [86]. Neutrophil infiltration and NETosis are less pronounced in luminal subtypes due to their less inflammatory TME, though limited studies suggest NETs may contribute to therapy resistance, particularly in luminal B tumors [86, 87]. HER2-enriched breast cancers present an intermediate immune profile, with increased TILs, PD-L1 expression, and enhanced immunotherapy responses [88]. NETs promote angiogenesis in HER2-positive tumors by releasing VEGF-A and damaging vascular endothelial cells, driving proliferation and neovascularization. This effect is amplified under hypoxic conditions, where hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) upregulate VEGF-A, accelerating tumor growth and metastasis [65, 89].

TNBC exhibits a highly inflammatory TME, marked by elevated TILs, PD-L1 expression, and extensive neutrophil infiltration, predominantly N2-type tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) that drive tumor progression through NETosis [86, 90]. NETs facilitate cancer cell migration and invasion via DNA fibers and neutrophil-derived factors, such as NE and MMP-9 [86]. Tumor-derived granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) and extracellular vesicles (EVs)—lipid-bilayered nanoparticles like exosomes and microvesicles released by tumor cells—trigger NET formation. EVs carry pro-NETotic cargo, such as G-CSF, HMGB1, mitochondrial DNA, and NE that trigger neutrophil activation via Toll-like receptors (e.g., TLR4, TLR9) and PAD4-dependent pathways. This results in ROS generation and chromatin decondensation, initiating NETosis in neutrophils and thereby facilitating ECM remodeling and pre-metastatic niche formation [91, 92]. On the other hand, NETs activate TGF-β signaling by releasing MMP-9, which cleaves latent TGF-β in the TME. Activated TGF-β upregulates transcription factors (SNAIL, TWIST, ZEB1), suppressing E-cadherin and promoting mesenchymal phenotypes, thus driving EMT and metastasis [93, 94]. TGF-β also modulates non-coding RNAs, including lncRNA MALAT1 and miR-181b-5p, which enhance TNBC aggressiveness [95]. Additionally, IL-8, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, recruits neutrophils to the TME via CXCR2 receptors, triggering NETosis and establishing a positive feedback loop that amplifies IL-8 production, EMT, immunosuppression and tumor progression [95, 96].

In estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) breast cancers, NETosis is less prevalent due to a less inflammatory TME. In this cancer subtype, TAMs secrete TGF-β, which triggers EMT and enhances invasiveness, potentially conferring resistance to endocrine therapies like tamoxifen [97]. Although there is a paucity of direct evidence linking NETs and tamoxifen resistance in ER+ breast cancer, the interrelationships of NETs, TGF-β activation, and EMT represent plausibly an indirect mechanism through which NETs could contribute to therapeutic resistance to tamoxifen.

Notably, NE-DNA complexes, alongside NETs, outperform traditional biomarkers like carbohydrate antigen 15-3 (CA15-3) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) in correlating with regional and distant metastasis and recurrence risk [46, 98]. Unlike CA15-3 and CEA, which are elevated in 73% and 55% of metastatic breast cancer cases, respectively, but lack specificity due to elevation in benign conditions, NET biomarkers better reflect tumor-host immune interactions [19]. Furthermore, NET-associated gene expression signatures correlate with immunotherapy response, highlighting their potential as predictive biomarkers for targeted therapies [98]. Despite their promise, clinical implementation of NET biomarkers faces challenges, including lack of standardized assays, variability in sample processing, and false positives due to NET induction in inflammatory conditions [19, 99]. Multiplexing NET biomarkers with tumor-specific antigens could further enhance diagnostic specificity and clinical utility across diverse contexts. NET-mediated pathways in breast cancer progression. NETs promote tumor growth and metastasis through various mechanisms, including immune modulation (T-cell suppression, dendritic cell dysfunction, and monocyte differentiation), secretion of pro-tumorigenic cytokines (IL-8, TGF-β), and activation of transcriptional regulators (SNAIL, TWIST, ZEB1, lncRNAs). NETs also contribute to angiogenesis, osteoclast/osteoblast modulation, and disruption of the blood–brain barrier, facilitating distant metastases to bone and brain

NETosis biomarkers are promising for breast cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment monitoring, but their use in the clinical setting must be accompanied with the caution surrounding specificity and caveats regarding interpretation [19, 100]. Using multiplexed biomarkers for NETosis holds considerable promise for improving the clinical utility of NETosis biomarkers, as it has been demonstrated that the combination of two biomarkers, e.g., MPO-DNA complexes with CitH3, can outperform single biomarkers for diagnostic purposes [101, 102]. Multiplex ELISA approach including antibodies against MPO, CitH3, and DNA has demonstrated increased sensitivity and specificity for detecting NETs, with some assays achieving an accuracy of > 90% [101].

Interpretation must consider specificity, as several biomarkers for NETosis are not specific to NET formation. Evidence published to date supports elevated neutrophil elastase, calprotectin, and citrullinated histone H3 in various inflammatory states, including inflammatory bowel disease, cardiovascular disease, autoimmune diseases, and other malignancies [103–106]. Calprotectin is elevated in various inflammatory conditions, with studies indicating significant elevation in familial Mediterranean fever, chronic kidney disease, and various cancers beyond breast cancer. The confirmation of the elevations noted above implies the need for clinical correlation and consideration of the overall inflammatory state of the patient [104, 105].

Advanced detection technologies have greatly improved the precision of NETosis biomarker detection systems. Digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) is even more amenable to quantification analysis of cfDNA and has greater sensitivity and absolute quantification abilities than standard PCR methods [107–109]. Flow cytometry-based NET detection allows for real-time characterization of neutrophil activation and the NET process, while multiplex immunofluorescent techniques allow for single-cell comparative detection of multiple components of NETs. These technologies have allowed for extremely sensitive detection of circulating NET remnants, but institutions can still struggle to standardize protocols [110, 111].

The clinical pertinence and therapeutic consequences of evaluating NETosis biomarkers go beyond diagnostic applications. NETosis biomarkers could assist clinical decision-making in various aspects of patient care through (1) prognostic stratification, as higher MPO-DNA complexes and CitH3 concentrations were correlated with lower progression-free survival and increased chance of metastasis; this could provide the means to determine whether certain patients are at higher risk and require more aggressive treatment, (2) monitoring treatment response, as serial evaluations throughout treatment of the NETosis biomarkers that were measured throughout this experimental study may provide early indications to the effectiveness of the therapeutic regimen (specifically for anti-NET therapies and immunotherapeutic approaches), (3) patient selection for anti-NET therapies, as suggested therapeutic approaches taking advantage of NETs via DNase I and NET inhibitors being developed could also benefit from a selection of patients based on the assessed biomarkers, (4) distinction from overall inflammation, as assessing NET biology would allow distinguishing tumor-associated NETosis from additional inflammatory processes, rather than just relying ont he overall inflammatory markers (such as C-reactive protein or interleukin-6) [19, 100, 112–115].

Therapeutic targeting of NETs is an exciting new avenue in cancer treatment, with preclinical studies showing that DNase I treatment can reduce metastatic burden in breast cancer models [113]. Creating adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated DNase I gene therapy and other anti-NET approaches highlights the potential value of NETosis biomarkers in the clinic for treatment selection and monitoring. Clinical trials using these approaches are now starting to emerge. Still, we await the development of robust clinical practices involving NETosis biomarkers that will require further data to establish both the predictability/reliability of the biomarkers and the therapeutic effectiveness of the interventions. We may then see these methodologies incorporated into routine clinical practice, although multi-site clinical trials based on practice patterns and standardized protocols will be needed to test their efficacy, reliability, and route of clinical application.

The incorporation of NETosis biomarkers into clinical practice needs to be reiterated with larger multicenter studies with standardized procedures and equal consideration of pre-analytical variance, assay standardization, and analytical and clinical interpretation [72, 114].

NETs as potential biomarkers in breast cancer

NETs are recognized as important features of tumor biology, especially in breast cancer, where they have been shown to play a role in tumor initiation, metastasis, and alteration of the tumor microenvironment. Table 2 presents a selection of NETosis-associated biomarkers and their relevance to breast cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy monitoring. There is now some burgeoning interest in NETosis-related biomarkers (citH3, MPO-DNA complexes, and cfDNA) as potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. CitH3 is released during NETosis via citrullination of histone H3 by the enzyme PAD4. While direct evidence linking higher levels of citH3 to breast cancer is limited, there have been studies in pancreatic and endometrial cancer that have confirmed the association between increased levels of citH3 and poor prognosis, suggesting the potential for citH3 to be a prognostic biomarker in breast cancer [46, 116]. MPO-DNA complexes, a second NET-specific marker, are also increased in the circulation of patients with malignancies. In breast cancer, higher concentrations of MPO-DNA complexes have been linked to tumor progression and early liver metastases, suggesting they are valuable in non-invasive monitoring of disease and risk profiling [117]. cfDNA is also a marker that was identified as a product of multiple forms of cell death, including NETosis and is increased in cancer patients and associated with tumor burden. While cfDNA is not an exclusive marker of NETosis, as demonstrated in endometrial cancer, the positive correlation between cfDNA and citH3 levels suggests that the two indicators should be used collaboratively to represent NET activity more closely. Additionally, a liquid biopsy that detects cfDNA is a minimally invasive way to assess the patient [116–118].

A multi-marker approach, particularly combining citH3, MPO-DNA complexes, and cfDNA, is likely more specific for breast cancer and prognosis. These combinations have been previously able to distinguish patients with cancer from healthy individuals with other cancers. Current clinical trials for breast cancer are more narrowly focusing on NET biomarkers. However, increased levels of NE-DNA complexes have been reported, together with NETs, over classical blood biomarkers such as carbohydrate antigen 15-3 (CA15-3) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) to correlate with patients with regional and distant metastasis and recurrence risk. Gene expression NET signatures correlating with response to immune therapy suggests potential for promising targeted treatment biomarkers [46, 98]. In contrast to current standards of care biomarkers CA15-3 and CEA, which are not specific and are also higher in benign disease, NET biomarkers will likely better differentiate tumor-host immune response. For example, CA15-3 was elevated in 73% and CEA in 55% of women with metastatic breast cancer. Again, neither biomarker is sensitive or specific enough for clinical use [19]. They may still have potential. However, there are barriers to NET biomarkers entering clinical practice: lack of standardized assays, processing and detection variability, and NETs can be produced due to inflammatory disease, increasing false positive risk. Methodological issues aside, calls have recently been made to establish standard operating procedures (SOPs) for sample collection, processing, and biomarker detection. NET markers could also be multiplexed with tumor antigens for specificity and, thus, greater clinical applicability in different contexts. The NET biomarkers citH3, MPO-DNA complexes, and cfDNA may offer potential as prognostic and diagnostic tools. They would create a cancer-specific patient assessment, stratification, and treatment if they could be used in practice. Moving forward, studies must address assay standardization and increased diagnostic specificity and prove they work in large cohorts of patients from diverse clinical settings [19, 99].

Patient-derived organoids and advanced 3D models for studying NETosis in breast cancer

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) are a paradigm shift in three-dimensional cell culture technology and exist as more advanced biological systems preserving cellular heterogeneity, architectural complexity, and molecular features of parent tumor tissues. The robustness and ability of self-organizing cell aggregates to exist as a more sophisticated in vitro system are challenged by two-dimensional cell cultures, limited to reproducibility, genetic variation, and spatial organization above the cell layer without being translated into cellular organization representative of tumor stroma. Animal models can vary across species, and some differ for ethical reasons. PDOs provide an experimental platform relevant for human interactions where tumor-targeting organoid variants exist with patient-specific genomic alterations, standard and experimental expression, and deliberate therapeutic vulnerabilities [119–122].

Due to recent regulatory measures from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and Food and Drug Administration (FDA), developing alternatives to the animal model by the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) in biomedicine, the clinical relevance of PDOs has taken a momentous turn. These organoid systems have limitations relevant to conventional preclinical systems based on biologically relevant platforms, which provide information with predictive ability for patient therapeutic response. The regulatory landscape is aligned with and facilitates the incorporation of organoid technologies in precision oncology research and practice. Drug sensitivity testing based on PDOs offers concordance rates of 70–90% with clinical outcomes and provides a valuable tool for determining personalized treatment stratifications [119, 121, 123].

Breast cancer PDOs have been effectively established across all major molecular subtypes (i.e., luminal A and B, HER2, and triple negative breast cancer) with organoids preserving their unique molecular signatures and treatment sensitivities in organoid culture. In particular, TNBC PDOs provide better than any other PDO system recapitulation of MYC-driven basal-like phenotypes and luminal progenitor-like populations, which can be utilized to study highly aggressive tumor biology and therapeutic resistance mechanisms. HER2-positive PDOs also maintain authentic HER2 expression patterns and exhibit expected responses to targeted therapies such as trastuzumab, while hormone receptor-positive PDOs maintain relevant estrogen and progesterone receptor expression patterns that remain within the variation reported among patient samples during extended periods of culture [120, 124, 125].

The evolution of immune-enhanced PDOs signifies a momentous stride in developing models of tumor-immune interactions that will provide important tools for the study of NETosis. These advanced systems incorporate patient-matched peripheral blood mononuclear cells, including neutrophils, T cells, and other immune-related aspects, to form complete tumor microenvironmental models that facilitate real-time study of immune cell behaviors. The organ-on-chip models take these capabilities a step further by incorporating microfluidic systems to control cellular interactions and dynamic capabilities to monitor neutrophil recruitment, migration, or NET formation processes. Advanced co-culture systems have successfully demonstrated that viable neutrophils can be maintained in organoid structures. Such studies have confirmed that neutrophils retain the expression of neutrophil-specific markers while responding appropriately to stimuli. This enables researchers to characterize the complex interactions between cancer cells and neutrophils, interactions that determine how neutrophils infiltrate the tumor microenvironment and subsequently initiate NET formation [126–128].

Models for PDOs have been important to clarify the NET-mediated mechanisms of breast cancer initiation and development, with evidence in the literature showing that in PDOs, neutrophil patterns of infiltration, NET formation, and NET-mediated effects on tumor cells are replicated. The 3D structure of organoids is more accurate in recapitulating how NETs interact with these cancer cells and promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition, influence metastasis potential, and mediate drug resistance. PDO studies have demonstrated that NET constituents, such as neutrophil elastase and DNA-histone complexes, have an impact on cancer cell invasion and migration, their role in pre-metastatic niche formation, and their ability to drive dormant cancer cell reactivation; all of which are challenging to study in 2D culture systems [19, 128, 129].

The highly refined organoid platforms are a great way to test potential anti-NET therapeutic modalities, allowing us to screen multiple NET targeting measures, including DNase I, PAD4 inhibitors, elastase inhibitors, and histone-targeting drugs. The ability to co-culture patient-matched immune cells along with organoids allows us to simultaneously identify how anti-NET therapies impact neutrophil activity and cancer cell behavior. Many studies have demonstrated the value of organoid systems in testing combination therapies targeting cancer cells and NET-forming neutrophils. For instance, treatment studies with Dihydrotanshinone I in breast cancer organoids demonstrated significant suppression of NET formation and cancer cell viability [72, 127, 130].

Organoid models yield benefits that include high fidelity regarding patient tumors, keeping the genetic, epigenetic, and phenotypic features intact, with greater than 85% concordance of driver mutations compared to the original tissues. Organoids also provide an advantage in maintaining the heterogeneity and intratumoral diversity that are often biomarker characteristics of patient cancers. Organoids can also incorporate immune cells, enabling a more thorough approach to studying complicated immune-tumor interactions. Due to their scalability, organoids can be built, expanded, and cryopreserved for downstream applications, allowing the ability to perform high-throughput drug screening. The utility of various imaging modalities allows researchers to monitor dynamic processes in real-time, such as NET formation and the migration of immune cells. Nevertheless, some challenges remain with this model system, specifically, the ability to provide an adequate representation of the tumor microenvironment, and despite continual technological advances, incorporation of a whole tumor microenvironment is not yet possible. Organoid models also cannot capture the ramifications associated with systemic treatment or interactions between distant organs. Furthermore, technical standardization continues to be an issue due to widespread variability among protocols that affect reproducible practices. The cost, complexity, and culture techniques needed for organoids represent a challenge over the traditional culture techniques researchers have experience with and depend upon. Finally, time is a deciding factor for future research projects, as prolonged culture times may eventually lead to genetic drift or loss of individual cell populations [121, 131–133].

The clinical translation potential of organoid models represents a valuable link between discovery and application regarding targeting NETs in cancer therapies. Importantly, these systems have specific utility in predicting biomarkers of drug resistance mediated by NETs, and for predicting responses to therapy, allowing for patient-specific drug-sensitivity testing that would assist in selecting patients who would most benefit from NET-targeting therapeutic interventions. The use of PDOs and their predictive ability have been validated in multiple clinical studies, providing validity for the utility of PDOs in developing companion diagnostics to guide the use of NET targeting agents in specific patient populations. Organoid biobanks are now being designed and formulated to provide tools to facilitate larger research developments and clinical trial designs, creating living biorepositories of tumor characteristics of patient categories and allowing researchers to focus on molecular signatures correlating with NETs to develop more suitable interventions. We anticipate harmonizing data of organoid-derived effects on specimens and biology with databases of clinical outcomes, which will synthesize drug discovery processes needed to bring NET targeting agents from bench to bedside for personalized medicine development to improve outcomes for patients with breast cancer [85, 121, 130, 134].

Crosstalk between NETosis and TANs in breast cancer

TANs in the TME polarize into antitumor N1 or protumorigenic N2 phenotypes. N1 TANs produce cytokines and ROS, activating T cells for antitumor immunity [117]. N2-like TANs preferentially undergo NETosis, triggered by tumor-derived factors such as G-CSF and TGF-β. G-CSF, secreted by breast cancer cells, stimulates neutrophil proliferation, mobilization, and activation, correlating with increased NET formation and poor clinical outcomes [7, 135].

Notably, G-CSF activates pathways involving relaxin-2 (RLN2) and MMP-9, enhancing breast cancer metastasis [136]. TGF-β not only polarizes TANs toward N2 but also directly stimulates NETosis, perpetuating a protumorigenic TME through a feed-forward mechanism [62, 136]. NETs amplify N2 TAN protumorigenic functions by creating an immunosuppressive environment, partly via IL-10 induction and the c-Met/STAT3 pathway, upregulating PD-L1 expression, reinforcing N2 polarization and suppressing antitumor immunity [136]. This IL-10/STAT3/PD-L1 axis accelerates metastasis and chemoresistance in preclinical models. Additionally, NETs expressing PD-L1 induce T-cell exhaustion, reducing cytokine production and proliferation [137].

The interplay between TANs and NETs presents opportunities for targeted therapies. Neutralizing protumorigenic neutrophil activities through reprogramming TANs from an N2 (protumorigenic) to an N1 (antitumorigenic) phenotype or inhibiting NETosis offers distinct strategies to enhance antitumor immunity [138]. Preclinical studies show that altering TAN metabolism, blocking IL-17, or inhibiting NET formation with PAD4 or NE inhibitors reduces NETosis and metastasis [139–141]. Future studies should use spatial transcriptomics and multiparametric imaging to map NETosis-prone TANs in the TME, uncovering NET-TAN interactions and novel therapeutic targets. Understanding the NET-TAN landscape in aggressive breast cancer subtypes like TNBC could guide targeted therapies, such as TAN reprogramming or NETosis inhibition, to boost antitumor immunity and improve outcomes [65].

Progress in technologies for detecting NETs in the clinical environment

Standard NET detection techniques like immunofluorescence microscopy, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), and flow cytometry also have important drawbacks that limit their clinical application. While immunofluorescence microscopy allows visualization of NET structures via detection of DNA-histone complexes, citH3, and MPO, it takes a long time to evaluate by hand, and standardized evaluation is difficult, hampering high throughput and broad clinical use. However, there is excellent specificity for NETs (as opposed to tissue necrosis) detection, but the method relies on a user determining exactly where to analyse, requiring extensive training, and is subject to inter-observer differences. ELISA-based methods provide quantitative analysis of NET biomarker features, e.g., citH3, MPO and DNA complex estimation, with a standardized protocol that permits reasonable throughput in clinical laboratories, however, while they have reasonable sensitivity and specificity they do not provide time sensitivity in early disease states, robustness in determining NET structure (and therefore possibly brush over some important and relevant pathophysiological issues. Flow cytometry provides automated and rapid analysis for NET-forming neutrophils (and considers large sets of analysed samples directly). On balance, flow cytometry should demonstrate a greater amount of standardization with less burden than immunofluorescence microscopy, but it also has limitations, such as no ability to analyse NET morphology and no ability to identify complex NET structures, which may be important in clinical settings [101, 142–144].

Microfluidic platforms are a novel method of detecting NETs, which permit ultra-sensitive analysis from small volume blood samples (e.g., 10 μL) and are characteristically made up of micro-post arrays and NET-array devices that analyze the NET structures while preserving structural integrity. The systems used citH3 staining for quantification purposes and demonstrated significant superiority over traditional NET quantification in terms of volume and speed. Microfluidics requires specific equipment and trained personnel to execute successfully compared to an ELISA, for instance, but offers increased sensitivity and can analyse dynamic NET formation with real-time imaging. Applications have recently been published, and demonstrated microfluidic platforms could be successfully applied to breast cancer research while elucidating patterns of NET release in clinically relevant microenvironments [145, 146].

Biosensors based on nanotechnology, including electrochemical biosensors and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) platforms, have tremendous sensitivity for detecting biomarkers associated with NETs such as microRNA (miR)-10b, miR-21, and miR-155. Each of these biosensing systems has detection limits that are several orders of magnitude less than traditional detection systems like ELISA, and provides rapid, point-of-care detection capabilities. Nonetheless, nanotechnology detection systems have drawbacks such as high research and development costs, difficulty manufacturing complex devices, and special expertise to operate and maintain. Recent advancements in the use of SERS-microfluidic biosensing platforms have also demonstrated great potential for label-free, multiplexed detection of components in NET with high specificity and selectivity, but they are limited by the requirement for an optimized experimental setup to diminish interference from biological complexity [143, 147, 148].

Machine learning (ML) methods have the potential to advance NET analysis significantly by allowing for the identification of NETotic cells and the modeling of the relationships between complex biomarkers and outcomes. Given the ability of these algorithms to accurately differentiate between NETs and other types of cell death, machine learning can address the limitations imposed by traditional techniques (e.g., manual pathological and molecular analysis) that cannot effectively remedy misclassification of constituent cellular debris. Several recent applications have described how machine learning has been successfully applied to develop prognostic models for patient stratification using NET-associated long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), achieving some individual studies’ diagnostic accuracies of over 85%. When compared to manual analysis, the accuracy and objectivity presented by machine learning offer an advantage for consistency and high-throughput analysis, although machine learning algorithms require massive training datasets and need to be validated in multiple, varied patient populations [143, 149–153].

In breast cancer specifically, these advanced technologies have facilitated important advances in both the biology and clinical associations of NETs. Microfluidic systems have shown increased levels of NETs in the plasma of patients with metastatic disease compared to localized disease, with plasma levels of NETs emerging as possible prognostic biomarkers. Machine learning applied to NET biomarker data has identified NET-related, distinctive molecular signatures associated with progression and treatment resistance in breast cancer. SERS-based detection platforms have shown promise in detecting NET-associated microRNAs related to breast cancer metastasis and clinical outcomes [113, 147, 151, 154].

The technological advancements described previously can transform breast cancer diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment monitoring. More advanced NET detection will enable earlier recognition of the patients at higher risk for metastasis, potentially affect individualized treatments, and enable the estimation of therapeutic responses in real-time. Nevertheless, successful clinical translation will require addressing several key challenges: there is a need for standardized operating procedures for sample collection, processing, and analysis between different platforms; needs to be established for validated biomarker cut-off values as part of larger multicenter studies; and ultimately, extensive clinical validation must be performed which can show diagnostic accuracy, prognostic value, and cost-effectiveness for different groups of patients. Regulatory-approved pathways to support new diagnostic platforms will also need to be built, especially for those based on artificial intelligence systems. Only by working together to create standardized operating procedures and conduct large-scale validation studies can these innovative technologies live up to their promise of improving outcomes for breast cancer patients [101, 113, 148, 150, 155].

Combination approaches targeting NETosis and immune checkpoints in breast cancer

NETs, initially identified as a pathogen defense mechanism, contribute to breast cancer progression by promoting immune evasion and therapy resistance, particularly in TNBC [61, 156]. The TME in breast cancer, enriched with cytokines like IFN-γ and IL-6, induces PD-L1 expression via JAK/STAT signaling, leading to T-cell exhaustion and immunosuppression [156]. NETs exacerbate tumor progression by enhancing cancer cell motility, invasiveness, and EMT, while inhibiting cytotoxic T lymphocyte activity and directly upregulating PD-L1 on tumor and immune cells, further impairing antitumor immunity [16, 157, 158].

TNBC is characterized by poor cytotoxic T lymphocyte infiltration and resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), a type of immunotherapy that targets the inhibitory receptors expressed on T cells (e.g., PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4) or their ligands on the tumor cells [159–161]. By releasing these “immune brakes”, ICIs restore T-cell function and allow the immune system to recognize and destroy cancer cells [162, 163]. ICIs such as antibodies targeting PD-1 or PD-L1 have transformed the treatment landscape for several cancers, but often have limited efficacy in TNBC because of the highly immunosuppressive TME [164, 165]. Elevated NET formation in TNBC correlates with larger primary tumors, high Ki-67 proliferation indices (> 50%), and lymph node metastasis, underscoring NETs’ role in disease aggression [166]. NETs expressing PD-L1 directly suppress T-cell responses, reducing ICI efficacy by fostering an immunosuppressive TME [137]. Preclinical studies suggest that combining NETosis inhibitors with ICIs could restore T-cell function and resensitize tumors to immunotherapy. For instance, DNase I, which degrades NETs, combined with anti-PD-1 antibodies, enhances CD8+ T-cell activity and reduces tumor growth in murine models [167, 168]. Similarly, PAD4 inhibitors suppress NET formation, decreasing metastasis and restoring immune responses [169]. TNBC cells also modulate immune checkpoints in CD4+ T cells, upregulating PD-1, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), and lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3) expression, reinforcing an immunosuppressive TME. NETosis inhibition with DNase I or PAD4 inhibitors reduces PD-L1 expression and enhances T-cell function, particularly when combined with anti-PD-1 therapy, improving CD8+ T-cell infiltration [170, 171]. Beyond ICIs, combining NETosis inhibitors with antiangiogenic agents or chemotherapy holds promise [100, 172]. NETs promote angiogenesis and metastasis, and their inhibition alongside antiangiogenic therapies could disrupt these processes [19]. Chemotherapy combined with NET-targeted therapies, such as nucleolin aptamer-mediated drug delivery systems, enhances tumor cell selectivity and therapeutic efficacy [173].

The heterogeneity of breast cancer necessitates identifying patient subsets likely to benefit from NET-targeted therapies. In TNBC, where therapeutic options are limited, combining NETosis inhibitors with ICIs or other modalities offers a promising strategy to enhance antitumor immunity and improve outcomes. NETosis and the microbiome: a new axis in breast cancer

The gut microbiome modulates breast cancer progression through interactions with NETosis, shaping the TME and immune responses. Microbial communities within the TME, notably Fusobacterium nucleatum, enhance tumor proliferation and migration by activating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling and upregulating vascular endothelial growth factor D (VEGFD) and p21-activated kinase 1 (PAK1) [174]. Spatially resolved multi-omics studies reveal that F. nucleatum-derived extracellular vesicles activate TLR4, promoting NETosis and fostering a proinflammatory TME that drives tumor growth [174, 175]. These vesicles enhance breast cancer cell viability and invasiveness via TLR4-mediated signaling [175–177].

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a Gram-negative bacterial cell wall component, is a potent NETosis inducer. Circulating LPS binds neutrophil TLR4 receptors, activating NADPH oxidase, ROS production, and NET release [178, 179]. Gut dysbiosis compromises intestinal barrier integrity, allowing LPS and other microbial products to enter circulation, fueling chronic inflammation and NETosis. Additionally, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as butyrate, produced by commensal bacteria, modulate systemic immune responses and neutrophil activity [180]. Correlations between circulating cfDNA and microbial DNA sequences, including Pseudomonas mendocina and Sphingomonas spp., are more prevalent in breast cancer patients than healthy controls, suggesting a link between specific microbial taxa, NETosis, and disease progression [181]. Microbiome-targeted interventions show therapeutic promise. Probiotics, such as Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG, suppress inflammation-induced NET formation via antioxidant mechanisms [182]. Lactobacillus acidophilus supplementation reduces proinflammatory cytokines and improves quality of life in breast cancer patients. Preclinical studies demonstrate that Bifidobacterium infantis 35,624 enhances doxorubicin’s tumor-suppressive effects, indicating synergistic potential with chemotherapy [183].

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) modulates the microbiome to enhance immunotherapy efficacy, as preclinical models show improved anticancer responses in recipients of FMT from immunotherapy responders [184]. Although direct evidence linking FMT to NETosis modulation in breast cancer is lacking, altering gut microbiota composition may reduce systemic inflammation and neutrophil activity.

Future research should leverage metagenomic and metabolomic approaches to elucidate the complex interplay between the microbiome, NETosis, and immune responses in breast cancer, particularly in aggressive subtypes like TNBC [185, 186]. Multi-omics studies have identified associations between microbial composition, metabolic pathways, and therapeutic responses [185]. Longitudinal studies and clinical trials are essential to identify microbial triggers of NETosis, characterize downstream metastatic effects, and develop targeted therapies to improve patient outcomes [187, 188].

Concluding remarks and future perspectives

NETosis fuels breast cancer progression by enhancing metastasis, suppressing immune responses, promoting angiogenesis, and increasing therapy resistance. Within the TME, NETs create an inflammatory setting that drives cancer cell dissemination and weakens antitumor immunity. Biomarkers such as citH3 and MPO-DNA complexes hold promise for diagnosing and predicting outcomes, yet inconsistent assay methods and external factors hinder their clinical application. The variability in NET detection methods, including immunofluorescence, ELISAs, and flow cytometry, limits study reproducibility due to differing specificity and lack of uniform protocols. Breast cancer’s diverse subtypes, notably TNBC, exhibit unique immune environments that influence NETosis and its metastatic effects, such as EMT induction. Small study cohorts reduce statistical reliability, underscoring the need for larger, multicenter trials with standardized approaches. Multi-omics analyses reveal NET-driven pathways, including TGF-β and PI3K/AKT/mTOR, that propel cancer advancement. Therapies targeting NETosis, like PAD4 inhibitors (e.g., GSK484), elastase inhibitors, or DNase I, show potential to reduce NET formation and enhance biomarker accuracy for citH3 and cfDNA, which are linked to advanced disease.

To advance NETosis research, standardized detection using microfluidics and SERS can provide reliable, sensitive platforms for clinical use. Large-scale, multicenter studies and longitudinal biobanks are vital to validate NET biomarkers across breast cancer subtypes, particularly TNBC. Combining multi-omics with patient-derived models will deepen insights into NET biology, guiding targeted therapies like PAD4 inhibitors, DNase I, or combinations with immune checkpoint inhibitors to boost antitumor immunity and improve patient outcomes.
