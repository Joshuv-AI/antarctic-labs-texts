Combining deep learning and microfluidics for fast and noninvasive sorting of zebrafish embryo
Authors: Alioune Diouf, Ferhat Sadak, Leandre Bereziat, Edison Gerena, Florian Fage, Abdelkrim Mannioui, Daniela Zizioli, Irene Fassi, Mokrane Boudaoud, Giovanni Legnani, Sinan Haliyo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSorting of zebrafish embryos remains a challenging task in biomedical research. There is a need for accurate and efficient methods to distinguish between embryos at Stage 1, the zygote period immediately after fertilization, characterized by the single-cell stage, as well as those at advanced developmental stages (above single-cell stage) and non-viable (Dead) embryos. Manual sorting is labor-intensive, error-prone, and time-consuming. Traditional automated techniques, such as fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) and robotic systems, are often invasive or prohibitively expensive, limiting their accessibility and scalability for routine zebrafish embryo sorting. This paper presents a novel approach that integrates deep learning with microfluidic technology to address these limitations. Our system utilizes a YOLOv8-based deep learning model for real-time embryo classification, whereas a microfluidic chip which is equipped with peristaltic pumps, ensures precise sorting with minimal manual intervention. Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations are performed to optimise the flow parameters, and experimental validation demonstrate the system’s high accuracy and sorting efficiency. The YOLOv8 model demonstrate a detection accuracy of 97.6 % and a processing speed of 10.5 ms. The sorting experiments demonstrate the system’s efficacy, with the Stage 1 class achieving a detection accuracy of 90.63 % and a sorting efficiency of 88.13 %. The Advanced class exhibited enhanced performance, with a detection accuracy of 93.36 % and a sorting efficiency of 91.80 %. The Dead class demonstrate the highest performance, with a detection accuracy of 99.03 % and a sorting efficiency of 96.60 %. The system demonstrate an average sorting rate of 2.92 s per embryo. This approach provides a reliable, cost-effective alternative to traditional methods, significantly improving the speed and precision of embryo sorting.Subject terms: Biomedical engineering, Computer science

Introduction

The zebrafish (Danio rerio) is an important model organism for the study of developmental genetics and the discovery of new drugs1–4. The use of the zebrafish has resulted in notable advancements in various medical fields, including hematopoietic5,6, cardiovascular and vascular disorders7–9. Zebrafish mutants are used to investigate a range of diseases, including tumors and neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease10,11 and Huntington’s disease12–14. They also help investigate neuromuscular diseases, including Duchenne muscular dystrophy15,16. Consequently, zebrafish represent an exceptional model for studying human diseases, with 80% of their genes shared with humans17. However, sorting of zebrafish embryos is critical for ensuring experimental precision and reproducibility.

In the context of drug screening applications, embryo sorting techniques ensure uniformity and viability. The process of sorting eliminates nonviable or unfertilised embryos, preventing contamination. This improves the reliability of high-throughput tests for drug efficacy or toxicity18. Early embryo separation prevents cross-contamination and excludes irrelevant samples, ensuring research reliability. The sorting of embryos at specific developmental stages allows researchers to obtain standardised samples, which is crucial for accurate comparisons, particularly in studies of gene expression and organ formation19. This approach eliminates variability caused by differences in development timing, thereby improving the reliability of the results20. However, manually handling zebrafish embryos is a time-intensive and tedious task because of their small size and large number of samples needed for experiments21. For example, manually positioning embryos into 96-well plates requires two skilled technicians for approximately three hours to fill 30 plates, with an avereage of 12 minutes per plate. This labor-intensive process is prone to errors and is physically demanding, limiting single technicians to three-hour sessions22.

Automated systems offer faster, more accurate, and consistent sorting, significantly improving productivity and experimental precision in zebrafish research. Image-based cell sorting (IBCS) techniques combine high-content imaging with computational analyses, offering detailed visual insights into individual cells and enabling the development of advanced sorting criteria for improved classification accuracy23. Deep learning (DL) enhances detection by automatically learning features from complex, high-dimensional data, making it an indispensable tool for image recognition, object detection, and classification in biological research. The integration of deep learning with robotic automation has the potential to transform biological research, enabling faster and more precise workflows, such as high-throughput screening and automated sorting.

Several software algorithms, including YOLO24 and Inception networks25, have achieved classification accuracies exceeding 90% for zebrafish embryos by recognising distinct image features. Further advancements by Alegria et al.26 includes the application of YOLOv4 for precise 3D microinjection, achieving notable throughput improvements and enabling large-scale genome manipulation and cryopreservation. Another study27 employed machine learning to enhance phenotype analysis and developmental accuracy. Tools such as EmbryoNet28 use DNN (Deep Neural Netwoks) model to identify zebrafish embryo signaling mutants in an unbiased manner whereas studies29 and30 have applied convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to classify phenotypes and detect abnormalities. A recent review of advanced CNN-based object detection algorithms31 highlighted YOLO as the fastest and most effective, outperforming SSD and Faster R-CNN in speed and accuracy. This efficiency makes YOLO particularly suitable for high-throughput research of zebrafish embryos.

Despite extensive research on zebrafish embryo classification using vision-based methods, there has been limited focus on developing fast and precise sorting systems that integrate deep learning with mechanical action for embryo selection. While classification involves detecting and identifying embryos visually, sorting requires applying contact or non-contact manipulation based on those detections to separate embryos effectively. Systems such as COPAS32 and BioSorter33 efficiently sort large biological samples but have notable limitations, such as high costs and limited quality assessment capabilities for sorted samples. These systems rely on expensive optical components and cannot evaluate the viability or quality of sorted embryos, limiting their applicability in high-precision research. Our previous work34 demonstrated the use of a fluidic system and robotic micromanipulator with visual servoing to sort zebrafish embryos. Compared to existing methods in the literature such as Graf et al.22 and Breitwieser et al.35, the system demonstrated a sorting success rate of 97.9% with a processing time of only three seconds per dead embryo, representing a significant improvement in both speed and sorting success rate.

Recent advances in microfluidics have enabled the development of sorting technologies that can automate sample processing with precise manipulation, making them well-suited for isolating individual particles or cells, as demonstrated by multiple recent studies36–41. These platforms require smaller sample volumes, resulting in reduced reagent costs, shorter analysis times, and less invasive sample extraction procedures for patients37. A key advantage of microfluidics is its closed design, which minimises the risk of cell damage and prevents contamination. Gentle sample handling and precise laminar flow control further ensure sample integrity and reduce cross-contamination. Additionally, automation and real-time monitoring further enhance safety by minimising human intervention and errors. Unlike other noncontact biological manipulation methods, such as dielectrophoresis, ultrasound, or magnetic fields, microfluidics poses no risk of damaging samples, as it does not expose them to potentially harmful external forces42.

However, microfluidic devices have limitations, including complex and costly fabrication and maintenance processes that require specialised expertise. The small size of zebrafish embryos, approximately mm in diameter, reduces the complexity of device fabrication, making microfluidics a practical and optimal choice for their handling. Hence, microfluidic devices integrated with imaging modalities can incorporate DL to analyse real-time information of zebrafish embryos and provide automated flow manipulation or resolving operational issues23. So, integrating microfluidic methods with DL has the potential to automate processes, potentially replacing manual manipulation steps, such as sorting and selecting zebrafish embryos, currently performed manually by researchers43.

This study presents an original approach for zebrafish embryo sorting that combines deep learning and microfluidics. The proposed system allows millisecond detection of the stage of the zebrafish embryo and efficient sorting in a noninvasive manner. While classification systems for zebrafish embryos and robotic sorting methods are well-documented and the development of fully automated sorting systems relying solely on microfluidics remains underexplored, the technology is in its infancy, not being commercially available at the time of this writing, but has great potential for high-throughput and safe embryo handling43. Building on our previous work, this study proposes a novel system designed to address the limitations of earlier approaches. The reliance of the COPAS32 and BioSorter44 systems on costly optical components results in significant expenses and constraints on their accessibility for precise research applications. Furthermore, earlier robotic sorting methodologies presented in Diouf et al.34 and Breitwieser et al.35 incorporating micromanipulators are characterised by their complexity, cost, and invasive nature. The pick-and-place mechanisms utilising micropipettes in these systems have been observed to carry a risk of damaging embryos and potentially compromising biological integrity and viability. These limitations have thus motivated the development of a novel approach that combines microfluidics with DL to provide a reliable, precise, non-invasive, and cost-effective alternative.

The study initiated with the simulation of a CFD model to evaluate the viability of the microfluidic chip in sorting zebrafish embryos across three developmental stages (Stage 1, Advanced and Dead). Following simulations, the microfluidic chip is fabricated using soft lithography techniques. The study then moved to the training of a DL model via the YOLOv8 algorithm, which enables accurate embryo recognition and sorting. The design of a T-shaped separator device is then proposed, to enhance sorting efficiency by sufficiently spacing embryos in flow. The control of water flow timing within the microfluidic chip is examined, with a particular focus on the detection of a critical localization in the microfluidic channel, that will be referred in this paper as the critical decision-making point or Intersection point. The final stage of the study entailed the validation of the hypothesis that the integration of the microfluidic chip with the DL model would result in the effective sorting of embryos and the determination of the optimal parameters for the sorting system.

Results

System overview

The system consists of a microfluidic chip designed to sort zebrafish embryos, a pump system (Kamoer Peristaltic Pump Aquarium Water Pump Flow) controlled by a microcontroller, and a personal computer (PC) to execute a program which uses a DL model trained with You Only Look Once version 8 (YOLOv8). The embryos are introduced into the system through channel S0 and sorted into one of three outlet channels: S3, S4, or S5, depending on their developmental stage (see Fig. 1).

Schematic overview of the sorting system. (a) the fluidic circuit, with input-output channels and control system. (b) to (d) fluidic sorting of detected classes. (e) the egg behaviour in input channel for stage detection.

The DL model is trained on a computer equipped with an Intel Core i9−14900HX processor (32 CPUs, 2.2 GHz), 128 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. Real-time microscopic images are used to evaluate the state of zebrafish embryos passing through channel S0. This enables the determination of whether the embryos are in the first stage of development, have died, or are in an advanced stage of development. The output of the real-time classification algorithm is employed as the input for a water flow control algorithm, which activates pumps to sort the embryos (Stage 1, Dead, or Advanced) into one of three outlets of the microfluidic chip, respectively S3, S4 or S5. A comprehensive illustration of the aforementioned process is depicted in Fig. 1a.

The control algorithm considers the time between image capture and embryo sorting, while mitigating disturbances. The sorting process is conducted by introducing water from channels S1 (P1) or S2 (P1) or both, and extracting it from the concerned outlet. This results in a hydrodynamic push-pull mechanism, which actuates the sorting process. This method minimises potential damage to cell viability and does not require specific buffer solutions45. The objective of pumps P3, P4 and P5 is to facilitate the extraction of the sorted embryos. The PC transmits the classification algorithm output to the microcontroller via the serial port at a data transmission speed of 115200 bauds, which is equivalent to 86.806 s. In the event of a Dead embryo being identified, the P2 is triggered to redirect the lines to the outlet S3 on a provisional basis, while the P3 pump is employed to attract the embryo. In the event that a Stage 1 embryo is present, pumps P1 and P2 are activated in unison to direct it to the centre, while pump P5 initiates a pull. Figure 1b-c-d illustrates the sorting phases. The T-shaped separator arranges embryos in the water flow, creating gaps to enable individual handling and analysis without interference. This separation enhances precision during sorting and subsequent processes. The distance between the embryos is a fundamental aspect of precise analysis, sorting, or further manipulation in biotechnology or laboratory applications. Owing to the dynamic motion of zebrafish embryos which resemble a ball in fluid, a critical decision-making point, termed the Intersection point is defined within the microfluidic chip. This allows a brief observation period for accurate detection and efficient sorting. The class with the highest confidence level, as determined by the model, is selected before it reaches the Intersection point (see Fig. 1e).

The findings of this study corroborate the efficacy of the design in localising mechanical load during the sorting process, while concurrently ensuring mechanical safety in other regions. Akagi et al.46 have demonstrated that shear stress levels of less than 0.1 Pa do not appear to be associated with deleterious effects on zebrafish embryonic development. However, values above this threshold may potentially compromise viability or morphogenesis. Considering the configuration and dimensions of the microfluidic chip, as well as the threshold value, safe sorting can be achieved for values below 4 mm/s.

As the magnitude of shear stress increases, the rate of sorting becomes significantly faster, indicating that enhanced hydrodynamic forcing effectively propels the embryo downstream. However, this acceleration exhibits diminishing returns beyond approximately 0.15 Pa, suggesting that the system approaches a performance plateau where further increases in flow intensity no longer yield proportionate reductions in exit time. This behaviour is indicative of a fundamental trade-off in the microfluidic sorting design. It is evident that an augmentation in flow rates engenders a concomitant improvement in throughput and sorting responsiveness. Conversely, excessive mechanical stress has the potential to compromise the physical integrity or developmental potential of the embryo. This is of particular significance for fragile specimens, such as zebrafish embryos, which exhibit limited tolerance to shear. The existence of a saturation zone is indicative of the presence of an optimal range of flow parameters, which are mechanically sufficient to ensure sorting yet biologically safe.

These findings enable the precise quantification of embryo exposure under varying flow conditions and informed the development of a transport model linking stress to sorting time. Model training results for zebrafish embryo detection

During the first model training, the network is trained for 100 epochs with a momentum of 0.932. The initial learning rate is set to 0.01 and gradually decreases to a final value of 0.0001, with a weight decay of 0.0005 to prevent overfitting. A batch size of 8 is used, and input images are resized to pixels. The optimization is performed using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), and a Mosaic data augmentation strategy is applied to enhance the model’s generalization capabilities. The dataset is initially trained with a default model of YOLOv8m (medium). The hyperparameters of YOLOv8 are subsequently optimized by employing a genetic algorithm aimed at maximising the fitness score over 300 generations. Figure 4c illustrates the evolution of the fitness score across generations. Generation 127 offers a superior fitness score and is selected for further study. The reduced validation loss of the optimised model implies better generalisation to unseen data. It suggests that the chosen hyperparameters enhance the model’s ability to extract robust features while avoiding overfitting. Lower variance in the validation curve also reflects improved regularization and higher resilience to noise or dataset imbalance.

With the same GPU used for training, the optimized YOLOv8 model was evaluated using a separate testing dataset consisting of 273 annotated images of zebrafish embryos, which were not part of the training phase. The model achieved an accuracy of 100 % in correctly detecting and localizing all zebrafish embryos in the test set, with high confidence scores for each detection, indicating both precision and reliability. Furthermore, the model demonstrated real-time performance, with an average inference speed of 10.5 ms per image when executed on the same GPU used for training. T-shaped separator device

After successfully training the model and achieving satisfactory results, the next step is to optimise the method for embryos in the chip to ensure accurate sorting. Since embryos leave input the reservoir in a random manner, there are instances where two embryos may be positioned too close together. This poses a challenge because the system relies on sequential detection by the DL model, followed by fluid deviation and sorting. If two embryos simultaneously enter the field of view of the camera, as depicted in Fig. 5a, it could disrupt the sorting process. Therefore, a robust embryo separation mechanism is called for maintaining sorting accuracy. The process ensures that embryos are spaced at a minimum distance d, as shown in Fig. 5b, to prevent complications arising from fluid dynamics and to guarantee smooth operation.

The present study draws inspiration from the principles of a variable-speed conveyor system for the purpose of achieving embryo separation. By adjusting the speed at which the embryos are transported, the system effectively facilitates their separation. The design incorporates a T-shaped separator that directs two distinct flows. One branch of the T channels carries a flow containing embryos entering the separator, while the other branch carries a fast-moving fluid devoid of embryos. Both flows converge at a single outlet, where the fast-moving fluid generates a separation effect between the embryos as they pass through the T-shaped separator.

Control Mechanism for waterflow timing at microfluidic junctions

To ensure optimal and continuous sorting in a rapid system, a critical decision point must be established where the timing of waterflow is controlled to direct embryos into the appropriate outlet channel. Embryos, which can be conceptualized as floating spheres in water, are capable of changing position and orientation as they move through the system. Consequently, an embryo entering the field of view may be detected multiple times in different orientations. The principal algorithm determines the most confident classification on the basis of these detections. The central decision point, referred to as the Intersection point marks the location where the sorting decision is finalised, and the process of redirecting the embryo to its designated outlet channel begins. To achieve this, a simple comparison is made between the pixel coordinates of the embryo’s detected center and the coordinates of the Intersection point prior to initiating the deviation process to the correct channel. A methodology is developed to identify this Intersection point regardless of the microfluidic chip’s position or orientation. It is defined as the point of intersection of the midlines of the three inlet channels (S0, S1, S2). Detection and Visualization of Intersection point

The Algorithm (1) is designed for detecting the Intersection point employing a combination of DL and traditional computer vision techniques. Figure 6Z.a to Fig. 6Z.f depict all the process. It commences with the loading of a YOLOv8 model, which is employed to generate an edge map of the inlet frame. The Hough transform is subsequently applied to detect lines within the aforementioned edge map. In the event of the detection of lines, the algorithm clusters them based on their angle and distance, utilising the Density-based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) algorithm. DBSCAN is used to cluster lines on the basis of proximity and orientation, which helps in identifying groups of parallel or similarly oriented lines. Thereafter, an average line is calculated for each cluster and lines are filtered by length to focus on prominent structures. Subsequently, the intersection points of the filtered lines are calculated, and their centre of mass is determined to serve as the Intersection point.

The DL model for segmenting the edges of the microfluidic chip is tested on a dataset comprising 61 images when utilising the same GPU for training. The results demonstrated 100 % accuracy with high confidence. To provide an overall evaluation of the developed algorithm, the pixel coordinates determined manually through the ImageJ approach for each of the 61 images in the testing dataset are compared to the corresponding image with those obtained through the algorithm developed in this study. In order to present and analyse the results, a series of equations and statistical methods are employed. The Error in this analysis is calculated as the Euclidean distance between the Intersection points identified by ImageJ and the algorithm. This Error captures the spatial difference between the two points in a 2D plane, using relative distances .

The graph in Fig. 6K presents a statistical evaluation of the intersection point detection method, highlighting its accuracy and reliability using the mean, median and standard deviation of Error. The mean error (8.98 pixels) is almost indistinguishable from the median (8.94 pixels), suggesting a symmetrical and unbiased distribution of detection errors. The proximity of these values to each other is indicative of consistent performance, devoid of any systematic deviation. The standard deviation range, spanning from 8.29 to 9.67 pixels, demonstrates a narrow spread of approximately ± 0.69 pixels around the mean, thereby indicating low variability. This narrow error margin thus confirms the precision of the method, a prerequisite for the pixel-level accuracy required in microfluidic applications. So, the data substantiate the method’s robust performance with regard to consistency. However, despite the encouraging outcomes, it is important to note the presence of certain limitations. Firstly, although the variability is low, an average error of approximately 9 pixels can be significant depending on the scale of the microfluidic system under study, where each pixel can represent several microns. This has the potential to restrict the applicability of the method in scenarios necessitating sub-pixel accuracy or in contexts involving particularly intricate geometries. Secondly, the method is dependent on a number of sensitive steps, including edge detection, the Hough transform, and the DBSCAN clustering algorithm. The performance of each of these steps is contingent on parameters (thresholds, tolerances, point density) that can exert a substantial influence on the final result. Any variation in image quality or contrast has the potential to compromise the robustness of the algorithm. For instance, poorly defined edges or image noise could disrupt line detection or generate false positives during the clustering process. Finally, the method does not appear to dynamically correct errors or adapt to cases where the intersection is partially visible or distorted, which limits its generalisation to various experimental conditions. In conclusion, while the overall performance is satisfactory in this instance, the method exhibits limitations with regard to absolute accuracy, dependence on analysis parameters, and sensitivity to image variations. This calls for caution before any extensive implementation.

To address this issue, a pipe system has been implemented to generate bubbles beneath the medium, lifting and dispersing embryos uniformly (see Fig. 7d). As shown in Fig. 7c-d a pipe is placed at the reservoir outlet to guide the embryos into a single-file stream, driven by gravity, as they enter the microfluidic chip. In the setup, a mixture of water and embryos is introduced into the filter. This filter is specifically designed to allow water to flow through while retaining embryos based on their size, ensuring effective separation. After filtration, the water exits through an outlet, while the embryos remain trapped in the upper section of the system. This configuration ensures that embryos are retained for sorting without being damaged, while the filtered water is safely removed.

This distribution is indicative of the extremely rapid developmental dynamics of zebrafish embryos. It should be noted that the duration of Stage 1 in zebrafish embryos is less than 45 minutes after fertilisation, a period that is under-represented in collected samples. This is primarily attributable to the time between the collection at the zebrafish facilities and the experimental location of this setup, despite the implementation of continuous flow capture conditions during the sorting method. While Stage 1 is the most annotated class in the training dataset, it exhibits the lowest classification accuracy (90.63 %) and sorting efficiency (88.13 %). This underperformance does not stem from limited representation, but rather reflects a directional misclassification tendency, with a substantial number of Advanced embryos being predicted as Stage 1. This attitude of the model is perceptible in the confusion matrix where it is clear that the advanced stages are sometimes confused with the Stage 1 (See the confusion matrix in Supplementary Figure S7). The primary source of these detection errors is the morphological similarity between Stage 1 and Advanced embryos, particularly around 2.25 hours post-fertilisation. During this developmental window, overlapping visual characteristics emerge, especially in phases of early cell division, where blastomere symmetry, yolk opacity, and cell boundary contrast are not clearly distinguishable. Consequently, the model is unable to establish a reliable boundary in feature space, and less distinct Advanced embryos are frequently projected into the outlet corresponding to Stage 1. Despite achieving a relatively high classification accuracy of 93.83 %, the Advanced class accounts for the highest number of misclassifications (n = 21). This is partly due to the high number of Advanced embryo used in experiments but also reflects its transitional morphology, which increases the likelihood of overlap with both Stage 1 and Dead classes. The confusion between Advanced and Dead is less prominent, indicating that the boundary between viable and non-viable phenotypes is better defined. The Dead class exhibits the most robust performance, with 98.96 % classification accuracy and 96.61 % sorting efficiency. Its distinctiveness in terms of phenotype enables effective separation in the feature space, and the minimal discrepancy between classification and sorting outcomes (n = 5) validates the mechanical consistency of the downstream system. In general, the efficiency of sorting is closely related to the accuracy of classification, suggesting that prediction is the bottleneck step in the pipeline. The substandard performance observed in Stage 1, despite the provision of extensive annotation, suggests the presence of a representation bottleneck, indicating the model’s inability to encode fine-grained early-stage morphological cues effectively. Addressing this issue will likely require the refinement of training strategies, and potentially the incorporation of temporal information to contextualize static morphological ambiguity. This is particularly salient in the context of zebrafish, where Stage 1 is rapidly surpassed within 45 minutes post-fertilization, further complicating the task of consistent early-stage detection.

Discussion

Given that the majority of zebrafish embryo sorting systems reported in the literature are automated and utilise robotics Diouf et al.34, Breitwieser et al.35, Graf et al.22, an investigation is carried out into the potential of sorting embryos using a microfluidic chip. This system eliminates the labor-intensive tasks typically performed by biologists in laboratory settings while offering a superior methodology for embryo viability through microfluidics. It enables noninvasive, precise sorting of embryos with controlled fluid flow, minimizing damage and cross-contamination. In order to address these considerations, a microfluidic chip has been designed on the basis of bifurcation laws, and is equipped with six channels, three inlets (S0, S1, S2) and three outlets (S3, S4, S5). However, instances of flow irregularities during experiments occasionally resulted in sorting errors. These are likely caused by transient flow disturbances or inconsistencies in the pressure gradients. The implementation of real-time flow monitoring and feedback control could serve to mitigate these issues. Additionally, future CFD models could incorporate variations in embryo size and density in order to simulate real-world conditions with greater accuracy, thereby improving sorting reliability.

The Table 4 provides a comparative analysis of embryo sorting and detection across different studies, highlighting the strengths and unique aspects of our current work. In terms of sample size, this study employed a total of 622 embryos for sorting experiments, which is less than the 4752 and 694 embryos used in the aforementioned studies. The discrepancy in sample size may impact the reliability of our findings, as larger samples can facilitate more comprehensive data collection. The results presented herein demonstrate the capacity to classify embryos across developmental stages, thereby addressing a limitation of previous studies in this field. To illustrate, the methodology proposed by Breitwieser et al.35 is concerned with the differentiation of rx3 embryos from non-rx3 embryos (including wild-type embryos, empty chorions, and coagulated eggs), but it does not consider the specific developmental stages involved. In contrast, the approach outlined by Graf et al.22 is limited to the classification of embryos as either fertilised or unfertilised, damaged, or healthy. Although the system demonstrates encouraging results in the classification of embryos into developmental stages, there is scope for further improvement, particularly in the case of Stage 1 and Advanced classes. The particularly high accuracy for the Dead class (99.03 %) is crucial for contamination prevention, as the removal of non-viable embryos is a primary objective in sorting workflows. The sorting of embryos at specific developmental stages is of pivotal importance in achieving synchronised samples. Synchronisation is of critical importance for accurate and reproducible comparisons in developmental biology, particularly in studies focusing on gene expression and organ formation. By ensuring that all embryos within a sample are at the same stage of development, researchers can minimise variability and improve the precision of downstream analyses. This capability allows for highly controlled experimental conditions, enabling deeper insights into stage-specific developmental processes, molecular pathways, and their implications for embryogenesis.

The system achieves an average sorting speed time of 2.92 s per embryo, which compares favorably to other reported methods in the literature, including rates of 3 s for Diouf et al.34, 14 s for Breitwieser et al.35, and 8 s for Graf et al.22 per embryo. This improvement is attributable to our microfluidic design, which employs a dedicated channel for each class. By directing the flow of embryos in a single step to their respective sorting channels, the necessity of complex, multistep robotic processes is eliminated. This average sorting time (2.92 s) indicates that sorting occurs within the biomechanical safety zone illustrated in Fig. 3d, thereby ensuring controlled shear stress exposure. This duration is indicative of an effective compromise between embryo safety and operational efficiency, avoiding both excessive stress and overly slow sorting.

These results highlight the efficacy of our approach, offering a reliable and efficient solution for embryo sorting while maintaining high accuracy and speed across diverse developmental stages. In our previous approach34, it was necessary to perform a pick-and-place operation to sort the embryos. In contrast, the new method is much more straightforward and also faster. Given the accelerated progression of these stages, the capacity to rapidly distinguish, categorise and sort embryos at different developmental stages is crucial. In some instances, a specific developmental stage of the embryo is of particular importance, as exemplified by microinjection, typically conducted at the one-cell stage (Stage 1). Moreover, our embryo detection speed of 10.5 ms per embryo is significantly faster than the 1-second detection time reported in35, highlighting the efficiency of our approach. Our system employs a DL approach utilising the YOLOv8 algorithm, which offers superior computational efficiency and real-time object detection capabilities. In contrast to conventional convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures or template matching techniques, YOLO processes the entire image in a single pass, treating object detection as a unified regression problem. It uses a grid-based approach to predict bounding boxes and classes simultaneously, eliminating the need for separate region proposals. Its lightweight architecture and parallel predictions maximize computational efficiency. This enables the system to process data effectively, particularly in scenarios involving zebrafish embryos passing through a microfluidic environment.

The system’s automation is achieved through microfluidic technology, as opposed to the use of robotic mechanisms, which are commonly utilised in analogous applications. The manipulation and analysis of samples at the microscale level can be streamlined through the use of microfluidics, which provides precise control over fluid flow at this level. This option not only reduces the overall system cost by minimising mechanical complexity but also improves scalability and accessibility, rendering it suitable for a wide range of research and clinical applications. One of the key distinguishing features of our approach is its noninvasive nature. In contrast to many comparable systems, which rely on invasive methods such as mechanical (Diouf et al.34, Breitwieser et al.35) or optical manipulation32, the microfluidic-based system preserves the structural and functional integrity of embryos throughout the process. Using noninvasive techniques is critical for maintaining embryo viability, as they mitigate the risks of stress or damage induced by physical contact or external forces. The system utilises a gentle, laminar flow to guarantee exact handling, thereby minimising stress and preventing physical damage to biological samples. This precision enables the safe and effective manipulation of fragile samples, thus preserving their integrity for developmental, toxicological, and biomedical research. This preservation directly contributes to improved outcomes regarding efficiency, accuracy, and overall sample quality. It is acknowledged, however, that future work should include a quantitative assessment of post-sorting viability and stress markers in order to further validate the biological safety of the platform.

The BioSorter shows similar performance, with reported efficiencies typically in the range under optimized fluorescent labeling33. However, our research has various limitations; an analysis of the results of the misclassification balance (see Table 3) indicates that while the sorting method achieves favorable performance, the detection model exhibits notable deficiencies, particularly for the Advanced and Stage 1 classes. It demonstrates that the number of incorrectly predicted instances is nearly double that of incorrectly sorted instances for these stages. This discrepancy highlights a deficiency in the accuracy of the detection process, which has a cascading effect on the sorting process and identifies key areas for improvement within the detection model. The main source of these detection errors is the morphological similarity between embryos in the Advanced (especially at 2.25 hpf) and Stage 1 stages of development. In many instances, these developmental stages display overlapping visual characteristics, particularly during specific phases of cell division, which presents a significant challenge to accurate differentiation. To address this issue, it would be necessary to integrate a higher resolution imaging system, capable of providing the level of detail required to differentiate subtle features. A promising approach would be to enhance the detection model with temporal context by incorporating time-series image data as opposed to relying solely on static images. By analysing morphological changes over a brief time period, the model is capable of acquiring developmental trajectories of embryos, thereby enhancing the accuracy of stage differentiation. Furthermore, the implementation of attention-based architectures (e.g., Transformers or attention modules within CNNs) has the potential to enable the model to focus on subtle yet critical visual cues that differentiate between similar stages. The augmentation of the training dataset with synthetic images or annotated time-lapse sequences has the potential to enhance the model’s robustness to intra-stage variability. The enhancement of detection accuracy, in particular for the Advanced and Stage 1 classes, would have the potential to significantly improve the overall performance of the system. By reducing classification errors, the precision of the sorting process could be further optimised, resulting in more synchronised samples and greater reliability in downstream analyses. Embryos that do not achieve optimal sorting are typically the result of inadequate separation, transient flow blockages, or insufficient water levels within the microfluidic chip. It is important to note that these issues are not a result of deficiencies in the sorting algorithm or methodology, which is performing as designed.

A significant challenge arises from the buoyancy of embryos within the fluidic environment, which can result in overturning and subsequent obscuration of key developmental stage markers. This can impede the accuracy of detection, as the orientation of the embryo is of critical importance in correctly identifying its stage of development. To address this challenge, two potential solutions are proposed for future investigation: the deployment of two cameras, one positioned above and the other below the microfluidic chip, could facilitate the capture of complementary views of the embryos, thereby ensuring the visibility of critical morphological features regardless of their orientation. Rotational Fluid Dynamics, the implementation of a method to gently rotate the embryos within the fluid would allow all sides of the embryos to be visible during the detection process, effectively eliminating errors caused by obscured features. Both approaches can potentially enhance detection robustness by compensating for embryo buoyancy and orientation, leading to improved sorting accuracy and system reliability.

The present study proposes a cost-effective, efficient, and accurate embryo sorting method, with faster sorting and detection speeds compared to existing robotic systems. Although our microfluidic approach is invasive, this compromise enables us to provide a cost-effective solution. Further testing with larger sample sizes could assist in refining the accuracy and validating the broader applicability of the system. Scaling beyond lab prototypes can be achieved by combining parallel microfluidic channels with multi-camera setups, allowing simultaneous processing and imaging of multiple embryos while maintaining resolution and leveraging low-cost hardware like Raspberry Pi for affordability. The modular system could support AI-driven classification, integration with microinjection and sorting devices, and automated data export, making it a scalable and practical solution ready for real-world deployment.

Methods

Experimental devices

The core of the experimental setup is the microfluidic chip, detailed in Supplementary Material in Section S1: Chip design and fabrication, Figure S1, which serves as the primary tool for embryo manipulation and sorting. The system is constructed around a multi-pump fluidic configuration, enabling precise regulation of flow rates. The primary pump (P0) is a Kamoer NKCP-S10B 12V peristaltic pump, which is commonly employed in aquarium applications and capable of delivering flow rates ranging from 19 to 65 milliliters per minute. The system is further augmented by five additional pumps (P1 to P5), which are of the same peristaltic type as the main pump, but are designed for lower flow applications, with adjustable output between 4 and 14 millilitres per minute. These pumps ensure versatile and accurate fluid handling depending on the experimental requirements. The system is controlled by a high-performance PC, an ASUS ROG G752VS laptop, which is equipped with an Intel Core i7−6700HQ processor, 12 GB of RAM, and a NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1070 graphics card. This supports both real-time processing and deep learning inference tasks. The central control unit is based on an ATmega 328p microcontroller, a reliable and widely used chip for embedded control, which is responsible for orchestrating the actuation of the pumps and interfacing with other components. The peristaltic motors are managed by a pump driver circuit based on a L293D motor shield, ensuring proper voltage and current delivery for motor operation. The acquisition of visual data is facilitated by an industrial camera (UI−3590CP-C-HQ, from IDS Imaging Development Systems), which provides high-quality imagery, a prerequisite for subsequent analysis. Two types of microscopy solutions are available for optical inspection, depending on the task: a NIKON SMZ800N stereo microscope and a Dino Lite AM7915MZTL digital microscope, allowing for flexible magnification and imaging conditions. Finally, the system leverages the power of deep learning for object detection and classification, employing the YOLOv8 model, a state-of-the-art object detection framework that is renowned for its accuracy and speed.

Data collection and labelling

A database of images from our previous work48 is combined with a new set, resulting in a total of 1366 images of zebrafish embryos. Wild-type adult zebrafish are maintained according to the standard protocol49 in the Animal Facility and Engineering Aquatic Models Platform of Sorbonne University. Embryos used in this study are collected through natural spawning on the day of each experiment from the same facility. Zebrafish are renowned for their prolific embryo production, with the embryos typically collected shortly after spawning. After collection, the embryos are incubated in E3 solution. It refers to a commonly used growth medium specifically designed for maintaining zebrafish embryos at the developmental stage around 3 days post-fertilization. This medium (34.8 g NaCl, 1.6 g KCl, 5.8 g CaClHO, 9.78 g MgCl.6HO) is crucial for providing the necessary nutrients, ions, and environmental conditions to support embryonic development and viability. The images are classified into three classes: Stage 1, Dead and Advanced stage. The data are split into training, validation, and testing sets using a ratio of 70:20:10, respectively. RoboFlow50 tool is used to prepare the dataset. The bounding box tool is used for annotating images. The dataset under consideration contains an imbalanced class distribution across 3120 annotations. Stage 1 is the most prevalent class, with 1,258 annotations (889 train, 257 validation, 112 test), followed by Advanced stage with 1,048 annotations (758/181/109). The Dead class is the least represented, with a total of 714 annotations (599/163/52), and is especially underrepresented in the test set (52 out of 273). Refer to Supplementary material Section S5 : Zebrafish embryo dataset for model training, Figure S5, Figure S6 and Table S3 for further details about the basic dataset.

In order to enhance the generalisation and robustness of the model when operating under realistic imaging conditions, a data augmentation procedure is implemented that involved the application of horizontal flipping and random rotations within . These transformations are well-suited for zebrafish embryos transiting through microfluidic channels, where slight orientation changes and lateral flips occur due to flow dynamics. The augmentations simulate natural variability without compromising morphological integrity, thereby enhancing the model’s capacity to detect embryos despite minor misalignments or pose variations introduced by the imaging system or fluidic motion. Following the augmentation steps of the dataset, a total of 3481 images are obtained with 7950 annotations.

Description and evaluation of YOLOv8 algorithm

The YOLO (You Only Look Once) model has made significant strides in the field of computer vision, representing a notable success. Developed by the Ultralytics company51, it has gained widespread recognition. YOLOv8 boasts improved detection accuracy and speed, making it a leading choice for various applications in computer vision. This version of YOLO is employed for training purposes. The architecture builds upon YOLOv5’s backbone with the introduction of the C2f module, replacing C3, to enhance gradient flow while maintaining a lightweight design. In YOLOv8, the loss consists of two parts: classification branch, which utilizes Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) Loss, and regression branch, responsible for bounding box prediction, which uses a combination of two independent losses: Distribution Focal Loss (DFL) and Complete Intersection over Union (CIoU) loss.

A model demonstrating high performance is achieved following a process that involves multiple steps. Hyperparameter tuning in object detection is the process of selecting the optimal values for parameters that shape the model’s architecture and training process. These parameters exert a significant influence on the model’s accuracy, precision, and recall. The process of hyperparameter tuning involves the systematic testing of different parameter combinations with the objective of identifying the configuration that maximises the chosen performance metric, such as accuracy or mAP. The basic loss function of YOLOv8 has not been altered. Instead, a genetic algorithm is applied to optimize the YOLO hyperparameters after using the classic YOLO loss function. The utilisation of genetic algorithms enables the optimisation process to investigate a diverse array of hyperparameter configurations, thereby facilitating the identification of superior solutions in comparison to traditional methodologies such as grid search or random search. The stochastic nature of genetic algorithms enables the escape from local minima, thus facilitating the discovery of robust hyperparameter combinations that enhance the performance of the YOLOv8 model. The initial step involves the generation of a population of hyperparameter candidates, which are set as the default YOLOv8 hyperparameters. Each candidate represents a distinct combination of hyperparameter values. Subsequently, each candidate is evaluated in accordance with the specified fitness function, as expressed by Equation 18. Subsequently, the candidates are selected for reproduction, with those demonstrating superior performance having a higher probability of being chosen. The selected candidates then undergo crossover, whereby aspects of two parent candidates are combined, and mutation, whereby some hyperparameters are randomly altered to introduce variability. The new generation of candidates then replaces the old one, and this process continues for a predetermined number of generations. This iterative approach effectively explores the hyperparameter space, with the potential to improve model performance and generalisation in YOLOv8. Subsequently, the generation of hyperparameters that attains the highest fitness score is selected.
