Genome-wide characterization of ZmCRY genes: unveiling stress response mechanisms and the role of ZmCRYPHR2 in salinity tolerance
Authors: Runyi Chen, Laikun Xia, Yuying Fan, Jienan Han, Yikai Wang, Xin Lu, Yue Pan, Gongjian Li, Shufeng Sun, Jiayi Liu, Xinyuan Mu, Zhiqiang Zhou, Zhuanfang Hao, Zhennan Xu, Jianfeng Weng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlue light serves as a crucial environmental signal regulating plant growth and development. The cryptochrome (CRY) family represents a key class of blue light receptors involved in these processes, as well as plant growth, development, and defense. However, the functions of CRYs in maize remain largely unexplored.ResultsIn this study, nine ZmCRY genes were identified and found to be unevenly distributed across five chromosomes. Gene structure and conserved motif analyses revealed that ZmCRYs within the same phylogenetic groups are highly conserved. Synteny analysis indicated a close evolutionary relationship between ZmCRYs and their homologs in Oryza sativa. Promoter analysis identified diverse cis-regulatory elements linked to light response, stress tolerance, and hormone signaling. RT-qPCR analysis showed that ZmCRYs respond to various abiotic and biotic stresses, including high salinity, drought, nitrogen deficiency, Fusarium verticillioides, and Puccinia polysora. Functional studies demonstrated that ZmCRYPHR2, localized in chloroplasts and the cytoplasmic membrane, plays a role in scavenging reactive oxygen and regulating maize salt tolerance. Haplotype 2 of ZmCRYPHR2 was identified as the preferred haplotype in a panel of 269 inbred lines.ConclusionsThese findings provide a comprehensive genomic and functional characterization of the ZmCRY gene family, with ZmCRYPHR2 identified as a pivotal regulator of salt tolerance, offering valuable genetic insights for the development of stress-resilient maize breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07252-9.Keywords: Cryptochrome, Haplotype, Maizes, Salt tolerance, Stress, ZmCRYPHR2

Introduction

From seed germination onward, plant growth and development are influenced by photoperiod, light intensity, and light quality, with light quality playing a particularly critical role [1]. Specific light wavelengths activate distinct photoreceptors, triggering signaling pathways that regulate developmental stages, secondary metabolism, and yield quality [2]. Among these photoreceptors, cryptochromes (CRYs) are blue light-sensing flavoproteins that transmit light signals via non-covalent binding of flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) and 5,10-methyltetrahydrofolate (MTHF) to their N-terminal domains. The first cryptochrome gene, identified in Arabidopsis thaliana, revealed CRYs’ essential roles in diverse biological processes including morphogenesis, circadian rhythms, seed dormancy, and stress resilience [3–5].

In Arabidopsis, six CRY family members have been identified based on domain characteristics, with most research focusing on CRY1, CRY2, and CRY3 (CRY DASH). CRY1, a nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling protein, is a key regulator of blue light-mediated photomorphogenesis, inhibiting hypocotyl elongation, promoting cotyledon expansion, and preventing chlorosis. CRY2, primarily cytoplasmic, regulates photoperiod-dependent flowering and accelerates floral transitions. Both CRY1 and CRY2 exhibit a degree of functional redundancy, enabling plants to adapt flexibly to varying light environments for optimized growth and development [6–8]. CRY3, localized to chloroplasts and mitochondria, participates in light-dependent ssDNA UV repair, although its precise roles remain less defined compared to CRY1 and CRY2 [9].

CRYs function as monomers in the absence of light. Upon blue light exposure, FAD absorbs light energy, inducing conformational changes that lead to dimer and tetramer formation. These structural rearrangements release the C-terminal domain, facilitating interactions with downstream proteins [10]. CRYs interact with approximately 30 different proteins to form regulatory complexes that modulate light-mediated physiological processes. These interactions enable plants to dynamically adjust their growth and development in response to environmental cues, ensuring optimal adaptation and survival [5, 8, 11].

Photoreceptors play a central role in detecting and transducing light signals in plants, regulating physiological processes such as growth, developmental transitions, flowering initiation, and stress responses. In Arabidopsis, AtCRY2 has been shown to enhance cold tolerance by influencing transmembrane Ca2+ flux by inhibiting of AtANN2/3 membrane localization [12, 13]. Conversely, AtCRYs negatively regulate drought tolerance by interacting with COP1, suppressing stomatal opening under blue light [14]. In rice, OsCRY1b improves salt tolerance by repressing genes involved in melatonin and brassinosteroid biosynthesis [15].

Recent studies reveal that CRY1 mediates the trade-off between plant growth and defense. Under blue light, AtCRY1 regulates stomatal aperture and promotes LURP1 accumulation, enhancing pattern-triggered immunity (PTI) against pathogens [16]. Additionally, AtCRY1 increases resistance to Pseudomonas syringae by upregulating pathogenesis-related (PR) genes [17]. While significant progress has been made in understanding CRYs’ roles in stress tolerance in various species, the functions of ZmCRYs in maize remain largely unexplored. To date, only one study has shown that ZmCRY1b enhances resistance to lodging by interacting with HY5 and modulating gibberellin (GA) signaling [18]. However, the expression patterns and biological functions of ZmCRYs under stresses remain unreported.

Research on CRYs has primarily focused on dicotyledonous species, with limited studies on monocotyledons like maize, especially regarding stress resistance. This study identifies ZmCRY genes using whole-genome sequencing and bioinformatics, analyzing their chromosomal distribution, gene structure, conserved domains, promoter elements, and phylogenetic relationships. Expression profiles were examined across tissues and in response to blue light, as well as under high salinity, drought, nitrogen deficiency, Fusarium verticillioides (F. verticillioides), and Puccinia polysora (P. polysora) stresses. ZmCRYPHR2 emerged as a key gene for salt stress tolerance, with phenotypic analysis confirming its role in enhancing maize salt tolerance. Additionally, three haplotypes of ZmCRYPHR2 were identified, with Hap 2 recognized as the superior variant. This study provides insights into the functional roles of ZmCRYs, offering a foundation for future research on ZmCRYs-mediated stress regulation.

Results

Identification and characterization of ZmCRY genes

Cryptochromes in Z. mays, characterized by the DNA photolyase domain (PF00875), were identified using a simple HMM search in TBtools and validated against the Zm-B73-REFERENCE-NAM-5.0.55 database. This analysis identified nine ZmCRYs: ZmCRYPHO, ZmCRYSF, ZmCRY1a1, ZmCRY1a2, ZmCRY1b, ZmCRY2, ZmCRY3, ZmCRYPHR1, and ZmCRYPHR2, distributed across five chromosomes (Fig. 1A). The proteins encoded by these genes display diverse physicochemical properties. ZmCRYPHO has the shortest coding sequence (486 bp) and encodes a protein of 162 amino acids, while the remaining genes range from 1,341 to 2,124 bp, encoding proteins between 446 and 707 amino acids. Predicted isoelectric points vary between 5.07 and 9.82, with most ZmCRYs being hydrophilic and unstable, except for ZmCRYPHO. Subcellular localization analyses suggest functional diversity: ZmCRY1a1, ZmCRY1a2 and ZmCRY1b localize to the cytoplasm and nucleus; ZmCRY2 spans the cytoplasm and outer membrane; ZmCRY3 is chloroplast-localized; and other ZmCRYs are distributed across the cytoplasm, nucleus, chloroplasts, and mitochondria, indicating a broad range of functional roles (Table 1).

Sequence characteristics of ZmCRY genes and physicochemical properties of proteins

Phylogenetic clustering, motif analysis, and structural studies revealed significant conservation among ZmCRYs. Members of the same phylogenetic group exhibit similar gene architectures, conserved motifs, and structural features. For instance, ZmCRY1 and ZmCRY2 share six conserved motifs. Specific motifs, such as motif 4 (absent in ZmCRY3 and ZmCRYSF) and motif 7 (absent in ZmCRY1b and ZmCRYPHR1), vary among genes, while branch-specific motifs like motif 9 are absent from Branches I and II. Conserved FAD_binding_7 and Cryptochrome_C domains are retained in Branch I members (Fig. 1B).

Secondary structure analysis shows that ZmCRYs are predominantly comprised of random coils (56.19%), followed by α-helices (34.08%) and extended strands (9.74%), indicating structural flexibility. Three-dimensional modeling reveals that the core DNA photolyase domain is centrally located, serving as a structural pivot (Fig. S1 and Table S1). Proteins within the same phylogenetic group exhibit similar 3D structures, while structural variations between groups correlate with their functional diversity. In summary, ZmCRYs display conserved gene structures and motifs within phylogenetic groups, supporting shared biological functions, while structural differences suggest diverse functional roles across the gene family.

Inter-species phylogenetic analysis, collinearity, and replication of ZmCRYs

To explore the evolutionary patterns of CRYs across different species, protein sequences from the CRY family in A. thaliana, G. max, S. tuberosum, and O. sativa were retrieved from the Hum database. Phylogenetic analysis categorized the CRY family into six distinct branches, each containing the core DNA photolyase domain. Proteins from O. sativa and Z. mays typically clustered together, showing high homology (Fig. 2A).

To better understand the evolutionary relationships between ZmCRYs and other species, collinearity analysis was performed between a dicotyledonous plant (A. thaliana) and a monocotyledonous plant (O. sativa). The results revealed no collinearity between ZmCRYs and AtCRYs, but nine collinear genes were identified between ZmCRYs and OsCRYs. This suggests that ZmCRYs likely emerged after the divergence of dicotyledonous and monocotyledonous plants (Fig. 2B).

Intraspecies collinearity analysis was conducted to explore potential replication events of ZmCRYs during maize evolution using TBtools. Three duplicate gene pairs were identified, including ZmCRY1s and ZmCRYPHRs. Notably, ZmCRY1s underwent two rounds of duplication during evolution, which may have contributed to their expansion and functional diversification. These results indicate that the duplication of ZmCRY1 and ZmCRYPHR genes has likely played an important role in maize growth and development (Fig. 2C).

Tissue-specific and blue light-induced expression patterns of ZmCRYs

To investigate the functional roles of ZmCRYs, RT-qPCR was performed to analyze their tissue-specific expression profiles in root, stem, leave, tassel, ear, silk, and bract tissues (Fig. 3A). All ZmCRYs showed significantly higher expression levels in leaves compared to roots, highlighting their role as photoreceptors. Except ZmCRY1b, ZmCRY2, ZmCRYSF, and ZmCRYPHO, which exhibited peak expression in silk, the remaining ZmCRYs have the highest expression levels in leaves. Additionally, gradient expression was observed in root, stem, and leaf tissues for most genes, except ZmCRYSF and ZmCRY2. These findings suggest that ZmCRYs may have distinct, tissue-specific roles in plant light perception and development.

As blue light receptors are involved in regulating various physiological processes in plants [5], blue light-induced expression patterns of ZmCRYs were also examined through RT-qPCR at multiple time points (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 12, and 24 h) following blue light treatment, with white light serving as the control. The results revealed strong blue light responses from all nine ZmCRY family members (Fig. 3B). Notably, ZmCRY1b, ZmCRYSF, ZmCRY3, and ZmCRYPHR1 exhibited increased expression across all time points compared to the control. In contrast, ZmCRY1a1, ZmCRY1a2, and ZmCRY2 showed an initial decrease in expression followed by a subsequent increase. Additionally, ZmCRYPHR2 displayed the most significant change in expression at 1 h of blue light exposure, after which it declined. These results demonstrate that ZmCRYs exhibit significant responses to blue light, with distinct expression patterns, indicating their complex role in blue light perception and signaling in maize.

Expression patterns of ZmCRYs under stress

Cis-regulatory elements in gene promoters are often linked to specific gene functions in plants [19]. According to predictions from the Plantcare database, the promoters of ZmCRYs are enriched with light-responsive elements, with all members containing the G-Box (CACGTT) motif, which is associated with blue light sensing. Additionally, the promoters of ZmCRYs feature cis-elements related to phytohormones, suggesting their involvement in the regulation of hormones, stress responses, and growth/development (Fig. S2).

To examine the roles of ZmCRYs under abiotic and biotic stresses, RT-qPCR analyses were conducted under conditions of high salinity, drought, nitrogen deficiency, and pathogen challenges, including infections by F. verticillioides and P. polysora. Under 300 mM NaCl treatment for 24 h, ZmCRY1a2, ZmCRY2, ZmCRYPHR2, and ZmCRYSF were significantly downregulated, whereas ZmCRY1b and ZmCRYPHO showed increased expression (Fig. 4A). In drought simulations using 250 mM mannitol, ZmCRY1a1, ZmCRY1b, ZmCRY2, and ZmCRYSF were upregulated, with ZmCRY2 exhibiting the greatest differential expression, suggesting its key role in drought response (Fig. 4B). Under low nitrogen conditions, ZmCRY1a1 was notably upregulated, while the expression of the other ZmCRYs decreased (Fig. 4C).

In response to pathogens, ZmCRYs were analyzed under F. verticillioides and P. polysora infections. All ZmCRYs except ZmCRY2 responded to F. verticillioides, with ZmCRY3 being the only upregulated gene (Fig. 4D). Following P. polysora inoculation, all ZmCRYs were downregulated, with ZmCRY3 showing the most significant reduction (Fig. 4E). These findings suggest that ZmCRYs play a critical role in responding to abiotic and biotic stresses, potentially contributing to environmental adaptation and pathogen defense.

ZmCRYPHR2 negatively regulates salt tolerance

Building on the observed differential expression of ZmCRYs under high salinity stress (Fig. 4B), and the significant downregulation of ZmCRYPHR2 in these conditions, ZmCRYPHR2 was selected for further investigation to explore its role in regulating maize salt tolerance. Subcellular localization revealed that ZmCRYPHR2 was primarily localized in the chloroplasts and plasma membrane (Fig. 5A). Next, the phenotypic effects of ZmCRYPHR2 were examined in mutant and overexpression lines under 300 mM NaCl stress, using vermiculite as the growth medium. The overexpression lines displayed more pronounced salt stress symptoms, including stunted plant height, yellowing of leaf tips, leaf curling, wilting, and visible greening of the leaves, when compared to the control. These lines also show significant reductions in SFR, PHR, RFR, and RLR. In contrast, the ZmCRYPHR2 mutants exhibited greater biomass relative to the B73 control. Chlorophyll content (SPAD values) was significantly lower in OE-1 and OE-2 lines under salt treatment, while it was higher in the ZmCRYPHR2 mutant compared to B73 (Figs. 5B-D). These results indicate that ZmCRYPHR2 negatively regulates maize’s response to salt stress.

Salt stress is known to induce the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative stress and increased cell membrane damage. To determine whether ZmCRYPHR2 influences ROS production, the expression levels of two key marker genes were analyzed. The results showed that ZmRBOH4, associated with ROS production, was upregulated in the OE-1 and OE-2 lines, while ZmAPX1, involved in ROS scavenging, was downregulated. In contrast, the expression patterns of these genes reversed in the ZmCRYPHR2 mutants (Fig. 6A).

Further analysis of antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT and POD) revealed reduced activity in the OE-1 and OE-2 lines compared to B104, while the mutants exhibited increased activity (Figs. 6B-D). Additionally, MDA levels, a marker of cell membrane damage, were significantly higher in the OE-1 and OE-2 lines, indicating greater membrane damage. The mutants, on the other hand, showed lower MDA levels, suggesting reduced membrane damage (Fig. 6E).

To assess O2− and H2O2 accumulation directly, DAB and NBT staining under 300 mM salt stress was conducted. Both staining methods showed lighter staining in B104, darker staining in OE-1 and OE-2 lines, and the opposite trend in the mutants (Fig. 6F). These findings suggest that ZmCRYPHR2 inhibits antioxidant enzyme production, thereby reducing salt stress tolerance and exacerbating cellular damage in maize.

Haplotypes of ZmCRYPHR2 associated with salt tolerance

To explore the natural variation of ZmCRYPHR2 across maize accessions, a candidate gene association analysis was performed on 269 inbred lines exhibiting a range of phenotypic responses to salt stress during the seedling stage (Fig. 7A, Table S1). By analyzing genetic variations in the coding regions of ZmCRYPHR2, three distinct haplotypes were identified based on traits such as SFR, PHR, RFR, and RLR. Among these, Hap 2 was most significantly associated with enhanced salt tolerance, particularly for all traits except RLR, suggesting its potential role in improving salt tolerance under high salinity conditions. Hap 1, which was the most prevalent haplotype in the population (59%), did not show significant differences in the four traits compared to Hap 2 (34%), while Hap 3, found in only 7% of the population, displayed minimal salt tolerance effects.

Dual mutations at positions 132.764.609 (Asp/Gly) and 132.764.642 (Gly/Asp) did not significantly affect salt tolerance, but a single mutation at position 132.764.642 (Gly/Asp) led to increased salt sensitivity (Figs. 7B–E). Population genetic analysis revealed that Hap 2 was predominantly distributed in the SPT, PB, and BSSS groups, whereas Hap 3 was primarily found in the LRC, MIX and Lan groups (Figs. 7F–M). These findings align with previous phenotypic evaluations of salt tolerance, which ranked the groups as follows: SPT > PB > BSSS > Iodent > PA > Lan (Lancaster) > Mix > LRC [20]. This study underscores the potential of Hap 2 as a valuable genetic resource for breeding salt-tolerant maize varieties.

Discussion

CRYs are essential blue light receptors in plants, playing a pivotal role in growth, morphogenesis, and the plant’s adaptive responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. Research on the CRY family has predominantly focused on the functional characterization of CRY1 and CRY2, with gene identification and functional studies conducted mainly in Gossypium [21] and S. tuberosum [22]. The present study investigated the ZmCRYs in maize, with an emphasis on their involvement in blue light perception and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. Nine ZmCRYs were identified through genome-wide analysis, revealing their physicochemical properties and gene localization (Table 1; Fig. 1). Evolutionary analysis suggests that ZmCRYs expanded through segmental duplications and showed higher homology with OsCRYs, offering insights into their functional diversification after the monocot-dicot divergence (Fig. 2). ZmCRYs displayed distinct responses across maize tissues and to blue light, with some exhibiting biphasic expression patterns, indicating complex regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 3).

Further analysis of the promoter regions uncovered cis-acting elements related to stress and hormone signaling, highlighting their roles in stress tolerance and adaptive growth regulation (Fig. S2). These findings align with previous studies that highlight plant cryptochromes’ involvement in responses to various adverse environmental stresses [12, 21, 23–26]. The upregulation of ZmCRY1a1, ZmCRY1b, ZmCRY2 and ZmCRYSF suggests their involvement in drought tolerance, while the varying responses across family members call for further exploration of their interactions in stress adaptation (Fig. 4B). This finding is consistent with studies in Arabidopsis and Sorghum [14, 27–30]. Research has also shown that AtCRY1 is induced under nitrogen deficiency and positively regulates flowering [31]. Under nitrogen stress, only ZmCRY1a1 exhibited stress-induced expression, and the potential antagonistic relationships between upregulated and downregulated members warrant further investigation (Fig. 4C).

This study also examined the responses of ZmCRYs to pathogen infections, focusing on two harmful fungi in China: the biotrophic P. polysora and the necrotrophic F. verticillioides. ZmCRYs were downregulated in maize infected with P. polysora, with ZmCRY3 showing the most significant reduction. In contrast, ZmCRY3 was upregulated following F. verticillioides infection, while other family members were negatively regulated (Figs. 4D-E).

Soil salinization affects over 800 million hectares of land, including 32 million hectares of agricultural land and may lead to the degradation of 50% of arable land by 2050 [32, 33]. Recent research suggests that blue light receptors, particularly CRYs, play a crucial role in plant responses to salt stress. For instance, OsCRY1b enhances rice salt tolerance, while TaCRY1a and SbCRY1a increase salt sensitivity, possibly through the abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathway [15, 30]. In this study, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that several ZmCRYs were responsive to salt stress (Fig. 4A). Specifically, ZmCRYPHR2, localized to chloroplasts and plasma membranes, was found to negatively regulate maize salt tolerance (Fig. 5). Chloroplasts, being highly sensitive to salt stress, are major sources of ROS in plants [34]. Overexpression of ZmCRYPHR2 reduced antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD, CAT, POD), increased ROS levels, and caused greater cellular damage, as evidenced by higher MDA levels (Fig. 6). By querying FPKM values of ABA-responsive genes, we identified significant up-regulation of PP2C family genes (Zm00001d009626, Zm00001d012401, Zm00001d025055) in OE-ZmCRYPHR2 transgenic lines (Fig. S4). Previous studies have shown that PP2C can inhibit the phosphorylation activation of SnRK2, thereby interfering with antioxidant defense processes mediated by PRODH2, CBSX1, etc [35, 36]. This data is in agreement with the findings that the activities of enzymes related to the clearance of reactive oxygen species in the ZmCRYPHR2 overexpression plants have decreased, while the accumulation of reactive oxygen species in the plants has increased.

Marker-assisted selection (MAS) has proven to be an effective tool for improving crop genetics. The study identified ZmCRYPHR2 as a key gene in enhancing salt tolerance, particularly through the superior haplotype Hap 2. This haplotype displayed strong performance in critical salt tolerance traits, offering valuable genetic resources for breeding salt-tolerant maize (Fig. 7).

Overall, these findings underscore the significance of ZmCRYs in light signaling, stress responses and their potential applications in crop breeding to enhance stress resistance. Further research is needed to explore the complex regulatory networks of ZmCRYPHR2 in salt stress adaptation.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of nine ZmCRYs in maize, classifying them into six distinct subgroups based on structural similarities and identifying three pairs of segmentally duplicated genes. Notably, over 38% of their cis-regulatory elements were found to be light-responsive, with additional elements linked to stress tolerance, hormone signaling and developmental regulation. ZmCRYs demonstrated tissue-specific expression, primarily in leaves and exhibited dynamic responses to various abiotic and biotic stresses, including high salinity, drought and pathogenic infections. Of particular significance, ZmCRYPHR2 was shown to enhance maize sensitivity to high salinity, as evidenced by reduced biomass, increased accumulation of O2− and H2O2, extensive cellular damage and diminished antioxidant enzyme activity. Additionally, the identification of the favorable ZmCRYPHR2 haplotype, Hap 2, represents valuable genetic loci for marker-assisted breeding. Collectively, these findings lay the groundwork for further investigation into the molecular mechanisms of ZmCRYPHR2’s under salt stress and provide insights for enhancing maize resilience through genetic improvement.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide identification and analysis of physicochemical properties of ZmCRY genes

The B73 RefGen_v5 genome sequences, gene coding sequences, protein sequences, and annotation files were obtained from the MaizeGDB database (https://www.maizegdb.org/, accessed on 10 September 2024) [37, 38]. To identify the DNA photolyase domain (PF00875) within the maize protein sequences, a simple hidden Markov model (HMM) search feature was employed in TBtools, using default parameters (E-value < 1e-10, identity > 50%) [39]. The identified ZmCRYs were further validated against the Zm-B73-REFERENCE-NAM-5.0.55 database on Phytozome (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/info/Zmays_Zm_B73_REFERENCE_NAM_5_0_55, accessed on 10 May 2024) [40]. For subsequent analyses, gene family members of ZmCRYs were defined using the longest transcript annotated in the reference genome.

Protein sequences of candidate ZmCRYs were re-examined for the presence of the conserved DNA photolyase domain (PF00875) using the SMART online tool (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/, accessed on 10 May 2024) [41]. Gene IDs, coding sequences, exon-intron structures, and amino acid counts were retrieved from MaizeGDB [38]. The physicochemical characteristics of ZmCRYs, including isoelectric point (pI), grand average of hydrophilicity (GRAVY), and instability index (II), were predicted using ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/, accessed on 12 September 2024) [42]. Additionally, CELLO v.2.5 software (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/, accessed on 12 September 2024) was used to predict the subcellular localization of ZmCRYs [43].

Molecular characterization of ZmCRY genes

The gene annotation file from the reference genome (V5) was utilized with TBtools (MCScanX) to generate a chromosomal localization map of the ZmCRYs [39]. Protein sequence files from O. sativa, Solanum tuberosum, A. thaliana, and Glycine max were acquired from the Gramene database (https://ensembl.gramene.org/, accessed on 15 July 2024) [44]. Amino acid sequences corresponding to the CRYs were aligned using Clustal X, and the resulting alignments were used to perform phylogenetic analysis in MEGA11 using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method. Genetic distances were calculated using the Poisson correction model, and the phylogenetic tree was validated through 1,000 bootstrap replications [45]. The finalized phylogenetic tree was visualized with iTOLs (https://itol.embl.de/, accessed on 15 July 2024) [46].

To explore the structural features of ZmCRYs, conserved motifs were identified using MEME (http://meme-suite.org/) with the number of motifs set to ten and default settings for other parameters [47]. The motifs were visualized using TBtools. Gene structure diagrams for ZmCRYs were created by integrating annotation files and gene IDs retrieved from MaizeGDB (https://www.maizegdb.org/, accessed on 10 July 2024) into TBtools. Upstream regulatory sequences, specifically 2000 bp upstream of the ATG start codon, were extracted using TBtools. These sequences were analyzed using PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html, accessed on 15 September 2024) to predict cis-element types, locations, and quantities, with results visualized in TBtools [39, 48].

Collinearity analysis of ZmCRYs was conducted with MCScanX. Both fragment and tandem duplications were identified using genomic sequences from Zea mays, O. sativa, and A. thaliana [49]. Secondary and tertiary structures of ZmCRYs were predicted using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/, accessed on 28 September 2024) and SOPMA (https://npsa.lyon.inserm.fr/cgi-bin/npsa_automat.pl?page=/NPSA/npsa_sopma.html, accessed on 28 September 2024) [50, 51].

Plant material and growth conditions

The maize inbred lines B73, Qi319, and B104, provided by the Institute of Crop Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, were utilized in this study. The maize plants were cultivated under field conditions in Changping, Beijing, China (40.21°N, 116.19°E). Each row was 3 m in length with a plant spacing of 25 cm, arranged in a randomized block design with three replicates. To investigate the response of ZmCRYs to abiotic stress, such as drought and low nitrogen, seeds of the wild-type (WT) B73 were germinated on moistened filter paper (480 × 350 × 41 mm) under controlled conditions (28 °C light/26°C dark, 16 h light/8 h dark) for 48 h until germination. After the main roots reached 2 cm in length, the germinated seeds were positioned at the apex of rolled filter papers and cultivated further in distilled water until the seedlings reached the two-leaf and one-heart stages. Uniformly grown seedlings (nine per experiment) were transplanted to ½-strength Hoagland nutrient solution. Three days later, the solution was replaced with a full-strength Hoagland nutrient solution [52].

The ZmCRYPHR2 fusion expression vector (CUB-ZmCRYPHR2-GFP) was constructed using the BstEII restriction site and driven by the Ubiquitin (Ubi) promoter. The plasmid was introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 competent cells (CAT#:AC1010) and subsequently used for genetic transformation. Transgenic seeds were generated using the B104 lines as the recipient by Beijing Origen Guofeng Biotechnology [53]. Additionally, the ZmCRYPHR2 mutant in the B73 genetic background was obtained from a maize ethyl methane sulfonate (EMS) mutant library (http://www.elabcaas.cn/memd/, accessed on 10 September 2024) [54].

Tissue-specific and blue light-induced treatment expression analysis of ZmCRY genes

To comprehensively analyze the expression profiles of ZmCRYs across different maize tissues, total RNA was extracted from seven distinct tissue types of the B73 inbred line during the grain-filling stage (60 days post-pollination). The sampled seven tissues included roots, stems, leaves, tassels, ears, silks, and bracts. Three biological replicates were collected for each tissue type to ensure reliable results.

For blue light treatment experiments, the maize inbred line Qi319 was cultivated under controlled conditions (28 °C light/26°C dark, 14 h light/10 h dark) until the V2 stage of maize seedlings. Plants were then exposed to blue light (B, 31 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) or white light (W, 34 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) for 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 12, and 24 h. Tissue samples were collected at each time point, with three biological replicates per treatment [55].

Stress treatments and tissue collection

At the V3 stage, the culture solution for maize plants was replaced with Hoagland nutrient solutions supplemented with 15% polyethylene glycol (PEG) (kg/V) or 0.04 mmol NO3− to simulate drought and low nitrogen conditions, respectively. Plants in the control group were maintained in standard Hoagland nutrient solution. Leaves were harvested from treated plants 16 h after PEG treatment and 5 days after low nitrogen treatment. For each treatment, three biological replicates were collected and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen [56, 57].

For P. polysora inoculation, B104 plants at the V7 stage were sprayed with a spore suspension of P. polysora using a spray inoculation method. Leaf samples were collected 48 h post-inoculation from the same position on each plant to ensure consistency. Three biological replicates were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen for further analysis [58].

The preparation for F. verticillioides inoculation involved single-spore subculturing on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium (BD Difco™ Potato Dextrose Agar, Sparks, MD) at 25 °C for 15 days. Colonies were harvested, crushed, and resuspended in sterile water to prepare a spore suspension at a concentration of 5 × 106 spores/mL. Artificial inoculation was performed by injecting 2 mL of the spore suspension into each ear of B73 plants 2–7 days after silking. Leaves were collected 48 h post-inoculation, with three biological replicates collected for analysis [59, 60].

To simulate high-salinity conditions, maize seeds were sown in pots containing vermiculite and irrigated with Hogland nutrient solution every five days. After the plants reached the V2 stage, a 300 mM NaCl treatment was applied. A control group was maintained under normal Hogland nutrient solution. Leaf samples were collected 24 h after the salt treatment from B73 plants, with three biological replicates flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen [61, 62].

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

RT-qPCR validation analyses were conducted using three biological replicates. Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure® Plant Total RNA Isolation Kit (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co. Ltd, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA integrity, quality, and concentration were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Waldbron, Germany), and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA synthesis was performed using the FastKing gDNA Dispelling RT SuperMix (Tiangen Biotech Co. Ltd, China).

Primers specific to the ZmCRYs were designed using NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/index.cgi?LINK_LOC=BlastHome, accessed on 11 October 2024). The maize Ubiquitin (Ubi) gene served as the internal control for normalization, and primer sequences are presented in Table S1 [63]. Expression levels of nine ZmCRY genes were quantified using a Bio-Rad CFX-96 PCR system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). The thermocycling conditions were set to 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 10 s and 58℃ for 30 s. Relative expression levels were calculated using the method of the 2−∆∆t method [64].

Subcellular localization of ZmCRYPHR2

The full-length coding sequence of ZmCRYPHR2 was amplified and cloned into the CPB-GFP vector at the BstEII restriction site, creating a ZmCRYPHR2-GFP fusion protein driven by the CaMV 35 S promoter. The resulting plasmid was introduced into N. benthamiana leaves via A. tumefaciens strain under GV3101 (pSoup P19) transformation. Subcellular localization was visualized using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM900, Jena, Germany) [65].

Determination of physiological indices

After 12 days of treatment with 300 mM NaCl stress, morphological traits were evaluated (n ≥ 9), including shoot fresh weight (SFW), root fresh weight (RFW), shoot length (SL), and root length (RL). Plant growth was documented with photographs. Salt tolerance coefficients were calculated using the formula: salt tolerance coefficient = stress indicator value/control indicator value. Ratios such as shoot fresh weight ratio (SFR), plant height ratio (PHR), root fresh weight ratio (RFR) and root length ratio (RLR) were determined. Chlorophyll content was measured using a SPAD-502 chlorophyll meter (Konica Minolta) in the third leaf of salt-treated plants and the fifth leaf of the control plants. Data processing, visualization, and statistical analysis were performed using Microsoft Excel 2013, SPSS 11.5, and GraphPad Prism 9 software. Statistical significance was assessed using multiple unpaired t-test.

Leaf samples were collected from B73, ZmCRYPHR2 mutants, OE-1, OE-2, and B104 plants after 12 days of salt treatment. Samples were placed in 2.0 mL centrifuge tubes and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and malondialdehyde (MDA) were determined using commercial assay kits: SOD-1-Y (BC0175), POD-1-Y (BC0090), and MDA-1-Y (BC0020). Superoxide anion levels were assessed using Nitro Blue Tetrazolium (NBT) staining (G4816), and hydrogen peroxide levels were evaluated with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) (DA1010). Expression levels of ZmCAT1 and ZmROBH4 in leaves were measured using RT-qPCR following 12 days of salt stress treatment [66, 67].

ZmCRYPHR2 haplotype analysis

Haplotype classification of ZmCRYPHR2 was conducted using sequencing data from 269 maize inbred lines. Full-length gene regions were screened based on single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) loci and haplotypes were identified with the geneHapR tool [58]. The analysis examined the distribution of haplotypes and their corresponding salt tolerance coefficients, including shoot fresh weight ratio (SFR), plant height ratio (PHR), root fresh weight ratio (RFR), and root length ratio (RLR).

Data processing, visualization, and statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel 2013, SPSS 11.5, and GraphPad Prism 9 software. Statistical comparisons were conducted using multiple unpaired t-test to evaluate differences among haplotypes.
