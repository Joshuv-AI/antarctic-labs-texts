Impact of Enterococcus faecium 129 BIO 3B-R on Helicobacter pylori eradication therapy side effects in adult patients: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study
Authors: Eri Tokunaga, Jun Inoue, Naoki Asaji, Katsuaki Oyama, Akihiro Soga, Yusaku Shimamoto, Masato Kinoshita, Takeshi Tanaka, Hiroki Hayashi, Takuya Ikegawa, Yuki Ito, Sayaka Ikeda, Norihiro Okamoto, Haruka Miyazaki, Yuna Ku, Daisuke Watanabe, Makoto Ooi, Namiko Hoshi, Hiroo Makimoto, Ikuko Yano, Eiji Umegaki, Yuzo Kodama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Managing the side effects of diarrhea, which is associated with intestinal microbial dysbiosis, is a crucial challenge in Helicobacter pylori eradication therapy. The aim of this study is to explore whether administration of a probiotic strain Enterococcus faecium 129 BIO 3B-R, a multi-antibiotic resistant lactic acid bacterium, influences the side effects of Helicobacter pylori eradication therapy in adults. Seventy-six adults undergoing this therapy were randomized to receive either Enterococcus faecium 129 BIO 3B-R or a placebo in a double-blind manner. No significant difference was observed in the incidence of diarrhea, the primary endpoint, or in any other secondary endpoints, including intestinal microbiota diversity, between two groups in the overall study population. However, in a post-hoc age-stratified analysis, participants aged 70 and older who used Enterococcus faecium 129 BIO 3B-R experienced tended to have more diarrhea during the eradication period but subsequently experienced significantly less diarrhea after eradication compared to the control group (23.1% vs. 60%). Treatment with Enterococcus faecium 129 BIO 3B-R also maintained higher α-diversity in their intestinal microbiota than those in the placebo group. Those data suggest that the administration of Enterococcus faecium 129 BIO 3B-R could potentially alleviate diarrhea and intestinal dysbiosis in over 70-year-old elderly patients undergoing Helicobacter pylori eradication.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-15864-2.

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori infects the gastric mucosa and is associated with diseases such as gastritis, gastroduodenal ulcer, and gastric cancer1. Accordingly, a worldwide consensus has been reached for H. pylori eradication therapy which uses the combination of antibiotics and gastric acid secretion inhibitors. However, 2–19.6% of patients experience serious side effects requiring discontinuation of treatment, such as diarrhea, fever, rash, pharyngeal edema, and hemorrhagic enterocolitis2,3. Diarrhea is a particularly common side effect, occurring in approximately 4–48% of patients, and can sometimes become severe3–6.

The occurrence of diarrhea has been suggested to be associated with changes in the gut flora caused by antibacterial therapy7. Antibiotic administration causes quantitative and qualitative changes in the gut flora, resulting in a reduction in normal microflora and overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria5. For example, when the balance of intestinal microorganisms is disrupted by antibiotics, Clostridioides difficile can become dominant and pathogenic, leading to symptoms8. C. difficile has been associated with approximately 25% of all antibiotic-associated diarrhea cases. Other opportunistic pathogens, such as Clostridium perfringens, Klebsiella oxytoca, and Staphylococcus aureus, Candida species and Salmonella species have also been associated with antibiotic-associated diarrhea9–12.

Concomitant use of probiotics during H. pylori eradication therapy is expected to reduce its side effects through various mechanisms. In their meta-analysis, Lu et al.13 found that concomitant use of probiotics significantly reduced the incidence of side effects, including diarrhea. Probiotics are known to strengthen the epithelial barrier and enhance defense against pathogens in several ways, including increase in mucin secretion by goblet cells and levels of IgA-producing cells associated with mucosal immunity, and enhancement of alpha-defensin expression by Paneth cells and tight junction stability of intestinal epithelial cells14. In addition, probiotics can supply short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), such as acetate, butyrate, and propionate, which preserve intestinal microbial diversity by promoting growth through cross-feeding between SCFA-utilizing commensal bacteria15.

Enterococcus faecium is a gram-positive, facultative anaerobic bacterium. In animal studies, E. faecium suppressed the growth of pathogenic bacteria in mice by stimulation of mucin and antimicrobial peptide secretion16. Human clinical trials have also reported that co-administration of E. faecium and Bacillus subtilis during antibiotic therapy attenuates the decrease in intestinal bacterial diversity17. A multi-antibiotic-resistant lactic acid bacterial preparation containing E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R has been reported in previous studies to suppress antibiotic-associated diarrhea in children and to prevent the decline of beneficial intestinal bacteria18. Furthermore, co-administration of E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R during H. pylori eradication therapy in adolescent patients has been reported to improve the decline in intestinal microbial diversity19. Therefore, while E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R is expected to suppress the decline in intestinal microbial diversity and improve diarrhea, its effects on the intestinal bacteria and diarrhea incidence during H. pylori eradication therapy in adults are unknown.

Therefore, in this study, we investigated the effects of concomitant use of E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R with primary H. pylori eradication therapy on the incidence of diarrhea and intestinal microbiota diversity in a randomized, double-blind, controlled trial.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

Individuals with positive H. pylori test results (urea breath test, fecal H. pylori antigen, blood/urine H. pylori IgG antibody, and rapid urease tests) evaluated at Kobe University Hospital between February 2018 and December 2021 were recruited. A total of 76 participants were enrolled. In this randomized controlled trial, eligible participants were randomly assigned to receive either E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R or placebo in a 1:1 ratio, based on a computer-generated randomization list. The randomization was conducted by an allocation specialist independent of the main research team. Participants were equally divided between the two groups, with each participant receiving medication packs numbered in advance by the allocator. E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R was acquired over the counter as the commercially available Biofermin-R. Both E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R and placebo were in powder form, and each dose was carefully prepared and packaged by the hospital’s Pharmacy Department. The study employed a double-blind method, where both participants and researchers were blinded to the treatment allocation, ensuring transparency and fairness in the intervention. Data on primary and secondary endpoints were collected throughout the study’s follow-up period.

The study conformed with the principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Clinical Research Ethical Committee of Kobe University Hospital (approval number C180029). The study was registered with the Japan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT) and the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN). The initial trial registration date for jRCT is 25/03/2019 (jRCTs051180155; https://jrct.niph.go.jp/latest-detail/jRCTs051180155/), and that for UMIN is 18/12/2017 (UMIN000030015; https://center6.umin.ac.jp/cgi-open-bin/ctr/ctr_view.cgi?recptno=R000034265).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Diagnosis of H. pylori infection based on both upper-gastrointestinal endoscopic findings suggestive of infection and at least one additional positive H. pylori test (e.g., urea breath test; serum or urine antibody test; stool antigen test; culture; or rapid urease test), in accordance with Japanese Helicobacter Study Group guidelines; (2) patients for whom H. pylori eradication therapy is clinically beneficial (3) provided informed consent; and (4) absence of allergies to disinfectants.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of H. pylori eradication; (2) use of antibacterial drugs or proton pump inhibitors within 4 weeks before the start of eradication therapy; (3) age < 20 years; (4) history of surgery for gastric cancer (excluding endoscopic treatment); (5) severe renal or liver disorder, or heart disease; (6) allergy to the drug used; and (7) inappropriateness for treatment as determined by the investigator.

Treatment

In accordance with Japanese clinical guidelines, all participants received a standard 7-day first-line H. pylori eradication therapy with oral vonoprazan (20 mg twice daily), amoxicillin (750 mg twice daily), and clarithromycin (200 mg twice daily). Participants in the E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R group (3B-R group) received probiotic strain E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R (Biofermin-R) at 3 g/day and those in the placebo group received placebo at 3 g/day as oral therapy for 14 days, beginning on day 1 of the 7-day H. pylori eradication regimen and continuing for an additional 7 days after completion of antibiotic therapy. E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R is a multi-antibiotic-resistant lactic acid bacterium; therefore, it was administered concurrently with the antibiotic treatment without adjusting the timing of intake. Because it is not acid-resistant, participants were instructed to take it after each meal.

The ingredients used to compound the placebo and E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R tablets were purchased commercially and prepared at the Pharmacy Department of Kobe University Hospital. The placebo was formulated using components found in Biofermin-R, specifically, barley starch, glucose, lactose, and precipitated calcium carbonate.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the cumulative incidence of diarrhea (Bristol stool form scale score20 of 6 or 7 points) in the E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R and placebo groups during 7 days of antibiotic treatment (day 0–7).

Secondary endpoints included the following: (1) changes in gut flora diversity at one day, one week, and at the eradication judgment (day 64 (± 7) after the end of antibiotic treatment); (2) occurrence of adverse events other than diarrhea; (3) frequency scale for the symptoms of gastroesophageal reflux disease scores (FSSG score)21 after treatment; (4) diamine oxidase activity (DAO) in the serum at one day after the end of antibiotic treatment; (5) cumulative incidence of diarrhea (defined by the Bristol stool form scale score of 6 or 7 points) during 7 days after antibiotic treatment completion (day 8–14); (6) frequency of bowel movements during and during 7 days after antibiotic treatment; and (7) proportion of stool properties according to the Bristol stool form scale during and during 7 days after antibiotic treatment. The evaluation of the intervention’s safety was based on the monitoring of adverse events other than diarrhea and the exacerbation of gastroesophageal reflux symptoms. As a post-hoc analysis, subgroup analyses based on age and sex were conducted on the primary and secondary endpoints. Secondary outcomes (6) and (7) were added during the study before the key opening and data analysis. These changes were made to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the intervention’s impact on gastrointestinal health and safety.

Sample collection

Fecal samples were collected at the following four time points: before antibiotic treatment, on day 7(± 2), day 14(± 2) of eradication therapy, and day 64 (± 7) (at the time of eradication judgment). Fecal samples were collected using a stool collection kit (TechnoSuruga Laboratory Co., Ltd, Japan, FS-0002®) and were stored at − 80 °C until use.

DNA extraction and 16S rDNA sequencing

The QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Manchester, UK) was used in the DNA extraction. Bacterial DNA was extracted from 200 mg of stool sample according to the manufacturer’s protocol and stored at − 80 °C.

Two-step thermal asymmetric interlaced polymerase chain reaction (PCR) method was used to amplify the V3-V4 region of the gene encoding 16S rDNA, and 16S rDNA sequencing was performed using the MiSeq™ system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The protocol was modified slightly from that of the manufacturer. In the first PCR step, DNA extracted from stool samples was amplified under PCR conditions (95 °C for 3 min, followed by 25 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, primer annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and final extension at 72 °C for 5 min) using 2 × KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems, KK2602, Woburn, MA, USA) and Amplicon PCR primers. Purification was performed using AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter, A63881, San Diego, CA, USA) in 80% ethanol. The forward and reverse primer sequences with overhang adapters were 5-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG-3 and 5-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG-3, respectively.

In a second PCR step, purified amplicons were subjected to PCR conditions (95 °C for 3 min, followed by 8 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, primer annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 30 s, and final extension at 72 °C for 5 min) using Nextera XT Index Primer (Illumina, FC-131-2001, San Diego, CA, USA), 2 × KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix, and sterile, purified water. DNA was purified using AMPure XP Beads and 80% ethanol. The DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit® 4 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Q33238) and TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, 5067-5584/5585), and approximately equal amounts of DNA from each sample were pooled. Pooled samples were sequenced using a MiSeq Reagent Kit v2 (500 cycles) (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) on a MiSeq System.

Microbiome analysis

QIIME 1.8.0 was used to perform sequence read processing, quality trimming, operational taxonomic unit definition, and taxonomic assignment. A total of 5000 paired-end reads randomly selected per sample were used for the analysis. Paired-end reads were merged using fastq-join. Chimeric sequences were excluded using USEARCH 6.1.544, and operational taxonomic clustering was performed using UCLUST with a threshold justification of 97%22. Based on 97% sequence similarity, operational taxonomic units were classified into bacterial groups at the genus level using the Greengenes Reference Database (version 13.8)23. α-diversity analysis (observed species, PD whole tree, Shannon, and Chao1), weighted UniFrac principal coordinate analysis, and permutational multivariate analysis of variance were performed using QIIME 1.8.0. Galaxy version 1.0 (The Huttenhower Lab, Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health) was used for linear discriminant effect size analysis to estimate the effect size for each taxon with significantly different abundances. Functional profiling of the intestinal microbiota communities was predicted by phylogenetic investigation of communities using reconstruction of unobserved states (PICRUSt, version 1.0.0) based on the Greengenes database (version 13.5). PICRUSt can predict metagenomic functions using marker genes based on 16S rDNA sequencing and reference genome databases24. The obtained sequence data were registered in the DNA Data Bank of Japan database under the accession number DRA016376.

DAO assay

DAO is used as an indicator of small intestinal mucosal maturity and integrity which inversely correlates with the degree of intestinal damage25,26. We measured serum DAO activity as a noninvasive, indirect marker in order to detect any intestinal mucosal injury associated with post-eradication diarrhea. Previous human studies have shown that circulating DAO activity indirectly reflects intestinal mucosal DAO activity and that more severe tissue injury leads to proportionally lower serum DAO levels27,28. The assays were conducted externally by Macrophi Inc. (Takamatsu city, Kagawa, Japan) by the Takagi method as previously described29. Briefly, 1.5 ml of cadaverine solution (30 mmol/l in PIPES buffer) was incubated at 37 °C for 5 min. Then, 0.1 ml of serum sample was added, mixed, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Following this, 1.5 ml of the color solution (DA-67, POD, ASOD in MES buffer) was added, mixed, and incubated for 60 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 0.05 ml of sodium diethyldithiocarbamate solution (30 mmol/l in water). The absorbance was measured at 668 nm using a spectrophotometer.

Sample size calculation

Assuming that past comparative trials with Lactobacillus and placebo groups during H. pylori eradication therapy are equivalent to this study, we calculated the required sample size using Pearson’s chi-squared test with a significance level of 5% and a power of 80%2. The calculated required sample size per group was 36 participants. Taking dropouts into account, we set the sample size at 38 participants per group, for a total of 76 participants.

Comparison between the groups for the incidence of diarrhea was performed using the chi-squared test. 95% confidence intervals for proportions were calculated using the Wilson score method. We analyzed the impact of E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R on the reduction of bacterial diversity owing to H. pylori eradication therapy. There were no differences in α-diversity, β-diversity or phylum-level microbial composition between the placebo and 3B-R groups before and after eradication therapy (Fig. 3B-E, Table 2, Table S3-8). No other adverse events besides diarrhea were observed. No differences were observed between the placebo group and the E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R group (3B-R group) in terms of the Bristol stool form scale and stool frequency at day 1–7 and day 8–14 after eradication, the FSSG scale at day 7 and day 64, and DAO activity at day 7 post-eradication (Table 2). Of note, we found that there was no significant changes in DAO activity before and after eradication, it suggests that the intestinal injury accompanying post-eradication diarrhea in our cohort may have been relatively mild, and as a result, serum DAO did not reflect any overt mucosal damage (Table 2).

Post-hoc analyses by sex and age

It is known that the composition and diversity of gut microbiota vary with sex and age30. Therefore, it is important to consider the variables of those two factors. We decided to conduct the post-hoc analysis firstly based on sex, however, for both the primary and secondary endpoints, no differences were observed between the groups, except for the suppression of the decrease in the Chao1 index ratio on day 7 after eradication in females in the 3B-R group (Table 3, Table S2-8). Because previous studies have indicated significant changes in the composition of intestinal microbiota in Japanese individuals around the age of 70 years31, we tailored our age stratified analysis approach accordingly. Consistent with previously reported findings31, microbial α-diversity was higher in older adults aged 70 and above compared to those of under 70 years of age, and there was also a significant difference in β-diversity between the two groups (Fig. 4A,B, Table 4). When compared between placebo and the 3B-R groups, no differences in microbial diversity or phylum-level microbial composition were observed both under 70 and over 70 years-old groups prior to eradication therapy (Fig. S1A, B, Table S1 and Table S4-8). The FSSG scores were significantly lower in patients aged 70 years and older; however, no differences were observed between the placebo and the 3B-R groups within each age group prior to eradication therapy (Table S1). Further, we found no differences in the incidence of diarrhea between the placebo and 3B-R groups from day 1 to 7 after eradication therapy initiation both in the older adults and in the younger adults, however, during days 8 to 14, a significant reduction in diarrhea incidence was noted among older adults in the 3B-R group (Fig. 5A). In patients aged ≥ 70, the intra-individual relative changes in gut microbial α-diversity following eradication therapy tended to be lower than those in patients aged < 70 (Fig. 5B). The α-diversity in the 3B-R group tended to be higher than in the placebo group on day 7 after the start of treatment (Fig. 5D, Table 4 and Table S2), on the other hand, this trend was not obvious in patients of under 70 years-old (Fig. 5C, Table 4 and Table S2). In PICRUSt analysis, the subjects aged 70 years and older in the 3B-R group showed decreased pathways, such as the “secretion system” and “bacterial secretion system”, which can contribute to secrete virulence factors for host invasion32 compared to those in the placebo group aged under 70. Discussion

The present study is the first randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study to examine the effects of E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R on the side effects of H. pylori eradication therapy and intestinal microbiota diversity in adults.

In the overall study population, we observed no significant difference in the incidence of diarrhea or all other secondary endpoints between subjects receiving E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R in conjunction with H. pylori eradication therapy and those given a placebo. However, in a post-hoc age-stratified analysis, E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R demonstrated a significant reduction in the side effects of diarrhea among subjects aged 70 years and older who underwent H. pylori eradication therapy. This subgroup also exhibited a tendency towards maintaining α-diversity of intestinal microbiota, alongside alterations in bacterial composition. These findings highlight the potential of E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R to alleviate the side effects of diarrhea in elderly patients by modulating the gut environment.

It is generally known that antibiotic treatment reduces gut microbial diversity5. However, in our study, the subgroup of subjects aged 70 years and older who were administered E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R showed a suppression of the decline in intestinal microbiota diversity after eradication therapy, and subsequently a reduction in the incidence of diarrhea during days 1–7 (Fig. 5). Previous reports have shown that the administration of E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R during H. pylori eradication therapy in adolescent subjects helped to maintain the microbial diversity19. Although the detailed mechanism by which E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R maintains intestinal microbiota diversity is not clear, it has been previously reported that the lactic acid produced by probiotics facilitates cross-feeding by other bacteria33–35. This mechanism might have been at work in our study as well. The preservation of intestinal microbiota diversity contributes to the reduction of diarrhea through the suppression of pathogens and enhanced mucosal defense due to increased mucin levels through the production of SCFAs, secretion of bacteriocins, and competition with pathogens for nutrients and habitat36–38. Our PICRUSt analysis also showed that amino sugar, nucleotide sugar, and galactose metabolisms, which are carbohydrate metabolic processes involved in the production of SCFAs, were enhanced (Fig. S2B). The reduction of diarrhea may have been mediated by the increased production of SCFAs.

In our study, we observed a significant increase in the ratio of Porphyromonadaceae and Parabacteroides in the 3B-R group aged 70 years and older, in addition to maintaining microbiome diversity (Fig. S2A). In animal experiments, it is known that fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) and probiotic administration can increase populations of bacteria such as Porphyromonadaceae and Parabacteroides, thereby improving symptoms of diarrhea caused by colitis39,40. These microbes have been shown to modulate metabolism and produce SCFAs, which may have contributed to the reduction of diarrhea in our study41,42.

One cause of antibiotic-associated diarrhea is the increase in the number of potential pathogens during antibiotic administration25,43,44. In our study, the data showed that the KEGG orthology groups of “Secretion system” and “Bacterial secretion system” were significantly decreased among participants aged 70 years and older of the 3B-R group in the PICRUST analysis (Fig. S2B). Those secretion systems are often associated with the pathogenic processes such as secretion of virulence factors, accordingly, E. faecium129 BIO 3B R might have played a role to suppress the activity of the potential pathogens32.

In our study, patients aged 70 and older tended to exhibit a decrease in α-diversity following decontamination treatment compared to patients under 70 (Fig. 5B). Among the patients aged ≥ 70, administration of E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R tended to suppress the decline in α-diversity —as measured by indices such as Shannon diversity and observed species—and significantly reduce the incidence of diarrhea during days 8–14 post-eradication (Fig. 5A,D). Furthermore, when comparing the changes in α-diversity between patients with and without diarrhea during days 8–14, those who experienced diarrhea tended to show declines in α-diversity—as measured by indices such as Shannon diversity and observed species (Fig. S3B, Table S9)—suggesting that the occurrence of diarrhea during this period may be associated with a reduction in microbial diversity. These results imply that E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R may effectively suppress diarrhea during the later phase by preserving gut microbial diversity. In contrast, during days 1–7 post-eradication, the 3B-R group showed a trend toward an increased incidence of diarrhea compared to the placebo group, although no significant difference in the degree of α-diversity decline was observed between patients with and without diarrhea during this early phase (Fig. S3A, Table S9). This suggests that the early-phase diarrhea (days 1–7 post-eradication) is likely related not only to gut microbiota dysbiosis, but also to additional factors.

A likely explanation is that the antibiotic regimen used for H. pylori eradication—particularly the use of clarithromycin and amoxicillin—directly affects gastrointestinal motility. Clarithromycin, as a macrolide antibiotic, exhibits prokinetic effects by stimulating motilin receptors, which can transiently enhance gut motility and induce diarrhea in the early phase of treatment45,46. Additionally, amoxicillin itself may enhance small intestinal motility due to its inherent effects, although the precise mechanism remains unclear; this enhanced motility may be associated with the early onset of diarrhea47. Thus, while E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R appears to preserve gut microbial diversity and reduce diarrhea incidence during days 8–14 post-eradication in patients aged 70 and older, its effect during the early phase (days 1–7) may be limited or may even contribute to increased diarrhea via mechanisms independent of α-diversity decline. Further prospective studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to elucidate the mechanisms underlying these early-phase adverse events and to optimize the timing of probiotic administration.

The strength of this study lies in its strict adherence to treatment allocation and identity concealment in a placebo-controlled, double-blind, randomized trial. A key limitation of our study is the relatively small sample size, and the subgroup analyses were conducted post hoc. Although we observed statistically significant differences in these analyses, the wide confidence intervals reflect imprecision arising from the small subgroup numbers. Therefore, confirmation in larger, prospective cohorts is warranted.

In conclusion, this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study did not observe beneficial effects of E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R in the general adult population. However, it was suggested that the co-administration of E. faecium 129 BIO 3B-R appears to have a beneficial effect by suppressing the decline in gut microbial diversity, thereby reducing the incidence of diarrhea following H. pylori eradication in the elderly, highlighting the need for further research.
