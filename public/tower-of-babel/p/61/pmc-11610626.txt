Health Literacy and Adherence to Therapy in Type 2 Diabetes: A Cross-Sectional Study in Portugal
Authors: Simone Rodrigues, Ana Isabel Patrício, Cátia Cristina, Flávia Fernandes, Gisela Marcelino Santos, Inês Antunes, Inês Pintalhão, Marília Ribeiro, Rita Lopes, Silvana Moreira, Sofia A Oliveira, Susana Pereira Costa, Susete Simões, Tatiana Carvalho Nunes, Luiz Miguel Santiago, Inês Rosendo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTherapy adherence is a key factor in the control of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Optimal self-care requires skills in health literacy (HL).ObjectiveThis study aims to analyze the relationship between HL and adherence to therapy and to understand the possible influence of other sociodemographic and disease variables.MethodsA multicenter, cross-sectional study was conducted in Portuguese in 13 different primary health care units in both rural and urban environments. A sociodemographic questionnaire and two validated instruments, “Medical Term Recognition Test” and “Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities,” were applied. The last value of hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) and the number of chronic medications were collected from clinical records. Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations were performed as well as multivariable linear regression to assess the association between HL and adherence to therapy.Key ResultsParticipants (n = 354) were on average age 63.67 ± 10.39 years, 57.1% male and 42.9% female, 68.4% with inadequate HL and an average HbA1c of 7 ± 1.18%. Better HL was correlated with higher adherence to the total of self-care activities, nonpharmacological therapy, and foot care. In multivariable linear regression analyses, better HL (β = 0.176, p = .003), less than minimum wage (β = −0.197, p = .001) and insulin therapy (β = 0.272, p = .001) were independently associated with increased adherence to overall self-care activities.ConclusionIn a representative sample of people with T2DM in Portugal, HL was a key factor for greater adherence to demanding self-care activities. [HLRP: Health Literacy Research and Practice. 2024;8(4):e194–e203.]

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic health problem with increasing incidence and a considerable health care burden worldwide (Reed et al., 2021). In 2021, 537 million adults (age 20 to 79 years) were living with diabetes and this number is expected to increase to 783 million by 2045 (International Diabetes Federation, 2021). Portugal is one of the countries with the highest prevalence of diabetes in Europe (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD], 2022), with around 9,8% of people between ages 20 and 79 years having the disease and a significantly higher prevalence with age (Sociedade Portuguesa de Diabetologia, 2023).

Effective management of diabetes requires self-care skills to make significant changes in lifestyle and for correct use of medication (Shrivastava et al., 2013). These skills require adequate HL (Fransen et al., 2012), defined by the Institute of Medicine (US) Committee on Health (2004) as “the degree to which individuals have the capacity to obtain, process and understand basic health information and services needed to make appropriate health decisions” (p.4). Low HL is associated with less knowledge about the disease, worse glycemic control (Powell et al., 2007), and poorer quality of physician-patient communication (Schillinger et al., 2004).

It is also known that nonadherence to medication is associated with poorer control and increased risk of complications (Ho et al., 2006; Nandyala et al., 2018). Adherence to therapy is defined by World Health Organization as “the extent to which a person's behaviour – taking medication, following a diet, and/or executing lifestyle changes, corresponds with agreed recommendations from a health care provider” (World Health Organization, 2003, p.3). However, the relationship between HL, treatment adherence and glycemic control is controversial in the existing literature. Several studies report that low HL is associated with nonadherence to treatment (Fan et al., 2016; Nandyala et al., 2018; Osborn et al., 2011) and poor glycemic control (Powell et al., 2007; Schillinger et al., 2002; Souza et al., 2014). Other studies found no association between HL and diabetes medication adherence (Thurston et al., 2015) or hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) (Bains & Egede, 2011) while some report that individuals with limited HL have better adherence to diabetes therapy (White et al., 2013).

In Portugal, one study considers the relationship between HL and adherence to T2DM therapy and reports good levels of adherence in a sample with low HL (Cruz et al., 2011). Two other studies report a significant positive association between diabetes control ability and diabetes-related knowledge, participants with higher literacy presenting better disease control abilities (Amorim et al., 2015; Oliveira, 2016;). Another study states a significant correlation between lower health literacy and the presence of diagnosed T2DM complications (Cravo et al., 2023). It is important to better understand this controversial association between HL and adherence to therapy to help health care providers and policy makers to develop adequate strategies to improve adherence to therapy among people with T2DM.

A pilot study in Portugal by Fernandes et al. (2018) found a statistically significant correlation between HL and higher adherence to nonpharmacologic therapy, but the study results could not be generalized due to small sample size. The purpose of this research was to extend the sample of that pilot study to obtain more data regarding the relationship between HL and adherence to therapy among people with T2DM in Portugal. Second, we aimed to understand the possible confounding influence of sociodemographic, socioeconomic and disease variables in the relationship between HL and adherence to therapy.

Methods

This was a multicenter, cross-sectional survey in primary care, using a convenience sample of adult patients with T2DM in Portugal. The sample size (n = 377) was calculated with the online tool (http://www.raosoft.com/samplesize.html), with a margin of error of 5% and a 95% confidence interval. The number of questionnaires to be collected in each region was proportionally calculated according to the national distribution of diabetes patients by sex and age (Direção-Geral da Saúde, 2017; Sociedade Portuguesa de Diabetologia, 2023). At the time this study was conducted, in mainland Portugal there were five Health Administrative Regions, Norte, Centro, Lisboa e Vale do Tejo, Alentejo, and Algarve, that managed the National Health Service. The Autonomous Regions of Madeira and Açores develop and administer their own Regional Health Services. Predicting possible dropouts and incomplete questionnaires, the numbers for each region were calculated for a total of n = 400.

After ethical approval, eligible patients with T2DM were purposively selected by their family doctors, invited volunteer collaborators, according to sex and age distribution sent to them, during a regular clinic appointment in 13 different primary health care units of 6 of the 7 mentioned Portuguese regions, both rural and urban areas. Alentejo was not included int the study due to no response of its Ethics Committee. Patients were included in the study if they were age 20 to 79 years (Sociedade Portuguesa de Diabetologia, 2023), with follow up in the primary health care center of recruitment and had a clinic appointment in a pre-specified time frame, were able to read and speak Portuguese, had a documented HbA1c measurement and were willing to participate and signed an informed consent. Data were collected from December 2017 to January 2019 using a self-completion printed questionnaire including sociodemographic variables and two validated scales, distributed by the invited family doctors to the patients that met the inclusion criteria, until the pre-specified necessary number was gathered for each doctor (Table A).

Sociodemographic variables were collected on sex, age, occupation, school education (higher vs. equal to or less than 4 years), salary (higher vs. equal to or less than the national minimum wage) and whether they were living alone. The socioeconomic level was determined by calculating the Socio-Economic Deprivation Index (SEDI)—from 0 to 3 points, with 1 point being assigned to each of the affirmative answers and 0 to the negative ones: “School education equal to or less than four years?”; “Salary equal to or less than the national minimum wage?”; and “Do you live alone?” (Lamnisos et al., 2019; Ribeiro et al., 2018). From the patient's clinical records, doctors collected the last HbA1c value, number of different drugs of chronic prescription, if the patient was on insulin therapy and which type of oral antidiabetic agents being prescribed.

The HL assessment tool used was the Portuguese language version of Medical Term Recognition Test (METER) (Paiva et al., 2014; Rawson et al., 2010). The Portuguese version of METER (Paiva et al., 2014) is a self-administered instrument comprised of 40 medical words (e.g., fatigue, stress, anemia) and 30 made-up non-words that intuitively sound like real medical terms (e.g., “midlocation”, “insomniate”, “depretion”). Adequate HL is defined as a score of at least 35 in 40 of the real words and 18 in 30 of non-words (Paiva et al., 2014), but could also be scored as the number of words correctly marked with highly similar results (Rawson et al., 2010).

The first scoring method was used for descriptive analysis and the second one for the other statistical analysis. The Portuguese version of METER showed a high degree of reliability, Cronbach's alpha of 0.92 for the first dimension and 0.83 for the second, and validity was assessed through association with education level and health-related occupation, in which physicians scored higher than any other group (p < .001) (Paiva et al., 2014).

Adherence to therapy was assessed with the Portuguese language version of the revised Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA) (Bastos et al., 2007; Toobert et al., 2000). The SDSCA assesses six self-care dimensions of the diabetes treatment regimen over the previous seven days: diet care (general and specific), physical activity, blood glucose monitoring, foot care, use of medication and smoking habits, Cronbach's alpha ranging between 0.68 and 0.36 in sub-scales (Bastos et al., 2007), and test-retest correlations were moderate (mean = 0.40) (Toobert et al., 2000). Answers are given in number of days per week with an equivalence between the answers and points attributed, on a 0 to 7 points scale, with 0 as the least desirable and 7 as the most, except for the dimension of specific diet, in which the values are reversed. Each of the first five dimensions was scored by calculating the average number of days of its respective items. Two of the three questions of section six (medication) could only be answered by patients who were insulin-treated. Therefore, the average score of this section considered only the first question “On how many of the last seven days, did you take your recommended diabetes medication?” The average of the first 5 dimensions, related to nonpharmacological self-care activities, was also calculated. The higher the score, the better the adherence to the therapeutic regimen. Dimension 7, regarding smoking habits, was analysed independently in the characterization of the sample with all its possible answers and was then recoded as smoker and non-smoker to perform the other statistical analyses.

Ethical Approval

Approval was obtained by the Faculty of Medicine of the University of Coimbra and by the Ethics Committees of all Regional Health Administrations in Portugal. Authorization was also granted from the National Commission of Data Protection (authorization number 14443/2017). Signed informed consent was also obtained from the participants.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analysis was conducted for all variables. The normality of the sample data was tested with Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and most variables did not demonstrate normal distribution. Therefore, nonparametric tests were used. Bivariate analysis using Spearman correlation and Mann-Whitney U test were performed first for HL and each section of SDSCA, then for the sections of SDSCA that were significantly correlated with HL and possible continuous or categorical confounders (sociodemographic, socioeconomic and disease variables). Then, the same bivariate analysis was used to assess associations or correlations between HL and possible confounders.

Multivariable linear regression with forward selection method were conducted to assess the association between HL and adherence to therapy. The dependent variables for each proposed model were the total of self-care activities and the total of nonpharmacologic self-care activities. The variables that were found to be significant (p < .05) in bivariate analysis were chosen for this analysis. Collinearity tests were performed, and the normality of residuals was tested.

Results

A total of 361 individuals with T2DM were recruited by the collaborators in the 13 primary health care centres, but seven were excluded for incomplete data. The final sample, without data from the region of Alentejo due to due to no response of its Ethics Committee, consisted of 354 participants with an average age of 63.67 ± 10.39 years, the majority being male (57.1%) and 42.9% were female. Regarding socioeconomic variables, n = 183 (54.5%) studied for 4 years or less, 201 (59.8%) earned more than the national minimum wage and 304 (86.1%) did not live alone. In relation to occupation, 198 (56.3%) were retired (Tables 1 and2).

Comparing HL (words) between the categories of possible confounders, significant differences were found between the categories of school education (p < .001), salary (p = .002), OAD (p = .001), and SEDI with a negative weak, significant correlation (rho = −0.317, p < .001). Higher scores of HL were found in people who studied more than four years, earned more than the national minimum wage, were not medicated with OAD, and had better socioeconomic level (lower SEDI) (Table 4).

Two multiple linear regression models were obtained to predict adherence to overall self-care activities and to nonpharmacological self-care activities, based on HL and on the variables found to be significant (p < .05) in bivariate analysis (school education, salary, insulin therapy and OAD) to understand what the independent predictors were. The results for each variable included in the final models are presented in Table 5. The model that predicts adherence to the total of self-care activities had a significant regression equation (F(3, 270) = 9.579, p < .001) with an adjusted R2 of 0,086. Participant's predicted adherence to overall self-care activities is equal to 4,041 + 0,016 (health literacy) – 0.347 (salary) + 0.807 (insulin), where HL is measured in points, salary is coded as 0 = equal or inferior to the national minimum wage, 1 = more than the national minimum wage and insulin therapy is coded as 0 = no, 1 = yes. Participant's adherence to overall self-care activities increased 0.016 days for each point of HL score, 0.807 days if they were insulin-treated and decreased 0.347 days if they earned more than the national minimum wage. Salary, insulin therapy and HL were significant predictors of adherence.

Discussion

The relationship between HL and adherence to therapy remains controversial in the existing literature (Bailey et al., 2014). In the present study, although not significantly different, people with higher HL tended to adhere less to medication. The same result has been reported by another study conducted in Portugal (Cruz et al., 2011), even though different measurement tools were used, the sample was smaller, and was mostly female. Another study in United States concluded that for 1-point increase on HL score, participants were 1.8 times more likely to optimally take medications, but the sample had very high levels of HL (Nandyala et al., 2018). Osborn et al. (2011) reported that low HL was related to less adherence to diabetes medications, and that HL explained racial disparities in adherence. Fan et al. (2016) investigated the relationship between HL and medication nonadherence distinguishing between unintentional (forgetting to take medication) and intentional (deciding not to take it) nonadherence. They found that limited HL was associated with increased unintentional nonadherence, but not with intentional nonadherence and proposed that distinguishing them when assessing nonadherence may elucidate the inconsistent results in the literature (Fan et al., 2016).

Patients with diabetes are expected to follow a complex set of nonpharmacological self-care activities (Toobert et al., 2000). A study with methodological similarities to ours concluded that HL is not directly related to self-care (Bains & Egede, 2011), but its sample was mostly of Black or African American participants and a different tool to measure of HL was used. An additional study conducted in Iran (Chahardah-Cherik et al., 2018) concluded that higher HL was positively associated with all dimensions of health promoting behaviors. Our study found that better HL is related to increased adherence to nonpharmacological therapy and specifically to foot care. This is important because it is known that improving self-care activities can significantly reduce the chance of developing long-term diabetes complications (Shrivastava et al., 2013). Foot disease, an important cause of increased morbidity and mortality among diabetes patients, results in important economic burden (Raghav et al., 2018).

The discrepancies in the literature on HL and adherence to therapy are evident, most HL assessment tools not capturing the complexity of the construct of HL. In a clinical setting, it is important that the instruments are brief to avoid withdrawal of participants so further investigation should focus on the development or adaptation of instruments that allow a complete assessment of HL-related skills easy to apply in clinical care. Discrepancy also arises from the fact that different studies do not adjust for the same potential confounders. Some studies adjust for all covariates, that are referred as confounders, in the existing literature. We opted to first assess which variables were significantly correlated with self-care activities or with HL, and those were the ones used in our linear regression analyses, so that the models predicting adherence to therapy were customized to the characteristics of our sample.

Although current findings are not consistent with the association between HL and adherence to therapy, some studies have advanced to a practical approach. An intervention program was developed in a primary care unit in Portugal, based on the education of patients with T2DM. With only four educational sessions, there were significant improvements in knowledge, self-care activities and HL level in a nonrepresentative sample (Lopes, 2014). These results revealed the importance of the therapeutic patient education, which improves clinical, lifestyle and psycho-social outcomes, now considered as a key element in the management of T2DM (Coppola et al., 2016).

Like other research conducted in Portugal (Cruz et al., 2013; Paiva et al., 2017), our sample presents mostly inadequate HL (68,4%). Although not statistically significant, it has been found that older patients tend to be less health literate, according to what has already been reported (Thurston et al., 2015). The older population is more vulnerable in the context of HL, so they deserve greater attention in the need of the development of skills to manage their disease.

Better HL, being on insulin-treatment or earning less than the national minimum wage were related to increased adherence to overall self-care and nonpharmacological activities. Therefore, having lower wages does not seem to be a constraint to greater adherence to important self-care activities, at least to the nonpharmacological ones, and this deserves further research. Regarding different dimensions of self-care activities, physical activity had the lowest adherence, which is one of the greatest challenges to patients with T2DM (Thomas et al., 2004). We did not find a significant correlation between HL and physical activity, but a large study of 3241 participants concluded that low HL was associated with insufficient physical activity (Geboers et al., 2016).

Self-monitoring of blood glucose levels was also of low adherence, similar to the results of another study conducted in Portugal that reported 33.6% of patients with diabetes performed daily monitorization of blood glucose (Oliveira, 2016). However, according to the newest evidence-based recommendation, self-monitorization of blood glucose levels should be discontinued in non-insulin–treated patients with type 2 diabetes (Colégio da Especialidade de Medicina Geral e Familiar da Ordem dos Médicos, n.d.). The average value of the last HbA1c measurement was 7.0 ± 1.18% (53 mmol/mol), slightly higher than the 6.8% (51 mmol/mol) previously reported by the National Diabetes Observatory for Family Health Units (Sociedade Portuguesa de Diabetologia, 2023). Although not significant, the last value of HbA1c tended to decrease with higher HL, in accordance with other studies (Schillinger et al, 2002; Souza et al., 2014).

This research article is a partial result of an academic thesis (Rodrigues, 2019) and has some limitations: the sample was a purposive one and it was smaller than the calculated one, with no data collected in the region of Alentejo despite the investigators' efforts. Nevertheless, the sample presented a similar distribution to the one of people with T2DM in Portugal (Ribeiro et al., 2018). An interobserver bias is also possible although detailed written instructions were sent to all the doctors who collected the data. Moreover, we used the unadjusted scoring method of METER, which is the number of words correctly recognized. This method is not validated for the Portuguese population, but it was used in the original version of METER and was considered a faster scoring method for clinical settings (Rawson et al., 2010). Furthermore, METER only assesses reading skills, so other important competences were not assessed, and self-reported adherence may be a source of biased data. However, most evidence indicates that this type of measure corresponds moderately to other adherence measures and can provide actionable information in the context of general practice clinical care (Stirratt et al., 2015).

Future research should investigate the longitudinal effect of more fully assessed HL on adherence to therapy and its impact on outcomes of diabetes, not only on metabolic control but also on its preventable complications. The investigators expect that these findings will stimulate more research towards finding how to practically identify patients with low HL in the clinical setting and which projects should be implemented to cope with this obstacle to adequate self-care among patients with T2DM.

Conclusion

In this multicentric study, better HL was associated with increased adherence to overall self-care activities and particularly to nonpharmacological therapy, after adjustment to socioeconomic and disease related variables. Improving HL may mitigate diabetic foot outcomes. T2DM is strongly related to inadequate lifestyle, so an important approach to achieve its effective management through greater adherence to self-care behaviors is the investment in strategies for improvement of HL.
