Oxidative DNA Damage Exacerbates the Mutagenic Potential of Alternative DNA Structures via Altered DNA Repair Processing
Authors: Alex W. Klattenhoff, Maha Zewail‐Foote, Arti Madan, Anna Chiu, Karen M. Vasquez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAlternative DNA structure‐forming (i.e., non‐B) sequences such as H‐DNA‐forming sequences are enriched at chromosomal translocation hotspots in human cancer genomes, underscoring their role in genomic instability. H‐DNA is particularly susceptible to DNA damage by reactive oxygen species (ROS), a common byproduct from both endogenous metabolism and environmental contaminants, thereby exacerbating its mutagenic potential. Oxidative lesions within B‐DNA are efficiently processed by base excision repair (BER), whereas H‐DNA is processed in a mutagenic fashion by nucleotide excision repair (NER). Thus, we speculate that the repair of oxidative lesions within H‐DNA will promote aberrant BER and NER processing, ultimately enhancing mutagenesis. Here, we examine the processing of oxidative damage within H‐DNA by measuring the changes in mutation frequencies and spectra, as well as the association with key NER and BER proteins in human cells in the presence or absence of specific DNA repair proteins. Our results demonstrate that oxidatively damaged H‐DNA serves as a substrate for both BER and NER and reveals an interplay between BER and NER proteins, which influences mutation outcomes. This novel framework establishes a link between oxidative stress, DNA repair, and H‐DNA‐associated mutagenesis, providing insight into how environmentally relevant DNA damage can drive sequence‐specific genomic instability at cancer‐associated hotspots.

Introduction

Genomic instability often occurs at specific mutation hotspots in the genome, suggesting that mutations are not random but predisposed at specific loci (Nakamura and Takada 2021; World Health Organization 2024). The inherent qualities of DNA sequence and structure contribute to the formation of mutation hotspots; for example, alternative DNA structures (i.e., non‐B DNA) are known to induce genomic instability (Zhao et al. 2010; Wang and Vasquez 2014). Genomic instability is also elevated by exogenous and endogenous factors, including oxidative stress (OS), which arises from cellular metabolism and environmental contaminants that can generate mutagenic DNA lesions (Valavanidis et al. 2009; van Loon et al. 2010; Ziech et al. 2011). These lesions can differentially impact non‐B DNA structures, influencing damage distribution and repair outcomes (Beckett et al. 2012; Broxson et al. 2014). Although genomic features, oxidative damage, and DNA repair pathways each contribute to genomic instability, their interdynamics have not been thoroughly investigated. We address this gap by investigating how oxidative stress‐induced DNA damage impacts the mutagenic potential of alternative DNA‐forming sequences throughout the genome and the repair proteins that process them.

Alternative DNA structures, or non‐B DNA, deviate from the canonical B‐DNA helix described by Watson, Crick, and Franklin (Mirkin 2008). These alternative DNA structures co‐localize with and are enriched at mutation hotspots in human cancer genomes (Bacolla et al. 2016; Wang and Vasquez 2023), implicating them in cancer etiology. H‐DNA, a well‐characterized mutagenic alternative DNA structure (Wang and Vasquez 2004; Wang and Vasquez 2023), occurs in the human genome ~1 in every 50,000 bases and is enriched in and around DNA regulatory sequences (Wang and Vasquez 2023). H‐DNA forms when a purine‐rich, mirror‐repeat DNA sequence folds back on itself to form an intramolecular triplex and an exposed single‐stranded DNA region (McKinney et al. 2020; Hisey et al. 2024). Using an H‐DNA‐forming sequence found near a translocation breakpoint hotspot in the human c‐MYC promoter associated with Burkitt lymphoma, we have previously demonstrated that the mutagenic potential of H‐DNA results from aberrant nucleotide excision repair (NER) processing and leads to the formation of DNA double‐strand breaks and subsequent deletions (Zhao et al. 2018).

Exogenous and endogenous factors also contribute to genomic instability through OS and the associated production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). ROS are generated during oxidative metabolism (Shimura‐Miura et al. 1999), inflammation (Tubbs and Nussenzweig 2017), and exposure to environmental stressors (Halliwell and Cross 1994; Gracy et al. 1999), and are elevated within tumors (Hoeijmakers 2009; Perillo et al. 2020). Importantly, guanine‐rich sequences and single‐stranded DNA regions, both found in H‐DNA structures, are particularly susceptible to ROS‐induced damage (Kawanishi et al. 2001; Chan et al. 2012; Lu et al. 2015). Consistent with this, we have shown that H‐DNA accumulates more oxidative DNA damage than B‐DNA in response to OS (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025). Although base excision repair (BER) typically recognizes and removes oxidative DNA lesions with high fidelity, we have also found that oxidative lesions within H‐DNA‐forming sequences are refractory to repair (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025). Since ROS are generated in proliferating cells and tumor microenvironments, they likely promote oxidative lesions within H‐DNA structures.

Recently, we found that H‐DNA‐forming sequences are hotspots for ROS‐induced DNA damage, which can lead to strand‐specific accumulation of oxidative lesions in H‐DNA structures over that of B‐DNA (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025). Oxidized pyrimidines preferentially accumulated in the single‐stranded, pyrimidine‐rich DNA region, whereas oxidized purine lesions were pronounced in the guanine‐rich DNA triplex region. Although oligonucleotides containing single oxidative lesions were still able to form H‐DNA, the oxidative lesions led to destabilization and/or conformational alterations of the H‐DNA structure. Importantly, the accumulation of oxidative lesions led to increased mutation frequencies and altered mutation spectra between B‐DNA and H‐DNA under OS in mammalian cells, suggesting differences in mutagenic processing of H‐DNA.

Although BER typically recognizes and removes oxidative lesions and NER recognizes and processes H‐DNA structures in a mutagenic fashion, we have recently shown that proteins from both the BER and NER pathways interact with H‐DNA at varying levels of OS, potentially modulating its mutagenic potential (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025). These findings led us to investigate how BER and NER, which both recognize structurally distorted DNA, process oxidative lesions within H‐DNA. We hypothesized that oxidative damage within H‐DNA is processed in a mutagenic fashion via aberrant BER and NER processing. Here, we investigate how BER and NER proteins contribute to H‐DNA‐induced mutagenesis under conditions of OS by evaluating the association of key BER and NER proteins with H‐DNA in the presence or absence of oxidative damage. Our results suggest that oxidative lesions within H‐DNA are processed through an intricate interplay between BER and NER proteins. Since H‐DNA‐forming sequences co‐localize with and are enriched at cancer‐associated genes, these findings establish a novel process linking OS to cancer etiology.

Materials and Methods

Construction and Preparation of B‐DNA and H‐DNA Mutation Reporters

Mutation reporters of about 5100 bp containing the H‐DNA‐forming sequence (5′‐ATTCGCGGGAGGGGCGCTTATGGGGAGGGAAC) or control B‐DNA sequence (5′‐AATTCGCGGGAGGGGCGCTTATGGAGGGGGAAC) were generated as previously described from stock pMycY and pMycAG plasmids, respectively (Wang and Vasquez 2004). Briefly, Escherichia coli cells were transformed with purified plasmids, cultured overnight, and reporters were purified using a Plasmid Maxi Kit (QIAGEN). DNA concentrations were measured spectrophotometrically using a Nanodrop and H‐DNA formation was verified using the S1 nuclease assay as described (Wang and Vasquez 2004; Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025).

Induction of ROS‐Induced Oxidative Stress

Supercoiled reporter plasmid DNA was incubated with 50 μM hydrogen peroxide and 3 or 10 μM of ferrous sulfate in a buffer containing 30 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.2), 25 mM NaCl and 4 mM MgCl2 to induce OS as we have previously described (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025). Reaction mixtures were incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Solutions of ferrous sulfate and hydrogen peroxide were prepared immediately before use.

Cell Lines

U2OS cell lines were cultured with DMEM media containing 10% FBS, 1% Pen/Strep, and 1% GlutaMax. Wild‐type U2OS cells (ATCC, #HTB‐96) were used unless otherwise specified, including the CRISPR‐Cas9 knockout U2OS cell lines for either OGG1, APE1, or XPA (provided by Dr. Robert Sobol, Brown University) and previously characterized (Bordelon 2023).

Mutagenesis Assays

Mutagenesis assays were performed as we have previously described (Wang and Vasquez 2004). Briefly, 3 μg of oxidatively damaged or undamaged mutation reporters containing either an H‐DNA‐forming sequence from the regulatory sequence of the human c‐MYC gene (pMycY) or a control B‐DNA‐forming sequence (pMycAG) were transfected into human U2OS cell lines using GenePORTER 2 (AMSBio). These mutation reporters also contain an SV40 origin and the large T antigen gene to allow for replication in the human U2OS cell lines. Reporter DNA was isolated from the human U2OS cells after 48 h of incubation using the Plasmid Mini Kit (QIAGEN). Isolated reporters then underwent two rounds of Dpn1 digestion (New England Biolabs) to remove any reporters that were not replicated within human U2OS cells or that did not enter the cells during transfection to circumvent differential transfection efficiencies. The remaining replicated DNA reporters were purified using phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation.

Blue‐white screening assays were then performed by transforming the electrocompetent Escherichia coli strain MBM7070 with purified reporters. Mutations in the supF gene directly downstream of the B‐DNA‐ or H‐DNA‐forming sequences were observed as white colonies on LB/carbenicillin agar plates containing X‐Gal and IPTG, whereas wild‐type sequences appeared as blue colonies. Mutation frequencies for each sample were calculated as a ratio of white mutant colonies over the total number of colonies (blue + white). Over 50,000 total colonies were counted in each of at least three biological replicates for each sample condition. Mutation spectra were determined using Sanger sequencing of 5–10 randomly selected mutant colonies from each biological replicate. Statistical comparisons were calculated using an unpaired, one‐sided Wilcoxon rank sums test (α = 0.05, GraphPad Prism version 10).

ChIP Assays

A modified protocol of the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling) was used for chromatin immunoprecipitation assays (ChIP). Kit reagents were used unless otherwise indicated. Briefly, pMyc reporters with or without oxidative stress were transfected into ~8 × 106 U2OS cells (ATCC, Catalog #HTB‐96) using GenePORTER 2 (AMSBio). After 24 h, cells were fixed, gently lysed to collect nuclei that were then processed into chromatin fragments. Fragmented chromatin in ChIP buffer was used to prepare an input control set aside for quantitative PCR analysis and the remainder was used to prepare immunoprecipitations with one of the following antibodies: the negative control Normal Rabbit IgG (DF 1/500, Cell Signaling), anti‐APE1 (DF 1/200, Proteintech) or anti‐XPA (DF 1/200, Abcam). Immunoprecipitations were complexed overnight at 4°C with rotation, precipitated with Protein A/G beads, washed, and eluted to isolate sample DNA that was associated with the respective target protein.

The sample DNA from four biological replicates was then measured using quantitative real‐time PCR (qPCR). Three technical replicates were loaded for each sample into a 96‐well plate with iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio‐Rad), forward (5′‐GCCCCCCTGACGAGCATCAC), and reverse (5′‐TAGTTACCGGATAAGGCGCAGCGG) primers on the same plate as its associated negative control and input control. qPCR reads were conducted using the CFX96 Real‐Time PCR System (Bio‐Rad) and associated software to determine percent input (% Input) for each sample. Individual Cq values were first averaged to calculate Cq technical means. Each Cq technical mean was then adjusted to the Cq technical mean of its associated input loading control and expressed as a percentage of input (% Input). % Input means were then calculated across the four biological replicates (n = 4). Statistical differences were finally calculated using an unpaired, one‐sided Wilcoxon Rank Sums test (α = 0.05, GraphPad Prism version 10).

Results

Alternative DNA structure‐induced genomic instability in human cells has been well established by our laboratory and others, including purine‐rich mirror‐repeat sequences capable of forming H‐DNA (Charlesworth et al. 1994; Wang and Vasquez 2004; Wang et al. 2008; Inagaki et al. 2009; Bacolla et al. 2016; Zhao et al. 2018; McKinney et al. 2020; Wang and Vasquez 2023). Further, we recently demonstrated that H‐DNA is more susceptible to DNA damage induced by OS compared to B‐DNA, which leads to the accumulation of oxidized pyrimidines and purines, including 8‐deoxyguanosine (8‐oxo‐dG) (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025). Since BER recognizes and repairs oxidative lesions and NER recognizes and processes H‐DNA structures, we sought to investigate how both these pathways may be involved in processing oxidative damage within H‐DNA. Based on our hypothesis, we investigated how BER and NER proteins process oxidative damage within H‐DNA and contribute to genomic instability.

The NER Protein XPA Impacts H‐DNA Mutation Frequencies and Spectra

NER is required for the mutagenic processing of H‐DNA (Zhao et al. 2018); however, little is known about how it processes H‐DNA containing oxidative lesions. To investigate this, we generated two mutation reporters; one containing a well‐characterized mutagenic H‐DNA sequence found near a translocation breakpoint hotspot in the c‐MYC gene in Burkitt lymphoma (H‐DNA), and a control B‐DNA sequence similar to the H‐DNA sequence, but with two base pairs switched to disrupt the mirror symmetry required for H‐DNA formation (B‐DNA) (Wang and Vasquez 2004). Mutation reporters were then subjected to no additional OS (endogenous; −) or to increasing levels of OS (+, ++) as previously described (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025). We subsequently used these mutation reporters to determine the impact of key DNA repair proteins from the BER or NER pathways on mutation frequencies and spectra.

Mutation reporters were transfected into human U2OS cell lines in the presence or absence of Xeroderma Pigmentosa, Complementation Group A (XPA), a key protein required for functional NER, to observe the effects of DNA repair processing using a blue/white screening assay. Similar to our previous studies (Zhao et al. 2018; Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025), the H‐DNA‐induced mutation frequency is significantly higher than B‐DNA (3.0‐fold increase, p = 0.01) in the absence of OS (Figure 1A). Additionally, this trend remains even with increasing OS (1.6‐fold increase, p = 0.05 and 1.6‐fold increase, p < 0.001, respectively) (Figure 1A).

B‐DNA‐ or H‐DNA‐forming reporter sequences were exposed to different levels of oxidative stress (−, +, or ++) and then transfected into human U2OS cell lines with either (A and B) wild type or (C and D) XPA knockout phenotypes to allow for DNA repair processing. Mutation frequencies were quantified (A and C) using a blue‐white screening assay. Each condition represents the average of at least three replicates (+SEM). Statistical comparisons between conditions were performed using a two‐way ANOVA with significance indicated as p < 0.05 (*), or < 0.01 (**). Mutation spectra were then determined (B and D) using Sanger sequencing, with at least 15 mutants analyzed per condition and classified as point mutations (1 bp), small deletions (< 15 bp), or large deletions (> 15 bp). Mutation spectra ratios are shown per condition as a ratio of mutation frequency.

As expected in the absence of XPA, the mutation frequency of H‐DNA is no longer significantly higher than B‐DNA in the presence or absence of OS (Figure 1C). Large deletions are characteristic of H‐DNA‐induced mutations, compared to the predominant point mutations of B‐DNA (Figure 1B), and the H‐DNA‐induced large deletions were substantially attenuated in the absence of XPA (Figure 1D). H‐DNA‐induced large deletion percentages in wild‐type cells were as follows: endogenous (−; 18%), lower OS (+; 27%), and higher OS (++; 47%) (Figure 1B), while in the absence of XPA, they were reduced to: endogenous (−; 0%), lower OS (+; 6%), and higher OS (++; 0%) (Figure 1D).

BER Proteins OGG1 and APE1 Impact H‐DNA Mutation Frequencies and Spectra

While oxidative DNA damage can be repaired by the NER mechanism, BER is the more prominent mechanism for the repair of oxidative DNA lesions. BER glycosylases are capable of recognizing oxidative lesions in both single‐stranded and double‐stranded DNA (Wallace 2013; Zhou et al. 2013; Schermerhorn and Delaney 2014); however, it is not known if BER can process oxidative lesions within H‐DNA. To investigate this, B‐DNA or H‐DNA reporter sequences were exposed to varying levels of OS and transfected into human U2OS cells in the presence or absence of key BER proteins (OGG1 or APE1) to determine the mutation frequencies and spectra. The BER knockout cell lines showed a consistent increase in mutation frequencies with increasing OS (Figure 2A,C), but with notable differences as compared to controls.

B‐DNA‐ or H‐DNA‐forming reporter sequences were exposed to increasing levels of oxidative stress (−, +, or ++) and then transfected into human U2OS cell lines with either (A and B) OGG1 knockout or (C and D) APE1 knockout phenotypes to allow for DNA repair processing. Mutation frequencies were then quantified (A and C) using a blue‐white screening assay. Each condition represents the average of at least three replicates (+SEM). Statistical comparisons between conditions were performed using a two‐way ANOVA with significance indicated as p < 0.05 (*), < 0.01 (**), or < 0.001 (***). Mutation spectra were then determined (B and D) using Sanger sequencing, with at least 15 mutants analyzed per condition and classified as point mutations (1 bp), small deletions (< 15 bp), or large deletions (> 15 bp). Mutation spectra ratios are shown per condition as a ratio of mutation frequency.

In the absence of 8‐oxoguanine DNA Glycosylase 1 (OGG1), the primary glycosylase that recognizes 8‐oxo‐dG lesions, there is a significant difference in the mutation frequencies between B‐DNA and H‐DNA at endogenous (−) and lower (+) levels of OS (5.7‐fold increase, p = 0.007 and 2.1‐fold increase, p = 0.02, respectively) (Figure 2A). This significant fold increase between B‐DNA and H‐DNA is notably higher than cells with OGG1 (Figure 1A). B‐DNA‐ and H‐DNA‐induced mutation frequency differences become even more pronounced in the absence of Apurinic/Apyrimidinic Endonuclease 1 (APE1), a key BER enzyme that cleaves the DNA backbone at AP sites that are generated after glycosylase activity. In the absence of APE1, the B‐DNA‐ and H‐DNA‐induced mutation frequencies remain significantly different at endogenous (−) and increasing levels of OS (5.22‐fold increase, p < 0.001; 5.17‐fold increase, p < 0.001; and 3.1‐fold increase, p < 0.001, respectively) (Figure 2C). A similar effect is observed in the mutation spectra. When compared to the wild‐type mutation spectra (Figure 1B), the B‐DNA‐induced mutants in the absence of OGG1 or APE1 remain primarily point mutations (Figure 2B,D). However, in the absence of OGG1 or APE1, large deletions represent a larger fraction of the H‐DNA‐induced mutants across all levels of OS (Figure 2B,D).

Differential Recruitment of BER and NER Proteins to H‐DNA With Increasing Levels of OS

Since H‐DNA‐induced mutagenesis was most impacted by the loss of the NER protein XPA and the BER protein APE1 under conditions of OS (Figures 1C and 2C), we next used chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) to measure the association of these repair proteins with B‐DNA compared to H‐DNA. We chose to focus on the BER protein APE1 as the significant differences between the mutation frequencies of B‐DNA and H‐DNA in the absence of OGG1 were not as pronounced. Following a similar experimental approach, B‐DNA or H‐DNA reporters were exposed to different levels of OS before transfection into wild‐type (WT), XPA KO or APE1 KO U2OS cells. We then immunoprecipitated chromatin fragments using antibodies against XPA, APE1, or with an IgG antibody used as a negative control to quantify the relative amount of B‐DNA or H‐DNA associated with each protein compared to the total DNA using quantitative PCR. Consistent with our recent findings (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025), the association of APE1 with B‐DNA increases with OS whereas its association with H‐DNA decreases (Figure 3A). Interestingly, the association of APE1 to B‐DNA and H‐DNA follows similar trends even in the absence of XPA (Figure 3B). Specifically, the association of APE1 with B‐DNA significantly increases from endogenous (−) to higher (++) levels of OS (6.8‐fold increase, p = 0.001) (Figure 3B). Conversely, the association of APE1 with H‐DNA significantly decreases from endogenous (−) to higher (++) levels of OS (3.5‐fold decrease, p = 0.02) (Figure 3B). These findings in the absence of XPA not only reiterate the wild‐type association trends of APE1 with B‐DNA and H‐DNA as previously described (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025), but also suggest that the association of APE1 to B‐DNA and H‐DNA occurs independently of functional NER (i.e., in the absence of XPA).

We have also previously shown that NER proteins associate with H‐DNA in human cells (Zhao et al. 2018). As found from our recent results (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025), the association of XPA with H‐DNA increases with OS but is not significantly associated with B‐DNA (Figure 3A). Similarly, XPA retains its association pattern with H‐DNA even in the absence of APE1 (Figure 3C), where XPA significantly increases its association with H‐DNA from endogenous (−) to higher (++) levels of OS (2.6‐fold increase, p = 0.001) (Figure 3C). These findings reiterate the wild‐type association patterns of XPA to H‐DNA as we previously described (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025), and further suggest that the association of XPA with H‐DNA is independent of BER interactions in the presence or absence of OS.

Discussion

H‐DNA is known to promote genomic instability via NER activity, which promotes its mutagenic processing and the formation of DNA double‐strand breaks (Zhao et al. 2018). Additionally, we have demonstrated that oxidative DNA damage accumulates preferentially at H‐DNA compared to B‐DNA. Oxidized purines such as 8‐oxo‐dG are enriched in the H‐DNA triplex region and oxidized pyrimidines are enriched in the single‐stranded region (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025). Other alternative DNA structures such as hairpins and G4 DNA have also been shown to be susceptible to oxidative damage within their loop and overhang regions (Steenken and Jovanovic 1997; Ohno et al. 2006; Clark et al. 2012; Ding et al. 2017) and are processed by DNA repair proteins (Fleming et al. 2015; Fleming et al. 2019; Roychoudhury et al. 2020). Together, these results suggest that non‐B DNA structures serve as hotspots for DNA damage accumulation where aberrant DNA repair processing potentially leads to increased mutagenesis. We thus hypothesized that both NER and BER proteins process oxidative damage in H‐DNA‐forming sequences and exacerbate its genomic instability.

Results from the current study are consistent with our previous findings that H‐DNA increases mutation frequencies, promotes large deletions, and is associated with XPA and APE1 at basal and elevated levels of OS (Zewail‐Foote et al. 2025). Here, we extend these findings by demonstrating that OGG1 and APE1 function as protective factors against H‐DNA‐induced mutagenesis, whereas XPA exacerbates H‐DNA mutagenesis following OS. We additionally demonstrate that XPA and APE1 independently associate with H‐DNA substrates after OS, and that this association is not influenced by the presence or absence of the other protein.

We first investigated the role of NER in H‐DNA‐induced genomic instability under OS by measuring the mutation frequencies and spectra with different levels of oxidative damage. As OS increased, both B‐DNA‐ and H‐DNA‐induced mutagenesis significantly increased (Figure 1A). Interestingly, although H‐DNA induced a significantly higher number of mutations than B‐DNA at all levels of OS (Figure 1A), the significant difference in mutation frequencies between H‐DNA and B‐DNA was no longer observed in the absence of the NER protein XPA (Figure 1C). We also found that large deletions, characteristic of H‐DNA‐induced mutations, were the predominant mutation type at all levels of OS for the H‐DNA‐containing template (Figure 1B) but were substantially reduced in the absence of XPA (Figure 1D). These results agree with our previous findings demonstrating that H‐DNA (in the absence of exogenous DNA damage) requires NER for its mutagenic processing (Zhao et al. 2018). NER proteins are also known to impact the mutagenic processing of other alternative DNA structures (Wang et al. 2006; Wang et al. 2015). Together, our results suggest that XPA plays a critical role in H‐DNA‐induced deletions in both the presence and absence of OS. We also observe that increasing OS elevates the mutation frequencies and point mutations of both B‐DNA and H‐DNA sequences independent of XPA.

Since XPA alone did not account for the observed increases in genomic instability at different levels of OS, we next measured the mutation frequencies and spectra induced by B‐DNA and H‐DNA in the absence of key BER proteins. The absence of OGG1 resulted in a significant increase between B‐DNA‐ and H‐DNA‐induced mutations at low levels of OS (Figure 2A), especially when compared to the observed difference within wild‐type cells (Figure 1A). These results suggest that the absence of OGG1 results in a greater increase in H‐DNA‐induced genetic instability as compared to B‐DNA at lower levels of OS. A similar yet more striking trend is seen in the absence of the BER protein APE1, with a significant increase in the H‐DNA‐induced mutation frequencies at all levels of OS (Figure 2C). The absence of BER proteins also increased the level of H‐DNA‐induced large deletions, with few detected in the B‐DNA samples at all levels of OS (Figure 2B,D). Taken together, the absence of key BER proteins appears to exacerbate the genomic instability of H‐DNA more substantially than B‐DNA, suggesting that BER plays an important protective role during OS to preserve the genomic integrity of H‐DNA sequences.

Interestingly, B‐DNA reporters exposed to OS conditions have elevated mutation frequencies in OGG1 knockout cells as compared to wild‐type cells (Figure 2A), reaching similar levels to H‐DNA reporters at the highest level of OS. In contrast, with APE1 knockout cells, B‐DNA reporters under OS have similar or lower mutation frequencies as compared to wild‐type cells (Figure 2C). These results suggest that OGG1 is imperative to minimize mutations in B‐DNA substrates following OS. However, APE1 appears to have backup or compensatory mechanisms in human cells that maintain error‐free repair in its absence. Consistent with other findings, the loss of APE1 can be compensated for by other enzymes such as the AP lyase activity of OGG1 and Nei‐like glycosylases such as NEIL1 (Vidal et al. 2001; Mokkapati et al. 2004). These compensatory mechanisms seem unable to efficiently repair H‐DNA substrates after OS, as indicated by the significant increase in H‐DNA‐induced mutation frequencies in the absence of APE1 (Figure 2C).

Overall, we observed increased H‐DNA‐induced genetic instability in the presence of NER proteins and in the absence of BER proteins at all levels of OS. We investigated if these NER and BER proteins are associated with B‐DNA and H‐DNA sequences at different levels of OS, and if they are dependent on one another. As OS increased in wild‐type cells, the association of XPA with H‐DNA increased but showed minimal interaction with B‐DNA (Figure 3A). Conversely, the association of the BER protein, APE1, increased with B‐DNA with increasing OS but diminished its association with H‐DNA sequences (Figure 3A). Importantly, the association of XPA or APE1 with H‐DNA or B‐DNA does not change in the absence of the other, indicating an independent recruitment of these DNA repair proteins to B‐DNA and H‐DNA at all levels of OS (Figure 3B,C). Taken together, our data suggest that XPA primarily interacts with H‐DNA, and its interaction increases as the interaction of APE1 decreases with increasing OS.

Although both NER and BER proteins interact with H‐DNA at different levels of OS, our hypothesis was only partially correct. The NER protein XPA increases its association with H‐DNA and exacerbates its genomic instability as OS increases, whereas the BER protein APE1 decreases its association with H‐DNA and protects its genomic integrity as OS increases. Our results suggest that with OS the genomic fidelity of H‐DNA becomes less stable compared to B‐DNA, especially with increased NER activity and a loss of BER processing. The accumulation of oxidative damage within H‐DNA appears to elicit an interplay between BER and NER proteins that are likely to recognize distinct structural features that lead to differential and mutagenic DNA processing. These findings suggest that non‐B‐DNA structures act as preferential sites for DNA damage under conditions of OS, recruiting both BER and NER proteins (and potentially other repair proteins) that increase genomic instability beyond what is observed with either oxidative damage in canonical B‐DNA or non‐B‐DNA alone.

These results outline a mechanistic framework that provides a significant conceptual link between environmental and endogenous OS, DNA structure formation, and differential DNA repair processing to the genomic instability requisite of many genetic diseases such as cancer. Alternative DNA structures are a driver of non‐random mutation hotspots, and characterizing H‐DNA sequences as mutagenic hotspots under OS provides a potential mechanistic framework for how structural features of H‐DNA and the involvement of distinct repair proteins contribute to genomic instability at certain genomic loci. These findings suggest that oxidative stress and non‐B DNA‐forming sequences are a driving force in the evolution of the human genome by providing genomic diversity within the population. Our findings also suggest that human tissues with decreased BER or increased NER activity may differentially exacerbate the genomic instability at non‐B DNA loci based on the amount of oxidative damage. Considering that many pre‐cancerous cells have oxidative microenvironments shaped by environmental and metabolic stressors, and dysregulated BER and NER pathways, non‐B DNA‐forming sequences may serve as genomic biomarkers in cancer screening and as potential targets for preventative therapy.
