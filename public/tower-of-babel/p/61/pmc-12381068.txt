A new medical image encryption using modular integrated logistic exponential map and multi-level Q-Sequence matrix
Authors: Abdelmajid H Mansour, Sherihan Aboelenin, Mohamed Meselhy Eltoukhy, Hala I Mohamed, Osama Elkomy, Khalid M Hosny
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtecting the confidentiality of medical images during storage and transmission is vital in modern healthcare systems. This paper presents an innovative and efficient encryption algorithm tailored for both grayscale and color medical images. The proposed method combines the Modified Improved Logistic Exponential (MILE) chaotic map with a multi-level Fibonacci Q-matrix to enhance security, randomness, and resilience. By overcoming the limitations of conventional one-dimensional chaotic systems, the MILE map significantly improves the unpredictability of the permutation and diffusion processes. The encryption procedure begins with extracting key-dependent parameters from the input image, which are then used to generate chaotic sequences for pixel permutation and XOR-based diffusion. Additionally, image blocks undergo multi-level Q-matrix transformations to bolster further the scheme’s resistance to statistical analysis, noise disruption, and differential attacks. Extensive experiments were conducted using standard evaluation metrics such as information entropy, correlation coefficients, NPCR, UACI, PSNR, and key sensitivity. The proposed scheme achieved strong performance, with an NPCR of 99.63%, a UACI of 33.47%, and entropy values nearing the ideal 7.999, indicating excellent randomness. Moreover, the algorithm is computationally efficient, requiring just 0.42 s to encrypt a 256 × 256 image, making it highly suitable for real-time and telemedicine applications. Overall, the proposed approach ensures robust protection for sensitive medical data and surpasses several existing image encryption techniques in performance.Keywords: Encrypting images, Encryption, Chaotic, Logistic map, Exponential map, Color medical images, Scrambled image blocks, Fibonacci, MILESubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Due to the rapid advancement of medical imaging technology, medical images are used to diagnose a wide range of disorders. Medical imaging requires a lot of processing power and storage. Therefore, third-party cloud-based services facilitate these computational and storage duties. Securing medical image data during transmission and storage is a critical concern. Medical images are exchanged across multiple networks, making it imperative to preserve them. The safe transfer of them necessitates authentication, integrity, and secrecy. Unauthorized use of these medical images could compromise the privacy of patient data1. Furthermore, if these pictures are responsible for a slight alteration, it can lead to a wrong diagnosis, which can jeopardize patients’ lives.

Generally, there are four main approaches to secure medical images: encryption2–4, steganography5–7, data hiding8–10, and watermarking11–13. Among these four approaches, encryption is primarily used to secure digital images, as it offers a higher level of security4,14,15. Techniques used for encrypting photos undergo two stages: the plain image is transformed into an unreadable and random one during the encryption stage, and the original contents of the image are recovered during the decryption stage.

To date, numerous encryption techniques have been proposed using various methods. Standard text encryption systems are unsuitable for digital image encryption, particularly for colored images with redundancy properties, strong correlation, and large volume16–18.

Medical imaging can be shielded from manipulation and privacy breaches with encryption. However, due to their massive data volume and tight correlation, medical images cannot be encrypted using traditional text-based encryption methods. To meet these requirements, new encryption algorithms that are both fast and secure are needed. Reliable algorithms for encrypting medical images have been proposed recently to reduce correlations and redundancies in the images. Either of these techniques uses selective encryption19 or techniques based on complete encryption20. While complete image encryption techniques encrypt the entire image, selective encryption focuses on encrypting only a particular portion of the image21.

Consequently, techniques based on complete encryption are thought to be far safer. Additionally, there are two types of full image encryption algorithms: optical image encryption-based and chaos-based, which connect items, sensors, and medical devices. The Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) is transforming healthcare through real-time diagnostics and remote patient monitoring22.

However, there are typically two significant obstacles that come with this connectivity: (i) The quick growth in the amount of space that medical information (images, videos, personal information, medical reports, etc.) can be stored, which increases the demand for storage devices and restricts the amount of bandwidth that can be used to transmit such large amounts of data. (ii) The IoMT ecosystem faces several significant security and privacy challenges. The patient’s data must be protected from tampering and unauthorized access. Robust encryption algorithms can be effectively employed in the IoMTs to guarantee the secure and private transfer of medical data23.

Implementing such procedures enables healthcare organizations to safeguard confidential patient information from potential misuse by cyber attackers24. Recently, researchers have offered some hybrid compression and encryption scheme implementations for medical images. Several chaos-based image encryption techniques have been put forth. A few are placed chronologically in this section, with the most recent items appearing first. Numerous parameters’ results support the robustness, efficiency, security, and speed of the method suggested by Gupta et al.25. The chaotic map function has been successfully applied to a range of photographs. According to the simulation results, the new method is sufficiently fast for image encryption while reducing the likelihood of a brute-force decoding attack. The key was created by Gashim and Hussein26 by integrating two maps. The key changes the pixels’ position and value during the dispersion and confusion phases.

Balaska et al.27 employed a 2D chaotic map in conjunction with the Grain-128a stream cipher algorithm to enhance security and sensitivity when selecting the first parameter. Next, a fixed length of 256-bit secret key is employed to construct the necessary parameters for the chaotic map. After that, the sequences will encrypt the visualization using a confusion and diffusion strategy. The simulated testing demonstrated the remarkable trustworthiness and effectiveness of the suggested method for encrypting photographs of any type or format.

Ye et al.28 employed the cryptosystem to generate the fundamental parameters for the quantum logistic map. Five-round substitution-permutation networks (SPN) provide a strong defense against differential-like attacks. Kari et al.29 introduced a set of innovative chaotic maps based on the discrete wavelet transform. The double chaotic function can be applied to improve encryption performance and quality. Dynamical Analysis and other methods revealed that the suggested map was often hyper-chaotic, highly sensitive, and complex. Therefore, the proposed chaos-based image cipher may be beneficial for various applications.

Alhumyani30 developed an image cipher based on the discrete cosine transform (DCT) and the chaotic Baker map (BM). The proposed image cipher first applies the DCT to the plain image, followed by rearranging the DCT coefficients using the BM. Numerous experiments demonstrated the superiority of this DCT-based BM image cipher in terms of statistical properties, noise immunity, differential analysis, and sensitivity.

Ibrahim et al.31 proposed a comprehensive medical image encryption system that combines two highly effective structures: chaotic maps and dynamic substitution boxes (S-boxes). Tests have demonstrated that the recommended design passes all security requirements, regardless of how it is implemented in examinations. Any key-dependent dynamic S-box can be generated using various chaotic map generation techniques.

Elghandour et al.32 proposed that chaotic sequences can be generated using a two-dimensional piecewise smooth nonlinear chaotic map. Next, these sequences are transformed into integers ranging from 0 to 255. Ultimately, the erratic patterns conceal the distorted image, resulting in a diffused effect. Their approach was rapid, safe, and impervious to multiple attacks.

Ye et al.33 are the authors of the revolutionary asymmetric image encryption system. The Rivest-Shamir-Adleman (RSA) asymmetric public key approach is first used to generate the initial values for a quantum logistic map. The Arnold scrambling technique achieves the fundamental concealing of visual information on the plain image. Lastly, exclusive-OR (XOR) diffusion is applied independently to the image’s rows and columns. Arnold’s map parameters are then computed. Laboratory testing indicates that a more uniform distribution of pixel values reduces strong correlations and enhances resistance to various types of attacks.

Ankita et al.34 proposed using the Arnold cat map (ACM) to scramble the matrix after separating color images into red, green, and blue channels and converting them to the bit plane from the pixel plane. Their system is more secure, immune to brute-force attacks, and sensitive to differential attacks.

The robustness of the proposed encryption method is validated by evaluating various performance and security metrics such as histogram analysis, correlation distribution, correlation coefficients, entropy, Number of Pixels Change Rate (NPCR), Unified Average Changing Intensity (UACI), and Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR)35. Several encryption strategies have been adopted in the literature for protecting medical images, including bitwise XOR diffusion, chaotic systems, edge-based transformation, and fast scrambling techniques36. Optical encryption approaches that utilize chaotic behavior and image blocking have also been explored to enhance image confidentiality37. In addition, compressed medical images have been encrypted using chaos-based schemes such as M-PWLCM, with control parameters securely exchanged among authorized users in the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) environment38. Other encryption schemes generate pseudo-random keys by combining logistic maps and 3D Lorenz attractors, increasing the unpredictability of the encryption process39. These developments highlight the growing demand for secure and efficient image encryption techniques, especially for real-time transmission of high-resolution medical images40.

In recent years, more advanced schemes have been introduced, including plaintext-dependent encryption models based on chaotic sequences41, multi-chaotic maps tailored for medical image security42, and dual-domain approaches combining chaos, DNA coding, and integer wavelet transforms43. Comprehensive reviews have further emphasized the strengths and limitations of contemporary encryption algorithms for medical applications44. Optical-based methods have continued to evolve, providing adequate protection and authentication for both grayscale and color medical images45. Some researchers have proposed joint compression and encryption frameworks using novel integer transforms and multi-parametric chaotic maps suitable for IoMT environments46. In contrast, others focused on efficient ECG signal compression using orthogonal moments46. Enhanced chaotic systems combined with dynamic DNA coding have also been utilized for image encryption47, as well as new chaotic map designs for more secure algorithms48. Recent works even integrated DNA cryptography with elliptic curves to safeguard medical data49.

To address limitations in existing methods, such as insufficient chaotic behavior, low key sensitivity, or excessive computational cost, this study proposes a hybrid image encryption algorithm that incorporates two key components. The Modified Integrated Logistic Exponential (MILE) map is utilized in both the permutation and diffusion stages, expanding the chaotic range and improving unpredictability by addressing the drawbacks of conventional one-dimensional maps. The Multi-Level Fibonacci Q-matrix is used to divide the image into random 2 × 2 blocks and perform multi-stage scrambling, which enhances resistance to statistical and differential attacks.

The proposed scheme is assessed using standard cryptographic metrics and compared with recent methods over the same medical image datasets. The results reveal that the proposed technique achieves superior security with reduced computational complexity, making it suitable for real-time medical applications.

Hosny et al.51 were among the first to employ the Fibonacci Q-matrix for image diffusion in the context of medical image encryption. Their approach integrated the matrix with a hyperchaotic system within a two-phase confusion–diffusion structure, resulting in improved key space, strong statistical resistance, and robustness against noise and data-removal attacks. The Q-matrix form allows the computation of large Fibonacci values efficiently via matrix exponentiation rather than iterative or recursive calculation. In our method, the encrypted image is divided into non-overlapping 2 × 2-pixel blocks, and the Fibonacci Q-matrix is applied iteratively on each block to shuffle pixel values. This multi-level processing (e.g., over multiple iterations or multiple image scales) increases the unpredictability of pixel positions and intensities, which strengthens the encryption against statistical and differential attacks. The benefit of using Fibonacci-based diffusion is that it combines mathematical complexity with computational efficiency, making it well-suited for lightweight image encryption schemes, especially in medical imaging, where both security and speed are critical.

The proposed method

The novel approach for encrypting medical grayscale images by integrating the MILE chaotic map with a multi-level Fibonacci Q-matrix framework. This hybrid method is designed to enhance both the security and performance of image encryption. The MILE chaotic map is chosen due to its simplicity, limited number of parameters, and strong chaotic behavior. It inherits properties of the logistic exponential map, overcoming typical limitations of 1D chaotic systems such as narrow chaotic ranges and high predictability. This makes MILE highly suitable for secure encryption processes. On the other hand, the Fibonacci Q-matrix, known for its computational efficiency and simplicity, is employed in multiple hierarchical levels to improve the scrambling and diffusion operations, thereby strengthening the cryptographic system. The proposed encryption algorithm is composed of two primary phases: permutation (confusion) and diffusion. key sensitivity and key space analysis

Keyspace

A secure encryption algorithm must have a sufficiently large key space to resist brute-force attacks, in which an attacker tries every possible key combination. According to Chai et al.57, the key space should exceed 2¹⁰⁰ to ensure resistance against such attacks.

In the proposed technique, the encryption key comprises four real-valued parameters, which are two initial conditions and two control parameters. Each parameter is encoded using 64 bits as per the IEEE 754 standard on double-precision floating point numbers. As such, the mantissa has roughly 52 bits of precision. Each parameter can, therefore, take on roughly 2^52 distinct values. Thus, the total key space can be estimated as . This is well beyond the suggested , which is considered a standard benchmark for cryptographic strength. The sheer magnitude of such a key space guarantees a high level of resistance against brute-force and exhaustive search attempts on the proposed encryption scheme.

Key sensitivity

A strong IE method should be sensitive to small changes in the keys used to encrypt or decrypt the image during encryption. The ciphered image obtained by slightly changing the key should differ significantly from the original image. Similarly, the decrypted images from the original key and those produced with a minor alteration in the decryption step cannot be statistically or visually identical.

This study examined the key sensitivity of the IE scheme in two distinct situations. In the first situation, the encrypted image is decrypted using the original key (Key 1) and Keys 2 and 3 by changing the decryption keys; it is clear that the decrypted image with Key 1 and the images decoded with keys that have a one-bit difference are not visually or statistically similar. Conclusion

This study presents a novel approach for encrypting medical images, combining the MILE map and multi-level Fibonacci techniques. The proposed map addresses the shortcomings of existing one-dimensional chaotic maps, including their limited parameter ranges and simple predictive capabilities. MILE exhibits more chaotic behavior over various parameter values. In contrast, confusion and diffusion operations are performed on a random selection portion of the plain image. The selected section of the plain image is then passed to MILE to calculate parameters responsible for rearranging the pixels’ positions and changing their values, resulting in a higher information entropy value. A multi-level Fibonacci Q-matrix is used to increase security. It can increase image scrambling and make the algorithm more susceptible to brute-force attacks. Thus, the IE strategy saves both time and money. It can protect plain images and withstand common attacks, such as differential, cropping, and chosen-plaintext attacks. As a result, the proposed technique offers high-level security and time complexity performance.

While the proposed encryption scheme exhibits strong security and effective performance, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the evaluation was conducted exclusively on 2D grayscale and color medical images, without extending the analysis to 3D or multi-modal datasets such as DICOM sequences or volumetric CT/MRI scans. Secondly, although the algorithm performs efficiently on medium-sized images (e.g., 256 × 256 pixels), its scalability to larger image resolutions or real-time high-definition video streams remains untested and may require further optimization. Lastly, the study did not assess the algorithm’s robustness against advanced cryptanalytic techniques, including those based on artificial intelligence or deep learning, which are increasingly relevant in modern security contexts. These limitations highlight important directions for future research and potential enhancements of the proposed method.
