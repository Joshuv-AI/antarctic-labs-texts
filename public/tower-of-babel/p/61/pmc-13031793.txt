Sintered poultry-base waste as low-cost adsorbent for the uptake of Congo red: insight into kinetics, isotherms and thermodynamics
Authors: Salah Ud Din, Arogundade Ibrahim, Ali Shawabkeh, Khairia Mohammed Al-Ahmary, Nuha Y Elamin, Saedah R Al-Mhyawi, Hamad AlMohamadi, Mohammad Elamin, Jamelah S Al-Otaibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe presence of dyes in wastewater constitutes environmental pollution which pose severe concern to not only human existence but also the entire eco-systems requiring urgent and effective treatment techniques. This research investigates the valorization of poultry-based waste as a sustainable and low-cost adsorbent for the uptake of Congo red (CR) dye from aqueous solutions. Poultry-based waste was thermally treated through sintering at 650 °C to form sintered poultry-based waste (SPBW), and its adsorption performance was compared with that of untreated poultry-based waste (PBW). Comprehensive characterization with SEM, FT-IR, XRD, BET, pHₚzc, and TGA indicated that sintering greatly improved the pore structure, surface properties, functional group stability, and adsorption site accessibility. Adsorption equilibrium was achieved within 120 min and 100 min for SPBW and PBW at a pH of 4.0 and 2.0, with SPBW exhibiting superior adsorption capacity and stability compare with PBW. Kinetic data reveal that CR uptake onto PBW and SPBW followed the pseudo-first-order model. Data from equilibrium investigation confirmed Langmuir fitted well with PBW, while Freundlich fitted well the SPBW. PBW and SPBW showed maximum monolayer adsorption capacities of 40.16 mg g−1and 60.23 mg g⁻¹ respectively. Thermodynamic determinations confirmed that the adsorption process was spontaneous and endothermic, with ΔH° values of 16.93 kJ/mol for PBW and 32.55 kJ/mol for SPBW. Desorption studies showed good regeneration potential over multiple cycles, most especially for SPBW. In all, the results demonstrate that sintered poultry-based waste is an efficient, environmentally sustainable, and reusable adsorbent for anionic dye removal from wastewater.Keywords: Adsorption, Congo red, Wastewater, Sintered poultry waste, ValorizationSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

The industrial activities like leather processing, textile dyeing, cosmetics, paper manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, plastics, and printing which is expanding rapidly has led to the discharge of large volumes of dye-contaminated wastewater into aquatic environments1,2. Due to their high color intensity, low cost, and chemical stability, synthetic dyes are widely used in these processes3,4. Nevertheless, it has been reported that about 10–20% of dyes used during industrial activities are lost to wastewater streams during dyeing and finishing operations5. These effluents are often released as untreated or inadequately treated into surface and groundwater bodies, inducing serious environmental pollution. The presence of dyes in water bodies disrupts aquatic ecosystems, reduces light penetration, inhibits photosynthesis, and degrades water quality, making the water unsuitable for domestic, industrial, and agricultural use4,6,7.

Congo red (CR), which is an anionic azo dye is extensively used in paper, plastic, textile, and rubber industries, is of particular environmental concern8. Structurally, CR is made up of two azo (–N = N–) linkages and aromatic rings, which impart high chemical stability and resistance to biodegradation. Due to its sulfonate groups, it is highly soluble in water and difficult to remove using conventional treatment methods because of its complex aromatic molecular structures9. Allergic reactions, skin and eye irritation, gastrointestinal distress, and potential carcinogenic and mutagenic effects have been reported to be associated with exposure to Congo red10. In aquatic environments, CR negatively impacts aquatic organisms and disrupts ecological balance, requiring its effective elimination from wastewater before discharge11.

Several conservative wastewater treatment techniques have been deployed for dye removal, including chemical oxidation, exchange, membrane filtration, chemical coagulation–flocculation, ion biological degradation, and advanced oxidation processes12–14. While effective under certain conditions, these techniques often suffer from excessive chemical consumption, high operational costs, incomplete dye removal, membrane fouling, and generation of secondary pollutants or toxic sludge13,15. Though membrane-based processes continue to advance16, adsorption has appeared as one of the most efficient and versatile techniques for dye removal owing to its high removal efficiency, simplicity, minimal sludge generation, and flexibility in design17.

Traditionally, the use of activated carbon as adsorbent has been the most widely reported; however, its high production cost, and limited availability restrict its large-scale application, especially in developing regions18. Accordingly, increasing attention has been shifted toward sustainable, low-cost adsorbents derived from agricultural and industrial wastes. Among the low-cost adsorbents which have been employed for the uptake of CR are various agro-waste materials like sorghum husk19, Buckthorn fruits20, Wheat husk21, melon seeds22, periwinkle shell activated carbon23, citric acid-modified Dicerocaryum eriocarpum24, and Spathodea campanulata flowers25. The attractiveness of biomass-derived adsorbents is because of their low environmental footprint, renewability, and abundant surface functional groups that allow electrostatic interactions, π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding, and ion exchange mechanisms26.

Recently, research advances in adsorption emphasize that performance is governed not only by surface area but also by adsorbent–adsorbate interactions, pore structure, surface chemistry, surface charge7–10. Research on functionalized composites proves that functional groups and surface charge modulation play decisive roles in dye binding mechanisms27. Mechanistically informed modeling methods, like surface energetics analysis and statistical physics interpretations, provide deeper insight into adsorption behavior beyond conventional Langmuir and Freundlich parameters28,29. More also, long-term stability and regenerability are increasingly recognized as critical factors for practical application29. However, some biomass-based adsorbents are still evaluated empirically, with limited comparative analysis, regeneration assessment or mechanistic interpretation30.

Poultry-based waste, most especially the feedstock wastes, is generated in large quantities by poultry processing industries and slaughterhouses, causing serious disposal and environmental challenges. Poultry waste is rich in cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin framework that is favorable for adsorption applications. Thermal modification methods like sintering and pyrolysis have been reported to improve structural stability, porosity development, and surface chemistry by eliminating volatile organic components and exposing active adsorption sites20–23. The resulting sintered material contains carbonaceous domains, which can promote multiple adsorption mechanisms including electrostatic attraction, ion exchange, hydrogen bonding, and π–π interactions, thus making sintered poultry-based waste (SPBW) a promising low-cost adsorbent for dye removal.

Despite increasing interest in waste-derived adsorbents, systematic studies comparing untreated and thermally modified poultry-based waste under identical conditions—while integrating kinetic, isotherm, surface charge, thermodynamic, spectroscopic, and regeneration analyses still remain limited. In addition, transforming these agro-waste into value added material for dye removal is a plus to sustainable environment.

Hence, this research investigates the valorization of poultry-based waste via chemical-free sintering at 650 °C to form a structurally stable adsorbent for Congo red uptake. A direct comparison between untreated and sintered materials is performed. The elucidation of structural property is conducted using FTIR, SEM, XRD, and BET techniques, while adsorption behavior is investigated via pHₚzc, kinetic (pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, and intraparticle diffusion), equilibrium (Langmuir, Freundlich, and Dubinin–Radushkevich), thermodynamic, and regeneration studies. By integrating mechanism, adsorption efficiency, and reusability, this work establishes a clearer structure–performance relationship for sintered poultry-based waste and contributes to sustainable wastewater treatment via waste valorization.

Materials and methods

Reagents and materials

Poultry-based-waste (PBW) was collected from local poultry processing outlets. Congo red dye (C₃₂H₂₂N₆Na₂O₆S₂, 35%) was used as the model pollutant. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 98%) were used for pH adjustment. All reagents were of analytical grade obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, India, and distilled water was used throughout the experiments.

Preparation and calcination of poultry-based waste

The collected poultry-based waste was washed thoroughly with hot distilled water to eradicate adhering fats and impurities, and then by oven drying at 105 °C. The dried sample was crushed and sieved to obtain desire particle size. Sintering was done in a muffle furnace at 650 °C for 2 h under limited oxygen conditions to formed sintered poultry-based waste. The resulting product was cooled, ground, and stored in airtight containers for further use and referred to as sintered poultry-based waste (SPBW), while the portion that was not sintered was referred to as Poultry-based-waste (PBW).

Characterization of the adsorbent

The adsorbent’s surface functional groups were identified using Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. A mixture of the dried adsorbent was ground with potassium bromide (KBr) and pressed into a pellet, which was scanned in the range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹ using a FT-IR-8101 M spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The morphology and elemental analysis of the adsorbent was studied using scanning electron microscopy (SEM-EDS) where samples were mounted on carbon tape and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity before imaging using a VEGA 3 LMU SEM (TESCAN, Czech Republic). Crystalline phases were measured by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis where finely powdered samples were placed in a sample holder and scanned at 2θ range of 10°–60° using a XPERT-PRO diffractometer (PANalytical Philips, Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation. The various peaks detected were compared with standard reference patterns to identify the crystalline phases. The specific pore volume, surface area, and pore size distribution of the adsorbent were measured using nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis at 77 K. The sample was degassed under vacuum at 150 °C for 6 h before analysis. Determinations were done using a Tristar II 3020 analyzer (Micromeritics, USA). The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method was used to estimate the surface area, whereas the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) approach was employed to determine the total pore volume and pore size distribution. The point of zero charge (pHpzc) of adsorbent was examined using the pH drift method, and zeta potential measurements were recorded across a pH range of 2–10. 25 mL of 0.01 M NaCl solution was measured into different conical flasks, and the initial pH (pHi) was adjusted between 2 and 10 with either 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH. Then, 10 mg of adsorbent was introduced into the solutions, and then agitated for 12 h at 100 rpm and room temperature to attain equilibrium. Thereafter, the final pH (pHf) was estimated and the pHₚzc was computed using the plot of ΔpH (pHf − pHi) versus pHi. The thermal property of the adsorbent was done using Thermogravimetric Analyzer (TGA: TGA 4000, Perkin Elmer, Haarlem, Netherlands) under a nitrogen atmosphere with a heating rate of 10 °C/min starting from room temperature to 650 °.

Batch adsorption studies

Congo red adsorption onto sintered poultry-based waste and poultry-based waste were carefully performed via batch adsorption experiments to examine the adsorption pattern under controlled conditions. For each experiment, a known mass of adsorbent (10–60 mg) was weighed and added to a 25 mL volume of CR solution of predetermined concentration and agitated for varying contact times (10–160 min) at constant speed of 150 rpm until equilibrium was attained in 250 mL conical flasks. Prior to agitation, the initial pH of the dye was adjusted in the range of 2–10 with either 0.1 M NaOH or 0.1 M HCl before the introduction of the adsorbent. Results and discussions

Characterizations

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy

Figure 1 illustrates the FT-IR spectra of the sintered poultry base waste before and after adsorption of Congo Red dye. The spectral profiles showed distinct alterations in absorption bands that indicate modification of surface functional groups following dye sorption. The spectrum before the adsorption displays broad absorptions in the high-wavenumber region (3460 cm⁻¹) consistent with O–H stretching associated with surface hydroxyl groups common in sintered poultry waste2,6. Additional band are observed at1650 cm⁻¹) attributable to carbonyl (C = O) vibration2,6. Those absorption bands noticed at 1150–1055 cm⁻¹ and 760 cm⁻¹ in the spectrum of the sintered poultry waste before to adsorption are attributed mainly to the ν₃ asymmetric stretching vibrations of C-O groups and Si–O–Si or Si–O stretching associated with vibrations of silica or ash-derived silicate phases which that present due to feed residues processing2,8. After CR adsorption, significant spectral changes are noticed. First, broad bands assigned to hydroxyl and amino functionalities were seen at 3270–3680 cm⁻¹ were showing a decrease in intensity and slight shift, signifying the involvement of these groups in forming bonds with the azo dye molecules. Comparable shifts have been documented in biosorbents after CR uptake, where π–π and hydrogen bonding interactions ease dye adherence to biomass surfaces37. Also, new features of peaks 1450–1660 cm⁻¹emerge post-adsorption assigned to aromatic and Azo peaks, indicating the presence of aromatic ring and azo (–N = N–) stretching modes from the adsorbed Congo Red which is in alignment with reports where CR adsorption produces discernible bands in similar regions due to dye incorporation onto adsorbent matrices38. Additional peaks shift appears in the region of 1133–1150 cm⁻ after CR treatment, which can be associated with the vibrations of C-O groups and Si–O–Si or Si–O stretching. The observed minor shifts and intensity changes in FTIR bands before and after adsorption is associated with oxygen-containing functional groups, indicating interaction of Congo red molecules with the adsorbent surface. These changes are interpreted qualitatively and are consistent with non-covalent interactions rather than definitive chemical bonding. Overall, the observed emergence of new bands and spectral shifts signify the contributions of π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic interaction mechanisms in CR adsorption, which is in alignment with mechanistic interpretations in recent literature37,38.

XRD analysis of SPBW before and after Congo red adsorption

Figure 2 denotes the X-ray diffraction patterns of sintered poultry-based waste (SPBW) before and after Congo red (CR) uptake. The patterns of diffraction before adsorption are characterized by broad, low-intensity peaks, signifying a predominantly amorphous structure with limited organic and inorganic composition. Typical thermally treated biomass-derived materials often depict this amorphous nature, where sintering at elevated temperatures (650 °C) promotes partial carbonization and ash formation instead of well-defined crystalline phases. The broad diffraction hump centered at approximately 2θ ≈ 17.2° is evidenced of amorphous nature of the carbonaceous matrix formed during sintering which does not correspond to a well-defined crystalline plane. Such broad characteristics are commonly noticed in biochar-like and thermally decomposed biopolymer materials and arise from carbonaceous and disordered organic domains rather than a specific lattice plane (e.g., partially carbonized proteins and aromatic clusters) that is consistent with the features of carbonaceous components in related carbon materials39.

XRD of SPBW before and after the sorption of CR.

The diffraction peaks seen at 2θ ≈ 21.2° and 22.4° are closely related to an amorphous carbonaceous matrix originating from the thermal decomposition of organic constituents in the poultry-based waste. Although native cellulose typically exhibits a (200) reflection close to 22.5°, complete thermal decomposition of cellulose occurs well below the sintering temperature employed in this study (650 °C). Therefore, these peaks are ascribed to an amorphous carbonaceous matrix domain, formed through depolymerization, dehydration, and aromatization of biomass during sintering. After the uptake of Congo red, the XRD pattern shows no emergence of new crystalline peaks, signifying that dye uptake does not induce the formation of secondary crystalline compounds. Nevertheless, a slight attenuation and increased diffuseness of the broad band at 2θ ≈ 15–23° is noticed, which is ascribed to surface coverage of the amorphous carbon domains by Congo red molecules. This feature affirms that adsorption occurs predominantly on the carbonaceous matrix via surface interactions, rather than through structural transformation of the adsorbent.

SEM morphology of SPBW before and after CR adsorption

Figure 3 represents the scanning electron microscopy micrographs of SPBW before (Fig. 3a) and after (Fig. 3b) Congo red uptake. Before to adsorption, the adsorbent surface displays a highly irregular, porous morphology with well-developed fissures, cavities, and interconnected channels. These characteristics are indicative of effective sintering-induced decomposition of organic matter and volatilization of low-molecular-weight compounds, leading to increased surface roughness and enhanced porosity.

SEM of SPBW before (a) and after (b) the sorption of CR.

After the uptake of CR, the SEM image displays partial surface coverage and pore filling by dye molecules. The open pores previously seen appear less pronounced, and the surface becomes comparatively smoother, indicating successful attachment of CR molecules onto the surface of the adsorbent and within internal pores. Comparable SEM investigations have been reported for thermally modified biomass and biochar adsorbents, where dye uptake caused pore blockage and surface coating effects11,40. The retention of structural potency after uptake of pollutant further confirms that SPBW possesses enough mechanical strength and resistance to collapse, supporting its suitability for repeated adsorption–desorption cycles.

BET surface area and porosity characteristics

Figure 4 illustrates the nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm, which shows a type IV isotherm with a pronounced hysteresis loop. This behavior is feature of mesoporous materials, signifying the presence of interconnected pores in the 2–50 nm range. The mesoporous structure is particularly advantageous for adsorption of large dye molecules such as Congo red, whose bulky aromatic framework requires sufficient pore accessibility. The total pore volume estimated at relative pressures close to unity is 0.132 cm³ g⁻¹ for PBW and 0.818 cm³ g⁻¹ for SPBW, suggesting an increase in pore volume after heat treatment. The BET study gave a specific surface area of 67.92 m²/g for PBW and 168.43 m²/g for SPBW indicating that the sintered material has a much larger accessible surface for CR molecules uptake which is in alignment with other observation from adsorption study confirming its superior performance. The hysteresis loop at intermediate pressures confirms capillary condensation within mesopores, whereas the relatively steep adsorption branch at low relative pressures (P/P₀ < 0.1) suggests the presence of micropores. The combine effects of micro- and mesoporosity facilitates adsorption performance by providing both high surface area and efficient diffusion pathways as observed. Increase pore volume and stabilize pore walls through inorganic ash formation, that improves resistance to pore collapse during adsorption are known to be caused by thermal sintering1,7. The BET findings thus support the superior adsorption capacity of SPBW compare to untreated PBW and support the kinetic results that intraparticle diffusion plays a vital role in the overall adsorption mechanism.

TGA/DTA analysis of poultry-based waste

Figure 5 shows the thermal behavior of poultry-based waste. The TGA profile displays three major stages of weight loss of dehydration, organic components and the formation of mineral-rich ash41. The first mass loss below 150 °C links to the elimination of physically adsorbed moisture. The second mass loss was seen between 200 and 400 °C indicating the thermal degradation of organic components like cellulose, hemicellulose, proteins, and residual fats of the poultry waste. The final stage was noticed above 400 °C which reflects the decomposition of more stable organic fractions and the formation of mineral-rich ash. The DTA curve illustrates endothermic and exothermic transitions that is in alignment with organic decomposition, dehydration, and structural rearrangement processes42. The formation and thermally stability of the inorganic phases is confirmed with the relatively stable mass beyond 600 °C, justifying the selection of 650 °C as the sintering temperature for SPBW preparation. These thermal features are in alignment with findings on animal-waste-derived adsorbents, where sintering above 600 °C yields structurally stable, ash-rich materials with improved adsorption properties43,44. Figure 6 illustrates the EDS analysis of the poultry-based waste showing a composition of carbon (45.2 wt%) and oxygen (36.3 wt%) as the most dominant elements, followed by nitrogen (8.5 wt%), sulfur (5.3 wt%), phosphorus (3.5 wt%), and calcium (1.2 wt%). The observed high contents of carbon and oxygen indicate that the material is predominantly organic in nature, characteristic of untreated poultry manure. The detection of nitrogen reflects the presence of proteins, uric acid, and other nitrogenous compounds typically found in poultry excreta. The presence of phosphorus and calcium are associated with dietary supplements and bone-derived residues, while sulfur may likely originate from amino acids and feed additives.

Role of contact time and initial Congo red concentration

The roles of contact time and initial CR concentration on the sorption performance of poultry-based waste (PBW) and sintered poultry-based waste (SPBW) are illustrated in Figs. 7 and 8 respectively. For the two adsorbents, it was observed that CR uptake rose rapidly during the initial adsorption period (0–60 min), and then with a slower approach to equilibrium. The quick initial uptake can be ascribed to the abundance of vacant active sites on the surfaces of the adsorbents and a high concentration gradient between the solid phase and the aqueous solution, which facilitates fast external surface sorption governed primarily by film diffusion2,5,6. For PBW, maximum adsorption was attained at 100 min and as the contact time rose beyond 100 min, the adsorption rate slowly decreased, suggesting progressive saturation of available adsorption sites and increased resistance to intraparticle diffusion. Equilibrium was reached at 120 min for SPBW across all investigated initial dye concentrations. A slight reduction in adsorption capacity was noticed for PBW at extended contact time (160 min), which may be attributed to surface restructuring effects or desorption of weakly bound CR molecules6,9. Whereas, SPBW demonstrated a stable adsorption plateau up to 160 min, signifying stronger dye–adsorbent interactions and enhanced structural stability imparted by the sintering process.

Adsorption behavior of CR onto PBW at different contact time and CR initial concentration.

Adsorption behavior of CR onto SPBW at different contact time and CR initial concentration.

The initial concentration of CR imparted a huge influence on adsorption capacity for both adsorbents as indicated from Figs. 7 and 8. A rise in dye concentration from 25 to 150 mg/L resulted in a corresponding increase in CR removal. When the dye concentrations were lower (25–50 mg/L), adsorption capacity was limited by the relatively low number of dye molecules in spite the availability of abundant active sites. On the other hand, at higher dye concentrations (100–150 mg L− 1), the increased concentration gradient provided a greater driving force for mass transfer, which led to a substantially improvement in the adsorption capacities [2, 6. 9]. At equilibrium (120 min and 100 min) for SPBW and PBW, the maximum adsorption capacities at an initial concentration of 150 mg L− 1 were found to be 38.93 mg g− 1 and 58.91 mg g− 1 respectively. The steadily higher removal capacity noticed for SPBW across all concentrations and contact times demonstrates its superior adsorption efficiency which could be ascribed to physicochemical modifications induced by sintering, like improved pore structure, increased surface area, and greater stability of surface functional groups7,11,13,19. Besides, the absence of desorption at prolonged contact times for SPBW suggests stronger binding interactions between the sintered adsorbent surface and the CR molecules likely due to stronger interactions governed by a combination of π–π or electrostatic attraction interactions between the carbonaceous surface, and the aromatic structures of CR as well as the hydrogen bonding with oxygen-containing functional groups25,32,37,38.

Effluence of pH

The effluence of solution pH on the uptake performance of PBW and SPBW (Fig. 9) suggests that both adsorbents displayed maximum Congo red uptake efficiencies at highly acidic pH (pH 2 for PBW and 4 for SPBW), which is in alignment with previous literature reports showing that lower pH values increase the adsorption of anionic dyes due to the protonation of adsorbent surface functional groups, leading to a positively charged surface that facilitates electrostatic attraction toward anionic Congo red molecules45. The pH-dependent adsorption property is closely linked to the point of zero charge (pHₚzc) of the adsorbents. At pH levels lower than the pHₚzc, the surface of the adsorbent becomes positively charged because of protonation of surface functional groups, which enhanced the electrostatic attraction toward the anionic sulfonate groups of Congo red. In contrast, at pH values greater the pHₚzc, surface deprotonation results in a negatively charged surface, leading to electrostatic repulsion and reduced adsorption19,25,31,35. In the present study, pHₚzc values of PBW (4.2) and SPBW (5.5) indicate that the adsorbent surfaces become increasingly protonated at pH values below these thresholds, resulting in a net positive surface charge. Remarkably, the optimum adsorption pH does not necessarily coincide exactly with the pHₚzc values but occurs at lower pH where surface charge density and functional group protonation are maximized. As the pH rose from 2 to 6, the adsorption capacity displayed a gradual decline, which may be due to deprotonation of surface sites and decline of positive surface charge density, resulting into decreased electrostatic attraction with the negatively charged sulfonate groups of Congo red, a phenomenon which has also been documented in studies on Congo red adsorption on biochar and biomass-derived adsorbents46. A more noticeable reduction in adsorption capacity at neutral to alkaline pH (pH 6–8) was seen for both PBW and SPBW, indicating the increased competition between anionic dye molecules and OH⁻ ions for active sites, as well as the prevalence of negative surface charges at higher pH levels that cause electrostatic repulsion with the anionic dye, consistent with the pH dependence reported in recent adsorbent investigations47. SPBW constantly displayed higher removal percentages compare with PBW across the entire pH range, which is likely due to the increased functional groups stability and improved surface chemistry after sintering, which may sustain stronger adsorbent- dye interaction forces (like π–π, hydrogen bonding, electrostatic) even under conditions of reduced surface protonation, supporting previous findings where surface modification enhanced acidic adsorption performance of dye adsorbents44.

Influence of dosage

The influence of adsorbent dosage on Congo red removal shows that increasing the mass of the adsorbent leads to a progressive rise in dye removal efficiency, a trend usually ascribed to the greater availability of active biding sites and increased surface area with higher adsorbent loads as documented in studies of biomass-derived adsorbents19. In the case of PBW (Fig. 10), the percentage removal rose from 54.27% at 10 mg to a maximum of 70.54% at 40 mg, while SPBW displayed a stronger response to adsorbent dosage, with removal efficiency increasing from 61.17% at 10 mg to 87.86% at 30 mg, indicating the positive impact of thermal modification on the creation of more active adsorption sites, which has been noticed in treated low-cost adsorbent systems showing superior performance relative to untreated analogues. This finding is in alignment with reports where improved adsorbent dosages enhanced removal efficiency because of higher adsorbent- adsorbate collision and reduced mass transfer resistance48. Above the optimum range, a slight decline in adsorption efficiency was noticed for both PBW and SPBW at 40–60 mg, a behavior that is consistence with literature findings where excess adsorbent dosages have been reported to cause site particle aggregation, overlapping, and ultimately a decline in effective surface area per unit mass19.

Effect of temperature

The impart of temperature on the uptake of Congo red by PBW and SPBW (Fig. 11) signify that temperature rose significantly which facilitates the removal efficiency of dye molecules. This reflects that higher thermal energy improves pore diffusion, enhanced the kinetic energy of dye molecules, and facilitates access to binding sites, as previously reported for biomass-derived sorbents where higher temperatures facilitate adsorption capacity19. For the two adsorbents, the uptake percentages increased with temperature at 298 K from 54.6 to 72.33% for PBW atb328 K and from 58.32 to 84.5% for SPBW at 338 K), depicting an endothermic adsorption process in this range, a trend similar that which has been documented for activated carbon and treated husk adsorbents where an increase in temperatures promote greater interaction between adsorbent surfaces and the adsorbate molecules19. At a temperature greater than 328–338 K, a slight leveling off and minor reduction in removal efficiency were noticed with PBW plateauing at 68.3% and SPBW declining slightly from 86.54% to 76.54%, inferring that excessive thermal agitation may promote partial desorption or weaken physical adsorption interactions, a phenomenon noted in thermodynamic analyses of Congo red adsorption where optimal temperatures exist above which adsorption efficiency reduces38.

Kinetic modeling of Congo red adsorption

The adsorption kinetics of Congo red onto poultry-based waste and sintered poultry-based waste were examined using by different models as indicated in Fig. 12 and the corresponding parameters are summarized in Table 1. The suitability of each model was determined based on the agreement between experimental and calculated adsorption capacities, correlation coefficients (R²), and percentage sum of squared errors (%SSE). The pseudo-first-order model (PFOM) produced higher correlation coefficients (R² ≈ 0.979–0.997 and 0.975–0.998 for PBW and SPBW respectively), suggesting that physical adsorption processes related to external surface interactions are involved6,31,35. This behavior is also supported by the closeness between experimental and calculated equilibrium capacities consistent and also the smaller values of sum of squared errors (%SSE) observed for the raw and the sintered poultry-based waste adsorbents. The increase in PFO rate constant (k₁) with rising initial dye concentration for both adsorbents as observed signify that enhanced concentration gradients accelerate the initial adsorption rate7,38. The pseudo-second-order model also fit the kinetic datasets for SPBW with R² values (≈ 0.979–0.988) and considerably low %SSE values. The PSOM performance signify that the adsorption process may involve extra valence forces through exchange between adsorbent surface functional groups and Congo red molecules or electron sharing which aligns with previous recent studies where PSOM is shown to better reflect the adsorption mechanism than PFOM for biomass-based adsorbents49.

Data from the intraparticle diffusion model displayed high R² values (0.935–0.983 for PBW and 0.965–0.988 for SPBW), implying significant contributions of intraparticle diffusion to the overall adsorption process. Nevertheless, the non-zero intercepts (C) inferred that intraparticle diffusion is not the sole rate-limiting step, but that, boundary-layer (film) diffusion also plays a vital role, most especially during the initial phase of adsorption31,35. Similar multi-stage behavior with quick surface adsorption followed by slower internal diffusion has been documented for porous biomass adsorbents in previous literature50.

The rate constant of the intraparticle diffusion (Kp) increased with initial dye concentration, indicating better penetration of CR molecules into internal pores under greater driving forces19,38. This higher Kp values for SPBW may indicate enhanced diffusion pathways and pore connectivity conferred by the calcination process which is in alignment with reports for thermally or chemically modified biochars demonstrating enhanced internal mass transfer51.

Isotherms investigations

Langmuir isotherm

The isotherms data of Congo red uptake by poultry-based waste and sintered poultry-based waste as determined from various models as illustrated in Fig. 13 and their parameters as listed in Table 2. For the Langmuir isotherm, higher value of correlation coefficients (R² = 0.987) for PBW than (R² = 0.946) for SPBW, signify that adsorption occurs on a homogeneous surface forming a monolayer32,35,36. It was noticed that SPBW displayed a much higher maximum adsorption capacity (Qmax = 60.23 mg g− 1) relative to PBW (Qmax = 40.16 mg g− 1), reflecting better accessibility and improved number of active sites after sintering. The separation factor (RL) values for the two adsorbents were below unity (0.601 for PBW, 0.244 for SPBW), affirming that the sorption process is favorable, with SPBW especially with lower value exhibiting strong affinity for Congo red19,32,36. This report is consistent with similar findings indicating that thermally or chemically modified biomass adsorbents display better monolayer adsorption and site uniformity11,13,19.

Freundlich isotherm

The assessment of the Freundlich model revealed that the adsorption capacity constant (KF) was higher for SPBW (42.102 mg/g(L/mg)¹/ⁿ) when compared with PBW (30.14 mg/g(L/mg)¹/ⁿ), while excellent fit for SPBW (R² = 0.998) highlight that the adsorption of CR by SPBW took place on heterogeneous surfaces with a range of site energies35,37,37. The 1/n values were both less than unity (0.726 for PBW, 0.115 for SPBW), indicating favorable adsorption and the lower 1/n for SPBW reflects stronger adsorbent- adsorbate interactions and pronounced surface heterogeneity, inferring that SPBW offers a broad distribution of active sites32,35.

Dubinin–Radushkevich (D-R) isotherm

The analysis of the D-R isotherm data revealed that SPBW again displayed higher maximum adsorption (qmax = 45.211 mg g− 1) relative to PBW (24.636 mg g− 1). Insight into adsorption mechanism was deduced from the value of the mean adsorption energy (E) obtained to be 0.605 kJ/mol for PBW and 7.053 kJ/mol for SPBW, indicative of predominant physisorption contributions32,36. High R² values (0.966 for PBW and 0.987 for SPBW) suggests a good fit to the D-R model, which is in alignment with trends observed in thermally modified biomass and biochar adsorbents where structural modification enhances interaction strength13,38. The observed different isotherm behaviors for PBW and SPBW can be ascribed to the physicochemical changes caused by sintering, as evidenced by FT-IR spectroscopy, BET surface analysis, and XRD characterization. In the case of PBW, the Langmuir model provides a better fit to the equilibrium data, suggesting adsorption on a relatively homogeneous surface with energetically similar adsorption sites and monolayer coverage. Also, BET investigation revealed that PBW possesses a lower specific surface area and pore volume, with smaller pore connectivity, indicating that adsorption primarily took place on external surfaces and readily accessible sites. Furthermore, FT-IR analysis of PBW reveal the presence of surface carbonyl and hydroxyl groups with relatively uniform distribution, supporting the assumption of energetically equivalent adsorption sites. The largely amorphous nature seen in XRD further suggests limited structural complexity, consistent with Langmuir-type adsorption behavior. On the other hand, SPBW displays adsorption behavior better explained by the Freundlich isotherm, which indicates multilayer adsorption on a heterogeneous surface with a distribution of adsorption energies. It is worthy to note that from BET analysis, the sintering of the biomass significantly increases the pore volume and surface area, signifying the development of new mesopores and enhanced pore accessibility. This structural evolution creates varying local environments and interaction strengths enhancing the adsorption sites. Thus, the combined effect of diversified functional groups, increased porosity, and altered surface morphology could have caused the wider distribution of adsorption energies in SPBW, favoring Freundlich adsorption over the idealized monolayer assumption of the Langmuir model. Consequently, the observed transition from Langmuir-dominated behavior in PBW to Freundlich-dominated behavior in SPBW indicates a fundamental shift from relatively uniform adsorption sites to a structurally heterogeneous surface induced by sintering.

The comparison of reported adsorption capacities in literature with the findings from this present work is as illustrated on Table 3. Under the investigated conditions (C₀ = 150 mg L⁻¹, pH 4, dosage = 30 mg), sintered poultry-based waste displayed a maximum adsorption capacity of 60.23 mg g⁻¹, which is comparable to or higher than several activated carbons and engineered biochars reported for Congo red uptake under similar acidic conditions (Table 3). Though some chemically activated or highly engineered biochars report higher capacities, these materials usually require higher processing costs, additional activation agents, or multi-step synthesis routes. Significantly, from this present study, SPBW demonstrates competitive adsorption performance without chemical activation, while exhibiting good regeneration efficiency and structural stability. When normalized by preparation simplicity and reusability, the adsorption efficiency of SPBW competes favorably with many documented activated carbons and engineered biochars. Although some engineered nanomaterials and chemically activated carbons report higher adsorption capacities, such materials often require multi-step activation, costly reagents, or sophisticated synthesis routes. On the other hand, the SPBW produced in this work is via a single-step thermal treatment without chemical activation, exhibits good regeneration performance over multiple cycles, and maintains effective adsorption under acidic conditions relevant to dye-contaminated industrial effluents. When these practical considerations are taken into account, the adsorption potency of SPBW is competitive within the class of low-cost, waste-derived adsorbents.

Thermodynamic analysis of Congo red adsorption onto PBW and SPBW

Thermodynamic parameters provide insight into the feasibility, heat effects, and randomness associated with the adsorption of Congo red onto PBW and SPBW. These were determined from Fig. 14 and their parameters as listed in Table 4. The data reveal that ΔG° values for both PBW and SPBW are negative at all studied temperatures (25–85 °C), with ΔG° becoming more negative as temperature rises (from − 1.55 to − 4.65 kJ/mol for PBW and − 1.65 to − 5.58 kJ/mol for SPBW). This negative ΔG° values show that adsorption is thermodynamically favorable and spontaneous for both adsorbents, with spontaneity enhanced at elevated temperatures19. Though the negative ΔG° values signify spontaneous adsorption; nevertheless, their relatively small magnitude indicates moderate driving forces characteristic of reversible physical adsorption instead of strong chemical bonding. Comparatively, SPBW demonstrates more negative ΔG° values across the temperature range compare with PBW, inferring that sintering improves the thermodynamic drive for adsorption which could be as a result of the improved surface energetics, like stronger affinity for the Congo red molecules or increased active site accessibility31.

The examination of ΔH° shows positive values of 16.93 kJ/mol for PBW and 32.55 kJ/mol for SPBW suggesting that in both cases, the adsorption is endothermic, implying that the process absorbs heat from the surroundings53. This infers that elevated temperatures favor adsorption, most likely by improving the mobility of dye molecules and enhancing penetration into pore structures or onto energetically favorable sites19,32. The data ΔS° reveal positive values (6.18 × 10⁻² and 1.13 × 10⁻¹ J/mol.K for PBW and SPBW, respectively) illustrate an increase in randomness (disorder) at the solid–solution interface during adsorption19,36. A rise in entropy is often linked with the displacement of solvent molecules or the release of water molecules from the adsorbent surface when dye molecules occupy active sites13,38. This displacement process causes a net rise in degrees of freedom (higher disorder) in the system. The greater entropy change for SPBW indicates that sintering may create more accessible or varied adsorption microenvironments, which when occupied by Congo red, lead to more significant reorganization of the system compared with PBW.

Desorption study

The desorption study (Fig. 15) shows that both PBW and SPBW still hold considerable amount of Congo red after adsorption, but desorption efficiencies reduced progressively with each successive cycle, which is in alignment with findings that repeated regeneration cycles typically reduce dye recovery because of structural changes and the progressive occupation of strong adsorption sites in the adsorbent43. Precisely, PBW desorption reduced from 71.41% in the first cycle to 56.71% by the sixth cycle, whereas SPBW which initially exhibited higher initial desorption efficiencies of 80.22% in cycle 1 reduced to 68.44% in the sixth cycles indicating a loss of accessible active sites and possible irreversible binding of dye molecules. It was observed that thought the desorption is progressive with successive cycle, it was however minimal with only 20.59% and 14.68% desorption efficiency observed for PBW and SPBW. The sintered sample displayed greater initial desorption efficiencies (80.22% in cycle 1) and gradual reduction in desorption efficiency (declining to 68.44% by cycle 6) compared to PBW, reflecting improved surface characteristics and stronger but more retrievable adsorption sites created by sintering, which aligns with previous findings indicating that thermally or chemically modified adsorbents often show superior regeneration behavior relative to unmodified counterparts43. The gradual rather than abrupt decline in adsorption efficiency over multiple regeneration cycles shows that the sintered poultry-based waste maintains structural integrity and functional stability under repeated adsorption–desorption conditions. In general, the sustained desorption percentages suggest that both PBW and SPBW can be regenerated and reused.

Mechanism of Congo red adsorption onto PBW and SPBW

The uptake of Congo red by poultry-based waste and sintered poultry-based waste proceeds via a multi-step, spontaneous, and temperature-dependent process that combines pore diffusion, surface interactions, and energetically driven binding phenomena. An integrated analysis of FTIR, zero-point charge, equilibrium, kinetic, and thermodynamic data provide comprehensive mechanistic insight as illustrated in Fig. 16. Findings from the combined experimental evidence shows that Congo red uptake onto PBW and SPBW is proposed to occur through multiple concurrent interactions. For instance, it was observed experimentally that FT-IR band shifts after adsorption which indicate the involvement of surface functional groups, while pHₚzc analysis confirms that surface charge varies with solution pH and influences adsorption behavior. Thus, the pHpzc-controlled pH dependence is proof of electrostatic binding between sulfonate groups of Congo red and protonated surface sites. Kinetic and isotherm modeling gave insight into rate-controlling steps and interaction strength but do not directly identify specific bonding mechanisms. Also, the magnitude of thermodynamic parameter of enthalpy change support physical interactions. Thus, the likes of electrostatic attraction between protonated surface sites and anionic sulfonate groups, π–π interactions between aromatic structures, hydrogen bonding, and intraparticle diffusion are proposed as contributing mechanisms consistent with the observed data. The relative contribution of each interaction cannot be isolated experimentally within the present study.

Conclusions

This research establishes a sustainable valorization approach for poultry-base waste via chemical-free thermal sintering at 650 °C, producing a functionally enhanced and structurally stable adsorbent for Congo red uptake from aqueous systems. Aside affirming adsorption performance, the study demonstrates how controlled thermal treatment modifies pore development, surface chemistry, and adsorption energetics, thus strengthening the structure–performance relationship in biomass-derived materials. The effectiveness of the adsorbent under acidic conditions is particularly useful for dye-house and textile effluents, which often require treatment prior to discharge. The enhanced adsorption performance of the sintered adsorbent demonstrates the importance of thermally induced structural stabilization, pore accessibility, and surface functional reorganization in enhancing dye–adsorbent interactions. Thus, the prepared adsorbent demonstrated a combined advantages of low-cost feedstock, simple preparation, reusability, and environmentally benign processing position sintered poultry-based waste as a promising alternative for resource-limited or medium-scale wastewater treatment systems when compare with highly engineered commercial carbons.
