Rechallenge with First-Line Anti-Tubercular Therapy After Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis in an HIV-Positive Patient with Tubercular Pleural Effusion
Authors: Akshdeep Singh Narula, Jerene Mathews, Febin Ashraf, Sagar Khadanga
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN), are severe cutaneous adverse drug reactions (SCARs), associated with a high mortality rate of 10%–30%.[1] While the management of these conditions are critical, identification of the culprit drug is also important; to avoid inadvertent intake of causative drugs, as well as to tailor therapy of the underlying condition. Algorithm of the Drug Causality for Epidermal Necrolysis (ALDEN) and patch test are helpful and easily available methods for identification of culprit drugs. However, in many cases, a single culprit drug is not identified and we are left with a list of suspects. Physicians usually err on the side of caution, leading to the overlabelling and sometimes restriction in critical therapies.[2] For example, in a case developing SJS/TEN secondary to firstline anti-tubercular drugs, the patient is usually transitioned to second-line drugs. However, second-line drugs have less efficacy. A drug-sensitive tuberculosis treated as multi-drug-resistant tuberculosis, possibly has a higher mortality due to the lower efficacy of second line drugs.[3] In immunosuppressed patients, this may be more of a concern.

Oral provocation testing is the most reliable method for determining the culprit drug when faced with multiple suspects. However, drug rechallenge is traditionally regarded as contraindicated in SJS/TEN because of the risk of recurrence and the paucity of supporting literature. Nonetheless, in immediate hypersensitivity reactions (including anaphylaxis) carefully supervised graded re-introduction/desensitization has been reported, particularly in the context of essential regimens such as first-line ATT.[4] We propose that in critical scenarios—such as the need to continue first-line TB therapy in an immunocompromised patient—a carefully planned and closely monitored drug rechallenge may be necessary.

A male in his 60s was diagnosed with tubercular pleural effusion, with pleural fluid cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification test (CBNAAT) positive for rifampicin-sensitive Mycobacterium tuberculosis. He was started on anti-tubercular therapy with first-line drugs: isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol regimen. Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) test conducted by immunoblot at the time of diagnosis of tuberculosis was negative. One month into the treatment, he developed oral and conjunctival erosions, along with skin blistering [Figure 1a and 1b].

He was subsequently diagnosed with TEN. The ALDEN score for all the suspected drugs was calculated and was as follows: rifampicin = 4+, isoniazid = 2+, pyrazinamide = 2+, ethambutol = 2+, and silodosin = -1 [Table 1]. Higher the ALDEN score, more likely the drug is to be causative. All suspected drugs were withheld.

We had a high suspicion of HIV positivity due to the concurrent presence of TEN and extra-pulmonary tuberculosis. A repeat HIV testing was performed by immunoblot after the development of TEN. It came out to be HIV-1 positive, and the results were confirmed by ELISA. The patient was effectively treated with an injection of hydrocortisone and intravenous immunoglobulin. Patient was started on the injection amikacin and tablet levofloxacin as a bridging therapy to avoid a flare of tubercular infection. After 2 weeks, re-epithelialization of lesions was complete [Figure 1c]. A patch test was performed with 30% concentration with with all the four first-line anti-tubercular drugs in petroleum jelly and was negative after 48 hours. Therefore, a plan to rechallenge with essential anti-tubercular drugs was formulated. The patient was counselled about the same and a written consent was taken.

Silodosin was re-challenged at its full dose first and was well-tolerated without any adverse effects. The order for rechallenge, based on the ALDEN score and a study by Lehloenya et al.,[5] was determined as ethambutol > pyrazinamide > isoniazid > rifampicin. The rechallenge protocol for ethambutol and isoniazid involved a gradual dose escalation: 1/4 of the dose on day 1, 1/2 of the dose on the following day, and the full dose on the subsequent day. This full dose was then maintained for the next three days. For pyrazinamide, which has a longer half-life, dose escalation was implemented at intervals of 2 days, and the full dose was sustained for an additional 3 days. Both ethambutol and pyrazinamide were well-tolerated. New oral erosions appeared with isoniazid on the second day of the rechallenge. Isoniazid was withdrawn and the patient was discharged on injection amikacin, tablet levofloxacin, tablet pyrazinamide, tablet ethambutol, and tablet silodosin. Antiretroviral therapy consisting of tenofovir, lamivudine and dolutegravir was started at this point and the patient tolerated it well [Figure 2].

Rechallenge with rifampicin was planned after healing of mucosal lesions. After 2 weeks, the mucosal erosions healed, however the patient was also clinically and radiologically better. After discussion with the pulmonologist, risk of reintroducing rifampicin appeared to outweigh the potential benefits; as the revised ALDEN score for rifampicin was 3+ and severe mucosal erosions had occurred twice [Table 1].

The patient responded well to the alternate anti-tubercular regimen. In the 12 months of follow up, patient remained disease free, with improvement in CD4+ counts.

We screened available literature on SJS/TEN spectrum, to the first-line anti-tubercular drugs and tabulated according to the culprit drug identified. Only cases where a systematic oral rechallenge would determine the culprit drug were considered in our review. Studies describing only pooled analysis of SCARs or cutaneous drug reactions were excluded [Supplementary Material].

Rifampicin appears to be the leading cause of SJS/TEN in a retrospective study with rechallenge in 46 patients with TB-associated CADR. The re-introduction reactions occurred within 72 hours of exposure to offending drug, which were mostly mild. They reported one mortality in re-introduction group (1/46), which occurred due to unrelated reason.[5] However, isoniazid appears more often in the smaller, scattered case reports. [Supplementary Material]. ALDEN assessment scoring also gives a drug notoriety score of +2 to rifampicin as compared to +1 to other first-line anti-tubercular drugs. It appears that, from the available data, both rifampicin and isoniazid are major culprits causing rechallenge reactions in TB-associated SJS/TEN.

It seems prudent from available data to present option of rechallenge to patients with SJS/TEN secondary to anti-tubercular therapy. This case report tries to provide a crude blueprint for rechallenging drugs in this scenario with heuristic risk-benefit calculation and ALDEN scoring at each stage.

Rechallenge can be done in TEN after weighing the need to reintroduce one or more of the suspected drugs, against the potential risks of rechallenge. The purpose of rechallenge is to establish safety, only the drugs that need to be reintroduced should be rechallenged. In cases where alternative drugs with similar efficacy are present for the treatment of underlying disease, rechallenge should not be attempted, and it is not a standard recommendation in SCAR. These cases require a multidisciplinary discussion with the clinician and the dermatologist, determination of risk-benefit ratio of the rechallenge of each drug, to uphold the ethical principle of non-maleficence.

Rechallenge starts with the drug associated with lowest risk of SCAR and then proceeds in the order of increasing risk. It appears that rifampicin and isoniazid are drugs associated with highest risk of causing SJS/TEN, and should be introduced last, or not at all.
