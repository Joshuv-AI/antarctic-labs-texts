Plasma amyloid-beta oligomer and phosphorylated tau: diagnostic tools for progressive Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Seong Soo A. An, John P. Hulme
Published: 2023

Introduction: Spanning the three stages of the Alzheimer’s disease (AD) continuum, amyloid-beta (Aβ40 and Aβ42) oligomers (AβO’s) and tau protein constitute a set of biomarkers ideally suited for the non-invasive monitoring of AD (Wolgin et al., 2022). AD progression is correlated with the presence of low molecular weight oligomers and not amyloid plaques. Moreover, low molecular weight AβO is present in the beginning and later stages of disease even when the plaque burden becomes prevalent. Furthermore, changes in AβO levels occur prior to the initial stages of the disease, suggesting they may be used to evaluate the risk of disease onset. Currently, the monoclonal antibody Crenezumab™ is considered the primary treatment for reducing low molecular weight oligomers in various animal models and humans. AβO’s can be sourced from blood, plasma, and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) and acquired quickly, permitting long-term storage and retrospective analysis. However, samples usually contain single molecular oligomer concentrations often requiring amplification (spiking/seeding or ultrasonic) or enrichment prior to measurement with conventional instrumentation and antibody-based platforms such as SiMOA or Sigma Millipore (An et al., 2017; Dominguez et al., 2022). In addition to AβO, other protein biomarkers that span all three stages of AD include total tau (t-tau) and CSF phosphorylated tau (p-tau). Tau is a microtubule-binding protein subject to phosphorylation, and its aggregation is a major hallmark of AD pathology. t-tau and some p-tau isoforms, such as p-tau threonine 181 and p-tau threonine 217, are elevated in AD CSF. However, t-tau CSF levels poorly correlate with plasma levels due to peripheral interference restricting its usage as a biomarker (Janelidze et al., 2020).

On the other hand, p-tau threonine 181, p-tau threonine 217, and p-tau threonine 231 plasma levels strongly correlate with CSF values highlighting their potential as AD early-mid stage (Braak 3–4) biomarkers as well as predictors of brain amyloidosis. AβO and tau are two markers that span the three stages of AD, making them ideally suited for monitoring disease progression. Yet, their attomole presence, cross-reactivity, and sensitivity to peripheral interferents limit their broad application requiring clinical samples to undergo significant pretreatment, which may include seeding/amplification or enrichment prior to measurement, increasing assay costs (labor and instrumentation) and limiting the potential for real-time monitoring and patient participation.

Seeding, amplification, and interference: Aβ oligomers are susceptible to seeding by soluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 peptides via secondary and primary nucleation that interact to produce highly ordered aggregates. Several protocols that utilize seeding include protein misfolding cycling amplification (PMCA), quaking induced conversion (QuIC), and multimer amplification. The latter two are included in the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention-listed diagnostic criteria for probable Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease (https://www.cdc.gov/prions/cjd/diagnostic-criteria.html). Aβ1–42 peptides act as seeds in PMCA and QuIC, promoting the growth and formation of AβO aggregates in less than 48 hours (Gong et al., 2021). When combined with numerous rounds of sonification or shaking, aggregation time can be reduced, leading to higher assay throughput. PMCA has exhibited > 90% specificity for AD, whilst QuIC versatility has been demonstrated in detecting fmol a-synuclein levels in the CSF of patients suspected of Parkinson’s disease (Du et al., 2020). Collection of CSF (lumbar puncture) is expensive and invasive, requiring highly skilled personnel to minimize the common side effects of bleeding and headaches, restricting its broader application within clinical settings. On the other hand, blood plasma is obtainable at fractional costs rendering it more suited to mass testing. An amplification protocol that utilizes blood plasma is the multimer detection system-oligomerized amyloid beta (Gong et al., 2021). Originally developed for detecting scrapie (prion disease) in sheep, multimer detection system-oligomerized amyloid beta utilizes an inexpensive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and conventional instrumentation to detect AβO plasma levels within 48 hours. A commercialized version of said technique (inBlood™ oligomer amyloid beta test), produced by People Bio Inc, Korea, discriminated AD patients from cognitively normal individuals with 100% sensitivity and 92% specificity, albeit with a sample size of 50 (Youn et al., 2020). Moreover, the commercial test showed its worth as a screening tool for individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), as well as a capacity for differentiating those with non-cognitive impairment or subjective cognitive impairment from those with AD (Dominguez et al.,2022). However, it should be noted that PMCA can also be used to measure AβO levels in blood plasma. For example, a recent investigation highlighting the ability of the c-Abl kinase inhibitor, imatinib, to reduce AβO levels in blood plasma was conducted using PMCA (Dominguez et al., 2022). Although blood plasma collection is considerably less invasive than CSF, it still requires additional processing comparable to whole blood. However, whole blood measurements would inevitably require the inclusion of costly selective membranes (polysulphone, polyvinyl chloride, or derivates thereof) or hexadecyl-alkylated cellulose beads (Saito et al., 2020) to restrict protein interferents such as albumin, fibrinogen, and immunoglobulins from reducing assay sensitivity. Other interferents that may also affect assay performance include gut microbial amyloid-like antigens (curli and fimbriae), which are known to cross-react with antibodies to amyloid plaques.

In retrospect, it is doubtful that the suggested gut amyloids would significantly affect assay performance given the parameters employed (Wang et al., 2017) and the sanctity afforded to blood by the gut microbiome; however, amyloids resulting from lung infections might. Residing in the pulmonary tracts with proximity to the blood (fractional oral microbiome), microbes Klebsiella pneumoniae and Staphylococcus Aureus can produce prion-like oligomers (microcin E492 & phenol-soluble modulins) capable of seeding peripheral events (Shahnawaz et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2021). In addition, Klebsiella pneumoniae microcin E492 shares 95% homology with human amyloid with the bacterium known to exacerbate mild chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (the third leading cause of death globally), indirectly elevating serum Aβ40/Aβ42 levels (Bu et al., 2015). Although the link between chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and AD requires clarification, increased serum Aβ40, Aβ42, and total Aβ levels were reported in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease compared with normal controls associated with cognitive decline (Bu et al., 2015; Higbee et al., 2021). Moreover, a longitudinal study demonstrated that chronic obstructive pulmonary disease could influence the risk of developing MCI, with midlife obstructive lung disease found to be associated with an increased risk of developing Alzheimer’s disease or MCI (Lutsey et al., 2018).

Whether microcin E492 can form a hybrid oligomer/aggregate with Aβ42 like many other human amyloids (tau, α-Syn, and TAR DNA-binding protein 43) would require further investigation. Moreover, a recent report highlighted the difficulties in differentiating pure AD from mixed pathology AD (Choi et al., 2021; Dominguez et al., 2022; Sengupta et al., 2022), suggesting such events might go undetected using the assay parameters set out by multimer detection system-oligomerized amyloid beta and other amplification protocols. Although to be fair, the proprietors of People Bio are currently developing variations of their assay (oligomer panel) to accommodate for other protein oligomers (α-syn and amylin) (Raimundo et al., 2020; Hardy-Sosa et al., 2022) that can occur in blood plasma.

Phosphorylated Tau: Even if AβO could be used to establish a correlation between pulmonary inflammation and AD onset, once an individual enters the early stages of AD, the prominence of AβO as a standalone diagnostic correlate begins to diminish (Janelidze et al., 2020; Babapour et al., 2021). Conversely, the diagnostic significance of tau begins to increase, specifically phosphorylated tau and its numerous isoforms, providing reliable predictions of Aβ– to Aβ+ conversion at the intermediate stage in MCI patients (Janelidze et al., 2020; Karikari et al., 2021). Even though CSF p-tau and total tau concentrations are well-established markers of AD progression, significant variations in concentrations between individuals of differing ethnicities occur, suggesting a tailored approach is required. However, this level of diagnostic refinement may not be possible (Mielke et al., 2021) as predicted AD costs (including care) are set to surpass $1 trillion by 2050 in the US unless cheaper alternatives are realized. Moreover, existing data show that p-tau levels correlate better with AD than total tau levels, the latter better reflecting central nervous system degeneration. To this end, the selectivity of p-tau blood plasma measurements might provide some relief in terms of primary care costs as well as reductions in patient sampling stress. From the diagnostic point of view, plasma p-tau isoforms, p-tau231, p-tau217, and p-tau181 measured on N-terminal tau forms have demonstrated similar performances and capacities to predict brain Aβ and tau compared with their CSF counterparts suggesting interchangeability for clinical purposes (Figure 1). Moreover, a recent head-to-head study of all three p-tau forms showed equivalent performances (Mielke et al., 2021).

However, the downside to plasma tau is that samples require significant enrichment, usually involving an immunoprecipitation technique (Gothenburg method) combining antibodies directed at pre­defined epitopes with a magnetic bead assay. The upside is that enrichment takes less than an hour, and samples are compatible with single molecule array and modern instrumentation. In recent years blood plasma markers have demonstrated similar performances to traditional measurement, permitting the transition from specialized to general AD diagnostics to begin in earnest. Taken together, plasma AβO and the numerous isoforms of phosphorylated tau represent a cost-effective way of monitoring AD progression in the clinic. Although outside of this narrative, it is worth noting that the move from clinic to patient-specific diagnostics has already begun with the emergence of salivary AD biomarkers (Schepici et al. 2020).
