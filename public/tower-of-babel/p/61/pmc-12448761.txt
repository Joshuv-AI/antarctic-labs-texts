A necessary paradigm shift: recognizing the surgeon-advocate in academic surgery
Authors: Adom Bondzi-Simpson, Betel Yibrehu, Amanpreet K. Brar, Nour Bakhache, Kennedy Ayoo, Elliott Yee, Chantal Valiquette, Tyee K. Fellows, David-Dan Nguyen, Armaan K. Malhotra, Najma Ahmed, Savtaj Brar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Health equity and the social determinants of health are increasingly prioritized in health care delivery across North America and globally, yet academic medicine remains ill equipped to support equity-focused advocacy. We argue that this stems mainly from a gap in recognizing advocacy as an academic pillar alongside research, education, and administration. Advocacy is undervalued in academic medicine, as reflected in teaching, hiring, and promotion criteria and what is published in academic journals. Health equity is essential for the health of populations, and the current structure of academic medicine should be redesigned to recognize, value, and support equity-based advocacy efforts. Advocacy can be integrated in 2 key areas: medical education and faculty roles and promotion pathways. A new academic role, the surgeon-advocate — whose work focuses on the engagement, knowledge dissemination, and administration of advocacy-based work to affect system change — is vital for a paradigm shift that accepts advocacy into the essential work of academic medicine. The concept of a surgeon-advocate is not new and is vital to our identities as physicians. However, formally embracing advocacy within academic institutions represents the paradigm shift needed to move closer to health equity goals. This analysis proposes a critical revision to academic surgery and, more broadly, academic medicine. We provide practical steps to intentionally weave advocacy and health equity into the fabric of academic medical institutions to improve how we practise and serve our patients.

Across North America, several governing educational bodies establish frameworks of core competencies that define the foundational skills that every practising physician must possess. For example, analogous to the 6 core competencies defined by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education, the CanMEDS framework from the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada (RCPSC) is organized under 7 roles: medical expert, professional, communicator, collaborator, leader, health advocate, and scholar.

The medical expert role is central in the CanMEDS framework, with the other 6 roles occupying equal positions around this core medical expert role. Despite this model of equivalence among the other 6 roles, there appears to be an inherent hierarchy within academic medicine in how we value these efforts. What we value is readily apparent in what we teach, how we recruit and hire faculty, how we assess promotion, what we publish in our journals, and how we measure academic success.

Health equity is fundamental to population health. However, health advocacy — the individual- and system-level efforts to realize health equity — remains undervalued in academic medicine.1 The current infrastructure of academic medicine is not equipped to support, measure, or reward equity-based advocacy efforts. This phenomenon may be partly owing to a lack of recognition of advocacy efforts as sufficiently academic compared with research or education.1

Advocacy can be defined as intentional action by a physician to address upstream determinants of health and create system change through their professional work, including ensuring access to care, mobilizing resources, addressing health inequities, and influencing health policy.2 We propose the establishment of a new academic role that leverages this definition of advocacy: the surgeon-advocate, who focuses their academic work on implementation of grassroots advocacy and advocacy knowledge translation or dissemination to address upstream factors that determine outcomes for patients and health systems. The thesis of this paper centres around the core competency of advocacy in models of medical education across North America.

Hierarchies of core educational competencies within academic institutions

Hiring, academic job descriptions, and promotion

The typical academic role description provides a window into what medical institutions have traditionally valued as the academic contributions of their faculty members. At the University of Toronto and many other institutions, these fall broadly into 3 streams: clinician-scientist, clinician-investigator, and clinician-teacher. Newer roles include clinician-ethicists, quality improvers, and administrators. Promotion focuses on 3 pillars: research, teaching, and creative professional activity.

Table 1 compares job descriptions from 3 major academic institutions in North America. These descriptions frequently mention core educational competencies like communicator, collaborator, leader, scholar, and professional, but the advocate role is conspicuously absent. This omission raises questions about how effectively the health care system can address health equity without explicitly prioritizing advocacy. Indeed, advocacy is often the primary driver of system-level changes in health policy and resource allocation for equity-seeking populations.

The academic curriculum vitae

The curriculum vitae (CV) serves as an essential tool in academic hiring, professional development, and promotion. However, advocacy work is difficult to capture in the prescribed format of the CV. For example, templates from the Association of American Medical Colleges do not include explicit sections for advocacy.3 This forces advocates to misclassify their work under headings like “educational activities,” “professional community activities,” “other scholarly products,” or “other creative products.” In doing so, advocacy work is, at best, misclassified and, at worse, literally othered. Published literature on building an academic CV is similarly devoid of spaces to showcase advocacy, favouring traditional research and scholarly work.4 The current, accepted format for academic CVs undervalues system-level knowledge translation of science into practice and public education, which is necessary to improve the health of populations.

Medical journal articles

Table 2 shows that major North American medical journals typically accept original research, commentaries, and reviews, but none explicitly accept papers that describe advocacy initiatives. Clinician-advocates are thus compelled to tailor their work to fit one of these research categories or other categories, such as quality improvement or educational initiatives, if they aim to see their work published in mainstream medical journals. This transformation changes the nature of the original effort and is different from the inherent goals.

Advocacy as a catalyst for other core competencies: the UpSurge mentorship program

By integrating advocacy into their work, physicians may become better communicators, collaborators, and leaders while fulfilling their roles as medical experts and professionals. For example, UpSurge, a resident-led initiative created in 2020, addresses the paucity of diversity in surgical training, particularly gender, ethnoracial, and social imbalances.5 The program provides mentorship, networking, and resources to help students from traditionally underrepresented backgrounds overcome barriers in surgical training.6 In its first 2 years, the program paired more than 130 Canadian and international students with surgical mentors, hosted surgical workshops, performed longitudinal outreach to high school students in priority neighbourhoods, offered scholarships for students with financial liabilities, secured more than $90 000 in funding, and expanded into multiple chapters across Canada. The program illustrates how advocacy naturally intersects with core educational competencies, fostering communication, collaboration, and shared leadership in diverse communities (Figure 1 and Figure 2).

New path forward

Advocacy in medical education

If advocacy is recognized as a formal academic pursuit in medicine, knowledge about how and why must be integrated into medical education. The development of clinician advocacy programs, like existing clinician investigator programs, would legitimize these activities. Clinician advocacy programs would train clinicians in advocacy skills, with assessment focused on community engagement, policy development, and addressing the social determinants of health, rather than traditional outputs like publications or grants. Furthermore, similar to the RCPSC clinician-educator area of focused competence program, formalized training, mentorship, and recognition of advocacy are required for ongoing professional development.7 This would cultivate a national community of practice and equip a generation of physician-advocates to address social determinants of health and health equity. To our knowledge, these gaps are present in most medical education models worldwide, exemplifying the importance of development in this area and the need to rethink how we cultivate and retain champions of advocacy in the future.

Recognizing advocacy in faculty recruitment and promotion

Advocacy builds community and trust, supports connection, and improves patient outcomes. Table 3 outlines modern surgeon-advocates who operate outside traditional academic tracks. These faculty members have been nationally recognized for their advocacy efforts; however, presumably, advocacy was not a part of their initial job descriptions in their university appointments.

Furthermore, the surgeon-advocate role could counter the minority tax, the additional burden placed on faculty from minority groups to participate in committee and other service or academic work by virtue of being one of only a few members of a minority group. Members of minority groups are doubly taxed when they engage in equity and inclusion work often without recognition, exacerbating burnout and isolation.8 Establishing a surgeon-advocate role may be a counteractive force to tackle burnout among surgeons who prioritize and excel in advocacy-based work without academic recognition.

Finally, standardizing how advocacy is captured and codified is crucial.9 Documenting advocacy work allows institutions to align advocacy with academic scholarship, making it a legitimate component for academic credit at all levels. This formal institutional recognition may catalyze physician advocacy initiatives by shifting them from extracurricular pursuits to mandates of defined appointments.

Advocacy in medical journals

Increasingly, we hear calls for more inclusive publications in medical journals on topics with an expanded view of academic work.10 Showcasing advocacy, quality improvement, and program evaluations outside of the traditional research-only paradigm is critical for medical journals. Physicians have always served as advocates for their patients. Examples such as clean water initiatives, asbestos safety, workplace safety, and tobacco are powerful examples. Recently, social issues such as environmental equity, health equity, and antiracism have been labelled as global public health crises. These require thoughtful discourse and innovative solutions that could be published in medical journals. Typically, medical education journals have answered this call; however, in addition to this, general medical journals should value and display these submissions to enrich and increase the relevance of the journals in the current social climate.

Conclusion

The advocacy role has been relatively undervalued in academic medicine, exemplified in the way faculty are hired, promotion eligibility is evaluated, and what is published in medical journals. Centred advocacy is instrumental in building a future that embraces health equity within academic institutions. Why? Because advocacy builds community and a sense of trust and is a vital component of quality health care. The role of the surgeon-advocate should be a new career track within academic medicine (Table 4). In this role, the surgeon-advocate focuses on addressing upstream determinants of health by creating system change to mobilize resources, influence policy, and improve health. The concept of a surgeon-advocate is not new. Advocacy is a vital part of the physician’s identity; it is a deep part of our history and why many of us choose to be doctors. However, accepting the advocacy role intentionally and building it into the infrastructure of academic medical institutions represents a much-needed paradigm shift to cultivate an environment where advocacy may flourish, pushing us closer to our goals of health equity.
