Machine learning model-guided selective use of temporary diverting ileostomy in rectal cancer surgery: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Shengli Shao, Yanqi Li, Jianghao Li, Lu Liu, Ying Zeng, Chunyue Song, Wenjun Huang, Lei Mu, Jichao Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe appropriate use of temporary diverting ileostomy (TDI) in rectal cancer surgery lacks standardized criteria. A randomized controlled trial was performed to evaluate whether the Risk-Guided Temporary Ileostomy Decision (RTID) system, a machine learning-based anastomotic leakage (AL) prediction model, could improve the suitability of TDI utilization. A total of 872 patients with stage I–III rectal cancer undergoing anterior resection were randomized 1:1 to surgeon discretion versus RTID-guided decisions. The final analysis included 750 patients (control, n = 368; RTID, n = 382). The RTID group showed lower overall TDI rate (18.6% vs. 40.5%; P < 0.001) and unnecessary stoma formation (17.7% vs. 41.3%; P < 0.001). Although a numerical increase in necessary TDI use was observed (55.6% vs. 10.0%), this difference was not significant (P = 0.057). Critically, the incidence of the co-primary safety outcome, AL, was comparable between the RTID and control groups (2.4% vs. 2.7%; P = 0.753), indicating that the pre-specified TDI reduction endpoint was met, while the study was underpowered to formally test non-inferiority for AL. RTID offers an objective tool to support TDI decision-making. The present study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (no. NCT04999007) on August 8, 2021.Subject terms: Rectum, Rectal cancer, Surgical oncology

Introduction

Anastomotic leakage (AL) continues to represent one of the most notable complications following rectal cancer surgery, with reported incidence rates varying from 3 to 10% across major cancer centers1–5. The clinical implications of AL extend far beyond immediate perioperative outcomes, being associated with mortality rates of 2–10% and accounting for more than 20% of short-term postoperative mortalities4,6. Growing evidence further indicates that AL may negatively impact long-term oncological outcomes, including higher rates of local recurrence and decreased overall survival4,7. Pathophysiologically, AL involves the leakage of enteric contents into the pelvic cavity, triggering a cascade of inflammatory responses that can lead to life-threatening conditions such as fecal peritonitis, systemic inflammatory response syndrome and sepsis8. Due to these potentially catastrophic consequences, temporary diverting ileostomy (TDI) has emerged as the gold standard protective measure, particularly for AL high-risk patients undergoing low anterior resection9.

The clinical implementation of TDI presents a complex paradox. The creation of a TDI is a technically straightforward, low-risk procedure that mitigates the severe consequences of AL. Nevertheless, it introduces substantial short- and long-term morbidity. Previous studies have demonstrated that more than half of patients with TDI experience clinically notable complications including peristomal skin dermatitis, stoma prolapse and retraction10,11, dehydration requiring hospitalization12, parastomal hernia and stoma closure-related complications13. More importantly, quality-of-life assessments consistently demonstrate markedly higher rates of low anterior resection syndrome among patients with TDI compared with non-TDI cases14–17. Current clinical practice reveals a striking discrepancy: a high rate of TDI implementation contrasts with a much lower incidence of the AL it is intended to prevent. A comprehensive meta-analysis of 8560 cases showed an overall TDI rate of 53.0%, in sharp contrast to the 8.0% AL rate18. A notable finding was that, among patients who developed AL, only 41% had received a TDI. Conversely, patients who did not experience AL, 57% of them received a TDI, implying that a large proportion of patients missed necessary TDI while numerous patients underwent unnecessary TDIs. This traditional decision-making strategy, predominantly based on surgeon experience rather than objective criteria, results in dual adverse outcomes: patients with high-risk AL who do not receive TDI may experience increased suffering from AL-related complications, while those without AL who received unnecessary TDIs may endure physical, psychological and financial burdens. Notably, previous studies demonstrated that the TDI rate has steadily increased in patients undergoing anterior resection for rectal cancer, likely reflecting a growing recognition of the severe complications associated with AL19,20. The fundamental challenge lies in developing reliable AL risk stratification tools. While numerous prediction models have been proposed, including nomograms, machine learning models, scoring systems and the Dutch Leakage Score21–25, their clinical adoption remains limited due to several key limitations, such as validation primarily in retrospective cohorts, absence of integration of real-time intraoperative variables, which often crucially inform the final surgical decision. More importantly, no existing system has demonstrated the capacity to provide clinically actionable guidance for TDI decision-making.

In this work, we show that a support vector machine-based predictive model integrating preoperative and intraoperative variables to quantify AL risk can optimize the use of TDI in rectal cancer surgery. In a randomized controlled trial involving 750 patients with stage I-III rectal cancer undergoing anterior resection, the Risk-Guided Temporary Ileostomy Decision (RTID) system significantly reduces overall and unnecessary TDI rates while maintaining comparable AL rates, establishing a risk-guided framework for selective TDI implementation.

Results

Participants

A total of 872 consecutive eligible patients with rectal adenocarcinoma between September 2021 and March 2025 were enrolled and randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to conventional management (control) and RTID groups. During the study period, 68 patients in the control group were excluded primarily due to surgery postponement, cancellation or intraoperative protocol deviations, such as unexpected metastasis discovery or necessary adjustment of surgical strategy, while 54 patients were excluded from the RTID group for comparable reasons. The final analysis included 750 patients, with 368 in the control group and 382 in the RTID group, as detailed in the flow diagram of enrollment (Fig. 1). All variables and primary outcomes for the final included patients were complete, with no missing data. Baseline characteristics between the control and RTID groups are summarized in Table 1, full details of the comparison are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Primary outcomes

The incidence of AL was comparable between the control and RTID groups （2.7% (10/368) vs. 2.4% (9/382), P = 0.753; Fig. 2a). Among the 19 AL cases, 13 cases were classified as grade B and 6 as grade C, with no perioperative mortality. The RTID group demonstrated a significantly lower TDI rate compared with the control group (18.6% vs. 40.5%; P < 0.001; Fig. 2b). Consistent with this reduction, unnecessary TDI procedures were also substantially less frequent in the RTID group (17.7% vs. 41.3%; P < 0.001). The proportion of appropriately selected AL patients for TDI was 55.6% (5/9) in the RTID group versus 10.0% (1/10) in the control group. No statistically significant difference was found for this exploratory outcome (P = 0.057; Fig. 2c). In this prospective cohort, the control group exhibited a higher TDI rate (40.5% vs. 31.2%) but a lower AL rate (2.4% vs. 4.0%) compared with prior retrospective research23. Annual AL and TDI rates of ~3000 patients from 2010 to 2025 were also analyzed, revealing a temporal increase in TDI implementation alongside a decline in AL rates (Fig. 2d).

The relationship between TDI and AL occurrence remains controversial. In the control group, the AL rate was numerically lower in patients who underwent TDI (0.7% vs. 4.1%), though this difference was not significant (P = 0.054). A divergent pattern emerged in the RTID group: patients who received TDI had a higher AL rate than those who did not (7.0% vs. 1.3%; P = 0.003; Fig. 2e). This pattern provides evidence for the model’s effective risk stratification, demonstrating that the RTID system successfully identified and allocated TDI to patients at genuinely higher baseline AL risk, while appropriately avoiding diversion in lower-risk patients. The significant association between TDI and AL in the RTID group thus reflects this successful risk-based selection.

Model performance

Effective risk stratification for AL provides a crucial foundation for guiding TDI decisions. The model demonstrated good discrimination, with a bootstrap-validated area under the curve (AUC) of 0.738 ± 0.070 (from 1000 resamples; Fig. 3a). Diagnostic performance metrics included a sensitivity of 52.6%, specificity of 83.4%, accuracy of 82.7%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 7.6%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 98.5%, resulting in a 7.6% AL rate in high-risk patients and a 98.5% non-AL rate in low-risk patients (Fig. 3b). Subgroup analyses revealed consistent predictive performance in the control group (AUC = 0.792 ± 0.073; Fig. 3d), with sensitivity of 50.0%, specificity of 84.6%, accuracy of 83.7%, PPV of 8.3% and NPV of 98.4% (Fig. 3e). This corresponded to an 8.3% AL rate in high-risk patients and a 98.4% non-AL probability in low-risk patients.

Notably, predicted risk probabilities were significantly higher in patients with AL compared with non-AL patients (P < 0.001; Fig. 3c, f, i), confirming a strong association with AL. The incidence of AL in the high-risk group was higher than the actual AL rates in each group (P = 0.028, 0.036, 0.002, respectively), demonstrating clinical utility. Interpretability

Interpretability is crucial for machine learning models as it helps elucidate the decision-making logic behind the model’s output and provides additional clinical insights. The ranking of variable importance is presented in Fig. 4a. When comparing patients with and without AL, tumor location, diabetes, and electrolyte disturbances demonstrated the most pronounced between-group differences (Fig. 4b). Other examined variables exhibited limited association with AL. Nevertheless, the prediction model achieved robust risk stratification (Fig. 3a). This notion was further assessed by calculating the AUCs of the model and individual variables, where the aggregated AUC of the model outperformed that of all single variables (Fig. 4c). These findings underscore the necessity of integrating multiple factors in TDI decision-making. As human cognition has inherent limits in processing and integrating multiple complex variables simultaneously, the model provides a more consistent basis for clinical decisions compared to reliance on traditional experience alone26.

Factors associated with TDI decision-making

Few studies have systematically evaluated factors influencing surgeons’ decisions to perform TDI9. As the patients in the RTID group received model-guided interventions, the factors that drive surgeons to TDI implementation were analyzed in the control cohort. AUC calculations identified operative time (reflecting procedural complexity) and tumor location (an established AL risk factor) as the strongest predictors, followed by number of stapler firings (indicating anastomotic quality) (Fig. 5a). Notably, these top three determinants represent intraoperatively perceptible variables, suggesting surgeons preferentially perform TDI in the cases with high-difficulty surgery, low anastomosis or technical dissatisfaction. This pattern implies TDI decisions are frequently made intraoperatively rather than with preoperative planning.

To further validate these findings, univariable and multivariable regression analyses were performed after excluding surgical approach and indwelling pelvic tube due to severe imbalance in their distribution (both at 99.7%). The multivariable analysis identified several independent predictors associated with TDI decision-making, including number of linear stapler firings [odds ratio (OR) = 1.78; 95% CI, 1.17–2.71; P = 0.007], operative time (OR = 1.01; 95% CI, 1.01–1.02; P < 0.001), placement of trans-anal drainage tube (OR = 0.09; 95% CI, 0.01–0.75; P = 0.027), tumor location (OR = 0.80; 95% CI 0.71–0.90; P < 0.001), tumor size (OR = 0.76; 95% CI, 0.62–0.93; P = 0.006), electrolyte disturbances (OR = 0.29; 95% CI, 0.13–0.64; P = 0.002), diabetes (OR = 0.31; 95% CI, 0.13–0.74; P = 0.009) and hypertension (OR = 0.41; 95% CI, 0.23–0.72; P = 0.002) (Fig. 5b). However, tumor size, electrolyte disorders, diabetes and hypertension showed inverse associations with TDI, which contradicts established risk paradigms. It remains unclear whether this reflects surgeons’ limited sensitivity to comorbidities and laboratory findings or represents confounding effects through other unmeasured variables related to TDI decisions. Conversely, stapler firings, operative time, trans-anal drainage and tumor location demonstrated expected directional associations. These results collectively suggest that intraoperative perceptions dominate real-world TDI decisions, preoperative factors (comorbidities, laboratory values) may be undervalued or overlooked during surgery, and cognitive biases likely favor immediately visible surgical factors over integrated risk assessment.

Discussion

In the present study, the feasibility of RTID for intraoperative TDI implementation was evaluated in patients with rectal cancer who received anterior resection. The results demonstrated that, compared with conventional management, RTID yielded a reduction in overall and unnecessary TDI use along with a potential increase in necessary TDI, while maintaining comparable AL rates.

To mitigate the severe clinical consequences of AL, TDI has been widely adopted. Current practice relies heavily on surgeons’ empirical judgment, leading to notable global variation in TDI rates, from 20.7 to 66.6% across Asia23,27,28 to 54% in the Netherlands29,30, and 89% in Australia/New Zealand31, despite AL rates generally remaining <10%18. This discrepancy was also pronounced in the present study, with a TDI rate of 40.5% in the control group contrasting with an AL rate of only 2.4–2.7%. Surgeons must weigh the risk of AL against the potential morbidity and quality-of-life impact of TDI when making stoma decisions. Current guidelines, such as the Chinese expert consensus and the American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons recommendations, rely on integrating a limited set of risk factors and lack robust, prospective validation for decision-making9,32. The risk of AL serves as one of the primary considerations for determining whether to perform a TDI. Several risk stratification models have been proposed, but the majority were validated using retrospective data, and their clinical efficacy remains to be confirmed21,33–36. In our previous study, a machine learning-based model was developed to predict AL and assess the feasibility of assisting selective implementation of TDI using data from 2240 patients. The model demonstrated good predictive performance in two independent cohorts (AUC1 = 0.78; AUC2 = 0.88). Importantly, the model development study suggested that the use of such a model could potentially reduce the overall TDI rate from ~30% to 19%, unnecessary TDI from 32% to 17%, and increased necessary TDI by ~40%23. Despite these promising results, the study was also limited by its retrospective design. To address this limitation, the present trial was performed to evaluate the effectiveness of the model to guide TDI decisions in real-world clinical practice. In the present study, the RTID group, which used the model for TDI decision, demonstrated a 21.9% lower overall TDI rate compared with the control group (18.6 vs. 40.5%) and a 23.6% reduction in unnecessary TDI (17.7 vs. 41.3%).

Interpretability analysis confirmed the clinical relevance of the model, as feature importance rankings aligned with established surgical understanding. The model’s robust predictive performance (AUC = 0.738) despite limited univariable significance underscores its capacity to capture complex, multifactorial interactions underlying AL risk, a key advantage over simpler risk-scoring methods. It should be emphasized that the absence of significant univariable associations does not indicate a variable’s lack of importance in the multivariable context, as machine learning models can capture complex interactions and nonlinear effects. These findings support the multifactorial nature of AL pathogenesis—a premise well-aligned with established theories from previous studies, providing preventive insights: Clinicians should focus not just on individual risk factors but on the combinatorial effects of risk factor clusters. This not only confirms its biological plausibility but may also offer clues for optimizing clinical intervention strategies by revealing previously underappreciated risk interactions. Beyond the mechanistic insights into AL risk prediction, the clinical decision-making process surrounding TDI merits further examination, as surgeons play a central role both in AL occurrence and TDI selection. Surgeons’ experience and skills correlate with AL incidence37, yet prospective evidence on how they, as TDI’s key decision-makers, shape TDI choices is lacking. TDI decisions are driven by both rational and non-rational factors. Surveys from Australia and New Zealand, and Italy showed consensus in extreme clinical scenarios but marked variability in moderate cases38,39, surgeon age under 50 years and risk aversion also independently influence TDI38,40. This highlights a challenge where certain factors may overshadow patient-specific risks in stoma decisions. Our findings corroborate this. High surgical complexity, low or unsatisfactory anastomosis increase TDI preference, reflecting rational technical decision-making, yet counterintuitive results including lower TDI in larger tumors or metabolic disorders may suggest a disconnect between decision and clinical practice or non-rational dominance. International studies from the UK and US confirmed non-rational influences such as recent criticism, unfamiliar anesthesiologists and recency bias on TDI41,42, with consistent findings from the UK and Italy43,44. These findings reveal the complex interplay between rational and non-rational factors in clinical decision-making. It should be noted that these findings are presented as observations from our specific cohort and should be interpreted with caution given the exploratory nature of the analysis and the potential influence of unmeasured confounding or the clinical context. Despite this, the factors associated with surgeons’ TDI decisions identified in this study offer valuable empirical data that could inform the future refinement of predictive models. For instance, understanding which intraoperative factors surgeons heavily weigh could help ensure that future models adequately capture these clinically salient variables, potentially improving both model performance and clinical acceptance.

The present study has several limitations: First, the non-blinded design and the use of a single surgical team, while ensuring technical consistency, may have introduced contamination bias as surgeons became familiar with the model’s logic over time. Future studies could adopt a double-blind design with comparable separate surgical teams implementing model-recommended and routine decisions to improve evaluation of the model. Furthermore, this trial was not powered for the exploratory outcome of decision appropriateness, and the observed positive trend, while notable, was not statistically significant. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm whether the RTID system reliably improves the accuracy of patient selection for TDI. Second, the relatively low AL incidence observed (2.4–2.7%), while reflecting improved surgical quality, may limit the study’s statistical power for model evaluation. Furthermore, with more than 40% stage III patients and exclusion of neoadjuvant therapy cases, the generalizability may be constrained. Surgeon technical skill was not assessed, representing an unresolved issue in the present study design. Third, as a single-center study, external validation of the model is needed, particularly for regions with notable baseline population differences. Despite the fact that the RTID system has been introduced into routine clinical practice at our institution following trial completion, with continued prospective monitoring of its performance and clinical impact, future multicenter, cross-regional studies are needed to accurately assess these limitations. Fourth, specific limitations pertaining to the model development process have been described in detail in our prior publication that introduced the model. In the context of the present trial, as clinical practice evolves, some included variables (e.g., laparoscopic surgery and drain use) have become nearly standardized (99.7%), which may affect the original feature weights and suggests opportunities for future model streamlining. The current sensitivity and PPV leave room for improvement, suggesting incorporation of emerging technologies such as indocyanine green fluorescence imaging as objective assessment indicators to enhance predictive performance. While currently a simple calculator, future integration with an electronic medical record system for real-time dynamic prediction model presents a promising direction, especially with the advancement of large language algorithms. Nonetheless, the model provides valuable foundational data for future development. Finally, while AL was a co-primary outcome, our sample size calculation was based on the anticipated effect on TDI rates. The overall low incidence of AL means that the study was underpowered to definitively conclude non-inferiority regarding safety. The observed comparable AL rates between groups are reassuring but should be interpreted with caution. The primary analysis was conducted on a per-protocol basis to evaluate the efficacy of the model when adhered to. While this approach provides a clear test of the model’s intrinsic logic, it may overestimate the effect size on TDI reduction compared to an intention-to-treat analysis and does not assess the strategy’s real-world effectiveness under conditions of non-adherence.

In conclusion, these data demonstrate that RTID (accessible at https://alrisk.21cloudbox.com/ or at the backup link https://rectal-app-yzs3fjcvvbfhbh87wqnryf.streamlit.app/) significantly reduces overall and unnecessary TDI rates without an obvious or significant impact on the observed AL rates (2.4% vs. 2.7%). Its robust predictive performance (AUC = 0.740) stems from comprehensive multidimensional risk assessment, particularly identifying clinically silent risk combinations often overlooked in conventional decision-making. The study revealed surgeons’ overreliance on intraoperative factors (such as operative time, location of tumor and anastomotic quality) while undervaluing preoperative risk indicators when TDI formation was made. To our knowledge, the present study has established a risk-directed TDI framework: Routine TDI for the patients with high-risk AL and avoidance for the cases with low-risk AL. RTID demonstrates an advance surgical decision-making from experiential to precision medicine, creating a system for selective TDI implementation.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (approval no. TJ-IRB20210643). The trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04999007) on August 8, 2021. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study design and participants

In the present single-blind, parallel group randomized controlled trial, eligible patients with rectal cancer at Tongji Hospital (Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China) between September 1, 2021 (first enrollment) and March 17 2025 (last enrollment) were enrolled. The inclusion criteria comprised: Age between 18 and 85 years, biopsy-confirmed adenocarcinoma, No prior anticancer therapy, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification I-III, preoperatively assessed as stage I-III tumors according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer Cancer Staging Manual 8th Edition, and written informed consent. The exclusion criteria included: Pregnancy or lactation, severe mental disorders or communication barriers preventing informed consent, planned neoadjuvant therapy, acute complications (bleeding or perforation) and concurrent malignancies. Withdrawal criteria involved: Delayed or canceled surgery, intraoperative identification of sigmoid colon tumors, extensive metastasis precluding resection, combined organ resection, altered surgical approach (Miles procedure, Hartmann procedure or colostomy), intraoperative pauses >30 min, positive air leak tests or surgeon non-compliance with intervention. Additionally, analysis of temporal trends in AL rates and TDI rates incorporated both the present study’s data and 2240 rectal adenocarcinoma cases from our prior retrospective study, with detailed methodology available in the previous publication23. Participant sex was recorded as male or female based on medical records and self-report. Age was recorded as a continuous variable and also categorized as <70 years or ≥70 years for baseline comparison. Sex and gender were not used as stratification factors in the randomization or primary analysis, as the study was powered for the primary outcome of TDI rate reduction, and no a priori hypothesis for sex- or gender-based differences in treatment effect was specified. The study protocol and statistical analysis plan are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Surgical procedures

The study procedures were carried out by a team of 12 gastrointestinal surgeons, all holding associate senior professional titles or higher, along with 2 graduate research assistants. Each participating surgeon possessed ≥10 years of clinical experience and performed ≥80 colorectal cancer resections annually. All eligible patients were randomly allocated to either the control group or intervention group, following standardized preoperative protocols that included bowel preparation 1 day before surgery and prophylactic antibiotic administration 30 min prior to surgical procedure. Total mesorectal excision served as the standard surgical technique for all cases, with comprehensive documentation of all predictive variables. In the control arm, following anastomosis construction, surgeons performed air leak testing. For cases demonstrating negative results, the web-based RTID platform (accessible at https://alrisk.21cloudbox.com/; the output is a risk score for classification, not a direct probability of AL) computed and archived AL risk scores. Notably, surgeons remained blinded to the risk assessment results and retained ultimate decision-making authority regarding TDI implementation in the control group. The intervention arm employed a structured protocol wherein patients with negative air leak tests were stratified by RTID-calculated risk: Cases scoring >0.5 underwent TDI, while those ≤0.5 avoided diversion. Additionally, the protocol incorporated an override mechanism permitting surgeons to exclude patients when fundamentally disagreeing with RTID assessments.

Randomization

The randomization list was generated using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp.) with a fixed seed value of 6, employing a 1:1 allocation ratio. The list was securely stored by data management personnel who had no involvement in data analysis or patient enrollment. Eligible patients who completed preoperative evaluation and provided written informed consent were referred to an independent research coordinator. This coordinator assigned participants to each group according to the predetermined randomization sequence. The treatment allocation was communicated to the surgical team for intervention. The randomization sequence was concealed from the enrolling investigators and the surgical team. Allocation was revealed to the surgical team only after the patient was deemed eligible to proceed with surgery.

Intervention

The intervention in the present study utilized the RTID system, a support vector machine learning model, previously developed using data from 2240 patients23. This support vector machine model demonstrated robust predictive performance for AL with AUC values of 0.78 (n = 550) and 0.88 (n = 545) in two independent test cohorts23. The 21 variables used for the initial model development were selected based on a combination of: (1) established clinical relevance derived from prior literature on AL risk factors in rectal cancer surgery, and (2) availability within routine perioperative documentation to ensure clinical utility. These relevant variables spanning demographic characteristics [sex, age, body mass index (BMI)], comorbidities [hypertension, diabetes, prior lower abdominal surgery, Brinkman index (cigarettes/day × years), alcohol use, ASA score], laboratory values (hemoglobin, albumin, electrolyte disturbances (preoperative serum sodium <135 mmol/L or >145 mmol/L, potassium <3.5 mmol/L or >5.5 mmol/L, calcium <2.25 mmol/L or >2.75 mmol/L)), tumor related features [tumor size, clinical tumor, lymph node, metastasis (TNM) stage, tumorous obstruction, tumor location (distance from anal verge)] and intraoperative factors (surgical type, operative time, number of linear stapler firings, trans-anal drainage tube, pelvic drain placement). To address the low incidence of AL, the model employed oversampling (1:1) and hyperparameter adjustment (kernel = linear, C = 100, probability = True) techniques during training. The model was integrated into the intraoperative workflow at a standardized time point: after the creation of the colorectal anastomosis, but prior to the final decision regarding TDI construction. Following a negative air leak test of the anastomosis, the surgical team entered all requisite preoperative and intraoperative variables into the system at this juncture. The resulting risk prediction was then provided to the surgeon to inform the final decision on whether to perform a TDI. To guide appropriate use, all input variables for the web platform were constrained to predefined, clinically plausible ranges. These ranges are provided as a reference to users to prevent entry of erroneous data. The details of each variable and its acceptable input range are provided as Supplementary Table 2.

Interim analysis

This trial was designed to conduct one interim analysis. The analysis was performed when half of the planned sample size had been enrolled, primarily to evaluate the safety of the intervention. The predefined stopping criteria for the trial were as follows: (1) The study would have been terminated for safety concerns if the incidence of AL and/or the rate of TDI in the RTID group was significantly higher than that in the control group, indicating potential harm. (2)The study would have been terminated for efficacy if the data had demonstrated that the TDI rate in the model-assisted group was significantly lower than that in the control group, indicating clear benefit and success of the model-guided strategy. (3) The study would have been terminated for futility if the analysis had shown that the model lacked predictive capability for AL (AUC < 0.5). (4) If surgeons had overridden the model’s recommendations in more than 10% of cases in the RTID group, that would have indicated a critical level of disagreement that could have compromised protocol adherence and introduced significant bias into the study outcomes.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes of the present study focused on AL and TDI rates. The TDI rate was specifically defined as the proportion of patients who underwent TDI during the initial surgical procedure. Patients who required secondary operation for TDI due to AL or intestinal obstruction were excluded from the stoma rate calculation, as the present study focused exclusively on primary intraoperative TDI decision-making.

The assessment of TDI decision appropriateness was based on a decision-analytic framework that juxtaposes intraoperative decision against the ultimate postoperative outcome. The following definitions were operationalized:

A necessary TDI: This was defined as a diverting ileostomy performed in a patient who subsequently experienced a clinical AL (Grade B/C). In this scenario, the TDI is considered to have been clinically indicated and beneficial, as it likely mitigated the severity of the leak.

An unnecessary TDI: This was defined as a stoma created in a patient who had an uneventful recovery without any evidence of AL. This classification reflects the clinical reality of overtreatment. These patients endure the morbidity and burden of a stoma without deriving a clear, observable benefit in terms of leak. While it is impossible to prove a TDI was unnecessary (as it may have prevented a leak), this classification pragmatically reflects the scenario of overtreatment, where a patient endures the stoma’s morbidity without deriving a clear, observable benefit in terms of leak mitigation.

AL was strictly classified according to clinical significance45, with only grade B and grade C included in the analysis. Subclinical AL (grade A) that did not necessitate alteration in the patient’s postoperative management was explicitly excluded from evaluation. The diagnosis of AL was established using a combination of clinical assessment and objective investigations. Definitive diagnosis was made when either of the following criteria were met: (i) Direct visualization of enteric content in the abdominal drainage tubes; or (ii) the presence of clinical symptoms (including but not limited to abdominal pain, fever, leukocytosis or signs of peritonitis) accompanied by corroborative imaging findings. The imaging criteria required either computed tomography evidence of perianastomotic abscess or encapsulated fluid collection, or demonstration of contrast extravasation on rectal contrast examination. AL cases were further stratified by management approach: Grade B AL represented cases where the clinical manifestations were adequately controlled through conservative measures including antibiotic therapy and maintenance of existing drainage procedures. By contrast, grade C AL were defined by their requirement for surgical reintervention, which included but were not limited to procedures such as abscess drainage or diverting stoma formation.

Sample size calculation

The sample size calculation was powered for the primary effectiveness outcome of TDI rate. Consequently, the study was not specifically powered to demonstrate non-inferiority in the primary safety outcome of AL rate, given its relatively low incidence. Based on preliminary data, the TDI rate was 23.0% in the model-assisted group compared with 33.3% in conventional practice. Assuming a two-sided type I error rate (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power (1-β) of 0.90, with a 1:1 allocation ratio between the RTID and control groups, the required sample size was calculated as 392 patients per arm. Accounting for a potential withdrawal rate of 10% during the trial, the total target enrollment was set at 872 patients. These calculations were performed using PASS 15.0.5 software (NCSS, LLC).

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and primary outcomes were compared between groups, excluding patients who met withdrawal criteria after randomization. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are expressed as counts (percentages). Intergroup comparisons were performed using independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-squared test for categorical variables. Additionally, the normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with a non-normal distribution were summarized as median [interquartile range] and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. The predictive performance of the model for AL was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis, with assessment of the area under the curve using bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value and accuracy. Feature importance analysis was employed to assess the interpretability of the machine learning model using the model’s inherent coefficients to rank the relative contribution of each predictor variable to the model’s output. Comparisons of TDI implementation rates between the RTID and control groups were performed using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. In the control group, factors associated with TDI decision-making were analyzed using univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses, variables demonstrating statistical utility in univariable analyses (p < 0.05) were included in the subsequent multivariable analysis. These analyses were performed on a per-protocol population. This included all protocol-adherent patients: control group patients who received usual care, and RTID group patients for whom the surgical team followed the model’s recommendation without override. All reported P-values were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp.) or Python 3.12.4.
