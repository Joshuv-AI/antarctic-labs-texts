Numbers matter: How pediatric endoscopy quality varies with annual procedural volume
Authors: Jeannie S Huang, Lillian Choi, Tom K Lin, Jenifer R Lightdale, Catharine M Walsh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThere is limited data linking pediatric endoscopist experience and procedural quality. We aimed to examine the associations of faculty physicians' years in practice and independent annual procedural volume (APV) with published quality indicators for pediatric endoscopy, including terminal ileal intubation rate (TIIR), cecal intubation rate (CIR), and total procedure time.MethodsWe extracted quality indicators, as defined by the Pediatric Endoscopy Quality Improvement Network (PEnQuIN), for ileocolonoscopies performed by faculty endoscopists at a pediatric tertiary‐care academic institution from October 2021 to May 2024. Endoscopists were categorized by years' experience and APV. Quality indicators were compared across groups using Kruskal–Wallis analyses. Multivariate modeling was performed to identify variables predicting terminal ileal intubation and TIIR ≥ 85%. Proportional hazards modeling was performed to identify variables predicting shorter procedure duration for combined esophagogastroduodenoscopy and ileocolonoscopy procedures.ResultsNine hundred and eighty‐five ileocolonoscopies were performed independently by 20 faculty endoscopists, with varying years' experience (<5 years: N = 7; 5–10 years: N = 3; ≥10 years: N = 10), and a median (interquartile range) APV of 19 (14, 45). Most procedures (71.7%) were scored as demonstrating adequate bowel preparation. Overall TIIR and CIR were 86.3% and 91.6%, respectively. In multivariate modeling, APV ≥ 40 was identified as predictive for TIIR ≥ 85% (p < 0.01) while faculty years' experience (≥10 vs. <10 years) predicted shorter procedure duration (adjusted hazard ratio [confidence interval]: 1.40 [1.23, 1.59]).ConclusionsOur data showed a significant association between pediatric quality indicators and individual endoscopist APV and years of experience. Our findings also suggest that performing ≥40 ileocolonoscopies annually may help ensure high‐quality endoscopic procedures in children.Keywords: children, colonoscopy, quality indicators

INTRODUCTION

Cecal and terminal ileal intubation rates (CIR and TIIR), along with total procedure time, are recognized as key quality indicators for pediatric endoscopy, as outlined in the Pediatric Endoscopy Quality Improvement Network (PEnQuIN) guidelines. 1 Terminal ileal intubation, in particular, is considered a critical quality indicator for pediatric endoscopy due to the frequent use of ileocolonoscopy for diagnosing and surveilling inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in children. The PEnQuIN guidelines established minimal acceptable standards for three key quality indicators for ileocolonoscopy: a TIIR ≥ 85%, a CIR ≥ 90%, and an adequate bowel preparation rate ≥80%. 2 In contrast, colonoscopy in adults is primarily performed for colorectal cancer screening, with CIR serving as a primary quality indicator. A minimum standard for CIR of ≥90% has been set by the joint American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) and American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) Task Force on Quality in Endoscopy 3 ; however, no minimum standard for TIIR is specified. Additionally, a minimum standard for bowel preparation adequacy of ≥85% has been established for outpatient adult colonoscopy.

During endoscopic training for both adult and pediatric endoscopists, procedural volume is often regarded as a surrogate for proficiency. However, experience alone does not guarantee competence. 4 Research in the adult setting has shown significant variation in the rate with which competency is achieved. 4 , 5 , 6 Regarding maintenance of competence, minimum annual procedural volume (APV) has been associated with higher colonoscopy quality among adult endoscopists. 7 Using CIR as a quality indicator, every 100 procedures performed annually has been associated with an increased odds of achieving cecal intubation. 7 However, the same analysis did not show conclusive associations between annual colonoscopy volume and other quality indicators, including adenoma detection and adverse event rates.

For credentialing purposes, competence thresholds have been established for both pediatric and adult endoscopy that outline the minimum number of colonoscopies a trainee must perform before competence can be formally assessed. 4 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 The North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (NASPGHAN) has recommended a competence threshold of 120 procedures for pediatric colonoscopy, 11 whereas adult requirements vary, ranging from 150 in Korea and Canada to 275 in the United States. 8 , 10 While the ASGE/ACG Task Force has not set a minimum annual colonoscopy procedure volume standard for practicing gastroenterologists to maintain competence, the British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) requires a minimum of 100 colonoscopies annually. 9 To date, there has been no data to inform minimum annual maintenance volume standards for pediatric endoscopic procedures, including ileocolonoscopy. The primary aim of our study was to determine the associations between faculty physicians' years in practice and independent APV with published quality indicators for pediatric endoscopy. Our secondary aim was to model the relationship between APV and procedural quality in pediatric endoscopy.

METHODS

Ethics statement

We were granted exempt status for this quality improvement study through established processes at the Institutional Review Board of the University of California, San Diego (UCSD) and Rady Children's Hospital.

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study of faculty endoscopists at a tertiary care pediatric center.

Faculty and procedural data

We identified all ileocolonoscopies performed at our institution between October 2021 and May 2024. At all procedures, endoscopy nurses recorded presence of faculty and trainees as well as procedure start and end times, anal insertion time, cecal intubation time and ileal intubation time (if achieved). All procedures performed independently by a single faculty member, without participation by other faculty members or trainees, were included in the analysis. We excluded any procedures performed in whole or in party by trainees; those involving other faculty members; procedures performed in cases involving multiple surgical services; as well as those in patients with prior partial or total colectomy and/or in whom terminal ileum intubation was not an a priori procedural goal.

Data were extracted from the electronic medical record (EMR) (Epic Systems). Procedural data extracted included cecal and terminal ileal intubation status (yes/no), Boston Bowel Preparation score (BBPS), and insertion time to ileum, insertion time to cecum and total procedure time (defined as time from scope insertion to full withdrawal). Additional procedural data, categorized binarily (yes/no), included performance of any additional endoscopic maneuvers beyond biopsies (e.g., polypectomy, capsule placement), bowel preparation adequacy (defined as total BBPS score ≥6, with a minimum score of 2 for each bowel segment assessed), and outpatient patient status at the time of the procedure.

Individual procedure reports identified from the EMR were manually reviewed for inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Definitions

Faculty endoscopy experience was characterized by years since graduation from an accredited pediatric gastroenterology training program and APV. Years of experience was categorized as <5 years, 5–<10 years, or ≥10 years from graduation. Faculty APV was determined by the average number of ileocolonoscopy procedures performed per calendar year. CIR was determined by dividing the number of cecal intubations divided by the number of ileocolonoscopies performed. Likewise, TIIR was determined by dividing the number of ileal intubations divided by the number of ileocolonoscopies performed. Overall CIR and TIIR were determined across all performed ileocolonoscopies while faculty CIR and TIIR were calculated based on individual procedural volumes.

Statistical analysis

To identify the optimal threshold for ileocolonoscopy APV (i.e., number of procedures per year) that predicts a TIIR ≥ 85%, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted at select APV thresholds. Since the APV ranged from 4 to 72 procedures per year, threshold screening was performed at increments of 10 procedures, starting from 10 up to 70 ileocolonoscopy procedures annually. Similarly, to determine the optimal threshold for years in practice predicting a TIIR ≥ 85%, ROC curves were plotted at 5 and 10 years of experience. In both instances, threshold selection was based on the highest area‐under‐the‐curve value and the lowest false positive rate.

Quality indicators, including TIIR, CIR, insertion time to ileum, insertion time to cecum, and total procedural time, were compared across faculty groups based on APV and years of experience thresholds (as identified through ROC curve screening described above). Chi‐squared analyses were used for categorical variables, and Kruskal–Wallis tests were used for continuous variables due to skewed distributions.

To model the relationship between APV and procedural quality in pediatric endoscopy, logistic regression modeling was used to calculate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) for predictors of achieving terminal ileal intubation (yes/no), with all variables showing significant univariate associations included in the model. Given a faculty volume of 20, to avoid overfitting, a general linear model restricted to two predictor variables (APV and faculty years' experience) was used to calculate the ability of faculty to achieve a TIIR ≥ 85% (yes/no). 12

Finally, Cox proportional hazards modeling was employed to identify variables that significantly predict shorter procedure duration, incorporating all significant univariate associations. As the majority of procedures performed were combined esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD)/ileocolonoscopies (N = 967), the analysis related to total procedure duration was restricted to this procedure type.

Statistical analysis was performed using JMP Statistical Software, version 18. Multiple univariate comparisons were adjusted for using the Bonferroni correction, in which the significance threshold was set at p < 0.05 divided by the number of comparisons made. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all multivariate modeling.

RESULTS

Over the study period, 985 ileocolonoscopies (967 EGD/ileocolonoscopies and 18 ileocolonoscopies) were independently performed by 20 faculty members (6 males and 14 females, 5 White and 15 non‐White) without the presence of a trainee. Seven hundred and fifty‐five procedures were excluded from the analysis due to presence of a trainee and/or other faculty member, involvement of multiple surgical services, and/or performance in patients with prior or total colectomy and/or in whom terminal ileum intubation was not an a priori procedural goal. Faculty members' years of experience varied (years since graduation from training): <5 years: n = 7; 5–<10 years: n = 3; ≥10 years:10). Faculty APV ranged from 4 to 72, with a median (IQR) of 19 (14, 45) independently performed ileocolonoscopies annually.

The optimal threshold for ileocolonoscopy APV to predict TIIR ≥ 85% (yes/no), identified by ROC screening, was 40 procedures per year (≥40 vs. <40 procedures annually). The optimal threshold for faculty years of experience to predict TIIR ≥ 85% was 10 years (≥10 vs. <10 years).

Across all procedures, 71.7% were determined to have adequate bowel preparation (BBPS score ≥6). Although the rate of adequate bowel preparation was higher among inpatients compared to outpatients (84.2% vs. 71.4%, inpatient vs. outpatient status, respectively), this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.22). Overall CIR and TIIRs across all 985 procedures were 91.6% and 86.3%, respectively. TIIR ≥ 85% demonstrated notable but not statistically significant variation by APV, but not faculty years of experience. In contrast, univariate evaluations of time‐based pediatric quality indicators revealed significant variation by both APV and faculty years of experience with the exception of total procedure duration (significant for faculty years of experience but not APV) (Table 1). The median total procedure duration across all ileocolonoscopies was 36 min with an interquartile range of 30–45 min. The median time to ileum across all procedures achieving terminal ileum intubation was 11 min with an interquartile range of 8–16 min. Terminal ileum intubation occurred within 10 min in 35.1% of all ileocolonoscopies, particularly among faculty with APV ≥ 40 ileocolonoscopies per year (48.1% vs. 25.7%, APV ≥40 vs. <40 ileocolonoscopies, p < 0.0001) and with ≥10 years of experience (42.3% vs. 28.6%, ≥10 vs. <10 years of experience, p < 0.0001) as compared with counterparts. Quality indicators also varied significantly by additional procedural maneuvers and bowel preparation adequacy, but not by outpatient versus inpatient status (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Data from this endoscopic quality improvement initiative revealed significant variation in TIIR for ileocolonoscopy procedures based on faculty APV, but not faculty years in practice. In contrast, total procedure duration varied based on faculty years in practice but not faculty APV. Furthermore, adequate bowel preparation was associated with both successful terminal ileum intubation and shorter total procedure times. These findings underscore that procedural efficiency is multifactorial, influenced by a range of clinical and logistical factors, and highlight potential areas for standardization and improvement across healthcare settings.

Relevant adult societies have endorsed PEnQuIN quality indicators for pediatric endoscopy including the ASGE and the Canadian Association of Gastroenterology (CAG). Our study supports the premise that quality indicators for endoscopy established for adult patients should not be applied to pediatrics, as there are distinct practice differences and varying procedural indications. 1 , 2 For instance, colonoscopy in adults is primarily used for colorectal cancer screening and surveillance, whereas ileocolonoscopy in children is most commonly employed for diagnosis and monitoring IBD. In turn, terminal ileal intubation is not a standard quality indicator in adult endoscopy, with reported rates of terminal ileal intubation varying widely from 40% to 91%. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 In contrast, TIIR in considered a key quality indicator in pediatric endoscopy, although its rates also vary considerably, from 42% to over 90%, among pediatric endoscopists. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Factors reported to negatively impact terminal ileal intubation in both children and adults include poor bowel preparation, the presence of an intestinal stricture, and after‐hours (vs. daytime) procedure timing. 19 Our data contribute to this literature by demonstrating an association between TIIR and proceduralist experience, as characterized by annual independently performed ileocolonoscopy procedure volumes.

Suggested maintenance colonoscopy volumes for adult endoscopists start at 100 procedures per year. 9 However, these standards may not be directly applicable to pediatric endoscopy. Although published procedure times for pediatric ileocolonoscopy 18 , 21 vary—possibly due to factors like colonic elasticity in younger children—they are generally longer than those for adult colonoscopy. 21 In pediatrics, published insertion time‐to‐cecum intervals range from under 10–30 min, with terminal ileal intubation almost always requiring additional time (up to 15 min longer). 18 In comparison, insertion times‐to‐cecum intervals are notably shorter in adult endoscopy, typically ranging from 5 to 10 min. 23 , 24 , 25

Recent pediatric studies have demonstrated that achieving an ileocolonoscopy insertion time of less than 10 min is both feasible and safe. 26 The data from this study corroborate these findings and further suggest that APV at a provider level as well as provider years of experience may influence the likelihood of achieving this. Specifically, in our study, faculty with an ileocolonoscopy APV of at least 40 and with at least 10 years of experience were more likely to achieve a terminal ileum intubation time of under 10 min than faculty counterparts.

To best understand the implications of our findings for pediatric endoscopy, it is important to consider several key differences between pediatric and adult practice. First, pediatric gastroenterologists rarely perform procedures more than 1 day per week, with many scheduled for “half day” procedural blocks with anesthesiologist assisted sedation. Second, upper endoscopies are performed approximately twice as often as ileocolonoscopies in children, addressing common procedural indications such as abdominal pain, feeding difficulties, eosinophilic esophagitis, and celiac disease. In pediatrics, ileocolonoscopy is commonly performed in combination with EGD for the diagnosis and management of IBD. The typical duration for pediatric combined EGD/ileocolonoscopy procedures (from intubation of the esophagus to rectal withdrawal) in this study ranged from 30 to 45 min, not including pre‐ or post‐sedation time or procedure room turnover. Given this, a pediatric endoscopist with a typical 3–4‐h half‐day procedure block each week is unlikely to perform 100 ileocolonoscopies annually, as demonstrated by the volumes reported in this study and in prior work. 27

Our study suggests that pediatric endoscopists with average annual ileocolonoscopy procedural volumes of less than 100 procedures per year can still achieve minimum standards for key pediatric endoscopy quality indicators. Our findings suggest that a threshold of 40 annual procedures is necessary to maintain high pediatric endoscopic quality. Further large‐scale, preferably multicenter studies are now needed to more precisely define the minimum APV required to ensure high‐quality pediatric endoscopy. If corroborated, a target APV of approximately 40 ileocolonoscopies could be considered as a potential pediatric endoscopy quality intervention for endoscopists who do not meet pediatric endoscopy quality benchmarks (TIIR ≥ 85% and CIR ≥ 90%). Target APVs may also serve as a reference point for discussions around credentialing standards, though further validation is warranted.

Finally, it is important to note that endoscopist audit and feedback have been shown to enhance endoscopic quality, particularly for low performers. 28 Current electronic medical record technologies enable real‐time monitoring of pediatric endoscopy quality indicators. 29 , 30 It is important to implement these tools at all centers performing pediatric endoscopy to track quality indicators, enable comparison across centers, and use the data to ensure and promote high‐quality endoscopic care. Prior integration of endoscopy quality benchmarks, including cecal and ileal intubation rates, within annual endoscopist feedback reports has been demonstrated. 29 Additionally, select electronic medical record systems can provide access to a real‐time endoscopy metrics dashboard, where proceduralists can view their ileal and cecal IRs, enabling real‐time audit and feedback. 29

A limitation of our study is that it was conducted at a single pediatric tertiary‐care institution, which may not be representative of other pediatric endoscopy centers. In addition, endoscopist APV was calculated based on number of independently performed ileocolonoscopies, which may underestimate overall procedural experience in clinical settings where endoscopists frequently perform procedures with trainees, other endoscopists, and other surgical services. Further research is required to demonstrate reproducibility of these findings across different institutions and practice settings, with a larger number of endoscopists, to better identify predictors of the minimum standard for a TIIR of ≥85%.

CONCLUSIONS

Our data show a significant positive association between pediatric endoscopy quality indicators and faculty independent APVs at a teaching hospital. Our findings further suggest that pediatric gastroenterology faculty should perform at least 40 ileocolonoscopies annually without the presence of a trainee or assistance from other faculty members, to ensure they can reliably provide high‐quality endoscopic care to children. Further research that aggregates and analyzes data from diverse pediatric endoscopy practice settings is needed to establish definitive minimum APV standards for pediatric endoscopy.
