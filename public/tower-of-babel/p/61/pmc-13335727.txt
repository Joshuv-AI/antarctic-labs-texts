Carbon framework controlled charge storage and ion-transport dynamics in hydrothermally synthesized MnS nanocomposite electrodes for advanced supercapacitors
Authors: Komal Ali Rao, Muhammad Ehsan Mazhar, Ammar Tariq, Muhammad Bilal, Malika Mehboob, Majed M. Alghamdi, Adel A. El-Zahhar, Ayesha Perveen, Amir Shahzad, Saira Sarwar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Contemporary supercapacitors require electrode materials that combine high charge-storage capability with rapid and reversible ion/electron transport, rather than relying solely on specific capacity. In this work, manganese sulfide (MnS) and its carbon-based nanocomposites (MnS/rGO and MnS/CNT) were synthesized via a facile hydrothermal route to investigate how different carbon architectures regulate charge-storage behavior, ion transport, and electrochemical kinetics. Structural and surface analyses confirm the formation of MnS with partial surface oxidation and its uniform integration within reduced graphene oxide (rGO) sheets and carbon nanotube (CNT) networks. Among the investigated electrodes, MnS/rGO exhibits a markedly enhanced specific surface area (222 m2 g−1) and an open mesoporous architecture, providing abundant electroactive sites and efficient ion-diffusion pathways. Electrochemical measurements demonstrate that MnS/rGO delivers the most favorable pseudocapacitive response, achieving a specific capacity of 404.2 C g−1 at 1.1 A g−1 and retaining higher capacity than MnS and MnS/CNT at elevated current densities. To directly link this performance to transport properties, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy is used to extract ionic conductivity, relaxation time, exchange current density, and kinetic rate constant for all electrodes. MnS/rGO shows the highest ionic conductivity (16.3 S m−1), the shortest relaxation time (0.077 s), and enhanced kinetic parameters (J0 ≈ 6.8 × 10−3 A g−1, k ≈ 2.4 × 10−8 cm s−1), evidencing faster ion migration and interfacial charge transfer than pristine MnS or MnS/CNT. Collectively, these results identify 2D rGO scaffolding as an effective strategy to co-optimize structure, transport, and pseudocapacitive kinetics in MnS-based electrodes, positioning MnS/rGO as a strong candidate for high-performance supercapacitors.

Introduction

The rapid depletion of fossil fuels and the increasing environmental burden of greenhouse-gas emissions have intensified the global push toward clean and renewable energy technologies. Ensuring the reliable integration of intermittent sources such as solar and wind requires efficient energy storage systems capable of buffering fluctuations in power generation and demand.1–5 In this regard, conventional batteries and capacitors are known as the most established energy storage systems, yet each suffers from inherent limitations. Batteries offer high energy density but their sluggish charge–discharge kinetics and limited cycle life restrict their performance at high rates, while traditional capacitors deliver excellent power capability but lack sufficient energy density. These limitations have driven growing interest in electrochemical supercapacitors (SCs), which uniquely combine fast charge storage, exceptional cycling durability, and enhanced safety.6–8

SCs store energy either by electrostatic (EDLC behavior) charge accumulation at the electrode/electrolyte interface or by fast, surface-confined redox reactions (pseudocapacitive behavior). These mechanisms enable them to deliver much higher power and longer cycle life than conventional batteries.9–11 Overall performance of SCs is dominated by the properties of the electrode material, including its electronic conductivity, accessible surface area, pore structure, redox activity, and mechanical/chemical stability during repeated cycling. In practice, carbon materials such as activated carbon (AC), reduced graphene oxide (rGO), and carbon nanotubes (CNTs), carbon nano fibers, MOF derived oxides and selenides, primarily provide electric double-layer capacitance due to their large accessible surface area and conductive networks.12–16 While transition-metal compounds (oxides, sulfides, hydroxides) and conducting polymers (graphene and carbides) employed to fabricate electronically conductive electrodes through multiple redox states and rich surfaces chemistry for multiple applications of energy storage devices. Recent research has therefore focused on engineering nanostructured electrodes that combine high surface area with hierarchical porosity and abundant redox-active sites. Researchers are integrating transition-metal-based phases with conductive carbon frameworks to simultaneously improve specific capacitance, rate capability, and long-term durability in SCs.17,18

Transition metal sulfides (TMS) such as CoSx, NiSx, MoS2, WS2, and SnS have emerged as an attractive class of pseudocapacitive materials for SC electrodes.19 They offer high theoretical capacitance, relatively good electronic conductivity, earth abundance, and multiple accessible oxidation states that support rich redox chemistry.19–23 A specific type of nanostructured carbon called reduced graphene oxide (rGO) is widely used as an electrode material in SCs due to its exceptional electrical characteristics, chemical stability, increased specific surface area, improved mesopore size distribution, and stronger conductivity.24,25 Compared to their individual components, composites that combine rGO with metal sulfides exhibit distinctive features and enhanced performance.25,26 Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are one-dimensional tubular nanomaterials created when carbon atoms hybridize with sp2. Materials scientists are interested in them due to their unusual structure and exceptional physicochemical features.27,28 Due to their exceptionally high surface area, exceptional mechanical and thermal stability, and high electronic conductivity, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), one of several conductive carbon-based additives, have been approved for use in the creation of composite electrodes with TMSs. It also offers a porous system with a larger porosity than graphene and serves as a frame or support for nanostructured pseudo capacitive materials.29 The cyclic stability of metal sulfide electrodes can be enhanced by a number of methods, such as the development of core–shell structures,30 the use of rGO31 and CNTs32–34 to create composite or hybrid electrodes.

For instance, Li et al., synthesized porous nanoflake-like CoS using bio-membrane (eggshell-membrane) support system at room temperature. The optimized CoS-20 electrode delivered a high specific capacitance of ≈365 F g−1 at 1 A g−1 together with ≈95% capacitance retention over 1300 cycles. These results highlight the importance of hierarchical porous sulfide architectures for stable pseudocapacitive storage.35 Similarly, Dar et al., synthesized pure and Fe-doped SnS nanoparticles via a solvothermal route and showed that Fe incorporation (up to 6%) significantly enhanced the specific capacitance from about 419 to 1242 F g−1. The optimized device delivered an energy density of ≈34 W h kg−1 at ≈3214 W kg−1 with ≈94% capacitance retention after 5000 cycles.36 Recently, Raza et al., designed Al2O3–MoS2 nanocomposites via a one-pot hydrothermal route and showed that an optimal 10 wt% Al2O3 loading (AM-10) achieved a high discharge specific capacitance of ≈509 F g−1, retained ≈78% capacitance over 4000 cycles. This composite exhibited a diffusion-controlled charge storage mechanism, underscoring the importance of engineered ion-transport pathways in sulfide-based electrodes.37 Kundu et al., synthesized MnS via a hydrothermal route and prepared MnS/CNT composites by ex situ mixing, where CNTs formed a conductive nanosheet network over irregular MnS spheres.38 Furthermore, Li et al., fabricated a γ-MnS/rGO composite via a one-pot solvothermal route, where MnS nanoparticles were uniformly anchored on conductive graphene sheets. The optimized electrode delivered a high specific capacitance of ≈802 F g−1 with ≈100% capacitance retention after 2000 cycles. These results indicate that graphene frameworks can effectively buffer MnS volume changes and enhance its conductivity for durable SCs operation.39

Collectively, these studies indicate that tailoring composition and nanostructure in sulfide systems is an effective route to boost capacitance, energy density, and cycling stability. Building on this insight, MnS-based composite materials with integration of electrically conductive carbon structures like CNTs, graphene, and porous carbon have been found as potential candidate for application of application, however, additional efforts are still needed to enhance the cycling stability, specific capacity, and overall electrochemical performance.

However, most MnS/carbon studies either focus on a single composite configuration or simply report capacitance and cycling data, without systematically comparing different MnS/carbon architectures and quantifying key ion-transport properties such as diffusion coefficient, ionic conductivity, transference number, and rate constant. In this work, MnS, MnS/rGO, and MnS/CNT nanocomposites are prepared via a hydrothermal route, and we explored their electrochemical behavior in depth. By extracting effective ionic conductivity, relaxation time, charge-transfer resistance, exchange current density, and kinetic rate constants from electrochemical measurements, alongside specific capacity and rate performance, this study aims to elucidate how rGO and CNT differently regulate ion and electron transport in MnS-based electrodes and to identify an optimized MnS/carbon architecture for high-performance SCs.

Experimental work

In this study manganese nitrate (Mn(NO3)2) thiourea (NH2CSNH2), ethanol (C2H5OH), acetone (CH3COCH3), and deionized (DI) water were employed as precursors and solvents, while rGO and CNT served as conductive carbon additives. For the synthesis of MnS, 1.61 g of Mn(NO3)2 and 1.37 g of thiourea were dissolved in 35 mL of DI water with molar ratio of 2 : 1. The solution was magnetically stirred for 15 min to ensure complete dissolution and homogenization and then transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave. The hydrothermal reaction was carried out at 180 °C for 24 h. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the resulting MnS precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 3500 rpm, washed repeatedly (five cycles) with DI water and ethanol, and dried at 80 °C for 6 h. The dried powder was then calcined at 550 °C for 4 h in a furnace to improve crystallinity and stability. Similarly, MnS/rGO and MnS/CNT composites were prepared following the same hydrothermal procedure, with the addition of 10 mL of rGO dispersion (containing 0.1 g of rGO powder) and 10 mL of CNT dispersion (0.1 g of CNTs powder), respectively into the precursor solution before sealing the autoclave. The mixtures were stirred for 15 min to ensure uniform dispersion of the carbon phase and then subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 24 h. The obtained MnS/rGO and MnS/CNT products were recovered, washed, and dried using the same steps as for MnS, followed by calcination at 550 °C for 3 h. A schematic illustration of the synthesis procedure is presented in Fig. 1(a–c). After the synthesis, the working electrodes were prepared using nickel foam as substrate.

Synthesis of (a) MnS, (b) MnS/rGO, and (c) MnS/CNT nanocomposites.

For the preparation of the working electrode (WE), nickel foam (NF) of approximately 1.5 × 1.5 cm2 sequentially cleaned by ultrasonication in ethanol, 5% HCl solution, and deionized water to remove surface impurities. The cleaned NF was then dried in an oven at 60 °C for 60 min. The electrode slurry was prepared by dispersing 24 mg of synthesized active material, 3 mg of carbon black, and 3 mg of PVDF in 150 µL of NMP solvent (N,N-dimethylformamide). The mixture was stirred for 1 h to obtain homogeneous slurry. A controlled amount of this slurry was subsequently drop-cast onto the pretreated NF substrate and dried at 60 °C for 8 h to ensure complete solvent evaporation and proper adhesion of the active layer. For the electrochemical analysis, active mass loading for all the materials (MnS, MnS/rGO, and MnS/CNT) were approximately ∼4.0 mg per electrode. The areal mass loading has been calculated to be approximately ∼1.78 mg cm−2, considering the area to be 2.25 cm2 (1.5 × 1.5 cm2). All the electrodes were synthesized by maintaining the same fabrication protocol to ensure uniformity and reproducibility. The electrochemical characterization has been done on the basis of the mass, and specific capacity of all materials has been calculated in terms of C g−1.

Characterization

The crystal structure of MnS, MnS/rGO, and MnS/CNT was examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using an Empyrean diffractometer (PANalytical) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) over a 2θ range of 10–80°. Surface chemistry and oxidation states were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-Alpha), while the elemental composition and spatial distribution were evaluated by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) coupled with elemental mapping. Morphology was investigated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a VEGA3 (TESCAN) microscope. While Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was used to identify Mn–S vibrations and carbon-related functional groups, and N2 adsorption–desorption measurements (BET analysis) were employed to determine specific surface area and pore-structure features of the composites. Electrochemical measurements were carried out in a three-electrode configuration using a platinum foil as the counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode in 3.0 M KOH aqueous electrolyte. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was performed in the potential window 0.0–0.4 V at scan rates from 5 to 100 mV s−1, and galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) tests were conducted at current densities between 1 to 20 A g−1. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was recorded over a wide frequency range to extract solution resistance, charge-transfer resistance, diffusion-related features, ionic resistance, and the characteristic relaxation time; these data were further used to estimate effective ionic conductivity and other ion-transport parameters of the electrodes.

Results and discussion

Structural analysis

The phase composition and crystallinity of the synthesized samples were examined by XRD in the 2θ range of 10–80°, as shown in Fig. 2(a). The pattern of pristine manganese sulfide displayed a series of well-defined diffraction peaks at 2θ ≈ 25.5°, 29.0°, 32.8°, and 36.1° which corresponded to the (111), (200), (210), (211) planes of cubic MnS2 (JCPDS 00-025-0549), confirming the successful formation of crystalline manganese sulfide.40 In addition to these reflections, weaker impurity peaks appear at ≈18.0°, 31.6°, and 60.2°, matching the (101), (200), and (215) planes of Mn3O4 (JCPDS 00-24-0734).41 These coexistence of MnS and Mn3O4 has been widely reported for MnS prepared under hydrothermal/solvothermal conditions, as MnS is thermodynamically prone to surface oxidation in the presence of dissolved oxygen and during post-annealing, leading to the formation of a thin Mn3O4 shell or intergrown oxide domains on the sulfide core. This mixed sulfide-oxide framework is further corroborated by the oxygen-rich EDS/XPS signatures discussed later. The carbon-related reflections near 2θ ≈ 26° and 43–44° can be assigned to the (002) and (100)/(101) planes of graphitic carbon (rGO/CNT), consistent with typical XRD patterns of reduced graphene oxide and multiwalled CNTs (JCPDS 75-1621 and JCPDS 41-1487), thereby confirming the presence of conductive carbon frameworks in both composites. Morphological analysis

The HR-SEM images of MnS, MnS/rGO, and MnS/CNT are presented in Fig. 3(a–c). Pristine MnS (Fig. 3(a)) shows densely agglomerated, irregular particles forming bulky clusters with very limited open porosity, indicating poor particle dispersion and a relatively low accessible surface area for electrolyte penetration. Such severe aggregation is expected to hinder ion transport and active-site utilization during electrochemical charge storage. In contrast, the MnS/rGO composite (Fig. 3(b)) exhibits a plate-like, sheet-based morphology, where MnS nanoparticles are distributed over wrinkled graphene layers, producing a flaky, layered structure with more open interparticle spaces. The rGO sheets act as a conductive scaffold that separates MnS clusters, suppresses agglomeration, and creates interconnected pathways for ion diffusion and electron transport, which is beneficial for enhanced electrochemical performance. Similarly, for MnS/CNT (Fig. 3(c)), MnS particles are wrapped and bridged by intertwined CNT networks, giving rise to a relatively well-separated, porous architecture with interconnected pores and channels. CNTs form conductive bridges between MnS particles, improving dispersion and providing continuous electron pathways along with additional mesoporous channels for electrolyte access. Overall, the incorporation of rGO and CNT transforms the compact MnS aggregates into more open, conductive hybrid frameworks.

Elemental analysis

The elemental composition of MnS and its carbon-based composites was examined by EDS, and the corresponding spectra and elemental maps are shown in Fig. 4(a–l). For pristine MnS (Fig. 4(a)), the EDS spectrum reveals Mn and S contents of 23.6 and 12.8 wt%, respectively, together with a dominant oxygen signal, indicating the presence of oxygen-rich species in addition to the nominal MnS phase. The high O content is consistent with partial oxidation of MnS, in line with the Mn3O4 impurity detected by XRD. In the MnS/rGO and MnS/CNT composites (Fig. 4(a)), the EDS analysis shows Mn, S, C, and O contents clearly. The significant C signal in both composites confirms that MnS is embedded by conductive carbon matrices (rGO sheets or CNT networks), in agreement with the SEM observations. The elemental mapping images (Fig. 4(b–l)) further demonstrate that Mn, S, C, and O are homogeneously distributed throughout the probed regions, indicating uniform dispersion of MnS within the carbon frameworks rather than phase segregation. The pronounced oxygen signal in EDS can be attributed to surface oxidation of MnS upon exposure to air and moisture. MnS is chemically sensitive and readily forms a thin layer of manganese oxide or hydroxide on its surface, which contributes to the strong O response in surface-sensitive techniques. Because XPS typically probes only the top ∼5–10 nm of the surface, the detected O is mainly associated with Mn–O/Mn–OH species rather than bulk MnS, and similar surface oxidation phenomena have been widely reported for transition-metal sulfides in the literature.

MnS/rGO exhibits the highest relative intensity of the graphitic C component, consistent with the more extensive rGO coverage observed in other characterizations. The O 1s spectrum (Fig. 5(a)) is broad and can be deconvoluted into multiple components: a low-binding-energy peak at ∼530.2–531.0 eV assigned to lattice or metal–oxygen species (Mn–O/Mn–O–S), a medium-energy contribution at ∼532.0–533.0 eV corresponding to C–O/C–OH groups, and a high-binding-energy shoulder at ∼534–535 eV attributed to adsorbed water and/or surface contaminants. MnS/rGO shows a greater proportion of oxygenated carbon species as compared to MnS/CNT, which is the partial reduction of GO with unoccupied oxygen functional groups that can increase the wettability and anchor MnS nanoparticles. Mn 2p spectra (Fig. 5(d–f)) show well-defined spin–orbit doublets with Mn 2p3/2 located at approximately 640.7–641.8 eV and Mn 2p1/2 at 652.2–653.5 eV, which are characteristic of Mn2+ in MnS. A weak satellite feature around 645–647 eV indicates minor surface oxidation associated with trace amounts of higher-valence Mn species. Notably, the Mn 2p3/2 peak of MnS/rGO shifts slightly toward lower binding energy compared with pristine MnS, suggesting electron transfer from MnS to the conductive rGO framework and an improved electronic coupling at the MnS/rGO interface. In contrast, MnS/CNT exhibits a small positive shift, implying a different electronic environment at the MnS/CNT interface. The S 2p spectra (Fig. 5(g–i)) show main doublets with S 2p3/2 at 161.6–162.5 eV and S 2p1/2 at 162.7–163.8 eV, confirming S2− in the MnS lattice.39 Additionally, MnS and MnS/CNT exhibit a minor high binding energy component near 168–170 eV, attributable to oxidized sulfur species (sulfate/sulfate-like surface species), whereas MnS/rGO shows the weakest oxidized-S contribution, indicating that rGO more effectively protects MnS from surface oxidation.43–45

FTIR analysis

Fig. 6 shows the FTIR spectra of MnS, MnS/rGO, and MnS/CNT, which exhibit the characteristic vibrations of metal–sulfide bonds together with carbon-related functional groups from rGO and CNT frameworks. All three samples displayed a strong absorption band in the 520–610 cm−1 region, assigned to Mn–S stretching vibrations, confirming the presence of MnS in each material.46 Additional weak bands between 400 and 500 cm−1 are usually attributed to sulfide-ion vibrational modes, further evidencing a continuous MnS bonding network within the composites. For MnS/rGO and MnS/CNT, an extra weak band appears around 860 cm−1 that is absent in pure MnS, which is characteristic of C–O–C/skeletal vibrations and thus indicates the presence of graphitic carbon domains from rGO sheets and CNTs in the composite matrix.39 The band near 1051 cm−1 is ascribed to C–O stretching, while the feature around 1424 cm−1 corresponds to O–H deformation vibrations arising from residual oxygen-containing groups on rGO/CNT and adsorbed surface moisture.47 Overall, the coexistence of Mn–S vibrations and carbon-related functional bands confirm the successful formation of MnS and its effective integration with rGO and CNT, while the additional oxygen-containing groups and defect-related functionalities are expected to enhance interfacial contact, surface activity, and ultimately the electrochemical performance of the composites.

BET analysis

Fig. 7 presents the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherm of the MnS/rGO nanocomposite, which shows a type-IV profile with a very narrow H3-like hysteresis loop at intermediate relative pressures, indicating the presence of mesopores associated with the stacked rGO/MnS architecture. The gradual increase in adsorbed N2 with P/P0 up to ∼0.6, followed by a steeper uptake at higher relative pressures (P/P0 ≈ 0.6–0.8), confirms the dominance of mesopores together with some larger interparticle/macropore voids that can facilitate electrolyte penetration and ion transport. The close overlap of the adsorption and desorption branches over most of the pressure range suggests a mechanically stable pore structure with minimal pore collapse or blockage. BET analysis of the linear region yields a specific surface area of 222.24 m2 g−1 for MnS/rGO, significantly higher than that of pristine MnS and MnS/CNT, providing a large electrochemically active interface combined with well-connected meso/macropores that support fast ion diffusion and are consistent with the superior electrochemical performance of MnS/rGO.

Electrochemical analysis

The electrochemical behavior of MnS, MnS/rGO, and MnS/CNT was evaluated by CV, GCD, and EIS in a three-electrode configuration using 3 M KOH electrolyte. Fig. 8(a–c) shows the CV curves of MnS, MnS/rGO, and MnS/CNT recorded at scan rates from 5 to 100 mV s−1 within a potential window of 0.0–0.4 V (vs. Ag/AgCl). All electrodes displayed clear anodic and cathodic redox peaks, confirming that the charge storage is governed by reversible faradaic reactions rather than purely ideal EDLC behavior. With increasing scan rate, the overall shapes of the CV curves are largely retained, and the enclosed areas expand, indicating good electrochemical reversibility; only a slight shift of the redox peaks toward higher potentials is observed, which is be ascribed to polarization effects at higher sweep rates.48,49 In the MnS/rGO and MnS/CNT electrodes, the CV profiles exhibit minor quasi-rectangular features superimposed on the redox peaks, reflecting the contribution of the conductive carbon network (rGO sheets or CNTs) to electric double-layer capacitance in addition to the pseudo-capacitance of Mn-sulfide/oxide species. To gain deeper insight into the charge-storage mechanism, the scan-rate dependence of the anodic peak currents was analyzed using the power-law relation i = avb. A log–log plot of peak current versus scan rate (Fig. 9(a–c)) yields b-values of 0.76, 0.79 and 0.41 for MnS, MnS/rGO and MnS/CNT, respectively. Values of b close to 0.5 indicate diffusion-controlled intercalation processes, whereas b ≈ 1 reflects surface-controlled (capacitive) behavior; therefore, MnS and especially MnS/rGO exhibit mixed but predominantly surface-controlled kinetics. In contrast the lower b-value of MnS/CNT suggests charge storage process becomes more diffusion controlled and battery type in nature. Although CNT incorporation improves electrical conductivity and enhances overall capacitance, it may also facilitate deeper ion diffusion intercalation within electrode leading to diffusion dominated kinetics and consequently lower b-value.24 The slightly higher value for MnS/rGO confirms that integrating rGO promotes faster surface redox and capacitive contributions compared with pristine MnS. The relative contributions of capacitive and diffusion-controlled processes for MnS/rGO were further quantified using Dunn's method51 based on i(V) = k1v + k2v1/2. Rearranging this expression to i(V)/v1/2 = k1v1/2 + k2 and plotting ip/v1/2versus v1/2 at the anodic peak potential (Fig. 9(d)) gives an excellent linear fit, from which k1 and k2 are extracted as 2.1 × 10−4 and 8.85 × 10−4 A (mV−1/2 s1/2), respectively. The term k1v corresponds to the capacitive (surface-controlled) current, whereas k2v1/2 represents the diffusion-limited contribution; both coefficients are positive and their relative magnitudes imply that diffusion dominates at low scan rates, but the capacitive term increasingly contributes as v increases. On this basis, the capacitive and diffusion-controlled fractions at each scan rate were evaluated as icap = k1v and idiff = k2v1/2, and their percentage contributions were plotted versus v (Fig. 9(e)). At 5 mV s−1, only 36% of the current arises from capacitive processes, while 64% is diffusion-controlled; however, the capacitive share steadily increases to 72% at 100 mV s−1, with the diffusion contribution decreasing correspondingly. This trend is characteristic of pseudocapacitive electrodes, in which slow scans allow ions to penetrate deeper into the active regions (diffusion-controlled storage), whereas fast scans emphasize near-surface redox and double-layer processes. The resolved deconvolution at 100 mV s−1 (Fig. 9(f)) further indicates that the capacitive current dominates across most of the potential window, accounting for ∼71.6% of the total response, whereas the diffusion component contributes ∼23.8%. Together with the high b-value (0.79) and quasi-rectangular CV features, these results demonstrate that MnS/rGO behaves as a hybrid pseudocapacitive electrode, in which the conductive rGO network accelerates electron transport and provides abundant surface sites for fast redox reactions, thereby suppressing diffusion limitations compared with pristine MnS and MnS/CNT.

EIS was employed to gain further insight into the charge-transport kinetics and interfacial resistances of MnS, MnS/rGO, and MnS/CNT electrodes, and the corresponding Nyquist and Bode plots are shown in Fig. 11(a–d). Generally, the Nyquist plots consist of a depressed semicircle in the high to medium frequency region followed by an inclined line at low frequencies, which reflect the combined contributions of charge-transfer resistance (Rct) at the electrode/electrolyte interface and diffusion of ions within the porous electrode (Warburg impedance).54 Our experimental data are well fitted by the equivalent circuit models shown in the insets of Fig. 11 and the close overlap between the experimental points and fitted curves indicates reliable fitting. For pristine MnS (Fig. 11(a)), the semicircle diameter is comparatively large, implying a higher Rct associated with sluggish ion diffusion and poor electronic conductivity. In contrast, the MnS/rGO and MnS/CNT composites (Fig. 11(b and c)) exhibit smaller semicircles, demonstrating that the incorporation of conductive carbon significantly reduces Rct by providing more efficient electron pathways and facilitating ion transport through the porous carbon network. Among the three samples, MnS/rGO shows the smallest semicircle, consistent with the highest electrical conductivity and fastest interfacial charge transfer due to the highly conductive rGO scaffold. The corresponding Bode plots in Fig. 11(d) show that MnS/rGO attains the highest maximum phase angle and a broader frequency range with capacitive response, indicating more ideal capacitive behavior and better electrochemical reversibility than MnS and MnS/CNT. Overall, the EIS results confirm that addition of rGO and CNT markedly improves the charge-transfer kinetics and ion-diffusion characteristics of MnS-based electrodes, with MnS/rGO exhibiting the lowest total resistance and the most favorable capacitive response; the extracted solution and charge-transfer resistance values are summarized in Table 4.

Transport properties

The ionic conductivity reflects how efficiently ions can migrate through the electrode/electrolyte interface during the redox process and is therefore a key parameter governing the overall electrochemical performance of SCs electrodes. The ionic conductivity (σ) of MnS, MnS/rGO, and MnS/CNT was calculated using eqn (3).55,563where L is the electrode thickness, Ri is the ionic resistance obtained from EIS fitting, and A is the effective area of the electrode.29 Based on this relation, the σ values for all three electrodes were estimated and are summarized in Table 4. Among them, MnS/rGO exhibits the highest ionic conductivity of 16.3 S m−1, indicating more efficient ion transport pathways within the rGO-supported MnS framework compared with pristine MnS and MnS/CNT. This enhanced ionic conductivity is consistent with the improved rate capability and higher specific capacity of MnS/rGO observed in the electrochemical measurements.

The charge–discharge kinetics of the electrodes were further evaluated using the relaxation time (τ), which characterizes how quickly the electrode returns to equilibrium after a perturbation and was obtained from the characteristic frequency f0 in the Bode plot as . The MnS electrode exhibits the longest relaxation time of 0.619 s, indicating relatively slow ionic dynamics and sluggish charge-transfer behavior. In contrast, MnS/rGO exhibits a much smaller τ of 0.077 s, revealing markedly faster ion and electron transport due to the highly conductive rGO network. MnS/CNT displays an intermediate relaxation time of 0.102 s, suggesting improved kinetics compared with pristine MnS but still inferior to MnS/rGO. These results demonstrate that carbon-based additives can effectively tune the electrochemical kinetics of MnS electrodes, with MnS/rGO providing the shortest charge-response time among the investigated samples. Rate constant and exchange current density analyses further corroborate that MnS/rGO exhibits the most favorable charge-transfer kinetics among the investigated electrodes. Building on the EIS-derived Rct, the exchange current density (J0) and kinetic rate constant (k) were determined using the relations J0 = RT/(nFRct) and k = RT/(F2RctC), consistent with the methodology adopted for Ni–ZnSe/rGO supercapacitor electrodes in the reference study.30 The MnS/rGO electrode yields the highest J0 of 6.8 × 10−3 A g−1 and the largest rate constant of 2.4 × 10−8 cm s−1, which are roughly twofold higher than those of pristine MnS, directly reflecting the substantial reduction in interfacial activation barriers and the accelerated charge-transfer process at the MnS/rGO–electrolyte interface. In comparison, MnS/CNT exhibits intermediate J0 and k values that are higher than those of MnS but lower than MnS/rGO, indicating that the CNT framework partially improves electronic pathways and interfacial kinetics, yet does not match the 2D rGO network in providing continuous, high-conductivity channels and abundant accessible redox sites. The combined trends in Rct, J0, and k are fully consistent with the ionic conductivity and relaxation-time results discussed above: MnS/rGO simultaneously offers the highest σ (16.3 S m−1), the shortest relaxation time (0.077 s), and the largest kinetic parameters, thereby establishing a coherent picture in which the rGO scaffold not only enhances ion diffusion but also promotes intrinsically faster electron-transfer and surface redox reactions. Overall, these findings confirm that integrating MnS with rGO is the most effective strategy among the three architectures for boosting both transport properties and interfacial reaction kinetics.

Conclusion

In this work MnS, MnS, MnS/rGO, and MnS/CNT nanocomposites were synthesized via a hydrothermal approach and systematically evaluated to establish clear structure–property–performance relationships for supercapacitor electrodes. XRD confirmed crystalline MnS with a cubic MnS2 phase and minor Mn3O4 arising from surface oxidation, forming mixed sulfide-oxide domains that can enrich faradaic charge storage through multiple redox states. Carbon-related reflections and elemental mapping verified the successful incorporation of rGO sheets and CNT networks into the MnS matrix, evidencing hybrid sulfide–carbon architectures with homogeneous elemental distribution and strong interfacial coupling. Morphological and XPS analyses showed that pristine MnS suffers from severe agglomeration and limited electrolyte accessibility, whereas MnS/rGO develops a well-dispersed, sheet-like architecture with MnS nanoparticles uniformly anchored on wrinkled graphene layers, yielding an open porous framework and suppressed sulfur oxidation with a favorable Mn 2p shift. MnS/CNT also improves dispersion through CNT bridging but offers less interconnected porosity and surface accessibility than MnS/rGO. FTIR confirmed Mn–S bonding together with carbon-related functional groups, while BET analysis revealed that MnS/rGO possesses the highest specific surface area (222 m2 g−1) and dominant mesoporosity, providing shortened ion-diffusion pathways and a large electrochemically active interface. Electrochemical tests demonstrated pronounced pseudocapacitive behavior for all electrodes, with MnS/rGO delivering the highest specific capacity of 404.2 C g−1 at 1.1 A g−1, superior rate capability, and longer discharge times than MnS and MnS/CNT. Dunn's analysis indicated a hybrid charge-storage mechanism in MnS/rGO, where diffusion-controlled processes dominate at low scan rates but surface-controlled capacitive contributions exceed 70% at 100 mV s−1, highlighting the role of the rGO scaffold in enabling rapid surface redox while mitigating diffusion limitations. Impedance analysis further showed that MnS/rGO exhibits the lowest charge-transfer resistance, highest ionic conductivity (16.3 S m−1), shortest relaxation time (0.077 s), and significantly enhanced exchange current density and kinetic rate constant, confirming accelerated ion transport and interfacial reaction rates compared with MnS and MnS/CNT. Overall, this comparative study demonstrates that synergistic integration of MnS with reduced graphene oxide is the most effective strategy among the examined carbon architectures for simultaneously enhancing surface area, electronic conductivity, ion-transport efficiency, and reaction kinetics. These results establish MnS/rGO as a promising high-performance supercapacitor electrode and provide a useful framework for rationally designing next-generation pseudocapacitive materials by correlating structural features with transport parameters and kinetic constants.
