Exploring patients’ experiences and perceived barriers to the utilization of rural comprehensive health centers among individuals living with chronic non-communicable diseases: A qualitative inquiry
Authors: Abdullah Azizi, Zahra Khiyali, Ali Taghinezhad, Mostafa Bijani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRural Comprehensive Health Centers (RCHCs) constitute a cornerstone of primary healthcare delivery and possess substantial capacity to provide essential services that enhance the health and quality of life of individuals living with chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs). The present study aimed to explore the experiences and perceived of patients with chronic NCDs and identify the perceived barriers influencing their utilization of Rural Comprehensive Health Centers in Fars Province, southern Iran.Design and methodsA qualitative study grounded in conventional content analysis was conducted. Data were collected through 25 semi-structured, in-depth interviews with patients diagnosed with chronic conditions. The data were analyzed using the methodological framework proposed by Graneheim and Lundman.ResultsAnalysis of the interview data yielded three overarching themes and seven subthemes. The principal themes included Structural and service accessibility barriers (distance, service hours, workforce shortages, and lack of equipment), Trust-related barriers (belief in traditional remedies and self-treatment, and Negative attitude and experiences to the utilization of rural comprehensive health centers (RCHCs)), and Health literacy and illness perception barriers (insufficient knowledge of self-care and disease management, and low perceived severity of illness).ConclusionThe findings indicate that access to and utilization of rural comprehensive health services among individuals living with chronic non-communicable diseases are influenced by a combination of organizational, sociocultural, and individual factors. These insights provide valuable guidance for healthcare policymakers and senior administrators seeking to develop effective strategies and targeted interventions to address barriers that hinder the optimal utilization of RCHCs.

Introduction

Chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are persistent, largely irreversible health conditions that require continuous care, monitoring, and long-term management throughout an individual’s life. 1 Over recent decades, these conditions have emerged as one of the most significant challenges facing public health systems worldwide, 2 placing substantial demands on healthcare resources and service delivery. 3 Current estimates suggest that NCDs account for approximately 71% of all deaths globally. 2 Moreover, nearly 17 million people die prematurely each year before reaching the age of 70 as a result of these diseases, with 86% of such premature deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries. 4 In Iran, a middle-income country, NCDs are responsible for 83.5% of all deaths and contribute to 78.1% of the total disease burden, according to Partovi et al. (2022). 5 Population ageing, rapid urbanization, and lifestyle changes associated with these demographic transitions have further accelerated the prevalence of chronic diseases. 6

In response to the growing burden of NCDs, the international community has prioritized their prevention and control within the broader sustainable development agenda. Through the roadmap endorsed at the Third High-level Meeting on NCDs (2018–2030), countries collectively committed to achieving a 25% reduction in premature mortality attributable to NCDs by 2025, supported by nine voluntary global targets and a range of evidence-based interventions.5–8 In alignment with these commitments, Iran has implemented several initiatives aimed at strengthening national efforts to prevent and control NCDs. These initiatives include the development of national policy frameworks for NCD management, the establishment of the National Committee for the Prevention and Control of NCDs, the formulation of the National Action Plan for NCDs (2015–2020), and the integration of the World Health Organization’s Package of Essential Noncommunicable Disease Interventions (PEN), nationally known as IraPEN, into the primary healthcare (PHC) system. 5

Since 1981, healthcare services within Iran’s PHC network have been organized according to a three-tier service delivery model. At the first level, urban and rural comprehensive health centers operate alongside urban health posts and rural health houses. The second and third levels comprise general hospitals and specialized or subspecialized hospitals, respectively. 9 Notably, approximately 80% of PHC services delivered at the first level are intended to be readily accessible to individuals and households. 10 Nevertheless, despite substantial governmental investment in the expansion and development of Rural Comprehensive Health Centers (RCHCs), patient utilization has not increased commensurately with infrastructural growth. In certain regions, attendance rates have even declined, whereas direct referrals to hospitals have increased considerably.11,12 Increasingly, patients bypass rural health houses and seek care directly from higher-level healthcare facilities. Such patterns place unnecessary pressure on secondary and tertiary healthcare services, disrupt continuity of care, and contribute to dissatisfaction among both healthcare providers and service users. 13

The extent to which individuals living with chronic diseases utilize primary healthcare services varies considerably across countries and healthcare systems. In many Nordic and European countries, strong primary care infrastructures, comprehensive health insurance coverage, continuity of care, and effective referral systems have collectively encouraged the use of primary healthcare centers for chronic disease management while reducing unnecessary hospital admissions and healthcare utilization.14,15 Existing evidence indicates that effective management of NCDs in rural settings is frequently constrained by limited patient awareness, restricted access to healthcare services due to long travel distances, shortages of healthcare personnel, attitudinal barriers, and various sociocultural influences. Collectively, these challenges may discourage rural residents from initiating and sustaining engagement with primary healthcare services. Similar circumstances have been reported across many low- and middle-income countries, where fragmented healthcare systems, geographical barriers, workforce shortages, and inadequate health literacy continue to undermine the utilization of preventive, monitoring, and follow-up services.16–18

Likewise, studies conducted in rural areas of the United States have shown that barriers such as inadequate transportation, insufficient health insurance coverage, socioeconomic disadvantage, and distrust of healthcare institutions can impede timely healthcare-seeking behavior and reduce the utilization of primary healthcare services among individuals living with chronic conditions. 19 These findings underscore the substantial influence of social, cultural, and structural contexts on healthcare utilization patterns, particularly in rural settings. Previous research has also demonstrated that patients frequently bypass primary healthcare facilities because of limited awareness of available services, inadequate knowledge of chronic disease management, ineffective communication with healthcare professionals, or concerns regarding the quality of care provided.20,21 Furthermore, evidence from health services research suggests that patients’ healthcare-seeking trajectories are strongly influenced by their perceptions of service accessibility, trust in healthcare providers, and structural constraints embedded within primary healthcare systems. 22 Within rural communities, additional factors, including preferences for specialist physicians, culturally embedded beliefs, previous negative healthcare experiences, and reduced confidence in local healthcare providers, may further discourage attendance at rural health centers. 20 Moreover, inadequate health literacy, coupled with fragmented continuity of care, may hinder long-term engagement with preventive services and sustained disease management, particularly among older adults living with chronic illnesses. 21

Research gap and significance of the study

Despite the importance of this issue, qualitative evidence examining barriers to the utilization of rural comprehensive health centers remains limited, particularly within the sociocultural context of rural Iranian communities.23,24 Most existing studies have been conducted in high-income countries whose healthcare systems differ substantially from those operating in rural Iran in terms of infrastructure, financing mechanisms, referral pathways, and patient expectations regarding healthcare delivery. 25 Consequently, findings derived from these settings may not adequately reflect the realities shaping healthcare utilization in rural Iran, where cultural norms, resource constraints, geographical remoteness, and inequities in healthcare access are likely to exert a more pronounced influence on healthcare-seeking behavior. Furthermore, a review of the literature reveals a notable lack of qualitative investigations specifically addressing barriers to the utilization of RCHCs within the distinctive sociocultural environments of rural populations. 25 Therefore, the present study sought to explore the factors underlying the non-utilization of rural comprehensive health centers among patients living with chronic non-communicable diseases in southern Iran. The significance of this inquiry lies in its potential to generate patient-centered insights into the reasons for underutilization by drawing directly on the perspectives and lived experiences of service users. Such evidence may inform health policy, enhance the quality and equity of healthcare provision, and support the development of interventions tailored to the unique needs and circumstances of rural communities.

Methods

This qualitative study was conducted between April and September 2025 using a conventional content analysis approach. Qualitative content analysis is particularly well suited to generating credible and dependable insights from textual data, as it facilitates the development of new conceptual understandings, the articulation of factual knowledge, and the formulation of practical recommendations for decision-making and policy development. 26 Through the systematic examination of textual narratives, this approach enables researchers to interpret underlying meanings and identify overarching themes and recurring patterns embedded within the data. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist 27 (Supplementary File 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligibility for participation was contingent upon participants’ willingness to take part in the study and their ability to communicate effectively. The sole exclusion criterion was withdrawal from the study at any stage, irrespective of the reason.

Sample and participants

A purposive sampling strategy supplemented by snowball recruitment was used to enroll 25 individuals diagnosed with chronic non-communicable diseases residing in Fars Province, southern Iran. Initially, a healthcare professional was identified by the research team to facilitate access to an information-rich participant for the first interview. Following completion of the interview, that participant was invited to recommend another individual with substantial experience and knowledge relevant to the phenomenon under investigation. Subsequent participants were recruited through referrals from previously interviewed individuals, thereby ensuring the inclusion of participants capable of providing rich and meaningful insights into the study topic. Data were collected through individual semi-structured interviews. Each interview commenced with a broad, open-ended question designed to encourage participants to provide detailed and authentic accounts of their lived experiences. As data collection progressed, more focused questions were developed iteratively in response to emerging findings and in alignment with the study objectives.

Before recruitment, the interviewer had no personal, clinical, or professional relationship with any prospective participant. Prior to obtaining informed consent, participants were informed of the interviewer’s role as a nursing faculty member and qualitative researcher, the objectives of the study, and the research team’s interest in understanding factors influencing the utilization of rural comprehensive health centers. Rapport and trust were established through respectful interaction, active listening, and assurances that participants’ responses would remain confidential and would not affect the healthcare services they received.

Data saturation was deemed to have been achieved when no new information emerged from the interviews and no additional categories or subcategories could be identified. 28 Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently, with interview transcripts reviewed and coded immediately following each interview. Saturation was operationally defined as the point at which successive interviews yielded repetitive codes, no new concepts, categories, or subcategories emerged, and the existing analytical framework adequately captured participants’ experiences. Following completion of the twenty-third interview, no new codes or categories were identified, and the research team concluded that thematic saturation had been reached. To verify the comprehensiveness of the findings, two additional interviews were conducted; however, these generated no new codes, categories, or insights.

All interviews were conducted by the corresponding author, an associate professor of nursing with extensive experience in qualitative research. With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded. Throughout the data collection process, the interviewer consistently reiterated the purpose of the study, maintained a neutral stance, fostered an atmosphere of trust and openness, and avoided leading questions or other forms of influence that might shape participants’ responses. Key interview questions included: “In your opinion, what challenges or factors contribute to patients not visiting the rural comprehensive health center?”, “Are you satisfied with the services provided by your local rural comprehensive health center?”, and “What suggestions do you have for overcoming barriers and improving service delivery in rural health centers?” To encourage deeper reflection and elicit richer responses, probing questions such as “Could you elaborate on that?”, “Can you provide an example?”, and “How did you feel at that moment?” were used when appropriate.

Before commencement of the main data collection phase, the interview guide was piloted with two individuals who met the eligibility criteria but were not included in the final sample. These pilot interviews enabled the research team to assess the clarity, comprehensibility, and relevance of the interview questions in relation to the study objectives. Based on participant feedback and researchers’ reflections, minor revisions were made to improve the wording, sequencing, and overall flow of the interview guide.

Potential participants were initially identified through purposive sampling in collaboration with healthcare personnel working in rural comprehensive health centers and health houses. Healthcare providers assisted in identifying individuals who were likely to meet the study eligibility criteria. The researcher subsequently contacted eligible individuals, explained the purpose of the study, and confirmed eligibility according to the predefined inclusion criteria. Participation was entirely voluntary. None of the participants who consented to participate withdrew from the study, and all completed the interview process.

Interviews were conducted individually either at a rural comprehensive health center or at a health house, with the location and timing determined in consultation with each participant. Following the collection of basic demographic information, interviews proceeded using the predefined core questions and were paused or terminated whenever participants experienced fatigue or discomfort. Interviews lasted between 45 and 65 minutes. At the conclusion of each interview, participants were thanked for their contribution and asked whether they would be willing to be contacted again should clarification or additional information be required. Following informed consent, participants’ telephone numbers were recorded to facilitate potential follow-up interviews and member-checking activities. All interviews were transcribed verbatim immediately after completion and reviewed repeatedly to ensure transcription accuracy and facilitate immersion in the data. Audio recordings were securely stored in a password-protected university cloud-based data management system.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using the conventional content analysis approach described by Graneheim and Lundman (2004), which comprises five sequential stages 1 : transcribing each interview immediately after completion 2 ; reading the entire transcript repeatedly to gain an overall understanding of its content 3 ; identifying meaning units and assigning preliminary codes 4 ; organizing conceptually related codes into broader categories based on their similarities and differences; and 5 integrating subcategories into overarching themes. 29

Consistent with this analytical framework, each interview transcript was read repeatedly in its entirety to facilitate deep immersion in the data and develop a comprehensive understanding of participants’ accounts. Segments of text, including sentences, phrases, and paragraphs containing information relevant to factors contributing to the non-utilization of rural comprehensive health centers, were identified as meaning units. These units were subsequently condensed while preserving their essential meaning and assigned initial codes that reflected their underlying content.

The resulting codes were then systematically compared and examined to identify recurring patterns, conceptual relationships, and distinguishing characteristics. Codes representing similar concepts were merged, and the coding framework underwent iterative refinement to enhance analytical precision, conceptual consistency, and interpretive coherence. Conceptually related codes were grouped into categories, which were repeatedly reviewed, compared, and validated against the original data through an ongoing cyclical process of reflection and analysis. Throughout the analytical process, constant comparison among codes, categories, and source transcripts was maintained to strengthen the credibility and trustworthiness of the findings. Ultimately, through sustained engagement with the dataset and interpretive synthesis of emerging patterns, overarching themes were developed to capture the essential dimensions of the phenomenon under investigation. 29 Data management and analysis were performed using MAXQDA software (Version 2007).

Rigor (trustworthiness of the data)

Methodological rigor and trustworthiness were established using Lincoln and Guba’s framework, which encompasses the criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. 30

Credibility was enhanced through both member checking and peer debriefing. As part of the member-checking process, interview transcripts and their corresponding coded outputs were returned to ten participants with chronic NCDs, who were invited to assess the accuracy of the coding and the interpretations derived from their accounts. Simultaneously, peer review was conducted by eight experts in qualitative research. These specialists critically evaluated the coding procedures, thematic structures, and interpretive decisions, thereby providing methodological verification and constructive analytical feedback.

Dependability was strengthened through the provision of a transparent, detailed, and systematic account of each stage of the analytical process. This documentation was supported by representative excerpts from the original audio recordings and textual data. Furthermore, two members of the research team independently reviewed the analytical outputs before meeting to discuss and resolve interpretive discrepancies, thereby promoting consistency and coherence throughout the analysis.

Confirmability was reinforced through participant verification of the identified themes and subthemes. In addition, a comprehensive audit trail documenting participant characteristics, interview conditions, and methodological decisions was maintained. To minimize the potential for interpretive bias, the researchers deliberately limited their engagement with the existing literature prior to data analysis, thereby reducing the likelihood of confirmatory influences during interpretation. Throughout data collection and analysis, bracketing was employed as a key methodological strategy to preserve interpretive neutrality. This process involved the deliberate suspension of researchers’ prior assumptions, professional perspectives, and experiential knowledge to facilitate a direct and authentic understanding of participants’ lived experiences. By approaching the data without imposing preconceived judgments or external interpretations, the researchers sought to ensure that the findings remained firmly grounded in participants’ own narratives. Throughout the research process, the coding framework was iteratively shared with the research team, thereby enabling the systematic incorporation of their feedback as part of an ongoing peer debriefing process. Following each interview, the interviewer synthesized the principal issues that had emerged and sought confirmation from participants to ensure interpretive accuracy through member checking. Furthermore, in order to strengthen the methodological rigor of the study, both the lead researcher and the research team consciously and systematically bracketed their preconceptions during the phases of data collection and analysis.

Transferability was enhanced through the provision of rich and detailed descriptions of the study context, participant characteristics, sampling procedures, and analytical processes. In addition, verbatim quotations from participants were included to illustrate key findings, thereby enhancing interpretive transparency and enabling readers to assess the applicability and relevance of the findings to other settings and populations. 30

Ethical considerations

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their enrollment in the study. The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the revised Declaration of Helsinki, which provides internationally recognized standards for research involving human participants. Participants were assured that their identities would remain anonymous and that all information provided would be treated with strict confidentiality. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Fasa University of Medical Sciences, Fasa, Iran (Ethics Code: IR.FUMS.REC.1404.084).

Distance

A considerable number of participants reported that the substantial distance between their homes and the nearest rural comprehensive health center constituted a major barrier to accessing healthcare services. Participants consistently described the difficulties associated with living in remote rural areas, noting that reaching healthcare facilities often required extensive travel. In some cases, the total time required for a round trip exceeded three hours, thereby discouraging routine healthcare utilization and delaying timely access to care.

As one participant stated: “The distance between our home and the rural health center is just too far. I don’t have any means of transportation, so I just give up and don’t go.” (Participant 5)

Another participant elaborated: “They never think about us villagers; there’s no proper road, nothing. With my condition and no way to get there, how can I go to the rural health center? Many times, I feel really sick and suspect my blood sugar is high, but because of the distance, I just ignore it.” (Participant 6)

Service hours

Several participants voiced concern over the limited operating hours of rural health centers, emphasizing the lack of access to care during emergencies: “The village health post operates part-time and not 24 hours. Many times, when my blood pressure went up, I had to travel to the city hospital because the center wasn’t open. If it had 24-hour service, I wouldn’t need to go that far.” (Participant 9)

Belief in traditional remedies and self-treatment

Several participants indicated a preference for traditional or self-directed treatment over professional medical care, often rooted in long-standing cultural beliefs: “Unfortunately, superstitions and incorrect health beliefs still exist in villages, passed down through generations—like self-medication and using traditional remedies. My grandmother often says, ‘You’ve spent all that money on diabetes medication and you’re just getting worse. Our traditional remedies are better than those doctors’ drugs.’” (Participant 17)

Negative attitude and experiences to the utilization of rural comprehensive health centers (RCHCs)

Participants consistently identified negative previous attitude and experiences of health care settings as a significant barrier to developing trust in healthcare professionals. Such experiences were perceived to undermine confidence in healthcare providers and to create a substantial obstacle to seeking and utilizing healthcare services. One participant emphasized that trust in physicians and healthcare personnel constitutes a fundamental prerequisite for effective healthcare utilization.

The participant further explained:“I myself have a painful experience with rural health centers. My uncle, who was 42 years old, had heart disease. One day he developed chest pain, and after we took him to the rural health center, unfortunately he died of cardiac arrest. This painful and distressing experience has always remained in my mind. Of course, the doctor and staff of the comprehensive rural health center tried very hard, but unfortunately my uncle died. This painful experience has led me to have a negative view of rural health services, and I no longer have any desire or inclination to go there, and my belief in and trust in such centers has decreased. I do not know whether this negative belief, which was formed in my mind because of a previous distressing experience, is right or wrong” (Participant19).

Although direct quotations for these subthemes were limited in the initial data, participants’ narratives clearly reflected the influence of inadequate health literacy and a diminished perception of the seriousness of chronic illnesses on their health-seeking behaviors. These insights underscore the pressing need for community-based health education and empowerment programs designed to enhance self-care capabilities and raise awareness about the risks associated with chronic diseases.

Lack of knowledge in self-care and disease management

Participants noted that a deficiency in knowledge about managing chronic conditions—particularly hypertension and diabetes—often leads to neglect of health and avoidance of professional care. One participant explained: “In my opinion, when a person knows nothing about their illness or its complications, they naturally become indifferent and avoid going to health centers. Personally, because I thoroughly understand diabetes and fear its serious complications, I pay close attention to my condition. I follow both my medication and dietary regimens carefully to keep the disease under control. However, many of my relatives and friends who lack awareness of their condition are indifferent and do not visit health or treatment centers.” (Participant 23)

I have acquired some knowledge about my condition, and because I understand the disease and its potential complications, I visit a rural comprehensive health service center or a hospital whenever I experience even a minor problem in order to prevent my condition from worsening”. (Participant16)

Reduced perceived severity of illness

Another frequently cited barrier was the lack of perceived urgency or seriousness regarding one’s illness, which often resulted in disengagement from care and a failure to seek timely treatment: “When someone doesn't take their illness seriously and has no fear of its complications, they naturally become careless. I believe there must be a sense of urgency or fear about disease consequences in order to motivate a person to follow up with treatment. At first, I didn’t pay much attention to my high blood pressure. But when my poor uncle suffered a massive brain hemorrhage at the age of 40 due to uncontrolled hypertension—resulting in total paralysis and now being bedridden, breathing through a tube—I became terrified of high blood pressure and have since been highly committed to managing it.” (Participant 25).

Discussion

The present study sought to explore the factors contributing to the underutilization of rural comprehensive health centers among individuals living with chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Through qualitative content analysis, three overarching domains emerged from the data: structural and service accessibility barriers, trust-related barriers, and health literacy and illness perception barriers.

The first domain structural and service accessibility barriers encompassed a range of distance, service hours, workforce shortages, and lack of equipment. Collectively, these factors were perceived to hinder patients’ ability to access appropriate healthcare services. These findings are consistent with those reported by Kamvura et al., 31 who found that limited access to medications and diagnostic resources, including blood pressure monitoring devices and urine test strips, adversely affected the management of chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and depression within primary healthcare settings in Zimbabwe. Similarly, Partovi et al. 5 identified deficiencies in workforce capacity and financial resources as major obstacles to the effective implementation of NCD prevention and control programs in Iran. Comparable findings were reported by Heydari et al., 32 who demonstrated that discrepancies between staff competencies and service expectations, together with imbalances between workforce availability and community healthcare needs, negatively affected the performance of comprehensive health centers. Furthermore, geographical remoteness from primary healthcare facilities, particularly when compounded by inadequate transportation infrastructure, has been identified as a major barrier to healthcare utilization among rural populations, especially older adults. 10 Consistent with these observations, Bidarpour et al. 33 and Williamson et al. 34 likewise recognized physical distance and rural isolation as important determinants of reduced utilization of comprehensive healthcare services.

Challenges related to distance and transportation have long been recognized as key determinants of healthcare utilization, particularly among underserved and geographically disadvantaged populations. Individuals residing in close proximity to healthcare facilities generally exhibit higher rates of service utilization, whereas long travel distances and transportation-related difficulties often discourage formal healthcare-seeking behavior and encourage reliance on self-medication and traditional therapeutic practices as alternatives to the financial and logistical burdens associated with travel.35,36 Geographic isolation continues to represent a significant challenge across many rural settings, where residents frequently live several hours from hospitals, specialist services, and advanced healthcare facilities. Under such circumstances, access to timely and appropriate healthcare becomes increasingly difficult, particularly when urgent intervention or specialized treatment is required. Consequently, rural populations often experience higher mortality rates from preventable conditions, including cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and respiratory disorders, largely because of delayed diagnosis and restricted access to timely treatment. 33

These challenges are further exacerbated by shortages within the healthcare workforce. Rural communities frequently encounter difficulties in recruiting and retaining primary care physicians, specialists, nurses, and mental health professionals. Consequently, patients are often faced with fewer healthcare options, prolonged waiting times, and the indirect effects of provider fatigue and burnout arising from excessive workloads. Moreover, many rural areas lack essential healthcare infrastructure, including advanced diagnostic technologies, specialty care services, and emergency medical facilities. Under these circumstances, patients are frequently required to travel substantial distances to obtain appropriate care. Taken together, these structural constraints, coupled with limited access to routine screening programs and preventive healthcare services, contribute to suboptimal chronic disease management, increased morbidity, and diminished quality of life. 35 The present findings are also consistent with evidence from several European countries, where more than 40% of residents living in sparsely populated rural areas are required to travel considerable distances to access healthcare services.37,38

Another important structural and service accessibility barrier identified, in the present study was the limited availability of healthcare services outside routine working hours, which appeared to reduce patients’ willingness and ability to attend rural health centers. Similar observations were reported by Ghorbani et al., 24 who found that older adults frequently miss or postpone healthcare appointments when the operating hours of rural healthcare facilities coincide with the work schedules of family members responsible for transportation and support. Extending service provision into evening hours may therefore represent an effective strategy for improving accessibility and promoting greater utilization of healthcare services. Furthermore, the integration of digital health innovations, including telemedicine and mobile health technologies, may offer practical and sustainable solutions for addressing many of these structural barriers. 24

The trust-related barriers identified in this study primarily involved deeply rooted beliefs favoring traditional remedies and self-treatment practices, together with a noticeable barriers of trust in formal healthcare systems and providers. Similar findings were reported by Kamvura et al., 31 who observed that some patients preferred seeking assistance from traditional or religious healers because such services were perceived as more affordable than private healthcare alternatives. Reliance on these options is often associated with limited medication availability within public healthcare facilities and the high cost of pharmaceuticals obtained through private pharmacies. 39 Influenced by family networks, peer recommendations, and longstanding cultural traditions, many participants expressed strong confidence in the therapeutic value of herbal remedies and frequently prioritized these treatments over prescribed medical therapies, even when conventional medications were readily available. 24 Although traditional medicine is often used alongside biomedical treatment approaches, 38 limited health literacy may increase the likelihood of inappropriate self-medication and contribute to delays in seeking professional healthcare services. 24

Mirsadeghi et al., 11 who identified distrust of healthcare personnel and perceptions of inadequate commitment to the care of older adults as important determinants of healthcare underutilization. Their findings suggested that effective provider–patient communication plays a central role in fostering trust, which, in turn, promotes greater engagement with healthcare services, a conclusion similarly supported by Yousaf et al. 40 Likewise, Hamidzadeh et al. 41 identified widespread public mistrust of community health workers as a significant barrier to participation in rural health education initiatives. According to these authors, such mistrust often stemmed from inadequate communication skills among healthcare personnel and service packages that failed to reflect the specific needs and expectations of rural communities.

Another important trust-related barrier in the present study was negative attitude and experiences to the utilization of rural comprehensive health centers (RCHCs). Eriksen,et al. (2023) who found that negative patient experiences with health care settings impact patients’ physical and psychological health, leading to suffering and hampering patients from involving themselves in their health care. 42 In a study by Mahboubi et al. (2026), older adults did not perceive service quality or trust in health personnel as barriers to the utilization of services. This finding stands in contrast to prior research, which has consistently underscored the critical role of healthcare providers’ attitudes, the nature and quality of services delivered, and the level of trust in service providers. Accordingly, the present findings diverge from those reported in that study. It is plausible that factors such as accessibility in terms of traffic conditions, geographical proximity to service centers, the sociocultural context of the place of residence, and the physical and psychological status of older adults collectively facilitate increased utilization of comprehensive health centers, particularly in relation to family-related and personal concerns. 43

An important observation arising from the present findings is that these barriers rarely operate independently. Rather, participants’ narratives suggest that organizational, sociocultural, and individual-level influences interact in complex and mutually reinforcing ways to shape decisions regarding the utilization of rural healthcare services. For many participants, substantial travel distances, transportation-related difficulties, and restricted service availability reduced opportunities for regular interaction with healthcare professionals. Reduced contact with providers, in turn, limited the development of trusting therapeutic relationships, leading some individuals to rely increasingly on traditional remedies, self-management practices, or advice obtained from family members and acquaintances. Such experiences were frequently accompanied by insufficient knowledge of disease management and a tendency to underestimate the seriousness and long-term consequences of chronic illnesses. Consequently, many participants perceived little need for routine monitoring, preventive services, or ongoing follow-up care. Taken together, the findings indicate that the underutilization of rural comprehensive health centers cannot be attributed to a single determinant; rather, it reflects the cumulative and interconnected effects of structural constraints, sociocultural influences, trust-related concerns, and individual perceptions of illness.

At the health literacy and illness perception barriers, inadequate knowledge of self-care and disease management, together with a diminished perception of susceptibility to illness, emerged as important contributors to reduced concern for personal health and lower levels of healthcare utilization. A substantial body of evidence has demonstrated that limited awareness and understanding of disease processes constitute major barriers to effective engagement with healthcare services. 44 For example, Shakibazadeh et al. 45 identified a range of psychological, social, and educational barriers that impede effective diabetes self-management, thereby highlighting the combined influence of personal and cultural factors. Consistent with these findings, previous studies have similarly identified limited health literacy as a significant obstacle to the prevention, management, and treatment of NCDs.46,47

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first qualitative investigation conducted in Iran to explore factors associated with the underutilization of rural comprehensive health centers among individuals living with NCDs from the perspective of patients themselves. The findings provide valuable evidence for healthcare professionals, policymakers, and public health decision-makers seeking to strengthen NCDs prevention and management strategies within the primary healthcare system. Nevertheless, an important limitation should be acknowledged. Specifically, the findings are context-specific and therefore cannot be generalized beyond the setting in which the study was conducted. Future research involving larger and more geographically diverse samples is needed to generate more comprehensive and representative evidence. Additionally, future studies should examine the potential contribution of digital health innovations, including mobile health applications and telehealth services, to improving healthcare utilization in rural communities, particularly in light of growing international evidence supporting their effectiveness in resource-constrained settings. 5

Limitations

The present study explored healthcare utilization exclusively from the perspective of patients. Future research should therefore incorporate the views and experiences of physicians and other healthcare professionals to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing healthcare utilization. Furthermore, because the study was conducted within the sociocultural and healthcare context of Iran, caution is warranted when interpreting and applying the findings to other settings. The distinctive cultural, social, and economic characteristics of Iran may limit the transferability of the findings. Accordingly, conducting comparable studies in other countries would facilitate meaningful cross-national comparisons and enhance understanding of contextual variations. Moreover, the findings should be interpreted within the realities of a resource-constrained low- and middle-income country (LMIC). Consequently, direct comparisons with high-income countries or healthcare systems characterized by extensive welfare provisions should be undertaken cautiously.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that Structural and service accessibility barriers, Trust-related barriers, and Health literacy and illness perception barriers substantially influence patients’ access to and utilization of rural comprehensive health centers for the management of chronic non-communicable diseases. These findings provide an important evidence base for healthcare policymakers and senior health administrators seeking to design and implement targeted interventions aimed at reducing barriers to rural healthcare access and strengthening the effectiveness of primary healthcare services for NCDs management. Healthcare professionals working in rural settings routinely confront the consequences of geographic isolation, workforce shortages, economic constraints, and sociocultural influences, all of which significantly affect patients’ ability to obtain appropriate healthcare services. Meaningful improvements in health outcomes and equitable access to healthcare among rural populations will therefore depend on coordinated, evidence-informed action accompanied by a sustained commitment to health equity.

One promising strategy involves the deployment of mobile health clinics capable of delivering diagnostic services, preventive interventions, and specialized care directly to underserved communities. In addition, the integration of digital health technologies that support the remote monitoring and management of chronic conditions, including diabetes and hypertension, may enhance disease control while reducing reliance on frequent in-person consultations. Such innovations have the potential to improve patient convenience substantially by minimizing the need for travel to healthcare facilities, an advantage of particular importance in rural settings where healthcare access is often constrained. When effectively integrated into rural healthcare systems, these approaches may contribute significantly to improving health outcomes, enhancing accessibility, and supporting the development of more sustainable models of chronic disease management and healthcare delivery.
