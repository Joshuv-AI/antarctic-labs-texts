Translational Gynecologic Oncology at Midlife and Beyond: A Difficult Bedside to Community Transition
Authors: Ranu Patni
Published: 2024

Gynecological oncology is one super specialty, the benefit of which if appropriately extended to the masses can significantly improve women’s health especially around midlife globally and mainly in the low- and middle-income countries.

In order to ensure that the benefit of gynecological cancer prevention, screening, management, survivorship issues, etc., in terms of best possible overall survival and progression-free survival reaches maximum number of people in any given community while maintaining as good a quality of life as possible, the concept, and tools of translational medicine (TM) need to be widely adopted and implemented in a customized manner.

According to the European Society of TM, an interdisciplinary branch of biomedicine supported by three pillars: bench, bedside, and community is called TM.[1] Currently, TM is focused on bench to bedside approach. “Bench” means the route/path leading to the development of a drug or technology whereas “Bedside” is the integration of “bench” into clinical care.

Another desirable approach is bedside to community transition of research which can help in evolving better medicines, technologies, and evidence-based practice guidelines. However, some gaps or lacunae called “Valleys of death” or transitional failures impede this desirable approach. Measures such as patient centric research and setting up of nontraditional research centers might help to bridge these valleys.

Past few decades have seen establishment of many new “bench” milestones in Gynecological Oncology. Many of them have successfully transitioned to “bedside” to “community.” Yet a lot remains desired in terms of community benefit especially in the resource restricted settings. Let us take the example of three benchmark developments and their transition in three most common gynecological malignancies.

CANCER CERVIX

In 2008, Harald zur Hausen received the Nobel prize in Medicine for his ground breaking work on Human Papilloma Virus being a necessary cause of cervical cancer.[2]

This “bench” discovery soon reached the “bedside” in the form of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine used for primary prevention of HPV infection. In the 1990s, Frazer and Jian Zhou succeeded in developing the HPV vaccine which was a virus like particle expressing virus capsid proteins.[3]

Pei et al. assessed the incidence of cervical cancer by age and histology before and after the introduction of HPV vaccination and found that for overall cervical cancer and cervical squamous cell carcinoma, incidence rate showed decreasing trends (–0.7%, and –1.0% annually, respectively).[4]

Community transition took place when screening and prevention strategies were incorporated in international cervical cancer prevention and screening guidelines. However, full benefit did not reach the masses due to a nonuniform approach among different resource settings.

Nour in 2009 mentioned that none of the technologies will be truly effective without outreach programs that educate/empower women and their communities on the prevention of cervical cancer.[5] In 2019, the WHO released the draft of a strategy to eliminate cervical cancer by adopting a triple pronged (90: 70: 90) approach by which WHO aims to achieve elimination of cervical cancer globally by 2030. This strategy has definitely given momentum to the mission of eliminating cervical cancer across the world including low and middle income countries. However, a lot still remains to be achieved and a multipronged, customized strategy needs to be implemented including mobilization of government, practitioners, nongovernment organizations, and the masses.

CANCER ENDOMETRIUM

The aim of surgical staging in endometrial carcinoma is to remove the primary tumor along with any resectable metastasis and to identify definite prognostic factors based on clinicopathological factors to tailor appropriate adjuvant therapy if required.

It was observed that histopathological findings are prone to a misdiagnosis as multiple studies have shown uncertainties between pathologists in about 30% of the cases.[67]

Due to this interobserver variation the need for a more robust and uniform classification was felt. The Cancer Genome Atlas performed a genome-wide analysis of 373 Endometrial cancers and identified four distinct groups: Polymerase epsilon ultra-mutated, microsatellite instability hypermutated, copy-number-low, and copy number high endometrial carcinoma.[8] Derived from these data Talhouk et al. developed ProMisE to identify similar subgroups (polymerase epsilon exonuclease domain mutated, mismatch repair deficient, p53 wild-type/copy-number-low, and p53-mutated/copy-number-high using a combination of immunohistochemistry and mutation analysis instead of genomic data.[9] In addition, their work incorporated survival analysis, anticipated clinical benefit as well as cost and accessibility of methods into the algorithm. This was indeed a “bench” milestone for cancer of the endometrium. This molecular classification was highly reproducible and showed good prognostic value.

The “bench” to “bedside” transition happened when the molecular classification was incorporated in the new staging of Endometrial Carcinoma by FIGO in 2023.

Ongoing trials such as PORTEC 4a are designed to study the outcomes of tailored adjuvant treatments based on molecular classification. On validation, these protocols are likely to get incorporated in international management guidelines and “community” milestone will be achieved.

However, adopting these guidelines and treatment protocols might be a challenge for limited resource settings due to deficient acceptance by the medical practitioners as well as the community. This might be due to a number of factors including complexity of the treatment algorithms, poor availability as well as affordability of the molecular markers, etc. Therefore, management guidelines, when formulated, should take into consideration all the above factors so that all the communities across the world are benefitted uniformly.

CANCER OVARY

Approximately 5%–10% of all cancers can be attributed to hereditary cancer syndromes. The most common hereditary gynecologic cancer syndromes, hereditary breast–ovarian cancer syndrome (HBOC) and hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer syndrome, account for approximately 10%–18% of ovarian, tubal, peritoneal, and endometrial cancer cases.

The invention of association, diagnosis and reporting of BRCA 1 and BRCA 2 genetic mutations with HBOC was a “bench” milestone for HBOCs. Sixty-six percent of BRCA 1 and BRCA 2-associated HBOC attributed to pathogenic variation in genes are due to BRCA 1 mutation. The bench to bedside transition happened when risk reducing strategies were identified, proven to reduce the risk of HBOC and started being used in clinical settings. Besides other measures such as oral contraceptive use, risk reducing salpingo-oophorectomy reduces the risk of ovarian cancer by 85%–90%.

A prospective cohort study of 2482 women with BRCA1 or BRCA2 pathogenic variants reported a 79% reduction in ovarian cancer mortality and a 60% reduction on all-cause mortality associated with risk-reducing oophorectomy.[10]

Currently prophylactic salpingectomy after childbearing followed by delayed oophorectomy at the time of menopause is also being studied. Community transition happened when NCCN Guidelines (Version 3.2019) recommended risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy in women with BRCA1/2 mutations after 35 years of age. NCCN Guidelines version 3.2024 have given detailed guidelines of BRCA pathogenic/likely pathogenic positive variant management.

However, even after availability of community protocols, adherence to or adoption of risk reducing strategies for HBOC in BRCA positive individuals is scanty. Acceptance of risk reducing procedures is quite low especially among women. Hence, the scope of awareness generation and health empowerment needs to be widened for maximum benefit to all.

A multi-pronged and inclusive approach involving the super specialists, the policy makers, the general practitioners and the public will go a long way in ensuring effective translational gynecological oncology at midlife and beyond. The desired upliftment and benefit from the unique specialty of gynecological oncology especially around midlife also requires nurturing and promoting dedicated gynecologic oncology and menopausal medicine facilities with an aim to develop bench to bedside to community culture in a customized manner based on the resource setting.
