Middle Holocene survival of marsupial megafauna on the north coast of New Guinea
Authors: Loukas George Koungoulos, Isaac Alan Robert Kerr, Sue O’Connor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe timing and causes of the extinction of Pleistocene megafauna are unresolved issues in the natural history of Australia and New Guinea (Sahul). In Australia, megafauna are believed to have become extinct by c. 41ka, but in the Highlands of New Guinea some species persisted until the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM), as late as c. 22ka. Here, we present the first evidence that one of these taxa survived beyond even this timeframe. We describe a manual phalanx from a megafaunal macropodid, probably referable to a quadrupedal, forest-dwelling member of the genus Protemnodon recovered from the Middle Holocene (6.8 - 5.3ka) archaeological deposit of Taora, a coastal rockshelter located west of Vanimo, Papua New Guinea. This late local persistence is likely a consequence of low human populations and a relatively small body size. Its disappearance from the region is coincident with broader decline in local mammalian diversity following post-glacial environmental change. Taora provides the first indication that any of Sahul’s megafauna survived beyond the end of the LGM and highlights geographic and chronological variability in this diverse group’s extinction history.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Zoology

Introduction

The timing and causes of the extinction of Pleistocene megafauna is a heavily contested issue in the prehistory of Australia and New Guinea (Sahul). Perspectives vary between a sweeping rapid extinction event from 41-46ka generally attributed to human hunting1,2, to a longer primarily environmentally-driven process that began well before the arrival of people, and in which several megafaunal taxa also persisted for thousands of years3–5. However, the purported late dating of fossil evidence for this scenario is extremely controversial, and the currently prevailing view is that Pleistocene megafauna had become extinct across Australia (including Tasmania) by c. 40ka6. Fossil evidence from New Guinea, on the other hand, indicates a more staggered extinction process that took place later than in Australia.

Key sites in New Guinea Highlands settings (Fig. 1) have produced fossils of several marsupial megafauna species of apparent late antiquity relative to Australian megafauna. These include the forest-dwelling kangaroo Protemnodon tumbuna (formerly assigned to the species hopei) in Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) to 16.2ka deposits at Kelangurr7; a Protemnodon-like molar at Kafiayawa older than c.13ka2,8; Maokopia ronaldi at 40 - 15ka in the West Baliem River sediments9; and Hulitherium tomasettii c.38ka at Pureni Swamp10. Most notably, Nombe produced Late Pleistocene remains of four extinct large marsupial species in possible association with human cultural deposit. Two of these, Protemnodon tumbuna and another large macropodid, likely the somewhat smaller Nombe nombe (formerly assigned to Protemnodon) have since been directly dated via U-Th methods to the LGM (c.27-22ka)11. Late survival of megafauna in the Highlands, potentially indicating c.20,000 years of coexistence with the earliest human settlers, was probably facilitated by low human activity across these landscapes, as their depauperate resources did not encourage large populations or long residency until much later development of intensive plant management regimes in the Holocene12.

New evidence suggesting late megafauna survival in New Guinea outside of the Highlands came with the recent discovery of a molariform tooth belonging to the genus Protemnodon in the archaeological deposit of Lachitu Cave in coastal Sandaun Province, Papua New Guinea (Fig. 1)13. The tooth was recovered from strata of inferred Middle Holocene antiquity, but appears to have been derived from an adjacent eroding breccia deposit. Marine shell from this deposit is of Middle Holocene age (OZI282: 6680 ± 90BP, 7320–6730 calBP), while analogous samples from breccias elsewhere in the cave produced Terminal Pleistocene dates (WK-17978: 15,357 ± 109BP, 18,120–17,410 calBP and WK-17979: 15,325 ± 97BP, 18,070–17,400 calBP). The earliest human activity at Lachitu is close to the timeframe in which the last Australian megafauna became extinct (ANU-7610: 35,410 ± 1400 BP, 42,000– 36,700 calBP)14, but its breccia deposits provide circumstantial evidence of the tantalising possibility that a Protemnodon species persisted locally until after the LGM.

Despite these promising signs, potential survival of megafauna in New Guinea until or beyond the LGM requires more robust demonstration through materials more reliably associated with securely-dated sedimentary strata. With the possible exception of Nombe, the existing body of material also lacks clear evidence of association with human activity indicating direct temporal overlap between megafauna and people at the same site. Here, we present the first evidence of this nature: a megafaunal macropodid phalanx from the archaeological site Taora, 3.5 km west of Lachitu.

Results

Archaeological context of megafauna at Taora

Taora (Site code: RIU) is a rockshelter set within a large tract of uplifted Late Pleistocene reefal limestone, presently 9–12 m asl in the Oenake mountain range and 450 m from the coastline between Vanimo (Sandaun Province, PNG) and Jayapura (Papua Province, Indonesia). Initial excavations in 199015 established that the site is located within a marine erosional notch at the base of a former sea cliff, created by wave action during the Middle Holocene sea level highstand. Marine trangressions filled the site with a clean, culturally sterile carbonate beach stand, but following their recession from c.6800 calBP people moved in to use the shelter, now located on the edge of productive marine habitats.

A rich deposit of shell, bone and lithic artefacts rapidly built up from human occupation over the next 1500 years, followed by a hiatus in use of a few thousand years, with deposition resuming in the Late Holocene. Subsequent excavation of an additional square (A) in 200416 produced new chronostratigraphic data which verified the basic geomorphological and depositional history of the site (Fig. 2).

The mid-Holocene phase at Lachitu spans a small chronological window of intensive human occupation, with 21 radiocarbon dates from over a meter’s depth of deposit all falling within a period of c.1500 years between 6800–5920 (ANU-7605) to 5790–5300 (ANU-7702) calBP (Table 1).

Unlike Lachitu there are no identifiable breccia deposits on Taora’s walls, speleothems or subsurface floors from which older materials could feasibly be reworked into its Middle to Late Holocene deposit. This is likely because the site is a cliff with a marine notch formation, and not a true speleal environment, such as a breached flank margin cave. Any potentially older deposits that accumulated below the cliff would either lie well below the unexcavated limit (the sterile Holocene beach sand unit), or more likely, were long ago gutted and washed out to sea. The sum of chronological and stratigraphic evidence indicates that a pre-Holocene derivation for any material within the excavated Taora deposit above the beach sand unit, including the megafauna phalanx, is extremely unlikely. Any reworking of materials in the deposit via disturbance or other processes as such only concerns materials originating within this timeframe.

The Taora phalanx was retrieved from clear original association with culturally-derived materials, largely comprised of marine shell and bones of terrestrial vertebrates, all of which otherwise exclusively represent extant taxa (Suppl. Table 1). The specimen’s surface condition (Fig. 4) is entirely consistent with other bone from the dated Middle Holocene contexts, which is characterised by variable degrees of thermal modification ranging from burning to calcination. Bones from the Middle Holocene contexts are a dark brown color suggesting indirect exposure to hearths, and feature a very light, thin layer of white-grey carbonate overlay across much of their surfaces. This condition is clearly differentiable from both the fresh, clean appearance of the more recent bone from the overlying Late Holocene levels, and the few pieces of bone derived from the clean carbonate beach sand unit, which are brittle, pale in colouration and feature adhering sand grains (Fig. 4). On the basis of condition it is thus most parsimonious to associate the Taora specimen with primary deposition occurring as a result of human activity during the Middle Holocene.

Comparative anatomy and morphology

The Taora megafaunal specimen is identified as a near-complete medial manual phalanx of a large macropodid marsupial. One side has a small, roughly oblong abraded surface with the thin cortical bone lost, exposing the spongy trabecular bone. It is fairly robust, with approximate maximum dimensions of 11.10 × 11.80 × 8.80 mm. Four sides have swollen, rugose surfaces corresponding to ligamentous and muscular attachments. Smooth articular surfaces cover two opposing sides, one slightly larger. The rounded, concave shape of the larger articular facet and the broad, mesially concave shape of the opposing facet immediately identify the element as a proximal or middle phalanx, either manual or pedal.

The large size, late Quaternary age, and geographical location of the specimen constrains its identity to that of a large marsupial in the families Macropodidae, Diprotodontidae, Thylacinidae, Thylacoleonidae or Palorchestidae; a monotreme (Zaglossus spp., the long-beaked echidnas); or two other large vertebrates common in modern New Guinea, pig (Sus scrofa), or cassowary (Casuarius spp.). All candidates for the identity of the Taora phalanx except the Macropodidae (kangaroos and their relatives) were readily removed from consideration on the basis of gross anatomical traits. A summary of these is provided here, with the detailed differential diagnostic traits presented in detail within the Methods. Cassowary and echidna are eliminated on the basis of developed palmar peaks absent in the Taora specimen, whilst pigs possess far deeper articular facets separated by a strong ridge. Thylacoleonid and palorchestid megafauna are not known from New Guinea, and their corresponding elements are respectively more elongate and much more robust. Thylacinids have far more elongate and gracile manual phalanges. Finally, Diprotodontid fossil species from New Guinea lack manual material, but Australian examples are highly rounded, asymmetrical, and possess a deep distal end.

Of at least 15 species of macropodid in the contemporary and Late Quaternary New Guinean contexts there are only two, both extinct, that are sufficiently large to be represented by the Taora specimen: Protemnodon tumbuna and Nombe nombe. P. tumbuna is one of seven species of the extinct genus Protemnodon, a member of crown tribe Macropodini18,19. The species range from the 50–90 kg, possibly quadrupedal forest dweller P. tumbuna to the 100–170 kg, arid-adapted and bipedal-hopping Protemnodon viator. No manual skeletal material is known for P. tumbuna, which is the only known member of the genus from Pleistocene New Guinea. However, a near-complete articulated distal forelimb has been described of its Pliocene New Guinean and eastern Australian probable ancestor Protemnodon otibandus. The morphology and proportions of the second–fourth middle manual phalanges of P. otibandus are similar to those of the Taora specimen. Measurements are similar, especially lengths, widths and distal height. The proximal end is not as distinctly deep, but the flexor tubercles are similarly broad and rugose. The collateral fossae of P. otibandus are similar in size to those of the Taora specimen and have raised proximal margins, though these are much narrower, less raised, and less rugose than those of the Taora specimen.

Nombe nombe is a species of basal macropodine kangaroo of estimated 50 kg body mass, known only from Nombe in the Highlands of eastern New Guinea20. Very little is known of the morphology of N. nombe, as only two partial dentaries have been confidently allocated, while a partial ulna may represent the species. The phylogenetic affinities of N. nombe are uncertain, but it is presently considered a basal macropodine with unknown tribe affiliation. The species has a peculiar molar morphology, with ‘S-shaped’ lophid crests on the lower molars. This autapomorphy, broad, low-crowned molars, the muscular morphology of the dentary, and the habitat surrounding the Nombe site have been interpreted to suggest a robust browsing-specialised kangaroo adapted to densely vegetated montane rainforest environments20.

As macropodids inhabiting dense forest environments in the modern day are generally slow-moving, P. tumbuna and N. nombe were probably predominantly similarly slow and quadrupedal, hopping only when alarmed, inefficiently, and for short periods19–21. This has implications for the functional morphology of the forelimb and manus, which would have had to support the weight of a large kangaroo more extensively than in predominantly bipedal macropodids, both living and extinct. In the case of P. tumbuna, the known fossil material strongly supports this hypothesis, with several independent interpretations identifying this taxon’s skeletal morphology as that of an obligate quadruped.

The robust morphology of the Taora specimen, with a tall proximal end, well-developed flexor tubercles, and large, deep collateral fossae, similarly approaches the morphology that would be expected of a large, mostly quadrupedal forest kangaroo. If the Taora specimen is indeed P. tumbuna, then in the absence of directly comparable material the differences between it and P. otibandus could be explained by relatively greater specialisation of P. tumbuna to densely forested Pleistocene environments. On the basis of comparative anatomy it can be formally allocated to either Macropodinae gen. et sp. indet. cf. P. tumbuna or N. nombe.

PCA (Fig. 5) comparing 16 morphological measurements from the Taora specimen to numerous extant and extinct macropodids (Supplementary Fig. 1) finds strong differentiation in medial manual phalanx morphology between the major groups: the tree kangaroos (Dendrolaginae), smaller extant wallabies, extant Australian kangaroos, and the extinct genera Sthenurus and Protemnodon. The Taora specimen is most closely associated with members of the genus Protemnodon, and within this group falls into the morphospace approximately between P. otibandus and P. viator. PC1 captures the vast majority of morphological variation within the sample (88.4%), largely separating small from large macropodids, and has very similar loadings across each measurement’s contribution (Suppl. Tables 2, 3). This indication of a strong allometric component is confirmed by a correlation between PC1 and size (geometric mean) of 0.99 (p < 0.01). Smaller members of Protemnodon are differentiated from other macropodids of comparable body size (including Macropus and Osphranter spp.) along PC2. The strongest contributors to difference in this respect are the pronounced eminences above the collateral fossae characteristic of the genus, the relative width of the collateral fossae themselves, and the maximum breadth of the midshaft.

Principal Components Analysis plot comparing the Taora specimen to extinct and extant Australian and New Guinean macropodids.

PCA with the allometric component removed to examine phalanx shape independent of size (Suppl. Fig. 2, Suppl. Tables 5, 6) indicates a surprisingly closer shape to the third medial manual phalanges of P. mamkurra and P. viator than to P. otibandus along PC1, but also shows separation from the known Protemnodon shape-space along PC2. Most major groups remain separated, with some rearrangement of their inter-group associations along PC1 which largely describes relative proximodistal elongation or compression. Importantly, the Taora phalanx’s shape has little in common with the modern extant dorcopsins (members of the tribe Dorcopsini, in our sample Dorcopsis atrata, D. muelleri, and Dorcopsulus vanheurni) along either of the two dominant shape trajectories.

This suggests it is unlikely to be an otherwise undescribed megafaunal species of dorcopsin, which are much smaller and today restricted to New Guinea. Alternatively, given the presence of the strong family-level allometric component it is possible that a very large dorcopsin might approach a manual morphology form closer to the aforementioned larger taxa. Resemblance to any tree kangaroos (subtribe Dendrolagina) appears firmly ruled out.

A neighbour-joining tree based on the Euclidean distances between specimens (Suppl. Fig. 3, Suppl. Table 6) echoes these observations, with P. otibandus and P. viator comprising six of the seven nearest samples to the Taora phalanx. The fifth, eighth, and tenth were O. robustus and the ninth O. rufus, reflecting their similar overall sizes and positions in PC1. Consistent similarities with Macropus and Osphranter are interesting given that molecular phylogenies recover Protemnodon within a clade of the derived crown tribe Macropodini, as a sister of Macropus to the exclusion of Notamacropus22. Two of the three Sthenurus stirlingi specimens were conversely the furthest removed from Taora amongst the sample, providing further confidence that the species in question represented at Taora is not a sthenurine kangaroo.

On the basis of osteological morphology N. nombe is phylogenetically considered a basal macropodine (member of the subfamily Macropodinae), placed in an unresolved basal group with other systematically-difficult taxa such as the extinct Pliocene species Watutia novaeguineae and the extinct Plio-Pleistocene Dorcopsoides species group20. Dorcopsoides is considered a member of Dorcopsini23, but the placement of this tribe as represented by the extant members is itself unresolved relative to the better-studied Macropodini and Dendrolagini tribes.

The balance of biogeographic, ecological, anatomical, and palaeontological evidence would imply that the Taora phalanx is more likely to belong to a member of the genus Protemnodon than to N. nombe, which can be regarded as a large macropodid with dorcopsin-like or unresolved basal affinities. Our analyses show that members of the Macropodini clade formed by Protemnodon spp. and Macropus + Osphranter spp. show relative similarity in medial manual phalangeal morphology when of similar body size compared to the Dorcopsini and more basally-related macropodids (e.g., Sthenurinae), and that this comparative similarity also applies when controlling for allometry. This would suggest that, were Nombe to prove to be a large dorcopsin, the form of this element would probably not resemble that of the Taora phalanx, which is Macropodini-like. However, because the relevant fossil material is not known and the systematic relationship between Nombe and the Dorcopsini or other basal macropodids is not well understood, the possibility that the Taora phalanx belongs to N. nombe cannot yet be totally ruled out.

Discussion

The Taora phalanx constitutes the first evidence that any of New Guinea’s megafauna survived beyond the Last Glacial Maximum. The late persistence of this smaller-bodied “megafauna” taxon is in some ways reminiscent of the case in Australia, where at least two macropodid taxa in which fully-grown adults exceed 50 kg in body mass (Macropus giganteus and Osphranter rufus) remain extant today, alongside three others which regularly exceed 40 kg in mature males (Macropus fuliginosus, Osphranter antilopinus, and Osphranter robustus). Evidence for much later survival - from the LGM to well into the Holocene - of at least two large macropodid taxa in New Guinea also in or above the 40–50 kg weight range supports demographic modelling of macropodiform marsupials as the major Sahul megafauna group least susceptible to extinction24.

The disappearance of megafaunal macropodids including Protemnodon from the Oenake area is coincident with a broader trend suggestive of major climate-driven environmental change. During the Pleistocene glacial period, compressed, lowered treelines owing to colder temperatures and the Massenerhebung effect25 supported montane forest communities, of the kinds which today only occur above 1000 m, throughout northern New Guinea’s low-lying coastal ranges. Warming during deglaciation at the onset of the Holocene subsequently began a transition towards the lowland rainforest and hill forest that covers the Oenake hinterland today. Palynological data from the nearby Cyclops Mountains indicates this process began at the Terminal Pleistocene-Holocene transition c. 10,750 ± 120 BP (13,000–12,500 calBP; ANU-4104) and was largely complete by the early Middle Holocene c. 7140 ± 120BP (8180–7700 calBP; ANU-4859)26.

Protemnodon otibandus, the Pliocene predecessor of P. tumbuna is believed to have inhabited lowland rainforest in eastern New Guinea, and was also present in eastern Australia. Strontium isotopes of Late Pleistocene rainforest-dwelling Protemnodon spp. in northeastern Australia reflect very small home ranges, possibly due to restrictions imposed by dietary preferences or locomotory capacity. This is speculated to have made Protemnodon spp. highly vulnerable to shifting climates, being unable to adapt to a changing environment or redisperse to refugia in remaining pockets of suitable habitat27. If the taxon present at Taora was a member of Protemnodon (as represented at Lachitu) and behaved similarly, this would have presented an extreme challenge to survival during Middle Holocene losses of local montane forests.

Preliminary faunal analyses of Lachitu’s archaeological assemblage confirm a former presence, lasting until the Middle Holocene, of several montane-adapted mammal taxa no longer extant in the surrounding region (the Oenake Range is mostly 400-600 m in elevation)16, including Attenborough’s echidna (Zaglossus attenboroughi)28. Archaeological fauna from two other lowland sites on the Bird’s Head with well-preserved Terminal Pleistocene to Late Holocene sequences (Kria and Toé Caves) attest to a similar chronological and taxonomic trend29, suggesting a widespread phenomenon affected many of New Guinea’s low-lying and isolated coastal ranges. Remnant montane-adapted mammal communities present in these ranges became isolated by tracts of floodplains, swamp and lowland rainforest and probably soon succumbed to increased hunting pressures during the Early and Middle Holocene.

Taora is located on the “northern route” pathway proposed for the earliest human settlers of Sahul30 and any megafaunal macropodid species inhabiting this region are hence likely to have been encountered by some of these colonists. Remarkably, if this is the case, they must have subsequently coexisted with people for at least 35,000 years, based on the oldest known human presence in the region of 42ka at Lachitu. A possible contributing factor to such long-term persistence is the historically very low human population size and density in the Oenake area31, with a near-total lack of extensive gardening activity and hence forest disturbance when compared to the Highlands settings where other examples of Late Pleistocene Protemnodon have been found.

The late survival of megafauna at Taora is reminiscent of other cases in New Guinea’s outlying northern ranges where the survival of vulnerable vertebrate species extirpated from surrounding areas is (or was) facilitated by extremely low human population density in the region. Examples include various rare mammals in the uninhabited upper Foja Mountains such as Zaglossus bartoni, Dorcopsulus vanheurni, and the weimang (Dendrolagus pulcherrimus)32. Deliberated absence of hunting activity within certain areas due to religious prohibitions can also have similar conservational effects that can lead to catastrophic outcomes when restrictions are lifted, for example, the tenkile (Dendrolagus scottae) in a district of the Torricelli Mountains33.

Increased sedimentation and cultural deposition rates from the Early to Middle Holocene at Lachitu and another Vanimo coast site (Watinglo) suggest substantial increases in local human activity, if not also population size and density, at this time16. On the Sepik coast to the east, human population size and density also appear to have grown substantially during the Middle Holocene following the formation of highly productive lagoon and estuarine habitats34. In the Jayapura-Lake Sentani region to the west, environmental disturbance via regular burning became established in the Early Holocene26. Indeed increased signs of human activity in New Guinea’s landscapes during this timeframe are observed across much of the island12. Intensification of anthropogenic pressures in the Early to Middle Holocene may have contributed to the final demise of sensitive taxa in vulnerable, isolated holdout populations such as Protemnodon sp. in the Oenake region.

In conclusion, the late survival of a megafaunal macropodid species until the Middle Holocene on the north coast of New Guinea highlights considerable variability in the timing and causes of extinction of Sahul’s megafauna. A minimum overlap of 35,000 years with human inhabitants of this region suggests its disappearance cannot be related to the first arrival of humans nor a rapid extinction event suggested to have marked the end of nearly all Australian megafauna by c.40ka. Rather, it appears to be part of a widescale turnover in mammal community composition across the lowlands of New Guinea effected during the Holocene, as a consequence of marked climatic change following deglaciation. Habitat loss likely placed many endemic New Guinean mammals at heightened risk of overhunting by concurrently growing human populations, and species of Protemnodon may have been particularly vulnerable to this interaction of climate and anthropogenic pressure.

Methods

Excavation, dating and interpretation of Taora

The first archaeological excavations and geomorphological assessment of Taora took place in 1989-199015, which established the site’s basic geomorphological history and sequence of cultural occupation. In summary, Taora sits at the base of a Late Pleistocene reefal limestone block which was once a sea cliff; mechanical erosion from waves and possibly bioerosion by marine invertebrates created a substantial overhang, forming a shelter useful for people. The height of Taora’s eroded face, presence of highly developed speleothem-like features, other secondary deposit formations and floor/wall morphologies that are typical of marine notches in tropical carbonate coastlines35,36 suggest that the original overhang “shelter” formation may have in part resulted from a sea-level highstand during the last interglacial period, prior to 125ka. However, marine wave transgressions from the subsequent Middle Holocene sea-level highstand appear to have further developed Taora’s notch formation, which became quickly infilled by a clean carbonate beach sand deposit. The cessation of marine transgressions shortly following c.6800 calBP through tectonic uplift events and the stabilisation of lower sea levels allowed people to move into and use the shelter.

The archaeological investigations at Taora, which produced the megafaunal macropodid phalanx were conducted by SOC and colleagues in 2004 through the excavation of a single 1 x 1 m test square (Square A). Excavation was conducted by digging 30 spits of variable (but generally 2–5 cm) depth, until reaching a contiguous unit of clean and archaeologically sterile carbonate beach sands that underlie the cultural deposit in all corners of the square, to a maximum depth of 178 cm. Square A was located directly next to two of the squares (X2 and X3) previously excavated by Gorecki in 1990 (Fig. 1). Square A’s excavation revealed a precise correspondence of stratigraphic units and features documented by Gorecki for squares X2, X3 and F, therefore allowing reference to radiocarbon dates from both projects for interpretation of the chronology of Taora’s deposit relative to the contents of the Middle Holocene cultural deposit.

Radiocarbon dating of unidentified marine shell samples from the 1990 excavations was performed at the ANU Dating Laboratory. Dated samples from the 2004 excavations include charcoal from unidentified plants, Turbo sp. marine gastropods shells, Heterocentrotus mamillatus, and Echinometridae spp. sea urchin test and spine fragments, and Canarium nut shell, and were dated using AMS methods at the University of Waikato Radiocarbon Dating Laboratory. Calibrations were performed in OxCal 4.4. and calibrated ranges are rounded to the nearest 10 years. Dates on terrestrial organic material were calibrated using a 50:50 mixed curve of the IntCal20 and SHCal20 curves as recommended for intertropical convergence zone settings37,38.

Gorecki and colleagues reported their radiocarbon dates with a flat -400 years BP reduction from each C value in lieu of a formal marine reservoir effect (ΔR) adjustment, while O’Connor and colleagues did not apply a marine reservoir effect adjustment to any dates. This is because there is a lack of reference ΔR data from marine invertebrates for the north coast of New Guinea, and no understanding of how ΔR may have changed over the considerable timeframe of the Vanimo Coast archaeological sites’ occupation. In the interest of consistency we restored the original uncalibrated values for Gorecki’s marine shell dates following O’Connor et al. before calibration using the Marine20 calibration curve39, with no ΔR correction applied. Nevertheless, even without ΔR corrections, the four different material types are broadly in agreeance regarding Taora’s chronology of occupation, as the plant-based material samples with (Wk-17902 and Wk-15256) return very similar Middle Holocene dates to the marine-based dates from similar depths and contexts.

Comparative elimination of non-macropodid candidates

Although extinction dates for many megafauna in Australia are now generally fairly well established, the same cannot be said of New Guinea’s late Quaternary terrestrial megafauna. The palaeontological record of New Guinea is poorly known, with only three strictly palaeontological sites having been explored in any depth, such that the diversity of the fauna is far from understood, and extinction dates are vague or unknown. We did not use the more confidently established extinction dates for Australian megafauna as evidence in the allocation of this specimen amongst potential marsupial families. However, by series of elimination based on morphological grounds and with biogeographic support, it was possible to confidently eliminate all candidates except large members of the family Macropodidae (kangaroos and wallabies).

Non-marsupial taxa were first removed from consideration. Phalanges of Zaglossus spp. (long-beaked echidnas) develop into a peak along the palmar rim of the proximal articular facet, a trait missing in the Taora specimen. Although the medial phalanges of pig (Sus scrofa) and some from the cassowary Casuarius spp. (Phalanx II of digit II, and phalanges III-IV of digit IV) appear superficially similar to the Taora specimen, they are readily distinguished by several characteristics.

S. scrofa has far deeper proximal articular facets separated by a strong ridge, much larger and deeper posterior tuberosities, and a notably thicker, rounder cross-section. Casuarius has distinctive, pointed dorsal and palmar peaks in the medial border of the proximal articular face, as well as much larger circular pits in the collateral fossae than any macropodid. Finally, both S. scrofa and Casuarius spp. have more asymmetrical and prominent trochlea, which are separated from one another on the posterior/palmar face, and on the distal articular surface by more deeply-incised intertrochlear notches/grooves.

It is considered extremely unlikely that the Taora phalanx represents a species of the Thylacoleonidae (’marsupial lions’) or Palorchestidae (’marsupial tapirs’). Firstly, there is no fossil evidence of either family known from New Guinea. The middle manual and pedal phalanges of the only known late Quaternary thylacoleonid, Thylacoleo carnifex, are much more elongate than the Taora specimen. The manual and pedal phalanges of the two known late Quaternary palorchestids, Palorchestes azael and Palorchestes parvus40 are larger than the Taora specimen, the former considerably so, and in addition are dorsopalmarly taller and more transversely compressed relative to length. The proximal and middle manual and pedal phalanges of Thylacinus cynocephalus, which is known from Pleistocene and Holocene deposits in Highland New Guinea, are considerably longer and more gracile than this specimen, excluding this species from consideration.

Two species of diprotodontid are known from the Pleistocene of New Guinea: Maokopia ronaldi and Hulitherium tomasettii. Size alone is a strong indicator that the Taora specimen does not belong to the 200+ kg New Guinean members of this family. Although manual material has not been described from any of the Plio-Pleistocene New Guinean diprotodontids, material from Pliocene and Pleistocene Australian diprotodontids preserves middle manual phalanges that are rounded rather than dorsopalmarly compressed, being deep at both the proximal and distal ends, and are notably less symmetrical.

Overall, the proportions and morphology of the Taora phalanx are only consistent with those of the manual or pedal phalanges of macropodines (subfamily Macropodinae, the ‘modern’ kangaroos and wallabies). Many members of this taxon were also eliminated on anatomical grounds. The specimen’s size and proportions are comparable with those of the second, third or fourth medial manual phalanges or the middle phalanx of the fifth toe. Some members of subfamily Sthenurinae, the extinct ‘short-faced’ kangaroos, were of comparable size and are known from late Quaternary deposits. However, for a few reasons, the Taora specimen is considered confidently non-sthenurine. Sthenurines are not known from New Guinea; the middle manual phalanges of sthenurine species are considerably more elongate than the specimen in question; and late Pleistocene sthenurines possess a highly reduced fifth pedal digit41,42.

The proximal facet in the Taora specimen is separated into two slightly medially and laterally tilted sections with a corresponding dorsal peak along the midline. This feature is typical of the middle manual phalanges of macropodines, and is typically absent from the fifth middle manual phalanx. In macropodines, the proximal and medial phalanx can be further distinguished by an elongate versus dorsopalmarly compressed form; the shape of the proximodorsal articular rim (flat in the former vs medially peaked in the latter); and the development of the palmar processes (much greater in the former). Manual and pedal medial phalanges can generally be distinguished on the basis of the former’s much lesser size and degree of elongation alone, these proportions being strikingly different in all taxa aside from the tree-kangaroos (species in the extant genus Dendrolagus and the extinct Bohra). The macropodid pedal manual phalanx is also relatively flat palmarly, whilst the manual is typically more concave.

The Taora phalanx is much larger than corresponding medial manual phalanges II-IV of any extant New Guinean macropodid (Supplementary Table 7), the largest of which by body mass is Notamacropus agilis, although this taxon is found only south of the Central Cordillera. The largest macropodid presently found in northern New Guinea is the grizzled tree-kangaroo Dendrolagus inustus. The Taora specimen is readily distinguished from all tree-kangaroos (subtribe Dendrolagina) including the large, extinct Pliocene taxon Bohra on the basis that this group exhibits relatively elongated medial manual phalanges on digits II-IV. Two other extant New Guinean genera, Dorcopsis and Dorcopsulus, exhibit a superficially similar broad, dorsopalmarly compressed medial manual phalanx morphology to Protemnodon, but cannot be mistaken for this taxon, also on the basis of their vastly smaller size and lack of any prominent eminences above the collateral fossae.

Just four extant Australian species are large enough to seriously entertain an affiliation with the Taora specimen: Osphranter robustus (common wallaroo), Osphranter rufus (red kangaroo), Macropus fuliginosus (western grey kangaroo), and Macropus giganteus (eastern grey kangaroo). The second to fourth middle manual phalanges of these species possess a similarly deep proximal end to the Taora specimen, and although they approach in approximate dimensions the Taora specimen, several morphological traits clearly differentiate the latter from any of these taxa.

The species in Macropus and Osphranter are more gracile, with smaller muscle attachment tubercles, more separated medial and lateral sections of the proximal facet, and much shallower collateral ligament facets lacking a raised proximal margin. Other differentiating traits of the Taora specimen versus the extant Macropus-Osphranter clade include a very short height relative to breadth giving a dorsopalmarly compressed proportion, and the enlarged, medially- and laterally- projecting eminences above the collateral fossae, features which are characteristic of Protemnodon but absent in other macropodine taxa.

Morphological comparison to known macropodids

The Taora phalanx was compared on the basis of sixteen linear measurements to a diverse sample of both extant and extinct macropodids from New Guinea and Australia (Supplementary Table 7). Measurements were taken with digital calipers by LGK and IARK. Since the anatomy of the medial manual phalanges of Protemnodon is variable between species we took measurements of digits II-IV. Digits I and V were ruled out for comparison: in macropodids, the medial manual phalanx does not exist for digit I (the proximal phalanx joins directly to the distal phalanx), and in digit V it is consistently much smaller and less symmetrical compared to digits II-IV, and could be easily excluded from consideration on this basis.

PCA and Euclidean-distance based neighbour-joining trees were generated to identify nearest matches to the Taora specimen from these measurements using Rstudio43 (version 2025.09.2 + 418) using the package ape44 (version 5.8-1). Pairwise Euclidean distances are the straight-line distances between two specimens in morphospace (here, along 16 dimensions) calculated between the square root of the sum of squared standardised measurement values for each specimen. Euclidean distances therefore can be interpreted as a measure of overall morphological similarity between specimens.

The neighbour-joining tree then uses Euclidean distances to identify the nearest pair of specimens to one another (forming a “node”), and to iteratively join nodes with the criterion of minimising branch length. The neighbour-joining tree is an unrooted clustering method that does not assume any phylogenetic relations; it only associates specimens based on morphological similarity.
