Plasma S100 Calcium‐Binding Protein A8/A9 and Cognitive Impairment After Acute Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Dahong Zheng, Xiangyan Yin, Yi Chen, Xiaoxiao Wang, Jiaojiao Wang, Zhengbao Zhu, Daoxia Guo, Yonghong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundS100A8/A9 (S100 calcium‐binding protein A8/A9) was suggested to play pathophysiological roles in the amyloid–neuroinflammatory cascade, contributing to cognitive impairment. However, little is known about the impacts of S100A8/A9 on poststroke cognitive impairment. We aimed to prospectively investigate the relationships between baseline plasma S100A8/A9 levels with cognitive impairment among patients with acute ischemic stroke.MethodsOur prospective cohort study included patients with acute ischemic stroke from an ancillary study of CATIS (China Antihypertensive Trial in Acute Ischemic Stroke). At the 3‐month follow‐up, cognitive impairment was evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment and the Mini‐Mental State Examination. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to estimate the association between S100A8/A9 and the risk of poststroke cognitive impairment (PSCI).ResultsA total of 618 participants were included, of whom 405 (65.5%) met the criteria for PSCI on the basis of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, and 323 (52.3%) on the basis of the Mini‐Mental State Examination. After adjusting for potential confounding factors, especially high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein, the odds ratios of PSCI for participants in the highest tertile of plasma S100A8/A9 levels were 2.27 (95% CI, 1.37–3.75) for Montreal Cognitive Assessment–defined PSCI and 1.62 (95% CI, 1.01–2.60) for Mini‐Mental State Examination–defined PSCI, compared with the lowest tertile. We observed that higher baseline levels of S100A8/A9 were associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment in a linear dose–response manner (P for linearity=0.030 for Montreal Cognitive Assessment; P for linearity=0.018 for Mini‐Mental State Examination).ConclusionsHigher baseline plasma levels of S100A8/A9 were associated with an increased risk of PSCI, suggesting the potential role of S100A8/A9 as a valuable predictive biomarker for PSCI.

Poststroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) is a common complication for stroke survivors. Approximately 1 in 3 patients develops mild cognitive impairment, 1 and 1 in 4 experiences severe cognitive impairment. 2 Given the high incidence of cognitive impairment after stroke and heavy burden for patients and caregivers, 3 , 4 it is urgent to identify reliable and effective risk factors for cognitive impairment.

The pathophysiological mechanisms of cognitive impairment after stroke remain unclear; however, evidence suggests that a vicious cycle involving the amyloid–neuroinflammatory cascade may impair cognitive functions. 5 S100A8 and S100A9 are cytosolic proteins predominantly expressed by white blood cells, particularly monocytes and neutrophils. 6 , 7 , 8 Under physiological conditions, these 2 proteins mainly exist and function as a heterodimeric complex, S100A8/A9 (S100 calcium‐binding protein A8/A9). 9 , 10 S100A8/A9 is a key protein complex involved in the regulation of inflammation, acting by enhancing TLR4 (Toll‐like receptor 4)/NF‐κB (nuclear factor κB) signaling pathway and promoting the generation of proinflammatory cytokines. Central administration of S100A8/A9 resulted in neuroinflammation, 11 which might influence the progression of neurodegenerative diseases by creating a pathogenic microenvironment in brain tissue and further promoting the aggregation of amyloid. 12 In addition, S100A9 is highly amyloidogenic under physiological conditions and undergoes amyloid formation through a nucleation‐dependent, autocatalytic growth mechanism. 12 Elevated S100A8 expression has been reported to exacerbate neuronal inflammation by promoting the formation of amyloid β plaques. 13 Therefore, S100A8 and S100A9 may act as triggers and important contributors to plaque formation and neurotoxicity, ultimately leading to cognitive deficits. 5 , 14 Collectively, these findings suggest that S100A8/A9 may play an important role in cognitive impairments by promoting inflammation and facilitating plaque formation. Research also indicates that S100A8 and S100A9 may directly cause cognitive dysfunction. Upregulation of S100A9 has been implicated in learning and memory impairments by promoting microglia M1 polarization in sepsis survivors, 15 and exogenous S100A9 administration can induce spatial memory impairment in mice. 16

Although existing research has demonstrated the potential predictive capability of S100A8/A9 to predict adverse outcomes after a stroke, 17 , 18 population‐based studies on the relationship between S100A8/A9 and cognitive impairment are scarce, particularly among patients with stroke.

We therefore investigated the association between plasma S100A8/A9 levels and subsequent cognitive impairment following acute ischemic stroke onset within a prospective cohort from the CATIS (China Antihypertensive Trial in Acute Ischemic Stroke) study.

Methods

Data described in the article, code book, and analytic code will be made available upon request, pending approval by the authors.

Study Design and Participants

This prospective observational study was based on a preplanned ancillary study, which was designed to test whether antihypertension would reduce the risk of cognitive impairment at 3 months among a subsample of CATIS. The details for both studies have been described previously. 19 , 20 CATIS was a multicenter, single‐blinded, randomized controlled clinical trial that aimed to evaluate whether immediate lowering of blood pressure (BP) within 48 hours after the onset of acute stroke would reduce death and major disability at 14 days or hospital discharge. From August 2009 to May 2013, 4071 patients aged ≥22 years who had ischemic stroke confirmed by computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging of the brain within 48 hours of symptom onset and who had systolic BP between 140 and 220 mm Hg were recruited. Patients with severe heart failure, acute myocardial infarction or unstable angina, atrial fibrillation, aortic dissection, cerebrovascular stenosis, or resistant hypertension; who were in a deep coma; or who were treated with intravenous thrombolytic therapy were excluded from the CATIS trial.

From August 2009 to November 2012, 660 CATIS trial participants were systematically selected before randomization from 7 participating hospitals for cognitive function assessment at their 3‐month follow‐up visit. Each of the 7 hospitals recruited 80 to 100 patients consecutively. At the 3‐month visit, 15 patients were lost to follow‐up, and 7 patients were deceased. A total of 638 patients completed the cognitive assessment. We further excluded 20 participants because they refused to offer blood samples or some collected samples were hemolyzed in storage or transport. A total of 618 participants were finally included in the present analysis (Figure 1).

Assessment of Plasma S100A8/A9 Concentrations and Potential Covariates

Fasting blood samples were drawn within 24 hours of patients' hospital admission. Plasma samples were separated and frozen at −80 °C in the Central Laboratory of the School of Public Health at Soochow University until testing. Plasma S100A8/A9 concentrations were measured using a commercially available ELISA kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN). Intra‐ and interassay coefficients of variation were 2.0% and 6.9%, respectively. Laboratory technicians responsible for performing the S100A8/A9 determinations were unaware of the baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes of the participants.

Data on demographic characteristics, medical history, clinical features, and subtypes of ischemic stroke were obtained at enrollment using a standard questionnaire by trained interviewers. Current cigarette smoking was defined as smoking ≥1 cigarette per day for at least 1 year, and current alcohol drinking means consumption of any type of alcoholic beverage ≥12 times in the past year. Plasma glucose and other clinical laboratory measurements were performed on fresh blood samples at admission. Three BP measurements were performed at baseline by trained nurses, according to a common protocol adapted from procedures recommended by the American Heart Association, 21 and we used the mean of 3 measurements in the analysis. Hypertension was defined as BP ≥140/90 mm Hg on repeated measurements or use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose level ≥120 mg/dL or use of antidiabetic drugs. Hyperlipidemia was defined as total cholesterol measurement ≥240 mg/dL, high‐density lipoprotein measurement <35 mg/dL, or use of lipid‐lowering agents. Physical activity was calculated from self‐reported durations of vigorous, moderate, and light activities and categorized into low, moderate, or high levels according to World Health Organization guidelines. 22 Stroke severity was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) by trained neurologists at admission. 23 In addition, NIHSS Cog‐4 is a composite score based on 4 NIHSS items, which could provide an indication of cognitive functioning in the absence of a Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE). 24 TOAST (Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) criteria was used to classify the ischemic stroke subtypes as large‐artery atherosclerosis (thrombotic), cardiac embolism (embolic), 25 and small‐vessel occlusion (lacunar), according to clinical characteristics and imaging data of the patients.

Assessment of Study Outcome

The study outcome was cognitive impairment at 3 months, assessed by trained neurologists using the MMSE and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). 26 , 27 The MMSE comprises 20 items evaluating cognitive domains including orientation, registration, attention and calculation, recall, language, and visual construction. 26 The MoCA is a 30‐item test assessing 7 cognitive domains: visuospatial/executive functions, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction, and orientation. 27 Previous studies have shown that individuals with ≤12 years of education tend to perform worse on the MoCA; to correct for educational effects, 1 point was added for participants with education <12 years on their total MoCA score (if <30). 27 Both the MMSE and MoCA have been translated into Chinese and validated as screening tools for cognitive impairment and dementia in the Chinese population; the maximum score for both assessments is 30. According to recommended cutoffs, MMSE scores are classified as 0 to 22 (severe cognitive impairment), 23 to 26 (mild cognitive impairment), and 27 to 30 (no cognitive impairment), whereas MoCA scores are classified as 0 to 19 (severe cognitive impairment), 20 to 24 (mild cognitive impairment), and 25 to 30 (no cognitive impairment). 28 , 29 In the present analysis, cognitive impairment was defined as MMSE score <27 28 , 29 , 30 or MoCA score <25. 29 , 31

Statistical Analysis

All participants were divided into 3 subgroups according to tertiles of plasma S100A8/A9 concentration. We used the generalized linear regression analysis to test for trend across the S100A8/A9 tertiles for continuous variables, and the Cochran–Armitage trend χ2 test was applied for categorical variables.

Logistic regression models were used to assess the association between plasma S100A8/A9 levels and cognitive status at 3 months. Binary logistic regression was applied to evaluate the risk of PSCI, whereas ordinal logistic regression was used to examine the association between S100A8/A9 levels and the severity of cognitive impairment. Plasma S100A8/A9 concentrations were categorized into tertiles, and odds ratios with 95% CIs were calculated by comparing the higher tertiles (tertiles 2 and 3) with the lowest tertile (tertile 1) as the reference group. We performed 3 multiple adjusted logistic regression models. Model 1 was an unadjusted model. Model 2 adjusted for age, sex, current cigarette smoking, current alcohol drinking, systolic BP, fasting plasma glucose, estimated glomerular filtration rate, antihypertensive treatment after admission to a hospital, baseline NIHSS score, NIHSS Cog‐4 score, time from stroke onset to randomization, medical history (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, coronary heart disease, and family history of stroke), ischemic stroke subtype, and use of hypoglycemic therapy. Model 3 adjusted the factors included in model 2 as well as hsCRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein). The selection of covariates was based on prior studies. 32 , 33 Second, the median of each group of plasma S100A8/A9 values was used as a predictor to test the linear trend among groups. Third, we also logarithm‐transformed the levels of plasma S100A8/A9, and odds ratio (95% CI) per 1‐SD increment of transformed plasma S100A8/A9 was calculated.

Additionally, subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the potential effect on the association between S100A8/A9 and cognitive impairment, using the following modifiers: age, sex, systolic BP, admission NIHSS score and Cog‐4 score, history of hypertension, family history of stroke, time from stroke onset to randomization, and receiving immediate BP reduction. Interactions between plasma S100A8/A9 concentrations and subgroup variables on the risk of cognitive impairment were tested by the likelihood ratio test with adjustment for the aforementioned covariates unless the variable was used as a subgroup variable.

Moreover, we used restricted cubic spline models to provide more precise estimates and to explore the shapes of the relationships between plasma S100A8/A9 and cognitive impairment with 4 knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles). 34 We also performed a sensitivity analysis, additionally adjusting for history of antiplatelet therapy, lipid‐lowering medication use, and physical activity level in addition to model 3.

Statistical analysis was conducted using the SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC); all P values were 2‐tailed, and a significance level of 0.05 was used.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registration, and Patient Consent

This study was approved by the institutional review boards at Soochow University in China and Tulane University in the United States, as well as ethical committees at the participating hospitals. CATIS is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier NCT01840072). All participants provided written informed consent.

Discussion

In this prospective study, we observed that participants with higher plasma S100A8/A9 level were significantly associated with increased risk and severity of cognitive impairment after acute ischemic stroke, even after adjustment for traditional risk factors. In addition, S100A8/A9 shows a positive dose–response relationship with PSCI. The significant association remained across different subgroups. These findings suggested plasma S100A8/A9 might be a risk factor for cognitive impairment after ischemic stroke and then could provide important predictive information for the risk and severity of PSCI.

Our study demonstrated that higher baseline levels of S100A8/A9 in patients with acute ischemic stroke were associated with an increased risk of PSCI at 3 months. An accumulation of S100A8‐positive microglia in the hippocampus was observed, which correlated with cognitive decline in tauopathy models. 35 Similarly, administration of exogenous S100A9 induced memory deficits through enhanced hippocampal glutamate release and dopaminergic disruption in aged brains. 16 These findings are consistent with our results and suggest that S100A8 and S100A9 play critical roles in neurodegeneration and cognitive impairment. Furthermore, S100A9 knockout improved spatial memory in Alzheimer disease mouse models, 36 pointing to its potential as a therapeutic target for poststroke dementia. However, research on vascular dementia remains limited. Since PSCI is considered a subtype of vascular cognitive impairment, our findings highlight the potential of future investigations into S100A8/A9‐targeted therapies for vascular dementia.

Previous studies have explored the association between S100A8/A9 and cardiovascular diseases. For instance, elevated S100A8/A9 levels have been shown to predict adverse cardiovascular outcomes in older adults. 37 Our prior research demonstrated that S100A8/A9 is involved in inflammatory mechanisms linked to atherosclerosis and plaque vulnerability, 18 and that there was a linear dose–response relationship, with higher S100A8/A9 levels predicting greater risk of death or major disability at 3 months after stroke. 17 Building upon this evidence, our current study is the first to extend these findings specifically to cognitive outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke.

The mechanisms by which plasma S100A8/A9 affects cognitive function after ischemic stroke remain unclear, but several potential pathophysiological pathways have been suggested. S100A8/A9 is known as an important proinflammatory factor. Central administration of S100A8, S100A9, or their heterodimer has been shown to activate the TLR4/NF‐κB signaling pathway and trigger neuroinflammation. 11 Neuroinflammation, in turn, is implicated in neurodegeneration by creating a toxic microenvironment and accelerating amyloid deposition. 12 , 38 S100A9 is highly amyloidogenic and contributes to plaque formation through nucleation‐dependent aggregation. 12 This makes S100A9 a trigger and an important contributor to plaque formation and neurotoxicity. 5 Similarly, elevated S100A8 expression promotes amyloid β plaque development and exacerbates neuronal damage. 39 These findings suggest that S100A8 and S100A9 may influence PSCI through inflammatory and Alzheimer disease–like pathological processes.

Given the pivotal role of S100A8/A9 in inflammation, we found that S100A8/A9 remained significantly associated with PSCI even after adjusting for CRP, suggesting that S100A8/A9 may independently serve as an inflammatory predictor of PSCI. Previous studies have shown that S100A8/A9 correlates more strongly with composite indices of inflammatory disease activity than CRP. 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 Similarly, we did not observe a significant association between CRP and PSCI (Table S2), underscoring the potential value of S100A8/A9 as a clinical biomarker.

Our study has important clinical implications and offers new perspectives on the role of S100A8/A9 in predicting PSCI. Previous research has shown that S100A9 inhibitors can alleviate the severity of colitis as well as systemic and neuroinflammation. 45 A vaccine targeting S100A9 was found to reduce thrombus formation in mice without increasing the risk of bleeding. 46 Moreover, blocking S100A8/A9 had shown effectiveness in reducing infarct volume, vascular inflammation, and neurologic deficits. 46 , 47 , 48 Therefore, clinical trials are needed to assess the effects of S100A8/A9 inhibition on cognitive impairment in patients with acute ischemic stroke.

There are several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, participants were drawn from a substudy of the CATIS trial, where patients with BP ≥220/120 mm Hg or those receiving intravenous thrombolytic therapy were excluded. This limits the generalizability of our study findings to all patients with acute ischemic stroke. However, it is reported that in China, patients with ischemic stroke with systolic BP ≥220/120 mm Hg who receive intravenous thrombolytic therapy are rare. Moreover, the baseline characteristics of participants in our study are similar to those in the China National Stroke Registry. Second, due to the observational study design, we are unable to establish a causal relationship between S100A8/A9 and PSCI. Third, we measured only baseline plasma S100A8/A9 levels. The lack of serial measurements (eg, at 1 week and 6 months) limits our ability to examine the dynamic changes of S100A8/A9 and their potential relationship with the progression of PSCI. Fourth, there remains a possibility of residual or unmeasured confounding (eg, dietary patterns) in this observational study, which should be considered when interpreting the results.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that elevated levels of plasma S100A8/A9 were associated with increased risk of PSCI. A significant linear relationship was observed between plasma S100A8/A9 and the occurrence of PSCI at 3 months, suggesting that S100A8/A9 is a potential biomarker associated with cognitive impairment following ischemic stroke. Further prospective research with larger cohorts is required to validate the clinical significance of S100A8/A9.
