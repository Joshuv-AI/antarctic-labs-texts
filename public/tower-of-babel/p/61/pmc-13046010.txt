Laparoscopic nephroureterectomy in Prune Belly Syndrome: a case report
Authors: Serena Reali, Leila Hartford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:The use of therapeutic laparoscopy is increasingly common in managing undescended testes in patients with Prune Belly Syndrome (PBS). We report, for the first time, a case of a child with PBS who underwent right laparoscopic nephroureterectomy.Case Presentation:A 7-year-old male with PBS, who had previously undergone multiple surgeries, was referred to our center. The patient presented to us with a grade 4 chronic kidney disease, a non-functioning right kidney, and a history of recurrent urinary tract infections. A laparoscopic right nephroureterectomy was performed.Discussion:Surgical details and technical aspects of the procedure are described, with particular reference to the unique challenges posed by the absence of abdominal wall musculature in this patient population.Conclusion:This case demonstrates that laparoscopic nephroureterectomy is both feasible and safe in patients with PBS. With appropriate technical adjustments, the lack of abdominal musculature does not preclude the use of laparoscopy in these individuals.

Introduction

Prune Belly Syndrome (PBS) is a rare congenital disorder characterized by a classic triad of features: deficiency of the abdominal musculature, bilateral intra-abdominal testes, and urinary tract abnormalities. The condition may vary in severity. The incidence is 3.8 cases/100 000 live births and is more common in infants of African descent[1]. Females account for only about 5% of affected patients[2]. Several theories have been proposed to explain the aetiology of PBS[2,3]. The two most widely accepted pathogenic hypotheses include: a transient urethral obstruction leading to bladder distension, with secondary effects on the development of the urinary tract, abdominal wall musculature, and testicular descent; and a primary defect in mesodermal development. The use of minimally invasive surgery in PBS has gained popularity in recent years, particularly for the treatment of cryptorchidism[5–8]. This report presents the first case of laparoscopic nephroureterectomy in a patient with PBS, focusing on the surgical technique.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist[9].

Case presentation

A 7-year-old male with PBS was referred to our Paediatric Surgical Clinic. He had previously undergone multiple surgeries at another institution. Diagnosed at birth with PBS, the patient presented with suspected urethral atresia, megacystis, and a patent urachus. A vesicostomy was performed during the perinatal period. At 1 year of age, cystoscopy revealed a posterior urethral stenosis. He subsequently underwent circumcision and a Sober-Y cutaneous left ureterostomy. At age three, he had vesicostomy closure, bilateral orchidopexy, and urethrotomy of the posterior urethra.

At referral, the patient was known to have grade 4 chronic kidney disease, a non-functioning right kidney (0% function on the most recent MAG-3 scan), and left hydronephrosis with a renal pelvis measuring 16.7 mm. He had a history of recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs) requiring multiple hospital admissions for intravenous antibiotics. Clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) was performed every 4 hours.

Cystoscopy revealed a tortuous urethra with no strictures. A voiding cystourethrogram showed a megacystis (450 mL of contrast instilled) and bilateral vesicoureteral reflux (VUR): the right ureter was dilated and tortuous with a blind-ending proximal segment (grade I VUR); the left distal ureter was dilated and tortuous, with contrast observed in the ureterostomy bag. No voiding phase was captured.

Following evaluation and after informed consent was obtained from the parents, the patient was admitted for a right nephrouterectomy. At admission, he was 8 years old and weighed 18 kg. Routine preoperative blood investigations were performed. A laparoscopic right nephroureterectomy was carried out.

Under general anesthesia, the patient was intubated, and a transurethral catheter was placed. He was positioned in the left lateral decubitus with the right flank elevated. Four 5-mm ports were used (Fig. 1). Figure 1.Trocar placement.

The first port was inserted in a supraumbilical position using the open Hasson technique for camera access. This technique consists of a skin incision (subumbilical, supraumbilical, or infraumbilical), followed by layer-by-layer dissection. The fascial sheath is then opened, and the trocar is placed under direct visualization. Pneumoperitoneum was established at 10 mmHg. Additional ports were inserted: one at the spinoumbilical line, another in the subcostal area, and the hepatic retraction port in the epigastrium. A 5-mm 30° laparoscope was used.

Intraoperative findings included a right pelvic-ureteric junction (PUJ) obstruction, a small dysplastic kidney, and a dilated distal ureter. The ascending colon was non-fixed. Mobilization of the hepatic flexure was unnecessary, as the right ureter, right gonadal and renal vessels, and inferior vena cava were clearly visible (Fig. 2). The mid-ureter was identified, isolated, suspended, and followed cranially to the renal hilum. Gerota’s fascia was opened, and the renal vessels were isolated, clipped, and divided. The kidney was mobilized, and the ureter was dissected caudally to the bladder, where it was divided. An endoloop was applied to close the distal ureteral stump. The kidney was extracted through an extended umbilical port site. Routine closure was performed. The operative time was 120 minutes. No significant intraoperative blood losses were recorded. The patient was extubated in the operating room and returned to the surgical ward. Intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis with amoxicillin–clavulanic acid was administered and continued for 24 hours. The transurethral catheter was removed on postoperative day 1, and CIC was resumed. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 2. The patient had no documented repeat UTIs in the subsequent 6 months postoperatively, with stable serum creatinine levels.

Histological analysis confirmed renal dysplasia and hydroureter. The ureter measured 90 mm, with a distal segment of 70 × 10 × 10 mm and a narrowed proximal segment of 20 × 3 × 3 mm (Fig. 3). In our patient, no abdominoplasty was performed because of the mild degree of dysmorphism and the absence of related symptoms. Further evaluation with urodynamic study would be advisable before considering reconstruction of the left urinary tract, including possible ureteral tapering and ureterovesical reimplantation. However, given the presence of a solitary kidney and advanced renal impairment (stage IV chronic kidney disease), we believe that maintaining urinary diversion represents the safest management strategy for this child.

Discussion

PBS is frequently associated with urinary tract abnormalities, often requiring surgical management. This report is the first to describe laparoscopic nephroureterectomy in a patient with PBS, with emphasis on the surgical technique.

Docimo et al[5] were the first to report the use of therapeutic laparoscopy in PBS, performing bilateral single-stage orchidopexy. Since then, other authors have described laparoscopic approaches for intra-abdominal undescended testes[6–8], including a series of 11 patients undergoing first-stage Fowler-Stephens orchidopexy at one center[7].

A laparoscopic approach has also been described for the management of abdominal wall defects. Franco et al[10] reported a modification of the Firlit abdominal wall plication, introducing laparoscopy to delineate the topography of muscular deficiency and to perform plication under direct visualization, thereby reducing the risk of bowel injury during the procedure. Smith et al[11] similarly used laparoscopy to map the areas requiring plication and to evaluate the anatomy of the epigastric vessels with a modified Monfort technique. More recently, Lopes et al[12] reported the first laparoscopic robot-assisted abdominoplasty in an adult patient with PBS, utilizing mesh reinforcement for repair of lateral abdominal wall bulging.

Three additional reports in the literature describe minimally invasive surgery in PBS patients. Wille et al[13] reported on robotic-assisted laparoscopic Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy. Tran et al[14] presented a case of urgent laparoscopic reduction of splenic torsion, with conversion to open surgery for a Ladd’s procedure due to malrotation. Loganathan et al[15] described a left nephroureterectomy with closure of a prior ureterostomy in a patient with pseudo-PBS, but without focusing on technical aspects.

A hallmark of PBS is deficient abdominal musculature, which necessitates specific adjustments in laparoscopic technique. In this case, the first trocar was placed via open access using the Hasson technique, with subsequent ports placed under direct vision. Due to poor abdominal wall tension and prior surgeries, we considered this method safer than the Veress needle, although some authors have successfully used the Veress approach[5,6,8]. Saxena et al, in their larger case series, found the open technique to be safest and most technically feasible in PBS[7].

A pneumoperitoneum pressure of 10 mmHg provided adequate working space, which appeared enhanced by the compliant abdominal wall. Standard paediatric laparoscopic instruments were sufficient, contrary to reports by Saxena et al[7]. Careful port incision sizing eliminated the need for port fixation.

Gastrointestinal anomalies, particularly fixation defects such as malrotation or absent fixation of the colon, are recognized in PBS[16]. In our patient, the cecum and ascending colon were not fixed, which obviated the need to mobilize the hepatic flexure and thus reduced operative time.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates the feasibility and safety of laparoscopic nephroureterectomy in PBS. The absence of abdominal musculature does not preclude successful minimally invasive surgery in these patients.
