Comprehensive Comparative Study of the Durability Wear Assessment of a Diesel Engine Fuelled with Jatropha Seed Oil and Diesel Fuel and Its Troubleshooting and Scheduled Maintenance
Authors: Pasupuleti Subrahmanya Ranjit, Zeeshan Ahmed, Swapnil Sureshchandra Bhurat, Veeresh Babu Alur, Elumalai Perumal Venkatesan, Olusegun David Samuel, Christopher Enweremadu, Algam Sai Kumar, Prabhakar Sekar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractExisting ecofriendly apprehensions about climate change have directed scientists to discover plant-based vegetable oils for use as fuels, such as straight vegetable oils and their biodiesels, because of their renewability, nontoxic nature, biodegradability, and environmental friendliness. This experimental study intended to reveal the tribological aspects of 90 °C preheated Jatropha curcas straight vegetable oil (PHSVO90) used in a 7.35 kW, 1000 rpm constant speed indirect injection (IDI) diesel engine and likened to conventional diesel operation by conducting an elongated term durability examination for 512 h as per IS:10000 standards. Tests were performed under encoded loading cycles in two segments: one with PHSVO90 and the second one with conventional diesel operation (CDO). Following the completion of these tests, all essential engine components were disassembled to assess physical deterioration and the accumulation of carbon deposits. Due to their different chemical compositions, PHSVO90 and diesel showed noticeable differences in their properties. PHSVO90 had a higher amount of carbon deposits, measuring 6.3 g. It also had a total acid number (TAN) of 9.11 mg KOH/g, a density of 986 kg/m3, and a viscosity of 381 cSt. In addition, an inductive plasma-based atomic emission spectroscope detected a greater amount of metal wear debris from spectrum metals with PHSVO90. The elements Fe, Cr, Al, Cu, Mg, and Pb exhibited concentrations 1.49×, 1.37×, 1.19×, 1.37×, 1.2×, and 1.18× higher, respectively, than those recorded during typical diesel operation. Wear of PHSVO90 fuel engine components like the cylinder head, cylinder bore/liner, piston, Gudgeon pin, small end bush of the connecting rod, big end bearing, crankshaft bearing, connecting rod bearing, and inlet and exhaust valves, along with guides, was detected to be marginally advanced compared to CDO. As per identified results, an appropriate maintenance protocol was developed for the PHSVO90 application. It is observed that, without any extensive modifications, PHSVO90 oil has proven to be a good substitute for IDI engines.

Introduction

The need to meet the energy requirement, provide employment, and reduce emissions had motivated the Union Cabinet Ministry to renew the National Policy on Biofuels in India (team product line, 2018). Further, the same policy also satisfies the current government initiatives like Make in India, Swachh Bharat Abhiyan and Skill Development, and National Rural Livelihood Mission. Along these lines, nonedible seed oils (NESOs) like Jatropha curcas, polanga, palm, babassu, castor, jojoba, and tung are preferred to replace the existing conventional regular petro-diesel fuel.

Among these NESOs, the Indian Government has expressed interest in using Jatropha curcas seed oil due to its capacity to flourish in dry and semiarid climates, higher yield, low maintenance, and physical and chemical qualities that are similar to diesel fuel (DF). India’s Union Cabinet Ministry has updated the National Policy on Biofuels for the year 2018, which has sparked a lot of interest in biofuels. This not only promotes the use of alternative fuels to meet energy needs and minimize emissions but also encourages local employment. Further, more than 5 tonnes of CO2 can be sequestrated by Jatropha curcas, which is an added advantage.1 India already took initiative to check the evaluation of CO2 sequestration by Jatropha curcas a decade ago.2 Even its biomass after extraction of the oil can be used as a biofertilizer.3 Further, life cycle assessment (LCA) and energy return on investment (EROI) suggest that making use of direct Jatropha curcas rather than its derived biodiesel is preferred due to its process time and cost involvement. Most of the researchers executed their work with Jatropha curcas straight vegetable oil and showed that the performance of the engine is on par with that of the diesel engine.4−12 However, due to the free fatty acid (FFA) content and high viscosity, which is nearly 10–12× that of conventional diesel at 40 °C, it needs to be verified with endurance tests.

Hence, comprehensive research is required to understand the degradation mechanisms of preheated Jatropha curcas straight vegetable oil (PHSVO) engines given the paucity of studies on these aspects. The deterioration and longevity of engine components are contingent on the quality of the lubricating oil and its usage. Lubricating oil becomes contaminated due to fuel dilution and abrasion particles. The dilution of the lubricating oil is influenced by the physical characteristics of the fuel. The effect of Jatropha curcas PHSVO on the degradation of the lubricating oil must therefore be investigated.

In addition, spectroscopy is the most effective technique for quantifying the amount of wear debris present in the lubricating oil. A diverse range of spectrometers can be employed for the assessment of metal wear debris using techniques such as atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), rotary-based electrode spectrometry (RDE)/atomic emission spectrometry (AES), inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES)/atomic emission spectroscopy (AES), or X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF), among others.13,14 A good quantity of endurance tests ranging from 100 to 512 h on various methyl ester blends (ROME, LOME) and SVO and its blends (Karanja) with diesel were conducted in the Engine Research Laboratory of IIT Kanpur by Avinash Kumar Agarwal and his team.15−20 The results revealed that biodiesel derived from SVOs showed good endurance aspects. Masjuki et al.21 conducted a 24 h endurance test with POME on a constant speed engine. Wear debris of the POME oil-based lubricating oil is less than that of the conventional diesel operation. On another hand, Fazal et al.22 revealed that engines operated with palm oil showed high deterioration of the lubricating oil compared to diesel operation. In another study, İlker Temizer, and Ayşegül Arı23 conducted long-term endurance testing on a diesel engine using diesel and 90% diesel/10% bioethanol fuels. The test engine ran on two fuels for 110 h at part load. EDX and SEM were used to analyze renewed rings in each fueled engine operation. Results were compared to those of the new piston ring. Analysis revealed increased abrasive wear on the initial rings for both fuels. A study indicated that diesel/bioethanol blend gasoline resulted in shorter wear lines at engine rings compared with diesel fuel. Due to its low carbon (C) concentration, bioethanol reduces ring wear compared to diesel fuel. Jai Gopal Gupta and Avinash Kumar Agarwal24 investigated the long-term compatibility of Karanja oil methyl ester (biodiesel) blends with existing common rail direct injection diesel engines in SUVs. A 274 h endurance test was done in two phases, with baseline mineral diesel and a 20% v/v Karanja biodiesel blend (KB20). After completing the first phase, the engine was dismantled, and the components were tested for carbon deposits and wear using a variety of techniques. To proceed with the endurance test for KB20, new engine components were installed, and the test was conducted. The KB20-fueled engine had decreased valve and crankpin wear but increased wear on liners, piston rings, pistons, the gudgeon pin, connecting rod end bearings, and main bearings. İlker Temizer and Bünyamin Eskici25 performed experiments utilizing diesel (D100), canola oil methyl ester (B10) in a 10% volume ratio, and canola oil methyl ester (B20) in a 20% volume ratio. The engine was then operated for approximately 150 h under these distinct fuels as part of long-term endurance testing. Lubricating oil samples were collected from engines that had expended different types of fuel. In comparison to diesel fuel, the density, TBN value, and sulfate ash concentration of the lubricating oil were higher in B10. Conversely, the viscosity, flashpoint, TAN, and moisture content decreased. Inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) and inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analyses revealed that the engine lubricating fluid utilized in the B10-fueled operation contained greater concentrations of metals than that in the D100 operation. Jarernporn Thawornprasert and Arrisa Sopajarn et al.25 examined the effect of palm fatty acid distillate ethyl ester–hydrous ethanol, palm fatty acid distillate ethyl ester, and diesel fuels on an unmodified direct injection (DI) diesel engine. The diesel engine components were subjected to a 500 h wear test using D30PE60E10 diesel. According to the findings, biodiesel in fuel mixes has the potential to produce a thin coating on the metal surface of engine components, hence reducing wear. However, after 100 h of nonstop usage of the D30PE60E10 mix, the engine would not start again as the fuel pump’s plunger, barrel, delivery valve, and valve holder all had several pores. When this gasoline blend is used, nevertheless, several factors may need to be considered in order to avoid corrosion. Yohanes Gunawan et al.26 examined the technical viability of using CPO, degummed CPO, and bleached palm oil (BPO) fuels in endurance tests lasting 500 operating hours, with each fuel being tested on diesel engines with a capacity of 20 kW. They assessed the performance of several parameters and quantified the amount of fuel consumed, pollutants emitted, oil dilution, and carbon deposited. Engines using CPO, BPO, and degummed CPO diesel experienced increased specific fuel consumption, deposit formation, and emissions of CO compared to engines using a 30% FAME blend in HSD or biodiesel 30. Diesel engines have the option to utilize CPO (crude palm oil), BPO (biodiesel palm oil), and degummed CPO. However, the usage of these alternatives leads to an increase in both fuel consumption and maintenance costs. Konareddy Harshavardhan Reddy and Kasianantham Nanthagopal27 studied low-viscosity biofuels derived from waste biomass, which were compared using long-term durability testing. The biofuel was subjected to a two-stage, 256 h long-term endurance test in accordance with Indian Standard 10000, Part IX-1980 using 10% and 20% waste biomass by volume. We compared the findings to those of a regular diesel fuel. After 256 h of operation, this long-term endurance study examined performance, emissions, and habits. Damage to engine parts caused by the long-term endurance test reduced their performance. The carbon buildup would drastically diminish the efficiency of the engine. The degradation of the lubricating oil obviously lowers engine performance. Diesel engine emissions are much higher after the endurance test compared to new engines according to durability research. Reduced emissions of carbon monoxide and unburned hydrocarbons are two byproducts of biofuel operations that use waste biomass, with the exception of nitrogen oxides. When compared to diesel fuel, biofuel has cleaner combustion. Diesel fuel had a lower flash point and kinematic viscosity during endurance tests compared to biofuel made from waste biomass. Compared with a mixture of 10% lemon peel oil and 10% diesel fuel, 20% lemon peel oil contains 19% more ash. The increased oxidation rate in low-viscosity biofuel causes the total base number of lubricating oil to decrease, which means that the lube oil deteriorates at a faster pace. Reduced engine life is caused by lubricating oil degradation and wear and tear on engine elements. In comparison to other fuels, the lubricant made from a 20% mixture of lemon peel oil wears piston rings more rapidly. Lastly, diesel engines may benefit from enhanced low-viscosity biofuel, which currently has little wear resistance. İlker Temizer and Ayşegül Arı27 executed experiments on an engine that was 90% diesel and 10% bioethanol to perform endurance tests for an extended period of time. A direct injection diesel engine that ran on two different fuels for 110 h at partial load had its lubricating fluid and ring wear studied. Testing the engine lubricating oil over an extended period of time allowed scientists to determine its ash content, density, viscosity, acid number, and flash point values. The presence of metal residues (Fe, Al, Cu, Pb, Zn, Mg, Mn, and Ni) in the lubricating oil, as shown by further investigation, showed attrition. We used the inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry method to examine lubricating lubricant samples taken at 55 and 110 h. In addition, for each fuel combination, the replacement piston rings were subjected to morphological and tribological tests. The first, second, and third piston rings were analyzed using energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy and scanning electron microscopy. Then, the metal traces in the oil started to move forward. After 55 h, the study of metal residues in the lubricating oil with B10 showed a higher quantity compared to diesel. Lubricating oil has a TAN value of 3.08 mg of KOH per gram as per the D100 operation. Nevertheless, this value dropped to 3.29 mg of KOH per gram during the B10 process.

Tadeusz Dziubak and Sebastian Dominik Dziubak28 provide a comprehensive analysis of the scientific advancements in the area of the relationship between engine intake air pollution and the premature failure of specific engine parts, including the piston, rings, cylinder, and plain journal bearing. Internal combustion engines are susceptible to increased wear and decreased reliability and durability when road dust is introduced into the engine cylinders with the intake air. Between 60% and 95% of road dust is composed of the minerals silica (SiO2) and corundum (Al2O3). The oil-coated cylinder liner walls collect around 10–20% of the dust material that enters the engine through the intake system. Mineral dust particles with a size below 2–5 μm are used, as this is the degree of accuracy provided by filter materials made of cellulose. When oil and dust mix, they form an abrasive paste that wears down engine parts, such as the piston, rings, and cylinder wall, where it touches. Additionally, the impact of dust accumulation on the air flow monitoring sensor and its degradation in operational efficiency was assessed, since it affects the engine’s output. However, this type of investigation was not carried out directly in this work, since the endurance tests were done in an area with an air quality Index of less than 50. As the authors used a realistic strategy, this sort of study can be examined in the future. Moreover, it is essential to maintain the lubricating oil in proper conditions to increase the engine life as well as to reduce the friction between sliding parts of the engine. It is also noticed that more than 6% of operation cost is involved in maintaining the lubricating oil (LUO).29 Hence, it is essential to monitor the performance of the LUO for the given fuel, which also helps the predictive maintenance of the engine.30 Even the health of the engine is also diagnosed by the contamination and condition of the lubricating oil. In general, LUO condition monitoring is done basically in two ways. The first one is to measure the physical and chemical properties like density (ASTM D 4052), viscosity (ASTM D 445), acid and base numbers (ASTM D 664), ash content (ASTM D 482), flash point (ASTM D 93), and heating value (ASTM D 240), and the second one is monitoring the wear debris of the lubricating oil.14 Literature related to the physiochemical properties of lubricating oil is shown in Table 1. As observed, scant reports abound on the wear and long performance features of IC engines powered with vegetable oils. However, detailed works exist on the combustion, engine characteristics, and spray features of diesel engines fuelled by alternative fuels in the literature. Most significantly, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, comparison of the durability and lubricating oil tribology of those engines operated on preheated JCO and DF are lacking in the literature. In this framework, the current work is necessary, where the behavior of 90 °C preheated Jatropha curcas straight vegetable oil (PHSVO90) with diesel fuel (DF) in tribological studies of lubricating oil is experimentally investigated. Numerous wear studies are undertaken on lubricating oil samples drained from PHSVO90 and DF fuelled engines so as to compare the performance of the two fuels based on the engine’s condition. It is worth mentioning that the lubricating oil properties need to be understood to avoid the deterioration of engine performance due to the increase in wear. Hence, this Article concentrates on evaluating the carbon deposits, physicochemical properties, and metal wear debris contamination of 90 °C preheated Jatropha curcas straight vegetable oil, conventional diesel, and their comparison to take precautionary steps for any deterioration in the lubricating oil, which affects the engine performance. This study mainly deals with a comparative assessment of durability aspects of the DF with preheated Jatropha curcas straight vegetable oil. The research will be of interest to stakeholders and researchers in the biodiesel industry and the automotive sector, as the study will provide a comprehensive understanding of the tribological aspects of unsaturated long-chain fatty acid-based 90 °C preheated Jatropha curcas straight vegetable oil-operated indirect injection diesel engine and foster information on the suitable maintenance protocol for selected fuel operation, which is similar to the vegetable oils discussed herein.

Methodology

Internal combustion engines should instead use alternative bio-origin fuels that are locally accessible when used in the rural agriculture division of a developing nation like India. The current study was carried out by formulating the qualities of common vegetable oil and getting them nearer to the properties of standard diesel. The system design tactic has been used to ensure that these changed fuels may be used in current diesel engines without requiring significant physical changes. India produces more than 6.5 million tonnes of nonedible oils. Several of these oils are still being produced, and it is anticipated that some additional plant-based forest-derived oils will have a significantly larger production capacity. Among the several NESOs in the nation, Jatropha curcas was selected for the current study, since India has the most land, nearly 33 million hectares degraded land, under Jatropha curcas cultivation and is one of the world’s major producers of Jatropha curcas. Fuel Used and Its Characterization

Jatropha curcas is a NESO that is utilized as a feedstock for second generation biofuel and represents one of the renown intriguing potential solutions to energy concerns, particularly transportation fuel. Jatropha curcas, often identified as the purging nut, is an oilseed plant type native to tropical America that now flourishes in numerous parts of the tropics and semiarid regions of Africa, Asia, and South America. This plant thrives in peripheral locations that are regularly subjected to dry and hot temperatures and have poor soils, where most other crops cannot flourish.41 Overall, the findings presented indicate that Jatropha curcas possesses both biochemical and physiological features that confer drought resistance and relative salt sensitivity throughout its early development phase. Furthermore, Jatropha may grow on nonagricultural and marginal sites that are unsuitable for food crops, and it can use wastewater to flourish. Jatropha has reduced fertilizer and pesticide requirements, as well as lower crop management expenditures, than many other energy crops. Jatropha has been a perennial plant that produces oil for more than 30 years. Because of their high specific weight, the seeds may be transported without degradation at a reasonable cost. Jatropha seeds contain 30–40% oil, which is easily extracted for processing. Every year, a Jatropha tree absorbs around 8 kgme of CO2. In a hectare, around 2500 trees may be planted, resulting in 20 tonnes of CO2 sequestration per year for a lifespan of 40–50 years.42 The physicochemical properties of the Jatropha curcas straight vegetable oil are mentioned in the earlier research paper,43 along with its 3D crystal structure/3D conformer44 shown in Table 2.

Physical Measurement of Wear

The prolonged operation of the engine resulted in the wear down of different moving components. Both engines were operated under identical conditions and experienced similar loading cycles. The sole deviation in the procedure was the utilization of two distinct fuels to operate both engines, allowing for a direct comparison of the impact of the new fuel on the durability of the engine hardware. This was considered a rigorous test to analyze the compatibility of the newly developed fuels with diesel oil. Prior to and following the completion of a prolonged endurance test, measurements of the essential components and the physical state were recorded. The discrepancy between these two dimensions indicates the amount of wear that occurred in these parts during the specified period of engine operation. Following the extensive endurance test, the engines were once again totally disassembled, as shown in Figure 3, and meticulously examined to assess the physical condition of different components. The wear of the engine was determined by precise measurements of the size of key components both before and after the extensive endurance test. These wear findings were valuable for comparing the performance of PHSVO90 and diesel fuel in terms of their impact on the wear of essential engine components, as shown in Table 7.

The results showed that PHSVO90 wear was marginally higher when compared to typical diesel operations. PHSVO90 is viscous and denser than diesel and has lower volatility, which affects spray formation and miscibility with air. It may also increase the spray penetration length, which directly impinges the cylinder walls or liner lubricating film. This causes the lubricating film in between piston and liner surface to be washed away, which affects the effective lubrication and is responsible for increased wear of the PHSVO90-operated engine compared to the diesel-operated engine.

Acid Number (AN)

The acid number is also represented as the total acid number (TAN) and indicates the amount of acid or its derivatives existing in the lubricant oil.

Here, the standard base is potassium hydroxide, the weight of the standard base is in milligrams, and the weight of the acidic components is in grams.

The fluctuation of the acid number (AN) with the duration of engine operation on lubricating oil is shown in Figure 7. As observed, as the engine run time increases, AN increases as well. When compared with traditional diesel operation, PHSVO90 showed greater AN in all recordings. The AN of 9.11 mg of KOH per gram was observed at the maximum engine duration, 512 h, compared to 1.19 mg of KOH per gram in conventional diesel operation. The increased AN shows that the lubricating oil characteristics are deteriorating as a result of oxidation or contamination with acid-based compounds.

Variation in total acid number (TAN) with engine run time.

Base Number (BN)

Base number (BN) or total base number (TBN) indicates the availability of the quantity of alkaline present in the lubricant oil, which neutralizes the acidic nature of the lubricant oil caused by the combustion products. TBN determines how effective the lubrication oil is to protect the engine parts from the corrosion. It is measured by the amount of standard base, i.e., potassium hydroxide (KOH) in milligrams, per amount of acidic compounds within the oil measured in grams.

In general, fresh lubricant oil consists of a higher TBN when compared to its used condition; from Figure 8, it can be see that, as the engine run time increases from 0 to 512 h, the TBN decreases. Further, the deterioration of the TBN with PHSVO90 is higher when compared to conventional diesel operation. The maximum TBN with PHSVO90 at 512 h is 2.01 mg of KOH per milligram, which is 0.6 less than the conventional diesel operation. As demonstrated in Figures 7 and 8, oxidation and unburned HC of PHSVO90 are both responsible for deteriorating the lubricant oil, resulting in a decrease in its base number and thickening of the lubricating oil.

Density

Density is a measure of the mass of the liquid for a given volume. Figure 9 depicts the variation of density with the engine run time under endurance test as per IS 10000 as per the procedure adopted in ASTM D 4052. As the engine duration increases, the density of the lubricating oil keeps on increasing for both DF and PHSVO90. The increase in the density of PHSVO90 is much higher than that of the conventional diesel due to the combined effect of metal wear debris, carbon contamination fuel dilution, and moisture content with the lubricating oil. After 512 h, the density of PHSVO90 lubricating oil is 986 kg/m3, which is 561 kg/m3 greater than that of conventional diesel operation. The results are consistent with the findings of previous researchers.35,36,55

Viscosity

Viscosity is one of the fundamental properties of the lubricating oil. In general, viscosity is the resistance to flow at a prescribed temperature and time. Figure 10 represents the variation of viscosity for conventional diesel and PHSVO90 at various engine run times at 40 °C. The viscosity of the PHSVO90 fuel-operated engine lubricating oil is higher when compared to that of conventional DF operation at all run times. Further, a maximum kinematic viscosity of 381 cSt was recorded with PHSVO90, which was 181 cSt higher than that for DF operation. The increase in the viscosity of lubricating oil with PHSVO90 fuel is because of oxidation and contamination with acid-based combustion products in the lubricating oil. The total acid number is evident in such a scenario. The same such results were even reported by earlier researchers.55,56

Ash Content

The ash content of the lubricating oil differentiates the availability of the noncarbonaceous material from carbonaceous material like metal sediments, oil, and soot particles from the exhaust, which will be oxidized and become CO2 during thermal decomposition. Further, the ash content will be measured in terms of the percentage mass basis of noncarbonaceous residues to the total amount of lubrication oil sample by following the testing procedure as per ASTM D 482. It indicates the metallic wear debris and foreign particles which may have entered or existed the engine from the surroundings. The contribution of foreign particles in both the engines remains the same due to their similar working environments. Hence, the ash content drawn from the engines primarily depends on wear debris. Corrosion from engine intake and exhaust systems also contribute the metal traces in the ash content of the lubricating oil.57

Spectrometric Analysis of Lubricating Oil

Being a mechanical system, the engine comprises many sliding parts, like the piston in the cylinder bore along with piston rings, the valve stem in its groove, connecting rod big-end and small-end solid bearings, and many more, that are exposed to friction, leading to its abrasion. The objective of the lubricating oil is to filter these metal sediments and hard material from the incomplete combustion and ensure the reliable, stable, and smooth running of the engine. The mixing of all these metal debris and incomplete combustion products makes the lubricating oil more viscous like a gel and affects the process of lubrication. Hence, it is essential to understand the healthy condition of lubricating oil by evaluating degradation and contamination in the process of the endurance test.58

Inductively coupled plasma-based atomic emission spectroscopy was adopted to measure the metal traces of the lubricating oil. The test was conducted as per ASTM D 5183-13e1 to trace the Fe, Cr, Al, Cu, Mg, and Pb of the lubricating oil from the diesel and PHSVO90 engines at different intervals, as expressed in Figure 12.

(c) Aluminum concentration vs engine run time. (d) Copper concentration vs engine run time. (e) Magnesium concentration vs engine run time. (f) Lead concentration vs engine run time.

Iron

Iron in its alloy form is the most exhaustively used metal in engine construction. In order to have good strength, rigidity, and manufacturability and an economical way to produce different components like the engine cylinder, valves, piston rings (both compression and oil), connecting rod, crank shafts, cams, and cam shafts, many components are made up of ferrous alloys.59,60 Hence, iron is one of the prominent metals used in engine construction. Thus, the presence of ferrous traces in lubricating oil may be due to wear of the different engine parts or oxidation of the rubbing surfaces from the engine parts. The resulting appearance of ferrous traces in lubricating oil during the endurance test is depicted in Figure 12a. Ferrous traces were increased up to around 300 h of operation and later slightly reduced. Friction associated with rubbing parts of the new engine is the prominent cause for the increase in Ferrous traces in the first 300 h. Further, Jatropha curcas PHSVO90 shows 1.49× more traces compared to DF operation due to Jatropha curcas being oxygenated and high in FFA, leading to an increase in ferrous traces compared to conventional diesel operation. Similar results were reported by the previous researchers35,55

Chromium

Chromium is used to improve the strength and hardness of the soft iron-made cylinder liners, piston rings, and bearing cages. These are primary sources of chromium traces produced in the lubricating oil. The variation of chromium traces with the engine run time is shown in Figure 12b. However, the content of chromium is minimal when compared to iron. Of course, the PHSVO90-based engine shows Cr traces 1.37× higher than those of regular diesel operation. The identical scenario was also observed by other researchers in their findings.35,55,61

Aluminum

Shims, bushings, pistons, and bearing cages are mainly responsible for aluminum traces in the lubricating oil. A small quantity of aluminum is used in engines as it is lightweight and aids in heat transfer. From Figure 12c, aluminum traces in PHSVO90 are 1.19× greater, i.e., marginally higher, than those in the conventional diesel operation. The same such results were noticed by earlier researchers.14,35,55

Copper

The primary source of the copper in the lubricating oil is the shield of the injector, connecting small end, thrust washers of the injectors, inlet and exhaust valve guides, etc. Figure 12d depicts the presence of copper traces in lubricating oil. It is observed that PHSVO90-based lubricating oil showed 1.37× more Cu traces when compared to pure diesel operation. Such similar findings were also noticed by other researchers in their experimental work.14,35,62

Magnesium

The presence of magnesium in wear debris can come from several causes, such as the depletion of additives, wear of cylinder liners, and wear of bearings. Magnesium-containing compounds are utilized as additives in detergents and lubricating oils. Even magnesium is one of the additives to the lubricating oil that acts as a detergent of the lubricant oil and is also a source of traces. Magnesium traces in PHSVO90 and diesel are shown in Figure 12e, PHSVO90 showed 1.2× more traces when compared to conventional diesel operation. Similar observations are enumerated elsewhere.35,62 The TAN analysis revealed a significant increase in the amount of KOH (7.92 mg/g) while using PHSVO90 compared to typical diesel operation. This indicates that the lubricating oil experiences degradation and accumulates higher levels of magnesium when using PHSVO90. Even some of the research findings shown that Mg traces in vegetable oil are similar to those in diesel oil.55

Lead

The presence of lead in wear debris may be attributed to wear occurring in bearings, paints, and the addition of grease. Figure 12f illustrates the lead traces in PHSVO90 and diesel engine lubricating oils. Lead traces in PHSVO90 are on par with those from diesel operation. A maximum of 1.18× higher traces were observed in PHSVO90 when compared to diesel operation. Conclusion

This investigation reveals the diesel engine health condition when the engine is operated using preheated Jatropha curcas straight vegetable oil at 90 °C and conventional diesel. Different tribological characteristics of LUB were analyzed through long-term 512 h endurance tests as per IS 10000.

Following the conclusion of these tests, all critical engine components were removed to analyze physical degradation and carbon deposits. PHSVO90 and diesel exhibit distinct features due to their varied chemical compositions. PHSVO90 showed a larger concentration of carbon deposits, measured at 6.3 g. It also had a TAN (total acid number) of 9.11 mg of potassium hydroxide per gram, a density of 986 kgmes/m3, and a viscosity of 381 cSt. Furthermore, an inductive plasma-based atomic emission spectroscope identified a higher level of metal wear debris from spectrum metals with PHSVO90. The concentrations of Fe, Cr, Al, Cu, Mg, and Pb were 1.49×, 1.37×, 1.19×, 1.37×, 1.2×, and 1.18× higher, respectively, than those measured during the usual diesel operation.

Through an inductively coupled plasma-based atomic emission spectroscope, higher metal wear debris levels of different metals such as Fe, Cr, Al, Cu, Mn, and Pb were discovered with the PHSVO90-operated engine lubricating oil compared to traditional diesel operation.

Through an inductively coupled plasma-based atomic emission spectroscope, higher metal wear debris levels of different metals such as Fe, Cr, Al, Cu, Mn, and Pb were discovered with the PHSVO90-operated engine lubricating oil compared to traditional diesel operation.

Furthermore, increased density, viscosity, and ash content were found for the PHSVO90 engine lubricating oil due to oxidation and availability of noncombustible elements such as wear debris.
