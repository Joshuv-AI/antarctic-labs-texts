Effects of nonpharmaceutical interventions during COVID-19 pandemic on pediatric asthma exacerbations and viral infections
Authors: Katherine Caid, Megan Tate, Shahwar Yousuf, Lillian Jones, Robert D Pesek, Akilah A Jefferson, Tamara T Perry, Daniel Liu, Grace Turner, Ashton Ingold, Susanna Hartzell, Bobby L Boyanton, Kim Cobb, Haley Long, Suzanne House, Dana Frederick, Rachel A Frenner, Erin Hathorn, Jing Jin, Scott Stewart, Joshua L Kennedy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe emergence of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) in March 2020 led to the implementation of nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) to curb its spread. Studies have shown that adult asthma exacerbations and viral infections decreased during NPI use. However, few studies have shown the effects of NPIs on pediatric asthma exacerbations and infections during and after the pandemic.ObjectiveThis study aimed to understand the impact of NPIs on asthma exacerbations and viral respiratory infections in pediatric patients at our institution from March 2018 to December 2022.MethodsThe medical record numbers of children with asthma exacerbations seen at our institution between March 2018 and December 2022 were analyzed. Subjects were categorized on the basis of timing of their exacerbation in relation to NPI enforcement. We used the results from clinical testing with the BioFire Respiratory Panel (BRP) to detect up to 22 respiratory pathogens and then correlated these results with asthma exacerbation severity.ResultsThere were 5,758 asthma exacerbations recorded, with a 50% decline in average weekly exacerbations during NPI enforcement. Of the 70,682 BRP tests performed, 87% returned a positive result for at least 1 pathogen. Several viruses (respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza, and influenza) had a decrease in positivity rate with NPIs, whereas rhinovirus/enterovirus positivity rates were unchanged throughout the pandemic. Asthma exacerbations with a positive BRP result required higher clinical levels of care during the admission.ConclusionNPIs were associated with significantly reduced numbers of asthma exacerbations and respiratory viral infections. The post-NPI period saw a return to prepandemic levels of asthma exacerbations and an unusual surge in respiratory syncytial virus infections, emphasizing the need for continuous monitoring and adaptive strategies in the postpandemic landscape.Key words: Nonpharmaceutical interventions, asthma exacerbations, viral infections, SARS-CoV-2, pediatric asthma

Since the outbreak of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) in March 2020, the virus has had a profound global impact, infecting more than 700 million individuals and resulting in nearly 7 million fatalities.1 The United States swiftly adopted a series of nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) to curb its rapid spread.2,3 These measures, which included practices such as social distancing, wearing of masks, and imposition of travel bans, varied in intensity and were enforced primarily from March 2020 to early 2021.2, 3, 4

Research from various parts of the world, including the United States, has shown a marked reduction in cases of respiratory viruses during the period when NPIs were most stringent.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Early in the pandemic, there was also a notable decline in hospital visits due to asthma exacerbations, suggesting a potential correlation between the implementation of NPIs and reduced asthma-related complications.10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 However, many of these studies were conducted outside the United States or focused primarily on the adult population.11, 12, 13, 14 Furthermore, recent research on pediatric asthma exacerbations in the United States during the pandemic halted its analysis around early 2021, thus not considering the emergence and potential impact of new SARS-CoV-2 variants and the reemergence of other viral pathogens.15, 16, 17

Our institution, which is the only pediatric hospital system in Arkansas, offers a unique perspective. Its primary facility in the capital city of Little Rock serves as a leading care center, housing the state's sole pediatric intensive care unit (ICU). Our network also includes a smaller community hospital and 7 outpatient clinics scattered across the state. Given its extensive reach, our institution is responsible for treating a significant portion of our state’s pediatric population, making it an ideal institution to study statewide trends. Our research aims to shed light on the patterns of viral respiratory infections and asthma exacerbations in our state from March 2018 to December 2022, particularly emphasizing the period of NPI enforcement. On the basis of preliminary observations, we hypothesize a direct correlation between the strict enforcement of NPIs and a decline in numbers of viral infections and asthma-related incidents.

Methods

Ethical review

The study protocol underwent ethical review by the institutional review board (approval nos. 262416 and 262286), which granted a waiver of consent and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) authorization.

Study population for the asthma cohort

To understand the relationship between asthma exacerbations and NPIs, we sourced medical record numbers (MRNs) for all pediatric patients with asthma who visited the emergency department (ED) or were admitted to the hospital between March 2018 and December 2022. These patients were identified by using specific International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, codes for asthma (J45). Records were cross-verified by the institutional asthma program, which maintained hospital admission lists during the same periods. We extracted demographic details, including age, race, ethnicity, and sex, from patients’ electronic records. Only patients aged 18 years or younger at their first visit or admission between March 2018 and December 2022 were included in the cohort. Each asthma exacerbation that resulted in a patient being seen in the ED or admitted to the hospital was included as a distinct event, meaning that a patient with multiple exacerbations would be counted more than once. In subsequent analyses, patients with multiple exacerbations were also considered separately from those with only 1 exacerbation.

Patients were categorized into 3 distinct periods based on the timing of their asthma exacerbation or viral infection. The time periods were based on NPI enforcement in our state: the pre-NPI period (March 2018 to February 2020), NPI period (March 2020 to February 2021), and post-NPI period (March 2021 to December 2022). The boundary of these periods was informed by the Containment and Health Index (CHI) and Government Response Index (GRI) from the Oxford-19 Government Response Tracker,4 which provided insights into the strictness of various policy measures. The CHI represented how 14 policy responses were enforced nationwide and at the state level (see Table E1 in the Online Repository at www.jaci-global.org). These policies included mask mandates, workplace closings, and event cancellations and limitations. On the basis of the number and strictness of policies enforced, each state was assigned a monthly CHI value between 0 and 100, with higher values signifying stricter enforcement. The GRI was a number between 0 and 100 that represented the stringency of the same 14 measures of the CHI with the inclusion of analysis of the presence of government-provided salary replacement and government-mandated freezing of financial obligations. Within each period, we grouped asthma cases by week and viral infections by month and performed a comparative analysis using the Tukey test, with P values less than .05 considered statistically significant.

To analyze whether a particular SARS-CoV-2 variant affected asthma exacerbations, we used pediatric subjects from our state with SARS-CoV-2 variant sequencing from our laboratory that was submitted to GenBank to identify the relevant variants during specific periods. The asthma exacerbation cohort was reclassified into 5 groups based on the predominant variant present during their exacerbation. Of note, SARS-CoV-2 testing was not performed on all asthmatic patients in our cohort, nor was viral sequencing performed on every positive SARS-CoV-2 sample at our institution. The groups were labeled pre-NPI (March 2018 to April 2020), pre-Delta (May 2020 to May 2021), Delta (June 2021 to November 2021), Omicron (December 2021 to March 2022), and post-Omicron (April 2022 to December 2022).18 Exacerbations were grouped by week within each period. The weekly exacerbations in the pre-NPI period were considered the baseline, and the percentage of change from this baseline was calculated for the weekly exacerbations in the other periods. ANOVA was used for statistical analysis, and P values less than.05 were considered statistically significant.

Study population for viral infections

To assess the number of viral infections at our institution from March 2018 to December 2022, we obtained results for clinical tests performed by using the BioFire Respiratory Panel (BRP), version (v) 2.0 and v2.1 (BioMerieux, Durham, NC). The BRP is a PCR-based test detecting 17 respiratory viruses (v2.0) and 18 respiratory viruses (v2.1). The viruses included on this test are adenovirus (ADV); the non-SARS coronavirus subtypes HKU1, NL63, 229E, and OC43; human metapneumovirus (HMPV); rhinovirus/enterovirus (R/E); the influenza A subtypes 2009 H1N1, H1, and H3; influenza B; the parainfluenza subtypes 1, 2, 3, and 4; and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). The v2.1 BRP added SARS-CoV-2 in January 2021. For analysis, influenza A and B were combined, as were the parainfluenza and non-SARS coronavirus subtypes. The monthly positivity rate (PR) was computed for each detected virus by dividing the total number of positive test results by the total number of BRPs performed. Our primary focus was on R/E, RSV, and SARS-CoV-2.

Finally, we combined the BRP and asthma exacerbation cohort data files by joining each by MRNs. This provided a sample of patients with both viral testing and asthma exacerbations. These patients were compared by the timing of NPI enforcement: the pre-NPI period, NPI period, and post-NPI period. We also considered those with both viral testing and asthma exacerbations by the level of care required (ED, inpatient, or ICU).

Statistical analysis

For descriptive analysis, categoric or continuous variables were compared by using the chi-square test or Wilcoxon rank sum test. We examined the trend of the proportions or medians of categoric or continuous variables across 3 time periods or the level of care required by using the Cochran-Armitage test, Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test, or Jonckheere-Terpstra test, where appropriate. P values less than .05 were deemed significant. All analyses were conducted by using R software v.4.1.

Results

Asthma exacerbations

Between March 2018 and December 2022, we recorded 5758 asthma exacerbations involving 3713 individual pediatric patients (aged ≤18 years at the first admission during the study time period). A breakdown of their care showed that 3934 asthma exacerbations were treated in the ED, 1554 required standard inpatient care, and 266 necessitated admission to the ICU.

When we mapped these exacerbations against the timeline of NPIs, we found that 1872 cases occurred before the NPIs (in the pre-NPI period), 509 during their enforcement (in the NPI period), and 1553 after their relaxation (in the post-NPI period). We observed that post-NPI patients were, on average, 1 year younger than those during the NPI period (during NPI, the mean age was 8.38 ± 4.54 years, whereas in the post-NPI period, the mean age was 7.30 ± 4.34 years). However, across the periods, there were no discernible differences in terms of sex, race, or ethnicity (Table I).

The enforcement of NPIs had a profound impact on asthma exacerbations. During the NPI period, the average weekly number of exacerbations decreased by approximately 50% versus during the pre-NPI and post-NPI periods (P < .0001). By the summer of 2021, exacerbations had returned to pre-NPI levels (Fig 1, A and B). Of note, we also found an increased severity for post-NPI exacerbations, with a higher proportion of patients in this period requiring inpatient and ICU care than during the pre-NPI and NPI periods (during the pre-NPI period, 25.5% required inpatient care and 4.0% required ICU care; during the NPI period, 26% required inpatient care and 4.6% required ICU care; and during the post-NPI period, 28.7% required inpatient care and 5.2% required ICU care [P for trend = .011]) (Table II).

When the numbers of asthma exacerbations were evaluated by the predominant SARS-CoV-2 variant, exacerbations decreased from baseline by 10.6% during the pre-Delta period, fitting with the decrease seen during NPI implementation. We found a 6% increase in exacerbations from the pre-SARS-CoV-2 baseline during the Delta period (P = .013). Exacerbations during the Omicron period decreased by 6.6% from baseline and returned to baseline in the post-Omicron period.

Multiple asthma exacerbations

Of the 3713 individual patients in the cohort, 2680 patients had only 1 exacerbation during the 3 time periods. There were 271 patients represented in 2 time periods, and 37 were represented in all 3 time periods. In total, there were 670 patients with multiple exacerbations in the pre-NPI era, 278 during the NPI period, and 708 during the post-NPI period (see Table E1). During the pre-NPI period, there was 1 subject who had 10 documented asthma exacerbations during the almost 2-year interval.

We found age differences in patients with multiple exacerbations. During the NPI period patients were older than during the pre-NPI and post-NPI periods (see Table E1). When the patients with multiple exacerbations were compared with the patients having only 1 exacerbation, race-related differences were noted. We found that 70% of patients with more than 1 exacerbation were Black, whereas only 56% of patients with 1 exacerbation were Black (P < .001). There were no statistically significant ethnic differences between those with 1 exacerbation and those with more than 1 (P = .399). Importantly, those who had more than 1 asthma exacerbation during the study period had an increase in ICU and inpatient admissions during the post-NPI period (31.7% ICU and inpatient admissions in the post-NPI period vs 29.5% during the NPI period and 30.5% in the pre-NPI period) similar to that observed among the patients with a single exacerbation (36.6% ICU and inpatient admissions in the post-NPI period vs 32% during the NPI period and 28.5% during the pre-NPI period), suggesting that those with more than 1 exacerbation did not skew the data toward higher levels of care (see Table E2 in the Online Repository at www.jaci-global.org).

Viral infections

Between March 2018 and December 2022, a total of 70,682 BRP tests were conducted at our institution. Of these, 61,592 tests (87%) returned a positive result for at least 1 of the 17 or 18 pathogens under scrutiny. Most of these tests (73.8%) were conducted after NPIs had been relaxed (Tables III and IV). The PR for any virus during the period of NPI enforcement was 52.2%, marking a decline from the 81% PR observed during the pre-NPI period and the 94.6% PR recorded after the relaxation of NPIs (P < .001).

R/E was predominant throughout the study, with 25,588 positive detections and an average PR of 36%. Before NPI enforcement, the average R/E monthly PR was 37.5%, with peaks observed in April, May (PR = 40%), and September (PR = 50%). The PR decreased to 20% during the initial months of NPI enforcement (P < .0001) and quickly rebounded to its prepandemic levels in August 2020. The PR remained unchanged from its prepandemic baseline throughout the remainder of the study (Fig 2, A).

For RSV, 8801 tests returned a positive result, with an average PR of 12% during the entirety of the study. Historically, RSV infections have peaked from September to February. However, RSV infections dramatically decreased during NPI enforcement, nearing zero at our hospital. Interestingly, in the post-NPI period, RSV infections saw an uncharacteristic surge from June to September 2021, peaking at a 41% PR in July. By 2022, the infections had returned to their more typical seasonal pattern, with an average PR of 30.8% from September to December (Fig 2, B). For comparison, the SARS-CoV-2 numbers are shown in Fig 2, C.

Other viruses, with the exception of ADV, had similarly dramatic reductions during the NPI period (see Fig E1 in the Online Repository at www.jaci-global.org). One of the more interesting findings is the change in HMPV, which was at its peak in March 2020 (97 positive test results, with a PR of 12.5%); after the introduction of SARS-CoV-2 and NPIs, however, this virus nearly vanished (in April 2020, there were 7 positive test results with a PR of 2.3%). HMPV remained at near-zero levels until the post-NPI period in July-August 2021.

Respiratory viral infections and asthma exacerbations

In our asthma exacerbation cohort, 1843 admissions had viral testing via BRP. Of these samples, 1323 (72%) were negative for all 17 or 18 respiratory viruses tested, whereas 462 (25%) were positive for 1 virus and 58 (3.2%) were positive for multiple viruses. In all, 59 (3.2%) viral tests were performed in the pre-NPI period, followed by 142 (7.7%) in the NPI period and 1642 (89.1%) in the post-NPI period. The PR for any virus during the pre-NPI period was 100%, whereas the PRs for any virus during the NPI and post-NPI periods were 31% and 25% respectively. The most common virus identified across all periods was R/E, which was responsible for 53 positive test results (90% of the positive test results) during the pre-NPI period, 43 positive test results (97.7% of the positive test results) during the NPI period, and 269 positive test results (64.5% of the positive test results) during the post-NPI period. In all, 6 and 51 samples, respectively, tested positive for RSV during the pre-NPI and post-NPI periods; however, only 1 sample with an RSV infection was identified during the NPI period. Additionally, 42 samples tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 during the post-NPI period; only 1 was identified during the NPI period.

The presence of a positive viral test result in those with an asthma exacerbation correlated with increasing levels of care in the hospital (59% of the affected patients required ICU care, 37% required inpatient care, and 16% required ED care [P for trend < .001]) (Fig 3, A and B). We evaluated these areas of care by virus in the asthma exacerbation cohort. Those subjects in the asthma exacerbation cohort who had a positive test result for R/E, RSV, ADV, or influenza had an increased frequency of being seen in the ICU, suggesting that they required a higher level of care (Table V). Those with a positive SARS-CoV-2 test result had a higher frequency of being seen in the ED and sent home than did those who were admitted to inpatient or ICU care (ED care, 3.4%; inpatient care, 0.9%; and ICU care, 2.1% [P = .002]).

Discussion

This study quantifies changes in asthma exacerbations and viral infections during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic in a pediatric population at a large tertiary medical center and its satellite campuses across the state. As hypothesized, there was a decrease in asthma exacerbations and viral infections during NPI implementation. Although this demonstrates the effectiveness of NPIs in limiting viral spread, it also suggests that reducing viral infections can lead to a decrease in asthma exacerbations.

Our data provide valuable insights into the effects of NPIs on asthma exacerbations and respiratory illnesses during the COVID-19 pandemic. The significant reduction in asthma exacerbations during the NPI enforcement period aligns with global observations. Taquechel et al found an 80% decrease in pediatric asthma hospital encounters at Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia during the first 3 months of the COVID-19 pandemic, attributing it to the reduced exposure to respiratory viruses thanks to NPIs.19 Similarly, in Turkey, Akelma et al observed fewer encounters of pediatric acute-onset wheezing during their lockdown, correlating it with a decrease in lower respiratory tract infections.20

In our study, the severity of asthma exacerbations increased following the relaxation of NPIs, with more patients requiring inpatient and ICU levels of care than required by patients with exacerbations occurring before and during the pandemic. Many families worried that seeking medical care during the pandemic could expose them to SARS-CoV-2 and neglected regular follow-ups with physicians because of this fear.21,22 Lack of routine medical care could have resulted in asthmatic patients no longer having access to medications or not having their medications adjusted as the severity of their disease changed. Although we did not gather information on prescriptions or use of controller medications for our cohort, we hypothesize that these factors could lead to more uncontrolled asthma in the community and more severe presentations of exacerbations. Other studies have similarly shown these types of problems in their cohorts.23,24

The viral infection data, particularly the data showing the significant drop in RSV infections during NPIs and the subsequent uncharacteristic surge of RSV, are consistent with global trends. It was observed by Britton et al in New South Wales, Australia, that RSV infections dropped by 95% from March to June 2020 versus in previous years.25 Additional studies performed in Argentina, England, Taiwan, and Turkey showed out-of-season surges of RSV once NPIs had been relaxed.25, 26, 27, 28 These studies suggest that although NPIs are effective in the short term, they can disrupt the typical seasonality of certain respiratory viruses.

Although the total number of viral infections decreased throughout the pandemic, R/E infection rates remained unchanged. The same observation was noted by Kirca et al.27 This persistence throughout NPI implementation was unexpected, especially as the PRs of other droplet-spread viruses, such as RSV, decreased to nearly 0%. R/E is associated with 60% to 80% of asthma exacerbations. As R/E infection is associated with asthma exacerbations,29, 30, 31 we expected that in a situation in which the numbers of asthma exacerbations were reduced, R/E infection rates would trend similarly.

There are several possible explanations for the persistence of R/E. The first is that R/E is a nonenveloped virus, which allows it to live longer on surfaces and be more difficult to inactivate than its enveloped counterparts such as RSV. An additional explanation is that R/E could be spread by aerosol instead of droplet transmission, thus making masking and social distancing less effective mitigation approaches.32,33 Further, we know that patients can test positive for R/E for several weeks following the initial symptoms. If the same patient tested positive for R/E and had additional BRPs performed in subsequent weeks for new-onset viral symptoms, he or she could repeatedly test positive for R/E, thus affecting the PR. The reasons for this repeated positivity could be secondary to the sensitivity of the PCR to detect these viruses. It could also be attributed to the many viruses that fall into the R/E category. All rhinovirus and enterovirus subtypes are reported as 1 entity on the BRP without further delineation. With more than 100 serotypes of rhinovirus and 200 serotypes of enterovirus, there are more than 300 different viral serotypes that all manifest as R/E, making it possible for patients to be infected with different R/E strains with each BRP.

Throughout this study, there was a more than 500% increase in the number of BRPs performed after NPIs versus before NPI implementation. One explanation is that our institution did not have any protocols in place to determine which patients should receive testing. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, having a specific viral cause of symptoms would often not alter patient management and specific viral testing was often forgone. Schools and day care centers often required SARS-CoV-2 testing during and after the pandemic before a child was allowed to return after an absence. It is possible that providers used comprehensive BRPs in place of individual SARS-CoV-2 tests, as BRPs could identify a specific viral cause for symptoms and allow patients to return to school earlier.

Finally, we note that a positive viral test result in the asthma exacerbation cohort increased the frequency of higher-level care when the patient was admitted to the hospital. This was particularly true of patients with positive test results for R/E, RSV, ADV, and influenza. This is consistent with data from this group, which previously showed that patients with asthma exacerbations and viral infections had more symptoms.34 On the other hand, subjects with asthma exacerbations and a positive SARS-CoV-2 test result had lower frequencies of admission to inpatient or ICU settings. We hypothesize that this finding may be related to findings from others showing that patients with allergic asthma have less ACE2 receptor, which is the receptor for SARS-CoV-2.35 Fewer ACE2 receptors could make it more challenging to establish a viable infection, thereby causing fewer symptoms in those who are exposed. More research in this area is needed.

We have identified several limitations of our study. First, our data cover only individuals from Arkansas, which may not be generalized to other parts of the country. However, many institutions across the country and the world have noted similar drops in numbers of asthma exacerbations and viral infections during periods of NPI use.16,23,36,37 Second, administration of viral tests increased by more than 500% during and after the pandemic. This may have introduced a bias toward a higher PR than in the pre-NPI and post-NPI periods.

In conclusion, NPIs were implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic to reduce SARS-CoV-2 spread. Implementation of NPIs correlates with a subsequent decrease in cases of other viral infections and a temporary change in their seasonality, as seen with RSV. The combination of NPI utilization and viral infection reduction correlates with the observed decrease in asthma exacerbations during the COVID-19 pandemic. As asthma exacerbations recurred with the return of viral infections and relaxation of NPIs, they occurred at a higher frequency than usual during the timing correlating with the Delta variant. As additional SARS-CoV-2 variants develop, monitoring their potential effects on asthma exacerbations and other respiratory viruses is important.

Clinical implications

NPIs were correlated with decreased numbers of asthma exacerbations and viral infections in pediatric patients during the COVID-19 pandemic.
