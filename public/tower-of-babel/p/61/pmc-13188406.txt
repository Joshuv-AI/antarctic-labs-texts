Tridax procumbens promotes apoptosis and suppresses markers of proliferation, growth, and metastasis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells
Authors: Ekundayo Samuel, Ian Eggleston, David Tosh, Oyeronke Odunola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe assessed whether Tridax procumbens (TP) extracts could be used therapeutically against pancreatic cancer and remain nontoxic to normal cell types. The crude extract from TP (CETP) was fractionated using hexane, dichloromethane, and ethyl acetate to obtain fractions (NHF, DCMF, and EAF, respectively). The pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell line (PANC-1) was cultured with (10, 20, 50, 100, and 250 μg/mL) dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (control), CETP, and CETP-fractions for 24 or 48 h. As a normal cell type, we cultured E11.5d mouse pancreatic explants for five days before treating with the test samples (20 μg/mL) in DMSO for a further 48 h. Cytotoxicity assays (MTT and Live-Dead) were conducted, and the expression of cellular biomarkers, such as vimentin, Ki-67, p53, p21, and caspase-3, was evaluated. DCMF elicited PANC-1 cell death (IC50 = 23.1 μg/mL) compared to CETP (IC50 = 114.2 μg/mL). There were significant elevations in p53 (2.8-fold), caspase-3 (2.9-fold), catalase (4.0-fold), p21Cip1/Wap−1 (4.4-fold), and ALP (5.0-fold) proteins in DCMF-treated cells compared to control. DCMF significantly suppressed PNA and GST-pi (2.3-fold), Ki-67 (2.7-fold), and vimentin (10.8-fold) in PANC-1 cells relative to control. Also, DCMF induced Bcl-2 perinuclear staining and cytoplasmic translocation of APC in treated cells. Phenotype morphogenesis was observed in DCMF-treated embryonic pancreas with immunopositivity for insulin, vimentin, and amylase (1.3-fold), cytokeratin-7 (1.5-fold), PNA (1.9-fold), and glucagon (2.8-fold). In conclusion, Tridax procumbens appear to exert its anticancer effect via induction of apoptosis, antioxidant enzymes, and suppression of growth, proliferation, and invasiveness in PANC-1 cells without any observable toxicity on the normal embryonic pancreas.Keywords: Tridax procumbens, Pancreatic cancer, Antiproliferation, Cytotoxicity

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is one of the most aggressive malignancies [1], with a five-year survival rate of less than five per cent [2]. There is no known early detection method for pancreatic cancer, and most patients present with no visible symptoms until it has metastasized [3]. For several decades, various intervention methods like chemotherapy have been applied in the treatment of cancer patients clinically. Interestingly, at least 50% of the available chemotherapeutic agents are obtained from plants and plant products, and with the global incidence of cancer estimated to exceed 30 million cases by 2040, there is an increasing proportion of the human population that depends on such materials to meet healthcare needs [4–6]. Over the last four decades, natural products have played a role in drug discovery and development [7, 8].

Medicinal plants and their products are used in the management of several human ailments, such as cancer [9]. Highly referenced among them is Tridax procumbens Linn. (Asteraceae), which is commonly referred to as Coat Buttons in English and Ewe Ehoro/Muwagun among the Yoruba people of western Nigeria [10, 11]. This plant is mainly found in the tropics [12] and is used in the management of wound healing, high blood pressure, haemorrhage, malaria, and dysentery [13, 14]. There are reports of its antibacterial and antifungal activities [15], analgesic, immune stimulation, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-hepatotoxic properties [15–17]. Tridax procumbens protects against arsenic-mediated damage in rats by suppressing clastogenicity and inflammation, and enhancing myelination and erythropoiesis [18, 19]. The cytotoxic effects of Tridax procumbens have been studied against breast, lung, and liver cancer cell lines, but its therapeutic potential against pancreatic cancer has yet to be fully explored [20–22]. This present study was, therefore, designed to address the dearth of information on the effect of Tridax procumbens on cellular markers of apoptosis, tumour growth, proliferation, and invasiveness in a pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell line and the normal cultured embryonic mouse pancreas.

Materials and methods

Plant extraction

Tridax procumbens leaves were collected from the University of Ibadan garden, Nigeria. Plant identification and authentication was performed independently by Mr. D.P.O. Esimekhuai, and specimen voucher number (UIH-22542) was deposited in the herbarium of the Department of Botany, University of Ibadan. The plant extraction was carried out as described previously [18]. Rotary evaporation was used to concentrate the filtrate under reduced pressure at 40 °C. The concentrate was stored at 4 °C as an ethanol extract and subsequently partitioned using hexane, dichloromethane, and ethyl acetate to obtain fractions (NHF, DCMF, and EAF, respectively) and the aqueous fraction (AQF). These dried fractions were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as vehicle and control.

Column and Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC)

The procedure was done in line with a previous study [23]. Briefly, 1 g of powdered DCMF was dissolved in dichloromethane and was pre-absorbed onto silica gel (ca 3 g), then loaded onto a silica gel column with dichloromethane as the mobile phase. Thereafter, the column was eluted at a constant rate under gravity (0—20% methanol in dichloromethane). The fractions were analysed by TLC, and fractions showing a similar pattern of separation on TLC were pooled together to give 90 mg of purified material. This material was used for further purification and identification of bioactive compounds from Tridax procumbens DCMF.

LC–MS and LC-Tandem MS

The DCMF were analysed by UHPLC/QTOF MS (Bruker, Germany). This was carried out in both positive and negative electrospray MS mode. The QTOF was linked to an UltiMate 3000 UHPLC (Thermo Fisher). Nebulizing gas, drying gas, and capillary voltage were set to 4 bar, 12 L/min and 4500 V, respectively with a 75—1500 m/z time-of-flight scan range at 220 °C. Tandem MS collision energy was placed on a scale of 100, 500 and 1000 m/z at 14.0, 20.0, and 30.0 eV, respectively. The liquid chromatographic separation was carried out utilising a RP column (Gemini 5 µm C18, 110 A, 250 × 4.6 mm, Phenomenex, UK); 1 mL/min flow rate, 35 °C, 10 µL volume of injection. The mobile phases A and B consisted of H2O and CH3CN, respectively, and both with 0.1% v/v HCOOH. Gradient elution T = 0 min, B = 5%; T = 20 min, B = 95%; T = 25 min, B = 95%; T = 28 min, B = 5%. The mass spectrometer was standardised by utilising an array of HCOONa cluster ions introduced by changing valve injection in the first minute of an individual run. Three parts of 1 M NaOH and ninety-seven parts of 50:50 H2O-isopropanol with 0.2% HCOOH were employed for the mass calibrant solution.

Isolation and culture of mouse embryonic pancreas

CD-1 mouse wild-type stocks were sourced from the University of Bath Animal Research Facility. Pancreatic tissues were dissected from the mouse embryo at E11.5d of development according to the method described by Burke et al. [24]. Briefly, an E11.5d pregnant female mouse was euthanised by cervical dislocation and the uterus (containing the embryos) was removed and placed in a 35 mm culture dish (Nalgene Nunc, Thermo Fisher) containing sterile phosphate buffer saline (9.55 g/L in MilliQ dH2O). The uterus was rinsed in dissecting media, Minimum Essential Medium Eagle (MEM) that comprised fortified Hank’s salts (10% FBS,2 mM L-glutamine; gentamicin, 0.01 mg/mL). The embryos were released using dissecting forceps and sharp scissors.

Cloning rings (SLS, UK) were placed on a coverslip (11—12 mm diameter) coated with 50 μg bovine plasma fibronectin to which 950 μl of PBS was added (1000 μg fibronectin was dissolved in 1 mL of MilliQ dH2O, stored at −20 °C) (Invitrogen) in a cell culture multiwell plate (1.9 cm2 culture area). 40 μl was added to each coverslip and allowed to air dry. 0.75—1 mL culture media (BME); with Earle’s salts (10% FBS; 2 mM L-glutamine; 0.01 mg/mL gentamicin) was then added. The dissected organs were transferred with the use of a Gilson p200 pipette (in which the tip was cut off) into the cloning ring and incubated (24 h, 37 °C, 5% CO2). The explant was cultured for five days to allow for the development of the organs. The tissues were treated with the extract, fractions, and characterised compounds from Tridax procumbens (20 μg/mL) in DMSO (0.2% v/v) for an additional two days. Cultures were treated with DMSO alone for the same duration as control.

PANC-1 cell culture

The pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell line (PANC-1) was obtained from the ATCC (England, UK). The cells were cultured according to the supplier’s instructions in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated foetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA.).

Cytotoxicity assays

Trypan blue exclusion test

Human PANC-1 cells were plated at 10 × 102 cells/well on a coverslip (Fisher) in a 4-well plate, incubated (24 h), and exposed to varying amounts (0.01, 0.02, 0.05, and 0.1 mg/mL) of Tridax procumbens extract and fractions for additional 24 or 48 h. After treatment, the culture medium was removed by pipetting, and the wells were briefly rinsed with phosphate bovine saline (PBS). The cells were treated with trypsin EDTA (200 µL) for 3–5 min in a 37 °C incubator. Trypsinisation was confirmed by viewing the cells under a transmitted light microscope (Tension) and stopped by adding complete DMEM (800 µL). The mixture was centrifuged (1000 rpm for 3 min), and the supernatant was decanted after which the cells were resuspended in 1 mL DMEM. 20 µL aliquot of resuspended cells was added to 20 µL of Trypan blue dye (1:1) for 1–3 min. The mixture was placed on a haemocytometer and examined immediately under the light microscope at low magnification to detect blue staining cells and scored for the total cell number. MTT proliferation test

The MTT {3-(4,5-dimethylyhiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide} assay was carried out as described by the manufacturer (Abcam, UK). Briefly, PANC-1 cells were plated (1 × 104 cell/well) in a 96-well plate, incubated for 24 h, and then treated with varying concentrations (0.02, 0.05, 0.1 and 0.25 mg/mL) of Tridax procumbens samples for another 24 h.

Live/Dead assay

Live/Dead assay was used to determine the presence of live and dead cells cultured on different coverslips using calcein AM and ethidium homodimer-1 dyes according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). PANC-1 cells were seeded at a density of 10 × 104 cells/well in a 4-well plate and incubated in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 24 h. The cells were treated with 20 μg/mL of crude extract and fractions of Tridax procumbens dissolved in DMSO for 24 or 48 h. DMSO (0.2%) was used as a control. After the treatment period, the Live/Dead cell assay was performed on the cells. Fluorescence of the live (green) and dead cells (red) were viewed under a confocal fluorescence microscope (LSM 880, Zeiss, Germany). Images of three randomly selected regions were collected, and cells were counted using Image J software.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence was carried out according to the method described by Burke et al. [24] on PANC-1 cells and pancreatic explants that were cultured for five days and treated with the extract, fractions, and characterised compounds of Tridax procumbens (20 μg/ml) in DMSO as control for additional two days. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) for 15 min (Sigma, St Louis, Mo, USA). In contrast, fixation of dissected embryonic tissues was performed in 10% formaldehyde, 0.1 M MOPS pH 7.4, 2 mM EGTA, 1 mM MgSO4 and 10% Formaldehyde (MEMFA) for 15–20 min (room temperature). After fixation, the coverslips were rinsed three times for 5 min each in PBS. Cells were permeabilised with 0.1% Triton X-100, whereas embryonic tissue was permeabilised with 1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15—20 min and rinsed gently several times in PBS before leaving at 4 °C until needed. For some antibodies (e.g., cytokeratin), antigen retrieval was performed after fixation using EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (Abcam) for 1—2 h in an incubator at 37 °C. Blocking was done to prevent non-specific binding of antigen with the use of a 2% blocking buffer (Roche) for 1 h. Tissues and cells were then incubated with primary antibodies diluted in a blocking buffer (as indicated in Table 1) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the coverslips were washed three times for 5 min each. The coverslips were then incubated with secondary antibodies in a 2% blocking buffer at room temperature (Table 1 shows the dilution of secondary antibodies). After 2 h, the coverslips were washed three times for 5 min each. Coverslips were mounted on microscope slides (VWR) utilising fluorescence mounting medium (Dako) and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) as nuclear stain (Invitrogen). The slides were viewed afterwards using a confocal microscope. Non-overlapping images were collected from the slides with confocal microscope, and fluorescence processed and quantified using Image J and Zen 2.3 lite software. The primary and secondary antibodies used are shown in Table 1.

Statistics

The data generated were analysed using ANOVA and Student’s t-test. Graph Pad Prism version 6 was used to carry out the procedures. Images collected with a confocal microscope were analysed with Image J and Zen 2.3 lite software. All results were expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean and significantly different at p < 0.05.

Results

The cytotoxic effects of Tridax procumbens on PANC-1 cells

We evaluated cell viability and cytotoxicity by using a triumvirate of tests: trypan blue exclusion (viability) (Fig. 1), MTT (proliferation) (Fig. 2), and live-dead (Fig. 3) tests on PANC-1 cells. Results from the trypan blue assay (Fig. 1) reveal that all the Tridax procumbens extracts attenuated PANC-1 cells viability in a time- and concentration dependent manner. EAF and DCMF showed the most significant activity leading to a 60% reduction in cell viability at 20 µg/mL upon 48 h treatment (Fig. 1B).

The activity of DCMF was also revealed by live-dead staining (Fig. 3). For DCMF, its activity was evident again with over 50% cytotoxicity on the PANC-1 cells at 20 µg/mL upon 48 h treatment compared with the less than 50% cytotoxicity of the CETP in similar conditions. Based on these results, DCMF was selected for further study and bioassay-guided fractionation.

Tridax procumbens induces apoptosis in PANC-1 cells

We hypothesised that the cytotoxic effect of the DCMF on the PANC-1 cells was mediated via the apoptotic pathway. The expression of two apoptotic markers, caspase-3 and Bcl-2 expressions was then determined (Fig. 4). DCMF significantly induced the expression of caspase-3 and Bcl-2 proteins in the PANC-1 cells (Fig. 4). Interestingly, we observed a cytoplasmic localisation of the Bcl-2 protein in the control group, and both cytoplasmic and perinuclear localisation in the DCMF-treated group (Fig. 4). There appears to be nucleolar expressions in the DCMF-treated group but this might represent artefactual staining (Fig. 4).

Tridax procumbens increases antioxidant enzymes activities in PANC-1 cells

Chemotherapeutic agents explore different mechanistic pathways to mitigate the development and spread of diseases. The cytotoxicity and induction of apoptotic markers by the DCMF in PANC-1 cells warranted the suggestion that DCMF could possess the potential to induce endogenous compounds or antioxidant enzymes. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the PANC-1 cells treated with DCMF displayed an elevation in the antioxidant function of both alkaline phosphatase and catalase enzymes (Fig. 5) and significant reduction in the expression of GST-pi (Fig. 6).

Tridax procumbens reduced growth, proliferation, and invasion markers in PANC-1 cells

Following the exposure of the PANC-1 cells to the DCMF of Tridax procumbens, there was significant upregulation of the tumour suppressor proteins, p53, p21, and APC in the DCMF-exposed PANC-1 cells compared to control (Figs. 7 and 8). In addition, the DCMF increased significantly the set of cells positive for p21Cip1/Wap−1 (Fig. 7). Reduced perinuclear localisation and enhanced cytoplasmic staining of APC protein was observed following the treatment of PANC-1 cells with DCMF (Fig. 8). Conversely, there was significant downregulation of markers of cell proliferation, growth, and invasiveness, Ki-67 (Fig. 8), vimentin (Fig. 9), CAII and PNA (Fig. 10) proteins in the DCMF-treated PANC-1 cells when compared with control.

Tridax procumbens did not cause any observable toxicity on the embryonic pancreas

Figures 11 and 12 show the embryonic pancreatic tissues which were treated with extract and fractions of Tridax procumbens for 48 h, fixed, and immunoassayed for proteins specific to the tissue type. Markers of the various pancreatic cells, insulin and glucagon (endocrine cell); amylase (acinar cell); cytokeratin 7 and peanut agglutinin (ductal cell); and vimentin (mesenchymal cell) were monitored. There was immunopositivity for insulin, glucagon, amylase, cytokeratin 7, peanut agglutinin, and vimentin in the extract and fractions-treated groups (Figs. 11 and 12). However, we noted enhanced branching morphogenesis of the ductal system in the DCMF-cultured embryonic pancreas based on increased expression of cytokeratin 7 and peanut agglutinin (Fig. 12).

Identification of bioactive compounds from Tridax procumbens DCMF

In order to identify bioactive components, the DCMF was analysed by LC–MS. Five distinct peaks with retention times of 12.8, 14.2, 14.4, 15.3, and 16.4 min, and associated MS–MS data showing fragmentation patterns were obtained (Figs. 13A-E). The chromatographic and spectrometric characteristics of these components are shown in Table 3. Following the analysis, these results were matched with the ChemSpider database. Based on this analysis, the identities of the main components of each peak were suggested to be tsibulin 1 (Fig. 13A), pterosin N (Fig. 13B), 5,7,3'-trihydroxy-2',4',5'-trimethoxyflavone (Fig. 13C), corchorifatty acid D (Fig. 13D), and capsiate (Fig. 13E), respectively.

Discussion

The effect of extracts from Tridax procumbens on markers of apoptosis, tumour growth, proliferation, and invasiveness in the pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell line (PANC-1), and cultured mouse embryonic pancreas, as a normal control tissue was assessed. The present study demonstrated that Tridax procumbens induced tumour suppressor proteins, p53 and p21 (Fig. 7) in PANC-1 cells. p53 protects the body cells by positively regulating various biochemical pathways, such as the cell cycle and apoptosis [25–27]. The induction of wild-type p53 levels, especially in breast, liver, and pancreatic cancer cells and the reactivation of wild-type p53 activity is of therapeutic importance [26, 28]. The wild-type p53 acts through the activation of a transcription target p21, whose major roles include cell cycle and apoptosis control [29, 30]. Only the wild-type and not mutant-type p53 can stimulate p21 levels and the induction of either apoptosis or arrest of the cell cycle [31, 32]. Therefore, the induction of p53 and p21 levels by DCMF suggests that the mechanism of action of DCMF could be through p53-dependent p21 gene transcriptional activation that usually occurs following DNA injury [33].

We hypothesised that DCMF could cause DNA damage, inhibit cell proliferation, and initiate apoptosis. To confirm this, the expression levels of apoptotic markers were monitored and cytotoxicity tests were conducted. The induction of caspase-3 (Fig. 4), the perinuclear staining of Bcl-2 (Fig. 4), coupled with the significant cytotoxicity (Figs. 1 , 2, and 3) of the PANC-1 cells by DCMF supports our hypothesis. The cytotoxic effect of DCMF on PANC-1 cells in this study was comparable with earlier reports of the cytotoxic effect of common therapeutic agents, such as doxorubicin and resveratrol in PANC-1 cells [34, 35]. Caspase-3 has been reported to participate in the morphological and biochemical changes of apoptotic cells with several nuclear targets despite the cytoplasmic localisation of pro-caspase 3 [36]. Also, Bcl-2 is an apoptotic inhibitor with reports suggesting that its perinuclear localisation promotes apoptosis [37, 38]. Thus, it could be argued that the eventuality of p53 and p21 upregulation in this study was through the apoptotic pathway as reported in previous studies [25, 26, 39].

The induction of catalase and ALP by DCMF suggests its ability to serve as an antioxidant agent, hence, the abrogation of oxidative stress. Catalase is a first-line cellular antioxidant enzyme and an important therapeutic target in cancer treatment which has been upregulated in neoplastic cells by chemotherapeutic agents [40–42]. Similarly, the induction of ALP expression by DCMF is comparable to the effect of some potent therapeutic agents, such as all-trans-retinoic acid on ALP expression in PANC-1 cells [43]. Several polyphenolic compounds like quercetin inhibit cell proliferation activity via induction of ALP functions in cancer cells [44, 45]. The reduction of GST-pi expression by DCMF in this study (Fig. 6) supports the point that Tridax procumbens could be beneficial against oxidative stress and by inference in cancer management. It was long reported that GST protects healthy cells from cytotoxic and cancer-causing agents [46]. However, the expression of GST-pi in cancer cells, an isoform of GST, correlates with malignant transformation, resistance to anticancer agents, and poor prognosis [47, 48]. The inhibition of GST-pi favours apoptosis and suppression of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, especially via Ki-76 reduction and caspase 3 cleavage [49, 50]. The reduction in GST-pi and elevation in both ALP and catalase activities in DCMF-treated PANC-1 cells suggest the potential antioxidant capability of Tridax procumbens. This may be linked to the bioactive compounds identified in the DCMF (Fig. 13).

The upregulation and translocation of adenomatous polyposis coli (APC) from nuclear to cytoplasmic localisation and downregulation of Ki-67 by DCMF (Fig. 8) could translate to the tumour suppressor activity of DCMF vis-à-vis the abrogation of cell proliferation. APC is a negative regulator of cell survival biomarkers, such as beta-catenin, in an activity in the cytoplasm [51, 52]. APC with cytoplasmic loci in physiological states has nuclear localisation in proliferating cells [51]. The dysregulation of APC and APC/GSK3/Axin complex allows beta-catenin from the cytoplasm to link up with TCF/LEF, leading to Wnt downstream target-effectors, such as Ki-67 upregulation, hence, the stimulation of cell proliferation [53]. Ki-67 protein is a cellular marker associated strictly with proliferation [54, 55]. Ki-67 expression in cells indicates tumour cell multiplication rates and this corresponds with initiation, progression, and invasion [54, 56].

The reduced vimentin expression in PANC-1 cells treated with DCMF (Fig. 9) suggests Tridax procumbens inhibitory role on cellular migratory capacity and tumour metastasis or invasiveness. Vimentin, a marker that is highly expressed in PANC-1 cells [57] is involved in the epithelial-mesenchymal transition process which allows mature polarised epithelial cells to go through biochemical changes and take up a mesenchymal phenotype of resistance to apoptosis, increased invasive capacity, and expression of extracellular matrix factors [58, 59]. The DCMF downregulates carbonic anhydrase II (CAII) (Fig. 10), a known cytosolic enzyme that has been implicated in tumours and a target of cancer therapy [60]. CAII downregulation in cancer cells portrays the ability of DCMF to cause possible DNA damage repair or channelling of the cells towards apoptosis [61]. This supports our hypothesis that apoptosis would be the eventuality, as observed with caspase-3 upregulation and Bcl-2 protein perinuclear staining.

In addition, DCMF showed its anti-malignancy attributes by downregulating peanut agglutinin (PNA), a type of lectin (Fig. 10). Some lectins, such as Ricin and Concanavalin A have anticancer functions [62, 63]. PNA has attracted researchers' interest, especially in cancer development [64, 65] due to its high Thomsen-Friedenreich (TF) antigen specificity [66]. The abnormalities in TF and galectin-3 expression encourage cancer progression and invasiveness [67–69].

The induced immunopositivity for the endocrine, exocrine, and ductal markers by DCMF in the pancreatic explants (Figs. 11 and 12) shows the lack of toxicity of Tridax procumbens. These markers were expressed by the various cell types of the pancreatic tissues which develop from the primordia and function to control metabolism [70, 71]. This finding including the enhanced branching morphogenesis of the ductal system in the DCMF-treated embryonic pancreas (Fig. 12) based on increased expression of CK7 and PNA showed that DCMF enhances differentiation in both the exocrine and endocrine pancreas. Similar observations were reported with a notable differentiating agent, retinoic acid, a member of the vitamin A family [72]. However, on the contrary, retinoic acid inhibits exocrine differentiation and suppresses morphogenesis in the pancreas [72, 73]. Differentiating agents that inhibited pancreatic differentiation mechanistically enhanced the secretion of laminin and the activation of apoptosis [72, 74]. Therefore, our finding looks promising with the inability of DCMF to inhibit exocrine differentiation. This could be relevant in an attempt to produce the various cell types of the pancreas in transplantation. Meanwhile, the purified compounds from DCMF in this study and their related analogues have been previously reported in other natural products and implicated with some biological activities, including anticancer properties [75–77]. Interestingly, the anticancer activity of these compounds has not been reported in Tridax procumbens, which we have provided in this study. While these results are encouraging, further investigations are required into the mechanism of action of Tridax procumbens vis-à-vis the use of pathway inhibitors such as inhibitor of apoptosis proteins (IAPs) or rescue assays, as well as the incorporation of in vivo models to validate efficacy and safety.

Conclusion

We have demonstrated in this study the anticancer property of Tridax procumbens and its non-toxicity towards normal embryonic pancreatic tissues. The dichloromethane fraction (DCMF) of Tridax procumbens had significantly higher cytotoxic activity on the PANC-1 cells and enhanced differentiation of both the exocrine and endocrine pancreas. The DCMF downregulated vimentin, PNA, CAII, GST-pi, Ki-67, and upregulated catalase, ALP, caspase-3, APC, p53, and p21Cip1/Wap-1 in the PANC-1 cells (Fig. 16). Five potential compounds were identified from DCMF which may be assessed further for their anticancer properties, namely tsibulin 1, pterosin N, 5,7,3'-trihydroxy-2',4',5'-trimethoxyflavone, corchorifatty acid D, and capsiate. In summary, Tridax procumbens extract appears to exert its effects via induction of apoptosis, antioxidant enzymes, and suppression of markers of growth and proliferation in pancreatic cancer cells.
