Introduction

Duodenal injuries account for less than 5% of blunt abdominal trauma, with most lesions involving the second portion of the duodenum. Injuries to the fourth portion (D4) are exceedingly rare due to its retroperitoneal location and the protection provided by the ligament of Treitz[1]. Their diagnosis is often delayed because clinical signs are subtle, and CT imaging may fail to identify the disruption, particularly when pneumoperitoneum is minimal or absent[2]. Delayed diagnosis of duodenal injuries has been reported in up to 30% of cases and is associated with significantly increased morbidity and mortality. Mortality rates range from 5% to 30%, particularly when surgical management is delayed beyond 24 hours[2].

The mechanism of injury in blunt duodenal trauma typically involves compression of the duodenum against the vertebral column, deceleration forces, or direct abdominal compression, such as the handlebar mechanism described in the literature. These mechanisms particularly affect fixed segments of the duodenum, including the duodenojejunal junction.

Complete transection of D4, extending to the proximal jejunum, is particularly uncommon and poses a diagnostic and therapeutic challenge. This case report is presented in accordance with the SCARE 2025 criteria[3].

Case presentation

A 40-year-old woman with no significant medical history presented to the emergency department following a road traffic accident in which the vehicle she was traveling in was struck by a motorcyclist. On admission, she was hemodynamically stable (GCS 15, SaO₂ 98%, BP 110/70 mmHg, HR 86 bpm). There were no abdominal wall bruises or external impact marks. Physical examination revealed mild epigastric tenderness without guarding or rigidity, and laboratory tests were within normal limits.

Contrast-enhanced CT scan (Fig. 1) demonstrated a segment IV liver contusion, moderate hemoperitoneum, subtle supramesocolic pneumoperitoneum, and mesenteric root infiltration suggestive of intestinal–mesenteric injury. The pancreas appeared normal. The patient had a left renal artery dissection associated with renal devascularization on initial CT imaging. However, renal function tests remained within normal limits, and there was no macroscopic hematuria or hemodynamic instability. In view of the preserved overall clinical stability and the absence of immediate life-threatening complications, a conservative management strategy was adopted with close clinical and biological monitoring. No endovascular or surgical vascular intervention was required during the hospital course. Importantly, the CT scan did not clearly identify duodenal discontinuity, illustrating the recognized diagnostic challenge associated with retroperitoneal duodenal injuries.

Based on CT findings, differential diagnoses included isolated mesenteric injury, hollow viscus perforation, pancreatic trauma, and retroperitoneal duodenal injury. The absence of pancreatic abnormalities on CT imaging reduced the likelihood of pancreatic trauma. However, the presence of supramesocolic pneumoperitoneum and mesenteric root infiltration raised suspicion for proximal small bowel injury, prompting surgical exploration.

In view of these findings, an exploratory laparotomy was performed. A large hemoperitoneum was encountered. Surgical exploration revealed a complete circumferential transection of the fourth portion of the duodenum, extending to the first jejunal loop, with shredded edges and retroperitoneal exposure. The pancreas was intact. Additional findings included a mesenteric root hematoma with active arterial bleeding and partial peritoneal stripping of the ascending colon.

According to the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) classification, the injury corresponded to Grade III duodenal injury.

Surgery was performed under general anesthesia through a midline laparotomy. After the evacuation of hemoperitoneum, careful exploration of the supramesocolic and inframesocolic compartments was conducted. Mobilization of the duodenojejunal angle was performed to expose the injury (Fig. 2). The ligament of Treitz was carefully mobilized to allow for adequate exposure and tension-free reconstruction. Definitive management consisted of debridement of devitalized tissue margins and performing a manual latero-terminal duodenojejunal anastomosis.

The anastomosis was performed using a single-layer interrupted technique with 3–0 absorbable polyglactin sutures. Adequate vascularization and tension-free anastomosis were confirmed (Fig. 3). Wide drainage using Delbet drains was placed in the retroperitoneal region.

Operative time was approximately 120 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 300 ml. Prophylactic antibiotics, consisting of a third-generation cephalosporin and metronidazole, were administered. Deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis included low molecular weight heparin and early mobilization.

The postoperative course was uneventful. The nasogastric tube was removed on postoperative day 3. Oral intake resumed on postoperative day 4. The drains were removed on postoperative day 5. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 8.

No postoperative complications occurred. According to the Clavien–Dindo classification, the postoperative course was Grade I.

Follow-up at 1 month and three months demonstrated favorable outcomes without fistula formation, abdominal pain, or readmission.

Discussion

Duodenal trauma is uncommon and typically results from high-energy blunt mechanisms, such as motor vehicle collisions[1]. The rarity of D4 injuries relates to the anatomical protection afforded by its retroperitoneal position and ligamentous attachment[4].

Epidemiologically, D4 injuries represent less than 1% of duodenal trauma cases. Complete transection of the duodenojejunal junction is even rarer and is usually associated with high-energy mechanisms[5].

Clinical presentation is frequently subtle, with minimal tenderness or peritoneal signs despite complete transection. Laboratory investigations may remain normal, contributing to delayed diagnosis[4].

Imaging is often nonspecific. A CT scan, although the modality of choice, has limited sensitivity in detecting isolated D4 injuries, especially when retroperitoneal air is scant or confined. Findings such as mesenteric root infiltration, unexplained pneumoperitoneum, or localized fluid should raise suspicion[2].

The associated blunt renal artery injury represents an uncommon but recognized vascular complication of high-energy abdominal trauma. Management strategies depend primarily on hemodynamic status, extent of renal ischemia, and associated injuries. In stable patients without ongoing hemorrhage, nonoperative management is generally preferred, particularly when there is no evidence of active bleeding or rapidly deteriorating renal function[6]. In the present case, despite radiological evidence of renal artery dissection with devascularization, the patient remained hemodynamically stable with preserved renal function, supporting a conservative approach. This decision was made in a multidisciplinary trauma context, prioritizing life-threatening hollow viscus injury over vascular intervention.

Management depends on lesion severity, pancreatic involvement, and ampullary integrity. In cases of complete transection with viable edges and an intact pancreas, primary duodenojejunal anastomosis provides definitive and effective treatment[6].

Alternative techniques described in the literature include pyloric exclusion, duodenal diverticulization, and damage control surgery[7]. However, these procedures are generally reserved for complex injuries with pancreatic involvement or hemodynamic instability[8].

Reported complications in the literature include duodenal fistula, intra-abdominal abscess, sepsis, delayed gastric emptying[9], and mortality rates ranging from 5% to 30%, particularly in cases of delayed diagnosis[10–12].

A review of the available literature revealed only isolated reports of fourth portion duodenal injuries following blunt abdominal trauma, most of which involve partial lacerations rather than complete circumferential transection. To the best of our knowledge, no previously published case describes a combined complete transection of the fourth portion of the duodenum extending into the proximal jejunum, associated with a concomitant renal artery dissection, managed successfully in a single-stage operative strategy. This highlights the exceptional rarity of the present case and its contribution to the existing trauma literature.

This report is limited by the relatively short duration of follow-up. Although the early postoperative outcome was favorable, late complications such as anastomotic stricture, adhesive small bowel obstruction, or functional impairment may develop beyond the reported follow-up period. Longer surveillance would be necessary to fully assess long-term outcomes following this rare injury pattern and reconstruction.

Conclusion

Complete transection of the fourth portion of the duodenum following blunt abdominal trauma is exceptionally rare and challenging to diagnose. A subtle clinical presentation and limited CT sensitivity may mask the severity of the injury. Early surgical exploration is essential when indirect radiologic signs suggest mesenteric or retroperitoneal trauma. Duodenojejunal anastomosis offers a safe and effective reconstructive option when pancreatic integrity is preserved.
