Neck migration of a K-wire following fixation of clavicle fracture: a case report
Authors: Clarence Sumbizi, Orujul Hassan, Athar Ali, Christopher Mwansasu, Alex Joseph, Irfan Rajput
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKirschner wires (K-wires) remain a simple and cost-effective option for clavicle fixation, especially in resource-limited settings. However, migration of K-wires is a rare but potentially fatal complication that can involve critical structures in the thorax, mediastinum, or neck. Preventive measures such as bending, burying, and early removal are essential but often overlooked.Case presentationA 35-year-old male underwent K-wire fixation for a displaced left clavicle fracture. Three months postoperatively, he presented with progressive shoulder pain and restricted motion. Imaging revealed migration of a 10 cm K-wire traversing the thoracic inlet into the lower cervical region, closely abutting the trachea and left subclavian vein. The wire was successfully removed through a low cervical-suprasternal approach under fluoroscopic guidance without neurovascular or airway injury. The patient recovered uneventfully, regaining full shoulder function by six weeks and remained asymptomatic at six months follow-up.DiscussionK-wire migration can occur silently within weeks to years after fixation due to muscle activity, respiratory motion, and unanchored wire ends. Neck migration is exceptionally uncommon but can endanger major cervical vessels and the airway. Vigilant radiographic surveillance and timely removal of migrated hardware are critical once migration is detected. This scenario is particularly relevant for orthopedic practice in resource-limited settings where rapid revision fixation is common and wire-end stabilization may be overlooked.ConclusionK-wire migration after clavicular fixation, though rare, carries significant morbidity. Secure fixation, bending or burying of wire ends, regular follow-up imaging, and prompt surgical retrieval of migrated wires are vital to prevent life-threatening complications.

Introduction

Clavicle fractures represent 2.6–10% of all adult fractures, with mid-shaft involvement accounting for ~ 80% of cases [1, 2]. While many fractures can be managed conservatively, operative fixation is increasingly used for displaced, shortened, comminuted, or high-demand injuries [3].

K-wires remain used for clavicular fixation due to low cost, simplicity, and availability particularly in resource-limited setting [4]. Despite their utility, K-wires are associated with a complication profile that, while infrequent, includes pin loosening, breakage, infection, and most alarmingly migration [5].

K-wire migration from the shoulder/clavicular region to the thorax, mediastinum, lungs, heart/great vessels, and spinal canal is well documented, and may occur weeks to years after implantation [6–8]. Although intrathoracic and mediastinal migrations are most frequently described, cervical/neck migration is rare but recognized. The mechanism is multifactorial, proposed mechanisms for migration include muscle action, respiratory excursion and negative intrathoracic pressure, bone resorption around the pin, inadequate anchoring or externalization of wire ends, and persistent micromotion [9].

Given potentially fatal sequelae, Prevention strategies include terminal wire bending/burying, radiographic surveillance, and early removal post-union [10–12]. We report a rare case of K-wire migration into the neck following fixation of a clavicle fracture presenting with only shoulder pain demonstrating how subtle symptoms can mask a life-threatening complication. This case illustrates the critical importance of wire-end anchoring and the risks of rapid revision fixation without proper wire stabilization. This scenario is particularly relevant for orthopedic practice in resource-limited settings where rapid revision fixation is common and wire-end stabilization may be overlooked. This case is reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 guideline [13].

Case presentation

A 35-year-old male presented with a three-week history of progressive left shoulder pain and restriction of overhead movements. He had previously sustained a displaced fracture of the left clavicle following trauma and underwent closed reduction and Kirschner wire (K-wire) fixation at an outside facility approximately three months prior to presentation.

The initial postoperative course was complicated within the first week by a superficial surgical site infection accompanied by loosening of the K-wire (Fig. 1). This was managed at the same facility, and approximately two to three weeks after the initial surgery, the patient underwent revision K-wire fixation. During the revision procedure, the wire was replaced; however, the terminal end was neither bent/anchored nor buried. Approximately three months after the revision procedure, the patient developed gradually worsening left shoulder pain associated with progressive limitation of shoulder abduction and overhead movements. There was no history of repeat trauma, fever, or systemic symptoms. On examination, localized shoulder discomfort was noted, but there were no signs of active infection at the surgical site. Cervical spine examination showed full range of motion without bruit. Neurological and vascular examinations of the left upper limb were unremarkable.

Plain radiographs demonstrated absence of the previously placed K-wire across the clavicle, with a linear metallic density projected into the upper mediastinum (Fig. 2). Subsequent computed tomography (CT) confirmed a 10 cm K-wire traversing the thoracic inlet, situated anterior to the trachea and adjacent to the left subclavian vascular structures, but without vascular or airway injury (Fig. 3). Intraoperatively, the patient was positioned supine with both upper limbs extended at about 70–80 degrees, under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. A transverse suprasternal incision (Sistrom approach) was made for low cervical/thoracic inlet access. With fluoroscopic (C-arm) guidance throughout, careful layer-by-layer dissection was performed: skin and superficial fascia; platysma muscle; investing layer of deep cervical fascia. The left sternocleidomastoid muscle and carotid sheath were identified.

The K-wire was identified in the pretracheal space, posterior to the carotid sheath and anterior to the trachea, abutting the left subclavian vein (Fig. 4). The wire was mobilized gently without traction and extracted under direct visualization. Vascular and ENT team standby was maintained throughout. No intraoperative vascular or airway injury occurred. Hemostasis was achieved with electrocautery and gentle pressure. The incision was closed in layers (platysma, fascia, skin) with no placement of drainage (Fig. 5– 6). The patient was discharged on postoperative day 2 with standard post-operative analgesia. No immediate complications occurred. At six-week follow-up, the patient was pain-free with full active shoulder range of motion, and radiographs showed bone healing with evidence of hard callus. Clinical discussion

K-wires are inexpensive, accessible implants used for clavicular and acromioclavicular stabilization, but their smooth profile predisposes to loosening and migration. Migration may occur silently and at variable intervals, with reported destinations including the thorax, mediastinum, heart and great vessels, lungs, and spinal canal [8, 14]. Neck migration is distinctly uncommon, and typically reflects either soft-tissue migration into the pretracheal/suprasternal space or transforaminal entry into the cervical canal (most often C6–T1), with presentations ranging from incidental findings to radiculopathy and rarely myelopathy [6, 15]. Our patient presented three months after fixation with progressive shoulder pain; imaging revealed a K-wire traversing the thoracic inlet into the neck without initial neurovascular compromise. This case reinforces that subtle musculoskeletal symptoms may be the only herald of a potentially life-threatening problem.

Although plate fixation is often preferred due to better stability and lower migration risk, K-wires continue to be used in some settings because of implant availability, cost constraints, and surgeon familiarity [4]. In this case, revision fixation was performed at an outside facility, and the exact decision-making was not documented; however, this highlights the real-world context in which K-wires may still be selected. When K-wires are used, terminal end anchoring and strict radiographic surveillance are essential to mitigate migration risk.

Several mechanisms have been proposed for migration. Muscular activity of the shoulder girdle, negative intrathoracic pressure during respiration, and gravitational forces may contribute. Inadequate wire anchorage and breakage further increase the risk [16]. In this case, the timeline suggests migration began after revision fixation and progressed silently over three months. Predisposing factors were: (1) absence of terminal wire anchoring (no bending or burying at revision), (2) lack of postoperative radiographic surveillance following revision (no baseline imaging to detect early loosening), and (3) continued shoulder girdle motion and respiratory forces facilitating progressive displacement into the thoracic inlet. Early radiographs after revision would likely have identified wire loosening or migration within days to weeks, enabling prompt retrieval before the wire traversed critical structures.

Published neck migrations include: (1) contralateral transit from the AC joint to the left neck requiring neck dissection [17]; (2) posterolateral C6 location after clavicular fixation in a child, removed percutaneously [18]; and (3) transforaminal C7–T1 migration into the cervical canal necessitating careful extraction [6]. Our case aligns with this spectrum but is notable for an extracanal, pretracheal trajectory closely related to major cervical vessels, successfully managed via a low cervical/suprasternal approach under fluoroscopy.

The importance of imaging in patients with unexplained pain or dysfunction after clavicle fixation cannot be overstated. Radiographs are useful for detecting wire absence or displacement, while CT scanning provides precise localization and relationship to vital structures [6, 19]. In our patient, CT imaging was crucial in surgical planning, as the wire lay close to the subclavian artery and trachea.

Consensus favors urgent extraction of migrated K-wires, including in asymptomatic patients, to prevent cardiovascular, airway, or spinal cord injury [20]. The surgical corridor is determined by the tip’s position and nearby critical anatomy; options include thoracoscopy/thoracotomy, sternotomy, or cervical exploration. Removal should be gentle and non-tractional, with vigilant vascular and airway protection. In our case, although the patient denied red-flag symptoms, cross-sectional imaging demonstrated a 10-cm wire traversing the thoracic inlet, pretracheal and adjacent to the left subclavian artery (Fig. 3). We therefore proceeded to urgent low cervical/suprasternal exploration under fluoroscopic guidance with vascular and ENT standby.

When alternatives exist, authors recommend avoiding smooth K-wires for clavicular/AC constructs. If K-wires are used, mitigate risk by: (i) bending and burying wire ends; (ii) considering threaded wires; (iii) strict radiographic surveillance; and (iv) early removal once union is achieved [9, 19]. Patient counselling about prompt review for chest/neck symptoms is essential.

Our case illustrates that even after a revision that looks fine, migration can still occur within weeks to months. This case highlights the uncommon but serious risk of Kirschner wire migration after clavicle fixation, a complication that can present insidiously with non-specific symptoms such as shoulder pain and restricted motion. Our patient’s outcome demonstrates how seemingly minor factors including early surgical site infection, reinsertion without a properly anchored wire, and inadequate follow-up can interact to permit progressive migration into dangerous anatomical territory. Published reports consistently warn that even though the absolute incidence of migration is low, the associated morbidity can be life-threatening, particularly when wires advance toward the mediastinum, lungs, or great vessels.

Conclusion

K-wire migration after clavicle fixation is rare but potentially lethal. Neck migration, though uncommon, demands a high index of suspicion when patients re-present with shoulder or neck pain and stiffness even in the absence of cardiopulmonary or neurologic symptoms. Urgent cross-sectional imaging (CT), multidisciplinary surgical planning, and timely retrieval are essential. Prevention requires secure wire fixation, terminal anchoring (bending and burying of wire ends), postoperative radiographic surveillance, and early hardware removal once union is achieved.
