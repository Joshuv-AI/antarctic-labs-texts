High night-to-night variability in obstructive sleep apnea severity is associated with prevalent cardiovascular disease
Authors: Bastien Lechat, Lucía Pinilla, Kelly Sansom, Andrew Vakulin, Pierre Escourrou, Jean-Louis Pépin, Sébastien Bailly, Sébastien Baillieul, Amy C Reynolds, Hannah Scott, Jack Manners, Peter Catcheside, Robert J Adams, Danny J Eckert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesHigh night-to-night variability in obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) severity is associated with cardiovascular risk factors such as high blood pressure and pulse wave velocity, yet associations with cardiovascular events remain unknown. This study investigates the association of multi-night OSA severity and night-to-night variability with the prevalence of non-fatal major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs).Materials and MethodsMulti-night data were collected from people who purchased and used an FDA-cleared under-mattress sensor to track nightly OSA severity (apnea-hypopnea index; AHI) and completed questionnaires on physician-diagnosed health conditions between 10/2022–12/2024. Mean OSA severity and variability (AHI standard deviation) were calculated over 6 months preceding questionnaire data collection. The primary outcome was a composite of non-fatal MACCEs, including myocardial infarction or heart attack, stroke, angina pectoris or coronary artery disease, and congestive heart failure. Logistic models assessed associations of OSA severity and variability with MACCE prevalence.ResultsAmong 3,159 participants (19% female, aged 49 ± 13 years, BMI of 29 ± 6 kg/m2), 142 (4.5%) MACCE cases were reported. Participants with moderate–severe OSA had higher odds of MACCEs versus those without OSA (OR [95%CI]; 1.45 [0.93, 2.25]). High night-to-night OSA variability (75th vs. 25th percentile; 8.0 vs. 2.8 events/h) was associated with 34% higher odds of having a MACCE, independent of OSA severity and other confounders (1.34 [1.04, 1.72]).ConclusionsHigh night-to-night variability in OSA severity is associated with higher odds of non-fatal MACCEs. These novel findings underscore the need for prospective trials to investigate the potential causal mechanisms between variability in OSA severity and incident cardiovascular events.Keywords: sleep disordered breathing, cardiovascular risk, cardiovascular events, chronic disease, OSA variability, phenotype

High night-to-night variability in obstructive sleep apnea severity is associated with cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., hypertension and pulse wave velocity) yet associations with major cardiovascular events remain unknown. This study shows that high night-to-night variability in sleep apnea severity is associated with higher odds of reporting a major cardiovascular event, beyond average sleep apnea severity and other confounders. These findings suggest night-to-night variability may reflect a high-risk patient subgroup currently missed by gold-standard single-night diagnostic practices. Mechanistic causal pathways between sleep apnea variability and cardiovascular disease warrant further investigation. Multi-night home monitoring using consumer sleep technologies offers a feasible approach not only to improve misclassification rates but also to potentially enhance cardiovascular risk phenotyping.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a prevalent chronic health condition with widespread multi-organ effects that contributes to significant global economic and societal burden [1–4]. Due to the substantial heterogeneity in OSA pathogenesis and disease consequences, prognosis and treatment responses vary markedly between patients. This poses challenges to identify those at most risk of adverse outcomes and to tailor treatment strategies effectively [5, 6].

The apnea-hypopnoea index (AHI), typically assessed on a single-night sleep study, is the standard metric used to diagnose OSA and can help guide treatment decisions [7]. However, the AHI, and other OSA severity metrics, can vary substantially from night-to-night within an individual [8]. It is estimated that 20% of people may receive an inaccurate diagnosis using conventional, single-night, gold-standard polysomnography (PSG) [7, 9, 10]. Additionally, up to 50% may experience a change in their OSA severity categorization across different nights [7, 10]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed substantial intraindividual variability in AHI, leading to high rates of misdiagnosis and severity misclassification with important implications for suboptimal therapeutic decisions [11].

In addition to influencing diagnostic accuracy, night-to-night OSA variability has been associated with clinically relevant disease outcomes. Emerging evidence suggests that high night-to-night variability in OSA severity is associated with poorer cardiovascular health [7, 12]. Independent of OSA severity, higher OSA variability has been associated with adverse cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors, including increased odds of uncontrolled hypertension, greater blood pressure variability, higher pulse wave velocity, and increased occurrence of atrial fibrillation events [13–15]. Additionally, a recent longitudinal prospective study reported an association between high night-to-night variability in respiratory disturbance index and the incidence of persistent atrial fibrillation [16]. Specifically, a 10% increase in the coefficient of variation was associated with an 89% higher risk of persistent atrial fibrillation (hazard ratio [95%CI]; 1.89 [1.26, 2.84]), independent of mean respiratory disturbance index [16]. However, the potential association of high OSA variability with major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCEs) has not been investigated. Accordingly, this study evaluated the independent cross-sectional associations between OSA severity and variability and prevalent non-fatal MACCEs, including myocardial infarction or heart attack, stroke, angina pectoris or coronary artery disease, and congestive heart failure.

Materials and Methods

Participants and study design

Data were acquired from people who purchased a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-cleared under-mattress sleep sensor for personal use (Withings Sleep Analyzer/Sleep Rx; WSA; FDA 510(k) number: K231667) and participated in the Withings research program between April 2022 and December 2024. The Withings research program is a study conducted to develop novel diagnostic tools and predictive algorithms. Within the research program, participants are asked questions related to their general health, doctor-diagnosed disorders, and health-related behaviors. The study was conducted in strict adherence to the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and HIPAA security standards. All participants provided consent through the Withings application for their deidentified data to be used for research purposes. When users sign-up to the Withings application, they are prompted to fill in their age, sex, height, and weight. This study was also approved by the Flinders University Human Research Ethics Committee (Project number: 4291). This was an unfunded, investigator-initiated study led by the Flinders University, FHMRI: Sleep Health research team.

Questionnaires

Questionnaires were delivered via the Withings mobile application between October 2022 and December 2024 to users that had been using a WSA for at least 1 month. In the questionnaire, participants self-reported conditions for which they received a doctor diagnosis (“Has a doctor ever told you that you had any of the following”). Heart conditions included hypertension or high blood pressure; angina pectoris or coronary artery disease; congestive heart failure; myocardial infarction or heart attack; atrial fibrillation; stroke; other heart or blood vessel conditions; or “no known heart or blood vessel condition”. Other doctor-diagnosed conditions included emphysema, or asthma, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; diabetes; obesity; metabolic syndrome; OSA; depression; anxiety; insomnia; other conditions, or “no known conditions”. In addition to doctor-diagnosed conditions, there were three additional questions on general health and behavior, including medication use (number of medications), smoking status, and alcohol intake. See supplementary material for exact phrasing on questions. Some participants (~30%) were sent questionnaire batteries multiple times (six months apart), in which case this analysis only included the first questionnaire entry.

OSA monitoring

The WSA is a sleep monitoring device placed under the mattress that quantifies the AHI, sleep duration, sleep timing, sleep staging, and markers of sleep quality (e.g. awakenings). Specifically, this device consists of a pressure-sensitive air bladder placed under the mattress that detects mechanical vibrations transmitted through the mattress. By filtering the pressure signal across different frequency bands, it separates out breathing movements, heart rate (via ballistocardiography), and body movements. A built-in microphone also captures breathing and snoring sounds. These signals are then processed by machine learning algorithms including a Random Forest Classifier to detect sleep/wake states and a 1D Convolutional Neural Network to estimate apnea and hypopnea events ultimately computing AHI and sleep metrics without any contact with the user [17]. Multiple independent studies in over 600 participants have validated WSA-estimated sleep duration against PSG [18–20]. Similar to other wearable/nearable devices [21, 22], the WSA tends to overestimate sleep duration by ~20–40 minutes versus PSG.

The WSA-estimated AHI has good agreement with in-laboratory PSG-derived AHI, with ~80% sensitivity and specificity to classify moderate-to-severe OSA across multiple validation studies [10, 17, 23], similar to other FDA-cleared wearables and nearables [24]. An initial validation study reported high accuracy for mild OSA classification (AHI ≥5 events/h, sensitivity = 89%, specificity = 75%), moderate-to-severe OSA classification (AHI ≥15 events/h, sensitivity = 88%, specificity = 88%), and severe OSA classification (AHI ≥30 events/h, sensitivity = 86%, specificity = 91%) [17]. When evaluated as a continuous variable, WSA-estimated AHI has minimal bias (mean [95% limits of agreement]: 1.6 [−0.7, 4.1] [17] and -1.3810 events/hour), and a moderate-to-strong correlation with PSG-derived AHI (r = 0.77 [25]; r = 0.40 [17]; r = 0.69 [23], r = 0.70 [10]). Additional validation studies have evaluated device performance using PSG soring based on the AASM 2012 criteria, including hypopnea definitions requiring a ≥ 30% airflow reduction with either ≥3% oxygen desaturation or an arousal [26]. The AHI estimated by the WSA cannot be reliably calculated for nights with a sleep duration of less than 5h17, thus these nights were excluded from the analyses. The device does not differentiate between central and obstructive apnea events. Previous literature suggests that a minimum of 14 nightly AHI recordings is required to obtain a stable estimate of OSA severity [7, 10].

Exposures

Nightly sleep recordings in the six months preceding each questionnaire entry were extracted. Participants with less than 14 available AHI recordings in that period were excluded [7, 10]. The averaged AHI over the six-month period was calculated and used to categorize OSA severity following clinically defined cutoff values [27]; <5 no OSA, ≥5 and < 15 mild, ≥15 and < 30 moderate, and ≥ 30 events/h sleep as severe OSA. Night-to-night OSA variability was calculated over the six-month recording period using the standard deviation of nightly AHI values for each individual [14].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was a composite measure of non-fatal MACCEs. The first endpoint (MACCE 0) comprised myocardial infarction or heart attack, and stroke. The second endpoint (MACCE 1) additionally accounted angina pectoris or coronary artery disease. The last endpoint (MACCE 2) further included congestive heart failure.

Statistical analyses

Separate logistic regression models were used to investigate associations between OSA severity and OSA variability with the prevalence of MACCE. Three different models were constructed per exposure. The first model was adjusted for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Missing weight values (~20%) were imputed using predictive mean matching. The second model was additionally adjusted for physician-diagnosed OSA (yes/no), alcohol consumption, and smoking status. The third model was additionally adjusted for physician-diagnosed obesity, insomnia, diabetes, depression, anxiety, and hypertension. All models that evaluated association between OSA variability and MACCE were additionally adjusted for average OSA severity. Since some of the conditions in the third model are likely to be mediators rather than confounders (e.g. hypertension [14]), model 2 was selected as the primary model for interpretation of the results. Multicollinearity between variables was evaluated using the variance inflation factors, a measure of collinearity with pre-specified cut-offs [28]. A variance inflation factor above 5 or 10 is usually considered as an indicator of multi-collinearity [28]. We tested for potential non-linear associations using restricted cubic splines with 3 knots. The logistic regression results were presented as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI), comparing the 75th percentile with the 25th percentile of the continuous exposure variable. In secondary analyses, we performed stratified analyses to determine whether the association between OSA variability and MACCE was consistently observed across clinically defined multi-night OSA severity categories (mild OSA and moderate-to-severe OSA). Models were implemented in the R programming language (version 4.3.3) using the rms package [29].

Results

Participant demographics

A total of 3,277 regular WSA users participated in the Withings research program. After applying exclusion criteria, 24 participants were removed due to missing demographic data (height, age, or sex), 25 participants were excluded for opting not to answer questions on CVD. Lastly, 69 participants were excluded for having fewer than 14 valid AHI measurements in the six months preceding their questionnaire entry. Demographic information for the remaining 3,159 participants by categories of average OSA severity is shown in Table 1. Most data originated from the United States (~73%), followed by Australia (~10%) and Canada (~7%). Most participants completed the questionnaire between January and April 2023 (Figure S1). Approximately 29% of participants who reported a physician diagnosis of OSA presented an average AHI below 5; 34% between 5 and 15; 22% between 15 and 30; and 15% over 30 events/h. In a model adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and mean AHI, physician-diagnosed OSA was associated greater night-to-night OSA variability (β [95%CI]; 0.76 [0.49, 1.03] events/hour increase). The number and proportion of MACCE event cases across quartiles of OSA variability are presented in Table S1. Demographics and rates of co-morbidities observed in our sample is similar to other large cohort and population studies in people with obstructive sleep apnea (Table S2).

Multi-night OSA severity and prevalent CVD

Moderate-to-severe OSA tended to be associated with MACCE compared to the No OSA group, particularly in the fully adjusted model (Figure 1). In our primary model specification (model 2), moderate-to-severe OSA was associated with an OR (95%CI); 1.51 (0.88, 2.60), 1.52 (0.97, 2.44) and 1.45 (0.93, 2.25) increase in the odds of MACCE 0, MACCE 1 and MACCE 2, respectively. Exact ORs and 95%CIs for OSA severity categories for all models are available in Table S3. The association between average OSA severity and MACCE was inconsistently non-linear (see Figure S2 and supplementary results). We found no significant associations between OSA severity and MACCE when OSA severity was considered a continuous variable (Figure S3).

OSA variability and prevalent CVD

Independent of average OSA severity, high night-to-night OSA variability (75th vs. 25th; 8.0 vs. 2.8 events/h) was associated with 31 to 42% higher odds of having a MACCE 2, depending on the model (Table 2). The association between OSA variability and MACCE 1 endpoint showed a similar effect size, but larger 95%CI likely due to the lower number of events (Table 2). There were no significant associations between OSA variability and MACCE0. There was no evidence of collinearity between model variables since all variance inflation factors were below 2.5. Interaction analyses showed no evidence of effect modification by prior physician-diagnosed OSA in the association between OSA variability and MACCE0 (p-value = .14), MACCE1 (p-value = .25), or MACCE2 (p-value = .22). Non-linear association between night-to-night variability in OSA severity, independently of averaged OSA severity, was not consistently significant across all outcomes, and little differences were observed in the exposure-response curves between linear vs. non-linear models (Figure 2).

Discussion

The current study shows that high night-to-night OSA variability is associated with a 34% increase in the odds of having reported a non-fatal major cardiovascular and cerebrovascular event, independent of average OSA severity, doctor-diagnosed OSA, and other socio-demographics and behavioral factors. These novel findings further add to the growing literature that indicates that highly variable OSA severity is associated with poorer cardiovascular health, and is currently uncaptured with gold-standard single-night diagnostic testing practices.

OSA severity, as measured using single-night AHI, has been associated with increased prevalence of CVD and greater incidence of cardiovascular events in some studies [30, 31] but not others [3, 6, 32, 33]. In addition, three randomized trials of continuous positive airway pressure treatment for OSA, determined using single-night AHI, have failed to demonstrate reduced risk of MACCE [34–36]. Similarly, we found inconsistencies in the association between our estimated average AHI and the prevalence of MACCE in our data. No associations were found when the AHI was considered as a continuous variable with a linear term, but there was a trend for an associations when AHI was modeled with splines. However, using OSA severity categories also suggested a potential association between multi-nights derived moderate-to-severe OSA and prevalence of MACCE. When further adjusting for other co-morbid conditions, also associated with OSA, the association between moderate-to-severe OSA and MACCE was significant and had a larger effect size than the other models. However, the interpretation of this model may be problematic, since many of the co-morbidities may be mediators rather than confounders, and may lead to overadjustment bias [37].

Previous studies have reported an independent association between high night-to-night OSA variability and higher odds of uncontrolled hypertension, increased blood pressure variability, higher pulse wave velocity, and increased risk of atrial fibrillation events [14, 15, 38]. These well-established cardiovascular risk factors could mediate, at least in part, the association between OSA variability with non-fatal cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events. Repetitive exposure to varying degrees of OSA severity may perpetuate a state of oxidative stress, sympathetic overactivation, and metabolic dysfunction, leading to vascular stress and susceptivity to cardiovascular pathology [39, 40]. CVDs are the leading cause of mortality worldwide and a major contributor to loss of health, reduced quality of life, and excess health system costs [41, 42]. Given the global burden of CVD, understanding the association between night-to-night OSA variability and CVD progression may have important implications for both patient management and public health strategies. Some studies have identified behavioral and environmental factors (e.g. smoking, alcohol intake, ambient temperature, social behaviors) that could contribute to higher cardiovascular risk among patients with highly variable OSA severity [7, 43–46]. Here, all models were controlled for a variety of confounding factors, including smoking and alcohol consumption, which did not affect the observed associations.

Reverse causality could also partly explain some of the findings, as having suffered a major CVD event may lead to higher variability in OSA severity. For example, high OSA variability has been observed among patients with established heart failure [47], hypothesized by the authors to potentially be due to an increase in rostral fluid shifts [48]. This is unlikely to fully explain the observed association in this study, since the effect size of the associations was consistent across MACCE definitions, including without inclusion of congestive heart failure. Although the evidence is still emerging and is limited by single-night estimates [49, 50], OSA severity following a stroke episode has been shown to be relatively stable [51, 52]. Major cardiovascular events are also associated with prevalent and incident insomnia symptoms [53], depression, and anxiety [54], which could induce higher variability in OSA severity through modified sleep structure [55]. However, our findings were robust after adjusting for previous self-reported diagnosis of insomnia, depression or anxiety. A potential causal pathway between OSA variability and incident cardiovascular events is also supported by a recent study that demonstrated a positive association between OSA variability and incident atrial fibrillation events [16]. Reverse causality is a ubiquitous limitation in pragmatic observational cross-sectional studies. Nonetheless, the current findings provide the first level of evidence and highlight the importance for prospective longitudinal studies as well as targeted mechanistic studies to fully understand this new line of investigation on the relationship between OSA variability and CVD.

There are some limitations which should be considered when interpreting the current findings. Data were limited to a selection of questions so as to not overburden participants. Therefore, clinical information regarding other co-morbid conditions, medication use, sleepiness, and potential OSA treatment status was unavailable. In addition, although we included self-reported physician-diagnosed insomnia in our fully adjusted model, no gold-standard assessment of insomnia was available, precluding further investigation of night-to-night variability in people with co-morbid insomnia and OSA (COMISA), a subgroup known to be at greater risk of CVD and all-cause mortality [56–58]. The population sample is also biased towards men, which precluded us from investigating sex-specific associations between OSA variability and CVD due to low power to detect an association in women. Thus, this study sample may not be representative of the wider general population and does not resolve the long-standing issue in sleep research of under-representation of women in such analyses. Participants were likely of higher socio-economic status since they could afford to purchase an unsubsidized sleep tracking device. In addition, participants in this cohort were younger and overall healthier than other clinical and population cohort studies (Table S2). Accordingly, this may represent a source of bias in this consumer sample towards younger individuals with a greater interest in health-related topics. Therefore, there is a need for prospective studies in a population-based sample to further validate our findings and their generalizability.

Another important methodological consideration of this study is the potential for survival bias, since we relied on self-reported MACCE and thus, did not have information on all-cause mortality and fatal cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events. In addition, the use of self-reported outcome data introduces the possibility for recall bias and misclassification. These limitations may have attenuated the magnitude of the observed associations between OSA severity or variability and adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Nonetheless, structured self-report of physician-diagnosed cardiovascular events using questions such as “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that you had…?” is widely employed in large epidemiological studies, such as the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey study [59] and the Sleep Heart Health Study [30]. In addition, validation studies demonstrate good agreement between self-reported cardiovascular events and general practitioner records (>85% concordance) [60, 61], as well as hospital-based records [61] (~80% accuracy), supporting the use of self-reported MACCE as a pragmatic approach in large-scale population studies.

The under-mattress sensor captures limited signals to detect respiratory events and associated physiological responses, and thus, does not calculate novel OSA metrics such as hypoxic burden, pulse wave amplitude drops, or delta heart rate, which have demonstrated independent and additional prognostic value for to predict cardiovascular events and mortality beyond the AHI [62–67]. Consequently, we were unable to examine the potential association of night-to-night variability in these novel physiological metrics with CVD. Since some of these novel markers have been shown to vary from night to night [68, 69], it is plausible that variability in these metrics may show differential associations with cardiovascular outcomes than variability in AHI alone. Future studies that incorporate multimodal physiological recordings are warranted to determine whether variability in these novel OSA metrics are associated with increased cardiovascular risk. Similarly, the device does not differentiate between different types of apnea events (hypopnea, obstructive vs. central apneas, etc.), and it is unclear if the presence of central sleep apnea may have influenced the observed associations.

In many areas of medicine, it is well established that single time-point assessments are insufficient to enable accurate diagnosis and risk stratification. In conditions such as hypertension and diabetes, continuous, ambulatory, and multi-day monitoring provide more informative indicators of disease outcomes than isolated measurements [70–74]. Blood pressure variability, for example, is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular events, all-cause mortality, vascular organ damage, atrial fibrillation, and dementia, independently of average blood pressure [75–80]. With advancements in sleep-tracking technology, multi-night home OSA assessments have become increasingly feasible [24]. This presents a valuable opportunity to examine how night-to-night variations in OSA severity influence health outcomes and emerges as a promising tool to identify patients most at risk of adverse outcomes.

In conclusion, we detected an independent association between high night-to-night variability in OSA severity, and a higher prevalence of non-fatal cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events. This novel finding underscores the importance of multi-night assessments for improved OSA diagnosis and management in clinical care. High night-to-night variability in OSA severity may represent a novel high-risk OSA patient subgroup, currently missed by current single-night gold-standard practices for OSA diagnosis.
