Feasibility of a blended learning approach to teaching comprehensive geriatric assessment: a mixed-methods educational study
Authors: Xiaoting Huang, K Sri Karpageshwary, Ying Wang, Anupama Roy Chowdhury, Angela H W Yap, Rachel Q M Ng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite its proven value in older adult care, Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) remains inconsistently taught in postgraduate training worldwide. Blended learning, which combines self-directed online modules with supervised clinical application, offers a promising approach to address these gaps.ObjectiveTo evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and educational promise of a theory-informed blended learning program.MethodsThis single-centre, explanatory sequential mixed-methods feasibility study included 12 postgraduate trainees rotating through geriatric medicine. Participants completed e-learning modules and applied CGA under clinical supervision. Outcomes included pre- and post-program confidence ratings (18-item CGA-specific scale), knowledge scores (standardized quiz), and qualitative feedback. Data was analysed using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and Braun & Clarke’s thematic analysis, with integration guided by the Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study framework.ResultsAll 12 participants completed the program, demonstrating high feasibility. Confidence across CGA domains improved significantly (mean increase: 1.2 ± 0.5 on a 5-point scale, p < 0.01), with the greatest gains observed in cognitive, mood, and care planning domains. Knowledge scores trended upward but were not statistically significant, underscoring the exploratory nature of this feasibility study (mean increase: 1.5 ± 2.7, p = 0.13). Qualitative findings reinforced the value of mentorship, real-world application, and structured learning progression.ConclusionsThis feasibility study demonstrated that a theory-informed blended learning model was practical to implement, well-received by learners, and associated with improved confidence. As one of the first studies to combine adult learning theory, logic modelling, and mixed-methods evaluation in CGA education, it provides a foundation for broader implementation in diverse, resource-constrained training environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09097-8.Keywords: Geriatric education, Comprehensive geriatric assessment, Blended learning, Postgraduate medical education, Mixed-methods research

Introduction

Background

Comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) reliably improves post-hospital outcomes when conducted in the inpatient setting for older adults during initial admission. A 2017 Cochrane review of CGA conducted for inpatient older adults reported higher home discharge rates and fewer institutionalisations [1, 2], findings mirrored in surgical cohorts that show reduced mortality, length of stay and cost, particularly after hip fracture [3]. These data have driven CGA’s adoption in cardiology [4], oncology [5] and other specialties treating older adults.

Although CGA is adopted in various specialities it has common constraints—limited geriatric faculty, non-standard training [6], short rotations [7]—faced across Asia–Pacific, North America and Europe.

Current gaps

Existing curricula available for CGA teaching are heterogeneous, classroom-heavy and weak on supervised practice [8]. A systematic review looking into barriers surrounding implementation identified inconsistent content teaching, minimal hands-on exposure and few outcome studies as significant challenges [9]. This is where a blended teaching approach can assist.

Blended learning that combines online modules with practical application offers several advantages [10–12] in medical education. It provides flexibility for learners to engage with the content at their own pace while maintaining the benefits of supervised clinical practice. This approach is particularly relevant for CGA training, where theoretical understanding is coupled with practical skills development. Evidence from comparative analysis suggests that blended learning can enhance knowledge retention and clinical competence while accommodating diverse learning styles and schedules [13].

There is currently no structured CGA teaching programme that has explored pairing theory-informed e-modules with structured bedside coaching. This creates the need for innovative yet evidence-based training solutions as no prior CGA educational intervention has been evaluated using a theory-driven, blended learning model combining e-modules, real-world supervision, and mixed-methods assessment.

Theoretical framework

Our proposed blended curriculum applies Knowles’ adult-learning [10] principles of autonomy, readiness and immediate applicability. Online cases present concepts; ward supervision reinforces them; graduated case complexity promotes reflection. Mapping each activity to the six principles guided both design and evaluation as shown in Fig. 1.

Study aims

This feasibility study aimed to (1) assess the acceptability and practicality of implementing a blended CGA training program, (2) explore potential improvements in clinician confidence and knowledge, and (3) gather qualitative insights to inform program refinement. The study was not powered to detect definitive effectiveness outcomes, but to inform future program scaling and evaluation design.

To address these aims, we employed a mixed-methods approach where feasibility was assessed through completion rates and resource utilization, knowledge improvement through pre-post quantitative measures, and program refinement through qualitative feedback analysis. This structured approach enables a systematic exploration of the study objectives while remaining consistent with the aims of a feasibility investigation focused on acceptability and implementation.

Methods

Study design

We used an explanatory-sequential mixed-methods design, reported per Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) [14], at Singapore General Hospital (SGH). Quantitative measures (knowledge quiz, confidence survey) were collected first; purposive qualitative sampling then explored learning processes. Integration occurred at sampling, analysis and interpretation.

Sample size and power considerations

Consistent with feasibility-study guidance [15, 16], we enrolled 12 postgraduate-year-2 or higher doctors rotating through Department of Geriatric Medicine at SGH. This number matched routine rotation size and allowed faculty supervision without extra resource strain. Eligibility required at least one month of ward experience and no prior formal CGA training. The cohort was adequate to test acceptability and logistics, not effectiveness.

Program components

Two weeks before the rotation, participants completed self-paced modules on geriatric syndromes and medication review. Interactive cases, quizzes and dashboards promoted active learning. Three geriatricians authored the content and piloted it with four non-study learners. During the rotation, each participant conducted ward and clinic CGAs under direct supervision and received structured feedback. Faculty time and institutional Learning Management System (LMS) capacity were tracked for scalability; full details appear in the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) [17] checklist in Table 3 of Supplementary Material.

Assessment methods

Clinical integration formed the cornerstone of our blended learning program. Learners applied their theoretical knowledge in both inpatient and outpatient settings under a geriatrician’s supervision. This practical component was crucial for developing competence in CGA implementation, with structured feedback sessions ensuring continuous improvement. Regular tutorials with geriatricians complemented the hands-on experience, providing opportunities for case discussions and reflective learning.

Knowledge evaluation employed a standardized quiz with a maximum score of 27 points. The instrument is formative, pilot in nature and validity testing is planned for the next study phase. The CGA confidence questionnaire used in this study was adapted from the domains and behavioural competency areas outlined in the 2017 “A Competency Framework for Interprofessional Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment” [18] developed by the Regional Geriatric Programs of Ontario. The version of the questionnaire utilised is present in the supplementary material. This framework defines detailed practice expectations across six core domains: Core Geriatric Knowledge, Screening and Risk Identification, Analysis and Interpretation, Care Planning and Intervention, Interprofessional Practice, and Professional Practice. Our questionnaire items were mapped to relevant behavioural statements and assessment domains to ensure comprehensive coverage of core CGA competencies.

An adapted 18-item, five-point Likert questionnaire assessed self-reported confidence across six Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment domains. Content validity was established through review by three geriatric medicine experts, and face validity was confirmed via cognitive interviews with two independent learners not involved in the study.

Semi-structured feedback questionnaires explored learning experience, feasibility and resource use. Questions focused on learning experiences and preferences, explored perceived barriers and facilitators, program component effectiveness and suggestions for improvement. Although this limited opportunities for open ended response and follow-up clarification opportunities, it provided rich contextual information that helped explain the quantitative outcomes and identify areas for program enhancement. Feasibility was assessed through recruitment and retention rates, program completion rates, resource requirement and program satisfaction.

Analysis approach

Quantitative analysis

We adopted conservative statistical approaches appropriate for the small sample size. Pre- and post-intervention comparisons were conducted using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, with exact p-values reported where applicable. For variables with non-normal distributions, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were presented instead of means. Given the multiple comparisons performed, results were interpreted with caution, emphasizing overall patterns and practical significance rather than isolated p-values. The analysis prioritized descriptive trends to reflect the exploratory nature of this feasibility study.

Qualitative analysis

Our qualitative analysis followed Braun and Clarke's [19] six-phase framework for thematic analysis, adapted for Excel-based data collection and analysis. Two researchers (XH, AY) independently conducted the analysis with oversight from a senior researcher (KS). The flow of this process is demonstrated in Fig. 1 in Supplemental Material.

Following Fetters’ typology [20], we connected data by sampling qualitative comments from learners with the greatest confidence change, merged datasets in a joint display, and embedded themes to explain discordant results. All three analyst-authors (XH, AY, KS) were also clinical educators in the programme. Each maintained a reflexive diary, and regular peer-debrief sessions were held to surface and bracket personal assumptions.

We acknowledged study limitations transparently, including sample size constraints and potential selection bias, while ensuring our conclusions remained appropriately bounded by the evidence. While the feedback questionnaires provided useful contextual data, they limited the depth and nuance of responses. The absence of follow-up probing may have constrained exploration of emergent themes. Future studies should consider using in-depth interviews or focus groups to enrich the qualitative dataset and enable deeper insight into learner experiences.

Logic model

To ensure systematic alignment between program components and desired outcomes, we developed a logic model (Fig. 2 in Supplemental Material) mapping the relationships between inputs, activities, outputs, and impacts. This model guided both program implementation and evaluation, while providing a framework for future scaling and sustainability planning. The model explicitly links our theoretical framework to practical implementation steps and measurable outcomes.

This approach ensured each component was aligned with adult learning principles and contributed to the intended outcomes. The feedback loops integrated within the model enabled continuous program refinement, informed learner experiences and observed outcomes. The model also facilitates identification of critical resources and processes needed for successful program implementation and scaling.

Results

Having established our methodological framework, we now present the findings following our sequential mixed-methods design.

Participant characteristics

The cohort comprised 12 learners with diverse training backgrounds and exposure to geriatric medicine (Table 1). Majority were senior learners (58.3% at PGY6 and above), complemented by junior learners at PGY2 (16.7%), PGY3 (16.7%), and PGY5 (8.3%). Posting duration varied, with half completing one-month rotations, 41.7% in three-month postings, and 8.3% undertaking six-month attachments. This distribution enabled evaluation across different experience levels and exposure durations.

Feasibility outcomes

All 12 participants fulfilled program requirements, suggesting high feasibility and adherence. Learner satisfaction was high (mean scores > 4.0) as shown in Table 1 of Supplementary Material.

Resource utilization

Faculty devoted approximately 2 h per learner for supervision and feedback. Developing the four e-modules took 60 h across three geriatricians. The existing LMS handled hosting and tracking without extra cost. Once built, content can be reused and supervision calibrated through monthly meetings, supporting sustainable delivery across rotations.

Quantitative outcomes

All 12 participants completed the blended learning program. Confidence scores across all 18 CGA domains showed statistically significant improvement, with a mean increase of 1.2 ± 0.5 (p < 0.01). The most substantial gains were seen in cognitive assessment (1.4 ± 0.5), mood evaluation (1.4 ± 0.8), malnutrition evaluation (1.4 ± 0.8) and comprehensive assessment planning (1.5 ± 0.5) as demonstrated in Table 1 of Supplementary Material.

Knowledge scores increased from a mean of 21.3 ± 4.6 at baseline to 22.8 ± 3.2 post-intervention (mean change 1.5 ± 2.7, p = 0.13). Although this change was not statistically significant, the narrower score range post-intervention suggests more consistent knowledge acquisition across participants. A weak positive correlation was observed between confidence gains and knowledge score changes (ρ = 0.27, p = 0.40).

Program satisfaction was high, with learners rating the online modules and combined tutorial/clinical application as 4.1 ± 0.7 and 4.2 ± 0.6 out of 5, respectively.

Integrated analysis

We identified several key interconnected findings which are represented in a joint synthesis table in Table 2 of Supplementary Material.

Integrated analysis of mixed methods findings

Effect-size analysis showed that the confidence outcomes were not just statistically significant but also practically important. Overall confidence improved by a very large margin (d = 2.58). At the domain level, gains were very large for “Assessment of an older person with multiple problems” (d = 3.43) and “Conducting an assessment within identified domains of the CGA” (d = 3.32). In contrast, the knowledge-quiz improvement was small (d = 0.33) and remained non-significant, suggesting limited cognitive change despite substantial boosts in self-efficacy. These quantitative patterns are unpacked in the Integrated Analysis (Table 2 of Supplementary Material), where learners’ reflections on mastering structured assessment steps echoed the large confidence gains, while reports of difficulty applying online content to bedside cases contextualised the modest knowledge-quiz change.

Discussion

This feasibility study provides early insights into the practicality and educational value of a theory-informed, blended learning program for Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA). Within a busy postgraduate clinical rotation, the program was successfully delivered with full completion rates, high learner satisfaction, and encouraging trends in self-reported confidence. These outcomes suggest that structured, blended instructional design—grounded in adult learning theory—can be implemented feasibly and meaningfully in routine clinical training environments.

Confidence gains mirrored learners’ written comments about handling complex cases, indicating that both data sources pointed to the same improvement. Learners progressed from checklist-based task performance toward a more holistic understanding of CGA as a clinical reasoning process. This shift highlights the value of a program structure that balances autonomy, supervision, and real-world application. By examining outcomes and learner narratives, we gained insight into the mechanisms that fostered these changes—such as graded complexity, mentorship, and flexible learning formats—demonstrating the strength of theory-driven mixed-methods evaluation in capturing nuanced educational transformation.

While improvements in knowledge scores were modest, qualitative feedback emphasised the significance of experiential learning with contextually integrated feedback. Learners highlighted the teaching approach to be ‘relevant to daily clinical practice’ and direct feedback allowing ‘ correction in real time setting’. These findings are consistent with broader medical education literature, which emphasises that confidence and competence develop through a combination of didactic content, reflective practice, and supported clinical immersion. The model tackles common constraints—limited geriatric faculty [21] and short rotations [22]—making it transferable to other health systems.

Replacing two lecture hours with e-learning freed faculty time without compromising outcomes where learner confidence scores still increased by 1.5 ± 0.5 points, demonstrating how blending e-learning with structured bedside teaching can optimise faculty time while maintaining educational quality. The application of Knowles’ ensured that the program aligned with learner needs, while logic modelling and mixed-methods evaluation supported structured implementation and meaningful assessment. These frameworks offer a replicable template for other educators.

Limitations

As a feasibility study with 12 learners, findings are provisional as it limits power, subgroup analysis and generalisability. The use of written questionnaires for qualitative data collection limited the depth and richness of learner responses when compared to interviews or focus groups. This approach constrained opportunities for follow-up clarification and potentially introduced social desirability bias. While content and face validity were established through expert review and learner feedback, the confidence questionnaire has not yet undergone formal reliability testing. Although analyst–educator dual roles could have introduced interpretive bias, continuous reflexive journaling and peer-debriefing were employed to enhance analytic credibility.

The very large Cohen’s d values for confidence reflect the narrow baseline spread of scores on a five-point Likert scale (SD ≈ 0.5). Small standard deviations inflate effect-size calculations, therefore, the magnitude reported should be viewed as an upper bound rather than a precise estimate of educational impact.

Finally, while this study employed a theory-informed, mixed-methods design, the integration of findings was affected by the unequal depth of data sources.

Future direction

The next phase of our study will incorporate an objective assessment focused on CGA performance coupled with chart audit evaluating completeness of CGA documentation to determine if learners are practising an observable skill. Pairing these competence measures with the existing survey will allow us to compare perceived and demonstrated ability in a larger trainee cohort. To enhance the robustness of future iterations, we plan to assess internal consistency using Cronbach’s alpha, evaluate test–retest reliability over a defined interval and conduct item-difficulty analysis for the knowledge quiz to strengthen measurement precision.. These analyses will strengthen the questionnaire’s utility as a valid and reliable instrument for evaluating CGA competency development in educational interventions.

Qualitative methodologies will be deepened to capture nuanced learner experiences and implementation facilitators. Concurrently cost-effectiveness and operational scalability will be evaluated to inform long-term integration into medical education curricula. We aspire for a staged roll-out—from single ward, to department, to other hospitals—so as to refine delivery before wider adoption.

Conclusion

Our innovative program showcases high completion and satisfaction rates with learners; this combined with observed improvements in learner confidence, particularly in complex domains of cognitive assessment and care planning within CGA realm, demonstrate the programme’s educational value. The feasibility data suggest that a theory-based blended curriculum can boost learner confidence.

The qualitative findings offer valuable insight into how experiential learning, mentorship, and multimodal instructional design contributes to learner engagement and the development of clinical reasoning. Despite being a feasibility study, the alignment with adult-learning theory, logic-model grounding and mixed-methods reporting provides a rigorous template for geriatric training programmes in resource-constrained settings.

In conclusion we have demonstrated that combining online learning with real time feedback and clinical integration allows learners to appreciate CGA application and benefit. Further studies are needed at additional sites to demonstrate reproducibility and scalability.
