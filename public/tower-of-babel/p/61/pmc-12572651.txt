Reactive co-sputtered Li–Nb–O thin films with tunable ionic conductivity and dielectric properties for energy storage applications
Authors: Samaneh Jafarpour, Hamid Naghshara, Hamaneh Zarenezhad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLithium niobate (LiNbO3) has emerged as a promising solid-state electrolyte for energy storage systems owing to its favorable ionic conductivity and distinctive physicochemical properties. However, conventional solution-based synthesis routes are limited by Li loss, compromising performance. This study reports for the first time the development of Li–Nb–O thin films using reactive magnetron co-sputtering of Nb and Li targets at various DC powers and constant RF power. This approach yields the growth of amorphous films at room temperature, as confirmed by X-ray diffraction. Morphological studies revealed that increasing DC power promotes grain growth. EDX analysis indicated a rise in Nb/O atomic ratio from 0.28 to 0.44 with increasing DC power (20–80 W). XPS experiments provided evidence supporting the dominant presence of Nb-oxygen bonds and a growing contribution from weakly bound Li species at higher Nb concentrations. Moreover, the Li-to-Nb atomic ratio was found to range from 2.31 to 1.36 with increasing DC power (20–80 W), corroborating the formation of a Li-rich compositional region. Temperature-dependent impedance spectroscopy demonstrated enhanced ionic diffusion in Li–Nb–O films with higher Nb content, attributed to a reduction in activation energy. The highest room-temperature ionic conductivity of co-sputtered films reached 1.33 × 10−7 S cm−1. Metal–insulator-metal capacitors based on the optimized Li–Nb–O thin films exhibited a high capacitance (~ 624.86 nF cm−2), a large dielectric constant (~ 141.21), and a low loss tangent. In this way, a electric double layer was formed at the electrolyte/electrode interfaces. These findings underscore the potential of reactive co-sputtered Li–Nb–O films as solid-state electrolytes for energy/charge storage applications.Keywords: Lithium niobate, Solid-state electrolyte, Energy storage, Magnetron co-sputtering, Ionic conductivity, CapacitorsSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

In recent years, miniaturized energy storage devices with high power density have garnered significant attention due to their critical role in powering portable, wearable, and implantable electronics1. The evolution of microelectronic products, such as smart devices, MEMS, microrobots, sensors, RFID tags, and biomedical implants, has intensified the demand for miniaturized energy storage devices2. Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) and supercapacitors are widely studied as efficient, reliable power sources for the consumer electronics and electric vehicles (EVs)3,4.

Miniaturization of commercial LIBs and supercapacitors with flammable liquid electrolytes is restricted by multiple factors, including design, size, shape, and integrity. Further limitations arise from reduced flexibility, safety concerns, leakage risk, processing costs, and lifespan5. To address these challenges, solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) have been proposed as promising alternatives to conventional liquid electrolytes6.

The most popular Li-ion conductors include nitrides (Li3N)7, oxynitrides (LiPON)8, NASICON-type (Li1.3Al0.3Ti1.7(PO4)3)9, perovskites (Li3xLa2/3-xTiO3)10, and garnets (Li7La3Zr2O12)11. In addition, sulfides (Li10GeP2S12)12, hydrides (Li2B12H12)13, and halides (LiAlCl4)14 represent important classes of Li-ion conductors. The implementation of specific SSEs in energy storage is constrained by low power density. This limitation arises from poor ionic conductivity and high interfacial resistance to Li-ion transport15.

Amorphous thin-film SSEs offer a promising approach to overcome low ionic conductivity and enable compact device integration16. Thin-film electrolytes can reduce contact resistance, often by 1–2 orders of magnitude. This improvement arises from reduced interfacial defects, stronger chemical bonding with bulk materials, and effective filling of voids17. Extensive efforts have been dedicated to advancing thin-film processing compatible with micro- or nanofabrication of power storage devices18. Thin-film deposition of various solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) has been extensively reported in the literature. Examples include Li3PO419, LiAlO220, LixTaOy21, Li1+xAlxGe2−x(PO4)322, Li–Al–Ti–P–O–N23, and Li3InCl624.

Beyond these, lithium niobate (LiNbO3) has gained attention as a multifunctional material for use in the Li-ion batteries25, transistors26, and electrochromic displays27. It is also employed in wave transducers28, electro-optic modulators29, waveguides30, and Q-switches31. Crystalline lithium niobate is well known for its unique properties, including ferroelectricity, piezoelectricity, pyroelectricity, and electrochromism. In addition, LiNbO3 exhibits acousto-optic interactions, photoelasticity, and photovoltaic effects32. Amorphous LiNbO3 exhibits high ionic conductivity owing to its structural disorder, making it a promising solid-state electrolyte. Ferroelectric LiNbO3 films demonstrate dielectric-like behavior in amorphous state33. Several studies report room-temperature ionic conductivities of LiNbO3 up to 10−5 S cm-1 for the glassy (amorphous) form, and 10−9–10−7 S cm−1 for the crystalline counterpart34.

LiNbO3 thin films have been deposited via various techniques, inclusing sol–gel35, chemical vapor deposition (CVD)36, epitaxial growth by melting (EGM)37, and molecular beam epitaxy (MBE)38. Other methods include pulsed laser deposition (PLD)39, atomic layer deposition (ALD)40, and magnetron sputtering41. Solution-based chemical methods typically require heat treatment to eliminate residual solvents. These processes in Li-containing film deposition is hindered by challenges such as Li loss, void formation, and thermal-stress-induced cracking. Notably, these issues arise even at moderate temperatures (400–600 °C)42. Pulsed laser deposition (PLD) is unsuitable for the industrial applications and mass production due to limited film area (~ 1 cm2)43.

Magnetron sputtering constitutes a viable and robust technique in the microelectronics, offering room-temperature fabrication of thin films with uniform thickness, high purity, and strong substrate adhesion. It also enables tunable composition and scalability from laboratory research to industrial production44,45. Generally, radio-frequency (RF) sputtering of ceramic targets is used for the deposition of Li-containing thin films, such as Li2MnO346, LiNbO347, and Li7La3Zr2O1248.

In this work, we report for the first time the synthesis of Li–Nb–O films via co-sputtering of niobium (Nb) and lithium (Li) targets in a reactive oxygen atmosphere. This approach allows precise control over the Li content, with the Li target serving as an independent and primary Li source. This study investigates the influence of co-sputtering parameters on the crystallinity, grain growth, elemental distribution, and oxidation states of Li–Nb–O films. Particular attention is given to the effect of deposition conditions on their ionic transport and dielectric performance. These analyses explore the potential of Li–Nb–O thin films as solid-state electrolytes for next-generation energy storage applications.

Experimental section

Thin film deposition

Li–Nb–O thin films were deposited at room temperature using a reactive magnetron co-sputtering system (MECA-2000), as illustrated in Fig. 1. Glass substrates were cleaned sequentially in acetone, ethanosl, and DI water for 15 min to ensure removal of surface contaminants, then mounted in a rotating holder at a speed of 8 rpm. The substrate-to-target distance was fixed at 15 cm. To ensure high-vacuum conditions, the chamber pressure was reduced to 4.5 × 10–5 mbar using rotary and turbo molecular pumps. In the next step, 25 sccm of argon and 5 sccm of oxygen gases were introduced into the chamber. Mass flow controllers were used to adjust the gas flow ratio. A stepper motor-controlled Highvac valve was employed to maintain working pressure at 6 × 10–3 mbar. Before deposition, the targets were pre-sputtered in Ar plasma for 20 min. Different DC powers ranging from 20 to 80 W were applied to the niobium (Nb) target, while the lithium (Li) target was maintained at a constant RF power of 100 W. The Nb content in the films was controlled by adjusting the DC power. To achieve a gradual variation in Nb concentration, the DC power was increased in 10 W increments from 20 to 50 W. Additionally, a higher DC power of 80 W was applied to investigate the effects of elevated Nb input on film composition.

Schematic diagram of the magnetron co-sputtering system.

The reactive co-sputtering parameters are summarized in Table 1. Lithium (Li) metal targets are highly reactive and readily oxidize upon exposure to air. To prevent degradation, the Li target was supplied sealed in paraffin oil. Prior to initial use, it was thoroughly cleaned with acetone and immediately installed into the sputtering chamber. Under high-vacuum storage conditions, the target exhibited excellent resistance to oxidation and contamination. The deposition rate and film thickness were measured using a quartz crystal microbalance (QCM). To prevent moisture absorption, the prepared samples were stored in a desiccator and characterized without delay using a comprehensive set of analytical techniques.

Thin fim characterization

The crystal structure of the thin films was investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD; Siemens D500) with Cu Kα radiation, scanning across a 2θ range of 20°–60° with a step size of 0.02°. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM; Tescan-Mira III) was used to examine the surface morphology of the films. To eliminate any charge effect, the samples were gold-sputtered. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX; Tescan Mira III), coupled with the FESEM system, was used to determine elemental distribution and chemical composition. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Thermo K-Alpha) was used to characterize the surface chemistry of thin films, providing detailed information on the binding energies and chemical oxidation states of the constituent elements.

The Li-ion conductivity and dielectric properties of the films were evaluated using impedance spectroscopy on blocking-electrode cells configured in an Al/Li–Nb–O/Cu structure. Cross-plane Al/Li–Nb–O/Cu devices were fabricated by sequential sputtering through stainless steel shadow masks, which were manually exchanged between deposition steps to define the multilayer architecture. Fabrication commenced with the deposition of a 150 nm-thick aluminum (Al) film as the bottom electrode via DC sputtering of an Al target at 190 W. Them, a 200 nm Li–Nb–O film was deposited via co-sputtering through a separate mask. Circular top electrodes were fabricated by depositing a 150 nm-thick copper (Cu) film via DC sputtering of a Cu target at 100 W. Figure 2 schematically depicts the fabrication steps. This structure serves as a metal–insulator-metal (MIM) capacitor. The active electrolyte area between the electrodes was 19.63 mm2. Electrical contact was established by attaching Au wires to the electrodes using silver adhesive paste. The impedance spectroscopy test were conducted using a KC-605 LCR meter, operating within a frequency range of 44 Hz to 100 kHz with an AC voltage amplitude of 10 mV. Temperature-dependent measurements were performed from room temperature to 110 °C, with 20 min equilibration at each step to ensure thermal stability.

Impedance spectra were analyzed by fitting the data using ZView electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) software (Version 3; available at [(https://www.scribner.com/software/68-general-electrochemistr376-zview-for-windows/)]). The Al/Li–Nb–O/Cu cells exhibited stable electrochemical performance, with no signs of lithium dendrite formation, instability, non-ideal behavior, or short-circuiting under low electric fields.

Results and discussion

Crystal structure analysis

The structural properties, including the crystallinity of the thin films, were examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis. No distinct peaks were observed in the XRD patterns of the Li–Nb–O films (Fig. 3), confirming their amorphous structure. This observation is consistent with the expected amorphous nature of sputter-deposited thin films at room temperature, as reported in literature49. During sputtering at low power, or without substrate heating or biasing, the low-energy particle impacts promotes growth along low-energy planes, yielding films with poor crystallinity. Recently, Rahn et al. reported that sputtered LiNbO3 thin films remained amorphous after annealing up to 350 °C and started to crystallize only at 500 °C50. In this study, crystalline structure formation was achieved either by depositing thin films with substrate heating or through post-annealing; however, both methods resulted in lithium loss. Consequently, room-temperature deposition was chosen as the preferred approach. Previous studies have reported that single-crystal LiNbO3 exhibits low ionic conductivity (~ 10−12 S cm−1 at 500 K), which limits its suitability as a solid-state electrolyte in LIBs51. Amorphous LiNbO3 thin films are anticipated to exhibit higher ionic conductivity, primarily due to their inherent structural disorder and the absence of grain boundaries, which commonly impede ion transport in the crystalline lattice52. Moreover, amorphous thin-film electrolytes provide excellent surface compatibility with electrodes, effectively reducing interfacial resistance. This reduction is crucial for mitigating short-circuit risks and enhancing safety of Li-ion batteries and capacitors23. It is emphasized that amorphous layers are of great importance in various scientific and technological fields. They are preferred for application in transparent electronics, thin-film transistors, solar cells, memory devices, gravitational-wave detectors, and corrosion-resistant coatings53,54.

Morphological characterization

Figure 4 presents FESEM images of the Li–Nb–O thin films. The surface morphology reveals a granular structure with the fine grains. Grain growth is evidently enhanced with increasing DC power, primarily due to two factors. First, higher DC power results in a greater number of target atoms being sputtered by Ar ion bombardment. Sputtered atoms with adequate kinetic energy can diffuse to favorable sites, facilitating effective atomic bonding and promoting grain growth. Secondly, the increase in substrate temperature due to high-energy bombardment promotes grain growth with well-defined boundaries55.

FESEM images and corresponding EDX elemental maps of Nb and O for Li–Nb–O films deposited at constant RF power and various DC powers: (a) 20 W, (b) 30 W, (c) 40 W, (d) 50 W, and (e) 80 W.

Energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy was conducted to analyze the elemental distribution and chemical composition of the Li–Nb–O thin films. The EDX detector cannot identify Li due to the inherently low intensity of its Kα X-ray emission line. As shown in Fig. 4, the EDX mapping results demonstrate a homogeneous distribution of the constituent elements, including niobium (Nb) and oxygen (O), across the films. This result indicates the absence of significant Nb agglomeration on surface, thereby promoting uniform material formation. Such uniformity is an essential criterion for consistent electrochemical performance and mechanical stability. Figure 5 displays the selected EDX spectrum for the Li–Nb–O sample deposited at a DC poswer of 80 W. Si peak is attributed to the glass substrate. The Nb-to-O atomic ratio was determined from the EDX measurements, yielding values in the range of 0.28–0.44, as presented in the inset table of Fig. 5. A systematic increase in the Nb/O ratio with increasing DC sputtering power was observed, reflecting the progressive increase in Nb content.

Chemical composition analysis

XPS analysis was used to characterize the elemental composition and oxidation states of the constituent species in Li–Nb–O films deposited at various DC powers. Figure 6a displays the XPS survey scan spectra of the films, confirming the presence of Li, Nb, O, and C, in agreement with previously reported results56. No impurities were detected except carbon, attributed to surface contamination; the C 1s peak at 284.5 eV was used as an internal standard to calibrate all XPS spectra for charge compensation. XPS is a surface-sensitive method that quantifies elemental composition in the near-surface region of a sample. The lithium content, as one of the most critical parameters affecting ionic conductivity, was experimentally evaluated by XPS analysis. However, due to the weak Nb signal in XPS spectra, the Nb atomic ratio was further determined using EDX analysis. The combined application of EDX and XPS enabled a quantitative assessment of the film composition.

(a) XPS survey spectra of Li–Nb–O films, (b) XPS analysis table summarizing all samples, (c) Nb 3d core-level spectrum, and (d) Li 1s core-level spectra.

The atomic percentages of the constituent elements are summarized in the table presented in Fig. 6b. Examination of the relative Li and Nb content in the film surface reveals how maintaining a relatively high constant RF power on the Li target, while varying DC power applied to Nb target, affects the chemical composition. To clarity regarding the compositional region of the Li–Nb–O ternary system, the Li-to-Nb content ratio was calculated, yielding values in the range of 2.31–1.36. This indicates the successful achievement of a Li-rich compositional region. As shown in Fig. 6b, the Li/Nb atomic ratio exhibits a general decreasing trend with increasing DC power. This result is in agreement with the corresponding enhancement in Nb content.

Figure 6c presents the high-resolution Nb 3d core-level spectrum of the Li–Nb–O film deposited at a DC power of 80 W. This sample was selected for detailed study because films deposited at lower DC powers contain reduced niobium content, resulting in significantly weaker Nb 3d signal intensity. The spectrum exhibits a Nb 3d3/2–Nb 3d3/2 doublet, with peak centers located at binding energies of 209.87 eV and 207.16 eV, respectively. These values with the spin–orbit splitting of approximately 2.7 eV are characteristic of Nb5+ oxidation state and align well with values reported in the literature57. In this study, the Nb 4s peak was not observed in the XPS spectra. This is due to the Li/Nb ratio exceeding 1.0, confirming the formation of a Li-rich phase.

Figure 6d displays the Li 1s core-level spectra of the films, each characterized by a single sharp and symmetric peak centered at ~ 54.78 eV. This indicates the monovalent chemical state of Li+ ions in all samples23. The comparable intensities of the Li 1s XPS peaks observed across the films can be ascribed to the application of constant RF power to the Li target during sputtering.

Figure 7 presents the O 1s core-level spectra of th Li–Nb–O thin films. The O 1s peaks were deconvoluted to 530.61, 531.45, and 532.67 eV, corresponding to Nb-oxygen, Li–O, and Li–OH bonds, respectively56,58. The presence of hydroxyl species is attributed to the absorption of moisture during exposure to the ambient atmosphere. Figure 7f depicts the variation in relative quantities of Nb–O and Li–O bonds as a function of DC sputtering power. The integrated area of the 530.61 eV peak gradually increased (3.19–11.51%) with rising DC power (20–80 W), indicating enhanced Nb–O bond formation. This enhancement is attributed to the higher Nb concentration achieved at increased sputtering power. In contrast, the fraction of O2− associated with Li–O bonds decreased from 79.81 to 70.52% over the same DC power range. This result suggests a greater contribution from weakly bonded Li+ ions, which predominantly govern ionic conductivity in disordered solids.

Figure 9a presents the a.c. conductivity of Li–Nb–O thin films measured at room temperature. Electrical conductivity in solid-state electrolytes is a thermally-activated process, governed by the migration of weakly bound ions under an applied electric field59. Frequency dependence of σ(ω) for all Li–Nb–O films follows Jonscher’s power law60:

Here, σdc is the dc conductivity, A is a pre-exponential factor related to polarizability, ω is the angular frequency, and n is an experimentally determined fitting parameter. The parameter n reflects the interaction degree between charge carriers and surrounding lattice. The exponent “n” is frequency-independent but can vary with the type of mobile ion, ion concentration, and system temperature. The universal dynamic response (UDR) in disordered solids is described by Jonscher’s law.

A significant increase in σ(ω) was observed as the DC sputtering power increased from 20 to 30 W, followed by a slight increase between 40 and 80 W. Ionic transport primarily depends on factors such as ionic polarizability, Li–O binding strength, Li ion contribution, hydration radius, and vacancy density. Additionally, Li+–Li+ Coulombic interactions and ion-electrode interactions play critical roles in overall transport behavior61. Here, enhanced ionic conductivity is directly correlated with the increased number of sputtered Nb atoms due to higher DC sputtering power.

With increasing Nb concentration, Nb atoms exhibit a strong tendency to occupy Li sites. This behavior is primarily attributed to the similar ionic radii of Nb (0.64 Å) and Li (0.76 Å), as well as the difference in bond strengths within the Li–Nb–O system. The Nb–O covalent bond (Eb ~ 567.31 kJ/mol) is considerably stronger than the Li–O ionic bond (Eb ~ 392.50 kJ/mol), favoring Nb substitution at Li sites and the formation of Li vacancies62. Substitution of monovalent Li+ by pentavalent Nb5+ disrupts charge neutrality, promoting the formation of additional Li+ vacancies. These vacancies serve as diffusion sites, facilitating Li-ion transport within the Li–Nb–O framework. Moreover, the presence of Li+ vacancies reduces Li+–Li+ electrostatic repulsion, thereby further enhancing ionic mobility63. XPS analysis confirms predominant Nb–O bond formation and a reduction in the concentration of bonded Li ions with increasing DC power.

Measuring dc conductivity of electrolyte films is challenging due to the ionic polarization at low frequencies and bulk ionic transport at high frequencies. The σdc value can approximated as σ(ω) at the frequency of peak in loss spectrum. This approximation agrees well with the results obtaine from fitting Nyquist plots (Z″–Z′) using an equivalent circuit model64. The room-temperature σdc values for the co-sputtered Li–Nb–O thin films are listed in Table 2. The maximum σRT value obtained was 1.33 × 10–7 S cm−1, comparable to previously reported results for LiNbO3 (Table 3). This value is even higher than that reported for material synthesized by other methods. For example, Wang et al. recently reported a σRT of 6.39 × 10–8 S cm−1 for Li–Nb–O thin films grown by ALD at a deposition temperature of 235 °C34. This indicates the potential of co-sputtering to produce thin-film electrolytes with improved ionic conductivity. In this study, Li–Nb–O films provide moderate σRT value, surpassing comparable amorphous systems such as Li–Ta–O (σRT ~ 10–8 S cm−1) and Li–Al–O (σRT ~ 2.85 × 10–9 S cm-1)21,65.

Figure 9b shows the capacitance-frequency curves of Li–Nb–O based MIM capacitors at room temperature. The low-frequency capacitance of the samples is presented in Table 2. The highest Ci value (625 nF cm−2) was achieved for film prepared at a DC power of 30 W. This behavior is directly related to the ionic conduction mechanism. Incorporation of Li ions into niobium oxide induces a complex process that enhances charge storage, associated with the electrode polarization effect. This is attributed to the formation of electric double layers (EDLs), resulting from the ion accumulation at the blocking electrolyte/electrode interfaces.

In the low-frequency range, Li+ ions are able to respond to external electric field and undergo long-range diffusion, leading to resistive behavior. With increasing frequency, sharp decline in capacitance is attributed to reduced effective ionic polarization. This behavior is due to the inability of Li+ ions to follow the rapidly oscillating electric field. At high frequencies, the plateau observed in Ci suggests that induced dipoles fail to respond to the electric field. This is related to the limited time available for dipolar rotation or ionic displacement66.

Figure 9 c presents room-temperature loss tangent plots; corresponding values at 100 Hz are listed in Table 2. The Li–Nb–O thin films with low ionic conductivity exhibit a pronounced relaxation peak. In contrast, higher ionic conductivity leads to a suppression of this feature, yielding a nearly flat loss profile with minimal dielectric loss value.

The loss peak delineates the low- and high-frequency regimes, reflecting resonance between the applied electric field and the characteristic dipolar relaxation frequency. At the resonant condition (ωτ = 1), power transfer from the external source to the dipolar system is maximized. This results in peak energy dissipation and the corresponding thermal response69. The absence of a loss peak arises from dominance of electrode polarization over dipolar relaxation, known as the masking effect. The observed decrease in the dielectric loss is attributed to the enhanced Li+ ion mobility. The accelerated response of Li–Li dipoles under applied alternating field leads to a reduced relaxation time. Sharma et al. reported that a zero relaxation time leads to the dielectric loss approaching zero (ɛ′′ = 0 for ωτ = 0) 70.

Temperature dependence of a.c. conductivity and dielectric properties

To investigate the thermally-activated diffusion of lithium ions, impedance spectroscopy was performed over the temperature range of 27–110 °C. Figure 10a, b display the Nyquist plots of Li–Nb–O films deposited at DC powers of 20 W and 30 W, respectively. To extract accurate information from the impedance diagrams, modeling and fitting of Nyquist plots were carried out using ZView software. The corresponding equivalent circuit used to model the system is shown in Fig. 8c. In this circuit, R0 represents the ohmic resistance of the electrical contacts. Typically, Nyquist plots exhibit a semicircle in the high-frequency region and an inclined line at low frequencies. The high-frequency semicircular arc reflects the bulk response of the films and is modeled by a bulk resistance (Rb) in parallel with a constant phase element (CPEg). At low frequencies, the inclined line is attributed to dominant ionic conduction. This feature is represented in the equivalent circuit by a constant phase element (CPEe), which captures stray capacitive behavior arising from ion accumulation at the electrode/electrolyte interface.

Conclusion

In summary, this study examined the structural, morphological, and electrical properties of Li–Nb–O thin films synthesized via reactive co-sputtering, with varying DC powers applied to the Nb target and a constant RF power for the Li target. X-ray diffraction confirmed the amorphous nature of all samples. FESEM analysis revealed that surface morphology was significantly affected by increasing DC power. EDX measurements showed a rise in Nb/O atomic ratio from 0.28 to 0.44 as the DC power increased (20–80 W). XPS studies confirmed the predominance of Nb–O bonding, along with an increased presence of weakly bound Li ions at higher Nb concentrations. Furthermore, the Li-to-Nb atomic ratio was found to vary between 2.31 and 1.36, consistent with the formation of a Li-rich region. Impedance spectroscopy demonstrated an enhancement in the ionic conductivity, reaching 1.33 × 10−7 S cm−1 at room temperature. Notably, the optimized Li-Nb–O capacitor exhibited a high areal capacitance (~ 624.86 nF cm−2), a maximum dielectric constant (~ 141.21), and a low loss. These results highlight co-sputtering as an effective route for developing Li–Nb–O thin films tailored for next-generation solid-state energy storage devices.
