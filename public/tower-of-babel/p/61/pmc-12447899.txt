Indoor airborne carcinogen synergy in children: systematic interdisciplinary approach to PM2.5-nicotine driven lung tumorigenesis and targeted intervention
Authors: Boyu Pan, Xiwen Feng, Fujian Jiang, Rui Li, Han Zhu, Kunpeng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractThis study integrates environmental engineering and bioinformatics to explore nicotine and PM2.5-induced lung cancer risks in children.

Introduction

Indoor air pollution emerges as an urgent and significant challenge to global health, contributing to the deaths of 3.2 million individuals in 2020 alone, including over 237,000 children under the age of five (World Health Organization, 2021). Among the most prevalent pollutants in indoor environments are nicotine and PM2.5, primarily originating from smoking and cooking activities (Lu et al., 2023, Lu et al., 2024). Smoking releases substantial amounts of nicotine and PM2.5, which not only heighten the risk of respiratory disease but also predispose individuals to neuropsychiatric disorders (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2014, Zeng et al., 2023). Additionally, cooking represents another major source of indoor PM2.5. Approximately one-third of the global population still relies on open flames or inefficient stoves for cooking, a practice that generates significant quantities of PM2.5 capable of penetrating deep into the lungs and even entering the bloodstream, thereby markedly increasing the risk of respiratory diseases among children (World Health Organization, 2022, Shu et al., 2024).

Globally, approximately 93 % of children under 15 years old (1.8 billion) are exposed to PM2.5 levels exceeding WHO guidelines, with low- and middle-income countries bearing the highest burden. For nicotine, passive exposure affects nearly 40 % of children worldwide, linked to 65,000 annual deaths from respiratory infections (GBD 2021 ASEAN Mental Disorders Collaborators, 2025, Schleich et al., 2025). These exposures are associated with a 1.2–1.7-fold increased risk of asthma and cognitive deficits (Bakhtiari et al., 2025). Such data underscore the need for localized studies like ours to inform targeted interventions.

To better understand the distribution and concentration of nicotine and PM2.5 in indoor environments, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations have emerged as a powerful predictive tool. By modeling airflows, particle dispersion, and pollutant transport mechanisms, CFD simulations provide a detailed spatial and temporal analysis of nicotine and PM2.5 concentrations under various indoor conditions. These simulations can incorporate critical factors such as ventilation efficiency, source emission rates, and human activity patterns, thereby offering valuable insights into pollutant accumulation and exposure risks to children within residential settings. The application of CFD not only aids in identifying high-risk areas and optimizing indoor air quality control measures but also bridges the gap between experimental studies and practical implementation.

In recent years, the carcinogenic effects of nicotine and PM2.5 on lung cancer, particularly non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), have garnered substantial attention. Recent studies reveal that nicotine can activate cell signaling pathways mediated by nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), specifically inducing NSCLC. Furthermore, it exacerbates oxidative stress by generating excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby accelerating the progression of lung cancer (Kang et al., 2024, Rao et al., 2024, Huang et al., 2025). For non-smoking lung cancer patients, PM2.5 is recognized as a critical carcinogen for NSCLC. Its mechanisms include inducing chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, activating oncogenes, and promoting genetic mutations. Evidence also suggests a potential synergistic carcinogenic interaction between nicotine and PM2.5 (Chen et al., 2024).

Early exposure to indoor pollutants such as nicotine and PM2.5 not only significantly elevates mortality risks among children but may also cause irreversible damage to the respiratory system's development. Moreover, through the induction of cumulative DNA damage and chronic inflammatory responses, such exposure substantially increases the likelihood of developing lung cancer (Mocelin et al., 2022). Therefore, this study systematically explores the combined effects of nicotine and PM2.5 on NSCLC, offering crucial insights into the underlying molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets, while also guiding strategies to safeguard children's health. Meanwhile, we also collaborated with a diverse range of disciplines, including environmental engineering, bioinformatics, and computational biology, to conduct comprehensive medical engineering interdisciplinary analyses. The establishment of SEIME is designed to address intricate medical engineering problems that arise from multiple interrelated factors, such as genetic predispositions, environmental influences, and lifestyle choices. By leveraging the strengths of each discipline, SEIME aims to develop innovative, sustainable, and effective solutions that can be applied to a wide range of medical and engineering challenges in the future. This initiative represents a significant step forward in the field of medical engineering, paving the way for more integrated and interdisciplinary approaches to healthcare and technology.

Methodology

CFD modeling

CFD simulation was applied using ANSYS Fluent to obtain the concentration distribution and flow characteristics. This model was developed as an example of a typical two-bedroom house as shown in Fig. 1. Lv et al. (2020) developed a CFD model to study the role of air curtain hoods in reducing respiratory exposure of people during cooking processes. Similarly in this study, a nicotine pollution source and a DPM particle source were set up in the screen porch and kitchen, respectively.Fig. 1The layout of typical two-bedroom house.

The layout of typical two-bedroom house.

Differentially expressed genes screening in nicotine- and PM2.5-related experimental samples

The nicotine-related experimental dataset GSE242791 was used from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). This dataset contains samples from 4 normal human bronchial epithelial cell (BEAS-2B) specimens treated with 1.25 µM or 2.5 µM nicotine for three weeks, as well as two untreated controls (Zhang et al., 2023). Simultaneously, the PM2.5-related experimental dataset, identified by the accession number GSE158954, was retrieved along with its chip information. This dataset comprises six samples, including three BEAS-2B specimens exposed to PM2.5 for five weeks and three corresponding controls (Merk et al., 2020).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis was conducted using the R language limma package (P ≤ 0.05 & |Log2FC| > 0.5). Volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated using the ggplot2 package. Furthermore, the identified DEGs were subjected to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis via the DAVID database (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/home.jsp). The relevant results were visualized as bubble plots.

Identification and analysis of common targets for nicotine and PM2.5

A Venn diagram was employed to identify the intersection of targets influenced by nicotine and PM2.5. The shared targets were then subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses using the above database (P ≤ 0.05). The functional distribution and pathway involvement of the identified targets were visualized.

Screening and enrichment analyses of core targets

The NetworkAnalyst platform (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/) was used to identify the key subnetwork and targets involved in the interaction of shared nicotine and PM2.5 targets. These core targets were further subjected to GO and KEGG pathway analyses using the above database (P ≤ 0.05) with the results visualized.

Prognostic and expression level analyses of core targets

STAR-counts data and corresponding clinical information for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) were obtained from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov), comprising 516 and 501 samples, respectively. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed on core targets, and the forestplot package was used to generate forest plots. Furthermore, we obtained STAR-counts data and clinical information for LUSC, LUAD and their adjacent non-tumor tissues from the TCGA database. These data were utilized to examine the expression level differences of prognostically significant targets between tumor and normal tissues, with statistically significant differences set at P ≤ 0.05. The results were presented in the form of boxplots.

Furthermore, key prognostic targets affecting survival were systematically studied through the single-cell sequencing analysis platform (https://lung.cancer-pku.cn/) to characterize their expression patterns within NSCLC T-cell subpopulations and to explore their potential regulatory roles in tumor immunity.

Drug repositioning and molecular docking analysis

The Connectivity Map database (https://clue.io/) was used to perform drug repositioning analysis on the commonly affected nicotine and PM2.5 targets with consistent expression trends, identifying potential small-molecule compounds. The chemical structures of these compounds were retrieved from the PubChem database, while the protein structures of key targets, such as GADD45A and NPAS2, were obtained from the PDB database. Protein and ligand structures were preprocessed using PyMOL (version 2.1.0) and AutoDock Tools (version 1.5.6). Subsequently, molecular docking analysis was conducted using PyRx software (version 0.8) in conjunction with AutoDock Vina.

In vitro functional assays

A549 cells (Procell, China; Cat# CL-0016) were used for functional studies. For proliferation analysis, cells were seeded at 10,000 cells/mL and assessed using CCK-8 assay. Colony formation assays were performed by plating 1,000 cells/well followed by 10-day continuous culture, with subsequent staining and colony counting for quantitative analysis. RepSox and SB202190 were obtained from Selleck corporation (China).

Statistical analyses

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). DEGs were identified using significance thresholds of P ≤ 0.05 and |Log2FC| > 0.5. Survival analyses were conducted using both Uni- and Multi-Cox regression models, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) computed. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to evaluate differences in gene expression between tumor and adjacent normal tissues. A P-value of ≤0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

CFD simulation

The CFD simulation results are shown in Fig. 2. It can be seen from Fig. 2A that the nicotine concentrations in the indoor area are all higher overall than those in the front porch adjacent to the outdoor area. Meanwhile, the spatial distribution of the indoor concentrations shows that there is a gradual decrease in the nicotine concentrations from the screen porch to Bedroom 1. Furthermore, the PM2.5 particulate sources in the kitchen will diffuse to the surroundings, be captured by the walls or escape through the windows (Fig. 2B), and the trends in nicotine and PM2.5 concentrations over time in the central area of the living room are shown in Fig. 2C. Transient simulations indicate that, since the living room is close to the kitchen, the PM2.5 concentration reaches a steady state faster than nicotine, taking approximately 50 min, while nicotine takes approximately one hour. In summary, nicotine concentrations ranged from about 0.25 μg/m3 to 1 μg/m3, which is close to Paul's (Paul et al., 2017) measured results and is a high exposure level. The PM2.5 concentrations ranged from about 150 μg/m3 to 600 μg/m3, which is much higher than the recommended value of 50 μg/m3 by Standards for indoor air quality (GB/T 18883-2022, 2022).Fig. 2CFD simulation result of mass concentration of nicotine and PM2.5.

Common targets of nicotine and PM2.5

Venn diagram analysis revealed 69 common targets shared between nicotine and PM2.5 (Fig. 5A). GO results indicated that these targets are closely related with negative regulation of epithelial cell proliferation, positive regulation of NF-kappaB transcription factor activity, negative regulation of DNA-templated transcription, cellular response to heparin, and cellular response to jasmonic acid stimulus. In molecular functions (MF), they are significantly associated with the cell surface, plasma membrane, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, apical plasma membrane, and extracellular exosome. In cellular components (CC), notable associations include phenanthrene 9,10-monooxygenase activity, indanol dehydrogenase activity, trans-1,2-dihydrobenzene-1,2-diol dehydrogenase activity, protein binding, and protein homodimerization activity (Fig. 5B). Using the NetworkAnalyst platform, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of common targets was constructed, yielding 22 subnetworks. Subnetwork 1, the largest and most important, comprised 203 nodes and 214 edges, far exceeding other subnetworks (e.g., subnetwork 2 with 16 nodes and 15 edges) (Fig. 5D). Subnetwork 1 contained 15 seed nodes: RRAGD, SGK1, KLHL13, RHOBTB1, CDKN1C, GADD45A, EGR1, BANP, AR, ZEB1, NPAS2, PLCG2, PDGFA, ARHGEF4, and TGFA, identified as hub genes for nicotine and PM2.5. GO analysis indicated that these hub genes are involved in positive regulation of the MAPK cascade, positive regulation of DNA-templated transcription, cell cycle, and intracellular signal transduction. In CC, these genes are primarily localized to the nucleoplasm, nucleus, nuclear speck, and chromatin, while in MF, they are linked to protein binding, DNA-binding transcription factor activity, DNA binding, and protein heterodimerization activity. KEGG analysis further revealed significant enrichment in pathways such as Glioma, Prostate cancer, Non-small cell lung cancer, and EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance (Fig. 5E and F).

Clinical relevance analysis of core targets

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses of 15 hub genes in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) revealed PDGFA as a significant risk gene in LUSC (HR > 1) (Fig. 6A and B), while in LUAD, GADD45A and NPAS2 were identified as risk genes (HR > 1), and PLCG2 and RRAGD as protective genes (HR < 1) (Fig. 6C and D). Further examination of TCGA data confirmed that PDGFA exhibited higher expression in adjacent non-tumor tissues in LUSC, while GADD45A and NPAS2 were significantly upregulated in LUAD tissues, with PLCG2 and RRAGD significantly downregulated (Fig. 7A–E). Discussion

Lung cancer ranks as one of the most common malignancies globally and is the primary cause of cancer-related fatalities (Martin-Deleon et al., 2021). NSCLC, the predominant pathological subtype, accounts for approximately 85 % of all lung cancer cases (Parente et al., 2022). Nicotine and PM2.5 are two critical carcinogenic factors in NSCLC, each promoting its onset and progression through distinct mechanisms. Moreover, their synergistic effects may exacerbate the disease burden among high-risk populations, such as children (Arfaeinia et al., 2023).

Although CFD simulation focuses on the diffusion, deposition, and concentration distribution of pollutants such as PM2.5 and nicotine in indoor air, and its core is to provide a quantitative platform based on physics and fluid dynamics, this part of the study lays a solid foundation for subsequent biomedical experiments on environmental exposure. Specifically, CFD simulations can not only reconstruct the spatial and temporal distribution characteristics of pollutants in different indoor contexts and accurately determine the concentration levels of pollutants in various critical areas, but also reveal the release dynamics of pollutants generated by cooking and smoking. For instance, several studies (Mohammadpoor et al., 2024, Eitzinger et al., 2005, Song et al., 2021) have demonstrated the capability of CFD in capturing transient pollutant release patterns, such as those from cigarette smoke or cooking fumes, which are critical for understanding short-term exposure risks. These data provide a scientific basis for simulating real exposure conditions in vitro or in animal models, allowing subsequent studies such as differential gene expression screening, co-targeting analysis, and drug repositioning to more closely match exposure levels in actual indoor pollution environments. In other words, the information on pollutant concentration and distribution obtained through CFD simulation can guide the setting of pollutant dose and the planning of exposure time in biological experiments, ensuring the consistency between experimental conditions and the real situation. While previous studies have made significant strides in using CFD to model indoor air quality, many have primarily focused on pollutant dispersion or ventilation efficiency (Martins et al., 2018), without extending their findings to biological or health-related outcomes. Some studies (Madureira et al., 2020, Paul et al., 2021) have utilized CFD simulations to quantify particle deposition in specific regions, thereby predicting health risks associated with indoor air pollutants and obtaining relatively reliable biological results. However, these studies often concentrated on single pollutants or simplified exposure scenarios. In contrast, this study investigates the combined effects of multiple pollutants, such as nicotine and PM2.5, which are more representative of real-world indoor environments, by integrating CFD simulations with bioinformatics and molecular docking analyses. This research provides a comprehensive framework that connects environmental exposure data with disease mechanisms and therapeutic discovery. Ultimately, this interdisciplinary research framework not only helps to reveal the causal relationship between pollutant exposure and biological effects, but also provides a holistic perspective from the physical to the molecular level for indoor environmental management and health risk assessment for childhood.

Nicotine and PM2.5 can induce chronic inflammation, remodels the tumor microenvironment, and strengthens angiogenesis, metastasis, and anti-apoptotic properties (Chen et al., 2024). Furthermore, PM2.5 activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) and interacts with EGFR to amplify tumor-related signaling pathways and increase drug resistance (Wang et al., 2024). This study identified 572 nicotine-related genes, including multiple members of the PCDHB subfamily, CCN2, and ANGPTL4. These genes are involved in cancer-related signalings such as PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and Rap, indicating their multifaceted roles in NSCLC proliferation, drug resistance, and tumor microenvironment regulation.

Survival analyses identified GADD45A and NPAS2 as risk genes for LUAD (HR > 1), while PLCG2 and RRAGD were protective genes (HR < 1). GADD45A, a stress response protein, is a key regulator of cell cycle checkpoints and DNA damage repair (Li et al., 2017). Previous studies have demonstrated that an epigenetic signature model incorporating GADD45A and 10 other genes effectively predicts overall survival (OS) in LUAD patients, offering a crucial reference for personalized therapy and clinical decision-making (Wang et al., 2020). This study further confirms GADD45A as an independent prognostic factor for LUAD and an important candidate for targeted therapy. NPAS2 (Neuronal PAS Domain Protein 2), a transcription factor with a PAS domain, plays significant roles in circadian rhythm regulation, neurodevelopment, tumor progression, and metabolic regulation (Zhang et al., 2022). In LUAD, NPAS2 and its upstream regulator ARRB1 function as oncogenes, critically influencing tumor malignancy and metabolic reprogramming (Wang et al., 2024). Additionally, NPAS2 binds to and stabilizes H2AX mRNA, enhancing DNA damage repair and reducing LUAD cells’ sensitivity to chemotherapy (Zhang et al., 2024). Prior studies have shown that a prognostic model incorporating NPAS2 and five chromatin regulators has significant predictive value for LUAD patient survival (Huang et al., 2023). This study further validates NPAS2 as an independent prognostic factor for LUAD, providing a scientific basis for subsequent targeted drug development. Single-cell sequencing analysis revealed distinct expression patterns of GADD45A and NPAS2 across T-cell subsets. GADD45A exhibited widespread expression, particularly elevated in CD4+ T-cell C9-CTLA4 subsets, suggesting its role in regulating immune microenvironment activation and exerting anti-tumor effects.

Molecular docking analysis identified four potential small-molecule drugs, with RepSox and SB202190 demonstrating strong binding affinities to GADD45A and NPAS2. RepSox, a TGF-β receptor kinase inhibitor, suppresses epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and reduces tumor cell migration and invasion by blocking the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway. It also modulates the tumor microenvironment to decrease immunosuppression, indirectly enhancing anti-tumor activity (Pitt et al., 2023). SB202190, a selective p38 MAPK inhibitor, mitigates cellular responses to stress and inflammation, arresting the cell cycle-particularly at the G2/M phase-and halting cancer cell proliferation (Tsai et al., 2017). This study highlights the association between MAPK cascade regulation by hub genes and the therapeutic potential of SB202190 against nicotine- and PM2.5-induced harm. Additionally, SB202190 balances pro-apoptotic (e.g., Bax) and anti-apoptotic (e.g., Bcl-2) factors, demonstrating multi-level anti-cancer effects. Preventive treatment with RepSox or SB202190 against nicotine- and PM2.5-associated LUAD not only exerts unique anti-cancer effects but may also synergistically enhance therapeutic outcomes by jointly modulating multiple targets, such as GADD45A and NPAS2.

Despite the promising findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, lifestyle-related confounding factors—such as diet, physical activity, and socioeconomic status—may influence the biological response to environmental exposures like nicotine and PM2.5. These factors were not included in the present study due to the nature of CFD modeling and bioinformatics analysis. Future integrative studies combining environmental, behavioral, and clinical data will be essential to fully understand their combined impact on NSCLC risk. Second, while PM2.5 was primarily treated as a particulate pollutant, it also serves as a carrier for adsorbed carcinogens such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), acting as a “Trojan horse” that facilitates the deep delivery of these harmful compounds into lung tissue. This mechanism may further amplify the synergistic effects of nicotine and PM2.5 in promoting tumorigenesis, highlighting the need to consider both the physical and chemical characteristics of indoor pollutants in future mechanistic and translational studies.

In summary, this study systematically elucidates the synergistic effects of nicotine and PM2.5 in childhood NSCLC, identifying GADD45A and NPAS2 as therapeutic targets for LUAD and screening potential small-molecule drugs, including RepSox and SB202190. However, certain limitations remain, such as the need for further validation of specific interactions between target genes and signaling pathways. The in vivo efficacy and safety of RepSox and SB202190 also require additional experimental and clinical investigation. Moreover, the long-term effects of nicotine and PM2.5 exposure in childhood and their dose–response relationships in childhood NSCLC progression warrant further exploration. In addition, the most important value of this work is also reflected in its innovative application of the SEIME model, which utilizes interdisciplinary approaches to solve multi-factors induced medical engineering problems and provides a complete theoretical basis and basis for later disease treatment.

Conclusions

The significant synergistic effects of PM2.5 and nicotine on children's respiratory and immunological health emphasize a critical need for targeted interventions and policy development to mitigate indoor air pollution. Despite limitations such as inadequate long-term exposure data, a small sample size, the absence of clinical validation analyses, and potential batch effects from diverse data collection methods, these findings underscore the urgency of addressing environmental factors impacting vulnerable populations. Meanwhile, our SEIME model was successfully used to construct a solution to the medical engineering problem induced by multiple factors, and provided a series of theoretical support for molecular mechanism exploration, disease treatment, etc, which set up a novel research model. Future research should focus on comprehensive studies to validate these results and explore effective strategies for improving children's health outcomes in polluted environments.
