Off‐Label Use of the Multifunctional Occluder for Transcatheter Patent Ductus Arteriosus Closure: An International Experience
Authors: Naychi Lwin, Caroline Ovaert, Sophie Malekzadeh‐Milani, Paul Padovani, Thomas Krasemann, Stanimir Georgiev, Oscar Werner, Ali Houeijeh, Stéphanie Douchin, Peter Kramer, Lorenzo Scarduelli, Lisa Bianco, Céline Grunenwald, Sébastien Hascoët, Zakaria Jalal, Nadir Benbrik, Bruno Lefort, Iñaki Navarro Castellanos, Stephan Schubert, Alain Fraisse, Alban‐Elouen Baruteau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscatheter patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) closure can be challenging in cases of large PDA, complex anatomy, and small patient size. We aimed to assess the feasibility, efficacy and safety of the off‐label use of the multifunctional occluder in transcatheter PDA closure.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on patients who underwent transcatheter PDA closure with the multifunctional occluder in 14 pediatric cardiology centers (5 countries) from 2018 to 2025.ResultsA total of 77 procedures were performed on 75 patients, including 5 adults (6.7%). Among the 70 children (93.3%; median age: 0.8 years [range, 0.1–10 years], median procedural weight: 6.9 kg [range, 2.1–32.0 kg]), 35 (50.0%) weighed ≤6 kg (median procedural weight: 4.0 kg [range, 2.1–5.9 kg]). The PDA was large, with a minimal ductal diameter of 3.8 mm (range, 1.5–7.0 mm), short in 54.5%, and unrestrictive with increased pulmonary artery pressures in 51.6% of patients. The morphology was predominantly conical (Krichenko type A: 51.9%), with window‐type or complex morphologies representing 26.7% (Type B: 18.2%, Type D: 7.8%). Both arterial and venous femoral accesses were obtained in 87.0% cases, with deployment being antegrade in 77.9% and retrograde in 22.1%. Successful device deployment and release were achieved in 74 cases (96.1%). The rate of major adverse events was 5.2% including 1 early (1.3%) and 3 late complications (3.9%). No other adverse events were reported.ConclusionsThe off‐label use of the multifunctional occluder device is a promising option for transcatheter PDA closure, demonstrating safety and efficacy in this series.Keywords: congenital heart disease, Doppler echocardiography, off label, outcomes, pediatric cardiologySubject Categories: Treatment

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

Transcatheter patent ductus arteriosus closure may not be suitable for certain patent ductus arteriosus anatomies, hemodynamic states, and patient‐specific factors when using the current available devices; consequently, skilled operators are exploring alternative and off‐label devices to overcome these limitations.

Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) closure is indicated in any patient with left‐to‐right shunt resulting in congestive heart failure, failure to thrive, pulmonary overcirculation with or without pulmonary hypertension, and left ventricular volume overload. 1 , 2 Transcatheter PDA closure has proved to be safe in ≥6 kg patients with various devices, 1 , 3 and in <2 kg infants in whom a 98% success rate with a 4% to 5% rate of major adverse events has been reported in recent large‐scale series using the Amplatzer Piccolo Occluder device (Abbott, Plymouth, MN, USA). 4 However, concerns regarding safety and feasibility remain when applied to the large PDA, particularly in cases of challenging anatomy, such as short ductus or window‐type ductus and small patient size, especially in 2 to 6 kg infants, an intermediate weight range where there is no dedicated Food and Drug Administration approved or Commission Européenne marked device. 2 , 5 , 6 , 7 Due to these challenges, some institutions may choose primary surgical closure as the initial treatment approach. In this complex PDA group, off‐label devices may be a suitable option for closure, but there remain concerns about peripheral vascular injury, procedure related hemodynamic instability, risk of device protrusion into adjacent structures, and device embolization. 2 , 5 , 8

First used in North America for transcatheter ventricular septal defect closure on a compassionate basis in 2021, the Konar‐multifunctional (Konar MF) ventricular septal defect occluder (MFO; Lifetech, Schenzhen, China) received the Commission Européenne‐mark approval in 2018, 5 years after first‐in‐human implantation. 9 The MFO device is a low‐profile, self‐expandable, double‐disc device made from nitinol wire mesh layers. 9 It is designed with 2 discs joined by an expanding cone‐shaped connecting waist. Its flexible waist and soft woven mesh provide high conformability with the defect to be closed. It can be screwed at both sides to allow anterograde (venous) or retrograde (arterial) deployement. 9 It is available in 8 sizes (5–3, 6–4, 7–5, 8–6, 9–7, 10–8, 12–10 and 14–12 mm), device sizes being given as left ventricular waist diameter (D2) and right ventricular waist diameter (D1). 10 The unstretched device length is 4 mm for all sizes. For the larger 4 sizes (9–7 and greater), there is a PTFE membrane with nylon thread sewn securely within the waist (Figure 1) to increase its occlusion capacity. 10 The recommended delivery system is the SteerEase delivery sheath (Lifetech, Shenzen, China), with sheath sizes ranging from 4 to 5 Fr (for devices up to 8–6) and 6 or 7 Fr for larger devices.

MFO use for transcatheter closure of challenging PDAs has been exceptionally reported in small case series. 10 , 11 , 12 We aimed to report the feasibility, efficacy, and safety of the off‐label use of the MFO for transcatheter closure of moderate to large PDA, in patients for whom traditional devices were considered not suitable.

METHODS

Study Design and Study Population

A multicenter, international retrospective cohort study was conducted in 14 tertiary hospitals from 5 countries. Patients of any age in whom transcatheter PDA closure was attempted with the MFO device between January 2018 and February 2025 were retrospectively analyzed to review procedural characteristics and outcomes. There were no exclusion criteria. The decision to use this device was based on operators’ clinical judgment, when they felt that this was the most appropriate device to use. All parents or legal guardians granted their informed consent for the procedure. Our study complies with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology cohort reporting guidelines. 13 In accordance with the Transparency and Openness Promotion Guidelines, the data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Institutional review board approval was obtained from Nantes University Hospital (GNEDS no. 22‐10‐240). The research study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Variables

Baseline Characteristics

Baseline characteristics screened for the study included age and weight at time of catheterization, gender, prematurity, birth weight, associated genetic syndrome, additional congenital heart disease, and comorbidity. Where available, we recorded symptoms associated with PDA, evidence of left ventricular volume overload or pulmonary hypertension, transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) characteristics (minimal ductal diameter, ductal length, maximal flow velocities on left pulmonary artery (LPA) and descending aorta (DAo), and left ventricular end‐diastolic dimension).

PDA Characteristics

Invasive hemodynamic parameters were recorded when available, and angiographic minimal ductal diameter and ductal length were recorded, as well as ductal morphology according to the Krichenko criteria. 14 Short PDA was defined as ductal length <4 mm. 5

Procedural Characteristics

Procedural access and route; MFO size, type, and size of delivery sheath; fluoroscopy time; and dose were evaluated. Procedural success was defined as adequate device placement and release within the PDA. A residual shunt was regarded as a postprocedural complication.

Procedural Outcomes

Device‐ or procedure‐related major adverse event were defined a priori (Table 1). 5 , 6 Outcomes were classified as early (≤30 days after device implantation) or late (>30 days). LPA and DAo obstruction was defined as a significant increase in LPA or DAo flow velocities with a peak velocity (Vmax) >2.5 m/s. 7 , 15

Outcomes

Patients were routinely discharged within 24 hours after the procedure. Routine outpatient follow‐up was usually arranged at 4 to 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months post procedure and then once yearly thereafter for at least 1 to 2 years before potential discharge. Prospective follow‐up was achieved until June 2024 and relevant data were recorded including length of follow‐up, late onset or worsening of device‐related LPA and DAo obstruction, late device embolization, late persistence of any residual shunt, device‐related hemolysis, thromboembolic complication, bacterial endocarditis, and unplanned catheterization or surgical intervention.

Procedure Characteristics

The device specifications are summarized in Table 2. Transcatheter PDA closure was routinely performed in the catheterization laboratory, under TTE or fluoroscopic guidance. Anticoagulation (intravenous heparin 100 IU/kg) and prophylactic antibiotics according to institutional protocols were administered at the start of the procedure. Hemodynamic assessment was performed as per operator preference. Biplane aortic angiography (30° right anterior oblique and 90° lateral views) allowed proper delineation of the PDA morphology and its angiographic classification, according to the Krichenko criteria. 14 The minimal ductal diameter and the ductal length were obtained predominantly from angiographic measurements. Baseline PDA size, maximal peak instantaneous gradient on echocardiogram (represented as peak velocity in meters per second) across the PDA, the LPA origin, and aortic isthmus were recorded before PDA instrumentation. The selected device had a right ventricular waist diameter at least 1 to 2 mm larger than the narrowest PDA diameter. The device was deployed through a delivery sheath via either an anterograde or a retrograde approach. Angiography and TTE were used to assess device positioning and absence of peridevice residual shunt, descending aorta and LPA device‐related obstruction. After device release, TTE was repeated to assess the outcome and to exclude new‐onset tricuspid regurgitation or pericardial effusion. Patients were transferred to the ward for overnight observation with vital signs and neurovascular monitoring. Postprocedure TTE was performed within 24 hours and before discharge to confirm stable position of the device, residual shunt, and absence of acquired LPA and DAo obstruction.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables are presented as median, interquartile range, and range. To assess the variables independency, chi‐square was used for categorical variables and Wilcoxon sum of ranks for continuous variables. Statistical tests were performed using StataMP (version 18). A 2‐sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 77 procedures in 75 patients were included in the study (Central Illustration).

Baseline Clinical Characteristics

There were 5 (6.7%) adults aged 17, 23, 26, 42 and 68 years. Of the 72 (93.5%) children (median age: 0.8 years [range, 0.1–10], median procedural weight: 6.9 kg [range, 2.1–32.0]), 37 (51.4%) weighed <6 kg (median procedural weight: 4.0 kg [range, 2.1–5.9]), of whom 8 (21.6%) were ex‐preterm (median birth weight: 1105 g [range, 511–2900], median gestational age: 32.5 weeks [range, 23.4–36.0]). A genetic syndrome was identified in 9 (11.7%) patients, as were associated cardiac malformations in 15 (19.5%), lung disease in 5 (6.5%) and major nonrespiratory comorbidity in 7 (9.1%) (Tables 3 and 4).

PDA Characteristics

PDA was large with a minimal ductal diameter of 3.8 mm (interquartile range [IQR], 3.0–7.0; range, 1.5–7.0) and short in 42/77 (54.5%, median ductal length: 6.0 mm [range, 2.0–35.0]). The PDA was larger in patients weighing <6 kg (median minimal ductal diameter: 4.5 mm [IQR, 3.0–5.0; range, 1.8–8.0]) compared with those >6 kg (median minimal ductal diameter: 3.0 mm [IQR, 2.0–4.5; range, 1.5–9.0]) (P=0.05). The PA pressure was recorded in 64 patients and was greater than two thirds systemic in 33 (51.6% of patients). Of the patients with pulmonary hypertension, 24/33 (72.7%) were in the <6 kg subgroup. Ductal morphology was conical (Krichenko type A) in 40 (51.9%), window‐type or complex in 20 (26.7%; type B: 14 [18.2%], type D: 6 [7.8%]), tubular (type C) in 7 (9.1%) and elongated (type E) in 10 (13.0%). There were fewer type A PDAs and more type B and more type C PDAs in patients <6 kg (51.9% versus 66.7%, P=0.08; 43.3% versus 12.1%, P=0.01).

Procedural Characteristics

The median fluoroscopy time was 9.5 minutes (IQR, 5.6–14.8; range, 1.3–34.3) and the median fluoroscopic dose was 135.05 cGycm2 (IQR, 65–538; range, 0.01–104 120) and 19.5 mGy (IQR, 10–27; range, 4–1237). Fluoroscopy‐free PDA closure was performed with TTE guidance in only 1 patient. Of the 77 procedures, 67 (87.0%) had both arterial and venous femoral access, 8 (10.7%) had venous‐only access, and none had arterial‐only access. There were no case of jugular access. The device was delivered anterograde in 77.9% and retrograde in 22.1%. A 5 Fr delivery sheath or smaller was used in two thirds of patients. No issue was reported with the use of the recommended SteerEase delivery system. However, in 19 patients, a delivery system other than a SteerEase Introducer was used to deliver the MFO and these included the Amplatzer TorqVue LP (Abbott Medical, Plymouth, MN, USA) in 9, the Amplatzer TorqVue 180 in 8, and the Launcher Guide catheter (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) in 2 patients. In 6 patients, a 5‐Fr delivery system was used to deploy an MFO 9–7 (2 patients), 10–8 (1) or 10–8 (1), and a 6‐Fr delivery sheath to deploy an MFO 12–10 (2), which are 1‐Fr smaller than the manufacturer’s recommended size.

Almost half of the devices chosen (46.7%) were larger than an MFO 8–6, with the following distribution: MFO 5–3 in 10 patients (13.0%), 6–4 in 18 (23.4%), 7–5 in 8 (10.4%), 8–6 in 7 (9.1%), 9–7 in 8 (10.4%), 10–8 in 13 (16.9%), 12–10 in 6 (7.8%) and 14–12 in 2 (2.6%) (Table 5). An alternative device had been previously attempted in 4 cases (5.2%) during the same procedure. In 2 cases, placement of an Amplatzer Duct Occluder I 10/8 resulted in significant residual shunts around the device. The operators attributed this to device suitability for the ductal anatomy rather than the effects of intravenous heparinization and therefore expected the shunts were unlikely to resolve. Replacement with an MFO 10–8 (PTFE membrane) resulted in immediate resolution of the shunt. In 2 cases, an Amplatzer Vascular Plug II 8 mm was placed with concerns of instability and migration and was successfully replaced with an MFO 7–5 and 9–7 respectively. In the series, 15 patients underwent successful device implantation following an adjustment in the size (by 1 or 2 sizes) of the originally chosen MFO that had been deployed but not released, within the same procedure. Among these, 4 patients required downsizing of the MFO due to concerns about obstruction of surrounding structures. The remaining 11 patients needed upsizing due to issues with device instability or the presence of a residual shunt. In our early experience, 2 patients required 2 device size adjustments.

Procedural Details in Pediatric Patients

The smallest patient in the series was a 2.1 kg premature infant with a Krichenko type A PDA (minimal ductal diameter: 3 mm, ductal length: 10 mm) that was successfully closed by anterograde deployment of an MFO 6–4 (Figures 2 and 3). The decision to use an MFO in this low‐body‐weight infant was related to the ductal dimension and shape, leading the operator to consider that the combination of 2 large retention discs and high flexibility/conformability of the device would be ideal to successfully closed this PDA. Despite the extraductal position of a 10 mm disc at the PA and aortic extremities, no protrusion of the device and no obstruction to the pulmonary or aortic blood flow was seen at the time of the procedure and during follow‐up echocardiography. Of the 2 largest PDAs in the series, one was in a 2‐year‐old, 10‐kg child with Trisomy 21 who was referred with a large PDA (minimal ductal diameter: 7.6 mm, ductal length: 9.5 mm) and 80% systemic PA pressures. The child had an attempt with an MFO 10–8 that prolapsed easily through the duct and had subsequent successful closure with a MFO 14–12 MFO delivered anterograde through a 7‐Fr SteerEase™ sheath (Figure 4). The other was in a 19‐month‐old infant with a Krichenko type A PDA (minimal ductal diameter: 10 mm, ductal length: 12 mm). Successful closure was achieved with an MFO 14–12 deployed anterograde through a 7 Fr SteerEase™ delivery system. A trivial residual shunt was observed at discharge and early follow‐up that had completely resolved at the latest follow‐up.

Procedural Failures

Among 77 attempted PDA closures (in 75 patients) with the MFO, procedural success rate was 96.1% (74/77 procedures). There were 3 procedural failures from a single institution during their early experience. The first patient, a 9‐month‐old, 7 kg child with a 5 mm diameter, 9 mm length Krichenko type A PDA, and greater than two‐thirds systemic PA pressures, had an attempt at transcatheter closure with an MFO 8–6. Despite several attempts, there were concerns with position and a significant residual shunt; therefore, the device was not released, and the child had surgical ligation via thoracotomy 4 days later. The second patient was a 5‐month‐old, 6.4 kg child with a 2 mm Krichenko type A PDA, a mildly hypoplastic descending aorta, and a large atrial septal duct. A 7–5 MFO device was deployed but not released. The intraprocedural TTE showed device protrusion causing mild acquired aortic obstruction, leading to device retrieval and successful PDA closure with an Amplatzer Duct Occluder II 5/4 (Abbott Medical, Plymouth, MN, USA) during the same case. The third patient was a 2‐month‐old, 3.3 kg infant with Trisomy 21 and a 5 mm Krichenko type B PDA. An MFO 8–6 was deployed anterograde through a 5 Fr SteerEase delivery sheath; however, there were significant difficulties with manipulation and proper placement within the duct. A subsequent attempt with a Ceraflex PDA Occluder (Lifetech, Shenzen, China) was also unsuccessful and consequently, the decision was made to proceed with surgical closure.

Procedural Outcomes

The occlusion rate on immediate postintervention TTE was 85.7% (66/77), including 22 patients with a trivial intraprosthetic shunt. Seven patients had a mild residual intraprosthetic shunt that resolved spontaneously within a few days and had completely disappeared at latest follow‐up TTE. Major adverse events occurred in 4/77 cases (5.2%) and are detailed in Table 6. One child had a significant residual intraprosthetic shunt with device‐related hemolysis requiring blood transfusion and required transcatheter retrieval and reintervention with a larger MFO device at 1 month after the original procedure. Two patients (2.7%) had transcatheter reinterventions. Two other patients (2.7%) who were closely followed up with serial normal TTEs died from device‐unrelated causes at 4 and 5 months after the procedure.

Of the 73 survivors within the cohort, postprocedural outcome was uneventful in 94.5% cases, as early and late device‐related complications occurred in 1 (1.3%) and 3 (3.9%), respectively, after a median follow‐up of 8 months (IQR, 6–18 months; range, 3–48 months). Early migration of an MFO 7–5 to the right PA occurred 6 hours after device implantation in a 4‐kg infant with a Krichenko Type B 6 mm width, 4.5 mm length PDA, who subsequently underwent percutaneous retrieval of the MFO 7–5 using a 10 mm ONE Snare (Merit Medical, Jordan, UT, USA) through a 5‐Fr Flexor Guiding sheath (Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN, USA) and successful implantation of a larger MFO 10–8 with good long‐term result (Table 6, Patient 1). After home discharge, persistence of a significant residual shunt with hemolysis requiring blood transfusion led to late reintervention in 1 (1.7%) patient (Table 6, Patient 2). An MFO 8–6 was initially implanted in this patient with a 4.5 mm width, 4 mm length PDA, and reintervention occurred at 4.4 kg. The device was easily snared with a 10 mm ONE Snare and retrieved percutaneously through a 7‐Fr Amplatzer Trevisio sheath 5 weeks after the initial procedure, with successful implantation of an MFO 9–7 achieving complete PDA closure (Figure 5). Device protrusion led to late surgical revision in 2 patients (2.6%). The aforementioned patient had an MFO 8–6 retrieved and a MFO 9–7 implanted at 4.4 kg for a 4.5 mm width, 4 mm length PDA, which led to acquired LPA obstruction and surgical device extraction with LPA plasty 8 months after device implantation (day‐1 LPA Vmax: 1.7 m/s, 8 months later LPA Vmax: 2.4 m/s with continuous diastolic flow; however, LPA caliber reduced to 2–3 mm diameter and perfusion scan near occlusion of LPA flow) (Table 6, Patient 3). The other patient was a 2.3 kg infant who had an MFO 7–5 implanted with a 4 mm width, 3 mm length PDA, which led to acquired obstruction of the LPA leading to surgical device extraction and PDA ligation 5 months later (day‐1 LPA Vmax: 2.0 m/s, 5 months later LPA Vmax: 3.2 m/s) (Table 6, Patient 4). There were no other major adverse events reported.

DISCUSSION

This is the first large‐scale, international series of transcatheter PDA closure using the MFO device. Our study confirms the feasibility and efficacy of this off‐label indication.

MFO Design Advantages

Transcatheter PDA closure is the gold standard of clinical practice and available duct occluder devices have demonstrated a commendable track record in safety, efficacy, and favorable long‐term outcomes. 1 , 3 , 5 , 16 The MFO device, originally designed and approved for transcatheter ventricular septal defect closure, possesses several design advantages with potential to address gaps in available devices for PDA closure across anatomical and hemodynamic variations. The MFO is a low‐profile, double‐disc device with cone‐shaped connecting waist. The larger devices have the added advantage of a PTFE membrane for immediate shunt obliteration. 9 The bidirectional screw‐in feature sets it apart, allowing the option of anterograde or retrograde deployment and in the desired configuration. The device’s inherent softness and low‐profile architecture offers significant advantages in conformity to atypical PDA shape and adaptability to surrounding structures potentially reducing the risk of aorta or LPA obstruction. The off‐label use of the MFO in our cohort of patients emerged as a necessity due to anatomic and physiologic challenges that were not amenable to conventional devices. A substantial proportion of our patients exhibited Krichenko type D and E anatomy, which is commonly challenging to manage with transcatheter closure. In addition, a significant number of patients had short PDAs and elevated PA pressures. The unique double‐disc profile, coupled with its supple and adaptable construction, confers a distinct advantage in conforming to large ducts, small aorta, and PAs, minimizing the risk of adjacent vessel obstruction in small patients, as illustrated in our series by successful PDA closure in 37 patients weighing <6 kg, including a 2.1 kg premature infant.

The double‐disc feature used in the pulmonary hypertensive ducts offers added security against embolization. The manufacturer’s guidelines recommend the use of the SteerEase system with its low profile and easy‐to‐use design. In our practice, we successfully downsized the sheath by 1‐Fr from the manufacturer’s recommendations, rendering the MFO particularly advantageous as more than half of its available sizes can be delivered through a 4‐Fr sheath. Moreover, our study revealed that the MFO can be reliably delivered and retrieved through a range of alternative delivery systems and guide catheters further enhancing its versatility and applicability in clinical settings. Our success in PDA closure with the MFO extends across a wide spectrum of cases, encompassing large PDA diameters up to 9 mm as well as short PDAs. Notably, we achieved favorable outcomes even in a small premature infant of 2.1 kg highlighting the significance of delivery sheath size in performing such delicate procedures.

Safety

The success rate achieved with the MFO device parallels that reported in the existing literature for transcatheter PDA closure using alternative or off‐label devices. 5 , 6 , 10 , 15 Our study demonstrates no procedure‐related morbidity and a complication rate similar to other reported cohorts. However, it acknowledges a higher complication rate compared with standard transcatheter PDA closure, which can be attributed to the specific challenges of this patient cohort and the learning curve associated with understanding the nuances of the device in this indication. Notably, no instances of tricuspid valve injury were observed, a benefit likely attributable to the versatility of device delivery options (anterograde and retrograde) and the use of low‐profile delivery equipment. Among the reported complications, cases of left PA obstruction and aortic obstruction were noted. This underscores the critical importance of meticulous device placement and a comprehensive understanding of the interaction between the device’s 2 discs and the surrounding anatomical structures. The MFO can also be easily retrieved into small sheath sizes, which presents an advantage to other conventional devices. There has been a significant learning curve, however, with increasing experience, a decrease in the incidence of left PA obstruction was observed, primarily attributed to operator proficiency in device placement tailored to the ductus anatomy and surrounding structures.

Implications for Management in PDA

The MFO is not intended as a replacement for established devices currently in use. The potential for the MFO extends beyond the scope of ventricular septal defect closure, demonstrating promise within our patient cohort for broader application in transcatheter PDA closure. Although existing PDA devices offer efficacy and safety, they do not accommodate all PDA shapes and hemodynamic states. Consequently, there exists a subset of patients who, owing to the nature of their PDA or prevailing hemodynamics, are deemed unsuitable for transcatheter closure and offered surgical ligation. Our aim is to bridge this gap by extending the percutaneous approach to encompass patients who may have been previously not suitable candidates for conventional transcatheter closure. Our series underscores the safety and efficacy of using the MFO in managing challenging scenarios such as large PDAs, pulmonary hypertensive states, and atypical PDA anatomies but, due to its low profile, the MFO may also be suitable to close the smaller PDA and could be considered as an alternative to the Amplatzer Duct Occluder II. One of the drawbacks to be acknowledged is the high incidence of early residual, trivial to mild intraprosthetic shunt observed with the smaller devices (without the PTFE membrane) but these shunts tend to resolve spontaneously by the subsequent outpatient follow‐up.

Study Limitations

First, the decision to use the MFO was at the discretion of the interventionalist, making it unclear whether some of the PDAs could have been closed with currently available on‐label, or other off‐label devices. Second, the retrospective data collection at multiple centers may have underestimated the incidence of adverse events during follow‐up. However, children with a new, off‐label used device had close follow‐up at all sites, which limits the likelihood of underreported clinically significant events. Third, our results may reflect the individual learning curves of the centers involved and the complication rate may be exaggerated due to the associated learning curve.

CONCLUSIONS

We report the first international experience with the off‐label use of the MFO device for transcatheter PDA closure. Safety and efficacy were demonstrated across a wide spectrum of anatomical and hemodynamic variations, especially in challenging cases such as types D and E PDAs, short PDAs, and elevated PA pressures. Additionally, the MFO device’s compatibility with alternative delivery systems, coupled with successful downsizing of delivery sheath size, underscores its versatility and applicability in clinical practice. These results remain to be confirmed by extensive experience and longer‐term outcomes data.
