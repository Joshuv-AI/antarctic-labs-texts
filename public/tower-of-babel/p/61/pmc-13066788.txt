Impact of fraction size on the efficacy of xylo-oligosaccharides in broiler chicken diets
Authors: Andrew D Wallace, Michael R Bedford, Gemma González-Ortiz, Natalie K Morgan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNumerous studies have reported the beneficial effects of supplemental xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS) on broiler performance and nutrient utilization. These effects are attributed to their prebiotic action, directly fueling beneficial bacterial species, and their ability to stimulate fibre-degrading bacteria in the gastrointestinal tract. Commercial XOS products typically contain mixtures with varying degrees of polymerisation (DP), ranging from xylobiose (X2) to xylohexaose (X6). This study aimed to determine the optimum DP range of XOS for broiler chickens. Two trials were conducted using 0 d-old Cobb 500 broilers (300 per trial, 60 birds per treatment, initial body weight 45.80 ± 0.15 g) fed wheat-based diets over 35-d period as three phases (starter d 0-12, grower d 12-23, and finisher d 23-35). In both trials, birds were fed one of five XOS treatments (150 g/tonne); no XOS (control), low-DP XOS, medium-DP XOS, high-DP XOS, or a standard commercial XOS mixture. In Trial 1, the XOS treatments were fed without xylanase, and in Trial 2 the XOS treatments were fed with xylanase (16,000 birchwood xylanase units [BXU]/kg). In Trial 1, birds fed the high-DP XOS had significantly lower feed conversion ratio corrected for mortality (cFCR) at d 0 to 35 (P < 0.001) compared to birds fed the control and medium-DP XOS treatments. Additionally, birds fed the control diet presented higher caecal concentrations of branched-chain fatty acids (iso-butyric and iso-valeric acids) compared to those fed the high-DP XOS (P = 0.025 and P = 0.035, respectively). In Trial 2, birds receiving the high-DP XOS showed improved cFCR compared to those fed the low-DP XOS (P = 0.037). Low-DP XOS fed birds also presented increased caecal Enterobacteriaceae abundance compared to those fed the control diet, medium-DP XOS or commercial XOS treatments (P = 0.008). In conclusion, supplementation with high-DP XOS was more effective in improving feed efficiency and modulating caecal fermentation than feeding no XOS or low-DP XOS.Keywords: Prebiotic, Oligosaccharide, Meat chicken

Introduction

Hemicelluloses, particularly arabinoxylan and related xylan-containing polysaccharides, are key structural components of cereal grains. Xylan consists of a xylose backbone with β-1,4-glycosidic bonds and various substituents, including arabinose, acetyl groups, lignin, other saccharides, and uronic acids (Ebringerová and Heinze, 2000; Kelly et al., 2021). In broilers, these polysaccharides can act as antinutrients, hindering nutrient absorption and reducing feed efficiency (González-Ortiz et al., 2019). In the presence of endo-xylanase, arabinoxylan undergoes partial hydrolysis, releasing a mixture of xylan-derived oligosaccharides, including xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS), arabinoxylan-derived oligosaccharides (AXOS), acetylated XOS, and other substituted hydrolysis products. The XOS are defined as unsubstituted β-(1 → 4)-linked XOS, typically comprising 2 to 10 xylose units. The XOS are fermented by beneficial fibre-degrading anaerobic microbes that populate the caeca, resulting in the production of valuable short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which provide the bird with supplementary energy (Svihus et al., 2013). These microbes release extracellular enzymes, such as xylanase and cellulase, that hydrolyse dietary polysaccharides into oligosaccharides (González-Ortiz et al., 2019; Thomas et al., 2011), reducing the antinutritive effects of these polysaccharides and increasing nutrient digestibility. Furthermore, the proliferation of these beneficial microbes in the caeca enhances competitive exclusion, suppressing pathogenic bacteria species, maintaining microbial balance, and supporting gut health (Maurer et al., 2024; Patterson and Burkholder, 2003; Xiao et al., 2017). Using targeted strategies, such as XOS application in diets, to enhance the abundance of beneficial fibre-degrading microbiota species could improve bird health and feed efficiency (González-Ortiz et al., 2019).

Oligosaccharides are actively transported into microbes via membrane transporters, which are highly specific for particular oligosaccharides (Kelly et al., 2021). Inside the microbe, these oligosaccharides are metabolised to release energy, producing SCFAs as by-products which are subsequently absorbed through the gut lumen (Ali et al., 2022). Each microbial species has a restricted range of enzymes and transport systems it can express. Given the metabolic expense of producing enzymes and transporters, bacteria regulate their expression using substrate-derived signaling molecules (Deutscher et al., 2006; Kelly et al., 2021; Rogowski et al., 2015; Thomas et al., 2011; Xiao et al., 2017). Microbes that rapidly adapt to these signals gain a competitive advantage, shifting the microbial community toward species best suited to the dietary substrates (Kelly et al., 2021; Thomas et al., 2011). Given the short lifespan of broilers, fortifying diets with appropriate signaling molecules may accelerate microbiota establishment and enhance feed efficiency. The XOS are recognized as such signaling molecules and have been shown to improve fermentation and utilization of dietary xylan, leading to improved gut health and bird performance (Morgan et al., 2024).

Exogenous endo-xylanase supplementation is widely used in poultry diets to counteract the antinutritive effects of dietary xylan (González-Ortiz et al., 2019). Xylanase cleaves β-(1 →4)-glycosidic bonds within xylan-based polysaccharides, solubilising the fibre and increasing the amount of fermentable material that reaches the caeca (Morgan, 2023; Svihus et al., 2013). This solubilisation can influence microbial adaptation in the gut (González-Ortiz et al., 2019; Morgan et al., 2020). When uninhibited, endo-xylanase acting on purified xylan predominantly produces xylobiose (X2) and xylotriose (X3); however, this pattern of hydrolysis rarely occurs in vivo. In vitro studies by Moura et al. (2007) and Gullón et al. (2011) demonstrated that beneficial microbes preferentially utilise XOS with small degrees of polymerisation (DP), namely X2 and X3, over higher-DP XOS. Low-DP XOS therefore function as prebiotics by providing readily fermentable substrates for the microbiota (Aachary and Prapulla, 2011). Commercially produced XOS products also contain high proportions of X2 and X3, with relatively few larger oligosaccharides such as xylopentaose (X5) and xylohexaose (X6) (Moura et al., 2007). The performance benefits typically attributed to xylanase and XOS supplementation are therefore generally assumed to derive from increased concentrations of X2 and X3. However, the assumption that improvements in broiler feed efficiency from XOS supplementation are driven solely by their prebiotic effects has recently been challenged. Studies such as Ribeiro et al. (2018) have shown performance benefits at inclusion levels far below those required to elicit a classical prebiotic response (<100 g/tonne). This has led to the proposal that XOS also act as signaling molecules, stimulating fibre-degrading microbiota to increase extracellular enzyme production. González-Ortiz et al. (2019) formalized this concept by defining low-dose XOS used to stimulate beneficial microbial adaptation as ‘stimbiotics’.

This study, therefore, hypothesized that low-DP XOS would behave primarily as prebiotics, whereas high-DP XOS would function as stimbiotics. This distinction is supported by microbial transport systems: ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, common in fibre-degrading bacteria, import larger oligosaccharides, while phosphotransferase system (PTS) transporters, which are more prevalent among opportunistic or pathogenic species, preferentially transport small disaccharides and monosaccharides (Deutscher et al., 2006; Jeckelmann and Erni, 2020; Kelly et al., 2021; Pokusaeva et al., 2011). On this basis, supplementing broiler diets with high-DP XOS was expected to preferentially stimulate fibre-degrading bacteria.

The first aim of this study was to assess the impact of fraction size on XOS efficacy with respect to bird performance. The second aim was to determine how supplemental xylanase interacts with XOS of varying DP. Xylanase may enhance XOS efficacy, by increasing substrate availability to the microbiota through the solubilisation of xylan-based polysaccharides, or may hydrolyse the supplemented high-DP XOS into smaller fragments such as X2 and X3, potentially diminishing the intended stimbiotic effects.

Materials and methods

The XOS fraction preparation

Low-DP XOS production

Low-DP XOS were extracted from a commercial 95% XOS powder (Alfa Chemistry, New York, NY, USA). Briefly, 200 g XOS was suspended in 1.8 L of ethanol, heated to 65 °C, and stirred for 30 min. The mixture was cooled to room temperature and stored at −30 °C for 16 h. The supernatant was then decanted, and the residue was re-extracted with 1.5 L of ethanol using the same procedure. The combined ethanol extracts were concentrated by rotary evaporation and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C, yielding about 25 to 30 g of low-DP XOS. Liquid chromatography-mass spectroscopy (LC–MS) analysis was used to confirm DP distribution. The ethanol-insoluble residue was retained for further fractionation.

Medium-DP and high-DP XOS production

Medium-DP and high-DP XOS fractions were prepared from a commercial 35% XOS product (AB Vista, Marlborough, Wiltshire, UK) and from the residue generated in Section 2.1.1. Crude XOS was mixed with a fourfold excess of methanol (w/v; e.g. 1 kg XOS in 4 L methanol) and mechanically stirred for about 4 h at room temperature. The methanol extract was decanted, filtered, and concentrated to near saturation. The concentrate was added dropwise to an equal volume of acetone to induce selective precipitation of higher-DP oligomers. The collected solids were filtered under dry nitrogen and dried at 60 °C. The filtrate was concentrated and re-precipitated twice more, generating three primary fractions. Each primary fraction was then subjected to two additional rounds of concentration and acetone precipitation, producing 27 total sub-fractions. All fractions were analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography-evaporative light scattering detector (HPLC-ELSD). Fractions enriched in X3 were pooled to produce the medium-DP XOS, and those enriched in X4 and above were pooled to produce the high-DP XOS. Non-qualifying fractions were discarded. Final pooled products were verified by LC–MS.

High-performance liquid chromatography-evaporative light scattering detector

The XOS distributions were analyzed using an Agilent HPLC-ELSD system (Agilent Technologies Australia Pty Ltd., Mulgrave, VIC, Australia) with a Rezex RPM Pb2+ column (300 mm × 7.8 mm; Phenomenex, Lane Cove, NSW, Australia) at 80 °C and water as the mobile phase (0.6 mL/min). Oligomers were identified using X2–X6 standards (Megazyme Ltd., Bray, County Wicklow, Ireland), and compositions were estimated from the relative areas of partially resolved peaks.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectroscopy

Concentrations of XOS in the final products were determined by 1-phenyl-3-methyl-5-pyrazolone (PMP)-derivatisation followed by LC–MS analysis. Samples were spiked with 1 μg/mL D-xylose-13C5 as internal standard, cleaned using Bond Elut C18 SPE cartridges (Agilent Technologies Australia Pty Ltd., Mulgrave, VIC, Australia), and treated with α-amyloglucosidase (pH 5, 50 °C, and 1 h) to remove α-glucans.

Derivatisation followed the established PMP protocol (Morgan et al., 2020) with minor modifications: samples were reacted with 0.5 mol/L PMP in 2 mol/L NH3 at 75 °C for 75 min, quenched with 3 mol/L formic acid, and residual PMP removed by triple chloroform extraction. PMP-derivatised XOS were further purified by C18 SPE and eluted in 40% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid.

Analyses were performed on an LC–MS system equipped with a Zorbax SB-C18 column (1.8 μm, 3.0 mm × 150 mm, Agilent Technologies Australia Pty Ltd., Mulgrave, VIC, Australia) at 60 °C. The mobile phase was isocratic 17.5% acetonitrile in water with 0.1% formic acid at 0.4 mL/min. Detection used positive-mode ion electrospray ionisation (ESI) with selected ion monitoring (SIM) for PMP-derivatised oligomers (X2–X6) and internal standard. Quantification employed XOS standards (Megazyme Ltd., Bray, County Wicklow, Ireland), prepared identically. Experimental design

Two trials were conducted, both with Cobb 500 mixed-sex broilers (300 + 5 spare birds per trial, initial body weight 45.80 ± 0.15 g) obtained from a commercial hatchery on the day of hatch. The chicks were randomized by weight and placed in 1.07 m2 floor pens, with 30 pens containing 10 or 11 birds per pen, approximately 60 birds per treatment, bedded on clean wood shavings. Pen allocation was randomized across the room. All birds received vaccination against Marek’s disease, infectious bronchitis, and Newcastle disease at the hatchery, in accordance with the Australian Code of Practice for the Distribution of Broiler Chickens (DAFF, 2022). Temperature settings followed Cobb 500 recommendations: 33 to 34 °C on arrival, followed by a gradual decrease of approximately 0.5 °C daily until a temperature of 21 to 22 °C was reached by d 21. The lighting regimen consisted of 24 h of light on d 1, with darkness increasing by 1 h/d until 6 h of darkness was reached, which was maintained for the remainder of the study.

Mortality was recorded daily, and any birds culled or found dead were weighed. Institutional and national guidelines for the care and use of animals were followed, and all experimental procedures involving animals were approved (AEC18–120) by the Animal Ethics Committee at the University of New England, New South Wales, Australia.

Dietary treatments

Both trials utilized the same basal diets. Diets were fed as three phases: starter (d 0-12), grower (d 13-23), and finisher (d 24-35). Prior to diet formulation, representative subsamples of feed ingredients were analyzed by near-infrared spectroscopy to predict proximate analysis, amino acid (AA) concentrations, and apparent metabolisable energy (AME) using AMINONIR PROX, AMINONIR NIR, and AMINONIR NRG (Evonik Nutrition & Care GmbH, Hanau, Hesse, Germany). The L-Lys, DL-Met, and L-Thr used in the diets were feed grade (≥ 98% purity). For each phase in both trials, sufficient basal diet was manufactured to feed all birds and was subsequently divided into five treatment groups. The Starter diet was fed as a crumble from d 0 to 7 and as pellets from d 8 to 12, while the grower and finisher diets were fed as pellets.

Table 2 presents the final diet formulation along with the analyzed dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), and gross energy (GE) content of the basal diet. The protein content of the dietary treatments was determined by measuring nitrogen using the combustion method (LECO Corp., St. Joeseph, MI, USA), with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as a calibration standard, and multiplying the nitrogen value by a factor of 6.25. Diet GE content was determined using an adiabatic bomb calorimeter (Model 6400, Parr Instruments Co., Moline, IL, USA), standardised with benzoic acid. DM was analyzed by oven-drying at 105 °C to constant weight (method 930.15, AOAC, 2005) using a laboratory oven (UNB 500, Memmert GmbH + Co. KG, Schwabach, Germany).

The dietary treatments included a control diet and four treatment diets; the control group received no additive, while the treatment group diets were supplemented with 150 g/tonne of either low-DP XOS, medium-DP XOS, high-DP XOS, or a standard commercial 95% XOS mixture. To ensure uniform distribution in the feed, XOS fractions were dissolved in water and thoroughly mixed into the basal diet for 15 to 20 min before cold-pelleting at 65 °C. In Trial 2, xylanase (Econase XT 5P, AB Vista, Marlborough, UK) was incorporated into all dietary treatments. Birds had ad libitum access to treatment diets and water throughout the trial.

Xylanase activity in the diets was analyzed externally by AB Vista Laboratories (The Mezzo, Singapore) using a Quantiplate ELISA kit (11351, EnviroLogix, Portland, ME, USA). Analysed xylanase activity was consistently < 2000 birchwood xylanase units (BXU)/kg in diets without xylanase and > 14,000 BXU/kg in diets supplemented with xylanase.

Response variables

Bird performance at d 35

The starting total pen weight and mean chick body weight (BW) were determined, ensuring there was no significant difference in starting BW across the dietary treatments. On arrival (d 0) and d 35, birds and feed were weighed per pen, to determine body weight gain (BWG) and feed intake (FI). The number of mortalities and weights of dead birds were recorded daily, and the feed conversion ratio corrected for mortality (cFCR) was calculated from d 0 to 35.

Sample collection

On d 34, approximately 3 fresh excreta droppings were collected from each pen by monitoring the birds and collecting the samples immediately post-defecation. Samples were then immediately immersed in methanol, mixed, and stored at 4 to 6 °C until required. On d 35, 4 birds per pen (two male and two female) were euthanized by cervical dislocation and ileum and caeca samples were collected and pooled per pen. Caeca samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −30 °C. Ileal digesta samples were immediately immersed in methanol, mixed, and stored at 4 to 6 °C until analysis.

Caecal xylanase and cellulase activity at d 35

In the caeca samples, xylanase concentration was analyzed by Megazyme endo-xylanase assay kit (K-XylX6), and cellulase activity was analysed by Megazyme endo-cellulase kit (K-CELLG3) (Megazyme Ltd., Bray, County Wicklow, Ireland), using a UV-spectroscopy at 510 nm (Cary 50 Bio UV–Visible spectrophotometer equipped with a Cary 50 MPR microplate reader, Varian Inc., Palo Alto, CA). Supplied calibrations were used to determine xylanase and cellulase activity in U/g.

Caecal volatile fatty acid (VFA) concentration at d 35

Approximately 500 to 600 mg of wet caecal sample was transferred to a 2-mL microtube, and the mass was accurately recorded. To each tube, 1000 μL of an internal standard solution containing 0.01 mol/L 2-ethyl butyric acid and 0.05 mol/L succinic acid-2,2,3,3-d4 (Sigma–Aldrich, Castle Hill, NSW, Australia) in 0.2 mol/L NaOH was added. The mixture was homogenized using a rotary mixer for 5 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min. 100 μL of the resulting supernatant was transferred to a new 2-mL microtube. 50 μL of 1 mol/L HCl and 1.5 mL of diethyl ether were added, and then the solution was mixed for 5 min. The bottom aqueous layer was removed, and the diethyl ether phase was dried over anhydrous sodium sulphate.

The diethyl ether (50 μL) phase was transferred into a glass 2 mL vial containing 400 μL of acetonitrile, 10 μL of dry pyridine, and 25 μL of N-tert-butyldimethylsilyl-N-methyl trifluoroacetamide solution (MTBSTFA; 1% N-tert-butyldimethylchlorosilane [t-BDMCS]). The vial was immediately sealed and heated at 80 °C for 30 min.

The prepared samples were analyzed using a GC–MS selective detector system, 7890A/5977C (Agilent Technologies Australia Pty Ltd., Mulgrave, VIC, Australia), equipped with a HP-5MS column (30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm), within 12 h of preparation. Peak identification and quantification were achieved by comparing retention times and single-ion chromatograms to known derivatised standards, targeting the following ions: m/z 117 (acetic acid), m/z 131 (propionic acid), m/z 145 (iso- and n-butyric acids), m/z 158 (iso- and n-valeric acids), m/z 261 (lactic acid), m/z 289 (succinic acid), m/z 173 (2-ethyl butyric acid), and m/z 293 (succinic acid-2,2,3,3-d4). The concentrations of caecal VFA were expressed as μmol/g of digesta.

Caecal microbiota concentration at d 35

Analysis of microbiota composition was determined in duplicate in the d 35 caecal digesta samples. DNA extraction from the samples was performed using an Isolate II Plant DNA Kit (Bioline [Aust] Pty Ltd., Alexandria, NSW, Australia) and QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (51504, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) with slight modifications, as described by Keerqin et al. (2017) and Kheravii et al. (2017). The purity of the extracted DNA was assessed by a Nano-Drop ND-8000 UV spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Only DNA elution that emitted ratios from 1.8 and above at a wavelength of 260/280 nm was used for PCR analysis. Following a 20 × dilution with sterilized water, the extracted DNA was analysed for Bifidobacterium spp., Lactobacillus spp., Bacillus spp., Ruminococcus spp., Bacteroides spp., and Enterobacteriaceae, by quantitative real-time PCR analysis, using a Rotorgene 6500 real-time PCR machine (Corbett Research, Sydney, NSW, Australia), and quantification was determined using Rotorgene 6000 series software 1.7. A threshold cycle averaged from the duplicate samples was used for quantification analysis. The number of target DNA copies was calculated using a standard curve constructed with plasmid DNA cloned with the amplicons. Copy numbers of plasmid DNA were calculated according to its mass, considering the size of the plasmid with amplicon insert. The resulting values were expressed as log10 (genomic DNA copy number)/g digesta. The species-specific 16 rRNA primers utilized are described in detail by Kheravii et al. (2017).

Ileal and excreta sample XOS concentration

Ileum and excreta samples were dried at 60 °C for 2 to 3 h under a steady flow of nitrogen to remove methanol and denature enzymes and were then lyophilised to remove water and ground into a fine powder. The powdered sample (500 mg) was transferred to a 10-mL centrifuge tube. Lipids were extracted by adding 8 mL hexane, mixing for 15 min at room temperature, and then centrifuging at 1500 × g for 10 min. The hexane supernatant was discarded, and samples were then dried under nitrogen at 40 °C. Oligosaccharides were extracted from the residual solid by adding 5000 μL water containing 1 μg/mL D-Xylose-13C5 and 0.05% NaN3. The sample was resuspended by vortexing and then mixing at room temperature for at least 1 h on a rotary mixer. The suspension was centrifuged for 15 min at 2000× g, and the resulting supernatant was used for LC-MS sample preparation, as described in Section 2.3.

Results

The XOS fraction preparation

The purity and DP distribution of the low-DP, medium-DP and high-DP XOS products and the commercial mixture are presented in Table 1. These results demonstrate that the method was effective in concentrating individual XOS fractions, resulting in products that were richer in the specific target DP XOS compared to the commercial XOS mixture. Overall, XOS purity decreased with increasing DP.

Effects of DP on XOS efficacy (Trial 1)

Bird performance

Table 3 illustrates that the DP of the XOS supplement significantly affected cFCR at d 0 to 35 (P < 0.001); birds receiving the high-DP XOS exhibited higher average BWG and lower FI, resulting in a significantly lower cFCR, compared to birds fed the control diet or the medium-DP XOS supplement. The average mortality across all treatments was 8.9% with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.388). There was no evidence of a feeding-phase effect on mortality; mortality was 2.6%, 2.6%, and 3.7% in the starter, grower and finisher phases, respectively (P = 0.560). Similar elevated mortality was observed in Trial 2 and in other studies run during and after this period with birds from the same supplier, suggesting a flock-quality issue as the underlying cause.

Discussion

The XOS are known to have stimbiotic properties, promoting microbial adaptation to dietary fibre and improving bird performance (González-Ortiz et al., 2019), through enhanced expression of fibre-degrading enzymes and specific transporters, resulting in increased production of valuable SCFAs. The DP appears to be an important factor influencing XOS functionality as a stimbiotic, and understanding how it shapes microbial responses is essential for optimizing feed efficiency and bird health. This study hypothesized that low-dose supplementation of high-DP XOS in broiler diets would stimulate fibre-degrading bacteria and improve bird performance. Results from both trials supported this hypothesis, with high-DP XOS significantly improving bird performance, regardless of xylanase inclusion. Methods developed in this study successfully fractionated commercial XOS into distinct DP-based fractions; however, the process was inefficient, not commercially viable, and resulted in unavoidable co-concentration of other carbohydrate-based impurities also present in the XOS products used. The presence of these impurities may contribute to the biological activity attributed to commercial XOS preparations, either enhancing or modifying their stimbiotic effects. As a result, the responses observed in XOS studies may not always reflect the action of defined DP fractions alone. Clarifying the contribution of these impurities requires improved separation or purification methods, which would allow future research to more precisely link specific XOS structures to functional outcomes.

A limitation of the study is that gastrointestinal sampling was performed only at d 35, providing an endpoint assessment rather than a temporal view of microbial adaptation. Although the positive performance effects were clear, the specific mechanisms of action of high-DP XOS remain unresolved. When XOS stimbiotics were fed in the absence of xylanase, no significant differences were observed in microbial populations or endogenous enzyme production. A likely explanation is that sampling at d 35 failed to capture early microbial responses; by this time, the microbiota had likely converged across all treatments, as all birds were housed in the same large facility under identical conditions and were fed the same basal diet. This convergence likely masked treatment-related differences that may have occurred earlier. Evaluating microbial responses at earlier time points is necessary to better understand how high-DP XOS accelerates microbial adaptation. This absence of detectable microbial or enzymatic effects also indicates a necessity to re-evaluate experimental designs in mechanistic trials. Smaller cohorts, sampled more intensively during early life stages, could offer more precise insights into the temporal dynamics of microbial and physiological adaptation in individual birds. This approach could complement large-scale production trials, where performance data reflects treatment effects, even when biological mechanisms are obscured by inter-individual variability.

Moreover, the composition of the basal diet, which was based on a standard commercial formulation, may have limited the extent to which stimbiotic additives could exert their full potential effects. Commercial diets are typically rich in directly digestible starches and proportionally lower in fermentable fibre, reducing the birds’ reliance on microbial carbohydrate metabolism for energy, thus potentially limiting the benefits the XOS stimbiotics could induce. While the effects of different XOS types on bird performance were clearly significant, future studies may benefit from using less refined, fibre-rich diets to improve model sensitivity and better detect microbial shifts and fermentation outcomes associated with stimbiotic supplementation. These types of diets would be more cost-effective, meaning this approach would also capture potential commercial benefits such as reduced cost per kilogram of live weight gain.

This study focused on polysaccharide metabolism as a tool to gain mechanistic insight into the effects of XOS DP. In vivo microbiota data is often difficult to interpret and ethically challenging to collect; tracking how polysaccharides are broken down and utilized within the gastrointestinal tract offers a clearer and more practical approach. Furthermore, collecting measurements from the excreta offers a non-invasive method to monitor changes in XOS metabolism and fermentation products over time, providing a means to track microbial adaptation across the bird’s lifetime. Improved analytical methods to quantify fibre degradation and link it to physiological responses will help clarify how stimbiotics exert their effects.

The XOS measured in the ileum and excreta likely reflect intermediate hydrolysis products, rather than unutilized supplements. The XOS concentrations are dynamic, depending on both production and uptake rates, and thus cannot be fully interpreted from a single collection time point. The DP distribution of XOS differed from that of the supplemented products but was consistent across treatments, including the controls. This indicates that the measured XOS originated from microbial hydrolysis of endogenous xylans, rather than the added supplements. The similarity across groups further supports microbiota convergence and reinforces the idea that high-DP XOS must act early to accelerate adaptations.

Many gut microbes do not produce their own extracellular enzymes and are therefore reliant on primary fibre-degrading bacteria to release fermentable sugars from complex polysaccharides. When hydrolysis products are insufficient, either due to a lack of fibre-degrading microbes, limited fibre availability, or reduced enzymatic activity, these dependent microbes may shift to AA fermentation as an alternative energy source. This shift places them in direct competition with the host for valuable AAs. The resulting fermentation process produces branched chain VFA, which serve as markers of protein fermentation (Smith and Macfarlane, 1997). Significantly higher levels of branched chain VFA were observed in birds fed the control diet compared to those receiving high-DP XOS, suggesting that the latter reduced AA fermentation. This supports the role of high-DP XOS in promoting more favourable carbohydrate-based fermentation and again highlights the importance of early microbial establishment through stimbiotic application.

A second hypothesis proposed in this study was that low-DP XOS may act as prebiotics rather than stimbiotics, supporting microbial populations by serving as a readily fermentable energy source without inducing enzyme production. However, testing this requires higher doses than those used in this study, at which point distinguishing prebiotic from stimbiotic effects becomes difficult. As such, this hypothesis remains unresolved.

The second trial investigated how the inclusion of xylanase modified the effect of supplemented XOS. The xylanase used is an endo-xylanase with negligible exo–xylanase activity, meaning it only cleaves internal β-(1 →4) linkages on unbranched xylan segments of DP 4 and above. Consequently, its action on dietary arabinoxylan increases the availability of low-to medium-DP oligosaccharides such as X2 and X3, which are readily utilized by opportunistic bacteria that do not produce fibre-degrading enzymes and lack ABC transporters. These bacteria normally rely on scavenging extracellular hydrolysis products, and thus often proliferate in the presence of supplemented xylanase. Xylanase may also hydrolyse supplemented high-DP XOS, reducing or altering their intended function. In this study, the performance data showed that the high-DP XOS retained their positive effect, outperforming the low-DP XOS treatment. This suggests that in this study the high-DP XOS were not substantially hydrolyzed in vivo and remained effective as stimbiotics. Several factors likely contributed to this, including the low inclusion rate of supplemented XOS, the high relative abundance of dietary xylan-based polysaccharides as the primary xylanase substrate, and fast digesta transit times. The added xylanase would have been far more likely to act on the more abundant dietary xylan-based polysaccharides than on the comparatively small amount of supplemented XOS. Notably, digesta transit is faster in younger birds, which may have further limited enzymatic access and degradation of supplemented XOS. This supports the broader hypothesis that stimbiotics are more effective when administered early in life, during the period of rapid microbial establishment and gut development, and highlights a potential advantage of using small doses of high-DP XOS in enzyme-supplemented diets.

Interestingly, birds fed the combined xylanase and low-DP XOS diet exhibited the poorest performance. A plausible explanation is that the low-DP XOS acted as signaling molecules that stimulated adaptations in non-fibre-degrading, cross-feeding bacteria, enabling them to optimize their utilization of low-DP XOS. Members of the Enterobacteriaceae are well characterized as lacking extracellular fibre-degrading enzymes and therefore rely on small, soluble oligosaccharides released by other microbes or by xylanase activity (Le Bouguénec and Schouler, 2011). These bacteria could consequently capitalize on the additional X2 and X3 generated when xylanase hydrolyses dietary arabinoxylan. Supporting this interpretation, Enterobacteriaceae levels were elevated in birds receiving low-DP XOS, and the absence of increased branched-chain VFA indicates that these microbes were adequately fueled by carbohydrate substrates rather than shifting to amino-acid fermentation. Such stimulation of opportunistic, cross-feeding taxa provides a coherent explanation for the reduced performance observed when xylanase was combined with low-DP XOS.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that low-dose supplementation with high-DP XOS can significantly improve broiler performance, both in the presence and absence of xylanase. These findings suggest that larger DP XOS molecules retain functional stimbiotic properties under commercial feeding conditions and that the degree of polymerisation is a key determinant of XOS efficacy. Notably, the positive effects of high-DP XOS contrast with earlier reports favouring small XOS molecules, suggesting the need to re-evaluate assumptions about XOS functionality. Further research using purified, structurally defined XOS fractions is required to elucidate the mechanisms involved and optimize their application in poultry nutrition.
