RYR2 Variants in Catecholaminergic Polymorphic Ventricular Tachycardia Patients: Insights From Protein Structure and Clinical Data
Authors: Alexander Chang, Halil Beqaj, Leah Sittenfeld, Marco C. Miotto, Haikel Dridi, Gloria Willson, Carolyn Martinez Jorge, Jaan Altosaar Li, Steven Reiken, Yang Liu, Zonglin Dai, Carl Tchagou, Sana Elsayed, Steven O. Marx, Andrew R. Marks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) is a rare inherited arrhythmia, with pathogenic variants in the RYR2 gene responsible for 60% of clinically well-defined CPVT cases. Diagnosis of CPVT often occurs after a major cardiac event, posing a severe threat to the patient’s life. A data set of patients with CPVT would improve the diagnosis and treatment of patients with CPVT.METHODS:This review cataloged clinical data on patients with RYR2-related CPVT variants from articles published up to October 2020 from PubMed, Scopus, and Embase. Variants were mapped to the structural domains of RYR2. Differences in the age of onset based on variant location and incidence of CPVT symptoms, and differences in treatment strategies were analyzed.RESULTS:In 221 publications analyzed, 964 patients with CPVT (351 male, 463 female) were identified with 263 RYR2 protein-coding variants and a median age of onset of CPVT of 11 years (interquartile range, 7–14 years). A web app was developed to allow users to query the database and is available at https://markslab-cpvtdb.org. The proportion of patients requiring treatments in addition to β-blockers varied between variants. The age of onset of CPVT differed significantly between RYR2 variants located in different exons, domains, and subdomains. Patients with variants in the core solenoid at the domain level, and the core solenoid (exEF-hand) and channel pore at the subdomain level, tended to have a lower age of onset compared with other regions.CONCLUSIONS:This study compiled a comprehensive data set of CPVT-associated RYR2 variants and their clinical phenotypes. The age of onset in certain domains (core solenoid) and subdomains (core solenoid[exEF-hand], channel pore) tended to be lower compared with other regions. Variability in patient phenotypes, such as age of onset and treatment efficacy, along with structural information on variants, suggests that patients may benefit from personalized interventions based on their variant.

Catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) is a malignant arrhythmia associated with stress-, exercise-induced syncope, and sudden cardiac death (SCD),1 in the absence of cardiac structural abnormalities.2 CPVT is recognized as a significant cause of SCD in children and young adults.3,4 Patients with CPVT have mortality rates of 30% to 50% by the age of 40 years.5 Thus, early recognition, risk stratification, and intervention are crucial to achieving positive outcomes.

CPVT is caused by pathogenic variants in the cardiac type 2 ryanodine receptor gene, RYR2, or the sarcoplasmic reticulum protein calsequestrin 2 gene, CASQ2.6–8 RYR2 variants are responsible for the autosomal dominant form of CPVT and account for ≈60% of clinically well-defined CPVT.9

RYR2 encodes ryanodine receptor 2 (RYR2), a homotetrameric intracellular Ca2+ release channel in cardiac muscle. It mediates the release of Ca2+ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and plays a key role in excitation-contraction coupling.10 During increased stimulation of β-adrenergic receptors of the heart (due to exercise or stress), RYR2 is phosphorylated by protein kinase A at Ser2808.11 This phosphorylation depletes the stabilizing subunit calstabin2,12 increasing both the sensitivity of RYR2 to Ca2+-dependent activation and the open probability of the channel, and places the entire channel structure into a primed state.13 The primed state leads to more readily activated channels at low, normally nonactivating cytosolic Ca2+ levels and ultimately pathological cardiac muscle dysfunction.12–14

β-blockers are the cornerstone therapy in the management of patients diagnosed with CPVT.15 However, there is significant variability in treatment efficacy, with near-fatal events in patients on β-blockers remaining unacceptably high.16 Flecainide, a sodium channel blocker, may be recommended in patients with recurring arrhythmias using β-blocker therapy.15 Rycals, a novel small-molecule class of drugs developed in our laboratory that restore calstabin2 binding, have been shown to stabilize the RYR2 channel and prevent sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ leak.17 With increasing evidence suggesting that β-blocker efficacy may be variant-specific,18,19 it becomes imperative that treatment regimens that are variant-specific are developed.

Although historically 4 CPVT variant hotspots have been identified (I exons 3–15, II 44–50, III 83–90, and IV 93–105),20 advances in sequencing technology have allowed for cost-effective complete screening of RYR2’s 105 coding exons.21 As a result, the Heart Rhythm Society and European Heart Rhythm Association expert consensus statement recommends a comprehensive genetic test for any patient with suspected CPVT, along with cascade screening in family members following identification of the CPVT-causative variant.22 However, as genetic screening has become more readily available, it has been discovered that ≈3% of ostensibly healthy individuals who are screened for RYR2 variants carry a rare protein-altering variant of RYR2,9 creating genetic noise that obfuscates interpretation of positive test results.20 These ambiguous variants, or variants of unknown significance, introduce a genetic purgatory for the physician: a clinical dilemma in managing a family whose variant is not well-studied.23 This phenomenon is exacerbated when considering treatment recommendations such as implantable cardiac defibrillator (ICD) placement in the context of potentially fatal arrhythmias.23 Thus, there is an essential need for research focused on distinguishing pathogenic CPVT-causing variants from rare nonpathogenic variants.

The present study was designed to create an extensive database of RYR2 variants associated with CPVT that will: (1) validate the current understanding of CPVT through analysis of the largest compilation of variant data based on comprehensive mining of the literature; (2) serve as a centralized, easily accessible data resource for physicians and patients to refer to for information on specific published RYR2 variants, including information about inheritance patterns, clinical manifestations, and treatment efficacies; and (3) form the foundation for future development of a machine learning-based pathogenicity predictor for variants of unknown significance.

Methods

Data Sharing

The data supporting the findings of this study are documented in the article and Supplemental Material. Institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required because the study exclusively used patient-level data extracted from publicly available literature. The PDB used to model the variants is accessible at https://doi.org/10.2210/pdb7ua5/pdb. All code related to the analysis conducted for this study have been made publicly available on GitHub at https://github.com/alexdaiii/cpvt_database_analysis. A web app developed to allow users to query the database is available at https://markslab-cpvtdb.org. This tool allows users to filter variants or patients based on presented conditions, age of onset, or variant location, and visualize variants on the RYR2 structure using the Mol* Viewer24 (Figure S1).

Study Selection and Data Extraction

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses reporting guidelines.25 Publication databases, including PubMed, Embase, and Scopus, were searched for articles published through October 2020. Keywords to narrow search results were used, including, but not limited to: catechol* polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, channelopathic cardiac diseases, cardiac arrhythmias, SCD, syncope, and RyR2. Only English-language studies reporting specific RYR2 variants associated with arrhythmias were included.

Data collected included, but were not limited to, demographic information, age of CPVT symptom onset, cardiac history, treatment, and efficacy of treatment. Two investigators working independently assessed the quality of each article and extracted the data. Reasons for exclusion were recorded and cross-checked for agreement. Disagreements in exclusion reasoning or discrepancies in data extraction were resolved by discussion and mutual consensus.

Variants using transcript coordinates and variants using protein coordinates were validated against the NM_001035.3 and NP_001026.2 reference sequences, respectively.

Structural Mapping

The human RYR2 cryogenic electron microscopy model (PDB: 7UA5) published by Miotto et al13 was used as the base structure to model the variants. Structural figures were created using ChimeraX,26 release 1.8, according to software instructions.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.4.1, and Python, version 3.12. The Mann-Whitney U test, 2-sided, was used to test the differences between the age of onset between groups. The test was only run when each group had at least 5 patients to ensure sufficient statistical power.

The association between a variant and a treatment being used or not was tested using pairwise Fisher exact tests on all possible combinations of the selected variants, with false discovery rate correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. The analyses of flecainide usage were performed only on patients identified from 2013 and onwards (when an expert consensus statement endorsed the use of flecainide15).

Results

RYR2 Variants Database and Clinical Characteristics of Patients With CPVT

Our search across the 3 platforms yielded 4510 articles, 1940 of which were excluded as duplicates. The remaining 2570 articles were screened by title and abstract, and 1688 articles—whose abstracts mentioned non-RYR2 variants or non-CPVT-related cases—were excluded. Eight hundred eighty-two full texts were reviewed, and 661 full-text articles—with only non-RYR2 CPVT variants, only non-CPVT-related cases, or without individual clinical data—were excluded. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarized in Table S1. The literature review workflow is shown in Figure 1. The 221 publications were ultimately utilized for data extraction and the risk of bias analyses are included as Data Set 1.

Data collected for each patient included specific RYR2 variant(s), inheritance pattern, family history, cardiac symptomatology, treatment plans, and efficacy (Data Set 2).

Data from 1342 patients were extracted from the 221 publications (644 females, 527 males, and 171 with no sex reported). Among all 1342 patients, 350 had their variant inheritance reported: 251 inherited their RYR2 variant, and 99 had a de novo variant. In addition, among all patients, 636 had their variant zygosity reported: 630 patients carried a heterozygous variant, and 6 patients carried a homozygous variant. Among all patients, there were 339 unique RYR2 variants at the transcript level, and 369 unique variants at the RYR2 protein level. Some variants were only reported at the protein level and some transcript-level variants mapped to the same protein change. There were 1310 patients with protein-altering variants, with 1272 patients carrying missense variants (Table S2).

Among all patients, 964 patients had a diagnosis of CPVT or a CPVT-like phenotype, 132 were asymptomatic carriers of an RYR2 variant, and 246 had non-CPVT diagnoses—such as long-QT syndrome (Figure 2A).

The subsequent analyses are for 964 cases with a primary diagnosis of CPVT or a CPVT-like phenotype (463 females, 351 males, and 150 with no sex reported). Of the 273 patients with CPVT and their variant inheritance reported, 186 inherited their RYR2 variant while 87 had a de novo mutation. Of the 492 patients with CPVT and their family history of SCD reported, 349 patients had a positive family history of SCD while 144 did not have a family history of SCD. In the 491 patients with CPVT examined for heart structure abnormalities, 447 had structurally normal hearts based on echocardiography. Among the 463 patients with CPVT examined for ECG abnormalities at rest, 76 patients had baseline ECG abnormalities, including bradycardia. Of the 321 patients with CPVT examined for polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (VT) during exercise/stress conditions, 315 experienced polymorphic VT, highlighting the importance of stress in patients with CPVT. Likewise, in 270 patients with CPVT and documented experience of syncope, 261 experienced it during exercise/stress conditions. In addition, in 113 patients with CPVT and documented sudden cardiac arrest, 97 experienced it during exercise/stress conditions (Table S3).

In 409 patients with CPVT, the age of symptom onset was reported. The most common onset symptoms were syncope (115 patients), cardiac arrest (28 patients), palpitations (7 patients), and sudden death (6 patients). The distribution of the age of onset of CPVT was right-skewed and did not follow a normal distribution. The median age of onset of these patients was 11 years (interquartile range [IQR], 7–14 years). The age of onset ranged from 0 to 66 years (Figure 2B). Among the patients with CPVT, 254 had both polymorphic VT status and age of onset reported. The age of onset in patients with polymorphic VT (median, 10 years; IQR, 6.6–13 years; n=224) was significantly different (P<0.001) compared with patients without polymorphic VT (median, 13.5 years; IQR, 10–19.8 years; n=30; Figure 2C). In the patients without polymorphic VT, patients still reported experiencing syncope. Among the patients with CPVT, 282 had both syncope status and age of onset reported. The age of onset in patients with syncope (median, 10 years; IQR, 7–14 years; n=234) is significantly different (P<0.001) compared with patients without syncope (median, 14.5 years; IQR, 11–32 years; n=48; Figure 2D).

CPVT RYR2 Variants Distribution

Among the 964 patients with CPVT in our database, there were 236 unique RYR2 variants at the transcript level and 263 unique variants at the RYR2 protein level. In the 937 patients with CPVT and protein-altering variants, 914 had missense variants, 18 had deletions, 4 insertions, and 1 had a frameshift.

The number of patients with CPVT reported per variant varied between 1 and 99 patients. The 3 most frequently reported protein-altering RYR2 variants associated with CPVT patients were the p.G357S (99 patients), p.R420Q (52 patients), p.R420W (27 patients) variants (Figure 3A; Data Set 2). A total of 76.43% of protein-altering variants in our database were reported in only 3 or fewer patients. It should be noted that the frequency of cases for the p.G357S variant was influenced by a large study that included genetic data from multiple generations of a single family,27 inflating its prevalence in our database beyond that of the general CPVT population.

At the transcript level, CPVT-associated variants were also unequally distributed among exons. Variants in the historical CPVT hotspots (I exons 3–15, II 44–50, III 83–90, and IV 93–105)20 accounted for 77.13% of all substitution variants. The 3 most commonly affected exons were exons 90 (43 variants), 47 (12 variants), 8 (10 variants; Figure 3B).

Age of Onset Variability Across Exons

We investigated whether the location of a variant in the RYR2 gene was correlated with differences in the age of onset. We used these data to determine whether a variant in one region was associated with a more severe phenotype, proxied by age of onset.

We compared the age of onset in the patients with CPVT and a single substitution variant affecting a single exon (with more than 5 patients) to the age of onset in patients with variants in other exons. The median age of onset in patients with variants in exon 102 was the lowest (median, 4 years; IQR, 4–5.5 years; n=6); the distribution differed significantly compared with that of patients with variants in other exons (n=352; P=0.003). Patients with variants in exons 45 (median, 4.5 years; IQR, 3–6 years; n=6) and 88 (median, 6 years; IQR, 6–8.5 years; n=15) also tended to have a lower age of onset and were significantly different (P=0.001 for exon 45, P=0.013 for exon 88) compared with other exons. In addition, the median age of onset in patients with variants in exon 93 was the highest (median, 15.5 years; IQR, 13.5–30 years; n=8); the distribution differed significantly compared with that of patients with variants in other exons (n=350; P=0.005; Table S4).

Structural Mapping and Effects on RYR2 Domains and Function

We analyzed the historical CPVT variant hotspots in the context of the atomic model: I (amino acids 44–466, blue); II (2246–2534, green); III (3778–4201, purple); and IV (4497–4959, cyan; Figure 4A and 4B). We noticed that these hotspots are clustered in the core of the RYR2 structure, suggesting that the outer region of the cytoplasmic shell (gray) has less influence on CPVT. It has been proposed that CPVT variants affect the interaction and dynamics between RYR2 domains. To better understand the effects of variants on the structure and microdomain environments of the channel, we mapped the p.G357S, p.R420Q/p.R420W, and p.S2246L variants into the previously published high-resolution structure of wild-type human RYR2 (Figure 4C through 4I).

The variant p.G357S probably disturbs the local β-turn secondary structure, stabilized by glycine residues, affecting the interaction within the N-terminal domain B domain and the interaction between the N-terminal domain B and the core solenoid (CSol) domains (Figure 4C and 4D). The variants p.R420Q and p.R420W disrupt the interaction network centered on R420, affecting the interaction between the N-terminal solenoid and N-terminal domain B domains (Figure 4E through 4G). The different size, polarity, and hydrophobicity of the substitute residues (Q420 and W420) have dissimilar effects on the flexibility of the N-terminal domain B domain, causing different degrees of symptoms. Patients with the p.R420Q had a significantly lower median age of onset (median, 10 years; IQR, 9–14.8 years; n=26) compared with the p.R420W (median, 18.5 years; n=8) variants (P=0.012). This agrees with the observation in the RYR2 cryo-EM structures of such variants, where the conformational changes induced by p.R420Q were larger than those by p.R420W.28 Finally, the variant p.S2246L, which also has an early age of onset, substitutes a small polar residue (serine) by a large hydrophobic residue (leucine). This drastic change introduces steric impediments that most likely affect the intradomain stability, increasing the domain flexibility (Figure 4H and 4I). Interestingly, the 3 sites analyzed seem to operate via different local structural mechanisms but have similar global consequences presenting as loss of inter-domain interactions and increased flexibility. Indeed, we and others have previously reported that single channel and Ca2+ imaging studies of p.R420Q/p.R420W, and p.S2246L mutant RYR2 channels have shown that these variants result in leaky channels,14,29–31 and knock-in mice with the p.S2246L, p.R420Q, and p.R420W variant have a CPVT phenotype.32,33

The RYR2 structure can be subdivided into protein domains and subdomains (Figure 5A and 5B, and Data Set 3). The median age of onset in patients with variants in the CSol was the lowest among domains with more than 5 patients (median, 8 years; IQR, 6–14 years; n=74). The age of onset in patients with variants in the CSol differed significantly (P=0.001) from that of patients with variants in other domains (n=320; Figure 5C and Table S5). The CSol domain is a signal transduction domain, connecting the regulatory cytoplasmic domains with the transmembrane domain. Variants in these domains may have more impact on channel regulation than variants in the cytoplasmic domains, which could explain the lower age of onset.

The median age of onset in patients with variants in the N-terminal domain (median, 12 years; IQR, 9–16 years; n=97) was higher compared with the median for all patients with CPVT (11 years). The age of onset in patients with variants in the N-terminal domain differed significantly (P=0.006) from that of patients with variants in other domains (n=297). Since the N-terminal domain domain contains the hotspot furthest away from the pore region (a functional domain of RYR2), the difference in the age of onset might be due to less influence in the allosteric regulation when compared with the other hotspots.

When grouped by the subdomains of RYR2, the median age of onset in patients with variants in the CSol(exEF-hand) was the lowest among subdomains with >5 patients (median, 8 years; IQR, 6–13 years; n=69). The age of onset in patients with variants in the CSol(exEF-hand) differed significantly (P<0.001) from that of patients with variants in other subdomains (n=325; Figure 5D and Table S5). Interestingly, CPVT linked to mutations in the EF-hand subdomain showed the highest median age of onset (median, 28; IQR, 10–31 years; n=5); but the distribution was not significantly different (P=0.187) from that of patients with variants in other subdomains (n=389). This could be due to this subdomain playing only a minor role in signal transduction and opening of the channel but having a major role in the termination of Ca2+ release and inactivation of the channel.34,35 Because the CSol domain contains both the core solenoid exEF-hand subdomain and the EF-hand subdomain, future studies involving the CSol domain should consider that such subdomains have distinct structural functions and impact on disease pathogenicity.

The median age of onset in patients with variants in the channel pore subdomain was lower (median, 8 years; IQR, 6–13 years; n=69) than the overall median for all patients with CPVT (11 years). The age of onset in patients with variants in the channel pore differed significantly (P=0.019) from that of patients with variants in other subdomains (n=367). Variants in the channel pore subdomain show a lower age of onset, which may be due to the intrinsic role of the pore region in the function of RYR2.

Therapeutics

The treatment for CPVT is multifaceted and often requires a poly pharmaceutical approach to effectively manage the arrhythmias. Of 601 patients with CPVT and detailed treatment records, 432 (71.88%) were treated with β-blockers (Figure 6A). Of these patients on β-blockers, 129 were treated with additional pharmacological interventions such as flecainide or verapamil. Furthermore, 129 of the cases that received treatment with β-blockers underwent an invasive therapeutic strategy such as ICD placement, left cardiac sympathetic denervation, or catheter ablation (Figure 6B and 6C).

We compared therapeutic strategies between patients with common variants taking β-blockers (p.G357S, p.G2337V, p.R420Q, p.R420W, p.C2277R, p.S2246L). We looked at combination flecainide and β-blocker usage in patients reported in 2013 and after. Patients with the p.G357S and p.G2337V variants were primarily treated with only β-blockers, while patients with the p.R420Q, p.R420W, p.C2277R, and p.S2246L variants were treated with combination therapy in greater proportions. For example, those with the p.S2246L variant frequently received flecainide (3/4 patients reported in 2013 and after) and ICD placement in addition to β-blockers (7/9 patients). Even at the same sequence location, a significantly larger proportion (P=0.008) of those with the p.R420W (7/9 patients) variant were treated with combination flecainide and β-blocker therapy compared with p.R420Q (1/15 patients; Figure 6D; Table S6). However, there was no significant difference in the proportion of patients receiving ICD intervention in addition to β-blockers between these 2 variants. (Figure 6E; Table S7). Finally, patients with the p.G2337V variant (10/11) were significantly more likely to report β-blocker effectiveness compared with patients with the p.R420W (5/17; P=0.031) and p.C2277R (2/8; P=0.047) variants (Figure 6F; Table S8).

Discussion

In this study, we compiled the largest and most comprehensive database of CPVT-associated RYR2 variants, to our knowledge. Available genetic and clinical information for each case were included in the database, to facilitate the characterization of variant phenotypes and elucidate trends in the data that could help guide future diagnosis and treatment of patients with CPVT.

Using these data, we were able to characterize the prevalence of certain hallmark symptoms of CPVT. Most patients inherited their variant, and most had a positive family history of SCD. However, we found that a greater proportion of patients with de novo pathogenic variants experienced sudden cardiac arrest compared with those with inherited variants. For this reason, it is important to consider genetic testing in young patients with suspected CPVT despite a lack of family history, as identifying and addressing a pathogenic variant could prevent future cardiac events. The difference in the incidence of sudden cardiac arrest between de novo and inherited variant cases may be partially attributable, however, to medical management of family members of index cases, who inherited a variant, whereas cases with de novo variants are often the first in the family to present with cardiac arrest.

The variant distribution in our database aligns well with the previously defined 4 variant hotspots discussed earlier, as well as Kapplinger et al’s refined hotspot regions (exons 3, 8, 14, 43, 47–49, 81, 83, 88–90, 93, 95, 97–101, 103, 105).20 In addition to these exons, our database showed many unique variants in exons 37 and 42; thus, we posit that these exons should be considered a part of hotspot II.

We collected data on variant locations and analyzed the phenotypic variability based on the domain, subdomain, and exon distribution. The age of onset of CPVT differed significantly at all 3 structural levels. Relatives of an index case found to have a variant in exon 45, which has a low age of onset, may require earlier intervention, more invasive management, or increased monitoring. Such patterns may have implications for genetic screening results and potential therapies. However, it should be noted that modifications at the same amino acid site still differ in their age of onset, such as the p.R420Q (median, 10 years; n=26) and p.R420W (median, 18.5 years; n=8) variants. The location of a variant should not be taken as the sole contributor to the variability in the age of onset.

The use of age of onset as a clinical proxy for relative pathogenicity is further corroborated by the 3 variants with an early median age onset in our database (p.N4178S, p.A4091T, and p.G3946S) being represented in Kapplinger et al’s list of Near-Definite Pathogenic Variants.20 Variants that have been functionally characterized showing evidence for leaky channels including p.R2474S,36,37 p.N2386I,37 and p.L433P37 also are located on exons frequently associated with an early age of onset. Finally, given that onset symptoms are usually syncope, or sudden cardiac arrest, the clinical utility of this measure may be significant to clinicians.

The aggregation of a large quantity of data for many different RYR2 variants makes the heterogeneity in phenotype severity between variants apparent. For example, between 2 unrelated families with the p.M3978I variant, every case was symptomatic and met clinical criteria for CPVT. In contrast, in one family carrying p.G155R, nearly all individuals were asymptomatic, and the diagnosis in this family was based on a 16-year-old boy who experienced sudden death while playing sports.38

Variants reported more frequently in the literature have the greatest heterogeneity within the same variant. For example, for p.R420Q, some cases experienced cardiac arrest, some were asymptomatic, and a few exhibited bradycardias. This increased variability is likely partially due to increased sample size compared with that of a rarer variant such as p.G155R. Rarer variants are likely to be more fatal and thus do not get passed down across generations. More detailed family trees are required to calculate the penetrance of specific variants. However, a machine learning model for accurately predicting pathogenicity for variants of unknown significance in large protein complexes would provide even greater utility.

Almost a third of patients in our review did not respond adequately to β-blockers, requiring additional antiarrhythmic medications and invasive therapies such as ICD placement or left cardiac sympathetic denervation. The most common medication given to these patients was flecainide. Liu et al39 found that while flecainide did indeed prevent arrhythmias in a CPVT RyR2 mutant mouse model, it had no effect on intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis. These findings suggest that the antiarrhythmic role of flecainide in CPVT is likely primarily related to its classic mechanism of inhibiting sodium channels, preventing propagation of arrhythmias that may be triggered by aberrant calcium handling rather than inhibition of RYR2 itself. However, the role of flecainide on RYR2 is under debate. While Kryshtal et al40 demonstrated a direct inhibition of RYR2 by flecainide, Bannister et al41 also found that flecainide did not directly affect RYR2 channel activity, suggesting its effects were mainly through sodium channel blockade.

These findings underscore how understanding RYR2 and its interactions with surrounding proteins could introduce novel and improved treatment options. In 2019, Park et al42 highlighted calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II activation as a key event in arrhythmia formation in patients with CPVT, identifying a target that could be inhibited to potentially treat RYR2-associated CPVT. As mentioned previously, Rycal mechanism of stabilizing the RYR2 closed state suggests that these drugs would be a promising option to address the channel dysfunction in patients with CPVT and thus could result in measurable clinical benefit.17

In conclusion, this project has produced a comprehensive compilation of CPVT-associated RYR2 variants and their associated clinical phenotypes. This database serves as a valuable clinical tool to inform physicians, educate patients, and facilitate research regarding CPVT and its treatment. We provided a browser-based user interface of the database for clinicians and researchers to obtain information on CPVT-related variants, patient cases, and publications. This can facilitate clinicians in gathering data on rare variants to help guide management based on patient presentation, genetic findings, and treatment options. Furthermore, the data analyzed provided insights into the frequency of the most salient clinical features of CPVT and the efficacy of currently available treatment options. The structural analyses of variants illustrate the structure-function relationships of RYR2. These findings help narrow the gaps in understanding the pathogenicity of less robustly studied CPVT variants and provide a potential building block for personalized diagnosis and treatment for patients with CPVT.

Limitations

There are several limitations in this study that should be noted. Due to the statistical model selected to evaluate association with the age of onset, this study only analyzed missense variants that affect 1 exon, domain, or subdomain. Much of the data set were missense variants; however, there were 2.5% of cases in this data set with nonmissense variants. We also evaluated differences in therapeutic use and efficacy among specific variants; however, these results should be interpreted with caution due to the limitation of small sample sizes. In addition, case reports, case series, and conference abstracts were used to construct the database—which tend to report novel variants or unique cases. Last, there were inconsistencies in the patient features reported by each article. Nevertheless, the present study is an important contribution to the understanding of the clinical and molecular basis of CPVT and suggests the need for early diagnosis and individualized approaches to CPVT management.
