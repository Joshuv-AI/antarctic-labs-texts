Benchmarking the prediction of responding cells to perturbations affecting both gene expression and cellular abundance using scRNA sequencing
Authors: Jae-Won Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Defining how cells respond to perturbations is crucial for understanding their biology. While previous works were designed to classify responding cells, methods for quantifying the response of each cell are not well developed. In addition, perturbations affecting only gene expression were considered for modeling. However, perturbation not only alters the gene expression of cells but also alters cellular abundance. But to date, no validation data is available to assess such perturbations. To address this issue, we utilized the clonal expansion of T or B cells as a new validation data type that can accompany both gene expression and alterations in cellular abundance after perturbation. Subsequently, we developed BRiCE, a new benchmark pipeline for assessing RC and Res, where RC refers to how well it can distinguish the responding cells against non-responding cells, while Res refers to how well it can quantify the responsiveness, and compares its performance with that of preexisting methods. However, none of the existing methods demonstrated predictive power. These results indicated that the current approach to understanding perturbation solely by gene expression is insufficient, and cellular abundance must be considered in perturbation modeling.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54526-9.

Introduction

Perturbation in biology is crucial for understanding biological mechanisms1–5. Perturbation can occur from diseases such as viral and bacterial infections, metabolic disorders, and cancers6–10. It is also possible to create a perturbation, such as gene knockout (KO) in mice or drug, chemical, and cytokine treatments, which can guide researchers in understanding the underlying molecular mechanism and clinical role5,11–16.

Previously, perturbed samples, such as treated samples, were considered to indicate that all cells responded to the perturbation. Owing to innovations in single-cell technology, it is now possible to detect cellular heterogeneity in a sample17,18. The responses of cells to perturbations are also different2–5,19,20. Indeed, the different responses of cells are reflected in the data. For example, in a virus-infected model, not all viruses infect the cells20–22. Cytokine treatments can be distinguished based on the phosphorylation of receptors23. During drug treatments such as cancer therapy, not all cancer cells are killed by the anti-cancer treatment24,25. In a gene KO approach, not all cells are KO, and it is possible to distinguish between different KOs using pooled CRISPR screening, such as Perturb-seq16,26. In addition, a partial overlap of cells occurs between the perturbed and unperturbed samples using standard scRNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) processing (Fig. 1A)2–5,19–22. Hence, if we assume that all cells in the perturbed sample respond, the true signal would be diluted owing to cell population mixing. Therefore, distinguishing between responding and non-responding cells in perturbed samples is important. Typically, new experiments are conducted using an experimental design with unreported perturbation, comparing control (or unperturbed) samples. Hence, many methods were developed to understand perturbation from such an experimental design. Initial approaches were used to predict the gene expression of the perturbed cells from the control sample using their models at the single-cell level4,27–31. But most of the methods were designed in a supervised learning scheme, which limits the usage of the newly generated data because it is likely to aim for new perturbations. Moreover, it was not sufficient since the model cannot determine which expression profile is associated with which attributes (ex: infected cell vs uninfected cell, gene KO cell vs non-KO cell, drug-affected cell vs nonresponding cell, etc.). Therefore, a more advanced method is needed to disentangle the data structures to discriminate the responding cells from the sample. To address these issues, scGen was first proposed to understand the perturbation in a self-supervised learning scheme using a variational autoencoder (VAE)-based approach in scRNA-seq data, which is mainly focused on predicting the gene expression of perturbed cells. After that, by applying a more complex design for modeling, many methods such as CPA, contrastiveVI, biolord, scDisInFact, and scCausalVI were able to disentangle the data into the shared space, which contains the intrinsic factor of a cell, cell types, or species, and perturbed spaces, which explain the perturbation2,3,5,19,20,32 (Fig. 1A). CINEMA-OT was also developed by using an optimal transport algorithm between perturbed and unperturbed samples for disentanglement20. As a result, the perturbed space was disentangled into responding and non-responding cells in terms of gene expression. These methods are often validated based on how well they can reconstruct the perturbed sample. In addition, they attempted to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) related to the perturbed sample2–5,19,20,32.

However, the prediction of the simulated perturbed data did not provide direct evidence to validate the prediction of the responding cells. Most validations with reconstructed perturbed data were not made at the single-cell level but only at the mean expression level from cell aggregates, omitting the usage of single-cell data. The choice of DEGs between the perturbed and unperturbed samples was arbitrary because it is not guaranteed that all DEGs are related to responsiveness. However, some gold standard approaches exist for validation, such as discriminating against infected cells or those not infected by viruses or bacteria using their transcripts from each cell, receptor phosphorylation, or barcode detection of guide RNA from CRISPR screening3,16,20–23,26. Although the IC50 data type is another option, it could not reflect the heterogeneity of the cell population33,34. However, validation by labeling data has some limitations in distinguishing a quantity of responses to perturbations across cells. Although many methods have attempted to measure responsiveness by the distance or difference in gene expression space (or latent space), it cannot be a gold standard because it is arbitrary to define the baseline.

The biggest pitfall of the previous approach was that the design of all validation methods was based solely on gene expression. However, the response of the cell does not occur solely in gene expression but also in its cellular abundance. Cells can also proliferate or die from certain perturbations24,25,35,36. Indeed, disease or perturbation samples exhibit large alterations in cellular abundance37–39. Subsequently, many cellular abundance analyses have been conducted in single cells.

However, to date, no validation data is available to assess such perturbations. In addition, no gold standard exists for reflecting the quantity of response, as it is unknown which genes act solely in responsiveness.

To address this issue, we utilized the clonal expansion of T or B cells as a new validation data type that can accompany both gene expression and alterations in cellular abundance after perturbation40–43. Subsequently, we developed “Benchmarking RespondIng cells by Clonal Expansion” (BRiCE), a new benchmark pipeline for assessing “Response Classification” (RC) and “Response effect size” (Res), where RC refers to how well it can distinguish the responding cells against non-responding cells, while Res refers to how well it can quantify the responsiveness, and compares its performance with that of preexisting methods.

Results

Prediction of responding cells by a clonal expansion model of T or B cells

T or B lymphocytes are activated upon antigen exposure40–43. Subsequently, the T cell receptor (TCR) or B cell receptor (BCR) recognizes the epitope loaded onto the antigen-presenting molecules (pMHC), prompting T or B cells to change not only their gene expression profile but also proliferate with the same TCR or BCR40–43. This process is called “clonal expansion,” and maintains the response to the same epitope by keeping the same TCR or BCR (Fig. 1B). Hence, if we were able to observe clonally expanded T or B cells, it would be easy to assess RC. Moreover, if T or B cells have a stronger activation or response, they would proliferate more40–47. This could be a guide for ordering Res based on the quantity of clonal expansion for each clonotype.

Performance measurement of RC using BRiCE

Inspired by the fact that the clonal expansion of T or B cells reflects responding cells, performance can be measured by identifying which cells are clonally expanded. However, most methods cannot directly assign which cell is responding or clonally expanded because not all methods were designed to distinguish responding cells from non-responding cells2–5,19,20,32. Users can still distinguish responding from non-responding cells by clustering using the perturbed space provided. For general usage, we leveraged this perturbed space to develop a performance measurement method. Because it is possible to obtain a graph for cellular embedding by cellular similarity from the perturbed space, either under the expression level or a cellular neighbor graph, we developed an RC method by measuring the entropy of the cellular graph by k-nearest neighbor (kNN), adopted from BatchBench, which was developed to evaluate the batch effect in scRNA-seq48. Here, entropy was measured based on responsiveness or clonal expansion instead of each batch (Fig. 1C). Hence, a lower entropy indicates better separation between responding and non-responding cells.

Performance measurement of Res using BRiCE

The Res can be measured as the extent to which a given cell has been clonally expanded. Most of these methods do not provide the Res for each cell. Hence, rather than measuring the Res for each cell, we developed a method to determine whether each method could align the cells alongside the quantity of clonal expansion or Res. Adapted from a previous approach, which leveraged the cellular graph, the performance of Res was measured using Spearman correlation coefficients (SCC) between the clonality of a given cell and mean clonality of the kNN from a corresponding cell (Fig. 1C). If the cells are aligned alongside a Res, the clonality of the neighboring cells should be similar to that of the given cell. Hence, a higher SCC reflects a better alignment of the cells with Res.

B cell vaccination and tumor-infiltrating T cell data processing

We collected pre- and post-vaccination data on human B cells from a single donor from 10 × genomics and colorectal cancer data from a public database representing both liquid and solid sample conditions49,50. For B cell data, we assigned the pre-vaccination sample as the “unperturbed” sample and the post-vaccination sample as the “perturbed” sample. For colorectal cancer data, we analyzed only T cells, not B cells, because only a few B cells remain after preprocessing. We selected only pretreatment datasets that contained both “Normal” (unperturbed) and “Tumor” (perturbed) samples (Methods). The main reason for maintaining a paired sample for both the unperturbed and perturbed samples is that it is better to maintain the same repertoire of BCR or TCR across samples. Moreover, we excluded the B or T cells that have already been clonally expanded from the unperturbed sample to only observe clonal expansion by “perturbation” (vaccination or neoantigen exposure). This resulted in only singlets remaining in the unperturbed sample, whereas only the perturbed sample contained clonally expanded B or T cells. In addition, singlets from the perturbed sample can be assigned as non-responding cells. Because the performance was conducted for each sample, we directly used the number of shared clonotypes as the clonality of B or T cells.

Benchmarking various methods with BRiCE

Using the dataset mentioned above, we benchmarked the performance of RC and Res using the existing methods. Among them, we selected scGen for the non-disentanglement method and CPA, contrastiveVI, scDisInFact, and scCausalVI for the disentanglement method, and CINEMA-OT for the optimal-transport-based method, which can distinguish the perturbation state of perturbed and unperturbed samples without prior knowledge, thereby reflecting a common situation for new experiments. The results from the standard scRNA-seq pipeline were compared using SCANPY (reference) as a baseline51. The representative results from the “P20” patient in tumor-infiltrating T cell data are shown in Figure S1,with the number of highly variable genes (HVGs) up to 2000 (Methods). As expected, the standard scRNA-seq data could not distinguish the clonally expanded T cells and singlets because it could not disentangle them using only the gene expression space. Surprisingly, none of the methods distinguish between clonally expanding cells or aligned cells alongside clonal expansion (Fig. S1). In addition to this sample, other results from the B cell vaccination data or tumor-infiltrating T cell data were similar or worse than the reference data (Fig. 2A and B; var: 2000). These results were maintained when the number of HVG was altered (Fig. 2A, B, S2, and Table S1). The results were robust across different k-neighbors (Fig. S3). Comparison of each test reveals future directions for predicting responding cells

After all assessments, we compared the performance across each test to infer future directions for perturbation prediction. Only the overlapping samples from “RC,” “Res,” and each test were used to observe which test showed the best performance. The 2000 HVG model was used for “Standard” and “Perturb Only.” As a result, CPA, scDisInFact, and scCausalVI were able to distinguish the responding cells when the data were separated by responsiveness, whereas neither the standard method nor perturbation-associated features improved performance, except contrastiveVI, which showed a slight improvement (Fig. 5A and B). It is widely accepted that a perturbed sample is composed of shared and perturbed spaces. Using the “perturbation” information only from the perturbed and unperturbed datasets was insufficient to disentangle the responding cells, but it was successful when using the information on responsiveness. Discussion

Understanding the perturbation effect at the single-cell level is important because of heterogeneity; however, no validation data exists to measure the perturbation effect on both gene expression and cellular abundance. Hence, we developed BRiCE by leveraging the characteristics of clonal expansion in T or B cells, which reflect the perturbation. Subsequently, we benchmarked the preexisting methods, but all failed to address the perturbations associated with both gene expression and cellular abundance. The main reason might be the design of these methods, which mostly focus on gene expression but not on cellular abundance2–5,19,20,32. In addition, no good validation dataset exists to assess performance.

One of the caveats of previous methods is that not all changes in the transcriptome result from perturbation; however, some of the methods utilize the transcriptome to measure the distance between cells without selecting the gold standard genes responsible for the perturbation. In addition, their validation by predicting the perturbed gene expression was over-evaluated because many of the methods showed almost perfect correlation with the mean expression of the cells; however, even with this perfect performance, it was more or less the same as the standard scRNA-seq pipeline from BRiCE.

Perhaps the fundamental assumption that gene expression alone is sufficient to detect the perturbation is wrong, as all methods failed for various perturbation-associated genes. In addition, the small number of “Expand DEG” from the standard pipeline implies only subtle differences in gene expression between responding and non-responding cells. Receptor phosphorylation occurs after the ligand binds to the receptor23. This indicates that not only gene expression but also other modalities, such as post-translational modification or even the epigenome and epitranscriptome, should be considered.

In addition, this may imply that, even in the perturbed latent space—assuming each method has successfully disentangled the shared space and perturbed space—there is another layer for cellular response (Fig. 5C). This implies that responding cells and non-responding cells can share a perturbed space within the perturbation field, which can be further distinguished by the responding space from the responding cells. Moreover, different levels of cellular response should also be considered, which may require further disentanglement in the responding space. Hence, efforts should be made to redesign this disentanglement to capture high-order complex structure. Since we observed the potential of the disentanglement based on a generative model (Fig. 4), it is quite promising to design the model in the future.

Although foundation models for scRNA-seq, such as scGPT, scFoundation, and scBERT exist, most focus on predicting gene expression in the perturbed state, which was insufficient for our benchmarking1,52–54. In addition, foundation models can be used for supervised learning by fine-tuning; however, as mentioned previously, most new experiments may not have been reported before. A zero-shot scheme might be possible; however, it is still not a convincing method owing to its weak nature55.

Currently, many efforts have been made to advance the AI field in scRNA-seq to obtain a confident in silico experimental model for unseen perturbations1. However, using BRiCE, we found that current perturbation models or validation sets were insufficient to develop such a model. Considering only gene expressions from current models may distort biology. Hence, researchers should also consider cellular abundance when developing a model with a suitable validation dataset.

A limitation of our study is that antigen recognition can induce transcriptional activation without necessarily leading to clonal expansion, even after sufficient stimulation time, due to factors such as suboptimal co-stimulation56, anergy57, exhaustion58, activation-induced cell death59, or technical noise. Hence, biological noise in the ground-truth labels might confound the assessment. Thus, our approach serves as a proxy for assessing the effects of perturbation. Still, clonally expanded T/B cells indicate that higher perturbation upon antigen exposure is possible, whereas non-expanded antigen-specific T/B cells are less perturbed. In addition, the lack of diverse validation datasets that capture perturbations in both gene expression and cellular abundance should be explored further. In particular, obtaining perturbation data that result in cell death is challenging, yet such data are crucial for modeling drug treatments, including cancer cell killing. The difficulty lies in distinguishing whether cell death arises from real biological processes or technical artefacts. In typical scRNA-seq pipelines, such as when using mitochondrial gene content for quality control, dying cells are routinely removed. Although some studies have attempted to use a dying cell signature from live cells, the actual endpoint of the dead cells has not been used, which is conceptually inconsistent for modeling60.

Conclusions

Nevertheless, BRiCE can guide perturbation modeling by suggesting the existence of another hidden space that should be disentangled within the perturbed space. The clonal expansion benchmarking dataset serves as a new validation set for evaluating perturbations in single-cell fields. In addition, BRiCE is not limited to the clonal expansion model, but it can also cover all kinds of perturbations, as it has been designed for a general purpose.

Methods

Data acquisition

Human B cell pre- and post-flu vaccination data from healthy donors were obtained from 10xgenomics49 (https://www.10xgenomics.com/datasets). Tumor-infiltrating T cell data were obtained from GSE23658150.

Data preprocessing

We followed the standard Seurat61 pipeline (v5.1.0) for both datasets.

For B cell data from 10x, bad cells were filtered (high mitochondrial gene percentage, high nFeature_RNA, and low nFeature_RNA) from “filtered_feature_bc_matrix.” From “filtered_contig_annotations.csv” file, which contains BCR information, we extracted confident BCR by “is_cell” = = true, “high_confidence” = = true, “full_length” = = true, and “productive” = = true. We excluded clonotypes if “raw_clonotype_id” is NA.

For tumor-infiltrating T cell data, we obtained the cell label from “GSE236581_CRC-ICB_metadata.txt. gz.” We only extracted “MajorCellType” = = “T” or = = “B.” Next, we followed the prefiltering step described in a previous study (high mitochondrial gene percentage50, high nCount_RNA, low nCount_RNA, and low nFeature_RNA). We further selected samples that have both “Normal” and “Tumor” conditions. We also selected pretreatment data (“Treatment” = = “I”). We obtained VDJ information from “GSE236581_VDJ_merge.txt. gz”. We followed the clonotyping steps described previously50. We only selected “full-length” from both alpha and beta. We obtained the CDR3 region from the alpha and beta chains. A clonotype was assigned to the CDR3 sequence.

B cells were excluded because only 36 cells remained after filtering. T cells were retained only if both “Normal” and “Tumor” conditions had more than 500 cells.

Clonality and clonal expansion assignment

For each individual, we measured clonality as the number of cells that shared the same clonotype (singlet, if not shared) from perturbed or unperturbed samples. We assigned “clonally expanded” if clonality > 1. We excluded cells from both the perturbed and unperturbed samples if they clonally expanded in the unperturbed sample.

Response classification (RC)

Normalized Shannon entropy, adapted from BatchBench48, was used to distinguish between clonally expanded cells and singlets in perturbed samples. The kNN was calculated for each cell using a predefined neighbor graph (‘distances’ metric from sc.pp.neighbors). Normalized Shannon entropy was calculated for each cell using kNN. A k value of 30 was used for the analysis.

Response effect size (Res)

The Res was determined only for clonally expanded cells in the perturbed samples. Average clonality was measured using the clonality from kNN (k = 5) for each cell. Spearman correlation coefficient (SCC) was measured between the clonality of a given cell and the mean clonality from kNN. At least 50 clonally expanded cells were required to measure the Res.

Reference (standard scRNA-seq pipeline) performance measurement

adata.layers[“counts”] = adata.X.copy() was performed to save the count matrix before processing. Then, a typical scanpy51 pipeline was used, including pp.normalize_total, pp.log1p, pp.highly_variable_genes, pp.pca, pp.neighbors (n_neighbors = 10, n_pcs = 30), and tl.umap. For highly variable genes (HVGs), the number of genes or a specific gene set was determined in each test. This objective was provided for all benchmarking methods for the input data.

scGen processing

We followed the “SCGEN: Perturbation Prediction” pipeline (https://scgen.readthedocs.io/en/stable/)4. The batch_key = “condition” parameter was defined according to the corresponding analysis design. pp.neighbors was computed using the latent_X = model.get_latent_representation() as provided by the pipeline.

CPA processing

We followed the “Predicting perturbation responses for unseen cell types (context transfer)” pipeline (https://github.com/theislab/cpa?tab=readme-ov-file)5. For CPA.setup_anndata, we only defined “perturbation_key” and “control_group” without any other “key” variables. “is_count_data” parameter was set to False since adata.X contains the normalized gene expression. The “perturbation_key” and “control_group” parameters were defined according to the corresponding analysis design. pp.neighbors was computed using the “latent_after” observations from the output of model.get_latent_representation(adata, batch_size = 2048) as provided by the pipeline.

ContrastiveVI processing

We followed the “Applying contrastiveVI to see the effects of stem cell transplants for leukemia patients” pipeline (https://github.com/suinleelab/contrastiveVI?tab=readme-ov-file)19. The “target_adata” and “background_adata” parameters were defined according to the corresponding analysis design. pp.neighbors was computed using the output of model.get_latent_representation(target_adata, representation_kind = ’salient’).

CINEMA-OT processing

We followed the “CINEMA-OT analysis of mock- > IFNb stimulation effects” pipeline (https://github.com/vandijklab/CINEMA-OT/blob/main/cinemaot_tutorial.ipynb)20. Obs_label, ref_label, and expr_label parameters were defined according to the corresponding analysis design. pp.neighbors was computed using the “de” object from “co.cinemaot.cinemaot_unweighted.”

scCausalVI processing (Citation after Nov. 5)

We followed the pipeline from “Data Pre-processing” in the Tutorial section (https://sccausalvi.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorial.html). condition_key, control_key, and conditions parameters were defined according to the corresponding analysis design. pp.neighbors was computed using the output of model.get_latent_representation(target_adata, representation_kind = latent_t).

scDisInFact processing

We followed the pipeline from “demo.ipynb” in the GitHub repository (https://github.com/ZhangLabGT/scDisInFact)32. condition_key parameter in the create_scdisinfact_dataset was defined according to the corresponding analysis design. Ks =[1,2] was used (batch size: 1, condition size: 2). pp.neighbors was computed using the PCA of z_d.

Perturbed DEG processing

DEG analysis between the perturbed and unperturbed samples was performed using pp.rank_genes_groups (method = ‘wilcoxon’). Genes with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were selected. Samples were excluded if the number of genes was ≤ 30.

Expanded DEG processing

DEG analysis between clonally expanded cells and singlets in the perturbed sample was performed using pp.rank_genes_groups (method = ‘wilcoxon’). Genes with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-values < 0.1 were selected. Samples were excluded if the number of genes was ≤ 30.

Expanded correlation processing

SCC was performed between gene expression and clonality of the perturbed sample. Genes with Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p-values < 0.1 were selected. Samples were excluded if the number of genes was ≤ 30.

Perturb only processing

From the perturbed sample, clonally expanded cells were assigned to “pseudo-perturbed” cells, whereas singlets were assigned to the unperturbed cells.

Comparison between the reference results

The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed between each method and the reference (two-sided). Multiple hypothesis correction was performed using the BH method.
