Theory of change for building stronger wildlife health surveillance systems globally
Authors: Liz P Noguera Z, Carrie Kappel, Jonathan M Sleeman, Marcela M Uhart, François Diaz, Claire Cayol, Keren Cox-Witton, Clare Death, Damien O Joly, Kevin Brown, Emma G Gardner, Sarin Suwanpakdee, Bernard Bett, Kim M Pepin, Kacey Yellowbird, Dina Saulo, Oliver Morgan, Sarah H Olson, Mathieu Pruvot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEmerging and re-emerging infectious diseases affecting wildlife have highlighted the need for wildlife health surveillance (WHS), given the interconnected role of wildlife in maintaining the health of natural resources, agriculture, and human populations. Although global policies and guidelines exist, a key gap remains in the implementation of WHS systems at local and national levels. A working group of local, national, and global stakeholders in WHS was established to address this gap. In this Viewpoint, we report a theory of change (ToC) developed to support implementation of WHS from local to global scales. Using established methods for developing a collaborative ToC, we leveraged our expertise and identified six transformative pathways to be implemented via collaborations across scales and contexts: mindset change, policy and investment, evidence-based practice, user-driven technologies, capacity enhancement, and mobilisation of a global community of practice. This ToC serves as a roadmap for the development of effective WHS systems that support adaptive management and implementation. WHS is fundamental to understanding the impact of health threats on biodiversity, domestic animals, and humans. This ToC also presents an approach to operationalise the integration of wildlife health into collaborative One Health surveillance systems.

Introduction

Healthy wildlife populations are the foundation for wildlife conservation, ecosystem service maintenance, income generation (eg, ecotourism, hunting), food security (eg, subsistence harvest), and the achievement of One Health objectives.1,2 One Health is a collaborative approach that links the health of people, animals, and ecosystems, recognising that shared environments and interactions drive health outcomes across species.3 This approach is essential for the management of endemic or (re-)emerging infectious diseases with wildlife reservoirs that affect people and animals. The recent emergence and global spread of highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 (clade 2·3·4·4b) has greatly affected the poultry sector, wild birds, and mammals globally.4 Large-scale amplification and circulation of this virus within and between poultry and wildlife populations has increased spillover to mammalian hosts, including humans, resulting in complex One Health challenges spanning conservation, domestic animal health, and public health.5,6 Recurring outbreaks of yellow fever in Brazil, Crimean–Congo haemorrhagic fever in Europe, and Ebola virus disease in central Africa are other examples.7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12 Human activities and continued encroachment into natural areas affect climate, landscape structures and connectivity, habitat availability, water and soil quality, and patterns of species interactions, challenging the existence of wildlife species. The loss of ecological integrity poses risks to human and animal health, with wildlife often serving as early indicators or sentinels.13 However, there is a large knowledge gap regarding the biotic, abiotic, and anthropogenic factors influencing both wildlife and human health. In fact, a proposed continuum-of-care socioecological model of public health includes ecosystem integrity as a key upstream determinant and nature protection as an essential intervention point for public health.14

A fuller understanding and appropriate management of these complex One Health issues require integrated wildlife health intelligence, especially across political borders.15 Nevertheless, coordinated and systematic wildlife health surveillance (WHS) is lacking globally.16 Countries show considerable disparities in the development of their WHS systems. Among 107 countries surveyed, 58% showed no evidence of a functional WHS programme.17 Generally, funding for WHS fluctuates in response to successive livestock or public health crises, with baseline funding rarely meeting existing needs. Even high-income countries that conduct established, nationwide, and centralised programmes report operational challenges related to programme development, cross-sectoral collaboration, funding sustainability, and data management.18,19 Low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) face substantial limitations in conducting comprehensive surveillance, often relying on sporadic approaches. These efforts, often dictated by foreign funding, are restricted in scope and duration, thereby hindering institutional capacity building, local ownership, and long-term sustainability. Nonetheless, local solutions and successful stories of WHS implementation in LMICs that overcome these challenges are increasing.20,21

Since 2011, the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) and its Working Group on Wildlife have promoted the global standardisation and coordination of WHS by designating national wildlife focal points. These focal points report wildlife disease events not covered by international standards to WOAH’s WAHIS-Wild database through national delegates.1,22, 23, 24, 25 In 2021, WOAH released a wildlife health framework that reinforced WOAH’s commitment to supporting WHS globally, enhancing national capacity, and further integrating WHS into One Health strategies. Later, in 2023, WOAH launched a network of wildlife health collaborating centres, called WOAH-WildNet, to strengthen and coordinate WOAH’s internal efforts on the topic.1 Despite this global leadership, gaps in WHS implementation at subnational and national scales remain owing to the scarcity of field-level networks to facilitate WHS implementation. Most countries have not allocated sufficient human and financial resources for wildlife health, and institutional mandates for WHS either do not exist or fail to maximise intelligence sharing across institutions. This situation represents a major gap in the ability to generate wildlife health intelligence globally.26 Accordingly, current top-down approaches should be complemented by bottom-up processes and other grounded approaches, including side-to-side (horizontal governance, network-driven, and peer-to-peer change) and inside-out, to enhance nationally coordinated initiatives and mainstream WHS systems.27,28

The Science for Nature and People Partnership (SNAPP) funded the creation of a working group, to strengthen WHS globally through a collaborative and evidence-based approach.29 This working group comprised representatives from international, national, and local organisations and aimed to address the gap between global coordination and local implementation while identifying ways to encourage consistent and effective WHS practices at national and subnational levels. This working group was founded on the premise that implementation of sustainable WHS at national and subnational scales exceeds the capacity of any single institution and that a consortium approach could better address this challenge by promoting cross-sectoral relationships that can be leveraged for capacity building and implementation. Here, we present a theory of change (ToC) to address the gaps between global policy and local-to-national implementation of WHS. A ToC is a structured, participatory, and adaptive framework in which stakeholders and rights holders articulate their collective aims, identify desired outcomes, and map the actions and conditions required to achieve them.27,30

Methods

A working group was convened to address the WHS implementation gap (appendix p 3). Membership of the working group was by invitation from the project leaders, with the aim of ensuring a diverse mix of relevant technical expertise, lived experience, organisational structures, geographical representation, and economic contexts. The working group developed the ToC as a roadmap to illustrate how a set of interventions could lead to desired changes. The ToC approach provides a systematic process to explore the range of drivers and levers required to create a change, an approach that is consistent with holistic system analysis that supports transdisciplinary and cross-sectoral approaches. The SNAPP working group developed the final ToC over two virtual workshops (4 h each), a 3-day in-person workshop facilitated by an experienced professional facilitator (CK), additional online debriefing sessions, and multiple rounds of drafting. In 2024, the SNAPP working group evolved into the Wildlife Health Intelligence Network (WHIN).

Adaptive challenge

Adaptive challenges are issues resulting from complex dynamics that require a collaborative learning process, a whole-system adaptation, and often a mindset shift rather than a narrow, expertise-guided technical solution. Barriers to implementation

Barriers to implementation are well documented and often arise from conflicting stakeholder priorities and values, resource limitations, and system complexity (figure 4).19 The ability of government entities to lead WHS might be limited by legal or funding constraints, the absence of an underlying WHS mandate, insufficient expertise, or a combination of these factors. Animal health mandates are usually held within veterinary services, whose main priorities are driven by a low number of economically important species. This focus on economically important species results in lower WHS prioritisation compared to other animal health priorities and reduces incentives to transparently report wildlife health intelligence because of perceived negative economic consequences and trade impacts. In this context, wildlife is perceived as a threat (a source of disease) rather than a resource to be conserved for the greater good. Traditionally, the environmental sector has had low awareness of, and influence on, animal and public health decision making. As a result, WHS priorities have mainly been defined from a human-centred and agriculture-centred standpoint, without considering the inherent value of wildlife, biodiversity, and ecosystems, even when deliberate efforts are made to implement a One Health approach. Inadequate institutional support has caused poor prioritisation of sustained WHS efforts despite its critical importance for operationalising One Health.Figure 4Barriers to implementation of wildlife health surveillance

As single institutions rarely hold the sole mandate for WHS, multi-institutional and multisectoral collaborative approaches are needed for implementation, which require adequate communication, coordination, and collaboration mechanisms.17 This coordination is further complicated when national and subnational levels of government operate under different regulations and policies. The complexity of WHS data and the lack of standards result in inconsistent systems that hinder information sharing and coordination across sectors and scales. As a result, these structural and operational barriers prevent the generation of coordinated WHS intelligence that supports One Health action.

Mindset change

Mindset change is a pathway that enables other pathways in the ToC. Three strategies for mindset change were highlighted. First, there is a need to understand and communicate the value of WHS as a benefit and not just as a cost (eg, costs associated with trade impacts). Economic analyses should be a core activity of this pathway (eg, Natural Capital Project), making a business case for WHS, including the non-market value of wildlife, such as willingness to pay for wildlife health or ecosystem services that incorporate the intrinsic value of wildlife. Second, there is a need to move beyond the utilitarian and human-centred perception of wildlife and WHS, and to fully integrate the intrinsic value of wildlife into decisions and prioritisation of WHS.31 In this nature-centred framework, WHS incorporates drivers of disease and their effects on wildlife populations and encourages linking of WHS systems with ecological monitoring programmes for comprehensive discernment of environmental changes.15 Third, the working group reflected on decolonising our approach to global cooperation on WHS, and the importance of cultural humility in addressing questions of wildlife value and WHS.15,32 Creating relationships among diverse groups, peoples, and nations is essential to generate the ethical space in which multiple worldviews can be represented, heard, and respected. This ethical space is essential as national governments engage with Indigenous Nations on the codesign and comanagement of WHS programmes, under the guiding principles of the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples.33

Policy and investment

This pathway focuses on increasing political will for WHS by fostering policy changes, allocating adequate funding and investment, and incentivising detection of health hazards. Political support for sustainable WHS can benefit from both top-down and bottom-up approaches. At the global scale, the wildlife health framework developed by WOAH and other tools (eg, Joint External Evaluation and the WOAH Performance of Veterinary Services) encourage and guide countries to implement WHS but are not legally binding and do not provide a clear pathway for national and local implementation. The WOAH guidelines on wildlife surveillance offer practical recommendations for national implementation, albeit without an enforcement mechanism. Opportunities exist (eg, the WHO Pandemic Agreement and the pending 2005 WHO International Health Regulations updates) to more strongly mandate the development and maintenance of WHS as part of coordinated multisectoral surveillance, similar to systems in place for livestock and human health.17,34,35 WHS supports the WHO Pandemic Agreement, which identifies actions for pre-epidemic preparedness, alert, outbreak response, and post-epidemic evaluation, and can be integrated into pending updates to the 2005 WHO International Health Regulations. At the national scale, the adoption of standard operating procedures is essential to provide bottom-up policy incentives to sustainably implement surveillance. WHS policy templates that clearly define roles, expectations, and readiness, and that can be adapted to local contexts, are essential to support effective implementation and operationalising One Health.

Locally relevant prioritisation of WHS objectives (ie, engaging local stakeholders and rights holders to identify surveillance priorities), along with stronger assessment of the value of functional WHS systems, would further incentivise local implementation and investment. However, the global nature of drivers of wildlife health (eg, environmental degradation, pathogen spread, climate change, trade, and pollution) warrants a strong role for global cooperation in funding country-level WHS systems. Although short-term project funding is valuable for responding to immediate localised threats, such efforts often lead to repurposing of resources towards the next emergency, resulting in unintended, short-lived infrastructure development and capacity building.36 Applying a mindset shift that leverages these short-term opportunities for long-term development is essential for building institutional resilience.

A challenge is ensuring that the spatial and temporal scales of investments—such as funding for infrastructure, training, data management, and policy development—align with the operational needs of WHS systems, thereby enabling the generation of wildlife health intelligence that benefits local and global communities.

Evidence-based practice

Data synthesis, research, and knowledge sharing activities are fundamental to supporting science-based decisions and tools for WHS (appendix p 5). A concerted focus on generating and sharing evidence for WHS is needed, particularly in the following areas: economic and cost–benefit analyses, prioritisation of surveillance efforts, assessment of existing WHS resources, development of performance metrics, and political science research addressing policy gaps and governance. There is also a need for science to facilitate the development of useful public data collection tools and repositories to better incorporate local knowledge sourced from community, citizen, student-based, and other networks, which can complement data from government-led efforts.37

User-driven technologies

This pathway addresses flexible and scalable technologies needed to improve WHS implementation. The need for standardised and open-source data collection and management tools has been a strong driver of collaboration among working group members. Wildlife health information sharing remains hindered by institutions that largely operate data systems in isolation and are constrained by sensitivities related to data sharing. Reducing data siloes will help to operationalise WHS and One Health. In this context, appropriate technologies developed through participatory and collaborative approaches can provide reliable, standardised, open-source data management solutions that enable information sharing and greater interoperability of existing systems. Advances in artificial intelligence could also contribute to making WHS information more accessible by automating the extraction and synthesis of data from diverse sources (eg, field reports, laboratory diagnostics, and remote sensing), enabling real-time alerts, and supporting multilingual dashboards for diverse stakeholders. The goal is for user-driven technologies to provide richer information and intelligence on wildlife health for decision makers and wildlife managers.

Capacity enhancement

This pathway explores how to mobilise existing workforces and enhance the skills, knowledge, and capacity for WHS, with the goal of empowering instead of acting on behalf of those concerned. Developing and consolidating training materials that are locally relevant and tailored to different roles within the WHS system are important for implementation.38,39 Delivery of training requires institutions and actors that are firmly embedded in local contexts. As such, connecting international and local WHS actors and organisations, and establishing communities of practice, will be instrumental in developing sustainable capacity-building models. Facilitating access to training materials through e-learning platforms in multiple languages would further support WHS implementation.37 Finally, even with adequate training, responding to large-scale wildlife health emergencies can be challenging for newly formed WHS systems, and this capacity can be enhanced through support and mentoring from a global network.

Mobilising a global Community of Practice (CoP): the WHIN

The expansion of the working group into a larger CoP, the WHIN, provides the main pathway for implementing the ToC. The network addresses a fundamental gap in national WHS implementation by leveraging multiple organisations and actors operating at different scales and areas of expertise. WHIN aims to function as a support system that links expertise and addresses gaps to design, guide, and support the implementation of comprehensive, coherent, and sustainable WHS by individual countries (appendix p 6). WHIN will mobilise organisations and individuals from multiple sectors and levels, including intergovernmental organisations (eg, WOAH, FAO, WHO, UNEP, IUCN), key global implementation actors (eg, Wildlife Conservation Society), regional networks, national WHS coordinators and actors, field practitioners, educators, researchers (eg, protected area managers, citizen science groups, naturalists, scientists, Indigenous communities, local conservation organisations, or universities), and laboratories. A WHIN charter and governance structure have been developed. The network promotes collaborations, lateral and South–South cooperation among LMICs, and bottom-up processes to develop new norms and policies. This model supports a decolonised approach to global cooperation by recognising and facilitating contributions from different contexts and worldviews, including those of actors in LMICs and Indigenous communities.

By working together as a WHS CoP, we can more effectively and synergistically address implementation challenges. For example, the establishment of minimum requirements and standards for WHS systems, and development of practical step-by-step guidance on operationalising WHS, standard operating procedures for network and laboratory activities, and evaluation and assessment tools, can be strengthened if codeveloped within the CoP. Variations in levels of resourcing and capacity result in diverse WHS strengths, ranging from methodological refinement in a few well-established systems to foundational system development in settings in which WHS is still being formed. By bringing people together, the collective knowledge and experience of the CoP, such as local successes and solutions, can be shared and replicated to achieve a more efficient and effective rollout of WHS.

Aims and expected impacts

The mission of WHIN is to establish a CoP that collaborates on WHS to support implementation everywhere, at all times, particularly in contexts in which WHS is most needed, such as vulnerable populations and threatened biodiversity. WHIN seeks to expand the CoP to collectively reduce barriers to WHS implementation (figure 4). This process involves capturing shared outcomes and the pathways to achieve them, aligning stakeholders around a common vision and purpose, building trust and buy-in, and proactively identifying barriers so that solutions can be developed in advance. Over the longer term, WHIN aims for most countries to have effective WHS systems supported by comprehensive guidance and standards, open access to wildlife health data-management tools, and broad support for One Health–driven and conservation-driven WHS. The impact of enhanced national WHS capacity, combined with effective coordination between global, national, and local organisations and institutions, will be facilitated by WHIN’s CoP. A CoP can effectively share existing solutions tailored to local contexts—whether addressing capacity enhancement, policy improvements, data and technology, or evidence-based surveillance planning—and amplify them across a global community of implementers. The collaborative structure also facilitates a collective shift towards nature-centred WHS. The ultimate goal is sustainable and healthy ecosystems supporting diverse wildlife and sustainable human societies.

Discussion

The ToC developed by the WHIN working group articulates practical steps to support WHS implementation across scales, thereby bridging the gap between global frameworks (eg, WOAH’s Wildlife Health Framework) and local realities. The framework builds on promising local and regional successes in LMICs, such as WildHealthNet in Southeast Asia and Thailand’s WOAH Twinning Program. WildHealthNet’s locally-driven approach successfully achieved event-based and targeted surveillance across Cambodia, Lao PDR, and Viet Nam, with parallel implementation and policy development resulting in national surveillance plans being adopted into legislation.20,40, 41, 42, 43 Similarly, Thailand’s system-wide capacity enhancement through needs assessment and participatory planning established a formal Thailand wildlife health network and a WOAH collaborating centre on wildlife health with government support.40,44 Both initiatives support this ToC’s emphasis on stakeholder engagement at all levels, iterative approaches, trust-building through collaboration, and context-specific solutions that integrate field implementation with policy development to create coherent and sustainable WHS systems.

Establishing WHIN as a CoP provides the central mechanism for implementing this ToC by bringing together multiple organisations, governments, and local communities at subnational, national, and regional levels. Such diverse institutions working collaboratively are well positioned to enhance WHS adoption through bottom-up, side-to-side, and inside-out approaches. Generating wildlife health intelligence requires structured systems with a universal language for complex data developed through collaboration with stakeholders and rights holders. One early WHIN initiative, HAWK, starts to address this urgent need in data and knowledge sharing by following best practices (ie, principles of FAIR [findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable] and CARE [collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, and ethics]).45 Because realities on the ground can change rapidly, regular evaluation and adaptation of this ToC will be crucial to ensure that WHIN remains on task.

This ToC and WHIN face important limitations. Despite efforts to diversify perspectives, our working group lacked diversity, representation, and transdisciplinary involvement. This limitation reflects broader challenges in WHS implementation, with most established systems and practical expertise concentrated in high-income countries.46 WHIN’s CoP approach aims to address these gaps through greater inclusion, cultural pluralism, and support for Indigenous and LMIC leadership. The creation of an Indigenous-led and community-led surveillance task force aims to identify local expertise for the development of these initiatives and to foster collaboration on best practices and priorities in community-led surveillance.47

WHIN’s consortium approach requires members to prioritise collective benefit despite potentially competing mandates. Tensions arising from competing mandates likely influenced the early development of WHIN and will continue to challenge implementation of the ToC. Nevertheless, collaborative approaches can promote more efficient and concerted use of limited resources, encouraging innovation through cooperation, rather than competition among key actors. Furthermore, current funding mechanisms are temporally and spatially fragmented, offering few opportunities for long-term investments that lead to sustainable WHS impact.

This ToC supports broader One Health strategies outlined in One Health High-Level Expert Panel’s One Health ToC, the Quadripartite’s One Health Joint Plan of Action, the Global Health Security Agenda, the Pandemic Agreement, and the Convention on Biological Diversity Global Action Plan on Biodiversity and Health.48, 49, 50 Stronger WHS helps countries to achieve multisectoral surveillance indicators across the International Health Regulations Joint External Evaluation and WOAH’s Performance of Veterinary Services evaluation tool. Although most wildlife health initiatives remain focused on zoonotic diseases, operationalising One Health requires attention towards understanding the environmental drivers affecting biodiversity, agriculture, and public health.

Embracing a new model of international cooperation for WHS depends on our collective will. Global WHS needs greater trust and collaboration among actors that engage and support countries in enhancing their WHS systems. Through focused efforts in capacity enhancement, standards development, research, data sharing, and advocating for wildlife, this ToC paves the way for tangible action. WHIN can link and leverage these efforts to support efficient global expansion of WHS. The success of this proposal depends on a collaborative and decolonised model of international cooperation for WHS.
