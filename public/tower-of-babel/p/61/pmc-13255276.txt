Exploring the antimalarial potential of (+)-2,2'-epicytoskyrin A: in vitro and in vivo studies of a bioactive metabolite from endophytic fungus Diaporthe sp. GNBP-10
Authors: Risqa Novita, Ahmad Fathoni, Danang Waluyo, Agik Suprayogi, Huda Shalahudin Darusman, Arifin Budiman Nugraha, Arif Nurkanto, Hilkatul Ilmi, Tomoyoshi Nozaki, Andria Agusta

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a major global parasitic disease, increasingly complicated by resistance to frontline antimalarial drugs such as chloroquine and artemisinin. This challenge underscores the urgent need for novel antimalarial agents with alternative mechanisms of action. Endophytic fungi constitute a promising yet underexplored source of structurally diverse bioactive metabolites. This study investigated the antimalarial potential of ( +)-2,2′-epicytoskyrin A (Epi-A), a bisanthraquinone isolated from Diaporthe sp. GNBP-10, an endophyte of Uncaria gambir (Hunter) Roxb., was utilized in both in vitro and in vivo models.MethodsIn vitro antimalarial activity was assessed against chloroquine-sensitive Plasmodium falciparum 3D7 using microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained thin blood smears. At the same time, cytotoxicity was evaluated in MCF-7 cells by measuring absorbance at 450 nm and calculating CC₅₀ values using GraphPad Prism. In vivo efficacy was evaluated in Plasmodium berghei–infected Swiss Webster mice. Animals were assigned to five groups: oral Epi-A (12.5, 25, and 50 mg/kg BW), artemisinin (20 mg/kg BW), and an untreated infected group. Treatment was administered for four consecutive days after the mean parasitaemia reached 10%. Histopathological analysis of the liver, spleen, kidney, and intestine was performed on day 8 pi using one mouse per group, while the remaining mice were monitored until day 15 pi for parasitaemia, survival, clinical manifestations, and body weight changes.ResultsEpi-A demonstrated potent in vitro activity, with an IC₅₀ of 0.24 µM, a CC₅₀ of 4.4 µM, and a selectivity index of 18.33. In vivo, on day 8 pi, histopathology revealed haemozoin deposition with mild tissue alterations in the liver, spleen, kidney, and intestine. Parasitaemia did not differ among Epi-A–treated groups but was significantly lower than in the negative control. Epi-A achieved > 50% inhibition (63.6%, 77.4%, and 50% at 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/kg BW, respectively), classifying it as a very good antimalarial activity. No post-treatment weight loss was observed, and only mild clinical signs were noted, compared with the severe manifestations observed in the negative control.ConclusionsEpi-A demonstrates potent in vitro and very good in vivo antimalarial activity, with low toxicity and potential organ-protective effects, supporting its promise as a candidate for further mechanistic studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05972-3.Keywords: Diaporthe sp. Background

Malaria continues to represent a formidable public health burden, with 263 million cases reported globally in 2023. The Southeast Asia Region, consisting of eight malaria-endemic countries, reported 4 million malaria cases, accounting for 1.5% of the global malaria burden in the year 2023. India was responsible for approximately 50% of the region's estimated malaria cases, while Indonesia contributed just under one-third of the total [1].

Drug resistance continues to compromise control strategies [2]. Resistance to frontline drugs such as chloroquine, quinine, and even artemisinin derivatives has increasingly limited treatment efficacy, underscoring the urgent need for novel antimalarial agents with distinct mechanisms of action [3]. Natural products have historically been a cornerstone of malaria therapy, exemplified by quinine and artemisinin, both of which are derived from plants. Over the past three decades, nearly two-thirds of new drugs have originated from natural sources [4]. While plants have been extensively studied, endophytic microorganisms, particularly fungi, are emerging as rich reservoirs of structurally unique metabolites with potential pharmacological applications. Within this group, Diaporthe species have yielded metabolites with potent antimicrobial and antiparasitic activities, yet their antimalarial potential remains insufficiently characterized [5, 6].

The rise of multidrug-resistant Plasmodium strains underscores the importance of investigating endophytic fungi as alternative sources of bioactive secondary metabolites for antimalarial drug discovery [7]. Chérigo et al. [5] found that endophytic fungi isolated from Panamanian mangroves, including Diaporthe sp., demonstrated potent antimalarial activity [5]. Similarly, Ferreira et al. [6] studied Diaporthe miriciae, which showed strong inhibition of P. falciparum, including multidrug-resistant strains. The ability of fungi to produce bioactive metabolites that are effective against resistant malaria parasites highlights their significant pharmacological value [6].

Indonesia, one of the world’s biodiversity hotspots, harbours diverse endophytic fungi with untapped potential for drug discovery. As part of our efforts to identify antimalarial drug candidates, we evaluated thousands of Indonesian bioresource extracts and natural bioactive compounds previously characterized in our laboratory. From these activities, it was successfully discovered that Diaporthe sp. GNBP-10 is an endophytic fungus found in Uncaria gambir (Hunter) Roxb. This wide variety produces bisantraquinone compounds with antimicrobial properties against Staphylococcus aureus and Candida tropicalis, showing a MIC value of < 16 μg/ml [8, 9].

(+)−2,2'-Epicytoskyrin A or Epi-A, derived from this fungus, has also been tested in vitro against several bacteria, including Bacillus subtilis, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Salmonella enteritidis, Salmonella paratyphi, and Escherichia coli, demonstrating lower MIC values than the antibiotics chloramphenicol and erythromycin [10]. Acute toxicity testing in male ddY strain mice revealed an LD50 of 1638.87 mg/kg BW, indicating low toxicity [11]. Subchronic toxicity testing demonstrated a mild hepatotoxic effect [12]. This suggests that Epi-A has potential as a new drug candidate. Based on these findings, we further conducted antimalarial studies of Epi-A in vitro and in vivo.

In vitro experimentation plays a pivotal role in early-stage screening of antimalarial candidates, providing a controlled environment for meticulous assessment of Plasmodium inhibition. To complement these findings, in vivo studies are employed to gather vital insights into the efficacy of these compounds under physiological conditions. Using murine models infected with Plasmodium berghei, researchers can effectively mimic aspects of human malaria and evaluate the therapeutic promise of antimalarial agents [13, 14]. Evidence from prior research suggests that compounds with strong in vitro performance often exhibit parallel effectiveness in vivo, highlighting the need for a dual assessment approach. Additionally, integrating both in vitro and in vivo techniques enables a deeper exploration into the compound’s mode of action [15–17]. This study assessed the antimalarial activity of Epi-A using in vitro assays against Plasmodium falciparum 3D7 and in vivo against Plasmodium berghei-infected mice, with a focus on therapeutic efficacy and histopathological alterations.

Methods

Production of Epi-A

The endophytic fungus Diaporthe sp. GNBP-10, an internal laboratory code or strain for the endophytic fungus, was isolated from young stems of Uncaria gambir (Hunter) Roxb. The isolation and identification procedures of the fungal strain were performed as previously described by Ilyas et al. [18]. The designation GNBP-10 refers to the isolate number assigned during the initial screening of endophytic fungi. The fungus was cultured in Potato Dextrose Broth (BD Biosciences) for 30 days at room temperature. Biomass and culture broth were extracted with ethyl acetate, concentrated under reduced pressure, and fractionated using Sephadex LH-20 column chromatography with ethanol as the mobile phase. The bioactive fraction was further purified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on a C18 column (CapcellPak, Shiseido) using an acetonitrile–water gradient. The identity and purity of Epi-A were confirmed by 1H- and 13C-NMR spectroscopy, consistent with previously published data [19].

In vitro culture of Plasmodium falciparum

The chloroquine-sensitive Plasmodium falciparum 3D7 strain was obtained from the Malaria Laboratory at the Centre for Natural Product Medicine Research and Development, Institute of Tropical Diseases, Universitas Airlangga, Surabaya, Indonesia. Parasite culture was conducted following the method of Trager and Jensen [78] with minor modifications, similar to the procedure previously described by Hidayati et al. [20]. Parasites were maintained in fresh human O-positive erythrocytes at 5% hematocrit in complete RPMI-1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 10% (v/v) human O-positive serum, 25 mM HEPES (Applichem Panreac), 50 µg/mL hypoxanthine (Sigma), 2 mg/mL sodium bicarbonate, and 10 µg/mL gentamicin (Sigma). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C using a modified candle jar. Human erythrocytes and serum were obtained from the Indonesian Red Cross, Surabaya, Indonesia. The culture medium was replaced daily, and parasitaemia was maintained below 5% to preserve optimal culture conditions and prevent culture collapse associated with insufficient nutrient supply [21, 22]. Parasite growth was monitored by microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained thin smears of infected erythrocytes (iRBCs). Synchronization of ring-stage parasites was achieved using 5% (w/v) D-sorbitol [23].

In vitro antimalarial activity

The in vitro antimalarial activity was conducted using microscopic measurement, as previously described, with slight modification [20, 24]. Stock solutions of Epi-A and artemisinin (Sigma-Aldrich) were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and serially diluted (0.01–100 µg/mL). Two microlitres of each compound were added to 24-well plates containing P. falciparum cultures (1% parasitaemia, 2% haematocrit), followed by incubation for 48 h at 37 °C. After incubation, thin blood smears were prepared, fixed with methanol, and stained with 10% Giemsa for 10 min. The percentage inhibition of parasite growth was determined by counting 1,000 erythrocytes per smear. IC₅₀ values were calculated by nonlinear regression using GraphPad Prism 7.0 software (GraphPad Co., Ltd., San Diego, CA, USA).

Cytotoxicity activity and selectivity index (SI) of Epi-A

Michigan Cancer Foundation (MCF7) human breast cancer cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), enriched with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), 2.1 mM glutamine, 2 g/L sodium bicarbonate, and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin solution (10,000 units/mL and 10,000 µg/mL, respectively; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were detached using 0.25% trypsin–EDTA and resuspended in fresh DMEM. A total of 100 μL of the cell suspension, containing 2 × 104 cells per well, was seeded into a sterile 96-well clear-bottom microplate (Corning, ME, USA) and incubated overnight under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). The test compounds, previously dissolved in DMSO, were added to the culture at 1 µL per well, resulting in final concentrations ranging from 3.8 to 975 µg/mL. Cells were then incubated for an additional 48 h under the same conditions. Control groups included cells treated with DMSO (negative control) and DMEM without cells (positive control for maximum inhibition). Following treatment, the cells were washed with 100 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) Cell Counting Kit-8 reagent (CCK-8; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) for 3 h at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Varioskan Lux plate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Each concentration was tested in triplicate, and the half-maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC₅₀) was calculated using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA) [25]. The selectivity index (SI) was subsequently calculated as the ratio of CC₅₀ to IC₅₀ [26].

In vivo antimalarial test

Experimental animals and parasites

The in vivo antimalarial test was conducted at the Animal Laboratory of the Agency for Health Policies Development, Indonesian Ministry of Health, Bogor. The animals were obtained from the Animal Laboratory of the Agency for Health Policies Development, Indonesian Ministry of Health, Bogor. Adult Swiss Webster female mice, weighing 27 ± 3 g, were used for in vivo anti-malarial experiments. Mice were acclimatized for 7 days before experimentation and housed in plastic cages under standard laboratory conditions (25 ± 5 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle), with free access to commercial feed and water. The Plasmodium berghei strain ANKA was sourced from the Agricultural Instruments Standardization Agency of the Indonesian Ministry of Agriculture in Bogor.

Ethical approval

The experiments on mice have been approved by the Health Research Ethics Commission of the National Research and Innovation Agency (BRIN) under accession number 101/KE.03/SK/09/2023.

Inoculation of Plasmodium berghei strain ANKA

The parasites, previously stored in frozen stocks at −80 °C, were thawed and injected intraperitoneally into donor mice at the start of the experiment. Plasmodium berghei was then serially transferred from the donor mice to healthy mice three times until parasitaemia levels reached approximately 50%. Afterward, the donor mice were euthanized, and blood was collected via cardiac puncture. The collected blood was diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Furthermore, 0.2 mL of iRBCs containing 1.0 × 10⁶ parasitized cells was injected intraperitoneally into each mouse in all groups on day 0 [27].

In vivo antimalarial curative test

The curative efficacy of Epi-A against P. berghei in the murine model was evaluated according to the method described by Cahyaningsih et al. [27]. Treatment was initiated when the mean parasitaemia of all mice reached approximately 10% [27]. Animals were then randomly allocated into five groups (n = 5 per group). Groups 1–3 received Epi-A at doses of 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/kg of body weight, respectively. Group 4 received artemisinin (TCI, Tokyo, Japan) at 20 mg/kg BW as a positive control, and group 5 served as the untreated group (negative control). All treatments were administered orally via a plastic gastric cannula for four consecutive days using 0.5% sodium carboxymethylcellulose (CMC-Na) as the vehicle [11, 12]. The efficacy of the curative treatment was assessed based on parasitaemia levels, clinical manifestations of malaria, changes in body weight, and Mean Survival Time (MST). It was further supported by histopathological evaluation of organs from the treatment groups, compared with those from the negative control group. Histopathological examination was performed on the first day after completion of treatment by collecting one mouse from each treatment group, while the remaining four mice in each group were monitored until day 15 post-infection (pi) to determine whether recrudescence occurred, which was associated with mortality between day 14 and 17 pi [28].

Parasitaemia measurement

Antimalarial activity was monitored by daily assessment of thin blood smears prepared from tail blood from day 1 to day 15 pi. The percentage inhibition of parasite development in the treated groups relative to the control group was calculated using the following formula [29]. Mean survival time was calculated on day 15 pi [28, 30]. Smears were fixed in absolute methanol for 2 min, stained with a 10% Giemsa solution in physiological saline (pH 7.2) for 30 min, and examined under a light microscope at 1000 × magnification using oil immersion to determine parasitaemia [27]. Clinical observation and body weight measurement

Clinical signs associated with malaria infection, including ruffled fur, reduced activity, hunching, and piloerection, were assessed daily in mice throughout the 15-day observation period [28, 30]. Body weight was recorded at regular intervals to monitor changes during infection and treatment [32]. Mortality was documented daily. At the end of the observation period, mice were euthanized simultaneously with ketamine (100 mg/kg BW).

Histopathological examination

The organs were collected to assess the effect of Epi-A on histopathological alterations [33]. Liver, spleen, kidney, and intestine samples were preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin prior to histological analysis. The tissues were then routinely processed, sectioned, and stained with Haematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) according to standard procedures at the Diagnostic Laboratory of the Agricultural Instruments Standardization Agency, Ministry of Agriculture, Bogor, Indonesia. Haematoxylin–eosin staining was selected due to its ability to provide clear delineation of tissue architecture, enabling the assessment of oedema, inflammatory cell infiltration, necrosis, parasite sequestration, and pigment deposition within the organs of malaria-infected mice (haemozoin) [30, 34, 35].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). IC₅₀ and CC₅₀ values were calculated using nonlinear regression of log-transformed compound concentrations against the percentage inhibition of parasite or cell growth. In vivo efficacy data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, and differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Discussion

There is a broad consensus that the development of new antimalarial agents that are specific, safe, affordable, and effective is urgently needed to compete with synthetic antimalarial compounds. Increasing attention has been directed toward endophytic fungi as a promising source of structurally unique bioactive compounds. Antimalarial bio-metabolites isolated from these fungi are being explored as a foundation for synthesizing new drugs to combat malaria and address the growing issue of drug resistance [36, 37].

However, studies on these symbiotic organisms and their bioactive metabolites remain limited. In recent decades, a marked decline in the discovery rate of new active chemical entities has been observed. While extensive investigations into plant sources are ongoing to identify novel therapeutic compounds, endophytic microorganisms have gained recognition for their crucial role in the search for natural bioactive substances with potential healthcare and pharmaceutical applications [6, 31, 32]. Despite increasing interest in endophytes as potential antimalarial agents, most investigations have focused on in vitro screening and chemical characterisation. Comprehensive in vivo studies of endophyte-derived antimalarial compounds are still limited. In this context, the present study expands current knowledge by evaluating the antimalarial potential of endophyte-derived compounds through both in vitro and in vivo approaches, highlighting a key novelty of this work. In line with studies using endophytes from Diaporthe species within Indonesia’s rich plant biodiversity, this research reports the first use of Epi-A as an antimalarial agent, tested in vitro and in vivo.

In line with the WHO recommendations and commonly accepted benchmarks in antiparasitic drug discovery, antiplasmodial efficacy is stratified into four levels according to IC₅₀ thresholds: high activity (IC₅₀ ≤ 5 μg/mL), promising activity (5 μg/mL < IC₅₀ ≤ 15 μg/mL), moderate activity (15 μg/mL < IC₅₀ ≤ 50 μg/mL), and weak activity (IC₅₀ > 50 μg/mL). For isolated pure compounds, an IC₅₀ value of ≤ 1 μg/mL is regarded as indicative of strong antimalarial activity [38]. Within this framework, the present in vitro assay indicates that Epi-A is highly active. In assessing potential antimalarial agents, two key parameters must be considered: the CC50 (50% cytotoxic concentration) and the selectivity index (SI). The CC50 denotes the concentration at which a compound is toxic to 50% of host cells. An effective antimalarial candidate should exhibit a high CC50, indicating it can be administered at relatively high doses without causing significant harm to normal cells. This parameter is vital for defining the therapeutic window, ensuring the compound’s efficacy against the parasite while maintaining host safety. The selectivity index, calculated as the ratio of CC50 to IC50 (50% inhibitory concentration) against the parasite, indicates how selectively the compound targets the parasite over host cells [39].

In the context of antimalarial drug development, candidate compounds should exert their primary activity against the blood stages of the parasite, as these forms are responsible for most malaria-associated clinical manifestations. Effective antimalarial agents must achieve adequate systemic exposure, penetrate infected erythrocytes, and reach intracellular parasite targets to selectively disrupt essential biological processes, ultimately leading to rapid parasite clearance. Selectivity, therefore, represents a critical determinant of drug candidacy, and compounds with antimalarial potential are generally expected to exhibit a selectivity index (SI) of 10 or higher. Values greater than 10 were considered indicative of low toxicity, whereas values below 10 were regarded as toxic [40]. Based on this criterion, the Epi-A evaluated in the present study demonstrated a favorable SI value of 18.33 (Table 1). These findings suggest that Epi-A represents a promising candidate for an antimalarial agent and merit further pharmacological and mechanistic investigation.

Mice were monitored for 15 days pi to further evaluate the persistence of antimalarial activity, disease progression, and the potential occurrence of recrudescence following treatment. In the untreated negative control group, mice generally succumbed to infection within 7–10 days pi [41, 42], with mortality occurring on day 11 pi in the present study. Therefore, the 15-day observation period was considered adequate to discriminate between transient parasitemia suppression and sustained therapeutic efficacy. Consistent with this rationale, Groups 1 and 2 maintained reduced parasitemia levels until day 15 pi, whereas Group 3 demonstrated a subsequent increase in parasitemia during the later phase of observation, suggestive of recrudescence. This finding is in agreement with previous reports indicating that recrudescence may occur approximately 10 days after completion of antimalarial treatment [43].

During drug administration, a reduction in parasitaemia was observed across all treatment groups, including the artemisinin control, indicating a rapid onset of antiplasmodial activity of both Epi-A and artemisinin against Plasmodium. Nonetheless, parasitaemia showed a temporary resurgence one day after treatment completion, followed by a reduction by day 3 post-treatment. This pattern may reflect the limited duration of effective systemic exposure, which may be related to a relatively short in vivo half-life. A similar phenomenon has been reported for artemisinin and its derivatives, which exhibit rapid parasite clearance but short plasma half-lives, often necessitating repeated dosing or combination therapy to prevent recrudescence [44]. Epi-A may exert its antimalarial effect through rapid parasite clearance accompanied by a relatively short half-life. This interpretation is consistent with the parasitaemia dynamics observed in the present study, in which parasitaemia fell markedly during the four-day treatment period, briefly increased after treatment cessation, and subsequently declined again by day 11 pi, albeit not to the same extent as during the treatment phase.

In this study, untreated P.berghei-infected mice exhibited a marked increase in parasitaemia followed by a spontaneous decline at later stages of infection. Similar observations have been documented in murine malaria models and are widely recognized as a characteristic feature of severe P.berghei infection rather than an indication of recovery, as reported by Noutong et al. [45], in which the parasitaemia profile of the untreated animal group closely resembled that observed in this study [45].

The transient decrease in parasitaemia is likely attributable to partial immune-mediated mechanisms, including macrophage activation and increased splenic clearance of parasitised erythrocytes, which temporarily constrain parasite proliferation. Moreover, the widespread destruction of both parasitized and non-parasitized erythrocytes results in pronounced anaemia, thereby limiting the availability of host cells for subsequent parasite invasion. Notably, a reduction in parasitaemia has also been observed during the pre-terminal phase of infection, when progressive metabolic dysfunction and organ failure create an unfavourable environment for parasite replication.

The curative evaluation of Epi-A in the present study demonstrated a clear association between parasitaemia, percentage inhibition, and MST. A reduction in parasitaemia was associated with greater suppression of parasite proliferation and prolonged survival in infected mice. Among the treatment groups, group 2 exhibited the most favourable outcome, characterised by the lowest parasitaemia, the highest level of parasite growth inhibition, and a longer MST compared with the negative control group (Group 5). These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that reduced parasite burden is commonly associated with decreased disease severity, including lower anaemia levels, reduced organ damage, and improved survival in malaria-infected hosts [32, 44, 46]. Furthermore, according to Abrham (2019), compounds that significantly prolong the survival time of infected mice relative to the negative control are considered to possess antimalarial activity, as observed in the present study [42]. These findings suggest that Epi-A's effective inhibition of parasite replication may play an important role in reducing malaria-associated pathology and improving host survival.

Epi-A groups exhibited more than 50% inhibition on day 8 pi (Table 2). According to the antimalarial activity classification proposed by Deharo and Munoz et al., in vivo antiplasmodial activity is categorized as moderate, good, or very good when an extract achieves ≥ 50% parasitaemia suppression at doses of 500, 250, and 100 mg/kg BW per day, respectively [47, 48]. Based on this criterion, Epi-A at the tested doses in the present study falls into the very good activity category. However, further studies are required to clarify its mechanism of action and evaluate its efficacy and safety in more advanced experimental models.

Inhibition of parasitaemia in the Epi-A groups was associated with milder clinical manifestations and improved host physiological status compared with the negative control group. Notably, mice receiving Epi-A did not develop severe malaria-associated symptoms, including pronounced anaemia, piloerection, hunching, or ataxia, as observed in group 5 (Table 3). Body weight loss is frequently used as an indicator of disease severity in malaria and is commonly associated with metabolic stress and a compromised host's physiological status. These alterations may occur because of metabolic disturbances and hypoglycaemia induced by Plasmodium infection [42, 49]. Accordingly, an effective antimalarial agent should ideally mitigate weight loss in infected hosts. In the present study, mice treated with Epi-A at different doses exhibited increased body weight by day 15 pi. In contrast, group 5 displayed a sustained decline in body weight throughout the experimental period (Table 3). This observation suggests that Epi-A may help preserve host physiological homeostasis during infection and prevent weight loss, in line with previous antimalarial studies [50–52]. This effect may be attributed to its therapeutic action in suppressing parasitaemia and improving food intake. Furthermore, acute and sub-chronic toxicity evaluations indicated that Epi A has low toxicity [11, 12], supporting its favourable safety profile and potential as a promising antimalarial agent. The preservation of body weight in the Epi-A groups further indicates reduced disease severity during infection. These observations were corroborated by histopathological findings demonstrating relatively limited tissue alterations in the organs of treated mice.

Histopathological assessment provided additional evidence supporting the antimalarial efficacy of Epi-A observed in the present study. Malaria infection is known to induce diverse tissue alterations driven by parasite sequestration, inflammatory responses, and microvascular obstruction within infected organs [51]. The accumulation of parasitised erythrocytes in the microvasculature may impair local blood flow and promote tissue injury, while haemozoin deposition and inflammatory cell infiltration can further exacerbate organ damage during severe infection [52]. Haemozoin reflects its origin as a by-product of haemoglobin degradation by malaria parasites [53]. Moreover, haemozoin serves as a reliable biomarker of malaria infection, being present throughout all erythrocytic stages of Plasmodium species [54].

Malaria is associated with altered iron metabolism due to the dependence of Plasmodium parasites on iron derived from haemoglobin catabolism [55]. Accordingly, haemozoin was observed in the liver of all infected groups. Notably, hepatic iron deposition appeared less extensive in the Epi-A- and artemisinin-treated groups compared with group 5, suggesting a potential modulation of malaria-associated iron accumulation, although haemozoin deposits remained because of the ongoing P. berghei infection [56]. In contrast, no haemozoin deposition was detected in normal liver histology; instead, a typical portal tract, comprising the hepatic vein, hepatic artery, and bile duct, surrounded by hepatocyte was observed in the liver of healthy mice [57].

The spleen, as a secondary lymphoid organ, plays a vital role in immune defence, blood filtration, and the reduction of parasitaemia. Structurally, the spleen consists of red pulp and white pulp, separated by the marginal zone; approximately 76–79% of a healthy spleen is comprised of red pulp. White pulp serves as the primary site for initiating humoral immune responses, especially against circulating antigens, while red pulp filters blood and removes pathogens and damaged red blood cells [58, 59]. In this study, alterations in both the red and white pulp were evident in group 5, including signs of pulp depletion, although the magnitude of splenic injury varied among groups. Haemozoin accumulation was observed across all experimental groups, with varying degrees of severity. The relatively mild splenic damage in Epi-A–treated groups suggests protection against immune-mediated pathology associated with P. berghei infection. Widespread haemozoin deposition has been linked to increasing parasitaemia in infected mice. These findings suggest that high parasitaemia, as reflected by abundant haemozoin formation, may suppress the immune system by impairing macrophage function [60] and enhancing host cytokine release [52], which, in turn, may contribute to the manifestation of clinical symptoms. As shown in Table 3, group 5 exhibited the most severe clinical manifestations, while the Epi-A–treated groups showed comparatively milder outcomes. This may be associated with Epi-A treatment, however, its effects on macrophage function and cytokine release require further investigation (Table 4).

One of the principal clinical hallmarks of severe malaria in humans is acute kidney injury, which may arise from obstruction of the renal microvasculature by P. falciparum-infected erythrocytes, thereby eliciting immune-mediated pathological responses within the glomeruli. In line with the present study, P. berghei likewise induced renal impairment. Groups 1–3 exhibited only mild renal alterations, characterised by minimal dilatation of Bowman’s capsule. In contrast, group 5 demonstrated severe renal pathology, including marked dilatation of both the proximal tubules and Bowman’s capsule. These findings suggest that Epi-A may reduce the severity of malaria-associated renal pathology. Furthermore, the histopathological changes observed in the artemisinin-treated group were less severe than those reported by Maslachah et al. [63] following repeated artemisinin administration, in which marked lesions, including cast formation, glomerulonephritis, tubular necrosis, and cortical congestion, were observed [61]. Notably, such pathological features were absent in the present study. This difference may be attributed to the fact that, in the present study, artemisinin was administered once daily for 4 consecutive days, resulting in milder renal pathological changes than with repeated dosing.

Infection with P. falciparum has been associated with more severe upper gastrointestinal complications than P. vivax, potentially due to intestinal mucosal disruption and increased gastrointestinal permeability. These pathological alterations may manifest clinically as nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhoea, and are thought to arise from a complex cascade involving sequestration of parasitized erythrocytes within the splanchnic microcirculation [62]. Plasmodium berghei is widely used as an experimental model because it shares key pathophysiological features with P. falciparum, including parasite sequestration in multiple organs such as the brain, lungs, spleen, liver, kidneys, and small intestine [63, 64]. Consistent with the present study's findings, parasite sequestration was observed in both the liver and the intestine.

The gastrointestinal microenvironment represents a key site of interaction for Plasmodium parasites and associated pathological processes [65]. Research on malaria-induced gastrointestinal pathology remains limited, with the most recent comprehensive study reported by Shimada et al. [68], who documented intestinal damage following P. berghei infection. These findings support the suitability of P. berghei as an experimental model due to its close pathophysiological resemblance to P. falciparum, including its capacity to affect intestinal tissue [66]. P. berghei parasites were detected in the intestinal tissue of group 4 in the present study (Fig. 5). In contrast, the Epi-A–treated groups exhibited preserved intestinal architecture. The increased abundance of goblet cells observed in group 4 may reflect an enhanced mucosal defence response, as goblet cells secrete mucus that protects the intestinal epithelium from microbial invasion, including Plasmodium. Parasites may therefore be trapped within the mucus layer and eliminated through peristalsis and faecal excretion. These findings indicate that Epi-A may limit Plasmodium sequestration across multiple organs, including the intestine, which could be relevant to mechanisms underlying parasite persistence and recrudescence observed in human malaria [67]. In humans, intestinal sequestration can lead to vascular damage and subsequent gastrointestinal complications, including impaired gastric motility or dilatation [68]. The present findings suggest that Epi-A may mitigate malaria-associated gastric alterations, however, its effects on gastric motility in humans remain to be established.

Furthermore, as a novel compound, Epi-A appears to possess a favourable safety profile, particularly when compared with several established antimalarial drugs known to cause significant adverse effects. Nonetheless, further investigations are necessary to comprehensively elucidate its mechanism of action. The antifungal activity is mediated by disruption of the cell membrane, leading to the efflux of cytoplasmic components and ions, as indicated by elevated Ca2⁺ and K⁺ levels [9]. Epi-A exerts its antibacterial activity mainly by targeting the bacterial cell membrane. It disrupts membrane integrity, resulting in enhanced cation efflux while largely preserving cytoplasmic contents. Moreover, Epi-A induces cellular damage, as indicated by small perforations on the bacterial surface [10].

As with many hydrophobic natural product-derived compounds [69], Epi-A also exhibits limited water solubility. Therefore, Epi-A was formulated with CMC-Na to improve its solubility. The results demonstrate that formulation with CMC-Na improved dispersion homogeneity and enhanced therapeutic performance compared with earlier preparations. CMC-Na is widely used across a range of applications, particularly in drug delivery systems. CMC-based hydrogels have gained considerable attention as safe drug carriers due to their non-toxic, biodegradable, biocompatible, and low-immunogenic properties [70]. Consistently, Jeong [71] reported that CMC-Na is biocompatible, exhibits minimal toxicity, and is suitable for drug delivery applications [71].

Although CMC-Na improves formulation uniformity, maintaining this homogeneity at higher concentrations remains problematic. In the present study, the highest dose (50 mg/kg BW) resulted in the lowest parasitaemia among the treatment groups. This may indicate that the compound's effective concentration diminishes upon reaching the target organs, thereby limiting its efficacy. To address this issue, further research into optimised drug delivery approaches is required. Reformulation into nanoparticle-based systems, for example, may enhance stability and bioavailability, ensuring that sufficient concentrations are delivered to the target site and thereby improving antimalarial efficacy [72, 73].

Taken together, the in vitro and in vivo data indicate that Epi-A exhibits promising antimalarial activity. This is supported by a low IC₅₀ value against P. falciparum 3D7, along with CC₅₀ and selectivity index values indicating preferential activity against the parasite with minimal cytotoxicity to mammalian cells. These results were corroborated by in vivo outcomes, including reduced parasitaemia, prolonged survival, and attenuated histopathological organ damage compared with untreated controls. The relatively mild histopathological alterations observed in the liver, spleen, and kidneys of Epi-A-treated mice further indicate that Epi-A may represent a safer therapeutic alternative. Based on an inhibition rate exceeding 50%, reflecting its ability to suppress P. berghei development by more than half, Epi-A can be categorised as a highly effective antimalarial agent.

Moreover, as a novel compound, Epi-A appears to have a favourable safety profile, in contrast to several established antimalarial drugs that are associated with notable adverse effects. Nevertheless, further studies are required to elucidate its mechanism of action fully.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The in vitro assay employed microscopic quantification of parasitaemia rather than alternative quantitative methods, such as lactate dehydrogenase (pLDH) assay, Histidine‐Rich Protein‐2 (HRP-2), or fluorescence-based assays, which offer greater sensitivity and throughput [74–76]. Future investigations incorporating inbred models and enzymatic or fluorescence-based assays are warranted to enhance precision and reproducibility. Artemisinin was selected as the positive control, although P. berghei–infected mice are generally more responsive to chloroquine [17, 77]. This decision was made to reflect the contemporary clinical relevance of artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) as the global standard for malaria treatment. The use of Swiss Webster mice, an outbred strain, may have contributed to inter-individual variability; however, this approach also provides a broader representation of host genetic diversity. Furthermore, histopathological evaluation was conducted using a single representative mouse from each group. As this was an initial exploratory study, the objective was to generate baseline data on treatment-related organ changes in comparison with the control groups. Moreover, Epi-A is already supported by baseline histopathological data derived from subchronic toxicity studies.

Conclusion

This study underscores the considerable antimalarial potential of Epi-A, as evidenced by both in vitro and in vivo findings. In the in vitro evaluation, Epi-A was categorised as a potent antimalarial, with a selectivity index (SI) exceeding 10, reflecting its preferential activity against the parasite relative to host cells. In the in vivo assessment, Epi-A was classified as a very good antimalarial. This was associated with post-treatment increases in body weight across all Epi-A–treated groups, milder clinical signs compared with the negative control, and comparatively less pronounced histopathological changes in the liver, spleen, kidneys, and gastrointestinal tract.
