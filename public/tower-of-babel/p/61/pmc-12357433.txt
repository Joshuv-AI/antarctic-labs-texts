Relationship between early neurological function and motor skills in Brazilian preterm infants: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wendy Gabrielle Franca de Sousa, Ana Luiza Righetto Greco, Maja de Medeiros, Alicia Jane Spittle, Cibelle Kayenne Roberto Formiga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreterm infants have an increased risk of neurodevelopmental delays, with consequences that may persist in later life. Thus, monitoring their development is needed for early detection and targeted intervention. This study aimed to assess the correlation between neurological function and motor skills in preterm infants from 2 to 6 months of corrected age after hospital discharge in an outpatient follow-up program.MethodsThis cross-sectional study involved 50 preterm infants with a mean gestational age of 32.0 (± 2.8) weeks and a mean birth weight of 1645.2 (± 576.5) grams. Neurological function and motor development were assessed once between two to six months of corrected age by using the Hammersmith Infant Neurological Examination (HINE) and Alberta Infant Motor Scale (AIMS), respectively. Data on pregnancy, type of delivery, and birth were obtained using interviews with family and medical records. The Spearman correlation coefficient assessed the association between HINE and AIMS scores.ResultsA significant correlation was found between HINE categories and AIMS subscales: cranial nerve function versus prone (r = 0.55; p < 0.001); posture versus prone (r = 0.69; p < 0.001); posture versus sitting (r = 0.67; p < 0.001); posture versus standing (r = 0.63; p < 0.001); tone versus sitting (r = 0.51; p < 0.001); tone versus standing (r = 0.49; p < 0.001); and total HINE versus total AIMS (r = 0.77; p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe present study found strong correlation between neurological function and motor skills in preterm infants during the first months of life. Whilst the HINE and AIMS can complete each other, where there are limited resources the HINE may be appropriate to administer as a single assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-05942-9.Keywords: Neurodevelopmental disorder, Neurological assessment, Motor skills, Prematurity, Preterm infant

Background

Preterm infants are at risk of developmental impairments due to interrupted intrauterine developmental processes, requiring individualized follow-up and targeted interventions at appropriate time [1, 2]. These infants face higher chances of adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes compared to full-term infants during both early and later development [3–6]. Early neurodevelopmental delays may be seen in preterm infants who go on to have a diagnosis of cerebral palsy (CP), developmental coordination disorder, and/or sensory impairment [7–9]. For this reason, specific assessment tools have been used to evaluate neurodevelopmental outcomes in this population [10].

The Hammersmith Infant Neurological Examination (HINE) has been shown effectively to predict long-term neurological and cognitive outcomes in preterm infants, such as identifying the risk for CP and cognitive delays in preterm infants, particularly when combined with the Bayley Scales of Infant Development [11]. Additionally, the HINE has proven to be a valuable tool for early identification of neurodevelopmental risks [12, 13]. Similarly, the Alberta Infant Motor Scale (AIMS) is a valuable tool for evaluating motor performance, showing that preterm infants, particularly those with brain injuries, exhibit worse motor performance than their full-term peers [14–16]. However, preterm infants have different gross motor trajectories on the AIMS from those of term infants [10].

Due to the increased risks of neurodevelopmental impairment, preterm infants should be monitored through follow-up programs which consist of periodic evaluation using specific and sensitive assessment tools to enable the early detection of neurodevelopmental delays. Although many assessment tools have better predictive value at older ages due to the maturation of neurological functions with age, waiting until these later ages to assess may not be an appropriate approach [10]. The combination of different tools allows a systematic approach, enhancing the comprehension of neurodevelopment and early detection of developmental delays [5, 17]. Nonetheless, in ambulatory settings - particularly in developing countries - where there is a high demand for the evaluation of at-risk infants and a limited availability of trained professionals, applying multiple assessment tools is often impractical due to time and resource constraints.

Rather than aiming to establish the superiority of combining the HINE and AIMS, the present study sought to investigate the degree of correlation between these two widely used tools. Understanding this relationship may have important practical implications, particularly in settings where only one of the instruments is available. In such contexts, demonstrating a correlation between HINE and AIMS scores could support the use of either tool as a viable alternative for monitoring neurodevelopmental status during early infancy.

This raises an important question: could the HINE serve as a more suitable tool for assessing preterm infants at younger ages in ambulatory settings, while also facilitating a clear and effective communication regarding neurological and motor development status to caregivers and supporting early, targeted interventions? While previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of both the HINE and AIMS in assessing specific developmental domains, evidence regarding the correlation between neurological functions and motor skills in preterm infants during the early months of corrected age remains limited. To address this gap, the present study aimed to verify the correlation between neurological functions and motor skills in preterm infants aged 2 to 6 months of corrected age following hospital discharge, in an outpatient follow-up program.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was approved by the research ethics committee of the Universidade Estadual de Goiás and Clinics Hospital of the Universidade Federal de Goiás (Goiânia), Brazil (no. 42042820.8.0000.8113). The study followed the Brazilian ethical principles and the Declaration of Helsinki. All caregivers of the participants signed the informed consent form.

Participants

A total of 50 preterm infants were eligible for an outpatient follow-up program between August 2021 and November 2023. Inclusion criteria were preterm infants born at less than or equal to 36 weeks and 6 days (ranging from 25 to 36 weeks and 6 days) of gestational age, aged 2 to 6 months of corrected age, enrolled in the follow-up program after hospital discharge and assessed using HINE and AIMS on the same day of outpatient attendance. Exclusion criteria were infants with incomplete data in the database, multiple malformations or congenital syndromes, or requiring gastrostomy or tracheostomy. Not all preterm infants followed in the outpatient program were included in the final study sample, as some infants presented for assessment outside the predefined corrected age window. Consequently, only those meeting the inclusion criteria within the appropriate age range were evaluated and included in the analysis.

Instruments

Sociodemographic, family income, pregnancy, delivery, maternal, and neonatal data (gestational age, birthweight, sex, Apgar score, clinical conditions, and duration and location of hospitalization) were collected using medical records and interviews with the caregivers. All information was recorded on an evaluation form.

The Brazilian version of the HINE [18–20] was used to assess the neurodevelopment of all infants during their outpatient attendance. The HINE is a gold-standard, highly sensitive instrument for detecting the risk of CP or developmental abnormalities. It consists of 37 items divided into three sections. The first section comprises 26 items, each scored from 0 to 3, with a maximum total score of 78 points. This section assesses five neurological functions: cranial nerve function (5 items; a maximum score of 15 points), posture (6 items; 18 points), movement (2 items; 6 points), tone (8 items; 24 points), and reflexes and reactions (5 items; 15 points). The second section comprises 8 items to assess motor function based on motor milestones, and the third section includes 3 items related to the behavioural state of the infant. The items in sections two and three are not scored in the test. A score ≥ 67 points at three months or ≥ 70 points at six months is considered optimal [21, 22]; scores ≤ 58 points at three months or ≤ 64 at six months indicate a risk for CP [11, 23, 24]; and scores ≤ 40 at three months suggest the presence of CP [17, 22]. Corrected age is recommended for preterm infants. It is valid from 2 to 24 months.

To assess gross motor development was used the Brazilian version of the AIMS [25, 26]. The AIMS is a standardized observational tool assessing gross motor development in infants from birth to 18 months or until independent walking. It consists of 58 items divided into four subscales: prone (21 items), supine (9 items), sitting (12 items), and standing (16 items). The total score is calculated by summing the points in each subscale, ranging from 0 to 58. The total raw score (with the chronological or corrected age) determines whether motor development is within the typical development percentile. The percentiles are divided into three classifications: < 5th percentile, indicating abnormal motor performance; between 5th and 25th percentiles, indicating suspicious motor performance; and > 25th percentile, indicating normal motor performance. Interrater reliability was set at 0.99 [25].

Procedures

After hospital discharge, infants were referred for the follow-up program for high-risk infants at the outpatient unit.

The HINE assessment was performed first, immediately followed by the AIMS on the same day during the follow-up appointment. Each evaluation was carried out jointly by at least two trained physiotherapists, who applied the instruments collaboratively and reached a consensus on scoring. The examiners were blinded to birth weight status and obstetric/perinatal characteristics. The infant was placed on a soft mat, and sound and visually attractive objects were used to encourage and stimulate the infant to move and perform postural transitions (prone, supine, sitting, and standing), allowing the demonstration of all motor skills. Infants remained active and alert state [27], and accompanied by their caregivers during all assessments. In case of lack of collaboration, the assessment was paused, and a second attempt was performed after the caregiver comforted the infant. The total duration of each assessment was approximately 30 min.

Data analysis

After collection, each data was coded and plotted into a spreadsheet according to chronological age, corrected age for prematurity, specific score in each item, final score, and classification in the assessments. Dependent variables were the total score from HINE and its categories (cranial nerve function, posture, movement, muscle tone, and reflexes and reactions) and from AIMS and its subscales (prone, supine, sitting, and standing). Neonatal, maternal, and sociodemographic data were considered independent variables.

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample and neurodevelopment and were presented as mean, standard deviation, and minimum and maximum values for continuous variables and absolute and relative frequency for discrete variables. The Levene and Shapiro-Wilk tests verified data homogeneity and normality, which did not meet the assumptions of normality. The Spearman correlation coefficient assessed the correlation between the scores from HINE categories and AIMS subscales. The strength of the correlation was interpreted as weak (0.10–0.29), moderate (0.30–0.49), and strong (0.50–1.0) [28]. Associations between categorical classifications of HINE (optimal, risk for cerebral palsy, and cerebral palsy) and AIMS (normal, suspicious, and motor delay) were evaluated using the Chi-square test of independence. Data were arranged and analyzed using SPSS version 25.0, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The results demonstrated a strong correlation between neurological functions and motor skills, suggesting that poorer neurological functions in HINE categories corresponded to low performances in AIMS subscales, especially antigravity postures (prone, sitting, and standing).

Most infants presented a risk for CP (62%) and motor delay (54%). Similarly, previous studies showed lower scores of neurodevelopment in preterm infants than in term infants between one month and two years [3, 4] and persistent delay in gross motor function, suggesting that motor sequelae may persist beyond the first year of life due to prematurity [5]. The high rate of infants classified as “at risk” for cerebral palsy in our study can be explained by the characteristics of our sample, which consisted of preterm infants with a history of neonatal intensive care and complications such as sepsis and abnormal cranial ultrasound findings, factors known to negatively impact neurodevelopment. We acknowledge that these clinical conditions may at least partially account for the elevated proportion of infants identified as at risk in our study. The identification of a greater number of infants as “at risk” also reflects the high sensitivity of the HINE at this age range (approximately 90%) [22]. Low scores during early infancy may reflect transient neurodevelopmental delays and do not necessarily indicate a definitive diagnosis. It is also important to consider that the sensitivity and specificity of the HINE are not as high in younger infants as in older age groups, which may contribute to the identification of a greater number of false positives at this stage. Nonetheless, the HINE is a tool for risk detection, not for definitive diagnosis, which reinforces the need for continuous clinical follow-up to ensure timely early interventions and to prevent the neglect of cases that may indeed evolve into persistent developmental delays.

Preterm infants commonly present increased risk for neonatal complications (e.g., late-onset neonatal sepsis), as evidenced by Stylianou-Riga et al. [29]. Neonatal complications related to respiratory distress syndrome, intraventricular hemorrhage, and retinopathy of prematurity may present an important association with low motor performance, suggesting greater neurological vulnerability [4]. Also, they may contribute to neurodevelopmental delay due to organs and systems immaturity [30–32]. These findings are similar to the present study since the most common neonatal complications were neonatal sepsis (42%) and cranial ultrasound abnormalities (80.4%). The presence of abnormalities in imaging tests was associated with functional deficits in the General Movement Assessment and HINE in infants between three and four months of corrected age [33]. Thus, low neurodevelopment was expected in the present study, even at young ages.

The cranial nerve function was moderately correlated with the prone posture. These data were partially consistent with previous results evidencing that infants with low scores in the cranial nerve function may have a three-fold chance of presenting motor delays at three months of corrected age [34]. The assessment of cranial nerve function evaluates responses to visual stimuli (black and white target) and auditory stimuli (rattle). For a high score, the infant needs to integrate visual and auditory stimuli with head movements, demonstrating coordinated bilateral responses corresponding to the development of primary sensorimotor coordination.

Preterm infants have increased risk for sensorimotor impairments related to hearing and vision [35] and other consequences [30]. Considering that sensorimotor coordination reflects the interaction with the environment and is the basis for the development of intentional movements at late ages, an inadequate response to visual and sound stimuli might negatively impact motor performance.

Low scores in posture and tone were also correlated with poor motor performance in the prone, sitting, and standing. The posture assesses asymmetries in the head, trunk, upper and lower limbs, and feet in the sitting posture, while the tone assesses the presence of changes, such as hypo or hypertonia. High-risk infants may present abnormal tone detected at six months of age, which tends to resolve by 12 months of age [36]. These transient tone abnormalities may affect the development of postural alignment and acquisition of motor skills. For instance, preterm infants presented a delay in the development of trunk control, and the ability of independent sitting occurred later than in their term infants [6]. Thus, the interaction between neurological functions and motor development is crucial for the acquisition and refinement of motor skills.

We observed a correlation between the total scores of the HINE and the AIMS, indicating that lower neurological function scores are associated with poorer gross motor performance. Similarly, the association analysis demonstrated convergence between the HINE and AIMS in identifying infants who require clinical attention. Although the tools assess different constructs, the results showed that 62% of the infants were classified by the HINE as being at risk for cerebral palsy (using cut-off scores of ≤ 58 at 3 months and ≤ 64 at 6 months), while 56% demonstrated motor delay according to the AIMS. In this regard, infants identified as at risk for cerebral palsy by the HINE presented a higher prevalence of motor delay (64.5%) and suspected motor development (35.5%) on the AIMS. No infants were classified as having cerebral palsy (score ≤ 40) according to the HINE. These findings suggest that, despite measuring distinct developmental domains, there is significant clinical overlap between the instruments in detecting infants who require referral for early intervention. This overlap indicates that, in practice, both tools tend to support similar clinical decision-making, particularly in the context of monitoring infants with biological risk factors.

These findings reinforce the validity between the two tools and demonstrate consistency in the results obtained. While the HINE provides a detailed assessment of neurological aspects, the AIMS is used for the observational analysis of gross motor development.

However, it is important to note that the predictive validity of the AIMS for detecting developmental delays is greater from 4 months of age onwards [10, 26]. During the first months of life, motor repertoire is less variable, which can make it difficult to distinguish between an actual motor delay and a reduced motor repertoire due to the immaturity of the central nervous system. Although these tools assess different constructs, it is important to emphasize that the HINE and AIMS can be used complementarity for early neurodevelopmental monitoring.

The HINE is a tool that assesses both neurological and motor aspects and has predictive power for cerebral palsy. Although its application is quick, it requires specific training to ensure standardized administration and reliable results, a factor that may limit its use in settings with a shortage of trained professionals, such as public health services in low- and middle-income countries. The AIMS, in turn, is restricted to the motor domain but allows for the observation of motor patterns across different postures, which can provide relevant clinical insights. Each instrument contributes differently to the identification of infants at higher risk for atypical development; therefore, the combined use of the HINE and AIMS is recommended in clinical settings to support more accurate decisions regarding referral to early intervention programs [37, 38]. In contexts where it is necessary to choose a single tool, we recommend the use of the HINE in early ages, as it evaluates both neurological and motor aspects, while the AIMS is limited to the motor domain. In situations where an assessor is not trained in the HINE and/or telehealth is needed the AIMS could be a suitable alternative.

Early identification of possible neurological changes and delays in neurodevelopment of preterm infants is essential for referral to intervention programs, which improve prognoses and sensorimotor development. Also, the findings may contribute to clinical practice by supporting the use of specific instruments for assessing preterm infants, reinforcing the need for continuous monitoring due to prematurity.

Despite the relevant findings of this study, we acknowledge several limitations. First, the monitoring of neurodevelopmental progression in preterm infants over time was limited due to the cross-sectional design of the study. Additionally, although neonatal variables were collected, they were not included in the statistical analyses, which may have limited the identification of potential factors associated with the classifications obtained from the HINE and AIMS assessments. Moreover, detailed information regarding the type, severity, and timing of neonatal complications such as transfontanellar ultrasound abnormalities, early and late-onset sepsis, and bronchopulmonary dysplasia was not collected. While these clinical variables could provide a more comprehensive characterization of the sample, their absence is unlikely to compromise the interpretation of the main findings since they were not included as independent variables. Given that such characteristics can significantly influence the neurological and motor development of preterm infants, we suggest that future studies adopt longitudinal designs and multivariate analyses to further explore the impact of neonatal and sociodemographic variables on child development outcomes. Finally, we recognize as a limitation the fact that the assessors were aware of the HINE results at the time of the AIMS application. Although they were blinded to the participants’ medical history, prior knowledge of the HINE scores may have introduced a confirmation bias in the interpretation of motor skills on the AIMS. We recommend that future studies consider administering the assessments with different evaluators to minimize potential bias.

Conclusion

This study found a correlation between neurological functions and motor skills in preterm infants during the first months of life, suggesting that low scores on the HINE were correlated with low scores on the AIMS, particularly in antigravity postures. Furthermore, the findings suggest that while the HINE and AIMS complement each other in assessing these infants, the HINE may serve as a viable tool for initial screening by healthcare professionals in resource-limited settings, optimize long-term motor outcomes, and promote more effective and accessible clinical follow-up.
