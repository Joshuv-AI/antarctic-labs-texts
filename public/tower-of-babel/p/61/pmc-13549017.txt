Toward the Synthesis of Pestalustaine A: Structural Revision and Formation of Original Strained Tricyclic Architectures
Authors: Wei Cao, Alicia Ross, Jean‐Pierre Baltaze, Régis Guillot, Menglin Xu, Rongzhen Qin, Bingcheng Yang, Isabel Wong, Katayoon Formul, Cyrille Kouklovsky, Dean J Tantillo, Vincent Gandon, Guillaume Vincent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report our quest toward the total synthesis of the reported structure of the sesquiterpene pestalustaine A and its unprecedented tricyclic skeleton. Our retrosynthesis involved a transannular Michael addition of a 1,3‐keto‐ester and enal‐containing bicyclo[4.4.1]undec‐3‐ene obtained from the ring expansion of a chiral γ,γ−disubstituted cyclohexanone and stereoselective Hosomi‐Sakurai reaction. Serendipity allowed us to uncover powerful divergent and unexpected cyclizations of this bicycle in ionic or oxidative conditions, giving access to complex tricyclic architectures such as tricyclo[5.2.1.12,5]undecane, octahydro‐1,5‐methanonaphthalene, octahydro‐12,6‐methanonaphthalene frameworks, and the octahydro‐1,5‐methanoazulene reported skeleton of pestalustaine A. The present synthetic study also led us to note a discrepancy between the reported structure of pestalustaine A and the 2D NMR data, which led us to propose a revised structure of the natural product to a caryophyllene‐type framework, a reassignment supported by computational prediction of NMR chemical shifts.Keywords: cyclization, Michael addition, natural products, sesquiterpenes, structure elucidation

Introduction

Pestalustaine A is a sesquiterpenoid isolated from pestalotiopis adusta, an endophytic fungus [1]. The proposed structure 1 of this natural product is unprecedented, with a novel 7,5,6‐fused and bridged tricyclic skeleton that displays six stereocenters, including three quaternary carbons (Scheme 1). The biosynthesis of the proposed octahydro‐1,5‐methanoazulene structure 1 of pestalustaine A has been postulated to arise from farnesyl pyrophosphate (2), a pivotal intermediate in the biosynthesis of sesquiterpenes. Hypothetically, cyclization of farnesyl pyrophosphate (2) through nerolidyl cation 3 might proceed via a very uncommon 1,7‐cyclization through secondary carbocation 4 to deliver cycloheptadiene 5 [2, 3, 4, 5]. It might be followed by an intriguing intramolecular (3 + 2) annulation between one of the alkenes of the 7‐membered ring and the appended allylic motif via an undefined mechanism [1]. The tricyclic skeleton 6 thus formed would then be subjected to a threefold oxidation to generate the secondary allylic alcohol and the two primary alcohols.

Proposed structure of pestalustaine A (1) and its hypothetical biosynthesis.

This original and strained terpenic skeleton of pestalustaine A (1) makes it an ambitious target for total synthesis despite the lack of reported synthetic studies [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. We were thus eager to tackle the total synthesis of this challenging target. Therefore, we devised a retrosynthesis in which the triol of the natural product would be obtained via the reduction of three carbonyl groups, namely an α,β‐unsaturated ketone, an ester, and an aldehyde or a second ester from 7 (Scheme 2). The C5═C6 double bond would be accessed through α,β‐desaturation of the cycloheptanone moiety of the tricyclic framework, and the quaternary carbon at C11 would arise from the stereoselective methylation of the enolate of 8. The pivotal step of our retrosynthesis would be the transannular 1,4‐conjugate addition of the 1,3‐ketoester function into the Michael acceptor moiety of the bicyclo[4.4.1]undec‐3‐ene 9 within the 10‐membered ring. The latter would be constructed by ring closing metathesis (RCM) of 10, which should itself be obtained from a diastereoselective 1,4‐addition of a carbonyl‐containing allylmetal‐reagent 11 to δ,δ‐disubstituted cycloheptenone 12. Finally, the latter and its homoallylic quaternary carbon would arise from the ring expansion of the known γ,γ‐disubstituted cyclohexenone [12, 13, 14, 15] 14 with methyl diazoacetate 13.

Results and Discussion

On the one hand, (±)‐cyclohexenone 14 was obtained from 1,3‐cyclohexanedione 15 via double alkylation of the corresponding enol ether 16 with allyl bromide and methyl iodide followed by Stork−Danheiser‐type manipulation via reduction of (±)‐17 with lithium aluminum hydride and acidic treatment [12]. On the other hand, Stoltz's enantioselective decarboxylative allylic alkylation of (±)‐allyl‐1,3‐ketoester 18 eventually delivered (−)‐14 and its quaternary stereocenter with an 87% enantiomeric excess after reduction of (+)‐17 (Scheme 3) [13, 14, 15].

Ring expansion for the synthesis of cycloheptenone 12. Abbreviations: DABCO = 1,4‐diazabicyclo[2.2.2]octane, HMPA = hexamethylphosphoramide, LDA = lithium diisopropylamide, pmdba = bis(p‐methoxybenzylidene)acetone, rt = room temperature, THF = tetrahydrofuran.

The access of δ,δ‐disubstituted cycloheptenone 12 was then granted through ring expansion of the thus obtained γ,γ‐disubstituted cyclohexenone 14 via its reaction with methyl diazoacetate 13 in the presence of trifluoroborane etherate [16, 17, 18]. Activation of the carbonyl by the Lewis acid (A) triggers the addition of the diazoacetate as a nucleophile, leading to diazonium intermediate B. 1,2‐Migration of the electron‐rich sp2 carbon is favored over the sp3 carbon, leading to a 1:9 mixture of two 7‐membered rings: the undesired γ,γ‐disubstituted cycloheptenone 19 and the expected δ,δ‐disubstituted‐β,γ‐unsaturated‐1,3‐ketoester 20. Treatment of this mixture in basic conditions led to conjugative isomerization of the major compound to the δ,δ‐disubstituted‐α,β‐unsaturated‐1,3‐ketoester 12. This ring expansion‐based strategy has the advantage of obtaining an enantioenriched quaternary carbon in a non‐activated position, that is, between two CH2 groups.

Introduction of a methacrylate moiety to 12 was then envisioned via a 1,4‐conjugate addition (Scheme 4). Unfortunately, neither the radical‐mediated reaction from methyl 2‐(tributyltinmethyl)acrylate 11a [19] nor the Hosomi‐Sakurai reaction [20] with methyl 2‐(trimethylsilylmethyl)acrylate 11b was successful [21]. We hypothesized that the latter allyl silane was too deactivated due to the presence of the ester group. Therefore, we selected 2‐(trimethylsiloxymethyl)allyltrimethylsilane 21 as a more nucleophilic reagent in the Hosomi‐Sakurai reaction, after which oxidation of the resulting alcohol could lead to the same product as the use of 11a,b [22]. Fortunately, with the help of copper triflate as a Lewis acid, the desired Michael addition was successful, leading to a mixture of four diastereoisomers (±)‐22a‐d in a 3:3:1:1 ratio, with the two major ones 22a,b, displaying the desired cis relationship between the two allyl groups. The inseparable mixture of diastereoisomers was subjected to ring closing metathesis with the Grubbs’ second‐generation catalyst, and the desired bicyclo[4.4.1]undec‐3‐ene skeleton as alcohol (±)‐23 was obtained [23, 24]. Its structure was resolved by x‐ray diffraction and displayed a trans relationship between the ester and the cycloheptene ring [25]. It appeared that only the cis‐trans diastereoisomer 22a was converted to a bicyclic compound in the presence of the Grubbs II catalyst; the cis‐cis diastereoisomer 22b did not cyclize, which could probably be explained by an unfavorable steric interaction between the ester and the adjacent allyl group. Attempts to epimerize 22b into 22a did not succeed. Indeed, the two minor diastereoisomers 22c,d, in which the two allyl groups are trans, did not undergo ring closing metathesis. Oxidation of allylic alcohol (±)‐23 with Dess‐Martin periodinane led to enal (±)‐9 [25]. For the Hosomi–Sakurai addition of 21 to enantioenriched (−)‐12, the deployment of a chiral ligand of the copper catalyst was envisioned in order to improve the cis/trans (22a,b/22c,d) ratio in favor of the former [26]. The use of the (R,R)‐t‐Bu bisoxazoline ligand [(R,R)‐BOX)] led to a mismatch effect with a diminished cis‐selective 1,4‐addition, which resulted in a lower yield of (−)‐23 over two steps after the ring closing metathesis and erosion of the enantiomeric excess from 87% ee of (−)‐12 to 82% ee of (−)‐23. Indeed, a match effect was observed with the enantiomeric [(S,S)‐BOX)] ligand, improving the cis‐selective 1,4‐addition and thus the overall yield of (−)‐23. Enantioenrichment was observed with isolation of (−)‐23 in 92% ee from 87% ee of (−)‐12 according to Horeau's principle [27].

Synthesis of the 7,7‐bridged bicycle. Abbreviations: AIBN = azobisisobutyronitrile, ee = enantiomeric excess, TBAF = tetrabutylammonium fluoride, TMS = trimethylsilyl.

The stage was thus set to evaluate the formation of the tricyclic framework and the target compound (±)‐8 via deprotonation of the theoretically more acidic 1,3‐ketoester of (±)‐9 and its transannular Michael addition into the enal (Scheme 5) [11, 28, 29]. To our surprise, in various conditions, another mode of cyclization was observed. As exemplified with cesium carbonate, alternative compound (±)‐24 was obtained via deprotonation in the γ‐position of the enal and 1,2‐addition of the resulting extended enolate C to the ketone. The addition of a Lewis acid such as Cu(OTf)2 that would activate the keto‐ester and facilitate the formation of the corresponding enolate in the presence of cesium carbonate did not lead to desired compound 8 or to (±)‐24 (see supporting information).

Original synthesis of tricyclic architectures. Abbreviations: DME = 1,2‐dimethoxyethane.

Reasoning that the desired Michael addition leading to 8 might be reversible, we used trimethylsilyl triflate in the presence of triethylamine in order to trap the potential enol of 8. However, compound (±)‐24 was also obtained in 60% yield.

Nevertheless, it is highly rewarding that compound (±)‐24 possesses the octahydro‐1,5‐methanoazulene skeleton of compound 8 and of the presumed structure 1 of pestalustaine A, despite that its substitution pattern does not correspond to the target compound. We also considered the use of secondary amines such as the Hayashi‐Jorgensen organocatalyst to induce the desired transannular Michael addition into 8, but without success (see Supporting Information).

We then investigated the use of in situ generated nickel‐allyl active catalyst to perform a cycloalkenylation using the Newhouse conditions [30]. Surprisingly, this led to a totally unexpected and intriguing compound whose tricyclo[5.2.1.12,5]undecane structure (±)‐25 was resolved via x‐ray diffraction of a crystal [25]. This original caged‐structure holds potential in medicinal chemistry due to the importance of compact, rigid, three‐dimensional, sp3‐rich polycyclic hydrocarbons in drug design [31, 32, 33]. It should be noted that in the absence of NiBr2(dme) and of diethyl allyl phosphate, the formation of 25 was not observed. We were puzzled by the transformation that had taken place. Nevertheless, we could propose the following mechanism via a cascade process. Formation of the less substituted nickel enolate D of the ketone could be followed by its transannular migratory insertion into the enal, leading to intermediate E. Addition of the resulting nickel enolate onto the aldehyde would be followed by addition of the resulting alkoxide of F into the ketone. A strain release would induce the C‐C bond cleavage of the former keto‐ester liberating nickel‐enolate G. Elimination of the acetate of the latter would induce the formation of the carboxylic acid and of the α,β‐unsaturated ester moieties to deliver (±)‐25.

Despite all of our efforts, formation of the enolate between the ketone and the ester does not seem possible. This might be explained by the inaccessibility of the corresponding hydrogen, which is trapped on the concave face of bridged‐bicycle (±)‐9.

Overall, the apparent impossibility of generating a reactive nucleophilic entity at C3 between the ketone and the ester has allowed access to complex tricyclic structures.

Nevertheless, we were still eager to connect the C3 position of the 1,3‐ketoester with the C2‐position. We thus turned our attention to the generation of radical species H at C3 between the ketone and the ester via single electron transfer (SET) and its transannular addition to the C2═C11 cyclic alkene of allylic alcohol 23 to lead to compounds 26a,b and the tricyclic architecture of pestalustaine A (Scheme 6). Indeed, oxidation of 1,3‐dicarbonyl compounds with Mn(OAc)3 into radical intermediates and subsequent cyclizations involving alkenes are well known [34, 35, 36]. Thus, treating (±)‐23 with Mn(OAc)3 in acetic acid at 60°C did not lead to any tricyclic product but to compound (±)‐27 and the introduction of a hydroxyl group at C3 between the ketone and the ester, which was diagnostic of the generation of radical H. While the formation of the enol at C3 is not favored as we just observed, here its oxidation into radical H drives the equilibrium of the reaction. Increasing the temperature to 120°C led, to our delight, to a tricyclic product that we thought to be enal (±)‐26b. However, we later found out that the carbon‐carbon bond was formed between the C3‐carbon of the keto‐ester and the C1 allylic position, that is, at the adjacent position of the one desired, giving rise to tricyclic enal (±)‐29 with an octahydro‐1,5‐methanonaphthalene skeleton.

Original synthesis of tricyclic architectures under radical conditions.

The formation of this architecture, isomeric to the one that was expected, would proceed via transannular remote C–H cyclization via 1,5‐hydrogen atom transfer [37, 38]. It would involve abstraction of an allylic hydrogen by the ketoester radical H rather than the addition to the alkene. The generated allylic radical I would then be trapped by the thus generated enol and, after further oxidation, would lead to the C1–C3 bond and to the original tricycle (±)‐29. Eventually, we found it to be more convenient for practical reasons to run the reaction in dichloroethane at 90°C, yielding allylic alcohol (±)‐ or (−)‐28 and tricyclic enal (±)‐ or (−)‐29 [39]. The allyl alcohol (−)‐28 could indeed be converted to enal (−)‐29 with Dess‐Martin periodinane.

Adding copper triflate derailed this reactivity with the formation of another unexpected product (±)‐31 with a rare octahydro‐2,6‐methanonaphthalene structure [25, 40, 41]. This compound would result from the transannular carbonyl‐ene reaction of the allylic alcohol with the activated ketone at C4 as in J, leading to the tricyclic aldehyde (±)‐30, which we were able to isolate on one occasion [25]. Oxidative cleavage of the C14‐aldehyde of (±)‐30 with Mn(OAc)3 via presumably a radical at C11 would lead to C11‐ketone (±)‐31.

At that time, we believed we had obtained structure 26b in lieu of 29 and we continued the synthesis toward the reported structure of pestalustaine A (Scheme 7). Hydrogenation of (±)‐28 over Pd/C led mainly to deoxygenation of the allylic alcohol into (±)‐33 for which we eventually resolved the structure through x‐ray diffraction analysis of a crystal, which allowed assignment of the octahydro‐1,5‐methanonaphthalene architecture obtained after the Mn(OAc)3‐mediated cyclization [25]. Thus, we found hydrogenation conditions of enal (−)‐29 over Pd/C at 60°C, which allowed us to reduce both the C2═C11 double bond and the C14 aldehyde to diastereoselectively lead to alcohol (−)‐32, whose configuration was determined according to NOESY NMR experiments. Reducing the aldehyde at this stage allowed us to minimize problems of selectivity during the desaturation of the α,β‐positions of the C4‐ketone via a Saegusa‐Ito protocol. Treatment of (−)‐32 with TMSOTf and trimethylamine was used to form the C4═C5 enol ether of the ketone but also to protect the C14‐primary alcohol. Then the addition of palladium acetate and 1,4‐benzoquinone (1,4‐BQ) under an oxygen atmosphere at 90°C induced the formation of the desired C5═C6 unsaturation as in (−)‐36 with a C14‐aldehyde resulting from the oxidation of the alcohol and in (−)‐35. The latter was converted to aldehyde (−)‐36 with Dess–Martin periodinane. The overoxidized compound (−)‐34 with an α,β‐unsaturated ketone and an α,β‐unsaturated aldehyde was also isolated in very small amounts, and its structure was resolved by x‐ray diffraction, which allowed us to confirm the tricyclic architecture [25]. α‐Methylation of aldehyde (−)‐36 at C11 with NaH in precisely controlled conditions led diastereoselectively to (−)‐37. The final stage was the reduction of the three carbonyl groups. However, it was not possible to accomplish all at once with LiAlH4, since this reagent notably led to 1,4‐reduction of the α,β‐unsaturated ketone. Therefore, the required 1,2‐reduction of the α,β‐unsaturated C4‐ketone accompanied by reduction of the C14‐aldehyde was conducted under Luche conditions into an inseparable mixture of two epimeric diols 38a,b. Finally, reduction of the C12‐ester of both epimers into the corresponding primary alcohol was achieved with LiAlH4 at 60°C and led to two separable triols: (+)‐39a as the major epimer and to triol 39b as the minor epimer, which was still contaminated with minor amounts of 39a. The octahydro‐1,5‐methanonaphthalene structure of 39a was confirmed by HMBC and selective INPET NMR experiments, while the relative configuration was confirmed by selective NOESY NMR experiments and in particular via the determination of interproton distances (see the supporting information) [42].

Functionalization of the octahydro‐1,5‐methanonaphthalene framework of 29 into an isomer of the proposed structure 1 of pestalustaine A. Abbreviations: BQ = benzoquinone, dr = diastereomeric ratio.

While the initial goal of reaching the proposed structure 1 of pestalustaine A was not completed, we instead synthesized an original tricyclic structure [43, 44]. The proposed structures of emericellins A,B were reported to display the same tricyclic skeleton as of 37 [43]; however, their structures were then revised [44]. Nevertheless, this synthetic study gave us the opportunity to question the originally proposed structure 1 of pestalustaine A. Before we obtained the x‐ray structures of compounds 33 and 34 and assigned with certainty the structure of the skeleton to the one depicted in Scheme 7 for compounds 29‐39, we thought that we had obtained the structure 1 of pestalustaine A instead of 39b. The 1H and 13C NMR data of our synthetic compound did not match those of the natural product. Therefore, we thought that the original proposed structure 1 of pestalustaine A may have been misassigned. Thus, we reviewed the reported 2D NMR data (COSY, HSQC, HMBC, and NOESY) of the natural product and discovered that some reported NOESY correlations were inconsistent with proposed structure 1, such as those between the two bridgehead protons (H2 and H9), between the methyl at C13 and the C(12)H2 of the primary alcohol; as well as between the methyl at C15 and bridgehead proton H9 (Scheme 8). Indeed, the interproton distances of a DFT computed energy‐minimized structure of the lowest‐energy conformation of 1 (Gaussian 16 (C.01) software package, PBE0 functional and def2‐TZVP basis set, CPCM‐solvation method with methanol) [45, 46, 47, 48, 49] were calculated to be 4.3 Å for H2‐H9, 6.7 Å for H12‐H13 and 4.4 Å for H9‐H15, for which observation of NOESY correlations are unlikely [50].

Structure revision of pestalustaine A.

Taking these facts into consideration, the tricyclic architecture 1 of pestalustaine A, which has no equivalent in other known natural products, appears to be not pertinent, and we set out to reanalyze the original NMR data to resolve its true structure. Unfortunately, the 13C NMR shifts of the two quaternary carbons C3 and C7 overlap, which makes the HMBC difficult to interpret. Nevertheless, the COSY spectrum shows correlations between C(1)H2 and C(8)H2 and also between C(9)H and C(2)H, which seem to respectively indicate that C1 and C8 on the one hand and C9 and C2 on the other hand are adjacent and not separated by one or three carbons as in reported structure 1. One important observation is that endophytic fungi of the Pestalotiopsis species are important sources of caryophyllene‐type sesquiterpenes [51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59].

After analysis of the 2D NMR data, we propose that structure 40 is a good candidate for the revised structure of pestalustaine A. This structure accounts for the observed NOESY interaction between the C(13)H3 and the C(12)H2 protons as well as between C(15)H3 and H9 as suggested by the DFT calculated energy‐minimized structure of the lowest‐energy conformation of 40 [45, 46, 47, 48, 49] for which the H12–H13 distance is 2.4 Å and the H9–H15 distance is 2.1 Å. Upon close inspection of the NOESY spectra, we could not see the H2–H9 correlations. Structure 40 also accounts for COSY interactions between the C(1)H2 and C(8)H2 protons and between the C(9)H and C(2)H protons.

In order to confirm that 40 is an adequate candidate for the revised structure of pestalustaine A, this structure was optimized with DFT, and its computed proton and carbon NMR chemical shift values were compared to the reported ones of the natural product [60, 61]. The same study was also performed with the originally assigned structure 1 of the natural product and structure 41, the C11 epimer of 40. For each isomer, the method begins with a conformational search. Low energy conformations were found using xTB‐CREST, then optimized using Gaussian 16 with a restricted B3LYP‐D3(0)/6‐31+G(d,p) level of theory and implicit solvation model (IEFPCM) for methanol [45, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66]. Conformations within 3 kcal/mol of the lowest energy conformers were used to calculate computational NMR shifts using the GIAO method at the mPW1PW91/6‐311+G(2d,p) level, and methanol as the solvent [67, 68]. Both proton and carbon isotropic shielding values were computed. These computed isotropic shielding values were converted using CHESHIRE to predicted NMR shifts, which were weighted based on their calculated free energies from the optimization calculations and averaged using the referenced code [50, 60, 69]. The predicted NMR shifts were compared to the experimental NMR shifts using DP4+ to determine which isomer predicted shifts were most similar to the experimental (Table 1) [70]. Indeed, this process ruled out the originally assigned structure 1 to be the natural product. In contrast, the caryoplhyllene‐derived skeleton of 40 and 41 was a better candidate. The predicted 1H and 13C shifts of our proposed structure 40 match well with the reported experimental shifts of pestalustaine A with lower largest chemical shift deviation and mean absolute deviation than 41. Thus, structure 40 appears to be the correct structure of pestalustaine A, having the closest predicted shifts to the experimental values.

Conclusion

Fascinated by the unprecedented and complex fused and bridged tricyclic skeleton of the reported structure of pestalustaine A (1), we tackled its total synthesis. Ring expansion of a γ,γ−disubstituted cyclohexenone, diastereosective Hosomi‐Sakurai reaction and ring closing metathesis allowed us to obtain a key bicyclo[4.4.1]undec‐3‐ene framework. Cyclization of the latter via a transannular Michael addition of the 1,3‐keto ester moiety to the enal was investigated. Surprisingly, divergent cyclizations were observed. On the one hand, migratory insertion of the less substituted nickel enolate of the ketone to the enal initiated a sequence of cyclizations and ring cleavage to yield an unexpected tricycloundecane framework 25. On the other hand, the extended enolate of the enal added in a 1,2‐fashion to the ketone yielding the carbon framework 24 of the proposed structure 1 of pestalustaine A, albeit with a different substitution pattern. We thus turned our attention toward a transannular radical‐mediated addition of the 1,3‐ketoester onto an alkene. Transannular remote C–H cyclization via 1,5‐hydrogen atom transfer delivered, after further functionalization, an isomer 39a of pestalustaine A. Finally, upon close re‐analysis of the reported 2D NMR data and a computational prediction of NMR chemical shifts, we revised the structure of pestalustaine A to 40, which contains a caryophyllene‐derived skeleton.
