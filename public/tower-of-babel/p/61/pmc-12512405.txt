Comparison of laparoscopic totally extraperitoneal repair with adjunct techniques and open surgery (TREPP) in female patients with incarcerated femoral hernia: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: An Zhang, Haisong Xu, Wenhao Huang, Peng Zhang, Yuehua Guo, Tianchong Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncarcerated femoral hernia often requires urgent surgical intervention, yet the optimal approach remains debated. Open surgery—particularly the transrectus sheath preperitoneal procedure (TREPP)—is commonly used, while laparoscopic totally extraperitoneal (TEP) repair is gaining traction. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical outcomes of laparoscopic TEP with adjunct techniques (aTEP) versus TREPP in female patients with incarcerated femoral hernia.MethodsThis was a single-centre, retrospective, comparative study conducted at the Shenzhen People’s Hospital between January 2020 and December 2023. Female patients with incarcerated femoral hernias who underwent either aTEP or TREPP were included. The aTEP technique involved a standard laparoscopic TEP repair with the addition of a small femoral ring incision, and final laparoscopic observation through the umbilicus to assess bowel viability. Surgical approach was determined based on clinical presentation, patient condition, and surgeon judgment. Baseline characteristics and intra-/postoperative outcomes were compared between groups.ResultsThe study included 42 patients, with 22 in the TREPP group and 20 in the aTEP group. The aTEP group demonstrated significantly better outcomes in several metrics, including shorter operative time, less intraoperative hemorrhage, lower pain scores at 24 h and postoperative day 7, reduced analgesic use, and shorter hospital stays.ConclusionLaparoscopic TEP, when supplemented with selective adjunct techniques, is a safe and effective surgical option for incarcerated femoral hernias in females. Compared with TREPP, it offers improved perioperative outcomes and faster recovery, particularly in patients without severe bowel compromise.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Total extraperitoneal (TEP), Adjunct techniques, Transrectus sheath preperitoneal procedure (TREPP), Incarcerated femoral hernia, Laparoscopic surgery

Introduction

Inguinal hernia is a common disease requiring surgical intervention. In Western developed countries, the annual incidence of inguinal hernia is about 3,800 cases per million individuals [1], with over 20 million surgeries performed globally each year [2]. Femoral hernias are a type of hernia, accounting for about 3% of all hernias and being more common in women [3]. A femoral hernia occurs when pelvic contents protrude through the femoral ring and may extend anteriorly into the saphenous hiatus in severe cases. The anatomical boundaries of the femoral ring are include the inguinal anteriorly, the pectineal ligament posteriorly, the lacunar ligament medially, and the femoral vein laterally [4]. Except for the lateral femoral vein, the femoral ring is encircled by firm ligamentous structures, which contribute to the high risk of incarceration or strangulation. Emergency surgery is often required, and up to 15% of patients may undergo bowel resection due to necrosis [5].

Among the open surgical approaches, the transrectus sheath preperitoneal procedure (TREPP) is frequently used to treat incarcerated femoral hernias because of its safety and simplicity; however, it is associated with increased surgical trauma, longer hospital stays, and a higher incidence of chronic pain [6]. In contrast, laparoscopic inguinal hernia repair, including intraperitoneal onlay mesh (IPOM), transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) repair, and totally extraperitoneal (TEP) repair, is increasingly adopted due to its benefits of reduced pain, quicker recovery, and better visualization of hernia contents [7, 8]. TEP repair, which is performed entirely in the preperitoneal space, avoids breaching the peritoneal cavity and results in fewer abdominal complications [9]. However, it requires greater technical expertise and is associated with a longer learning curve [10].

Surgical management of incarcerated femoral hernias remains a subject of clinical debate. While open repair remains the most commonly employed method [6], several scholars have reported the feasibility of laparoscopic preperitoneal approaches in experienced hands [11]. However, in cases of incarcerated hernia, reduction of hernia contents may be challenging, and accurate intraoperative assessment of bowel viability is critical. To address these challenges while preserving the advantages of the TEP approach, we applied standard laparoscopic TEP with adjunct techniques, including partial incision of femoral ring structures (such as the inguinal or lacunar ligament) and post-repair intraperitoneal bowel inspection via the umbilical port. These steps aim to enhance safety and facilitate decision-making in emergency laparoscopic repair.

This retrospective study aims to compare the clinical outcomes of TEP with adjunct techniques (aTEP) and open surgery (TREPP) in female patients with incarcerated femoral hernias.

Materials and methods

Data collection

This study retrospectively collected data on female patients diagnosed with incarcerated femoral hernias who underwent aTEP or TREPP at Shenzhen People’s Hospital (The Second Clinical Medical College, Jinan University) between January 2020 and December 2023. Patients were categorized into two groups based on the type of surgery they underwent. The inclusion criteria were: female sex, age ≥ 16 years, and a diagnosis of incarcerated femoral hernia confirmed by clinical examination and preoperative CT scan. Exclusion criteria included patients under 16 years of age, hernia sac diameter > 6 cm, or history of previous inguinal surgery.

All patients underwent preoperative CT scanning to assess hernia contents and sac size (Fig. 1). Detailed information on surgical indications and risks was provided to each patient, and questions were answered by the surgical team. Informed consent for surgery was obtained from all patients. The final choice of surgical method (aTEP repair or TREPP) was based on a combination of patient preference and the operating surgeon’s judgment, taking into account the degree of hernia incarceration, suspected bowel compromise, comorbidities, and surgical accessibility. Given the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent for data analysis was waived, and ethical approval was granted by the institutional ethics committee.

Preoperative computed tomography (CT). Red circle: the hernial sac

To reduce inter-operator variability, all surgeries were performed by senior surgeons with over 10 years of experience and more than 100 TEP repairs completed. Data collected from medical records included patient demographics (age, BMI), lifestyle factors (alcohol and tobacco use), and comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension). Intraoperative variables included operative time and estimated blood loss. Postoperative outcomes included pain level (measured using the Visual Analog Scale, VAS), analgesic usage (intravenous parecoxib sodium and oral tramadol), hospital stay duration, and complications. VAS scores (0 = no pain, 10 = worst pain) were recorded at 12 h, 24 h, and postoperative day 7. Discharge criteria included VAS < 4 and absence of clinical concerns [12].

Definition of aTEP

In this study, aTEP repair (Fig. 2) refers to a standard laparoscopic totally extraperitoneal hernia repair with two adjunct intraoperative techniques: (1) Partial incision of the lacunar and inguinal ligaments to facilitate reduction of the incarcerated hernia sac when necessary; (2) Insertion of a laparoscope into the peritoneal cavity via the umbilical port at the end of the procedure to assess bowel viability. These steps were designed to improve access, reduce intraoperative difficulty, and allow direct evaluation of ischemic bowel, differing from previously reported modified TEP procedures involving pseudo-sac inversion and fixation [9].

Surgical techniques

TREPP group

Under general anesthesia and in the supine position, a 6–8 cm incision was made superior to the pubic tubercle along the inguinal ligament (Fig. 3A). After dissection through the skin and subcutaneous fat, the anterior sheath of the rectus abdominis muscle was incised longitudinally. The rectus abdominis was bluntly separated to access the preperitoneal space via the transverse abdominal fascia. Dissection proceeded to expose the Retzius and Bogros spaces, Cooper’s ligament, the pubic tubercle, and the femoral ring. The hernia sac was explored and its contents assessed. If the sac could not be easily reduced, the inguinal ligament was partially incised to widen the neck. If necrotic bowel or omentum was identified, resection and anastomosis were performed without mesh placement (Fig. 3B). In such cases, a tissue-based repair using the McVay technique was performed, in which the transversus abdominis aponeurosis and conjoined tendon were sutured to Cooper’s ligament to reconstruct the femoral canal. Otherwise, a 15 × 9 cm self-fixing polypropylene mesh (Covidien) was placed to cover the myopectineal orifice.

aTEP group

Conventional TEP repair ports were used. A 12-mm incision was created 10 mm lateral to the midline on the hernia side below the navel, and a 10-mm trocar was inserted into the anterior peritoneal space for viewing. Bluntly push the anterior peritoneal space with a laparoscope to the upper edge of the Retzius space at a pneumoperitoneum pressure of 13 mmHg. Two 5-mm operative holes were placed at two equidistant points on the line between the observation hole and the pubic symphysis under laparoscopic vision. Blunt and sharp dissection dilates the Retzius space to expose the pubic symphysis and Cooper’s ligament. Then, access the affected side Bogros space and dilate it in the same way to create sufficient space for the operation. A definitive diagnosis of femoral hernia is dependent upon the location of the hernia sac. (Fig. 3C) The hernia sac was attempted to be gently retracted to the head side, and external compression over the hernia was applied to retract it back into the abdominal cavity. If the hernia cannot be retracted, a partial incision is made in the lacunar and inguinal ligament at the inner upper part of the hernia sac by an electric hook under direct vision (Fig. 3D), then retracted the hernia after widening the hernia neck. (Fig. 3E) Following hernia retraction, a 15 × 9 cm self-fixing polypropylene mesh (Covidien) is inserted through the viewing hole into the preperitoneal space to cover the myopectineal orifice. The mesh’s medial edge should surpass the pubic symphysis, the lateral edge should extend below the anterior superior iliac spine, and the inferior edge should go beyond Cooper’s ligament to fully cover the myopectineal orifice. No special fixation measures are required to hold the self-fixing mesh. A preperitoneal drain may be left, depending on the surgical wound. Under laparoscopic guidance, the pneumoperitoneum was gradually deflated, followed by the closure of the surgical incisions with sutures.

Finally, the laparoscope was inserted into the abdominal cavity via the umbilical port to inspect the previously incarcerated bowel. Bowel viability was evaluated based on serosal color, presence of peristalsis, and arterial pulsation. If viability was confirmed, the operation was concluded (Fig. 3F). If ischemia or necrosis was observed, the operating ports were rearranged, and laparoscopic bowel resection was performed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS version 27. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using independent-samples t-test or Welch’s t-test (if variance equality was not assumed). Non-normally distributed data were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR), and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons were made using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 for a 2-tailed test was established.

Results

Between January 2020 and December 2023, 42 patients from our medical center were included in the study, fulfilling the inclusion criteria. Of these, 20 patients underwent aTEP surgery, and 22 underwent TREPP surgery. The two groups had comparable mean ages (54.8 ± 16.7 vs. 54.7 ± 13.2 years) and median BMI (27.8 (26.2–28.7) vs. 27.7 (26.4–28.6) kg/). The median hernia diameter was 4.0 cm (range: 3.6–4.5) in the aTEP group and 4.5 cm (range:4.0–5.6 cm) in the TREPP group, without no statistically difference (P = 0.105). The mean preoperative hernia incarceration time was 8.7 ± 3.6 h in the aTEP group and 10.6 ± 3.1 h in the TREPP group, without no significant difference (P = 0.792). Comorbidities were present in 6 patients (30.0%) in the aTEP group and 5 patients (22.7%) in the TREPP group. Smoking and alcohol consumption history also showed no significant difference between the two groups. Details of these baseline characteristics were summarized in Table 1.

The aTEP group demonstrated significantly shorter operative times (58.6 ± 20.8 min) compared to the TREPP group (106.1 ± 17.4 min, P < 0.05). This difference may reflect a higher incidence of bowel resection and more complex surgical cases in the TREPP group. Intraoperative blood loss was significantly lower in the aTEP group (18 ± 9.7 mL) versus the TREPP group (67.5 ± 16.8 mL, P < 0.05). Bowel resection due to necrosis was required in 2 patients (10.0%) in the aTEP group and 5 patients (22.7%) in the TREPP group; although the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.414), it may reflect selection bias in which more severe cases were treated with open surgery.

Postoperative pain levels at 12 h were comparable between the two groups (VAS > 7, P = 0.092). However, at 24 h post-surgery, patients in the aTEP group exhibited significantly lower VAS scores (4.1 ± 1.2) compared to the TREPP group (5.6 ± 1.7, P < 0.05). This trend persisted on postoperative day 7, with VAS scores of 2.0 ± 0.6 (aTEP) versus 3.5 ± 1.3 (TREPP) (P < 0.05). Analgesic consumption also differed significantly, with mean tramadol usage of 87.5 ± 79.3 mg in the aTEP group and 168.2 ± 52.4 mg in the TREPP group (P < 0.05).

The median postoperative hospital stay was significantly shorter in the aTEP group (approximately 2 days) compared to the TREPP group (approximately 5 days, P < 0.05). This may be partially attributable to the higher rate of bowel resection and recovery burden in the TREPP group. The incidence of postoperative complications, including wound infection and seroma, was not significantly different between the groups (P = 0.608), though the TREPP group had one additional case of each. Discussion

Due to the relatively low incidence of incarcerated femoral hernias, published data on their optimal management remain limited. However, incarcerated femoral hernias are prone to complicated content necrosis, which can have serious consequences; they still remain a clinically important condition [13]. Traditionally, open surgery—particularly TREPP—is the most widely adopted approach due to its familiarity and accessibility in emergency settings [14]. Nevertheless, laparoscopic techniques have gained popularity for their minimally invasive nature, reduced postoperative pain, and enhanced visualization of intra-abdominal structures [8, 15, 16].

One of the technical challenges in treating incarcerated hernias lies in the difficulty of reducing the hernia sac due to swelling and the narrow femoral ring. Open surgery addresses this by incising the inguinal ligament to widen the ring [11], and similar techniques have been adapted in laparoscopic repairs [9]. Among the laparoscopic approaches, IPOM and TAPP penetrate the abdominal cavity, increasing the risk of mesh infection in the presence of necrotic bowel [8]. TAPP also requires suturing of the peritoneum, adding procedural complexity [17]. In contrast, TEP repair is performed entirely within the preperitoneal space, avoiding peritoneal violation and reducing the risk of mesh contamination.

In this study, we employed a standard TEP approach supplemented with two adjunctive techniques: (1) a partial incision of the lacunar and inguinal ligaments to facilitate hernia content reduction, and (2) insertion of a laparoscope into the peritoneal cavity after mesh placement to assess bowel viability. This approach reflects an adaptation of TEP rather than a modification of the procedure itself. By integrating targeted adjunct techniques, the laparoscopic TEP approach may be rendered more feasible in emergency settings involving incarceration, without compromising its core principles.

In our cohort, patients who underwent aTEP demonstrated more favorable perioperative outcomes compared to those who received TREPP, including shorter operative times, less intraoperative bleeding, lower pain scores, and shorter hospital stays. Notably, the longer operative times in the TREPP group may reflect a higher proportion of complex cases requiring bowel resection. This suggests that surgical technique selection was influenced by clinical severity—an acknowledged source of selection bias in our retrospective design. Indeed, bowel resection occurred more frequently in the TREPP group (22.7% vs. 10.0%), although the difference was not statistically significant. This disparity likely reflects a tendency to allocate more severe cases to open surgery, further complicating direct comparisons between groups.

Regarding mesh placement, conventional wisdom advises caution when bowel necrosis is present, due to concerns about infection [18]. However, recent studies have shown that mesh placement can be safe and effective in selected cases of incarcerated or even strangulated hernia [19–21]. In our study, a 15 × 9 cm self-fixing polypropylene mesh was used all aTEP cases, including two patients who required bowel resection after mesh placement. Neither of these patients experienced postoperative complications, supporting the potential feasibility of preperitoneal mesh placement in selected emergency contexts.

The use of laparoscopic intraperitoneal observation after hernia reduction offers an additional advantage by enabling magnified assessment of bowel viability. Compared to open inspection, which may rely on visual cues or warm saline gauze, laparoscopic evaluation allows direct observation of peristalsis, color, and vascular pulsation. This assessment typically occurred 20–30 min after reduction, potentially allowing for reperfusion and more accurate judgment. While our data do not show a statistically significant difference in bowel resection rates, this strategy may have contributed to intraoperative decision-making.

Notably, the reduced postoperative hospital stay in the aTEP group may also be attributed to both the lower incidence of bowel resection and the minimally invasive nature of the procedure. Patients who underwent TREPP were more likely to require bowel resection and experienced more surgical trauma, both of which are known to prolong recovery and hospitalization.

Despite these encouraging findings, our study has several limitations. It was a single-center, retrospective analysis with a small sample size, and the lack of randomization introduces potential selection bias. Surgical decisions were made based on intraoperative judgment and patient condition, which likely influenced outcomes such as operative time and complication rates. Additionally, long-term follow-up was limited, and recurrence rates were not assessed.

In summary, our study indicate that standard laparoscopic TEP repair supplemented by adjunctive intraoperative techniques may be a feasible and safe option for managing incarcerated femoral hernia in female patients. These adjuncts may expand the applicability of TEP in emergency settings without altering its fundamental structure. However, prospective randomized trials are needed to further evaluate its safety, long-term outcomes, and optimal patient selection.

Conclusions

Laparoscopic TEP, when supplemented with adjunctive techniques, may represent a feasible and safe surgical strategy for managing incarcerated femoral hernias in female patients. Compared to open surgery (TREPP), aTEP offers shorter operative time, less bleeding, reduced postoperative pain, and faster recovery, while also facilitating better assessment of incarcerated tissue viability. Nevertheless, given the retrospective design, limited sample size, and potential selection bias, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further large-scale, prospective studies are needed to validate the applicability and safety of adjunct-enhanced TEP approaches in emergency settings and to define optimal indications for their use.
