Perspectives and Preferences of People with Type 2 Diabetes for the Attributes of Weekly Insulin
Authors: Felicia T. Gelsey, David Schapiro, Katherine Kosa, Caroline Vass, Magaly Perez-Nieves, Anna Pierce, Jiat Ling Poon, Dana DiBenedetti, Carol Mansfield
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDaily insulin administration can be burdensome for people with type 2 diabetes (PwT2D) and can impact treatment adherence. This study investigated preferences for once-weekly, long-acting basal insulin for treatment of PwT2D.MethodsAn online discrete-choice experiment was administered to PwT2D in the USA. Qualitative interviews informed the selection of six attributes: reduction in A1c level after 6 months, amount of time spent in optimal blood sugar range each day, number of serious low blood sugar events, number of nighttime low blood sugar events, change in weight because of the insulin over 6 months, and frequency of administration. Each participant completed eight questions offering a choice between two long-acting insulins; questions varied according to an experimental design. A fixed treatment choice question asked about preferences for daily versus weekly insulin, holding other treatment features constant. Data were analyzed using random-parameters logit models, and heterogeneity was explored through subgroup analyses.ResultsFour hundred sixty-six PwT2D completed the survey (mean age, 57; mean A1c, 7.5%; 59.0% female); 33.3% of these were currently on a basal/bolus regimen, 34.3% used basal only, and 32.4% were insulin naive. Respondents placed the most importance on avoiding a 10-pound weight change and equal importance on the largest change in the number of serious and nighttime low blood sugar events per year and achieving the longest time in range included in the choice questions. There was significant heterogeneity in preferences by experience: insulin-naive respondents had stronger preferences for scheduled and flexible weekly insulin over daily insulin; 67.6% preferred flexible weekly over daily insulin, all else being equal.ConclusionPwT2D valued insulin efficacy and reducing treatment-related adverse events, with heterogeneity in the relative importance of administration frequency. All else being equal, respondents preferred weekly over daily basal insulin. These findings provide insights into the preferences of PwT2D considering weekly long-acting insulin.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-024-01652-0.

Introduction

Diabetes is a chronic disease associated with serious long-term complications and sometimes with premature death [1, 2]. Currently, it is estimated that 328 million people worldwide live with type 2 diabetes (T2D) [3]. Rates of T2D are increasing, and it is predicted that > 590 million individuals will have a T2D diagnosis by 2035 [3].

Insulin is indicated for people with T2D (PwT2D) if adequate glycemic control cannot be achieved through lifestyle modifications and/or through antidiabetic therapy with oral and/or non-insulin injectable medications. PwT2D typically start insulin therapy on daily basal (long-acting) insulin and may eventually progress to adding bolus (short-acting or mealtime) insulin. However, the insulin landscape is evolving, and several trials have investigated the safety and efficacy of once-weekly basal insulin. Although insulin is highly effective for glycemic control, the literature documents several barriers to insulin uptake and has shown how frequency of administration can impact a person’s quality of life [4–6]. For instance, Peyrot et al. found that up to 20% of people with diabetes report intentional insulin omission; the authors found that having more scheduled daily injections was one of the key risk factors for omission [7]. In another study, people with diabetes who were taking daily mealtime insulin reported a range of psychological and social impacts, reinforcing the need to examine preferences for weekly versus daily insulin therapy [8].

Although these studies suggest that weekly insulin therapy may be well received by people with diabetes, little is known about the preferences of PwT2D for frequency of administration or how these compare to preferences for other insulin treatment features, such as efficacy (glucose control) and risks (hypoglycemic events, change in weight). Discrete-choice experiments (DCEs) are a stated-preference method commonly used to quantify individuals’ willingness to make tradeoffs among different treatment features [9].

The primary objectives of this study were to use a DCE to elicit and quantify preferences of PwT2D for treatment attributes associated with basal insulin (e.g., benefit, safety, dose, and frequency of insulin treatments) and assess tradeoffs that PwT2D were willing to make among the different treatment features. A secondary objective was to explore the degree of heterogeneity by investigating, through a split-sample subgroup analysis, the extent to which individuals’ demographic and clinical characteristics were associated with different preferences for the attributes of basal insulin treatment. An exploratory objective was to estimate the proportion of respondents who would prefer a weekly basal insulin over a daily basal insulin, all else equal and identify individual characteristics (e.g., sociodemographics, treatment experience) associated with this preference.

Methods

Study Design

For this study, a cross-sectional, web-based DCE survey was developed and administered to PwT2D. The study design and analyses were developed following good research practices as defined by ISPOR (The Professional Society for Health Economics and Outcomes Research) guidelines and standard practice in preference studies [10–12].

Ethical Approval

The study was reviewed and deemed exempt from full review by the independent RTI International institutional review board. This study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments. All participants gave written informed consent. Respondents who participated in this study were informed that the results may be published and were reassured that, if the results of this study were to be presented at scientific meetings or published in scientific journals, the data would be presented in summary form and no information would be included that could identify them or their answers personally.

Survey Instrument

To identify relevant attributes and levels for the DCE, we first conducted in-depth qualitative telephone interviews with 21 PwT2D (15 insulin experienced and 6 insulin naive) to better understand their experiences with diabetes and its treatments. Specifically, these interviews focused on participants’ experiences with currently available daily basal insulin treatments and their preferences for a once-weekly, long-acting basal insulin. The qualitative interviews found that many PwT2D who were insulin naive were resistant to initiating insulin, and some felt it would be an “inconvenient” treatment. All participants were generally receptive to the possibility of a once-weekly basal regimen, reporting that fewer injections and greater convenience/flexibility would be less stressful and would relieve worries about taking or remembering insulin administrations.

Using the results of the qualitative interviews, clinical trial data, published data, information about long-acting basal insulins that are currently available or under development, and information from subject matter experts, we identified treatment attributes and levels that were clinically relevant to PwT2D. The six attributes were reduction in hemoglobin A1c (A1c) level after 6 months, time spent in optimal blood sugar range each day, number of serious low blood sugar events, number of nighttime low blood sugar events, change in weight because of insulin over 6 months, and frequency of taking long-acting insulin. A draft version of the DCE survey instrument was evaluated in nine individual pretest interviews conducted by telephone. Pretest interviews used a cognitive debriefing technique called “think aloud,” [13] in which participants were asked to verbalize their thoughts as they completed the draft survey. After completing the survey in this manner, participants were asked a series of debriefing questions to determine whether they understood the definitions and instructions, accepted the hypothetical context of the survey, and successfully completed the choice questions in the survey instrument as instructed. The pretest interviews resulted in minor revisions to the survey to ensure that survey respondents understood the questions.

In the final survey instrument that was administered online, respondents were presented with a series of eight DCE questions that each asked them to choose between two hypothetical treatment profiles. The profiles did not reflect actual treatments but were rather combinations of attribute levels generated from an experimental design and generically labeled as “Insulin A” and “Insulin B” in each choice question. The final attributes and levels included in the DCE survey to inform the hypothetical treatment profiles are shown in Table 1, and an example of a discrete-choice question is shown in Fig. 1. The survey contained an additional fixed direct-elicitation question to determine whether respondents preferred weekly rather than daily insulin, all else being equal in terms of risks (< 1 clinically significant and < 1 nocturnal hypoglycemic event per year) and benefits (up to 0.5 point reduction in A1c and 18 h spent in range) (see Supplementary Figure 1).Fig. 1Example of a DCE question. The survey instrument contained questions on the respondents’ experiences with T2D, descriptions of the attributes, comprehension questions, the eight DCE choice questions, a direct-elicitation question, and demographic questions.

Study Population

Potential participants were identified by SurveyEngine through online panels and invited to be screened for study eligibility. If eligible, they were provided with a unique link to the survey. PwT2D were recruited to the study if they were aged ≥ 18 years, were able to read and understand English, provided informed consent, resided in the US at the time of the study, had a self-reported physician diagnosis of T2D (received at least 6 months before the survey), and were not currently using an insulin pump but were currently receiving medication for their diabetes. As no once-weekly insulins were approved at the time of data collection, it was anticipated that participants would only have experience with daily insulin injections. Targets were used to ensure that approximately one-third of study participants were currently treated with basal-bolus insulin injections (with or without other diabetes medicines), one-third were treated with basal insulin (with or without other diabetes medicines), and one-third were treated with a diabetes medicine but had no experience with insulin treatment (i.e., were insulin naive).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the sample of PwT2D. A regression analysis using a random-parameters logit (RPL) model was performed to estimate preference weights from the attributes in the DCE. Preference weights indicate the relative strength of preference for each attribute level included in the survey. More-preferred outcomes have higher preference weights. The statistical significance of differences between adjacent attribute levels was determined using a Wald χ2 test. Preference weights from the RPL model were used to calculate the conditional relative importance of each attribute, which was calculated as the difference between the most- and least-preferred level for each attribute.

Using a series of χ2 tests, we also explored whether there were systematic differences in preferences between subgroups of participants by characteristics of interest. These characteristics included those with experience with different treatment regimens, those with experience with low blood sugar events, and those with different levels of glycemic control. To analyze responses to the direct-elicitation choice question, a logistic regression model with covariates (i.e., indicators of various statuses related to sociodemographics and disease and treatment experience, as well as other variables of interest) was used to determine the association between respondent characteristics and treatment choices. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed in STATA 16 (StataCorp; College Station, Texas). Data from all respondents were analyzed together.

Results

Respondent Characteristics

The survey instrument was completed by 466 PwT2D. Demographic characteristics are presented in Table 2. The mean respondent age was 57 years; 59.0% of respondents were female, and 28.3% had received a diagnosis of T2D < 6 years ago. Equal proportions of respondents were currently on a basal and bolus regimen (n = 155, 33.3%), were currently on a basal-only regimen (n = 160, 34.3%), or were insulin naive (n = 151, 32.4%). Overall, 13.7% of respondents were currently using a continuous glucose monitor (CGM), and 8.6% had used a CGM in the past. The mean self-reported A1c was 7.5% (standard deviation [SD], 1.4%; n = 399), and the mean self-reported target A1c was 6.4% (SD, 0.8%; n = 365). To ensure respondents understood the choice tasks, two comprehension questions were included, which asked respondents to identify (1) the treatment profile that kept them in the optimal blood sugar range for the longest amount of time and (2) the treatment profile that resulted in the greatest number of serious low blood sugar events per year. A total of 59.7% of respondents correctly identified the treatment profile that kept them in the optimal blood sugar range for the longest amount of time, and 35.0% correctly identified the treatment profile with the highest number of serious low blood sugar events. After answering the comprehension questions, respondents were provided with text explaining the correct answers. Preference Weights

Figure 2 plots the mean preference weight estimate for each attribute level. Preferences for attribute levels were generally ordered as expected, with better levels being preferred to worse levels. Larger differences between preference weights indicate that respondents viewed the change as having a relatively greater effect on overall utility. Respondents preferred greater reductions in A1c and more time in optimal blood sugar range, losing 5 pounds because of the insulin relative to gaining 5 pounds, and fewer serious and nighttime hypoglycemic events. The conditional relative importance (CRI) of each attribute reflects the maximum change in utility achievable with that attribute, conditional on the attributes and levels selected for inclusion in the study. Over the ranges presented in the survey, the change in weight because of insulin from gain 5 pounds to lose 5 pounds had the greatest CRI, accounting for 25.9% of the total possible change in utility. Subgroup Analysis

Significantly different preferences were observed between subgroups defined by respondents’ insulin experience (insulin naive [n = 151] vs. insulin experienced [n = 315]) and experience with serious low blood sugar events (no serious low blood sugar events in the past year [n = 243] vs. at least 1 serious low blood sugar event in the past year [n = 206]).

In the insulin-experienced subgroup (Supplementary Figure S2), the difference in preferences was largely driven by the frequency of administration of long-acting insulin while maintaining blood sugar control. Insulin-naive respondents had stronger preferences for the 2 weekly insulin options over daily insulin than did respondents with insulin experience. Furthermore, respondents with insulin experience were more sensitive to changes in the reduction of A1c level than were the insulin-naive respondents.

In the subgroup defined by experience with serious low blood sugar events (Supplementary Figure S3), preferences were largely driven by the number of serious low blood sugar events per year, the number of nighttime low blood sugar events per year, and the reduction in A1c level. The results suggest that preferences for the remaining attributes were similar across respondents who had experienced a serious low blood sugar event and respondents who had not.

Direct-Elicitation Logit Analysis

In the fixed direct-elicitation question, where all treatment attributes were the same between the two profiles except the frequency of administration, 67.6% of the sample chose the flexible weekly insulin treatment over the daily insulin. Individuals with no prior experience with injectable treatment (p < 0.001) and those self-identifying as female (p = 0.067) were more likely to choose a flexible weekly insulin, whereas those who reported that their current diabetes treatment fits their lifestyle were more likely (p = 0.026) to choose a daily insulin, as were those who answered both comprehension questions incorrectly (p = 0.008). The other variables explored were not statistically significant.

Discussion

The aim of this DCE study was to understand the importance that PwT2D place on features of long-acting insulin treatment, including treatment schedule (i.e., weekly vs. daily insulin injections). Given the attributes and levels presented in this study, PwT2D placed the most importance on change in weight due to insulin therapy from gaining 5 pounds to losing 5 pounds, followed by increased time in optimal blood sugar range to the whole day or almost all day, avoiding 2–4 serious low blood sugar events, and avoiding 2–4 nighttime low blood sugar events. Achieving the maximum A1c reduction of up to 1.5 points relative to a reduction of 0.5 points was less important than the other benefit and risk attributes, possibly because of the sample’s relatively low baseline A1C (7.5%). Respondents were not willing to give up improvements in time in range and A1c or to accept more low blood sugar events to switch to a weekly basal insulin. However, 67.6% of PwT2D preferred the flexible weekly dosing schedule to daily dosing when all other attributes were held constant, indicating a preference for weekly dosing.

Numerous stated-preference studies have assessed treatment preferences, including for frequency of administration, among PwT2D; however, many of these have focused on preferences for non-insulin antidiabetic medications (e.g., sulfonylureas, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists) [14]. For example, Mansfield et al. [15] quantified preferences among PwT2D in Germany and Spain for the benefits, risks, mode of administration, and dosing frequency of several treatments used in the PwT2D population. Although a useful starting point, these diabetes medication preference studies typically include treatment attributes not relevant to basal insulin therapy (e.g., oral administration, multiple daily injections) and/or they do not specifically sample PwT2D who have experience with insulin.

One example of an insulin-focused DCE study is that by Hauber et al. [16], which elicited preferences for attributes of insulin therapy from nearly 1000 PwT2D in Canada. The authors estimated the relative importance of glucose control, A1c levels, the number of mild-to-moderate hypoglycemic events per month, and the number of daily insulin injections. Although preferences for less-frequent administrations were not explored, studies using other preference-elicitation methods have shown that people with diabetes prefer fewer injections [17, 18].

In this study, we found systematic differences in preferences across subgroups (i.e., insulin experience and serious low blood sugar event experience). For instance, PwT2D who were insulin naive had stronger preferences for scheduled weekly insulin and flexible weekly insulin over daily insulin than did respondents with insulin experience. Medication adherence is driven by many factors, and educational support involving an ongoing exchange of information between patients and their care teams is important in chronic diseases, such as T2D [19]. In this current study, respondents without insulin experience preferred weekly therapy, pointing to an opportunity for care teams to provide ongoing education about treatment options and support to people who are considering initiating insulin. Similarly, current insulin users may not have the same fears as insulin- or injection-naive respondents about daily injections, which may reflect their experience with insulin or injectable therapies or other strategies they have adopted to manage their treatment regimen. More broadly, the results in this study highlight the need for communication between physicians and people with diabetes about the potential risks and benefits of therapeutic options before, during, and after insulin treatment has been initiated.

This study has several strengths. First, the survey and the DCE design were informed by qualitative interviews that aimed to understand how people felt about different insulin treatments and identify treatment attributes and levels for insulin-based treatments that were important to PwT2D. This was particularly important, as even PwT2D (two-thirds of the sample) who had insulin experience would only have experience with daily injections. Following good experimental design and research practices, once the attributes and levels were identified, the survey was carefully designed and pretested using in-depth interviews with participants with diabetes in the US. Nevertheless, several limitations are also worth noting. First, all answers to treatment and other survey questions (e.g., weight, height) were self-reported. Second, the treatment profiles were hypothetical and may not replicate the experience of talking to a healthcare professional about insulin treatments, where other considerations may come into play, such as cost or access to care. Third, the study also used a convenience sample and may not be representative of all people with diabetes. Finally, the online setting of the survey also may have influenced respondents’ choices. Studies have shown that COVID-19 adversely affected treatment adherence among people with chronic diseases, such as T2D [20, 21]. Although the survey contained selected questions about COVID-19, we cannot know the extent to which the results were affected by the COVID-19 pandemic.

Conclusions

The study confirms the importance of glycemic control for PwT2D, especially time in optimal blood sugar range and avoiding potential side effects, such as hypoglycemia and weight gain. Although, on average, respondents were not willing to trade weight gain or glycemic control for changes in dosing frequency, respondents preferred weekly over daily basal insulin in cases where efficacy and safety profiles were otherwise equivalent. Insulin-naive respondents expressed strong preferences for weekly over daily basal insulin regimens, indicating that this kind of flexibility could be particularly important for people considering initiating insulin therapy. Results from this study suggest that novel insulins offering both noninferior glycemic and weight control and less-frequent dosing may be needed to better match people’s current preferences for insulin therapy.
