Clinical profile, treatment patterns, and early outcomes of colorectal cancer at Kassala Police Hospital, Eastern Sudan: a 10-year retrospective cohort study
Authors: Abdel Latif Khalifa Elnaim, Salma Salah Hassan Mohamed Ali, Rawah Suliman Mohamed Ahmed, Ismail Sagap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn low-resource settings with limited access to multimodal care, colorectal cancer is characterized by delayed diagnosis and a high incidence of emergency presentation. Robust contemporary longitudinal data from Eastern Sudan are scarce. This is a retrospective cohort study recruiting all patients who underwent colonic or rectal resection for colorectal cancer between June 2013 and June 2023. Extracted data included demographic, clinical, operative, oncologic, and outcome variables. Fisher’s exact tests, χ² tests, t-tests and ANOVA were used for bivariate analysis. Independent predictors of outcomes were identified using multivariable logistic and linear regression models. Kaplan–Meier methods and Cox proportional hazards models were used to analyze time-to-event outcomes, with competing-risk analysis applied for recurrence. The study included 604 patients (59.4% male), with a mean age of 53.5 years. Most patients (495/604, 82.0%) had stage II disease, emergency cases were 25.8% (156/604), while 63.2% of cases were rectal and left-sided cancers. A statistically significant association was identified between emergency surgery and advanced stage, higher morbidity, anastomotic leak, intensive care unit admission, mortality, and recurrence (p < 0.001). Multivariable analysis revealed that independent predictors of mortality included advanced stage, emergency operation, advanced age, postoperative morbidity, and intensive care unit admission. Predictors of recurrence included advanced stage and left-sided/rectal tumor location. Hospital stay was significantly prolonged by postoperative morbidity and stoma formation. The highest recurrence rate (26.3%) occurred with rectal cancers, with underutilization of neoadjuvant and radiotherapy. In Eastern Sudan, a significant portion of colorectal cancer presents as an emergency at an advanced stage, associated with high rates of morbidity, recurrence and mortality. While rectal tumors were disadvantaged by limited access to multimodal therapy, right-sided tumor location was protective. For survival and other outcomes, there is an urgent need for earlier detection and increased accessibility to chemoradiotherapy.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Emergency surgery, Anastomotic leak, Recurrence, Sudan, OutcomesSubject terms: Cancer, Gastroenterology, Oncology, Risk factors

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major health concern, standing as the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide1–4. The incidence of CRC and its related mortality is steadily increasing across low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), including sub-Saharan Africa, which is attributable to rapid urbanization, dietary transitions, and lack of screening programs5–7. Concurrently, a global observation of early-onset CRC among adults under 50 years was reported, a trend with significant implications for diagnostic pathways and health system readiness8–11.

In LMICs with resource-limited settings, delayed diagnosis and advanced stage at presentation are attributable to limited access to advanced diagnostic modalities, including endoscopy, and specialized care for CRC, as well as low public awareness. Additionally, healthcare disparities and lack of structured screening programs and referral pathways contribute to the magnitude of emergency presentation12–15. Intestinal obstruction and perforation occur in 20–40% of CRC presentation worldwide; however, the proportion is substantially higher in many LMIC16–18, which is linked to poor postoperative outcomes including prolonged hospital stay, higher morbidity, mortality, recurrence and shorter survival19–25. Emergency presentation was usually associated with biologically aggressive cellular type of carcinoma with invasive behavior, presenting with advanced tumor stage, further worsening outcomes17,22,26.

Management of CRC emergencies remains complex and controversial. In well-equipped centers, multimodal strategies including self-expanding metal stents (SEMS) as a bridge to surgery, damage-control resection, selective use of primary anastomosis, and enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) have been linked to improved perioperative outcomes27–31. In a low-income setting, open emergency resection with a high stoma creation rate is the main practice due to a lack of advanced options. This contributes to higher anastomotic leak rates, increased perioperative mortality and shorter survival24,32–35. The guideline-concordant multimodal care adoption is negatively influenced by disparities in access to neoadjuvant therapy, adjuvant chemotherapy, and radiotherapy in low-resource settings, which disproportionately affect rectal cancer patients36–39.

Although the CRC burden is rising in Africa, high-quality longitudinal data on surgical outcomes remain scarce. Most reports reveal heterogeneous datasets from small series or single-year audits40–42. Several studies have evaluated the impact of emergency presentation, operative procedure, postoperative outcomes, including intensive care unit (ICU) admission, morbidity, mortality, recurrence, and management protocols for CRC care. Granular reporting of key quality indicators, including early oncological outcomes, stoma creation and its closure, and anastomotic leak, remains particularly scarce43,44.

Kassala Police Hospital is the colorectal referral unit for Eastern Sudan, serving a cohort facing substantial socioeconomic and geographic challenges to timely access to specialized care. Clinical observations suggested a high incidence of emergency presentation of CRC with obstruction or perforation, requiring major intervention in critically ill patients. However, systematic regional data were lacking.

The aim of this retrospective cohort study is to: (i) describe presentation of CRC and staging patterns; (ii) outline surgical and oncologic management strategy; (iii) evaluate postoperative outcomes including morbidity, ICU admission, mortality, recurrence and survival; and (iv) assess predictors of adverse outcomes using multivariate analysis. By providing longitudinal hospital-based data, the study aims to establish baseline benchmarks and inform improvements in CRC care in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This is a retrospective hospital-based cohort study conducted at the Department of General and Colorectal Surgery at Kassala Police Hospital, Kassala, Eastern Sudan. Our hospital serves Kassala state and the surrounding states, as well as nearby countries, in managing emergency and elective CRC cases. The study spanned a 10-year period from June 2013 to June 2023.

Study population and eligibility criteria

All adult patients with histologically diagnosed colorectal cancer and managed at Kassala Police Hospital during the study period were screened for enrolment.

Inclusion criteria were: All patients with Age ≥ 16 years with histologically confirmed colorectal cancer diagnosed and/or managed at Kassala Police Hospital in the period between June 2013 and June 2023.

Exclusion criteria were: all patients with benign colorectal disease, including benign polyps and colonic obstruction without malignancy, were excluded from the study. Patients with incomplete clinical or operative documentation, unknown outcome or missing histological report were also excluded. Exclusion was extended to patients who presented with recurrent CRC and for whom information about the index surgery was unavailable. This study used a census sampling protocol, including all eligible patients with adequate documentation in the final cohort. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration (2013). Data sources and data collection

To ensure data completeness, we used multiple complementary hospital sources, including surgical wards records and discharge registers, operative theatre logbook and operative notes, histopathology database, radiology database, ICU records, mortality registry and outpatient clinic follow-up records. A standard structured extraction form was used for data collection by a trained team. A manual chart review was performed, and discrepancies were resolved by rechecking against the original documents. For quality assurance, a 10% random sample underwent double-entry verification.

Study variables

Demographic variables included age in years, gender, residence, ASA classification, and presence of comorbidities (Diabetes mellitus (DM), ischemic heart disease (IHD), and hypertension). Clinical variables included primary presenting complaints such as altered bowel habits, lower gastrointestinal bleeding, anemia, intestinal obstruction, abdominal/rectal mass, or perforation. Tumor characteristics included primary tumor location from the caecum to the rectum, grouped as right-sided, including the caecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, and proximal transverse colon. Left-sided included the distal transverse, splenic flexure, descending, recto-sigmoid colon and rectum (upper and lower). The tumor stages in the study were II, III, and IV. Operative and management variables included procedure type, including right hemicolectomy, extended right hemicolectomy, left hemicolectomy, extended left hemicolectomy, sigmoid colectomy, low anterior resection, ultralow anterior resection, abdominoperineal resection (APR), colostomy, diverting stoma alone, and palliative bypass. Additional operative data included findings such as obstruction, perforation, metastatic disease, stoma formation, or primary anastomosis. Oncologic treatment variables included neoadjuvant chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and adjuvant chemotherapy.

Postoperative outcomes variables included postoperative morbidities within 30 days of discharge, classified using the Clavien–Dindo score. Other specific complications included SSI, anastomotic leak, pneumonia, cardiac events, thromboembolism, intra-abdominal abscess, and sepsis. Other outcomes included ICU admission, Length of hospital stay (LOS; days), mortality, tumor recurrence during follow-up, stoma closure and time to closure (months).

Sample size

The clinical observation of routine caseload (1–2 CRC cases per week) expected 500–1000 cases over a 10-year period. The total number of participants was 604 after exclusions, which exceeded the minimum requirement for complication-rate precision and conformed to the events-per-variable (EPV)criteria for logistic regression. For sample size calculation, we proposed (α = 0.05, expected complication rate 30%, precision ± 5%). This suggested a required minimum of ~ 323 cases; using p = 0.50 yielded ~ 385 cases. The presented data supported multivariate modelling with 5–10 per model, in which we reported 71 morbidity cases, 52 ICU admissions, 42 anastomotic leaks, 75 mortality cases, and 72 recurrence cases.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26.0) was used for conducting statistical analysis. Descriptive, comparative, multivariable, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses were performed using a predefined statistical analysis plan. Data is managed with numerical coding to ensure standardization. A complete case analysis was used, as missing data was < 10%.

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR), as appropriate. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables. For all participants, outputs were stratified by clinically relevant domains, including emergency vs. elective, tumor stage, tumor site, stoma status, and rectal vs. colon cancers.

Bivariate comparative analyses were performed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, for normally distributed continuous variables, an independent-samples t test was used, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used for skewed continuous variables. For multi-group comparison, one–way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. Predefined comparisons included Emergency vs. elective, Right-sided vs. left-sided/rectal tumors, Stage II vs. Stage III–IV, Stoma vs. no stoma, and Rectal vs. colon cancers.

Multivariable regression analyses were performed for postoperative morbidity, ICU admission, Anastomotic leak, mortality, Tumor recurrence, and survival. Candidate predictors were selected based on clinical relevance and bivariate analysis results. This included age, sex, The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class, comorbidity, emergency surgery, tumor site, tumor stage, stoma formation, anastomosis, ICU admission, neoadjuvant and adjuvant therapy when appropriate. Reports include adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% CI and p-values.

Linear Regression was used for LOS in relation to age, sex, ASA, emergency surgery, tumor stage, stoma, morbidity, and ICU admission. LOS was inspected for normality; non-log-transformed results were reported for interpretability. For outcomes with sparse events, particularly mortality, penalized logistic regression using Firth’s correction was applied to reduce small-sample bias and quasi-separation, ensuring more reliable effect estimates.

Follow-up was recorded in months, with a maximum follow-up of 60 months. Time to death and time to recurrence were treated as continuous variables; patients without events were censored at last follow-up. Overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods and compared across clinically relevant strata (tumour stage, emergency presentation, tumor site, and stoma status) using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression was employed to identify independent predictors of mortality and recurrence, adjusting for demographic, clinical, operative, postoperative, and oncologic treatment variables. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using standard diagnostics.

Mortality and recurrence were analyzed as continuous time-to-event outcomes measured in months from the date of index surgery to event occurrence or last follow-up, with censoring applied to patients without events. In addition to survival analyses, complementary multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to identify predictors of all-cause mortality and tumor recurrence as binary outcomes during the entire follow-up period, facilitating comparison with prior studies reporting dichotomous endpoints. Prespecified subgroup analyses included rectal cancer and emergency-only cohorts, with dedicated regression models applied where appropriate. Sensitivity analyses excluded palliative cases and re-ran key models using complete-case data; for outcomes with sparse events or model instability, penalized regression techniques were applied to ensure robustness of effect estimates.

Results

Study cohort and baseline characteristics

A total of 604 adult participants with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of CRC over the 10-year study period from June 2013 to June 2023, were enrolled in the study. The mean age of participants was 53.5 ± 13.9 years, with a higher incidence of young CRC: 31.1% of the study cohort were aged < 50 years, unlike reports from high-income settings. The incidence peak was observed in the fifth and sixth decades, and declined progressively thereafter. Male patients accounted for 59.4% (n = 359) of the participants, while females accounted for 40.6% (n = 245). Most patients live in Kassala city or the surrounding rural areas, while others are from outside the state.

Documented comorbidities among the study cohort were reported in 6.3%, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and ischemic heart disease. On evaluating the physiological status of participants, they were predominantly low risk, as 93.4% of patients were classified as ASA I.

For the mode of presentation, the highest frequency was altered bowel habits, followed by lower gastrointestinal bleeding, intestinal obstruction, anemia, abdominal or rectal mass, while perforation was the least frequent. Regarding operative timing classification, 156 (25.8%) patients underwent emergency surgery, while 448 patients (74.2%) underwent elective operations. Regarding tumor location, left-sided tumors were predominant, accounting for 63.2% of cases, whereas right-sided tumors comprised 36.8%. The most frequent anatomical subgroup was lower rectal tumors, representing 20.0%. Stage II tumors predominated (495/604, 82.0%), with stage III and IV disease in 92 (15.2%) and 17 (2.8%) patients, respectively (Table 1).

Operative characteristics and perioperative management

The operative procedures performed depend on the primary tumor location, disease stage, and mode of presentation. These procedures included right and extended right hemicolectomy -for cecal, ascending, hepatic flexure, proximal transverse colonic tumors- left and extended left hemicolectomy-for distal transverse, splenic flexure, descending colonic tumors-, sigmoid colectomy for sigmoid colon tumors, while rectal tumor underwent anterior resection, low anterior resection (LAR), ultralow anterior resection (ULAR), abdominoperineal resection (APR) depending on location and local invasion. Hartmann’s procedure, diverting stoma alone, and palliative bypass were offered for advanced disease or patients with a clinically critical presentation. A primary bowel anastomosis was possible in 497 patients (82.3%), while 164 patients (27.2%) underwent stoma formation at the index operation. Stoma creation was substantially higher among emergency presentations, rectal tumors, and advanced-stage disease. Emergency presentation was the primary indication for ICU admission in 52 patients (8.6%), predominantly due to postoperative complications. The median length of hospital stay (LOS) for the entire cohort was 9 days (IQR 7–10) (Table 2). Longer stays were reported with emergency cases, rectal procedures, post-stoma creation and stage IV patients. Inspection of length-of-stay residuals revealed an acceptable approximation to normality, and linear regression assumptions were reasonably met; therefore, non-transformed LOS estimates are reported for clinical interpretability (Fig. 2).

Oncologic treatment patterns

Adjuvant chemotherapy uptake was exceptionally high in this cohort; 589 patients (99.0%) received adjuvant chemotherapy, 490 of 495 patients (99.0%) with Stage II disease, and 99 of 109 patients (90.8%) with Stage III–IV disease, reflecting institutional practice towards intensified adjuvant treatment. When stratified by anatomical site, uptake was 399 of 406 (98.3%) for colon cancers, 153 of 161 (95.0%) for rectal cancers, and 37 of 37 (100%) for rectosigmoid tumors. The discrepancy between subgroup totals and the overall cohort reflects differences in anatomical stratification, including rectosigmoid tumors and a small number of cases with incomplete subgroup classification, while the total (n = 589) includes all patients receiving adjuvant chemotherapy.

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy were exclusively offered to rectal cancer patients, with radiotherapy predominantly restricted to lower rectal Stage III tumors. Among the study cohort, 7 of 161 rectal cancer patients (4.3%) received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, while 16 patients (9.9%) received radiotherapy. On evaluating rectal cancer subgroups, analysis demonstrated that radiotherapy was predominantly administered in Stage III disease (14 of 24 patients, 58.3%), whereas 7 of 24 Stage III rectal cancer patients (29.2%) received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, indicating underutilization of guideline-recommended neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy.

Early postoperative outcomes

On evaluating early postoperative outcomes, complications occurred in 71 patients (11.8%). Major complications (grade ≥ III) according to the Clavien–Dindo classification were observed in 5.3% of patients. Surgical site infection (SSI) occurred in 1.5% of patients, while anastomotic leak occurred in 42 of 497 patients with primary anastomosis (8.5%). Other clinically significant morbidities, including sepsis, cardiopulmonary events, and thromboembolic complications, were reported in8%of patients. In-hospital or 30-day mortality occurred in 12.4% (75/604). Mortality occurred disproportionately among emergency presentations, patients with Stage III–IV disease, and those requiring ICU admission (Fig. 3; Table 2).

Early postoperative outcomes and complication profile following colorectal cancer surgery.

Stomas were created in 164 patients (27.2% of the total cohort), of whom 96 (58.5%) underwent stoma reversal during follow-up, with a median time to stoma closure of 6 months (IQR 4–6 months). On assessing stoma reversal by disease stage, we found that it was performed in 47.5% of patients with stage II disease, 78.3% with stage III disease, and only 40.0% with stage IV disease. Regarding clinical presentation, stoma closure was performed in 78.1% of emergency surgery cases compared to 42.9% with elective surgery.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, tumor stage was the strongest independent predictor of postoperative morbidity. Stage III CRC had more than threefold increased odds of postoperative morbidities [aOR 3.46], while Stage IV disease was associated with a markedly higher risk (aOR 7.23). Given the low event rate in some strata, penalized regression models were applied for mortality analysis, and results were consistent in direction and statistical significance with the primary models.

The independent predictors of ICU admission included increasing age (aOR of 1.08 per year), higher ASA class (ASA ≥ II; aOR of 176.76), advanced tumor stage (Stage IV; aOR of 232.08), and postoperative complications (aOR of 36.13; Table 4).

Follow-up, mortality, and recurrence timing and patterns

The maximum follow-up duration was 60 months; the median was 36 months (IQR 36–48). Follow-up was truncated at the time of death for non-survivors. Among the 75 (12.4%) deaths, more than half occurred within the first 6 months, followed by 7–12 months and 13–24 months. Late mortality after two years was uncommon and reported among patients with advanced-stage disease or emergency presentation. Deaths after three years were rare. Tumor recurrence occurred in 72 patients (12.3%). No recurrences were observed in Stage I disease. Recurrence was documented in 24 of 499 Stage II patients (4.8%) and in 48 of 105 Stage III patients (45.7%), with Stage III disease accounting for 66.7% of all recurrence events. Most frequently, recurrence occurred in the second year, followed by the third, fourth and fifth years, mainly affecting rectal tumors, emergency cases and Stage III disease (Fig. 4).

Survival analysis

In evaluating overall survival, the Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a progressive decline over time. The median overall survival was not reached during the follow-up period; however, estimated overall survival rates were 92.0% at 1 year, 89.7% at 2 years, and 87.2% at 3 years. Disease-free survival similarly declined over time. The recurrence-free survival rates are 95.6% at 1 year, 89.8% at 2 years, and 86.4% at 3 years. There was a statistically significant difference in survival by tumor stage and emergency presentation (log-rank p < 0.001 for both) (Fig. 5).

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis for overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) following colorectal cancer surgery. Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrating overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS), stratified by tumor stage and emergency versus elective presentation. On assessing overall survival using Cox proportional hazards modelling, several variables remained independently associated with mortality. The higher ASA class had nearly a threefold increased hazard of death with a hazard ratio [HR] of 2.91, while emergency surgery conferred a fourfold higher mortality risk with an HR of 3.90. Postoperative complications were independently associated with reduced survival (HR 2.55). Tumor stage was the strongest factor influencing mortality, with Stage III disease associated with a sevenfold increase in death risk with HR of 7.04 and Stage IV disease conferring an extreme mortality hazard with HR of 39.09 compared with Stage II disease.

Predictors of mortality and recurrence

Increasing age was an independent predictor of mortality, with an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of approximately 1.07–1.08 per year (p < 0.01). Emergency surgery remained a dominant predictor of mortality (aOR 21.78, 95% CI 5.72–82.90; p < 0.001).

Advanced tumor stage was strongly associated with mortality. Stage III disease demonstrated markedly increased odds of mortality, while Stage IV disease showed extremely high effect estimates, reflecting sparse events in these strata.

In models incorporating postoperative variables, postoperative complications (aOR 65.37, 95% CI 9.15–467.02) and ICU admission (aOR 36.13, 95% CI 1.61–808.87) were independently associated with mortality.

These large effect sizes should be interpreted with caution, as sparse event distribution may inflate odds ratios despite the use of penalized regression techniques. Gender, tumor location, and stoma formation were not independent predictors of mortality (Fig. 6; Table 4).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis of factors associated with tumour recurrence. Forest plot showing adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals from multivariable logistic regression analysis.

In a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model, increased mortality was independently associated with higher ASA class, emergency surgery, advanced tumor stage, stoma formation, and postoperative morbidity. On evaluating the impact of tumor stage, the analysis revealed that Stage II- III (HR 7.04, 95% CI 3.16–15.68) and Stage IV (HR 39.09, 95% CI 14.18–107.77) were strongly associated with mortality. Emergency presentation conferred a four-fold increase in risk (HR 3.90, 95% CI 1.88–8.10).

Using Competing-risk Aalen–Johansen analysis in assessing recurrence labelling death as a competing event, and Cause-specific Cox with death censored. The analysis demonstrated that the cumulative incidence of recurrence was 3.5% at the first year, 8.3% at the second year, 11.9% at the third year, and 12.3% at the fifth year. The independent predictors of recurrence in cause-specific Cox regression restricted to Stage II–III disease were Stage III disease, rectal tumor location, stoma formation, and anastomotic leak.

Recurrence risk was further evaluated using a Cox proportional hazards model. Recurrence analysis was restricted to patients with stage II–III disease following curative-intent resection, as stage IV disease was not applicable for recurrence analysis. In this model, Stage III disease was the dominant predictor of recurrence, with more than a twenty-fold increased hazard HR of 22.84 compared with Stage II disease.

Rectal tumor location was independently associated with a substantially higher recurrence risk, with an HR of 7.31. Additional predictors of recurrence included anastomotic leak (HR 3.70), stoma formation (HR 1.76), and higher ASA class (HR 1.90), underscoring the combined oncologic and physiological determinants of disease relapse.

Stoma outcomes and reversal

A stoma was created for 164 patients (27.2%); 96 patients (58.5%) underwent stoma reversal, with a median time to closure of 6 months (IQR 4–6). Reversal was more common in elective cases and in patients with Stage II disease, whereas it was delayed or not performed in patients with Stage III–IV disease, emergency presentation, or ongoing chemotherapy requirements.

Bivariate comparative analyses

Emergency surgery was predominantly performed for more advanced disease stage (p < 0.001/Stage III–IV: 53.8% vs. 5.4%) and was associated with substantially adverse outcomes, including higher stoma creation (p < 0.001/46.8% vs. 20.3%), postoperative complications (p < 0.001/19.2% vs. 6.2%), ICU admission (p = 0.012/13.5% vs. 6.9%), recurrence (p < 0.001/24.4% vs. 6.7%), mortality (p < 0.001/37.8% vs. 3.6%), and longer LOS (p < 0.001/median 10 vs. 9 days) (Fig. 7).

Unadjusted comparative effects of key clinical factors on mortality and tumour recurrence.

Tumor stage exerted a dominant influence on outcomes. Compared with Stage II disease, Stage III disease was associated with significantly higher postoperative morbidity (p < 0.001/25.3% vs. 6.0%), ICU admission (p < 0.001/18.7% vs. 5.4%), tumor recurrence (p < 0.001/48.4% vs. 4.8%), mortality (p < 0.001/56.0% vs. 2.8%), and prolonged hospital stay (p < 0.001/median 12 vs. 9 days).

Patients underwent stoma creation were exposed to adverse outcomes across all major endpoints including higher postoperative complications (p = 0.010/14.6% vs. 7.7%), ICU utilization (p = 0.025/12.8% vs. 7.0%,), recurrence (p < 0.001/28.7% vs. 4.8%), mortality (p < 0.001/25.6% vs. 7.5%), and longer hospital stay (p < 0.001/median 10 vs. 7 days).

On assessing the impact of tumor location, the analysis revealed that right-sided tumors showed more favorable perioperative and oncologic outcomes, including lower recurrence rates in relation to left-sided and rectal tumors, showing 4.5% vs. 15.2% and p < 0.001 and shorter LOS, showing median 9 vs. 9–10 days and p = 0.009/. However, no significant differences were demonstrated with postoperative morbidity, ICU admission, or mortality. On comparing rectal and colonic tumors, it was demonstrated that rectal tumors had significantly higher stoma creation (67.1% vs. 12.6%; p < 0.001) and recurrence rates (24.2% vs. 6.5%; p < 0.001). Whereas colonic tumors exhibited significantly higher mortality with values 15.6% vs. 3.7% and p < 0.001.

Multivariable regression, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses

Rectal cancer subgroup

The rectal subgroup analysis revealed that 161 patients presented with rectal cancer, and 29 (18.0%) underwent emergency surgery. The frequency of tumor stage showed that Stage II occurred in 137 (85.1%) patients and Stage III in 23 (14.3%) patients. A stoma was created for 108 (67.1%) patients, postoperative complications were reported in 12 (7.5%) patients, ICU admission was indicated for 14 (8.7%) patients, and in-hospital mortality occurred in 6 (3.7%) patients. While the median LOS was 10 days (IQR 7–12), tumor recurrence occurred in 41 (25.5%) patients.

In the rectal cancer subgroups multivariable analysis, Stage III tumor was a strong independent predictor of stoma formation, with an adjusted OR of 13.55 (95% CI 1.58–116.27) and p = 0.017. Additionally, male sex was independently associated with increased odds of stoma creation, with an adjusted OR of 2.12 (95% CI 1.03–4.36; p = 0.040). Other findings included high stoma creation and recurrence rates among lower rectal Stage III tumors.

Emergency surgery subgroup

Within the emergency surgery subgroup, 156 patients underwent emergency colorectal cancer surgery; Stage III disease was present in 76 (48.7%), Stage II in 72 (46.2%), and Stage IV in 8 (5.1%). A stoma was created in 73 (46.8%) patients. ICU utilization was noted in 21 (13.5%) patients, postoperative complications in 30 (19.2%) patients, mortality in 59 (37.8%) patients, and tumor recurrence in 38 (24.4%) patients. The median LOS was 10 days (IQR 7–12).

Within this subgroup, multivariable regression analysis identified Stage III disease as the strongest independent predictor of mortality, with an adjusted OR of 37.17 (95% CI 9.52–145.14) and p < 0.001.

Discussion

Overall interpretation and principal findings

This study represents one of the largest and most granular single-center colorectal cancer outcomes datasets reported from Eastern Sudan and sub-Saharan Africa. Beyond descriptive epidemiology, this study provides comprehensive longitudinal outcomes data, including perioperative, operative, and oncological information. These included stoma formation and reversal, ICU admission, survival analysis, competing-risk modelling for recurrence, and multivariable predictors of adverse outcomes, which are underreported domains in African CRC literature40–44.

The analysis revealed several significant key findings. Advanced CRC stage and emergency surgery were the dominant determinants of postoperative complications, ICU utilization, recurrence, and mortality, consistently across bivariate, multivariable, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses. Although right-sided tumor location was favorable across several perioperative and oncologic endpoints in unadjusted analyses, this effect was not observed with multivariable adjustment. Despite substantial systemic barriers in LMICs, adherence to adjuvant chemotherapy was exceptionally high, underscoring an important institutional strength. Collectively, these findings emphasize that delayed diagnosis, acute physiological compromise, and emergency surgery remain predictors of poor CRC outcomes in resource-limited settings19–25.

Although right-sided tumor location was deemed favorable in unadjusted analyses, it was not protective in multivariable models. After adjustment, right-sided location was not an independent predictor of overall postoperative morbidity, demonstrating that laterality alone does not mitigate global surgical risk. However, right-sided tumors showed a strong and independent protective effect against anastomotic leak, with a 73% reduction in leak risk compared with left-sided and rectal tumors. This likely doesn’t reflect broader oncologic or physiological advantages; rather, it is attributable to anatomical and technical factors, including superior vascular supply, reduced pelvic dissection, and lower anastomotic tension. Accordingly, tumor laterality should be interpreted as a procedure-specific modifier of anastomotic integrity, rather than a universal determinant of postoperative or survival outcomes.

Epidemiology, age distribution, and presentation patterns

Early-onset CRC is a recognized global phenomenon increasingly reported in African and Middle Eastern cohorts. Similarly, our results reported a relatively young mean age of 53.5 years, with nearly one-third of patients aged under 50 years8–11,45–48. In contrast, the median age spans across the sixth to seventh decade across CRC cohorts from high-income countries1–4. Proposed causative factors include demographic structure, dietary habits, westernization, increasing obesity, environmental factors, alterations in the microbiome, and genetic predisposition45–49. These findings urge a critical appraisal of age-based CRC screening guidelines derived from high-income countries (HICs) populations to African settings11,16.

Emergency presentations in this cohort, predominantly due to intestinal obstruction or perforation, accounted for approximately one-quarter of cases. In contrast, emergency presentation rates declined to below 15% in HICs due to the expansion of screening and early detection pathways; rates of 30–60% continue to be reported across sub-Saharan Africa12,50–53. The core contributors to late presentation and delayed diagnosis in LMICs include limited public awareness, limited access to colonoscopy, prolonged referral pathways, and financial constraints12,14,54–56. Compared to the high capture of chronic medical illnesses in HICs datasets, the study revealed a lower documented prevalence of comorbidities in this cohort. This should be interpreted cautiously, as chronic conditions are frequently underdiagnosed or under-recorded in underserved populations, potentially underestimating operative risk57,58.

Tumor stage distribution and oncologic context

On assessing disease stages, Stage II disease predominated in this cohort. Although the distribution is numerically accurate, it differs from many HIC reports, in which Stage III disease is more prevalent at diagnosis37,53. In the present setting, this pattern is likely attributable to a combination of limited preoperative staging, reliance on postoperative pathology, particularly in emergency resections, and potential stage migration. Similar observations have been documented in other African CRC cohorts and highlight the limitations of staging accuracy in resource-limited environments40,42. An additional contributing factor may be suboptimal lymph node harvest, particularly in emergency resections where adherence to oncologic principles may be compromised by physiological instability, contamination, or limited operative time. Inadequate nodal assessment can result in under-staging (stage migration), artificially inflating the proportion of Stage II disease. This phenomenon has been described in resource-limited and emergency surgical settings and should be considered when interpreting stage distribution in this cohort.

Survival outcomes and timing of adverse events

Although the median overall survival was not reached at 60 months of follow-up, at 36 months of median follow-up, overall and disease-free survival declined progressively over time. This reflects relatively favorable short- to mid-term survival among patients surviving the early postoperative phase. Despite markedly different resource environments, estimated survival rates at 12, 24, and 36 months were similar to those reported in several LMICs and selected HIC cohorts38,39,59–63. However, outcome heterogeneity was substantial. More than 50% of mortality occurred within the first 6 months, and the majority of recurrences clustered within the first two years. These patterns remain consistent with international reports documenting that most CRC failures occur early after surgery, particularly among cohorts with advanced disease or postoperative morbidity38,39,55,63. The compounded impact of tumor burden, limited perioperative optimization, malnutrition, and sepsis in LMIC settings has led to disproportional accounting of emergency presentation and advanced disease stage for early mortality and recurrence24,25,64–66.

Emergency surgery as a central prognostic determinant

Even after multivariate adjustment, emergency surgery emerged as one of the strongest independent predictors of mortality, a finding consistent with robust international evidence showing a stepwise increase in mortality risk from elective to urgent to emergency CRC surgery19,24,67. Although emergency CRC surgery is a predictor of adverse outcomes even in HICs, its impact is amplified in LMICs due to limited access to preoperative optimization, critical care, interventional radiology, and advanced perioperative support24,25,68. This occurs through a causal pathway initiated with acute presentation leading to physiological derangement, increased postoperative morbidity, ICU admission, and early mortality.

Operative strategy, stoma formation, and anastomotic leak

Stoma creation rate was 25%, predominantly high in emergency and rectal cancer cases, which conforms with oncologic safety principles prioritizing risk mitigation in physiologically compromised patients54,62,69. Although stoma formation was an independent predictor of mortality, this association is likely attributable to confounders by indication rather than causation, as stoma creation is preferentially performed in unstable or high-risk patients—a pattern consistently demonstrated in both LMIC and HIC cohorts60,61. Although propensity score matching could further mitigate confounding by indication, this was not performed due to sample size and event limitations; however, multivariable adjustment and consistent findings across models support the robustness of this association.

In this study, anastomotic leaks occurred in 8.5% of all colonic anastomoses, within accepted international benchmarks46,47. Anastomotic leak is an independent risk factor for increased recurrence in competing-risk models. This link is attributed to inflammatory responses, immune dysregulation, and delays or omission of adjuvant therapy38,55,63. Due to anatomical, physiological, and biological differences, right-sided resections demonstrated lower leak rates and improved outcomes, consistent with reports from large HIC registries26,53.

Oncologic therapy: institutional strengths and systemic gaps

The exceptionally high uptake of adjuvant chemotherapy, exceeding 90% across most stages, is one of this cohort’s most notable strengths. This rate approaches levels in high-income countries and surpasses reported levels from many African series, where chemotherapy access remains restricted36–39. Despite systemic constraints, this finding underscores the effectiveness of institutional coordination and patient engagement. However, multimodal rectal cancer therapy was underutilized, which was attributable to limited access to radiotherapy, the absence of routine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based staging, and high rates of emergency presentation, constraining the use of neoadjuvant protocols. Several studies from LMICs reported similar barriers to oncology systems, leading to the higher recurrence burden observed in rectal cancers in this cohort39,54,61.

Predictors of morbidity, mortality, and recurrence

Multivariable analyses revealed that advanced stage, emergency presentation, age, and procedure-related risk were the primary drivers of postoperative morbidity and ICU admission, patterns aligning with global surgical oncology reports51,52,59. As postoperative morbidity is an independent risk factor of mortality, prevention of postoperative complications is a critical modifiable target in reducing mortality rates27,28. Stage III disease and rectal location were the strongest predictors of recurrence, which was dominated by tumor biology, reflecting nodal disease burden and pelvic recurrence risk38,39,59 (Fig. 6). Right-sided tumors again demonstrated favorable behavior, supporting emerging evidence of sidedness-related biological differences in CRC26,31,53 (Fig. 5).

The magnitude of effect estimates for mortality in the multivariable logistic regression models — particularly for advanced tumor stage and higher ASA class — should be interpreted in light of sparse event counts within these high-risk strata. Such data configurations may lead to quasi-separation, predisposing to inflated odds ratios and wider confidence intervals, even when penalized regression techniques are applied. To support the robustness of the identified predictors, the directionality and relative strength of these associations were consistently corroborated by Cox proportional hazards modelling and sensitivity analyses.

Rectal cancer subgroup considerations

Rectal cancers displayed a distinct pattern characterized by higher recurrence and stoma creation rates but comparatively lower incidence of early mortality. This pattern demonstrates younger age and elective surgical procedures, counterbalanced by restricted access to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and advanced pelvic staging. African reports declared similar challenges across rectal cancer research, which contrast sharply with high-income countries’ settings, where multimodal therapy is standard36,39,65.

Strengths

This study’s strengths include its large sample size, detailed operative and outcomes data, long-term follow-up, and the use of advanced analytical models, including survival and competing-risks modelling.

Limitations

Limitations include the retrospective study design, incomplete pathological profiling, limited access to radiotherapy, and potential under documentation of comorbidities, all common in LMIC surgical oncology settings. Additionally, surgeon-level data (general vs. colorectal specialist) were not available, precluding analysis of its impact on outcomes, particularly in emergency settings.

Recommendations

The study findings highlight the significance of earlier diagnosis, enhanced perioperative optimization, enhanced emergency CRC pathways, improved access to radiotherapy, and strengthened surveillance within the first two years after surgery—particularly for Stage III and rectal cancers.

Conclusion

Colorectal cancer in Eastern Sudan is characterized by delayed presentation, frequent emergency surgery, and early postoperative morbidity and mortality. The strongest determinants of morbidity recurrence and mortality were advanced tumor stage and emergency presentation. Rectal and left-sided tumors exhibited the worst recurrence pattern, reflecting both tumor biology and challenges in providing multimodal therapy. Whereas right-sided tumors showed a favorable anastomotic profile, but after adjustment, no independent survival advantage was demonstrated. Despite these limitations, major institutional strength is demonstrated with the exceptionally high adherence to adjuvant chemotherapy. More than half of mortality and the majority of recurrences are reported within the first two years after surgery, highlighting the critical vulnerability of the early postoperative and surveillance period. Focused investments in early diagnosis, perioperative optimization, radiotherapy availability, and structured post-operative surveillance—particularly during the first two years—are critical to narrowing outcome disparities between low-resource and high-income settings.
