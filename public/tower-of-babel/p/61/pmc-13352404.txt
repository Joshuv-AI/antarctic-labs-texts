Prevalence and risk factors of hypertensive disorders during pregnancy in Somalia: A multilevel analysis of the 2020 Demographic and Health Survey
Authors: Abdisalam Mahdi Hassan, Abdirahman Omer Ali, Muhyadin Yusuf Dahir, Nimo Mohamoud Barakale, Md. Moyazzem Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) are significant contributors to maternal mortality, particularly in low-resource settings like Somalia. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of this condition during antenatal care (ANC) and identify associated risk factors among pregnant women in Somalia.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed data from 3034 women aged 15-49 years who had at least one ANC visit for their most recent live birth, using the 2020 Somalia Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS). Multilevel logistic regression was employed to identify associated individual and contextual factors.ResultsThe overall prevalence of reported HDP during ANC was 11.0%. In the adjusted multilevel model, factors significantly associated with of reported HDP included primary education (Adjusted Odds Ratio [AOR]: 0.62, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.43–0.90), secondary education (AOR: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.19–0.79), attending four or more ANC visits (AOR: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.31–0.69), and delivering in a health facility (AOR: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.41–0.71). Pronounced regional disparities were observed, with women in regions such as Banadir (AOR: 4.94, 95% CI: 2.36–10.32) reporting a significantly higher rate of HDP than those in the Awdal region.ConclusionFindings reveal that women with higher education and more frequent ANC visits had lower odds of reporting HDP, a counterintuitive result that suggests complex underlying factors in diagnosis and reporting. Urgent, targeted interventions are imperative to address regional inequities and understand the drivers of HDP to improve maternal health outcomes.

Introduction

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), encompassing conditions such as gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, and eclampsia, represent a formidable global health challenge and are leading contributors to maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality worldwide [1,2]. These conditions significantly elevate risks for adverse outcomes, including preterm birth, stillbirth, low birth weight, and maternal death [3]. Conversely, hypotension during pregnancy, although less common, can also pose risks [4]. Consequently, routine blood pressure (BP) monitoring during antenatal care (ANC) is a cornerstone of safe maternity care, enabling the early detection and timely management of abnormalities, thereby mitigating the potential for severe complications [5]. For many pregnant women, particularly in resource-limited settings, this fundamental assessment can be pivotal in averting life-threatening events, underscoring its critical public health importance. Globally, HDP affects an estimated 10-15% of pregnancies [6]. However, the burden is disproportionately borne by low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where outcomes are often more severe due to constrained healthcare systems and limited access to care [7]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, for instance, HDP contributes to approximately 16% of maternal deaths, exceeding the global average [8]. This disparity highlights the critical need for effective ANC, including consistent BP monitoring, in regions facing substantial healthcare delivery challenges [6]. Eastern Africa, including nations like Somalia, faces a significant HDP burden, often exacerbated by intersecting socioeconomic vulnerabilities and fragile health infrastructure [9].

Somalia presents a unique and challenging context for maternal healthcare delivery. Decades of conflict and political instability have resulted in a fragmented and severely under-resourced healthcare system [10]. The nation grapples with some of the world's highest maternal mortality ratios, reflecting deep-seated systemic weaknesses [11]. Access to and utilization of essential health services, including ANC, remain critically low, especially in rural, remote, and conflict-affected areas [8]. Pregnant women's ability and willingness to seek care are further impeded by pervasive poverty, low female literacy and autonomy, limited health information, and socio-cultural norms [12]. This complex interplay of factors creates significant barriers to receiving timely and adequate care, including fundamental interventions like BP monitoring during pregnancy.

Despite the established importance of clinical assessment in ANC for preventing adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes, there is a paucity of context-specific evidence from Somalia. While valuable research exists from neighboring countries like Ethiopia [13] and Tanzania [14] examining factors influencing ANC components, the unique socio-political landscape, security situation, and healthcare system constraints in Somalia necessitate a dedicated investigation. To date, there is a lack of in-depth studies that have utilized nationally representative data to specifically examine the prevalence and determinants of HDP during pregnancy within Somalia. The outcome variable in the study is based on whether the respondent reported being diagnosed with HDP during an ANC visit for her most recent live birth in the five years preceding the survey. Therefore, this study aims to address this critical knowledge gap by leveraging data from the recent Somalia Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS)-2020. This study determines the prevalence of reported hypertensive disorders of pregnancy among women in Somalia who attended at least one ANC visit and identifies the individual, household, and community-level factors associated with reporting HDP.

Methods and materials

Study design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional design and analyzed secondary data from the Somalia Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS) 2020. The study setting is Somalia, a country facing significant challenges in healthcare delivery due to protracted conflict and instability, which particularly impact maternal health services [15]. The SHDS 2020 provides data collected across all 18 pre-war administrative regions, encompassing urban, rural, and nomadic populations.

Data source and study population

The data source for this analysis is the SHDS 2020, the first comprehensive, internationally comparable health and demographic survey conducted in Somalia. The survey was implemented by the Directorate of National Statistics (DNS) of the Ministry of Planning, Investment and Economic Development, Federal Government of Somalia, in collaboration with the Ministry of Health and Human Services and Federal Member States' statistical offices, with technical assistance from ICF through The DHS Program. Fieldwork took place primarily between February 2018 and January 2019. The study population for this specific analysis includes women of reproductive age (15-49 years) who had at least one live birth in the five years preceding the survey and reported attending at least one antenatal care (ANC) visit for their most recent birth during that period. All incomplete and missing observations are deleted from the dataset. After applying these inclusion criteria to the SHDS dataset, the final analytical sample size for this study was 3034. This sample comprises all eligible women captured within the SHDS 2020 dataset who met the study's inclusion criteria.

Sampling strategy

The SHDS 2020 utilized a multi-stage stratified cluster sampling methodology designed to generate a probability sample representative at the national level, for urban, rural, and nomadic populations separately, and for the 18 pre-war administrative regions.

Stratification: The country was first stratified into its 18 administrative regions. Within each region, the population was further stratified into urban, rural, and nomadic strata.

Stage 1 (Cluster Selection): In urban and rural strata, Enumeration Areas (EAs) from the 2014 Population Estimation Survey of Somalia (PESS) served as the Primary Sampling Units (PSUs). EAs were selected independently within each stratum with probability proportional to size (PPS), where size was the estimated number of households from the PESS sampling frame. In nomadic strata, settlements identified as Toghers, normally used by Nomads (TNS), served as PSUs. TNS was selected with PPS based on estimated household numbers.

Stage 2 (Household Listing and Segment Selection - Urban/Rural): A full household listing operation was conducted in all selected EAs. For large EAs, segmentation was performed, and one segment was randomly selected as the Secondary Sampling Unit (SSU) with probability proportional to segment size (household count).

Stage 3 (Household Selection): In selected urban/rural EAs/SSUs, 30 households per cluster were selected using systematic random sampling from the newly created household lists. In selected nomadic TNS, 30 households were targeted for selection using systematic random sampling. If a TNS contained fewer than 30 households, all households were included.

Individual Respondent Selection: Within each selected household, all women aged 15-49 who were either permanent residents or visitors present the night before the survey were eligible and invited to participate in the individual woman's questionnaire.

Data collection

Data were collected by trained field teams using standardized questionnaires that were translated into local languages and pre-tested. Interviews were conducted face-to-face with eligible respondents using tablet computers (Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing - CAPI), which helped ensure data quality through built-in checks and automated skip patterns. Fieldwork supervision included regular check-ins and data quality reviews.

Variables and measurements

Outcome Variable: The outcome variable was whether the respondent reported having been diagnosed with HDP during an ANC visit for her most recent live birth in the five years preceding the survey. This was derived from the SHDS question asking if, during the pregnancy, they were told they had high blood pressure. The DHS measures HDP based on self-reported hypertension during pregnancy rather than a clinically verified HDP diagnosis. It was coded as a binary variable: “1” if the woman reported having HDP, and “0” if not. We acknowledge that this variable is based on maternal recall, a standard approach in DHS surveys, but it is subject to potential recall and reporting biases.

Independent Variables: Based on existing literature [5,[16], [17], [18]] and data availability in the SHDS, independent variables were categorized as follows:

Individual-Level Factors: Maternal age (15-19, 20-24, 25-29, 30-34, 35-39, 40-49 years), maternal education (No education, Primary, Secondary, or Higher). sex of household head (Male, Female), household wealth index (Poorest, Poorer, Middle, Richer, Richest), parity (Primiparous (1 birth), Multiparous (2-4 births), Grand multiparous (5+ births)), timing of first ANC visit (<4 months, 4-6 months, >6 months, or never attended), number of ANC visits (<4 visits, 4 or more visits), and place of delivery (Home, Health facility).

Contextual Factors: Region: The 18 pre-war administrative regions (Awdal, Waqoyi Galbeed, Togdheer, Sool, Sanaag, Bari, Nugaal, Mudug, Galguduud, Hiiraan, Middle Shabelle, Banadir, Lower Shabelle, Bay, Bakool, Gedo, Middle Juba, Lower Juba - list simplified if needed for analysis/reporting), and place of residence (Urban, Rural, Nomadic).

Multilevel model

Using multilevel logistic regression, the significant factors contributing to postnatal care in Bangladesh have been identified. Individuals are considered level-1, and clusters are level-2 in this study hierarchy. Silvia introduces the applications of multilevel modeling for nested and hierarchical data [19]. The residuals at level 1 are assumed to follow normality, and the residuals at level 2 are assumed to follow multivariate normality; moreover, the residuals at levels 1 and 2 are independent. Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using STATA version 16. Survey weights provided in the SHDS dataset were applied throughout the analysis to account for the complex sampling design and ensure nationally representative estimates. Frequencies and percentages were calculated to describe the characteristics of the study population and the prevalence of HDP during ANC, stratified by independent variables. Due to the hierarchical nature of the SHDS data (individuals nested within households, which are in turn nested within clusters/EAs), a multilevel logistic regression analysis was employed to identify factors associated with the outcome variable while accounting for clustering effects. The multilevel logistic regression analysis inherently handles missing data through list-wise deletion for the variables included in the model. The authors sequentially fitted four models: Model 0 (Null Model): No independent variables, to assess the extent of cluster-level variance using the Intra-Cluster Correlation Coefficient (ICC). Model I: Included only individual-level factors. Model II: Included only contextual factors. Model III: Included both individual and contextual factors. Model comparison was performed using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values, with the model exhibiting the lowest AIC and BIC considered the best fit. Variables with a p <0.20 in the bivariable multilevel analysis were considered candidates for the final multivariable model. However, in the final multivariable model, factors with a p <0.05 were considered statistically significant predictors of having HDP during ANC. Results were presented as Adjusted Odds Ratios (AOR) with their corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). Moreover, no sensitivity analyses were performed in this study.

Results

The analysis included data from 3034 women aged 15-49 who reported at least one ANC visit for their most recent live birth in the five years preceding the SHDS 2020 survey. The socio-demographic, obstetric, and healthcare utilization characteristics of the study participants are presented in Table 1. The overall prevalence of reported Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy (HDP) for the most recent pregnancy was 11.0% (334 out of 3034 women). This highlights a significant gap in the provision of this essential ANC service in Somalia. Regarding sample distribution, women were distributed across various age groups, with the largest proportions in the 25-29 (28.7%) and 20-24 (22.5%) year categories. Most households were male-headed (68.2%). A large majority of respondents had no formal education (71.2%). The sample was distributed across wealth quintiles, with over half categorized in the “Rich” quintile (56.4%) based on the DHS wealth index calculation (see Methods). Participants were drawn from all 18 pre-war regions, with the largest proportions residing in Banadir (19.7%, largely urban) and Woqooyi Galbeed (9.9%). The results of the Chi-square tests presented in Table 1 also examine the association between participant characteristics and reported HDP during ANC. Level of education (p <0.001) and wealth index (p =0.001) were statistically significantly associated. Counterintuitively, women with no education reported the highest rate of HDP (13.0%), while those with secondary (4.4%) and higher education (4.4%) reported the lowest. Similarly, the association with wealth was nonlinear; the middle quintile had the highest rate (14.4%), followed by the poorest (12.5%), and the richest had the lowest rate (9.2%). Parity approached statistical significance (p =0.079), with primiparous women having the lowest rate (8.0%). The timing of the first ANC visit was not significant (p =0.210). The number of ANC visits (<0.001) and place of delivery (<0.001) were highly significantly associated with reported HDP, exhibiting unexpected patterns. Women with fewer than 4 ANC visits had a higher HDP rate (13.1%) than those with 4 or more visits (4.3%). Similarly, women delivering at home reported a higher rate of HDP (15.1%) than those delivering in a health facility (6.5%). Significant regional variation (p <0.001) was observed, with rates ranging from 3 to 5% in Togdheer, Galgaduud, Awdal, and Woqooyi Galbeed to 17-20% in Hiraan, Middle Shabelle, Bakool, Banadir, and Bari.

Four distinct models for predicting HDP during pregnancy were compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and the results are given in Table 2. To comprehensively investigate the multifaceted determinants of HDP, Model III was selected for the final multivariable logistic regression analysis. The results of multilevel mixed-effects logistic regression models are presented in Table 3, which identified several significant predictors of HDP assessment during pregnancy among 3034 women. In this model, Adjusted Odds Ratios (AORs) below 1.00 indicate lower odds of reporting HDP, while AORs above 1.00 indicate higher odds, relative to the respective reference category. Compared to women with no formal education, those with primary education (AOR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.43–0.90, p<0.05) and secondary education (AOR: 0.39, 95% CI: 0.19–0.79, p<0.05) had significantly lower odds of reporting HDP. Women who attended four or more ANC visits exhibited significantly lower odds of reporting HDP (AOR: 0.46, 95% CI: 0.31–0.69, p<0.001) compared to those with fewer than four visits. Delivery in a health facility was also associated with decreased odds of reporting HDP (AOR: 0.54, 95% CI: 0.41–0.71, p<0.001) when contrasted with home delivery. Pronounced geographical disparities in HDP were observed. Relative to the Awdal region, women residing in Bari (AOR: 3.34, 95% CI: 1.41–7.89, p <0.05), Nugaal (AOR: 2.50, 95% CI: 1.07–5.81, p <0.05), Mudug (AOR: 2.96, 95% CI: 1.25–6.99, p <0.05), Hiraan (AOR: 3.79, 95% CI: 1.56–9.16, p <0.05), Middle Shabelle (AOR: 2.81, 95% CI: 1.26–6.27, p <0.05), Banadir (AOR: 4.94, 95% CI: 2.36–10.32, p <0.05), and Bakool (AOR: 2.58, 95% CI: 1.14–5.86, p <0.05) demonstrated significantly higher odds of having HDP during pregnancy. Discussion

This study provides the first nationally representative analysis of reported hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) during antenatal care (ANC) in Somalia, revealing a prevalence of 11.0% and identifying key individual and contextual factors associated with its reporting. The findings reveal a complex public health landscape, characterized by unexpected associations between healthcare utilization and reported HDP, as well as stark regional disparities. The overall prevalence of reported HDP (11.0%) is within the range of 5-15% seen in global studies and is comparable to figures from other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [21] and falls far short of the universal coverage expected for such a fundamental component of ANC aimed at detecting hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) [22]. For instance, studies in neighboring Ethiopia [23] and Tanzania [16], which often highlight challenges, also report higher utilization of specific ANC components; however, a direct comparison of BP assessment prevalence requires careful consideration of study populations and methodologies. The exceptionally low rate in Somalia likely reflects the severe under-resourcing and fragmentation of its healthcare system [24], further exacerbated by ongoing conflict and limited access to services, particularly in remote and insecure areas [25]. The visits to clinics for ANC also give medical professionals a chance to quickly identify pregnant women who are at high risk for HDP and other issues so that proper therapy can be started. In the end, this will lessen the burden of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality [26]. However, the burden of HDPs was significant in urban health facilities, with a large proportion of their women receiving quality ANC and having great information on HDPs, as compared to mothers receiving treatment at periurban health facilities [27]. In order to manage hypertensive disorders of pregnancy in Sub-Saharan Africa, promising strategies include improving prenatal care and screening, and educating mothers [28].

Findings depict that higher maternal education has lower odds of reporting HDP than illiterate women. Findings also suggest that attending four or more ANC visits and delivering in a health facility are associated with lower odds of reporting HDP, consistent with findings from other LMIC settings [29,30]. This aligns with WHO recommendations for optimal ANC contact [31] and the general observation that engagement with the formal health system increases exposure to recommended interventions. This may reflect the benefits of regular maternal health care, including early detection and management of elevated blood pressure, provision of preventive interventions, improved health education, and timely access to skilled obstetric care. Additionally, greater utilization of maternal health services may indicate higher health literacy and better socioeconomic conditions, both of which are associated with improved pregnancy outcomes. Moreover, in low-resource settings, women with higher health care utilization may be more likely to receive a diagnosis or recall hypertension during pregnancy, which could influence prevalence estimates. A previous study also reported that compared to women with basic education, those with higher education were six times more likely to have sufficient knowledge about pregnancy-related hypertension problems [26]. Generally, women who attend more ANC visits and deliver in facilities have greater opportunities for HDP monitoring by trained personnel; however, the study's findings are inconsistent with the existing literature and established clinical understanding. This may have happened because of a self-reported study. It is also possible that misreporting and underserved populations lead to higher-than-expected diagnosis rates within these groups. These findings underscore that the relationship between healthcare access and HDP reporting in Somalia is not linear and warrants a deeper, qualitative investigation. These unadjusted associations starkly contrast with the multivariable results and the existing literature [32,33]. However, the adjusted model, which accounts for a wider array of variables and clustering, provides a more reliable picture, indicating that formal education and engagement with the health system are indeed positive determinants.

A significant finding of this study is the noticeable regional disparities in HDP. Women in several regions (Bari, Nugaal, Mudug, Hiraan, Middle Shabelle, Banadir, and Bakool) had significantly higher odds of HDP compared to Awdal. These geographical variations likely reflect inequities in healthcare infrastructure, the availability of trained health personnel, security situations, and the accessibility of services across Somalia's regions [8,34]. Such disparities highlight the need for appropriate actions to improve healthcare access, quality, and infrastructure to close health disparities, especially in underprivileged communities. Therefore, to guarantee equal health outcomes nationwide, policymakers should consider region-specific approaches, such as interventions, and allocate resources effectively [35]. The finding that urban residence was not a significant predictor in the final model, after controlling for other factors, including region, is noteworthy, suggesting that within regions, factors such as facility availability and quality may be more dominant than a simple urban-rural dichotomy. Since lifestyle plays a crucial role in population health, particularly cardiovascular health, a healthy E(e)SEEDi lifestyle [36,37], is a vital strategy for preventing hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) and improving clinical outcomes. In particular, it's helpful to reduce pronounced regional disparities in HDP through fair, accessible, and easy universal health coverage (UHC). Moreover, the Hi-APPLE model [38], is also crucial for improving maternal and women's health, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), and for achieving the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

The findings have direct implications for achieving several of the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). The low prevalence of HDP directly impacts SDG 3, Target 3.1 (reduce the global maternal mortality ratio) and Target 3.2 (end preventable deaths of newborns and children under 5), as undetected and unmanaged HDP are major contributors to maternal and perinatal mortality [39]. Improving access to and quality of ANC, including routine BP monitoring, is crucial for achieving SDG 3.8 (achieve universal health coverage, including financial risk protection, access to quality essential health-care services). Furthermore, the association between maternal education and BP assessment uptake underscores the interconnectedness with SDG 4 (Quality Education) and SDG 5 (Gender Equality), as empowering women through education can lead to better health-seeking behaviors and outcomes [40]. The observed regional disparities also speak to SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities).

Limitations of the study

This study has a few limitations. First, the analysis is based on cross-sectional Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) data, which capture exposure and outcome information at a single point in time. Consequently, temporal relationships between risk factors and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) cannot be established, and causal inferences cannot be drawn. The observed associations should therefore be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Second, the outcome variable was based on self-reported symptoms and/or diagnoses of HDP, which may be subject to recall bias and misclassification. Women may have difficulty accurately recalling pregnancy-related complications, particularly when pregnancies occurred several years before the survey. Moreover, self-reported measures may underestimate or overestimate the true prevalence of HDP compared with clinically confirmed diagnoses. Unmeasured variables at the individual, household, or community level (e.g., quality of care at facilities, specific local health initiatives, perceived quality of care) might still influence the outcome. Furthermore, the survey does not capture whether the BP measurement was performed correctly to identify women with HDP and others, or if appropriate action was taken based on the readings. The DHS measures HDP based on self-reported hypertension during pregnancy rather than a clinically verified HDP diagnosis. A further study may consider the clinical data to get more insightful findings.

Conclusion

The prevalence of reported HDP during ANC in Somalia is 11.0%, revealing a significant maternal health burden. Higher education, more ANC visits, and facility delivery were associated with lower odds of reporting HDP. Stark regional disparities persist, with significantly higher odds of HDP reported in several central and southern regions compared to the north. Addressing this challenge requires a multi-pronged approach: strengthening health system capacity for accurate diagnosis and management, investigating the root causes of the counterintuitive risk patterns observed, and prioritizing resource allocation to underserved regions to mitigate geographic inequities and improve maternal health outcomes across Somalia.

Ethical considerations

The SHDS 2020 protocol was reviewed and approved by the relevant ethical review committees in the Somalia Directorate of National Statistics, the Somalia Directorate of National Statistics ethical review board, and the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of ICF. During the original survey, informed consent was obtained from all participants before interviews were conducted. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality was assured. This study utilized publicly available, anonymized secondary data obtained through registration and approval from the DHS Program website. As the data were fully anonymized and publicly accessible for research purposes, further ethical approval for this specific secondary analysis was not required. The researchers had no access to any personal identifying information of the survey respondents.
