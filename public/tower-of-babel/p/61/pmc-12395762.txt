Correlation between the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio with the severity of new-onset coronary artery disease in various glucose metabolic states: a retrospective study
Authors: Hongyun Shu, Sisi Han, Feng Zhao, Zhihai Xie, Weiping Qiu, Zubing Zhang, Qiaowen Li, Guojun Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammation plays a crucial role in promoting coronary artery disease (CAD). The Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) is a novel comprehensive indicator reflecting systemic inflammation, with previous studies indicating its association with cardiovascular events and mortality. However, the correlation between NLR and the severity of new-onset CAD at different glucose metabolism has not been explored. This study aims to determine the relationship between NLR and the new-onset of CAD and severity of coronary artery lesions in patients with varying levels of glucose metabolism.MethodIn this retrospective study, 1689 patients with acute coronary syndromes who underwent coronary angiography were included. Based on patients’ medical history and fasting blood glucose levels, subjects’ glucose metabolism status was categorized as normal glucose regulation (NGR), pre-diabetes mellitus (Pre-DM), and diabetes mellitus (DM). The severity of CAD was assessed using the Gensini score (GS), and subjects were divided into three groups based on GS tertiles. The relationship between NLR and CAD was validated through logistic regression analysis. The Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) was employed to determine the quantitative relationship between NLR and GS in different populations.ResultLogistic regression analysis revealed a significant association between elevated NLR levels and increased odds ratio (OR) of CAD in the fully adjusted model, regardless of the glucose metabolic state. Specifically, in individuals with NGR, the OR was 1.13[95%CI: (1.07, 1.20), P<0.001]; in Pre-DM, the OR was 1.11[95%CI: (1.01, 1.21), P<0.001] and in DM, the OR was 1.08[95%CI: (1.02, 1.15), P = 0.009]. It is noteworthy that there was no statistical correlation between NLR and the severity of CAD in the DM population, while an association was observed in non-diabetic patients. The RCS analysis confirmed a nonlinear relationship between NLR and the GS in total population and NGR group (P for nonlinear < 0.05), with distinct inflection points, which was not observed in the Pre-DM and DM groups.ConclusionThere is a correlation between NLR and the occurrence of new-onset CAD, and more importantly, NLR reflects the severity of CAD to some extent, and this correlation is more common in patients with NGR and Pre-DM; however, in patients with DM, NLR does not accurately reflect the extent of coronary artery disease.Graphical abstract Keywords: Coronary artery disease, Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, Coronary angiography, Coronary artery disease severity, Inflammation

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is a heart disease caused by myocardial ischemia and hypoxia due to narrowing or occlusion of the coronary arteries, and is currently the largest cause of death worldwide [1, 2]. Elevated levels of multiple inflammatory cells and inflammatory factors increase the risk of cardiovascular events [3–5]. Recent studies have elucidated the relationship between certain leukocyte subtypes and CAD: neutrophil is the key cells in initiating immune-inflammatory responses, while the activation of the inflammatory pathway further accelerates coronary artery narrowing [6, 7]; lymphocytes, another important subtype of white blood cells, play a significant role in the body’s immunity [8]. Abnormal lymphocyte counts often indicate increased inflammatory levels in the body, while low lymphocyte levels are considered a potential risk factor for cardiovascular disease [9]. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) integrates these two white blood cell subtypes, representing two distinct yet complementary immune systems, serving as a valuable biomarker for systemic inflammation and immune balance.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is becoming more common globally, and one of its typical features is chronic inflammation, and there is an association between inflammatory biomarkers and the development of DM and its complications [10]. Pre-diabetes mellitus (Pre-DM), also known as an intermediate stage between normal glucose regulation (NGR) and DM, represents a high-risk state for developing DM, as elevated glucose can upregulate chronic inflammation markers and promote the aggregation of white blood cells predominantly neutrophils [11, 12]. Notably, there are significant differences in inflammation levels among populations with different glucose metabolic status (NGR/Pre-DM/DM) [13, 14]. Prospective cohort studies have confirmed that elevated NLR levels are positively associated with increased overall mortality and cardiovascular mortality in adult DM and Pre-DM patients. However, in the general population, the relationship between NLR and coronary lesion severity is controversial: some studies support its predictive value [15], whereas others, based on whole populations, have found no significant association [16]. This contradiction suggests that there may be unrecognised factors at work. Whether the predictive ability of the NLR for CAD severity is modulated by glucose metabolic status is not clear. Stratified studies in patients with new-onset CAD are particularly lacking. The aim of this study was to elucidate whether there is a difference in the correlation between NLR and new-onset CAD and its coronary lesion severity (quantified by the Gensini score), and to provide a new perspective for accurate risk stratification.

Method

Study population

The retrospective study enrolled 2413 patients at the Affiliated Qingyuan Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University (Qingyuan People’s Hospital) between 2022 and 2023. These patients underwent coronary angiography for CAD-related symptoms such as chest tightness or pain. All patients met the diagnosis of acute coronary syndromes (ACS), according to the latest guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), the ACS cohort includes unstable angina and acute myocardial infarction [17]. Exclusion criteria included individuals under 18 or over 75 years old, those with a history of prior coronary angiography or coronary artery bypass surgery, malignancies, infectious diseases, severe liver or kidney dysfunction, thyroid dysfunction, severe infections, autoimmune diseases, or patients lacking key medical records such as fasting plasma glucose (FPG), lipids and and complete blood counts. As per the 2012 ACC/AHA guidelines, CAD was defined by at least one coronary artery stenosis of ≥ 50% (i.e., diameter > 2 mm) [2]; hypertension was defined as blood pressure consistently at or above 140/90mmHg or the use of anti-hypertensive medications [18]. According to the World Health Organization’s diabetes guidelines, the diagnostic criteria for DM included FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2 h blood glucose ≥ 11.1mmol/L on oral glucose tolerance test, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or current use of anti-diabetic medications. Pre-DM was defined as FPG levels between 5.6 mmol/L and < 7.0 mmol/L or HbA1c levels between 5.7% and < 6.5%. Patients not meeting the criteria for DM or Pre-DM were considered to have NGR [19]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared (where weight is in kilograms and height is in kilograms). Ultimately, 1689 patients met the inclusion criteria and were recruited for the study. Specific exclusion details are presented in Fig. 1.

Ethical statements

This study has been authorized by the Institutional Review Board (IRB-2024-092) of the Affiliated Qingyuan Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University (Qingyuan People’s Hospital). Due to its retrospective nature, a waiver of informed consent from the ethics committee has been obtained. The study was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, ensuring that all patient information is protected and used solely for medical research.

Data source and collection

Data on all subjects were collected from electronic medical records by a qualified study coordinator. Baseline demographic and clinical information included gender, age, height, weight, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure on admission, smoking and drinking status, clinical history including hypertension, and medication use (anti-hypertensive and anti-diabetic drugs). Blood samples were obtained from all patients before coronary angiography for laboratory tests. FPG specimens were collected on the second day of hospital admission. All blood samples were collected within 24 hours of the patient’s admission to the hospital. NLR was calculated based on the results of blood tests and the formula: absolute neutrophil count(10^9/L) divided by lymphocyte count(10^9/L). The Gensini score was used to quantitatively assess the degree of stenosis of coronary artery lesions: luminal stenosis less than 25%, 25 to 50%, 51 to 75%, 76 to 90%, 91 to 99%, and 100%, and given 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and 32 scores, respectively; and the resulting score was multiplied by the coefficients of the vessels in which the lesions were located: 5 for the left main coronary artery, 2.5 for proximal segment of the left anterior descending coronary artery and proximal segment of the circumflex artery, 1.5 for the mid-segment of the left anterior descending coronary artery, 1 for the right coronary artery, the distal segment of the left anterior descending coronary artery, the posterolateral artery, and the obtuse marginal artery, and 0.5 for the rest of the branches; the total score of coronary artery lesions in each patient was the sum of the scores of the various branches of the vessels, and the higher the score represents the more severe stenosis of the lesion[20].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis in this study was conducted using SPSS (version 26.0, IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.2.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data for continuous variables that conformed to normal distribution were represented by mean±standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using One-Way ANOVA test. Skewed variables were expressed by median and interquartile range (IQR), and analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. The NLR was analyzed as a continuous variable (per SD increase) or categorical variable (quartiles), grouped according to quartiles where the I quartile represented the lowest level and the IV quartile represented the highest level. Categorical variables were represented by counts or percentages (%), with the Chi-squared test (or Fisher’s exact test) used for comparison.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to validate the correlation between NLR and new-onset CAD in the total population and to calculate the odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Stratified analyses were performed according to glucose metabolism status. In multivariate logistic regression analyses, we adjusted for potential confounders, mainly those with P-values<0.05 in baseline characteristics (i.e., age, sex, BMI, smoking, hypertension and history of medication, diabetes mellitus and history of medication). Simultaneous covariance analysis was used to detect covariance between variables, where variance inflation factor (VIF)≤ 5: acceptable covariance, and VIF > 10 was usually considered as the presence of severe covariance and excluded from subsequent analyses. Within NLR quartiles, and we formulated three separate models to examine the interactions between the NLR and CAD: Model 1, unadjusted status; Model 2, adjusted with age, gender, and BMI as covariates; and Model 3, improved by introducing variables from Model 2, combined with smoking and drinking status, history of related diseases and medication use, and lipid profile. We standardized the data to explore the relationship between NLR and risk of new-onset CAD per SD increment.

Furthermore, in order to scrutinize the association between NLR and severity of new-onset diagnosed CAD, logistic regression analysis was conducted again, we employed logistic regression analysis with NLR as a categorical variable. Additionally, to further investigate the quantitative relationship between NLR and GS, we utilized a restricted cubic spline (RCS) models. We used RCS with 3 to 5 knots to flexibly model potential nonlinear relationships between the exposure and outcome. The curve trajectory was statistically tested for nonlinearity using the P-Monlinear value (reported in each panel), which evaluates whether the spline terms significantly improve model fit over a simple linear model. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) around the curves were calculated using the Delta method (or Bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations), accounting for both the uncertainty in the spline coefficients and model error. A two-tailed P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for differences.

Results

Baseline characteristics analyses

As shown in Table 1, according to the tertiles of the GS score for each patient, they were categorized into three groups: T1 group (GS < 5 points); T2 group (5 ≤ GS ≤ 66 points); and T3 group (GS > 66 points). The baseline characteristics table provided a detailed comparison of three groups based on various demographic, clinical, and biochemical parameters. Notably, the median age was highest in T2 group at 59 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 52-68) compared to T1 group with a median age of 57 years (IQR: 50-65) and T3 group with 59 years (IQR: 54-67), with a P-value of <0.001 indicating a statistically significant difference. Additionally, significant differences were noted in several clinical markers, such as systolic blood pressure (SBP), FPG, glycosylated hemoglobin A1C(HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C); low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), C-reactive protein (CRP), creatinine (CREA), white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophils (Neu), and monocytes (Mon) (all P <0.001). Regarding clinical parameters, significant variations were noted in hypertension, diabetes status, use of antihypertensive drugs, and antidiabetic drugs exhibited significant variations (P <0.001 and P = 0.040, respectively).

Relationship between NLR and new-onset CAD

We investigated the correlation between NLR and new-onset CAD. The results showed that in the total population, the quartile increase of NLR was significantly positively correlated with CAD risk. After adjusting for confounding factors (mainly those with univariate P-values<0.05 and those considered clinically relevant), the ORs slightly increased, with the OR of the quartile IV reaching 2.94 (95%CI: 2.08-4.14) in Model 3 (P for trend<0.001). Additionally, each SD increase in NLR was associated with a 55% higher CAD risk (OR=1.55, 95%CI: 1.32-1.81 Table 2).

In multiple comparisons, we use Bonferroni correction to control the overall Type I error rate. The logistic regression analysis revealed significant associations between NLR quartiles and glycemic status. Compared to the reference group (NLR quartile I with NGR), NLR quartile IV with NGR showed significantly increased odds (OR=2.37, 95% CI 1.51-3.72, P<0.001, adjusted P=0.002). Among individuals with Pre-DM, NLR quartile IV demonstrated the strongest association (OR=3.53, 95% CI:1.80-6.94, P<0.001, adjusted P=0.003), while NLR quartile III also showed significant unadjusted association (OR=1.99, 95% CI 1.01-3.95, P=0.048). In the DM, NLR quartile IV exhibited the highest odds (OR=2.96, 95% CI 1.80-4.85, P<0.001, adjusted P<0.001), followed by NLR quartile II (OR=1.74, 95% CI 1.11-2.71, P=0.015). Other comparisons did not reach statistical significance after adjustment for multiple testing (all adjusted P>0.05). The results suggest a dose-response relationship between higher NLR quartiles and increased odds across all glycemic status categories, with the strongest effects observed in the highest NLR quartile IV (Table 4).

Discussion

This study conducted a stratified analysis of the population, assessing the relationship between NLR and the new-onset of CAD and the severity of CAD in different glucose metabolic states. Regardless of the total population, NGR, or Pre-DM, the NLR was found to be associated with new-onset CAD and showed a positive correlation with the degree of CAD. However, in the DM, although NLR is associated with the occurrence of CAD, NLR is not associated with the severity of coronary artery. For increase of per SD in NLR, the risk of CAD increased by 55%, 63%, 58%, and 43%, showing consistent results in trend analysis. NLR, as a novel inflammatory marker, can assess the body’s chronic inflammatory status, playing a certain predictive role in the occurrence of CAD.

Inflammation causes atherosclerosis, with neutrophils being the most abundant subtype of white blood cells that increase first during bodily inflammation[21, 22]. Neutrophils exacerbate vascular wall inflammation, accelerating various stages of atherosclerosis. Current research indicates a correlation between neutrophils and CAD occurrence[23]. Lymphocytes, as representatives of adaptive immune responses, may infiltrate blood vessels and initiate inflammatory signal transduction. A reduction in lymphocytes during the regulation of inflammation can worsen the atherosclerosis process[24]. The dynamic changes of neutrophils and lymphocytes will impact vascular remodeling and structure[25]. NLR is a valuable parameter for assessing systemic inflammation. Previous studies have shown a strong predictive effect of NLR on CAD[26, 27]. Research has also demonstrated a strong association between NLR and adverse outcomes following acute myocardial infarction, including myocardial infarction recurrence and cardiovascular mortality[28, 29]. The present study was similar to their results, with a 1.55-fold increase in CAD risk for each SD increase in NLR (SD=4.27) in the fully adjusted models. Stratified analysis on the population revealed that this trend persisted in NGR, Pre-DM, and DM with NLR associated with a 1.63, 4.57 and 2.49-fold increased risk of CAD, respectively, which was not addressed in previous studies.

Besides this, to the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to elucidate the relationship between NLR and the severity of CAD in different glucose metabolic states. Other studies have also classified the severity of CAD using different criteria. Arbel et al. categorized CAD severity into 4 classes (0, 1, 2, 3) based on the number of coronary lesion vessels, showing that patients with NLR > 3 had more severe CAD (P<0.001) and worse prognosis (P=0.01)[30]. Our study utilized the Gensini score to assess the severity of CAD and explored the relationship between NLR and CAD in different glucose metabolic states. It was observed that in non-diabetic individuals, there was a significant statistical correlation between NLR and the severity of new-onset CAD. However, in the diabetic population, all models failed to demonstrate a statistically significant correlation between NLR and the severity of CAD. This may be related to the following mechanisms. Endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity is reduced by approximately 50% in patients with DM, and vascular endothelium-dependent diastolic function is impaired by a mechanism independent of the inflammatory response. The dominance of endothelial dysfunction may obscure the predictive value of the NLR when it attempts to reflect inflammation-driven atherosclerosis. For example, vascular smooth muscle cell calcification is 2- to 3-fold higher in patients with DM combined with CAD than in patients without DM, and this process is driven more by disturbances in calcium and phosphorus metabolism than by inflammation alone. Insulin resistance can also affect lipid metabolism, causing dyslipidemia that accelerate the formation and progression of atherosclerotic plaques[31, 32]. Patients with DM often use a combination of statins (lipid modulation), which reduce the NLR by approximately 12%-18%, and antiplatelet agents (e.g., aspirin), which indirectly affect NLR calculations by inhibiting platelet-leukocyte aggregation. Such multidrug interventions may mask the predictive value of the NLR itself, especially in fully adjusted models, where correction for drug factors may overdilute the independent effect of the NLR[33]. These may partly explain the fact that we did not find a direct relationship between the NLR and CAD in the DM population in our study.

In the comparison of baseline data, there was no significant difference in lymphocyte levels among the three groups with different GS scores. However, through multivariate logistic regression analysis, we confirmed the association between NLR and the severity of CAD. This suggests that, compared to individual neutrophil or lymphocyte counts, NLR has a stronger predictive ability for the severity of CAD. Numerous studies have also shown an association between NLR and adverse outcomes in CAD events compared to single inflammatory markers, indicating that an increased NLR is a strong and independent predictor of overall mortality and cardiovascular mortality in the general population[34]. The current study identifies NLR as a predictor of CAD severity in non-diabetic populations. This finding has direct prognostic relevance: elevated NLR levels may signal accelerated atherosclerosis progression. Previous studies have established that high Gensini scores correlate with increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including myocardial infarction and cardiovascular mortality[35, 36], these findings link prior NLR and adverse cardiovascular disease outcome events. Our data suggest that NLR monitoring could enhance risk stratification, particularly in NGR and Pre-DM subgroups where NLR>6.8 (inflection point) was associated with severe coronary lesions (Gensini>66). Notably, the absence of NLR-CAD severity correlation in DM patients implies that alternative pathways (e.g., endothelial dysfunction, advanced calcification) dominate disease progression in this group. This aligns with evidence that diabetic CAD exhibits distinct pathophysiology, such as impaired vascular repair mechanisms and accelerated plaque calcification.

NLR is a cost-effective marker of inflammation and is easily available[37]. Currently, noninvasive imaging including echocardiography, computed tomography, SPECT and PET nuclear imaging, and Cardiovascular magnetic resonance play a The key role in diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment decision-making in acute or chronic CAD is played by A more comprehensive characterization of obstructive CAD can be achieved by sequential independent testing or a hybrid approach [38]. A recent study demonstrated that in the noninvasive diagnosis of CAD, Resting global longitudinal strain (GLS), coronary flow velocity reserve in the left-anterior descending coronary artery (CFVR-LAD) and delta wall motion score index (d-WMSI) on SE have proven prognostic value and have the advantage of being semi-automated and reproducible measurements[39]. However, it appears that no single imaging measurement alone reflects coronary occlusion very accurately, and the combined application of multiple measurements increases time and economic costs to some extent. Against this backdrop, the present study evaluated the novel biomarker NLR for predicting CVD events, a marker from serology that, in combination with imaging, may improve the ability to predict CAD coronary lesions. Importantly, the study's stratified analysis provided more targeted results, which are invaluable for healthcare professionals. Furthermore, NLR can serve as an initial assessment tool for evaluating the severity of coronary artery stenosis, aiding clinicians in making appropriate treatment decisions. SGLT2 inhibitors bridge the gap between diabetes and CAD through the triple mechanism of “hypoglycemia - anti-inflammation - vascular protection”. Studies have confirmed that CAD patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors have lower NLR and better clinical prognosis. In addition to the direct effect of lowering blood glucose levels, it may also be related to other beneficial cardiovascular effects of SGLT2 inhibitors, including reducing adipose tissue-mediated inflammation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production[40, 41]. NLR-guided individualized treatment strategies could be explored in the future, which may further optimize primary and secondary prevention of CAD. While our study confirms NLR’s association with CAD severity in non-diabetic populations, its modest discriminatory power warrants comparison with established biomarkers. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), the most extensively validated inflammatory marker in cardiovascular disease, typically shows stronger associations with CAD risk (ORs 1.5-2.5 per SD increase) in large cohorts[42]. In clinical practice, hs-CRP is more indicative of stage-specific inflammatory responses and has a short-term application advantage compared to NLR. For individuals with metabolic disorders, NLR may better reflect long-term chronic inflammation. In contrast, our observed NLR OR of 1.55 per SD, while statistically significant, suggests more limited standalone predictive value. This aligns with meta-analyses indicating NLR’s primary utility as a complementary rather than replacement biomarker. Therefore, these two methods have different clinical applications, and their complementary use could lead to more satisfactory predictive outcomes.

Nevertheless, we acknowledge some limitations of this study that cannot be ignored. Firstly, as a single-center retrospective study, our research only utilized data from a specific region in China, with the majority of patients residing in the same city. This lack of population diversity may introduce bias. Secondly, the Gensini scoring system used to assess the severity of CAD overlooked evaluations of coronary artery branching, vascular calcification, and vessel tortuosity, potentially underestimating the extent of lesions and impacting our findings. Furthermore, despite the review of angiographic results by two expert physicians, some level of subjectivity remains. Lastly, our study cohort included patients with stable angina and acute coronary syndrome. It is worth noting that immune inflammatory cells such as neutrophils and lymphocytes will elevate after CAD to participate in the cardiac healing process[43, 44], differences in the timing of blood specimen collection from subjects may cause experimental errors. The present study did not include other commonly used indicators of inflammation in addition to the traditional ones such as interleukin-6 as well as calcitoninogen, which makes our study limited and may exaggerate the role of NLR in the pathogenesis of CAD. Finally, although we adjusted for many potential confounders, the presence of individual differences may reduce the statistical robustness of the findings. Future studies that consider these factors need to be analyzed in well-designed, larger prospective studies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a significant correlation between the NLR and the new-onset CAD severity as quantified by the Gensini score. Critically, NLR shows potential as a predictor of CAD severity, particularly among non-diabetic patients. These findings underscore the underappreciated role of chronic inflammation in CAD pathogenesis, beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors. For clinical practice, the routine accessibility of NLR positions it as a crucial tool. Dynamic monitoring of this readily available, comprehensive inflammatory marker could be pivotal for identifying individuals at high risk for severe CAD.
