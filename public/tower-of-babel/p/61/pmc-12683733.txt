Response to “When AI Meets Coronary CT: Overcoming Challenges and Enhancing Accuracy in CAD-RADS Reporting”
Authors: Dabin Min, Kwang Nam Jin, Chang Min Park
Published: 2025

We sincerely appreciate the thoughtful comments of Dong et al. [1] regarding our recent article titled “Large Language Models for CAD-RADS 2.0 Extraction From Semi-Structured Coronary CT Angiography Reports: A Multi-Institutional Study” [2]. Their suggestions underscore the important considerations for the development and validation of artificial intelligence tools in the structured reporting of coronary CT angiography.

The letter first raised concerns regarding the use of synthetic reports—a limitation that we acknowledge. Synthetic reports were an unavoidable choice for ensuring patient privacy during inter-institutional data sharing. They were generated by experienced cardiac radiologists based on actual institutional reports and were minimally modified to resolve privacy issues while preserving the clinical context. We agree that synthetic reports do not completely reproduce the heterogeneity and complexity of real-world reports. Accordingly, we plan to conduct prospective and retrospective studies using de-identified institutional reports, with appropriate data protection protocols.

The absence of output calcium scoring was also mentioned as a limitation. We would like to clarify that our study did not aim to calculate calcium scores de novo. Instead, in line with the definitions of Coronary Artery Disease-Reporting and Data System (CAD-RADS) 2.0, our task was to evaluate whether large language models (LLMs) could accurately extract recorded calcium scores from the reports and correctly map them to plaque burden categories (P1–P4). Thus, the scope of our study was the faithful reading and transformation of reported values into CAD-RADS classifications, and not the independent quantification of coronary calcium.

Another concern was that the misclassification of stenosis severity (e.g., CAD-RADS 3 vs. 4A) may affect clinical decision-making. Our work was primarily designed to provide a quantitative assessment of the model performance. Nevertheless, we agree that a systematic evaluation of the clinical consequences of LLM misclassification is important. We plan to compare the model predictions with historical institutional reports to assess the frequency and clinical implications of these discrepancies.

The authors further noted that despite the use of chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting, errors such as threshold misapplication and misinterpretation of qualitative terms persisted. We concur that prompt engineering alone has certain limitations. In subsequent studies, we plan to explore domain-specific fine-tuning approaches that may better align model reasoning with CAD-RADS definitions.

Institution-wise variability, particularly the lower accuracy at Institution A, was also highlighted in the letter. As shown in Figure 1, the stenosis severity extraction performance at Institution A was notably reduced compared with that at other sites. For example, under few-shot CoT prompting, Claude-3.5-Sonnet and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B achieved accuracies of 0.700 and 0.300, respectively, which improved to 0.767 and 0.600 under zero-shot CoT prompting. This suggests that, in some cases, the use of internal few-shot examples reduced rather than enhanced generalizability. However, the performance at Institution A remained markedly lower than that at Institutions B–E, and Gemini-1.5-Pro showed a decline in accuracy (0.833 to 0.533) under zero-shot CoT prompting. These findings indicate that optimizing instructions and examples based solely on internal data likely limits the performance of institutions with distinct reporting styles. To address this issue, future studies should examine the inclusion of institution-specific samples as few-shot examples and the development of more generalized instructions using multi-institutional data.

Finally, the authors suggested the potential value of multimodal inputs. While we acknowledge the possible utility of multimodal integration in coronary CT interpretation, the CAD-RADS 2.0 classification is defined based on textual report content, not on imaging or biomechanical parameters. Thus, multimodal input is not essential for the primary task addressed in this study, although future extensions may explore this approach.

In summary, building on the constructive suggestions of Dong et al. [1], we plan to fine-tune multi-institutional datasets with CAD-RADS labels to improve generalizability, evaluate the effect of institution-specific examples versus generalized instructions on cross-institutional performance, and analyze historical institutional reports to assess the clinical significance of classification discrepancies. We are grateful for their valuable feedback, which we believe will help to refine and advance the role of LLMs in CAD-RADS reporting.
