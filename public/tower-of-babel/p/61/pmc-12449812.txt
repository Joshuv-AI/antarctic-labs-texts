Hydrogels loaded with aqueous supramolecular fullerene complex for the promotion of diabetic chronic wound healing
Authors: Yue Wang, Hui Fang, Guofeng Zhou, Guanzhao Zhang, Xiao Wang, Zhiyong Zhang, Yingjie Zhao, Bo Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImpaired chronic wound healing in diabetes remains a significant clinical problem, with excessive oxidative stress being a major cause of the delay. In this study, we developed a water-soluble 6HPB@C60 complex by encapsulating fullerene C60 molecules within a cubic supramolecular cage 6HPB. Subsequently, 6HPB@C60 was loaded in the sodium alginate hydrogel to create an antioxidant system (6HPB@C60 gel) for diabetic wound healing. 6HPB@C60 gel exhibits a homogeneous and interconnected porous microstructure, demonstrating excellent tissue adhesion, low cytotoxicity, and excellent antioxidant capacity. Diabetic chronic wounds heal faster with 6HPB@C60 gel, which eliminates reactive oxygen species continuously. By regulating macrophage polarization, pro-inflammatory states are reduced, while at the same time cell proliferation, epithelialization, and collagen deposition and alignment are also facilitated. In conclusion, a possible solution for chronic wound management can be found in the 6HPB@C60 gel, which works significantly to accelerate diabetic wound closure and improve wound quality.Keywords: Diabetes, Wound healing, Water-soluble molecular cage, Fullerene, Anti-oxidant

Introduction

There are currently 463 million people who suffer from diabetes mellitus worldwide [1], and the number is expected to increase to 578 million in 2030, and 700 million in 2045 [2]. Diabetic cutaneous ulcers (DCUs) rank among the most severe complications associated with diabetes, affecting up to 25 % of diabetic patients and often presenting with an elevated risk of delayed wound healing, prolonged pain, amputation, and even premature mortality [3,4]. Normal wound healing is a well-coordinated biological process involving sequential and overlapping phases of hemostasis, inflammatory cell recruitment, cellular proliferation, and tissue remodeling [5,6]. As a result of diabetic wounds, each of these phases is compromised, which disrupts and delays the healing process [7,8]. Diabetic metabolic disorders induce mitochondrial superoxide overproduction, and activate the main pathways implicated in the pathogenesis of diabetic complications [9,10]. Through these pathways, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as hydroxyl radicals, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide (O2−.), and other reactive derivatives reach peak levels. This process initiates redox regulation, activates various proinflammatory pathways, and results in persistent and ineffective inflammation [8]. Inflammation is a crucial step in preparing for the wound repair. A delay in resolving inflammation can lead to a chronic, deregulated response and exacerbate tissue damage [11]. In addition, the uncontrolled accumulation of ROS causes significant damage to endogenous stem cells, growth factors, and nucleic acids in the wounded tissue, thereby severely compromising their regenerative potential [11] As a result of diminished expression of growth factors, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and keratinocytes are less capable of proliferating, migrating, and differentiating, which inhibits angiogenesis [8]. Since ROS play a key role in wound healing, extremely high levels of oxidative stress, coupled with a significant reduction in antioxidant enzyme activity, are major factors contributing to non-healing in diabetic wounds [11,12].

It is realistic to treat cutaneous injuries immediately to improve healing rates and reduce complications [13,14]. For wound treatment at DCU, wound dressings have been developed with great effort [15]. Hydrogels are ideal for wound dressings due to their high porosity, soft consistency, adjustable mechanical properties, and biocompatibility [[16], [17], [18]]. As an anionic polymer, sodium alginate has been extensively investigated for a variety of biomedical applications due to its biocompatibility, biodegradability, nontoxic nature, and high absorption capacity [19,20]. With the addition of Ca2+, gelation can easily be induced. Gels generated from this process can be used to carry out controlled and targeted drug delivery at wound sites, thereby increasing patient compliance and prolonging therapeutic effects. Furthermore, these properties can protect the wound from bacterial load, provide a moist microenvironment, and promote epithelial regeneration and granulation tissue formation for faster wound closure [[21], [22], [23], [24]].

Antioxidant-incorporated biomaterials have been proven to be a viable strategy for chronic skin wound management in tissue engineering [25]. The study of carbon-based materials, particularly fullerenes, is an important research area in nanoscience [25]. Fullerene exhibits potent properties as a scavenger of free radicals and an antioxidant [26]. It also demonstrates antimicrobial activity [27], antiviral effects [28], and cytoprotective abilities [29], and can be utilized for drug delivery [30] as well as tumor therapy [31]. Through diverse chemical modifications, fullerenes and their derivatives can exhibit excellent biological activity [32]. C60, as a representative of the fullerene family, has a highly symmetric cage-like structure [25] Since C60 has intrinsic spherical and hydrophobic properties, many efforts have been made to enhance its water solubility [33]. However, most attempts have focused on modifying the chemical structure by introducing hydrophilic groups, which often result in some loss of performance, particularly in terms of the electron-deficient characteristic. The typical double-bond opening reaction in C60 modification can impact its overall conjugation [34]. It remains a challenge to maintain the original structure of C60 while significantly improving its water solubility. Supramolecular self-assembly holds an ideal promise for addressing the challenges by constructing nanostructures with specific cavities such as micelles and vesicles triggered by traditional amphiphiles [35]. Here, the self-assembly of small water-soluble molecules enabled us to construct a water-soluble molecular cage with precise structures. By regulating the molecular design, the resulting cage can selectively encapsulate C60. This approach ensures the preservation of molecular integrity and biomedical activities without compromising the structure of the fullerenes, offering a promising avenue for applications in the biology and medicine field. In addition, this compact assembly of nanoparticles effectively mimics antioxidant enzyme (SOD) activity, further enhancing the clearance of ROS. It was confirmed that 6HPB@C60 can significantly improve H2O2-induced cell damage, alleviate oxidative stress, and promote cell migration in vitro. We combined 6HPB@C60 with SA/Ca+ gel to construct an adhesive hydrogel for the treatment of wound surfaces in diabetic rats. As expected, this 6HPB@C60 gel could accelerate ROS clearance, reshape the oxidative microenvironment, and demonstrate promising therapeutic effects in facilitating diabetic wound healing. In this work, we demonstrated that 6HPB@C60 has high antioxidant catalytic activity and could effectively treat diabetic chronic wounds.

Experimental section

The synthesis of target chemicals and gels

The synthesis of HPB was obtained by methylation of the compound after the classical heck reaction. For the details, see the Supporting Information. The parent hydrogel was prepared by the cross-linking of sodium alginate and Ca2+. The target hydrogels were physically stirred by adding the target compounds to the parent hydrogel. The final concentration of 6HPB and the complex 6HPB@C60 in H2O at 25 °C were 1.6 × 10−5 mol/L while C60 in o-dichlorobenzene at 25 °C was 2.6 × 10−6 mol/L.

SOD activity of 6HPB@C60

The SOD (superoxide dismutase) activity of 6HPB@C60 was examined by using a total SOD assay kit with NBT (Nitrogen blue tetrazole) from Beyotime Biotechnology. It is an NBT-based chromogenic reaction kit that detects SOD activity. SOD can catalyze the disproportionation of superoxide anions to produce hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and oxygen (O2). The reaction system involving xanthine (X) and xanthine oxidase (XO) generates superoxide anions (O2−.), reducing NBT to a blue formazan, which exhibits strong absorption at 560 nm. SOD can clear O2−., thus inhibiting the formation of formazan. The darker the color of the reaction solution, the lower the activity of SOD, the higher the vice versa. The activity level of SOD can be calculated by colorimetric analysis. The absorbance test was measured by Spark® Multimode Microplate Reader from Tecan.

Cell culture and cytotoxicity detection

HFF-1 (100406) cells, derived from normal human skin fibroblast cells, were purchased from BeNa Culture Collection. HFF-1s were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 15 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Biochannel) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (P/S, Biosharp). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 atmosphere and 95 % relative humidity. HFF-1 cells (approximately 5000 cells per well, volume of 100 μL) were seeded into each well of the 96-well plate. After cell adhesion, different concentrations of 6HPB@C60 and H2O2 were added to each well and incubated for another 12 h. Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay.

Intracellular ROS elimination ability

The intracellular antioxidant ability of 6HPB@C60 was measured by the Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit. HFF-1 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells in 12-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with 6HPB@C60 (60 nM) for 4 h followed by stimulation with H2O2 (200 μM) for another 16 h. After that, DCFH-DA (10 μM) was added to the supernatant and incubated for an additional 40 min. Finally, fluorescence images were obtained using inverted laser confocal microscopy.

Cell migration capacity in vitro

The migratory capacity of HFF-1 cells was evaluated using the scratch assay method. HFF-1s were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well in a 12-well plate, and the monolayer was then scraped with a 20 μL pipette tip. Subsequently, the cells were washed using PBS to remove debris and 6HPB@C60 (60 nM) was added and incubated for 4 h. Afterward, each well received H2O2 (200 μM) treatment for 16 h. Finally, fluorescence microscopy was employed to capture images, which were subsequently analyzed using Fiji software to quantify changes in migration area.

Dead cell staining assay

The hydrogel was immersed in the culture medium for 24 h, and the extract was used for in vitro cell proliferation studies. Calcein-AM/PI double-staining was used for live/dead cell detection. HFF-1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, the hydrogel extract medium was added to the above-mentioned Wells. After another 24 h, removed the culture medium and 500ul Calcein AM/PI detection working solution was added to each well and the cells were co-stained for 30 min. After incubation, the morphology of the treated HFF-1 cells was observed and photocaptured by using the fluorescence microscope. Cells were stained green to indicate live cells, otherwise dead cells were stained red.

In vivo hemostatic ability of the hydrogel

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (200–250 g) were obtained from Jinan Pengyue Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China). In vivo hemostatic performance of the hydrogel was evaluated using the liver hemorrhage model of rats. Male SD rats (200–250 g) were randomly divided into five groups: without any treatment (Control), sodium alginate hydrogel alone (SA/Ca+ gel), sodium alginate hydrogel with 6HPB cage water solution (6HPB gel), sodium alginate hydrogel with 6HPB@C60 (6HPB@C60 gel), and human epidermal growth factor gel (HEGF gel). Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane, and then the abdomen was cut to expose the liver. The left hepatic lobe tissue measuring approximately 2.5 cm in length and 0.5 cm in width was excised. A pre-weighed filter paper was placed beneath the resection site, followed by application of the hydrogel to cover the bleeding area, whereas the control group was allowed to bleed naturally. After 2 min, the filter paper was weighed to quantify blood loss.

Diabetic wound model construction and treatment

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (200–250 g) were subjected to a 12-h fasting period followed by intraperitoneal administration of STZ (70 mg/kg) in order to establish a model of type 1 diabetes. The fasting blood glucose levels were measured using a glucose meter (OneTouch). After 2 weeks of drug administration, the rat blood glucose levels consistently exceeded 16.7 mmol/L, along with weight loss, polydipsia, polyphagia, and polyuria, indicating the successful establishment of the diabetes model. The diabetic rats were anesthetized using isoflurane, and their dorsal hair was shaved and disinfected with 75 % ethanol. Four circular full-thickness wounds (10 mm in diameter) were incised on the central dorsal skin of each rat using a sterile biopsy punch. Rats were randomly divided into 5 groups, and different hydrogels were applied locally on the wound surface: Control, SA/Ca+ gel, 6HPB gel, 6HPB@C60 gel, and HEGF gel. Subsequently, the wounds were covered with sterile gauze and secured with an elastic bandage. Each rat was housed in a separate cage and provided with sufficient water and a balanced diet. The wound dressings were replaced every 4 days while capturing photographs of the wounds. Ultimately, the wound images were analyzed using Fiji software.

Histological staining

On days 8 and 12 post-wounding, diabetic rats were sacrificed by injecting an excess amount of 4 % chloral hydrate, and the regenerated wound tissues were harvested and fixed in 10 % formaldehyde solution. Fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin for standard histological processing, and sections of about 5 μm of thickness were prepared. Subsequently, the histological sections were stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) dye to visualize the pathological changes, while Masson's trichrome staining was employed to observe collagen formation in the wound tissue sections. Additionally, histopathological examination was performed on the major organs of rats (including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) using H&E staining.

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining

Tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen repair using EDTA. Nonspecific binding was blocked by incubating with 5 % bovine serum for 1 h. Immunohistochemical staining was performed using the primary antibodies Ki67 and Cytokeratin 10 to assess cell proliferation and epithelial regeneration, respectively. Alpha-SMA, which is highly expressed in myofibroblasts, was selected to validate the transformation of fibroblasts during wound healing. Additionally, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were used to detect inflammation in the wound tissue. A rabbit two-step immunohistochemical detection kit was employed as the secondary antibody. After incubation with DAB chromogen for 2 min, the specimens were washed and counterstained with hematoxylin for 2 min. Images were acquired by scanning the sections using a KF-PRO-005-EX digital pathology scanner.

For immunofluorescence staining, CD206 and CD86 primary antibodies were utilized to detect macrophage expression in the wound tissue. FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG was employed as the secondary antibody for visualizing CD206 and CD86 expression. The nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for a duration of 5 min. Immunofluorescence images were acquired using a microscope, and quantitative analysis was performed using the Fiji software.

Transcriptome sequencing

To investigate the role of 6HPB@C60 gel in diabetic wound healing, fresh wound tissues were collected on day 8 post-injury for transcriptome sequencing. Library preparation and sequencing were conducted by BGI. Differential expression gene (DEG) analysis, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were performed to compare transcriptional profiles across groups, providing mechanistic insights into the effects of 6HPB@C60.

RNA isolation and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assessment

Diabetic rats were euthanized on day 8 post-trauma, and the wounded tissues were collected, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently stored at −80 °C for real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Total RNA was extracted from the tissue using the Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Subsequently, reverse transcription was performed utilizing the PrimeScripRT kit (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China). The corresponding genes were then amplified employing the SYBR PreMixExTaq (Accurate Biology, Hunan, China) amplification and detected using the ABI StepOne Plus detection system in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The expression level of the related genes was determined utilizing the 2 ΔΔ Ct method. Primers for these related genes (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) are provided below.

Western blotting

The rats were sacrificed on day 8 post-injury, and the wound tissues were collected.

Protein extraction was performed using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors from skin tissues. The extracted proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5 % skim milk in TBST, followed by incubation with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The next day, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. After washing, the antigen-antibody complexes were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence detection system.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis in this work was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.4.3. Data are represented as mean ± standard deviation. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze differences between multiple sets of data. The P values were calculated by ANOVA.

Results and discussion

The synthesis and characterization of 6HPB

A hexagram amphiphile HPB was synthesized with six hydrophilic vinylpyridyl groups attached to a hydrophobic hexaphenyl benzene core (Fig. 1a). The detailed synthesis procedure is described in the Supplementary Material. The solubility of HPB in water is around 10 mg/mL at 25 °C. As illustrated in Fig. S1, the 1H NMR spectrum of HPB in D2O reveals a highly intricate pattern while it is distinctly clear and aligns well with the anticipated structure in DMSO-d6. The formation of aggregates in water is suggested by this observation. After a slow cooling process of recrystallization in the mixture of water/ethanol (150 μL, 9:1 by vol.), a single crystal of HPB (10.0 mg) was obtained from a screw cap vial (2 mL). The process was set in a thermo-block that could control precisely the rate of heating or cooling. Firstly, heat the equipment to 100 °C within 10 min and maintain 10 min at this temperature to ensure all the HPB has completely dissolved. Then, the set-up was slowly cooled down from 100 °C to 25 °C within 1 h to obtain the final single crystals. The atomic-resolution structure of HPB was revealed through single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis, which indicates that the hydrophobic effect is able to create a cage-like structure in water (Fig. 1b). Six HPB molecules interact, forming a cube where each molecule serves as a square face. The six hydrophilic ends tend to associate, creating a hydrophilic periphery for the cube. This process results in the construction of a hydrophobic cavity with a diameter of approximately 1.4 nm. The cage's hydrophobic nature and suitable size led to the idea of encapsulating hydrophobic C60 within its cavity (Fig. 1c).

The nanoencapsulation of C60 molecule

The 6HPB cube cage was then used to encapsulate the C60 molecule in water. The process involved heating a solution of HPB (6 mg) in a mixture of ethanol/H2O (100 μL, 1:9 by volume) to 100 °C. Simultaneously, a saturated solution of C60 in ethanol and o-dichlorobenzene and (100 μL, 1:9 by volume) was heated to 100 °C and added to the HPB solution. The combined solution was maintained at 100 °C for 10 min, followed by a gradual cooling from 100 °C to room temperature in 1 h, resulting in the formation of dark-brown co-crystals.

Co-crystals under optical microscopy appeared to be of high quality. However, the weak signals at high angles prevented single-crystal X-ray diffraction from providing detailed structural information. The introduction of C60 induced some disorder in the crystal, which is expected given the typically disordered nature of highly symmetric C60 molecules as guests within crystal cavities. However, UV–vis absorption spectra of 6HPB and 6HPB@C60 exhibited significant differences. The complex displayed a broad C60 absorption peak, confirming the presence of C60 (Fig. S2a). Additionally, the fluorescence spectrum revealed a noticeable decrease when C60 was encapsulated in the cage 6HPB (Fig. S2b). These results demonstrate that 6HPB and C60 can self-assemble in water successfully. Geometry optimization of the established 6HPB@C60 models was performed using the Materials Studio Forcite Module, demonstrating a perfect match between the cavity of cage 6HPB and the size of C60.

The SOD activity of 6HPB@C60

NBT as an O2−. indicator could be reduced to formazan after the reaction of xanthine (X) and xanthine oxidase (XO). If the system had SOD activity, it would occur an absorbance change at 560 nm. As shown in Fig. 1d and e, after the addition of 6HPB@C60, the signal intensity at 560 nm increased prominently in a time-dependent manner and it stabilized in about 30 min. With the increase of 6HPB@C60, the ΔA in the absorbance becomes smaller, which indicates that 6HPB@C60 has SOD-like activity.

The synthesis of SA/Ca+, 6HPB and 6HPB@C60 gel

SA/Ca+ gel was synthesized through the backdrop method of sodium alginate and CaSO4, in which CaSO4 is a crosslinker. Magnetic stirring 15 mL sodium alginate aqueous solution with a mass fraction of 22 % dissolved into a uniform solution at 50 °C. Then, 2 mL calcium sulfate with a mass fraction of 2.2 % was added to it drop by drop. After the reaction for 6 h, the SA/Ca+ gel was obtained. 6HPB gel and 6HPB@C60 gel were synthesized by simply dropping 6HPB (1 mM) and 6HPB@C60 (1 mM) in 0.1 wt% of SA/Ca+ solution and reacting for 8 h at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL (Fig. 2a). As shown in Fig. 2b, SA/Ca+ gel is pure and transparent while the 6HPB@C60 gel is darker in color due to the addition of C60 (Fig. 2c and d). SEM test showed the uniform three-dimensional network structures of both 6HPB gel and 6HPB@C60 gel indicating the successful preparation of two hydrogels (Fig. 2f and g).

Cytotoxicity and intracellular antioxidative activity of 6HPB@C60

The excessive accumulation of ROS at wound sites would hinder cell proliferation and survival. In this experiment, we investigated the cytoprotective ability of 6HPB@C60 under conditions of oxidative stress. As shown in Fig. 3a, after 12 h of incubation, 6HPB@C60 exhibited no significant cytotoxicity towards human epidermal fibroblasts (HFF-1s). Conversely, the viability of cells was concentration-dependently affected by H2O2, with the addition of 200 μm H2O2 resulting in a reduction in cell viability by 20 %–30 % compared to control cells (Fig. 3b). Subsequently, we investigated the impact of 6HPB@C60 on cell viability in the presence of H2O2 and demonstrated that at a concentration of 60 nM, it effectively mitigated hydrogen peroxide-induced cellular damage (Fig. 3c).

To mimic an in vitro oxidative stress microenvironment, H2O2 was added to HFF-1 cells. The intracellular levels of reactive oxygen species were then assessed using DCFH-DA. Compared to the control group, the intracellular fluorescence signal significantly increased after H2O2 treatment. However, the addition of 6HPB@C60 led to a noticeable reduction in fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3d and f). This indicates a remarkable scavenging effect of intracellular reactive oxygen species by 6HPB@C60. Scratch assay was used to detect whether the clearance ability of ROS by 6HPB@C60 could increase the migration of HFF-1s. Following a 20-h incubation, the migration level of HFF-1 cells in the H2O2-induced damage group was lower, while the cell mobility was significantly increased in the 6HPB@C60/H2O2 group (Fig. 3e and g). The results suggest that 6HPB@C60 could promote skin cell migration by,alleviating the oxidative stress mediated by ROS.

Biocompatibility of hydrogels

An optimal wound-repairing biohydrogel must possess outstanding biocompatibility. In vitro cytotoxicity of 6HPB@C60 gel was evaluated using Calcein-AM/PI live/dead staining [36]. As shown in Fig. 4a, no significant differences in dead cell numbers were observed among groups treated with different hydrogel leachates, with relative cell viability consistently maintained above 99 %. Then, Live/Dead staining assay was used to test the biocompatibility of the hydrogels. These results confirmed that 6HPB@C60 exhibited no obvious cell proliferation inhibition in vitro. Subsequently, Control, SA/Ca+ gel, 6HPB gel, 6HPB@C60 gel, and HEGF gel were applied to diabetic wound rats, followed by histological analysis on day 12 post-treatment. Heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were collected for H&E staining. Results demonstrated no significant organ alterations or discernible toxicity after complete degradation of these four hydrogels in vivo (Fig. 4b). These results collectively confirmed the excellent biocompatibility of the hydrogels in both in vitro and in vivo settings.

In vivo hemostasis of hydrogel

Hydrogels have emerged as a promising solution for addressing bleeding issues due to their biocompatibility, tunable properties, and hemostatic capabilities [37]. We evaluated the hemostatic efficacy of hydrogels using a post-hepatectomy bleeding model [38]. As shown in Fig. 4d and e, the blood loss from the liver incision in the control group (untreated rats) was approximately 170 mg, whereas the hydrogel-treated groups exhibited significantly reduced blood loss. Among them, the 6HPB@C60 gel and HEGF gel groups demonstrated the most substantial reduction in bleeding, with the 6HPB@C60 gel showing the lowest blood loss, indicating excellent hemostatic performance. This superior hemostatic effect may be attributed to the hydrogel's ability to seal the bleeding site and adhere firmly to the surrounding tissue. The porous structure of the hydrogel facilitates rapid blood absorption, promoting platelet accumulation within the hydrogel matrix, thereby minimizing blood loss [39].

Hydrogels accelerated the wound healing

To evaluate the obtained hydrogel's performance in chronic trauma, a full-thickness skin wound model in STZ-induced diabetic rats was established. The residual wound areas gradually decreased during the treatment process, and in the same stage of healing, the group of 6HPB@C60 gel exhibited significantly smaller wounds compared to the Control, SA/Ca+ gel and 6HPB gel groups,even outperformed the HEGF gel group. It demonstrated the highest healing rate among all treatment groups, indicating that fullerene plays a key role in promoting chronic wound healing (Fig. 5b and c). The formation of granulation tissue is essential for wound healing. H&E staining revealed a significant augmentation in the thickness of granulation tissue in both the 6HPB@C60 gel and HEGF gel groups compared to the control group (Fig. 5d and f). On day 14, both the 6HPB@C60 gel and HEGF gel showed almost complete regeneration of the epidermis, hair follicles, and adjacent sebaceous glands within the wound bed (Fig. 5e). The impaired function of fibroblasts and collagen deposition in the presence of hyperglycemia in diabetic wounds hinder the reconstruction of granulation tissue, inevitably leading to delayed wound healing. Therefore, the deposition and organization of collagen in regenerated skin tissue are key indicators for assessing the quality of wound healing. In Masson's trichrome staining, the wounds of the Contral, SA/Ca+ gel and 6HPB gel groups exhibited myofiber and cellulose accumulation with relatively low levels of interstitial collagen content. However, significant collagen deposition was observed in the 6HPB@C60 gel and HEGF gel groups. Furthermore, the wound collagen fibers treated with 6HPB@C60 gel exhibited a significantly enhanced tightness and uniform distribution (Fig. 5e and g). The results demonstrate that 6HPB@C60 gel possesses the ability to stimulate collagen deposition, rectify abnormal collagen fiber deposition patterns, promote granulation tissue formation, and expedite the healing process of diabetic wounds.

Hydrogels promoted re-epithelialization

Skin regeneration relies on a series of intricate and orderly cellular behaviors, mainly including cell proliferation and re-epithelialization, which plays a pivotal role in regulating wound healing [40]. Ki67 is a typical proliferative intranuclear marker [41], and the immunohistochemical results indicated that the expression level of Ki67 cells in the 6HPB@C60 gel-treated wounds was significantly higher than that in the other groups (Fig. 6a and d), indicating that the hydrogel-loaded with fullerene promoted the proliferation of cells in the harsh wound microenvironment. Epithelial regeneration rate is regarded as a compelling criterion for assessing wound healing. Cytokeratin 10 (CK 10) is usually expressed in spinous keratinocytes and is a marker of mature epidermis basal layer cells [42]. To investigate the impact of 6HPB@C60 gel on re-epithelialization in diabetic wounds, we conducted immunohistochemical staining to assess CK10 expression in wound tissues of each group. At day 12, the epithelium remained significantly defective in the SA/Ca+ gel and 6HPB gel groups, whereas the 6HPB@C60 gel and HEGF gel groups exhibited nearly integral epidermal layers (Fig. 6b and e). The overall results demonstrate that 6HPB@C60 gel effectively accelerated cellular proliferation and exhibited a significant capacity to facilitate re-epithelialization.

Fibroblasts represent a heterogeneous group of mesenchymal cells, encompassing pericytes, stromal progenitor cells, and bona fide fibroblasts [43,44]. Although these subsets share similar phenotypes, they exhibit distinct functional characteristics. In response to tissue injury, they suspend their specialized functions and acquire reparative features, differentiating into highly contractile α-SMA-positive myofibroblasts that contribute to fibrotic repair [43,45]. The high expression of α-SMA in myofibroblasts enhances contractility and promotes wound healing [46]. To verify the transformation of fibroblasts during wound healing, we performed immunohistochemical staining for α-SMA on tissue sections collected on day 8 post-injury. The results showed that all treated wounds exhibited a certain amount of α-SMA expression, with a significant increase in the 6HPB@C60 gel group. This indicates that 6HPB@C60 can promote the transformation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, thereby accelerating scar repair (Fig. 6c and f).

The anti-oxidant capacity of hydrogels

Chronic wounds are closely associated with excessive oxidative stress caused by ROS. Overproduction of ROS and persistent oxidative stress can cause tissue and cell damage, which then triggers an inflammatory cycle and intensifies oxidative stress. To evaluate the correlation between the antioxidant capacity of the hydrogel and wound healing, ROS levels in the wound tissues were detected by immunofluorescent staining. Diabetic wound tissues were extracted 8 days post-wounding, followed by staining tissue sections with DCFH-DA for analysis of ROS expression. The results demonstrated that the ROS density in 6HPB@C60 gel was comparatively lower than in the other groups (Fig. 7a and b), consistent with our anticipated results and indicating that the fullerene-loaded hydrogels play an excellent role in eliminating the ROS from diabetic wounds.

Anti-inflammatory effect of hydrogels

Prolonged low-grade inflammation is a characteristic hallmark of diabetes wounds, which delays or impedes the process of wound healing [47]. Macrophages play a crucial role in regulating the various stages of the inflammatory response during wound healing through diverse and continuous biological activities [48]. A proper switch from M1 (inflammatory) to M2 (anti-inflammatory) macrophages to the tissue remodeling phase at the end of the pro-inflammatory phase is a critical step into normal wound healing [11]. However, metabolic abnormalities in diabetes and overexpression of oxidative stress lead to dysfunction of macrophage response and impaired M1 to M2 phenotype conversion [49,50]. The synthesized 6HPB@C60 gel facilitates the healing of diabetic wounds, which may be due to its modulation of macrophage polarization and alleviating the inflammatory response. Herein, double-positive immunofluorescence staining of macrophages M1 and M2 was performed. The effect of the antioxidant activity of hydrogels on macrophage polarization was further investigated by analyzing the expression of CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker). Confocal laser scanning microscope images revealed that the SA/Ca+ gel, 6HPB gel, and HEGF gel groups promoted the expression of CD86 (M1 macrophages). In contrast, the 6HPB@C60 group displayed relatively weak fluorescence intensity and a reduced number of M1 macrophages (Fig. 8c and d). Moreover, 6HPB@C60 gel groups exhibited increased CD206 (M2 macrophage) expression.

Several proinflammatory cytokines secreted by M1 macrophages, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 can be used as indicators to evaluate diabetic wound inflammation [11]. In order to further elucidate the inflammatory response in different treatment groups, we conducted an immunohistochemical analysis to evaluate the expression of inflammatory markers in diabetic wounds. Immunohistochemical images and quantitative analysis revealed that levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were significantly lower in the 6HPB@C60 gel group (Fig. 8a–d), which is consistent with the results obtained from ROS and inflammatory cell staining. Consequently, 6HPB@C60 gel hydrogel alleviates diabetic wound inflammatory responses effectively. The results were consistent with those of the PCR assay (Fig. 8e), indicating that the 6HPB@C60 gel has good anti-inflammatory activity, which can accelerate the inflammatory process and promote wound healing.

Exploring the potential antioxidant mechanisms of 6HPB@C60 gel by RNA sequencing analysis

Given the significant therapeutic effect of 6HPB@C60 gel on rat skin wounds, we further investigated its underlying mechanism in promoting wound healing through transcriptomic analysis. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on wound tissues from the following groups: Control, SA/Ca+ gel, 6HPB gel, 6HPB@C60 gel, and HEGF gel. Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis, a critical component of RNA-seq studies, was conducted using the DESeq2 package. Genes with a p-value <0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 2 were considered significantly differentially expressed. As shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 9a), no significant DEGs were detected between the SA/Ca+ gel or 6HPB gel groups and the Control groups, whereas 1096 DEGs were identified between the 6HPB@C60 gel and Control groups.

Subsequently, we focused on the DEGs between the 6HPB@C60 gel group and Control group. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that on day 8 post-injury, treatment with 6HPB@C60 gel enhanced cell migration, cell proliferation, epithelial regeneration, angiogenesis, collagen remodeling, and wound healing (Fig. 9b). These findings suggest that 6HPB@C60 gel has excellent potential to accelerate healing in diabetic chronic wounds. Furthermore, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis highlighted significantly upregulated pathways, including the Calcium signaling pathway [51,52], cGMP-PKG signaling pathway [[53], [54], [55]], cAMP signaling pathway [56], and Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. 9c). These pathways are interconnected and may indirectly contribute to biological antioxidant processes, suggesting that the therapeutic effects of 6HPB@C60 gel originate from multifaceted mechanisms. Further studies are required to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Additionally, heatmap analysis confirmed significant upregulation of a subset of genes in the aforementioned pathways (Fig. 9d), among which Atp2a1 [57], Calml3 [58], Calml5, and Cacna1s play roles in multiple pathways. We analyzed the expression levels of the aforementioned genes in different treatment groups on day 8 post-injury using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). As shown in Fig. 9e, the 6HPB@C60 gel treatment significantly upregulated the expression of Atp2a1, Calml3, Calml5 and Cacna1, with Atp2a1 exhibiting particularly prominent overexpression. Furthermore, Western blotting (WB) analysis confirmed that the protein level of Atp2a1 was also markedly increased after treatment with 6HPB@C60 gel (Fig. 9f and g).

In summary, the 6HPB@C60 gel promoted the enrichment of signaling pathways associated with accelerated chronic wound healing. The calcium signaling pathway, cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, and cAMP signaling pathway may play potential roles in its antioxidant function, though the complete molecular network requires further investigation.

Conclusions

Chronic wound healing is a complex biological process, and the effective reduction of ROS levels is considered crucial for various healing events, including inflammation, reepithelialization, and collagen deposition. In this study, we successfully developed an adhesive hydrogel (6HPB@C60) by combining a water-soluble fullerene complex with sodium alginate. This hydrogel not only exhibits antioxidant properties but also effectively guides macrophage polarization, actively contributing to the subsequent stages of the wound-healing process. Furthermore, 6HPB@C60 possesses low cytotoxicity and good antioxidant capacity. Obviously, as a result of the remarkable extracellular and intracellular antioxidant properties, as well as the anti-inflammatory properties, 6HPB@C60 exhibits excellent efficacy at reducing persistent inflammatory responses and constant oxidative stress in diabetic chronic wounds. Notably, a significant effect of 6HPB@C60 on diabetic wound healing can be seen in the formation of granulation tissue, collagen deposition, cell proliferation, and epithelization. The combination of these factors results in enhanced diabetic wound closure. Therefore, a hydrogel containing fullerene can provide diabetic chronic wounds with the appropriate wound dressing.
