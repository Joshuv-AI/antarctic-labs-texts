Effects of perioperative intravenous lidocaine on postoperative recovery quality in thoracoscopic surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jianbo He, Dong Chen, Zhongquan Zhu, Dong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThoracoscopic surgery, while minimally invasive, is associated with significant postoperative pain, nausea and vomiting, and hemodynamic instability, which can impede recovery. Intravenous lidocaine has been proposed as a multimodal analgesic adjunct due to its potential anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties. This meta-analysis aims to evaluate the effects of perioperative intravenous lidocaine on recovery outcomes in patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, a systematic search was conducted in seven databases (CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, CBM, PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library) from their inception to August 2025 for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing intravenous lidocaine versus saline placebo in thoracoscopic surgery. Primary outcomes were postoperative pain scores (VAS/NRS) and incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV). Secondary outcomes included postoperative pulmonary complications (PPC), intraoperative fentanyl consumption, postoperative opioid consumption, hemodynamic parameters (MAP, HR), and Quality of Recovery-40 (QoR-40) scores. Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager 5.4. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (ID: CRD420251183680).ResultsFifteen RCTs involving 1103 patients were included. Lidocaine significantly reduced pain scores at all time points (0–48 h), with peak effect at 2 h (MD = -1.68, P < 0.00001). PONV incidence was lower in the lidocaine group (OR: 0.42, P < 0.00001). Although not statistically significant, a trend toward reduced postoperative pulmonary complications was observed (OR: 0.82, P = 0.26). Intraoperative fentanyl consumption (MD: -92.10, P < 0.00001) and postoperative opioid use (MD: -0.64, P = 0.03) were significantly lower in the lidocaine group. Lidocaine also attenuated hemodynamic responses during intubation and extubation (MAP and HR, P < 0.00001) and improved QoR-40 scores (MD: 2.98, P = 0.003). Regarding safety, reported adverse events were minor and transient, but current evidence is insufficient to fully characterize the safety profile due to inconsistent monitoring and reporting across trials.ConclusionPerioperative intravenous lidocaine enhances recovery after thoracoscopic surgery by improving analgesia, reducing PONV and opioid use, stabilizing hemodynamics, and promoting overall recovery quality. It should be considered within multimodal ERAS protocols. Further standardized RCTs are warranted to optimize dosing and assess long-term benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03794-z.

Introduction

Thoracoscopic surgery represents a significant advancement in minimally invasive thoracic interventions, offering reduced tissue trauma and accelerated recovery compared to open procedures [1]. Nevertheless, this approach still imposes substantial physiological challenges due to one-lung ventilation, surgical manipulation, and port placement, which can trigger profound inflammatory responses and nociceptive stimulation [2, 3]. The complex nature of post-thoracoscopy pain, involving both somatic and visceral components, frequently leads to impaired respiratory function, delayed mobilization, and suboptimal recovery outcomes when inadequately managed [4]. Additionally, these procedures exhibit a high incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting, further compromising recovery quality and patient satisfaction [5].

Contemporary anesthetic practice favors multimodal analgesic strategies to mitigate the limitations associated with conventional opioid-based regimens, such as respiratory depression, gastrointestinal impairment, and the risk of opioid-induced hyperalgesia [6]. Intravenous lidocaine has reemerged as a promising adjunct in this context [7, 8]. Evidence suggests its anti-inflammatory properties through cytokine modulation, inhibition of neutrophil activation, and attenuation of central sensitization via glycine receptor potentiation and NMDA receptor antagonism [9–12]. These mechanisms support its potential to ameliorate surgical stress response, reduce opioid requirements, and enhance hemodynamic stability during critical surgical phases [13, 14].

Although prior meta-analyses has investigated perioperative lidocaine administration in diverse surgical settings, evidence specifically pertaining to thoracoscopic procedures remains fragmented and inconclusive [15–18]. The influence of perioperative lidocaine administration in thoracoscopic surgery on key anesthetic outcomes, such as intraoperative opioid consumption and hemodynamic stability, as well as on patient-centered recovery metrics, has not been thoroughly examined in the current literature. Furthermore, while systemic lidocaine is generally considered safe within therapeutic ranges, its perioperative safety profile has not been comprehensively assessed in the context of thoracoscopic surgery. This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore aims to synthesize available evidence from randomized controlled trials evaluating the effects of perioperative intravenous lidocaine on postoperative recovery quality and safety specifically in thoracoscopic surgery. Our research focuses on quantitative assessment of postoperative pain, incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), postoperative pulmonary complications (PPC), intraoperative fentanyl requirements, postoperative opioid consumption, hemodynamic parameters including mean arterial pressure (MAP) and heart rate (HR), and Quality of Recovery 40 scores (QoR-40). We also systematically collected and summarized reported adverse events to preliminarily assess its safety. The findings will provide crucial evidence for optimizing anesthetic management and enhancing recovery protocols in thoracic surgery practice. The PROSPERO registration ID was CRD420251183680.

Materials and methods

Literature inclusion criteria

The research question was developed using the Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study design (PICOS) framework in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [19]. Eligible studies include adult patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery under general anesthesia. The anesthesia protocol involved standard preoperative preparation, radial arterial cannulation upon operating room arrival, and continuous hemodynamic monitoring. Induction was achieved using propofol, fentanyl, midazolam, and rocuronium, followed by endotracheal intubation. Anesthesia was maintained with total intravenous anesthesia under continuous hemodynamic monitoring throughout the procedure, and postoperative analgesia was administered as needed. In the experimental group, patients received an intravenous bolus of lidocaine prior to anesthetic induction, followed by a continuous infusion maintained until the end of surgery. The control group received an equal volume of 0.9% saline solution administered in the same manner. Primary outcomes included postoperative pain scores (VAS or NRS) and the incidence of PONV. Secondary outcomes consisted of PPC, intraoperative fentanyl consumption, postoperative opioid consumption, MAP, HR during surgery, and QoR-40 scores. Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in English or Chinese from the inception of each database were included.

Literature exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria comprised duplicate publications, secondary analyses, and studies lacking critical outcome data. Non-randomized designs were also excluded, including case reports, observational studies, expert opinions, commentaries, narrative reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, unpublished conference abstracts, and preclinical research involving animal or in vitro models.

Search strategy

A comprehensive, systematic literature search was executed across seven databases: CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP Database, CBM, PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library. The search encompassed all records from database inception through August 2025. The complete search strategies are provided in Supplementary Materials. Search keywords were: “thoracoscopy”, “video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery”, “lidocaine”, “local anesthetic”, “anesthesia”, “peripheral nerve block”, “perioperative”, “postoperative”, “intraoperative”, “clinical trial” and “randomized controlled trials”. Additionally, reference lists of included studies were manually scanned to identify potentially missed articles. Grey literature was not systematically searched. Two independent reviewers performed the initial screening of titles and abstracts against the inclusion/exclusion criteria, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible articles. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient (κ = 0.88), indicating excellent agreement. Discrepancies at any stage were resolved through team discussion or, if necessary, adjudication by a third senior reviewer.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted systematically by two independent reviewers using Microsoft Excel 2010. The extracted data items included the first author, year of publication, sample size per group, sex distribution, age of participants, Body mass index (BMI) values, intervention protocols for each group, and outcome measures. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through consensus with arbitration by a third investigator.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) tool for randomized trials, assessing seven domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, participant/personnel blinding, outcome assessor blinding, data completeness, selective reporting, and other biases. Two independent researchers categorized each domain as “low risk”, “high risk”, or “unclear” of bias, with unresolved discrepancies adjudicated by a senior methodologist.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted using Review Manager 5.4. For binary data, odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used as the primary statistical metrics. For continuous data, mean differences (MD) with 95% CI were applied. Heterogeneity among studies was quantified using the I² statistic. A fixed-effect model was utilized when I² was less than 50%, while a random-effect model was adopted for I² values of 50% or greater. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses were conducted for outcomes with substantial heterogeneity to explore potential sources. Where specific sources could not be identified, a narrative description was provided. Publication bias was assessed through funnel plot asymmetry evaluation.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This systematic review and meta-analysis is a secondary analysis of data from previously published studies. Therefore, ethical approval and patient consent were not required for this work. However, all included original studies obtained ethical approval from their respective institutional review boards and informed consent from all individual participants.

Results

Literature search results

The systematic retrieval across seven databases (CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP Database, CBM, PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library) initially yielded 364 candidate records. After removing duplicates with EndNote X9, 87 articles underwent title and abstract screening. Among these, 27 records were excluded, including eight reviews or protocols and 19 publications with unrelated content. Following full-text assessment, 8 non-randomized trials were excluded, along with 26 studies due to irrelevant interventions or control groups, and 13 studies with ineligible outcomes. Ultimately, 15 randomized controlled trials meeting the predefined PICOS criteria were included in the meta-analysis. Characteristics of included studies

The 15 included studies [20–34] were all randomized controlled trials (RCTs). A total of 1103 patients were included in the analysis. In the intervention group, subjects were administered a loading dose of intravenous lidocaine before the induction of anesthesia, which was subsequently followed by a maintenance infusion that continued until surgical conclusion. Patients assigned to the control group were given an equivalent volume of 0.9% normal saline placebo using an identical administration protocol. Evaluated endpoints encompassed pain intensity after surgery using visual analogue or numerical rating scales, prevalence of postoperative nausea and vomiting, quantities of fentanyl used during the operation, intraoperative mean arterial pressure and heart rate, and scores on the 40-item Quality of Recovery questionnaire. Methodological quality assessment

Methodological rigor was evaluated with the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) tool (Fig. 2). All studies claimed to use randomization. Among these, four [24, 26, 27, 31] utilized a random number generator, nine [20–22, 28–30, 32–34] employed random number tables, and two [23, 25] omitted a description of the randomization method. Only five studies [24, 25, 27, 29, 31] provided details pertaining to allocation concealment. Blinding of both participants and outcome assessors was implemented in ten studies [22–24, 26, 27, 29, 31–34]. However, whether blinding was used in other trials remains unclear. Five studies [22, 25, 26, 32, 34] documented participant dropouts, which may have been related to intolerance to pain. No selective outcome reporting bias was detected. Five studies [20, 28, 29, 31, 33] exhibited incomplete reporting of baseline characteristics, such as gender and BMI, suggesting a possible risk of other bias (Fig. 3).

Meta-analysis

Postoperative pain scores (VAS/NRS)

A total of ten randomized controlled trials [20, 21, 23–26, 28, 29, 32, 33] reported postoperative pain scores using either the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) or Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) at various time points. As shown in Table 2, the meta-analysis of VAS scores demonstrated that patients receiving intravenous lidocaine reported statistically significant reductions in pain compared to saline placebo at all measured time points, from immediately post-surgery (0 h) through to 48 h (P < 0.05). The analgesic effect exhibited a time-dependent pattern, with peak efficacy observed at 2 h (MD = -1.68, 95% CI: -2.07 to -1.30, P < 0.00001) and sustained significant relief through 24 h (MD = -0.94, P < 0.00001) and 48 h (MD = -0.42, P = 0.01). Analysis of NRS scores showed a similar but less consistent trend (Table 3). A significant reduction in pain scores was observed at 24 h (MD = -0.81, 95% CI: -1.35 to -0.27, P = 0.003). However, no statistically significant differences were found at 1 h (MD = -0.92, P = 0.05) or 48 h (MD = -0.38, P = 0.30). Considerable heterogeneity was observed across both VAS and NRS analyses, indicating variations in clinical protocols or patient characteristics that may influence the absolute magnitude of pain reduction. A sensitivity analysis excluding studies with high risk of bias did not substantially alter the overall results or heterogeneity, suggesting robustness of the findings. Nevertheless, the consistent direction of effect across both assessment tools supports the overall conclusion that lidocaine provides meaningful analgesic benefits following thoracoscopic surgery.

Incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV)

The effect of intravenous lidocaine on the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) was evaluated through a meta-analysis of twelve randomized controlled trials [20, 21, 23–28, 31–34] involving patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the included studies showed low heterogeneity (I2 = 30%). Using a fixed-effect model, pooled results indicated a statistically significant reduction in the incidence of PONV in the lidocaine group compared to controls (OR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.30 to 0.59, Z = 4.92, P < 0.00001). These findings suggest that perioperative intravenous lidocaine administration contributes to improved recovery quality, thereby promoting Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS).

Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPC)

As illustrated in Fig. 5, Postoperative pulmonary complications were analyzed using data pooled from three trials [20, 21, 34] enrolling 240 patients. Meta-analysis revealed a consistent trend toward fewer PPCs in the lidocaine group compared to the control group (OR: 0.82, 95% CI: 0.39–1.72, P = 0.60). Despite the lack of statistical significance for the pooled estimate, the directional consistency across studies suggests a potential protective effect of lidocaine against complications, including atelectasis, pneumonia, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, and respiratory failure, which may reduce the need for interventions such as oxygen therapy and reintubation. This finding indicates that intravenous lidocaine may contribute to improved pulmonary outcomes after thoracoscopic surgery, warranting further investigation in larger-scale trials.

Intraoperative fentanyl consumption

A meta-analysis of three randomized controlled trials [21, 28, 32] evaluating intraoperative fentanyl consumption was performed, as summarized in Fig. 6. Considerable heterogeneity was observed among these studies (I² = 71%), suggesting substantial variability in clinical protocols or patient populations, and warranting cautious interpretation of the pooled results. Using a random-effects model, the analysis revealed a statistically significant reduction in intraoperative fentanyl consumption in patients receiving intravenous lidocaine compared to saline control (MD: -92.10, 95% CI: -132.58 to -51.61, P < 0.00001). These results indicate that perioperative intravenous lidocaine administration significantly decreases opioid requirements during thoracoscopic surgery. Sensitivity analysis excluded one study [28], thus reducing heterogeneity (I2 = 30%), but did not change the significance of the result (MD: -73.88, 95% CI: -108.62 to -39.14, P < 0.0001).

Postoperative opioid consumption

Data on postoperative opioid consumption was available from two randomized controlled trials [26, 32], comprising 194 patients. Considerable between-study heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 62%), leading to the adoption of a random-effect model for the analysis. The meta-analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in the number of postoperative opioid consumption in patients receiving perioperative intravenous lidocaine compared to the placebo group (MD: -0.64, 95% CI: -1.21 to -0.06, P = 0.03). This result underscores the opioid-sparing effect of lidocaine in the postoperative period, which may help mitigate opioid-related adverse effects and support enhanced recovery (Fig. 7).

Mean arterial pressure (MAP)

Meta-analysis was performed on studies [21, 22, 25, 26, 29, 30, 32, 34] reporting mean arterial pressure (MAP) across five anesthetic stages: pre-induction, pre-intubation, post-intubation, pre-extubation, and post-extubation, as summarized in Table 4. Considerable heterogeneity was noted during intubation and extubation phases, necessitating the use of a random-effects model, whereas low heterogeneity was present at pre-induction and post-extubation (I2 = 0%), allowing for fixed-effects modeling. Significant reductions in MAP were observed at immediately following intubation (MD: -8.54 mmHg, 95% CI: -11.94 to -5.13, P < 0.00001) and after extubation (MD: -6.80 mmHg, 95% CI: -8.84 to -4.76, P < 0.00001). No statistically significant differences were detected at pre-induction, pre-intubation, or pre-extubation stages. These results suggest that intravenous lidocaine contributes to improved hemodynamic stability by attenuating the hypertensive response associated with endotracheal intubation and extubation.

Heart rate (HR)

Heart rate (HR) was assessed across pre-induction, pre-intubation, post-intubation, pre-extubation, and post-extubation in eight studies [21, 22, 25, 26, 29, 30, 32, 34]. Substantial heterogeneity was identified across several stages (I2 = 49% to 79%), prompting the use of random-effects models for most comparisons. As presented in Table 5, significant reductions in HR were observed following intubation (MD: -7.52 bpm, 95% CI: -10.61 to -4.42, P < 0.00001), before extubation (MD: -4.15 bpm, 95% CI: -6.03 to -2.27, P < 0.0001), and after extubation (MD: -5.88 bpm, 95% CI: -8.94 to -2.82, P = 0.0002). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were detected during pre-induction or pre-intubation phases. These findings indicate that intravenous lidocaine attenuates the tachycardic response to intubation and extubation, thereby promoting greater hemodynamic stability during high-stimulus periods of thoracoscopic surgery.

QoR-40 scores

Three studies [20, 29, 34] comprising 240 participants reported postoperative recovery quality assessed by the Quality of Recovery-40 (QoR-40) questionnaire. Moderate heterogeneity was observed among the studies (I2 = 45%, P = 0.16). Meta-analysis under a fixed-effect model demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in global QoR-40 scores among patients receiving intravenous lidocaine compared to the control group (MD: 2.98, 95% CI: 1.00 to 4.97, Z = 2.94, P = 0.003). As visually presented in the forest plot (Fig. 8), these findings indicate that perioperative lidocaine infusion is associated with a meaningful improvement in patient-reported recovery quality following thoracoscopic procedures.

Publication bias

Publication bias was assessed for the outcome of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) incidence. Asymmetry in the funnel plot (Fig. 9) suggested the potential presence of publication bias among the included studies. This implies that the estimated treatment effect may have been inflated, possibly due to the selective omission of studies with non-significant or negative results.

Safety and adverse events

Safety surveillance for lidocaine-related adverse events was inadequate in most of the 15 RCTs. Of the few events reported, these were minor and transient, encompassing a single case of bradycardia, hypotension, and metallic taste [23] and another of perioral paresthesia [29]. One trial reported an absence of lidocaine-related adverse events [28], but the remaining studies did not provide specific safety data pertaining to lidocaine. Consequently, the available evidence is inadequate to robustly characterize the safety profile of perioperative intravenous lidocaine in thoracoscopic surgery.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis represents a comprehensive synthesis of evidence regarding perioperative intravenous lidocaine in thoracoscopic surgery. Pooled results from 15 randomized controlled trials demonstrate that lidocaine administration is associated with significant benefits across multiple dimensions of postoperative recovery. The analgesic efficacy of lidocaine was evident, as reflected by significant reductions in VAS and NRS scores across multiple time points, underscoring its potential as a non-opioid analgesic adjunct [35, 36]. The time-dependent effect, most pronounced at 2 h and sustained through 48 h, suggests that lidocaine not only mitigates acute nociceptive stimuli but may also modulate longer-term inflammatory and neuropathic pain [37, 38]. Furthermore, lidocaine exhibited a clear opioid-sparing effect, evidenced by reduced intraoperative fentanyl consumption and a decreased need for postoperative analgesic rescue [39, 40]. This reduction in opioid exposure thereby lowered the incidence of PONV, a well-known side effect of opioids [41, 42]. Hemodynamically, it attenuated the hypertensive and tachycardic responses associated with endotracheal intubation and extubation [43]. Patient-reported recovery quality, assessed via the QoR-40 questionnaire, was also significantly improved in the lidocaine group [44]. This aligns with the holistic goals of ERAS protocols [45, 46].

Mechanistic insights and clinical interpretation

The observed benefits can be interpreted through the pharmacological actions of lidocaine. Its analgesic efficacy is attributed to the modulation of voltage-gated sodium channels, which inhibits ectopic neuronal discharge and attenuates peripheral and central sensitization [47]. The sustained analgesic effect beyond the immediate postoperative period suggests additional mechanisms may be involved, potentially including the attenuation of neuroinflammatory pathways and modulation of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor activity [48]. The reduction in PONV is likely multifactorial, stemming primarily from its opioid-sparing property. Lidocaine’s potential anti-inflammatory effects on visceral afferent pathways and gastrointestinal function may also contribute [49, 50]. The favorable hemodynamic profile during airway manipulation points to a sympatholytic effect, stabilizing cardiovascular responses to high-intensity stimuli [51]. Together, these align well with the core principles of ERAS, emphasizing analgesia with minimal opioids, reduced side-effect burden, and enhanced patient comfort.

Methodological considerations and limitations

While the results are suggestive of benefit, several methodological aspects warrant consideration. The heterogeneity observed in pain score and fentanyl consumption analyses may be attributable to variations in surgical techniques, lidocaine dosing regimens, and baseline patient characteristics. Considerable heterogeneity among studies reporting fentanyl use may reflect differences in anesthetic management, surgical stimulus, and lidocaine infusion protocols. The moderate heterogeneity in QoR-40 results may reflect subjective cognitive differences in assessment. This analysis has limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the limited number of studies reporting certain outcomes constrains the strength and generalizability of the findings. Second, the geographical concentration of included studies (14 of 15 from China) may limit the applicability of our results to other populations, where surgical protocols and perioperative care standards profiles may differ. Third, variations in lidocaine dosing and concomitant analgesics may further contribute to outcome heterogeneity. Finally, safety assessment was markedly limited. As noted in the results, most included trials did not systematically monitor or report lidocaine-related adverse events. The few reported events were minor and transient, but this likely reflects under-detection rather than an absence of risk. The current evidence is therefore insufficient to robustly characterize the safety profile of perioperative intravenous lidocaine in this surgical population.

Clinical implications and future directions

The evidence from this meta-analysis lends support to considering intravenous lidocaine as a component for evaluation within enhanced recovery protocols for patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery. To inform clinical practice, future research should prioritize several key areas. First, standardization of lidocaine protocols is essential; a detailed summary of the dosing regimens from the included studies is provided in Supplementary Table 1, highlighting the current variability. Second, rigorous and prospective safety monitoring must be incorporated to establish a comprehensive safety profile, addressing the current evidence gap. Third, while not currently standard in thoracic ERAS pathways, exploring the efficacy and safety of extending lidocaine infusion into the postoperative period represents a valuable direction for research. Ultimately, the routine implementation of intravenous lidocaine should be preceded by more high-quality, standardized RCTs to conclusively confirm both its efficacy and safety.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this meta-analysis suggests that perioperative intravenous lidocaine is associated with improved pain control, decreased PONV, reduced opioid use, stabilized hemodynamics, and enhanced quality of recovery in patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery. These benefits support its inclusion for evaluation within multimodal ERAS protocols and highlight its role as a promising adjunct in modern thoracic anesthesia. However, the findings call for further high-quality, standardized randomized controlled trials to optimize dosing, confirm long-term benefits, and establish its position relative to other analgesic techniques before routine clinical recommendation can be made.
