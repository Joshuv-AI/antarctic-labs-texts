Beyond the root: licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra L.) fruit extract modulates oxidative stress and apoptotic markers in PC12 cells
Authors: Parvin Jahanbani, Leila Hosseinzadeh, Matin Mahmoudi, Fereshteh Jalilian, Mahdieh Eftekhari, Masoud Modarresi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rising global prevalence of neurodegenerative disorders underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies. Oxidative stress is a well-established central driver in the pathogenesis of conditions like Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease. While the root of Glycyrrhiza glabra L. (licorice) is a renowned source of natural antioxidants, its fruit remains a largely unexplored reservoir of bioactive compounds with potential neuroprotective properties. This in vitro study aimed to systematically evaluate the neuroprotective potential of different licorice fruit extracts and elucidate the underlying mechanisms against hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-induced oxidative damage in PC12 neuronal cells. Different licorice fruit extracts were prepared sequentially with n-hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, methanol, and water by the maceration method. The protective effects of these extracts against H2O2-induced cytotoxicity were assessed using the MTT assay. Phytochemical profiling of the active ethyl acetate extract (EA extract) was performed using TLC, total flavonoid assay, and HPLC–DAD. EA extract exhibited the strongest protective activity. Pre-treatment with non-toxic concentrations of EA extract (12.5 and 25 µg/mL) significantly increased cell viability against H2O2 (IC50 = 70 µg/mL approximately 2.06 mM) by 33% and 38%, respectively. This extract possessed the highest total flavonoid content (50.08 ± 0.31 mg of quercetin equivalents per gram of dry extract) among all extracts, and HPLC–DAD analysis confirmed the presence of glabridin 27.75 ± 0.01 mg per gram of dry EA extract. EA extract also notably restored mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced caspase-3 activity, and decreased ROS production in H2O2-stressed cells. Our findings indicate that the ethyl acetate extract of licorice fruit attenuates H2O2-induced oxidative stress in PC12 cells, and its neuroprotective effect is likely associated with its high flavonoid content. Introduction

Free radicals, particularly reactive oxygen species such as H2O2, play a central role in cellular damage and disease pathogenesis; while antioxidant systems normally maintain redox homeostasis, excessive ROS production disrupts this balance and leads to oxidative damage1–3.

Recent review has highlighted the multifaceted roles of reactive species in biological stress responses, encompassing both their physiological functions as signaling molecules and their pathological effects when produced in excess4.

Oxidative stress in the brain is associated with neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s, Alzheimer’s, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis5,6.

The prevalence of neurological disorders has increased rapidly over the last 3 decades7. Despite extensive research, these diseases remain incurable, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies8.

Medicinal plants have gained attention as potential treatments due to their rich secondary metabolites, which exhibit antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. These plant-derived compounds can protect neurons by reducing oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and protein aggregation, often through multiple synergistic mechanisms, making them promising candidates for neuroprotection9–11.

Licorice, with the scientific name Glycyrrhiza glabra L. is one of the most popular medicinal plants in the Fabaceae family. Pharmacological studies have depicted that different extracts and pure compounds of this species possess diverse biological properties, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antiviral, antibacterial, and antidiabetic activities. Various studies have demonstrated the antioxidant effect of different parts of licorice, such as roots, leaves, and aerial parts12–17.

Licorice contains bioactive compounds, such as glycyrrhizin and glycyrrhetinic acid, and flavonoids, which contribute to its therapeutic potential18–21. Notably, licorice-derived flavonoids, including glabridin, hispaglabridin A, and hispaglabridin B, have demonstrated potent antioxidant effects by inhibiting lipid peroxidation and protecting against human lipoprotein oxidation in vitro22. Multiple studies have demonstrated that polysaccharides and proteins from seeds of various Glycyrrhiza species exhibit antioxidant properties both in vitro and in vivo23–25.

Classical texts of traditional Iranian medicine recommend the use of decoctions prepared from both the root and fruit of licorice for the treatment of various ailments, particularly digestive disorders. Notably, the fruit decoction, traditionally known as Sous, is regarded as more potent and therapeutically effective than the root decoction26.

While previous studies have primarily focused on licorice roots, leaves, aerial parts, and seeds, the neuroprotective potential of its fruit remains underexplored. Given the diverse bioactive compounds in licorice, its fruit may also possess significant antioxidant properties. Investigating the effects of licorice fruit extracts on oxidative stress-induced neuronal damage could provide valuable insights for preventing and treating neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s. This study investigates the effects of different licorice fruit extracts against H2O2-induced oxidative stress in PC12 neuronal cells, aiming to identify novel therapeutic candidates for oxidative stress-related neurodegeneration and provide insights into preventing and treating diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s.

Phytochemical profile of the active EA extract

To establish a chemical basis for the observed neuroprotective activity, the active EA extract was subjected to basic phytochemical characterization.

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC), fingerprinting of the EA extract was performed using two distinct mobile phase systems to achieve comprehensive resolution of its constituents. The first system consisted of ethyl acetate/n-hexane (7:3, v/v), while the second system employed ethyl acetate/methanol (7:3, v/v). Both chromatograms revealed well-resolved phytochemical profiles with distinct band patterns and Rƒ values, confirming the complexity of the fraction (Supplementary Fig. S1A,B). Under UV light at 254 nm, both chromatograms showed no distinct quenching zones, indicating the absence of strongly UV-absorbing chromophores such as free hydroxylated flavonoids or simple phenolic acids1. Crucially, under UV 366 nm, both systems revealed multiple well-resolved bands exhibiting bright blue and blue-green fluorescence. This fluorescence pattern is suggestive of coumarins and lipophilic flavonoids, classes of compounds extensively documented in Glycyrrhiza species27,28.

Total flavonoid content (TFC) was quantified using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method with quercetin as the standard29. The EA extract was found to be rich in flavonoid compounds, with a content of 50.08 ± 0.31 mg of quercetin equivalents (QE) per gram of dry extract. For comparative purposes, the TFC of the other sequential extracts was also determined (Table 1). The EA extract possessed the highest flavonoid concentration, which correlated directly with its superior protective activity in the MTT assay.

HPLC–DAD analysis and identification of glabridin

The ethyl acetate fraction was analyzed by HPLC–DAD at 280 nm under the optimized chromatographic conditions described in material and methods section. The chromatogram revealed a complex mixture of constituents, with several major peaks distributed across the retention time range of 5–48 min (Fig. S3).

Identification of glabridin: An authentic glabridin standard was analyzed under identical chromatographic conditions, exhibiting a distinct peak at a retention time of 37.15 min (Fig. S2). A peak with the same retention time was observed in the EA extract chromatogram (Fig. S3). To confirm the identity of this peak, its UV spectrum was extracted from the DAD data and compared with that of the glabridin standard. As shown in Fig. S3, the UV spectrum of the peak from the EA extract (227 nm, and 280 nm = λmax) was identical to that of authentic glabridin, providing unambiguous identification30,31.

Quantification: The glabridin content in the EA extract was determined using a calibration curve established with the authentic standard (R2 > 0.997). The concentration was calculated to be 27.75 ± 0.01 mg per g of dry EA extract.

Other constituents: Several other major peaks were observed in the chromatogram; however, due to the lack of commercial standards, these compounds remain unidentified. The chromatographic fingerprint is provided as a reference for future phytochemical studies.

Discussion

When exposed to excessive oxidative stress, neurons may activate the apoptotic pathway. The complex balance between the physiological signaling roles of reactive oxygen species and their pathological overproduction is critical in determining cell fate4. Among the hallmarks of apoptosis are mitochondrial dysfunction and activation of caspases. Various studies have shown that in neurodegenerative diseases, dysfunction and death of neurons are caused by the biochemical pathways of apoptosis. Indeed, the recognition that the induction of neuronal cell apoptosis plays an important role in disease progression has led to increased research on interventional tactics to control neuronal apoptosis. Considering that oxidative stress is associated with several diseases that include increased cell death, the use of antioxidants can be a strategy to protect neurons32.

While synthetic compounds face clinical limitations due to toxicity and carcinogenic risks, plant-derived antioxidants offer a safer alternative8,33.

Licorice (Glycyrrhiza glabra) is particularly notable for its antioxidative properties, attributed to bioactive constituents such as triterpenes, saponins, flavonoids, isoflavonoids, chalcones, Coumarin, and glycyrrhizic acid13,14,16,17,34–41.

The current study examined the effect of various licorice fruit extracts on H2O2-induced oxidative stress in PC12 neuronal cells. In this study, the widely used maceration method was employed for extraction. Due to their varying polarities, phytochemicals exhibit differential solubility; therefore, solvents of increasing polarity were used sequentially for extraction42. The polarity of the selected solvent to some extent indicates the chemical nature of the extract compounds. The sequential extraction yielded fractions with varying efficiencies (Table 1). While the methanolic and aqueous extracts showed higher yields (6.65% and 3.56%, respectively), the ethyl acetate (EA) fraction exhibited a notably lower yield (0.19%). This is a common observation in phytochemistry, where yield is not inherently correlated with bioactivity. The EA solvent, with its intermediate polarity, selectively extracts specific classes of semi-polar secondary metabolites. The low yield of the EA fraction suggests a selective enrichment of these particular compounds rather than a broad spectrum of plant constituents. Crucially, it is precisely this semi-polar fraction that demonstrated the most potent neuroprotective activity in our assays.

The decoction technique is one of the extraction methods widely employed in traditional medicine because of its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and environmental compatibility43. This method facilitates the disruption of rigid plant cell walls, thereby enabling the release of bioactive constituents that cannot be efficiently extracted through simple soaking or infusion. Decoction is particularly effective for the extraction of water-soluble and heat-stable compounds, including alkaloids, tannins, phenolic compounds, and flavonoids44,45. Licorice (Glycyrrhiza spp.) root decoctions have long been utilized in traditional medicine for a wide range of therapeutic purposes46,47. Several studies have reported the anti-inflammatory properties of decoctions containing licorice47.

Among the tested extracts at equal concentrations, the ethyl acetate (EA) extract showed the most significant protective effect on PC12 cells. In subsequent experiments, we evaluated the effect of pre-treatment with licorice EA extract (at 12.5 and 25 μg/mL concentrations) on cell viability, mitochondrial membrane potential, caspase-3 enzyme activity, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels under oxidative stress conditions (70 µg/mL approximately 2.06 mM)).

Our findings showed that EA extract, used at both concentrations, protected against cell death caused by H2O2 and improved mitochondrial membrane potential. It also reduced caspase 3 enzyme activity and ROS production. The present results are consistent with previous studies, in particular confirming the findings of Siracusa et al. and Li et al., who showed that the ethyl acetate fraction of licorice has better anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects compared to other solvent extracts48,49.

In both tests measuring mitochondrial membrane potential and caspase 3 enzyme activity, the lower concentration of the extract (12.5 μg/mL) showed greater effects than the higher concentration (25 μg/mL). This observed pattern, where efficacy does not increase linearly with dose, is consistent with the non-linear dose–responses occasionally reported for bioactive phytochemicals. Such patterns may arise from the dual and dose-dependent biological activity of these compounds. At low to moderate concentrations, these compounds exert cytoprotective effects by selectively activating adaptive cellular defense mechanisms, such as inducing redox-sensitive signaling pathways. However, with increasing doses, these same compounds may switch from protective to cytotoxic activity. At higher concentrations, the decrease in selectivity and increase in chemical reactivity led to excessive redox cycling, which leads to the formation of reactive oxygen species. This oxidative load disrupts cellular homeostasis, damages macromolecules, and ultimately causes cell death. As a result, maximal biological efficacy is often observed at moderate doses, creating an inverted U-shaped response curve. This phenomenon is seen in many phytochemical compounds such as quercetin, naringin, orientin, and withanolide A. However, confirmation of a definitive dose–response relationship would require testing a wider range of extract concentrations in future studies50–52.

To date, a wide range of phytochemicals belonging to diverse chemical classes have been identified in licorice (Glycyrrhiza spp.), including saponins, coumarins, chalcones, and multiple subclasses of flavonoids such as isoflavones, flavanols, isoflavanes, and isoflavanones53,54. This aligns with the phytochemical profile of Glycyrrhiza species, where many of the most bioactive compounds—such as prenylated flavonoids (e.g., glabridin, hispaglabridins) and chalcones (e.g., isoliquiritigenin)—exhibit optimal solubility in solvents of intermediate polarity like ethyl acetate. Furthermore, studies have shown that ethyl acetate is particularly effective in selectively extracting total flavonoids and specific potent compounds like glabridin from licorice root with high purity, despite lower overall mass yield. Therefore, the low yield but high bioactivity of our EA fruit extract strongly indicates the concentration of potent, semi-polar neuroprotective agents, warranting its selection for detailed mechanistic study and future isolation efforts.

Various compounds have been extracted from the ethyl acetate extract of licorice root, including flavonoids such as glabrene, glabrol, glabrone, glabridin, 3′-OH-4′OMe-4′-O-methyl-glabridin, 4′-OMe-3′-hydroxy-4′-O-methyl-glabridin, hispaglabridin A, and hispaglabridin B55,56. In a study by Yewale et al., it was found that ethyl acetate exhibits high selectivity for extracting bioactive compounds, such as glabridin and total flavonoids, from licorice root. The stability of glabridin in this solvent was higher than in other organic solvents57.

Glabridin is a prenylated isoflavone extracted from the licorice plant. The antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective activities of this compound have been demonstrated in various studies31,58. Glabridin has been shown to inhibit oxidative stress damage and apoptosis of neuronal cells. Yu et al. demonstrated that it exerts its neuroprotective effect by blocking the mitochondrial apoptosis pathway, including suppressing Bax and caspase-3 expression and reducing Bcl-2 expression59.

Based on previous studies, the phenolic compounds present in the total extract of Licorice exert protective effects on SH-SY5Y cells. This protection was achieved through two key mechanisms: prevention of ATP depletion and modulation of caspase-3/7 activities induced by MPP + exposure60. Flavonoids from stem and leaf of G. glabra modulate oxidative markers such as caspase 9, caspase 3, and superoxide dismutase (SOD) and activate the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway61.

In the study of Ahn et al., it was found that the aqueous extract of Glycyrrhiza uralensis F. has protective effects on PC12 cells against apoptosis induced by amyloid-β peptide (Aβ25-35)62. Also, Hosseinzadeh et al. showed that Glycyrrhiza glabra L. root extract and carbenoxolone protect PC12 cells against oxidative stress and apoptosis induced by serum/glucose deprivation63.

The cytoprotective effect of the EA extract against H2O2-induced oxidative stress in PC12 cells likely involves multiple mechanisms, with a key role for the upregulation of endogenous antioxidant enzymes. The EA extract, potentially rich in flavonoids and chalcones, as mentioned above, may activate the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway. Under oxidative stress, H2O2 generates excessive ROS, which can overwhelm cellular antioxidant defenses and deplete GSH, a critical intracellular antioxidant. The selected fractions may enhance Nrf2 dissociation from its inhibitor, Keap1, promoting Nrf2 translocation to the nucleus. There, Nrf2 binds to antioxidant response elements, upregulating the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase. These complementary actions likely contribute to the overall protective effects64,65. However, these proposed mechanisms remain hypothetical and require direct experimental validation in future studies, such as measuring Nrf2 nuclear translocation or the expression of its target genes.

The complementary application of normal-phase TLC and reversed-phase HPLC–DAD revealed distinct phytochemical profiles within the bioactive ethyl acetate extract. While HPLC–DAD confirmed the presence of glabridin (27.75 ± 0.01 mg per gram of dry EA extract). In comparison with the study by Esmaeili et al., in which glabridin was quantified in 70% hydroethanolic extracts of the root, our results suggest that the glabridin content in the ethyl acetate extract of the fruit may be higher30.

Our finding is consistent with previous studies reporting that ethyl acetate fractions are enriched in glabridin, with glabridin content reaching up to 35.2% in ethyl acetate fractions66. The TLC fingerprint under UV 366 nm exhibited multiple blue and blue-green fluorescent bands. This fluorescence pattern is suggestive of coumarin and lipophilic flavonoid derivatives, although their precise identification requires more extensive phytochemical investigations.

Consistent with previous studies reporting that the ethyl acetate fraction derived from licorice root possesses a high flavonoid content, our findings also confirm that this extract is rich in flavonoids67.

Despite the partial characterization of individual compounds, the fraction demonstrated a markedly high total flavonoid content (50.08 ± 0.31 mg QE/g extract), establishing a direct quantitative link between flavonoid enrichment and the observed neuroprotective efficacy. This correlation is strongly supported by a substantial body of evidence documenting the antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and mitochondrial-protective properties of flavonoids in neuronal injury models68,69.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the neuroprotective effects of licorice fruit extract are likely attributable, at least in part, to the synergistic contribution of its constituent flavonoids and possibly coumarin compounds, with glabridin serving as a confirmed though quantitatively minor bioactive marker within a more complex phytochemical matrix. Definitive identification of the specific compounds responsible for the observed bioactivity will require further phytochemical studies, including isolation and structural elucidation.

An important consideration for future research is whether the neuroprotective effects observed in the H2O2-induced oxidative stress model extend to other neurotoxic insults relevant to neurodegenerative diseases. Previous studies have demonstrated that licorice-derived compounds exhibit protective effects against diverse neurotoxic agents, including amyloid-β (Aβ) in PC12 cells62, serum/glucose deprivation-induced apoptosis63, and MPP + -induced toxicity in SH-SY5Y cells60. Given that many of these insults converge on common pathways of oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, it is plausible that the ethyl acetate extract of licorice fruit may provide broad-spectrum neuroprotection. However, direct experimental validation using specific models of glutamate excitotoxicity, Aβ toxicity, and mitochondrial toxin exposure is required to confirm this hypothesis and will be addressed in future studies.

Study limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while we have provided a comprehensive phytochemical fingerprint of the active ethyl acetate fraction—including TLC profiling with two mobile phases, total flavonoid quantification, and HPLC–DAD identification and quantification of glabridin—the identity of the other major peaks observed in the chromatogram remains unknown due to the limited phytochemical information available on licorice fruit. Isolation and structural elucidation of these compounds by preparative HPLC and NMR spectroscopy are required and are planned for future studies.

Second, the use of undifferentiated PC12 cells, while a well-established model for initial oxidative stress screening, limits direct physiological extrapolation to mature neurons, as they lack the full phenotypic and functional characteristics of NGF-differentiated or primary neuronal cells. Therefore, confirmation of these findings in more physiologically relevant models, including NGF-differentiated PC12 cells, primary neuronal cultures, and ultimately in vivo animal models of neurodegenerative diseases, is essential before drawing definitive conclusions about the therapeutic potential of licorice fruit extract.

Third, this study lacks a standard antioxidant positive control (e.g., quercetin or N-acetylcysteine). The inclusion of such a reference compound is essential for benchmarking the relative potency and translational relevance of the observed effects. Its absence limits the interpretability of the findings, as the magnitude of protection cannot be compared to established neuroprotective agents. This limitation is acknowledged, and future studies must include appropriate positive controls to enable meaningful benchmarking. Finally, our mechanistic exploration was focused on key downstream markers of oxidative apoptosis (ROS, ΔΨm, caspase-3); upstream signaling pathways, such as Nrf2/ARE or PI3K/Akt, and the expression of other apoptosis-related proteins (e.g., Bcl-2/Bax) remain to be investigated in future work.

Conclusion

In summary, this comparative screening study demonstrates, for the first time, that the ethyl acetate fraction of Glycyrrhiza glabra L. fruit possesses significant protective activity against H2O2-induced oxidative damage in PC12 cells. The protective effects, evidenced by increased cell viability, restored mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced caspase-3 activity, and decreased intracellular ROS levels, suggest the involvement of antioxidant and anti-apoptotic mechanisms.

The potent bioactivity of the low-yield ethyl acetate fraction indicates a selective enrichment of semi-polar bioactive compounds, which are known for their neuroprotective properties. Phytochemical profiling revealed that this fraction is rich in total flavonoids and contains glabridin, a well-documented neuroprotective isoflavonoid, although its low abundance suggests that other constituents may play a dominant role.

These findings highlight licorice fruit as a promising and underexplored source of neuroprotective agents, warranting further investigation. The observed effects are likely attributable to the synergistic interaction of multiple bioactive constituents, with glabridin serving as a confirmed but quantitatively minor component within a complex phytochemical matrix.

Future studies should include standard antioxidant compounds (e.g., quercetin or N-acetylcysteine) as positive controls to enable meaningful benchmarking of the extract’s relative potency. Additionally, isolation and structural elucidation of the major active constituents are necessary to validate and expand upon these initial findings.

Further mechanistic studies in more physiologically relevant neural models, including NGF-differentiated PC12 cells or primary neurons and animal models, are necessary to validate these initial findings and elucidate the precise molecular pathways involved.

Materials and methods

General instruments and chemicals

N-hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate, methanol, deionized water, 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium (MTT), fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCF-DA), Rhodamine-123, Triton X-100, and FBS were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, MO, USA). Also, Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM-F12) was purchased from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA) and Caspase-3 Detection kit (CASP3C-1KT) was purchased from Sigma, respectively.

Ethics approval

Plant material was prepared according to relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation, including the Medicinal Plant Specialist Group Species Survival Commission of IUCN (The World Conservation Union) and Law on Protection and Exploitation of Forests and Meadows (Iran, approved in 1967 with the latest amendments in 2020). Additionally, this initiative has received approval from the Ethics Committee at Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences in Kermanshah, Iran (Ethics Code: IR.KUMS.REC.1401.491, Approval Date: 2023-01-24).

Plant material and extraction

The Glycyrrhiza glabra fruits were collected from regions with a latitude of 34° 12′ 52.3″ N and a longitude of 47° 17′ 30.7″ E in Durood Faraman (Kermanshah province, I.R. Iran). Some plant samples were prepared. The plant was identified and authenticated by a taxonomist [Dr. Arash Sotoodeh]. A voucher specimen (No. 215-028-001) has been deposited in the Herbarium of Kermanshah Faculty of Pharmacy.

The collected fruits were dried. 385 g of licorice fruit were ground. Various extracts were prepared using n-hexane, chloroform, ethyl acetate (EA), methanol, and water to increase polarity sequentially. The solvent-sample ratio was 10:1. Extraction with each organic solvent was performed three times (24, 48, and 72 h, respectively) by the maceration method at laboratory temperature. The aqueous extract was prepared separately using a water bath maintained at 80 °C for 30 min. The prepared extracts were concentrated and dried using a rotary evaporator. The dried extracts were kept at − 20 °C for further investigation.

Cell culture conditions

The PC12 cell line (derived from a rat adrenal pheochromocytoma) is a well-established and widely used in vitro model for studying oxidative stress-induced cytotoxicity, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuroprotective mechanisms, due to its neuronal characteristics and reproducible response to insults such as H2O2. Although not mature neurons, PC12 cells possess key features of dopaminergic neurons, including substrates for dopamine synthesis and relevant membrane receptors70,71.

The PC12 cell line was obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran (Tehran, Iran). Cells were cultured in normal conditions consisting of DMEM-F12 with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated FBS, 1% streptomycin/ penicillin, and incubated in suitable conditions, 5% CO2, 90% humidity, and temperature of 37 °C.

Investigation of biological effects

MTT assay

Cytotoxicity of the extracts and H2O2 was investigated using the MTT method, and then the IC50 of the compounds was calculated. 15–20 × 103 cells were seeded in each well of a 96-well culture plate and then treated with different concentrations of each G. glabra extract in DMSO (12.5, 25, 50, and 100 µg/mL) and H2O2 in deionized water (12.5–200 µg/mL). The final concentration of DMSO in all treatment wells did not exceed 1% (v/v). It must be noted that the untreated cells (control group) and cells that exposed with H2O2 also received DMSO. After 24 h, the medium was replenished with 0.5 mg/mL MTT solution, and the plates were further incubated for 3 h at 37 °C. Thereafter, 100 μL DMSO was added to the wells for solubilizing the crystals of formazan. The absorbance at 570 nm corresponding to different treatment groups and the control was detected on the Eliza microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA). The IC50 value was also defined as the concentration at which 50% of cells were killed. All the MTT assays were carried out in triplicate.

Investigating the level of caspase 3 activity

Caspases are a family of protease enzymes whose activation is required to initiate cell apoptosis. One of the members of the caspase group is caspase-3, which is known as a key zymogen in apoptosis72,73. In order to find out the mechanism by which samples exert anti-apoptogenic activity, caspase-3 activity was determined based on the instructions of Sigma colorimetric caspase kit. For this purpose, PC12 cells were cultured in 6-well tissue culture plates and incubated for 24 h. Three different groups of cells (control, treated-H2O2, and extracts-pretreated H2O2-treated groups) were prepared and incubated. Then, the treated cells were centrifuged for 5 min at 1300 rpm and were lysed in 15 μL of the cell lysis buffer. The substrate reaction buffer containing caspase-3 substrate was added to the supernatant and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was then measured at 405 nm using a plate reader (BioTek, H1M)74. All treatment groups, including vehicle controls (1% DMSO), were processed under identical conditions.

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement (MMP)

Mitochondria play an important role in cell death and the permeability of the mitochondrial membrane determines the extent of cell damage. Therefore, mitochondrial membrane permeability can be a suitable target for new neuroprotectors75,76. In this study, mitochondrial membrane potential was investigated using Rhodamine-123 fluorescence dye.

The influx of rhodamine depends on the negatively charged protonic mitochondrial membrane potential within the mitochondrial matrix. During the process of apoptosis, mitochondrial depolarization causes rhodamine 123 to leave the mitochondria, thus reducing the intracellular fluorescence intensity77. PC12 cells were treated with the selected extracts in 6-well tissue culture plates for 24 h. After that, the IC50 concentration of H2O2 was added to the cells and incubated for another 24 h. At the end of the treatment, cells were incubated with rhodamine 123 (10 µL, 20 µM) for 30 min at 37 °C. Thereafter, 1-mL Triton X-100 was used for lysing the treated cells, and the amount of their fluorescence was measured with a microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm (BioTek H1M, USA).

It is important to note that non-ratiometric nature of Rhodamine-123 may limit specificity.

All treatment groups, including vehicle controls (1% DMSO), were processed under identical conditions.

Investigating the amount of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS)

7-Dichlorofluoroacetate (DCF) reagent was used to determine reactive oxygen species. This substance has a high fluorescent property in the presence of ROS. The intracellular ROS formation study was conducted on different groups of cells: (1) Control group, (2) Cells treated with H2O2 for 24 h, and (3) Cells pretreated with non-toxic concentrations (determined by MTT assay) of different extracts for 24 h and subsequently treated with H2O2 for another 24 h. After the treatment period, these groups were incubated with 10 µL DCF at 37 °C for 30 min. Thereafter, cells were lysed with Triton X-100, and fluorescence was measured at 488 nm excitation/528 nm emission using a fluorescence microplate reader (BioTek, H1M)74.

It must be noted that although DCF is prone to auto-oxidation and non-specific fluorescence, background subtraction using dye-free controls, serum-free dye loading, and standardized incubation conditions were employed to minimize variability. All treatment groups, including vehicle controls (1% DMSO), were processed under identical conditions.

Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis

TLC fingerprinting of the ethyl acetate fraction was performed on silica gel 60 F254 precoated aluminum plates (20 × 20 cm, 0.2 mm thickness; Merck, Germany). The fraction was dissolved in methanol, two distinct mobile phase systems were employed for development: (A) ethyl acetate/n-hexane (7:3, v/v) and (B) ethyl acetate/methanol (7:3, v/v). Chromatography was performed in a saturated glass chamber at ambient temperature. After development, plates were air-dried and visualized under UV light at 254 nm and 366 nm using a UV cabinet (Camag, Switzerland).

Determination of total flavonoid content (TFC)

Total flavonoid content was determined using the aluminum chloride colorimetric method29, with slight modifications. Briefly, 500 µL of each extract (1 mg/mL in methanol) was mixed with 2 mL of distilled water and 150 µL of 5% sodium nitrite (NaNO2) solution. After 5 min incubation, 150 µL of 10% aluminum chloride (AlCl3) solution was added and allowed to stand for another 6 min. Subsequently, 2 mL of 4% sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution was added, and the volume was adjusted to 5 mL with distilled water. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly, and the absorbance was measured at 510 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (BioTek Instruments, USA). Quercetin was used as the standard, and a calibration curve was constructed in the concentration range of 0–100 µg/mL (R2 > 0.98). Results were expressed as milligrams of quercetin equivalents (mg QE) per gram of dry extract. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

HPLC–DAD analysis

HPLC analysis was performed using a Knauer HPLC system (Berlin, Germany) equipped with a diode-array detector (DAD). Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Eurospher C18 reversed-phase column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 µm particle size; Knauer, Germany) maintained at ambient temperature.

The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (solvent A) and deionized water (solvent B). Gradient elution was performed as follows: 0–60 min, gradient from 30% A:70% B to 70% A:30% B. The flow rate was set at 0.8 mL/min, and the injection volume was 20 µL. Detection was monitored at 280 nm, and UV spectra were recorded from 200 to 500 nm.

Standard preparation: A stock solution of glabridin standard (≥ 98% purity, Merk, Germany) was prepared in methanol at 1 mg/mL and serially diluted to obtain five calibration concentrations (range 0.1–1 mg/mL). Each concentration was injected in triplicate. The calibration curve was constructed by plotting peak area against concentration, and linear regression analysis was performed (R2 > 0.997).

Sample preparation: The ethyl acetate extract was dissolved in HPLC-grade methanol at 8 mg/mL, filtered through a 0.45 µm syringe filter, and injected under the same chromatographic conditions.

Statistical analysis

In the present study, all the experiments were conducted in triplicate and reported values were represented as the mean value ± SEM. Individual groups were compared with Tukey’s post hoc test or Student’s t-test with a value of P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
