Regulatory T cells in liver metastases: emerging and divergent roles in tumour progression
Authors: Grace Wu, Ti Yang, Sepideh Gholami, Danielle DePeralta, Matthew Weiss, Meihong Deng, Ping Wang, Hai Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiver metastases (LMs) pose a significant burden of morbidity and mortality, resulting in a worse prognosis for many primary malignancies. Despite advancements in cancer treatment, such as immunotherapy, molecular therapies, additional lines of chemotherapy and optimisation of surgical resection, effective therapy against hepatic metastases remains elusive. Recent studies in immuno-oncology have implicated distinct tumour microenvironment (TME) signatures in the hepatic metastatic niche, which interplay with the intrinsic microenvironmental features specific to each primary tumour or organ of origin. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) have been implicated in the immunosuppressive nature of the hepatic TME, yet the exact mechanisms of interaction have not been fully elucidated. Discrepancies in number, function and proportion of Tregs to other tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes have been documented, with conflicting findings in the LMs from different primary tumours. These results may be attributable to the underlying biology of each organ-specific tumour and the unique hepatic TME that forms during the stepwise progression of the metastatic cascade. In this review, we explore the often-contradicting findings of intrahepatic Tregs in LMs from different originating tumours and offer insight into potential mechanisms for these observed differences with implications for future therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

The presence of liver metastases (LMs) portends a worse prognosis in many solid tumour malignancies. Not only are LMs associated with worse overall survival (OS) and clinical decline, but they are also less responsive to immunotherapy.1 This was initially demonstrated in melanoma in response to anti-programmed cell death-protein 1 (PD-1) therapy, but has since been replicated in multiple other cancers, including non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and urothelial cancer.2 Rates of LM response to immunotherapy have been reported to be less than 25% across cancer types.3 Specific mechanisms for this phenomenon are still being explored, though there is evidence that LMs can modulate immune function directly compared with other metastatic sites.4 Furthermore, the presence of tumour antigens within the liver has been shown to induce distant and systemic antigen-specific immunosuppression.5

The highly specialised immune microenvironment of the liver reflects its complex immune functions. The liver plays an important role in metabolism and detoxification, receiving most of the venous drainage from the intestines. This delivers not only circulating tumour cells and nutrients that can foster tumour growth, but also a myriad of antigens to the liver. The hepatic immune microenvironment therefore requires tolerance to food and microbial antigens from the gut while mounting immunity against true pathogens.6 However, this balance results in relative immune unresponsiveness, which has important implications for effective cancer therapy.

Regulatory T cells (Tregs), while not unique to the liver, are an important component of ‘portal vein tolerance’, in which antigens delivered to the liver can induce both local and systemic tolerance.7 Under physiological conditions, Tregs are critical mediators that maintain immune homeostasis by suppressing the activation of effector T cells (Teffs).8 In the tumour microenvironment (TME), activated hepatic Tregs drive immunosuppression locally and systemically, compromising both intrahepatic and extrahepatic antitumour immunity. Emerging evidence indicates that Tregs exhibit distinct phenotypes depending on their tissue of origin. This is further complicated in tumour-infiltrating Tregs, where a complex relationship exists between Tregs derived from the primary tissue of origin, the metastatic environment and the peripheral blood.9 It is therefore crucial to understand the functionality of Tregs in the context of their tissue-specific properties and within the immune landscape of the liver metastatic niche. In this review, we explore the often-contradicting findings of Tregs in the presence of LMs from different originating tumours and offer insight into potential mechanisms for these observed differences and implications for cancer immunotherapy strategies. We address LMs from differing primary tumours by incidence, beginning with colorectal cancer (CRC) which represents the most frequently resected hepatic metastasis.10 Experiences in colorectal liver metastases provide the basis for patient-derived specimens with corresponding robustness in basic science investigations.

Treg maturation and differentiation trajectories

Tregs are a specialised subset of CD4+ T cells essential for maintaining immune homeostasis and self-tolerance. They develop primarily in the thymus under the control of three key signals: T cell receptor (TCR) recognition of peptide–MHC ligands, costimulatory interactions between CD80/CD86 and CD28, and activation by interleukin (IL)-2 and IL-15.11 Tregs are classified as thymus-derived (tTregs) or peripherally induced (pTregs). The tTreg subset, which is predominant recognises autoantigens and prevents autoimmune responses, whereas the pTreg subset differentiates from naive peripheral CD4+ T cells in response to antigens, particularly in intestinal tissues enriched in transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and retinoic acid.12 Both subsets require IL-2 for survival and function, and in vitro induction of Tregs (iTregs) is strongly dependent on TGF-β.13 Phenotypically, Tregs are defined by high expression of forkhead box P3 (FoxP3) and the IL-2 receptor α-chain (CD25), with the CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ profile serving as a canonical marker.14

Regulatory T cells as mediators of immunosuppression

Tregs are a subset of CD4+ T cells characterised by the expression of the transcription factor FoxP3, which is their most specific marker and master regulator.15 Physiologically, Tregs are key mediators in immune homeostasis by maintaining tolerance to self-antigens.16 Tregs employ a variety of mechanisms by which they exert effects on or are in turn affected by other immune cells to decrease immune responsiveness, which are central to preventing autoimmunity. These specific mechanisms are extensively reviewed elsewhere but include Treg modulation of Teff through secretion of cytokines and cytotoxic molecules, expression of immune checkpoint receptors that directly suppress Teff and interference with Teff metabolism.1719

In immuno-oncology, Tregs can promote tumour development because their restraining of Teffs prevents effective killing of tumour cells. Tregs are found in increased numbers at tumour sites, and once in the TME, they become reprogrammed and develop stronger immunosuppressive properties.16 Locally, Treg inhibition of Teffs impairs cancer immunosurveillance and fosters tumour cell survival. Tregs are also directly recruited to and induced by the TME,20 where Tregs metabolically outcompete Teffs, increasing Treg activation and proliferation while suppressing Teffs activity.21 Systemically, Treg interaction with intratumoural antigens within the hepatic TME results in immunological dysfunction that can lead to impaired tumour immunity at extrahepatic sites.5

However, while Tregs were traditionally considered immunosuppressive, contradictory data exist for the prognostic value of infiltrating Tregs across solid tumour types.22 As emerging research characterises the functionality of Tregs, they have been shown to promote wound healing and tissue regeneration,23 in addition to maintaining tolerance. Tregs may therefore play a dual role in tumourigenesis by promoting the advancement and development of tumours via immunosuppression while also suppressing the chronic inflammation that has been linked to many types of cancers.24 They may have contradictory roles across the continuum of cancer development, progression and metastasis, that is, having an initially anti-inflammatory and protective role against inflammation-induced tumour development but eventually contributing to reduced antitumour immunity via their immunosuppressive properties. This may also be attributable to distinct subpopulations of Tregs possessing unique functionality within human tumours.19 Clinical research has shown that infiltration of subgroups of Tregs may be associated with improved prognosis in specific cancers, such as in gastric cancer and CRC.25 26 For example, Tregs expressing higher levels of FoxP3 (FoxP3high Tregs) exhibit stronger inhibitory function than FoxP3low Tregs, which are weakly inhibitory.27 Variation in methodology and characterisation of Treg subsets across studies,28 as well as inherent time-dependent fluctuations in immune cell populations in both animal models versus clinical trials, contribute to the heterogeneity seen in the literature.

Tregs surface molecules involved in tumour development and immunosuppression

Tregs in the TME have unique surface molecules that enhance their suppressive capabilities. TME Tregs express high levels of immune checkpoint (IC) molecules, cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) and PD-1, which forms the basis for immunotherapy.29 Importantly, failure of immune checkpoint blockade and therapy resistance has been attributed to the preferential activation and enhancement of Tregs in the TME,30 indicating that dual therapy with IC inhibitors alongside Treg depletion may be crucial for effective therapeutic outcomes.

Other Treg-specific surface molecules have therefore become targets for therapeutic intervention via depletion or inhibition of function. These include chemokine receptors such as C-C motif chemokine receptor (CCR4), which allows Tregs to be trafficked into tumour tissue; costimulatory molecules, such as glucocorticoid-induced TNFR-related (GITR) protein, CD27, inducible costimulator (ICOS), and OX40, which play important roles in Treg expansion; and co-inhibitory molecules such as T-cell immunoglobulin and immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif domain (TIGIT) and lymphocyte activating 3 (LAG3), which increase Teff exhaustion31 (figure 1).

Crosstalk between Tregs and the tumour immune microenvironment

Tregs and the TME

Characteristics of the TME and tumour cells contribute to immunosuppression via Treg-mediated mechanisms. Various immune escape pathways are employed by tumours depending on the tissue of origin, that is, through increased recruitment and activation of Tregs, decreased recruitment and activation of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (CTLs), increased exhaustion of CTLs or a combination. Secretion of chemokines results in chemotaxis of Tregs to the site of the tumour, with specific chemokines being dependent on tumour tissue type.20 Enhancement of Treg suppressive function also occurs via tumour cell secretion of cytokines such as TGF-β.32

Additionally, the acidic and hypoxic environment of the TME that results from increased metabolism of cancer cells allows for increased immunosuppressive Treg function.33 34 Higher levels of ATP to adenosine conversion in apoptotic Tregs via CD39 and CD73, and the activation of adenosine A2A receptor pathway also suppress immune cells.35 Apoptosis of tumour cells, in addition to Tregs and the rapid generation of self-antigens, are hypothesised to activate Tregs in the TME and may partially explain why tumour lines with high mutational burdens respond better to immune checkpoint therapy.35

Tregs and liver resident cells

The liver is home to a unique population of resident cells, including liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), Kupffer cells (KCs), hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), and hepatocytes with antigen presenting capabilities, which contribute to liver tolerance.7 Liver dendritic cells (DCs), KCs and LSECs are all capable of inducing Tregs, though LSECs are the most efficient at TGF-β mediated Treg induction via their membrane tethering of TGF-β.36 KCs can also induce Tregs via secretion of prostaglandins and IL-10.3 37 In the context of immuno-oncology, HSCs have been shown to promote angiogenesis and induce apoptosis in Teffs while promoting Treg expansion.7 Similar to the interaction between myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and Tregs, the crosstalk between KCs and Tregs can create an immunosuppressive environment that prevents the establishment of an effective CTL response.38

Tregs and Teffs

As the regulators of Teffs, Tregs restrain antitumour immunity by suppressing Teff function in the TME. Teffs differentiate from naive T cells to become effector T cells, such as CD4+ helper T cells, or CD8+ CTLs. Several mechanisms have emerged as distinct features of Treg suppression. First, Tregs inhibit Teffs through the secretion of soluble mediators such as inhibitory cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β, IL-35) or cytotoxic molecules that directly kill Teffs (perforin, granzyme).18 Tregs also affect Teff metabolism; one key mechanism involves the competitive consumption of IL-2 in the TME, which both Tregs and Teffs require for proliferation. Lastly, direct cell-cell interactions also occur via checkpoint molecules and inhibitory cell surface markers such as CTLA-4, which prevent stimulation and action of Teffs.35 Specifically, constitutive expression of CTLA-4 on Tregs blocks antigen presenting cells (APCs) from being able to prime and activate naïve Teffs.35 APCs are similarly affected by inhibitory cytokines and cytotoxic molecules secreted by Tregs. Clinically, a high ratio of Tregs to Teffs in the TME has been shown to correlate with poor prognostic outcomes across multiple solid cancers.31 In murine models, depletion of Tregs re-establishes antitumour immunity while slowing tumour growth.30

Tregs and MDSCs

In addition to Teffs, Tregs also interact with MDSCs to reinforce a suppressive immune microenvironment. MDSCs are another important driver in the TME and are classified into two subtypes based on their properties, monocytic MDSCs or granulocytic MDSCs.39 Importantly, they are pathologically activated and only appear in disease states. The crosstalk between Tregs and MDSCs regulates the activity and expansion of each, forming a positive feedback loop of induction, proliferation and activation.40 Mechanisms include secretion of soluble mediators by both types of cells (TGF-β, IL-10), metabolic cooperation within the hypoxic and acidic TME and direct cell-cell interactions via checkpoint receptors (including programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)/PD-1, CTLA-4, LAG-3, T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM-3), and TIGIT) that influence recruitment and induction.40

Tregs and NK cells

CD3+CD56+ natural killer T (NKT)-like cells, which co-express NK and T cell markers, represent a critical bridge between innate and adaptive immunity.41 Recent evidence has demonstrated that NKT cells comprise a heterogeneous lymphocyte population that includes CD4+, CD8+, TCRγδ+, mucosal-associated invariant T and invariant NKT (iNKT) cells.42 Tregs modulate iNKT cell activation and proliferation, thereby shaping their homeostasis and effector functions in vivo.43 Conversely, NKT cells can negatively regulate the differentiation of iTregs through interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) production, underscoring the bidirectional nature of their interaction.44 Furthermore, although Treg cells generally exert a suppressive influence on antitumour immunity, NKT cells contribute to natural tumour surveillance.41 Evidence indicates that Treg cells can inhibit the antitumour activity of NKT cells, at least in certain experimental settings.45 For instance, Treg cells induced by genetic immunisation reduce both the number and functional activity of NKT cells, thereby promoting tumour growth.45 Conversely, it has been reported that CD1d-restricted T cells—rather than Tregs—are primarily responsible for downregulating natural tumour immunity.41 Notably, this suppression is not mediated by classical invariant NKT cells (type I NKT cells), but rather by CD1d-restricted, non-classical NKT cells (type II NKT cells) that express diverse TCR repertoires.41 The precise functional interplay between NKT cells and Tregs remains to be fully elucidated.

Tregs and neutrophil cells

The innate immune system plays a vital role in cancer immune surveillance and collaborates closely with adaptive immunity to establish a comprehensive immune network within the nonalcoholic steatohepatitis-related hepatocellular carcinoma (NASH-HCC) microenvironment. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) promote the differentiation of Tregs by inducing metabolic reprogramming of naive CD4+ T cells, thereby suppressing immune surveillance during the precancerous stage of NASH and facilitating tumour development.46 Similarly, in the inflammatory immune milieu, NETs enhance the transcription and activity of enzymes involved in cholesterol metabolism, acting as a metabolic switch that drives lipid metabolic reprogramming in naive CD4+T cells. Increased intracellular cholesterol synthesis subsequently promotes Treg differentiation and impairs their immune inhibitory function.47

Role of Tregs in liver metastases

Early clinical studies have evaluated numbers of circulating Tregs in the presence of LM, as this approach is less invasive than obtaining tumourous tissue; however, this fails to capture the complexity of the TME. Primary tumour Tregs, the circulating Tregs, and the liver resident Tregs are all sources of Treg in metastatic tumours (table 1). Notably, T cell densities differ in matched primary tumours and their corresponding LMs,48 and in the expression of surface markers such as PD-1.49 Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has also demonstrated that primary liver cancers, such as hepatocellular carcinoma, colorectal liver metastases and primary CRC tissue, have distinct immune features.50 Tumour-specific, rather than organ-specific, recruitment and activation of Tregs occur in both primary and metastatic liver cancers. Primary liver cancer is further characterised by tumour-activated Tregs, a low ratio of non-suppressive Treg subsets and high infiltration of ectonucleoside triphosphate diphosphohydrolase 1 (ENTPD1+) Tregs and MDSCs.

Tregs can be stimulated by tumour-associated antigens and immunosuppressive factors secreted within the tumour microenvironment, such as TGF-β and IL-10. Compared with metastatic liver tumours, primary liver cancer exhibits a more suppressive immune microenvironment.51 Research indicates that Tregs from patients with metastatic liver cancer display a more activated phenotype, expressing CTLA-4+, C-X-C chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4+), PD-1+ and ENTPD1+. Conversely, ICOS+ Tregs are uniquely found in patients with primary liver cancer and contribute to effective immune escape.52 Notably, primary liver cancer has a reduced population of non-suppressive Tregs, characterised by low FoxP3 demethylation rates and an impaired capacity to suppress effector T cell proliferation, relative with metastatic liver cancer.53 In metastatic liver cancer, Tregs are activated by soluble factors, including exosomes, released from the primary tumour (eg, CRC or breast cancer (BC)) into circulation, which induce systemic immunosuppression and promote Treg generation and expansion.

Furthermore, the liver functions as a nutrient reservoir and exhibits intrinsic immune tolerance due to its constant exposure to gut microbiota antigens via the portal vein. Its microenvironment, comprising KCs, LSECs and others, is predisposed to induce Tregs and promote immune tolerance (table 1). Metastatic cancer cells exploit and reinforce this tolerogenic environment on colonisation of the liver.54 At the same time, multi-omic studies have revealed that the TME signatures in metastatic sites can be explained by the features intrinsic to the tissue even with differing primary tumours, that is, liver metastases may in general have differing levels of immune activation and infiltration compared with lung or other metastases.55 It is therefore important to evaluate patterns within the context of a metastatic host organ, while allowing for nuance that results from the shaping of the liver TME from immune cell interactions with specific tumour antigens.

Liver metastases pose a clinical challenge with limited treatment options due to the inefficacy of immunotherapy. Preclinical models have demonstrated the establishment of an ‘immune desert’ in the liver, where activated CD8+ T cells are sequestrated from the circulation and subsequently undergo apoptosis.4 In the presence of LMs, Tregs have increased activation and are more potent than in disease without LMs, expressing higher levels of immunosuppressive surface markers.3 While the exact mechanisms by which Tregs mediate both local and systemic antitumour immunity are still being studied, there is evidence that it is Treg modulation of intratumoural monocytes in response to tumour antigens within the liver that leads to systemic suppression of antitumour immunity.5

Among the different primary tumours that metastasise to the liver, colorectal, breast, lung (both SCLC and NSCLC), pancreatic, gastric, neuroendocrine tumours and melanoma (cutaneous and uveal) occur with the highest frequency,10 which we aim to address in this review (figure 2).

Colorectal

CRC represents a major global health burden with up to 50% of patients developing LMs during their illness.56 Complete surgical excision remains central to curative treatment of colorectal liver metastases (CRLMs),57 yet up to 75% of patients undergoing liver resection subsequently recur, most within the first 3 years.56

CRLMs represent a unique opportunity for characterising cellular and molecular immune signatures of LM, as surgery remains central to curative-intent treatment in contrast to other stage IV cancers where metastatic disease precludes resection.10 In primary CRC, high levels of FoxP3+ in human tumours are associated with a lack of distant metastases (defined separately from hepatic metastases), indicating protective effects on immunosurveillance.58 Infiltration of a subset of Tregs expressing CD30+ and OX40+ was also associated with improved OS,25 likely due to suppression of the chronic, microbiome-related inflammation in the gut that can be pro-tumourigenic.59 Tregs may therefore play an initially protective role in the pathogenesis of the primary tumour, but contribute to metastasis development and proliferation later in the disease course.

Clinically, contradictory data exist regarding the utility of Tregs as a prognostic indicator for CRLM due to discrepancies in methodology of quantifying immune cells in hepatic and tumourous tissues.6062 Intratumourally, the ratio of Tregs to Teffs is a prognostic factor, with higher Treg:Teff ratio as an independent predictor of shorter OS.60 62 However, high infiltration of Tregs peritumourally was associated with better survival,61 though notably there was no consensus on how these areas are defined across studies. Tregs are also increased intratumourally as compared with peritumourally in tertiary lymphoid structures of patients with resected CRLM; calculation of an immune score from these data correlated with recurrence-free survival and OS.63 Expansion of CRLM-derived TILs showed a correlation of Treg numbers with CD8+ T cells expressing high levels of TIM-3, another marker of Teff exhaustion.64 However, both the number of Tregs and levels of TIM-3 ultimately did not correlate with clinical outcomes. Examination of Treg densities in CRC and corresponding CRLM primary showed higher epithelial infiltration of Tregs in both types of tissues in patients with a worse prognosis.65 Without drawing conclusions on clinical outcomes, one group demonstrated that levels of Treg/T helper cell were consistently higher in the intratumoural, invasive margin and adjacent tissue of primary CRC than in their matched CRLM, with intratumoural CRC with the highest numbers.48

Phenotypically, Tregs isolated from patients with CRLM demonstrated increased activation and immunosuppressive function, including upregulation of inhibitory markers and immune checkpoints PD-1, TIM-3, LAG-3, GITR and CTLA-4 (Online supplemental table 1). Tregs from both tumour and blood expressed higher levels of inhibitory markers GITR and CTLA-4.66 Treatment ex vivo with GITR ligand or blocking antibodies to CTLA-4 reversed Treg immunosuppression.67 This was redemonstrated specifically in mismatch repair proficient CRLM TILs68 and with LAG3 blockade.69 Before surgery, Tregs isolated from blood restrained CD3+ T cells by decreasing expression of IFN-γ and TNF-α.70 COX-2 expression in Tregs was higher in patients with recurrent disease, although interestingly, inhibitory markers PD-1 and CTLA-4 were not altered in this population.70 Tregs from microsatellite stable (MSS) CRLM expressed high levels of immune checkpoint PD-1 and ICOS as identified on scRNA-seq.71

A proposed mechanism for Treg suppression of antitumour immunity in CRLM is through their role as the primary source of IL-10 in metastatic sites.54 Treg-derived IL-10 upregulates monocyte expression of PD-L1, which restrained CD8+ infiltration and subsequent killing of tumour cells. The signalling pathway hepatocyte growth factor/mesenchymal–epithelial transition factor (HGF/c-Met) was also found to be upregulated,72 as well as mRNA regulation of Tregs via RNA binding protein YTH N6-Methyladenosine RNA Binding Protein 2 (YTHDF2).73 Increased TNF signalling in the TME promoted YTHDF2 expression, which in turn regulated nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signalling and enhanced suppressive function of Tregs. Metabolically, increased lactate in the TME mediates M2 macrophage polarisation by suppressing NF-κB, which upregulates Treg expression of PD-1 and immunosuppressive function.74 On a cellular level, Spp1+ tumour-associated macrophages, which are predominant in CRLM, have been implicated in multiple studies.50 71 75 Tregs were enriched and associated with hypoxia-related molecule SLC2A1 in CRLM, which also promotes an increased proportion of Spp1+ macrophages.75

Lastly, the role of Tregs in treatment response to chemotherapy and immunotherapy is an active area of research. Tregs have been implicated in immunotherapy resistance, where PD-L1 monotherapy administered to mice suppressed subcutaneous tumour growth, but not growth of LM.76 Combination treatment with polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid, a synthetic double stranded RNA that activates innate immunity by binding to toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3), saw the most significant decrease in Tregs compared with other immune cells, leading to enhanced Teff:Treg ratio, which correlated with eradication of liver tumours. This corroborates other work on immunotherapy resistance, where Treg-depleting anti-CTLA-4 therapy in combination restored responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy.5 A subset of highly proliferative CD29+ Tregs, which proliferate in response to PD-1 therapy, may be a potential target.77 In a cohort of MSS CRLM treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT),78 a time-dependent difference in Treg:Teff ratio was observed at short interval re-evaluation (<9.5 weeks) versus long interval. The twofold increase suggests that the effectiveness of Teff antitumoural immunity may be transient and that immunosuppression increases after NACT administration in correlation with distant liver metastatic intratumoural Treg levels.

Breast

In BC, distant metastasis remains a significant source of morbidity, with 50–70% of cases involving the liver and a median survival rate of 2–3 years.79 Similar to other solid organ tumours, breast cancer liver metastasis (BCLM) are associated with a poorer prognosis and lower response rates to immunotherapy.80 While Treg infiltration is a known independent prognostic factor in metastatic BC,81 limited data exist on the role of Tregs within the liver TME.82

In postmortem autopsy studies, LMs from BC were found to have lower TILs but higher fractions of activated Tregs, specifically expressing TIGIT+ and CTLA-4+ as compared with lung metastases.83 Interestingly, another study comparing breast tissue and LMs corroborated a decrease in T cells in LMs, but also a decreased number of Tregs and their genes FoxP3 and CTLA-4.84 As authors observed increased tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), immunosuppression in BCLMs was hypothesised to be driven by innate immunity as opposed to adaptive immunity (online supplemental table 1).

Within the TME, Tregs were found to have the most interactions with other cell populations in BCLM, especially with cancer cells, which recruited Tregs through the chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 10 (CXCL10)-C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 3 (CXCR3) ligand receptor pair.85 The signalling pathway interleukin 33 (IL-33) /growth stimulation expressed gene 2 (ST2) may also play a role as administration of IL-33 accelerated tumour growth and development of LM alongside higher infiltration of IL-10 producing ST2 expressing Tregs.86 Concomitant with increased MDSCs and DCs, these ST2+ Tregs may suppress NK cell and Teff cytotoxicity to restrain antitumour immunity.

Several studies have sought to investigate Tregs in the context of increasing efficacy of immunotherapy. In a model of triple negative breast cancer (BC), a bio-nanoparticle vaccine was used to enhance PD-1 inhibitor response, with a significant reduction in Tregs after combination treatment.87 Matrix metalloproteinase inhibitors were used in combination with anti-CTLA-4 antibody, which decreased the ratio of Treg/Th17 and the number of macroscopic liver metastases.88 Novel oncolytic adenovirus rAd.sT also decreased Tregs and enhanced response to anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 therapy.89 Hepatic arterial infusion (HAI) with experimental drug CBL0137 in combination with PD-1 blockade depleted MDSCs and notably did not increase the number of Tregs, which was found to occur in 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)-based HAI.90 Lastly, prophylactic vaccination with DCs reduced LMs and reshaped the TME.91 Interestingly, CTLs were increased, but so were Tregs; restoration of antitumour immunity may perhaps be due to an overall increase in T lymphocytes and not dependent on the absolute number of Tregs. Additionally, time-dependent fluctuations in immune cell populations may not be captured in singular time points.

Gastric

While gastric cancer incidence is lower in the USA, it remains the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide.92 The liver is a frequent site of gastric cancer metastasis, with an incidence of 5–34% and portending a poor prognosis.93 The role of Tregs in the pathogenesis of primary gastric cancer has been extensively reviewed elsewhere.94 Mechanisms include Helicobacter pylori infection as a carcinogen and subsequent reshaping of the TME via macrophage secretion of IL-10 and TGF-β to induce gastric Tregs and DC recruitment of Tregs via CCR4 (online supplemental table 1).

In the context of LMs, studies are inconsistent on whether primary gastric cancer or its paired hepatic metastases harbour higher numbers of Tregs. One study found that Tregs are significantly higher in the primary tumour lesion, but noted higher immunosuppressive effects in the liver.95 Lower infiltration of Tregs in both the primary lesions and metastatic lesions was correlated with improved prognosis. In contrast, updates to a clinical trial examining multikinase inhibitors in combination with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy (regorafenib plus nivolumab or lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab) found opposite results.96 Multiplex immunohistochemistry (IHC) of tissue samples showed greater TAMs and Tregs and fewer CTLs in the liver as compared with the primary gastric tumour. Anti-PD-1 therapy in combination with multikinase inhibitors showed promising antitumour activity regardless of the presence of LM. Lastly, pathological response to chemotherapy was assessed in a cohort of patients and correlated with the amount of tumour infiltrating Tregs. A reduction in Tregs was correlated with pathologic remission of gastric cancer LM. Interestingly, there was no correlation with infiltrating CTLs.97

In the TME, resident stromal cells known as gastric cancer tissue-derived mesenchymal stem cells were found to induce Treg differentiation from naïve CD4+ cells while promoting cell migration, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and development of LMs in vivo.98

Pancreatic

In aggressive malignancies such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), which has a 5-year survival of 8–9%,99 a persistent obstacle to treatment is the presence of distant metastases at the time of diagnosis. Treatment failure has been theorised to be associated with specific characteristics of the PDAC TME, previously characterised as an immune ‘desert’ with a high infiltration of Tregs and lack of Teffs.99 For this reason, immunotherapy has not been effective in PDAC due to the highly immunosuppressive TME (online supplemental table 1).

Phenotypically, one study compared hepatic metastases from PDAC to pulmonary metastases from PDAC due to worse prognosis in LMs and poorer response to immunotherapy. Interestingly, multi-omic analyses revealed that lung TME had higher infiltration of all immune cells, including both Tregs and Teffs.55 Tregs from PDAC primary versus matched LMs both display high levels of immune checkpoint genes, with Tregs from LMs displaying significantly higher expression of all five checkpoints evaluated: CTLA4, TIGIT, ICOS, tumour necrosis factor superfamily member (TNFRSF4), TNFRSF9.100 Tregs-ductal cell signalling was also increased, whereas other T cell-ductal cell signalling decreased in PDAC LMs.

In an early evaluation of combination radiotherapy (RT) with immunotherapy, Azad et al found that PD-L1 blockade with RT increased CD8:Treg ratio in liver metastases while also reducing metastatic burden in mouse model of LSL-Kras(G12D); LSL-Trp53(R172H); Pdx1-Cre (KPC cell line).101 PDAC samples after stereotactic body RT (SBRT) were found to be enriched with signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) which inhibited efficacy of SBRT while promoting proliferation of metastases.99 STAT3 knockout in Tregs decreased metastases and proliferation while increasing Teff function (NK, DC, CD4). Dual targeting of STAT3 on Tregs and NK cells using an antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) strengthened the Teff response, increasing both circulating and infiltrating Teff populations while inhibiting local and distant disease progression. A combination of ASO and RT increased survival in mice. Similarly, SBRT was combined with intravenous R848, a TLR7/8 agonist, to potentiate antitumour effects of SBRT.102 Combination therapy of SBRT to the pancreatic primary tumour and intravenous R848 decreased Tregs while increasing CD8+ T cells in the pancreatic TME, and importantly, produced similar results in hepatic metastases.

Tregs have also been linked to chemoresistance in PDAC. D’Costa 2017 found that upregulation of the cytokine tissue inhibitor of matrix metalloproteinases 1 (TIMP1) in PDAC lesions correlated with increased liver metastases and infiltration of CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ Tregs.103 Inhibition of TIMP1 resensitised tumours to gemcitabine and RT while decreasing the number of Tregs.

In the context of immunotherapy, the computer-based assessment and treatment (COMBAT) trial evaluated the combination of BL-8040, a CXCR4 antagonist, in combination with pembrolizumab and chemotherapy.104 CXCR4 attracts immunosuppressive myeloid cells into the microenvironment. CXCR4 blockade was found to be synergistic with anti-PD-1 therapy. In one patient with microsatellite stable pancreatic cancer who had developed progression of hepatic metastases on two lines of chemotherapy and after microwave ablation, BL-8040 and pembrolizumab as third-line therapy resulted in partial response. There was a nadir of 39.7% tumour reduction, including the hepatic metastases. Although Tregs within liver metastatic tumours were not directly evaluated, flow cytometry demonstrated reduction in circulating Tregs on both BL-8040 monotherapy and in combination with pembrolizumab.

Kerzel and colleagues engineered a lentiviral vector platform to harness macrophage delivery of interferon alpha (IFNɑ) to liver metastases from CRC and PDAC.105 This was combined with anti-CTLA-4 therapy due to observations that resistant mice had expanded populations of a subset of Tregs that expressed high levels of exhaustion markers, CTLA-4, LAG-3, PD-1 and immunosuppressive markers such as IL-10. Notably, these Tregs lack expression of FoxP3. While individual therapy with engineered macrophages or CTLA-4 reduced the growth of LMs, only combination therapy reliably eradicated established LMs, which was shown in both CRC and PDAC mouse models.

Lung

Worldwide, lung cancer is the most prevalent malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality.106 In the discussion of pulmonary TME, most studies focus on the lung itself as a site of metastasis rather than as the source for LMs, likely due to the prevalence of metastases to the lung and the poor prognosis of metastatic primary lung cancer. NSCLC accounts for 80–85% of lung cancer, with LMs occurring in 20% of patients.106 Interestingly, there are conflicting data on the efficacy of immunotherapy in the presence of LMs, and it appears that combination IC inhibitor (ICI) with chemotherapy provides a synergistic effect with similar median OS (mOS) regardless of LM status.107

SCLC is highly aggressive and the liver is the most common site of metastasis, correlating with a prognosis of mOS of 3–4 months.108 While combination chemotherapy and immunotherapy have improved outcomes in extensive SCLC without LMs, the same has not been true for patients with LM.109

Liang and colleagues characterised a subset of highly immunosuppressive Tregs, FOXA1+ Tregs (defined as CD4+CD25+PDL1high), which are notably low in FoxP3 while being high in forkhead box A1 (FoxA1) and regulates the expression of PD-L1. Notably, clinical samples were obtained from the blood, primary lung cancer tissue, and malignant pleural effusions, not LMs, and demonstrated enriched FOXA1 Tregs in those with LM compared with those without.110

Kidney

LMs are associated with poor prognosis in kidney cancer, though are more infrequent, representing approximately 18% of metastatic cases.111 The majority (85–90%) are associated with extrahepatic disease.112 As such, the current literature on Tregs in RCC evaluates metastatic disease (mRCC) as a whole with less emphasis on specific metastatic liver TME. Regardless of LM status, clinical trials on immunotherapy have demonstrated improvements in response rates and survival with combination immunotherapy and multikinase inhibitor compared with multikinase inhibitor alone, though there is a trend towards better outcomes in patients without LM.113

In a comparison of peripheral blood lymphocytes in patients with mRCC and those without, patients with disease had higher levels of circulating peripheral Tregs and enrichment of CTLA-4 gene expression.114 An increase in circulating Tregs was observed after two cycles of IL-2 immunotherapy, and those who were poor responders had a higher expansion of Tregs than those who responded. In a single-cell multiplex study, FoxP3+PD-1+ Tregs and FoxP3+ Tregs were found to have increased infiltration in mRCC lesions (bone, lung, viscera, other), though interestingly, there was no difference among the different metastatic sites, perhaps due to low sample size.115 FoxP3+PD-1+ Tregs were also closely associated with exhausted CD8+PD-1+ tumour specific Teffs on multiplex imaging, suggesting a cell-cell interaction that can contribute to the development of resistance after PD-1 blockade. Another study found that PD-1+ Treg density in the primary tumour after anti-PD-1 therapy was linked to shorter progression-free survival (PFS) and OS in mRCC, though notably this was not the case for overall Treg density.116 These findings suggest that dual treatment with anti-PD-1 agents in conjunction with inhibition of Tregs may synergise therapy and reduce resistance, especially to prevent hyperprogressive disease, where rapid tumour growth occurs on ICI treatment. Further work is needed to articulate these nuances within the RCC hepatic metastatic niche.

Melanoma

The driver of mortality in cutaneous melanoma (CM) is the presence of visceral metastases. Among these, CM metastases initially occur in the liver in 14–20% of cases, which may serve as a significant determinant of clinical outcomes.117

An early clinical trial depleted Tregs using diphtheria toxin and IL-2 in 16 patients with melanoma with metastases to the liver, lungs and lymph nodes.118 Follow-up imaging with CT/positron emission tomography showed promising regression of metastases in five of those patients (online supplemental table 1). Repopulation of T cells after treatment coincided with the appearance of melanoma antigen-specific CD8+ T cells, suggesting that transient depletion of Tregs allowed a shift in the balance of Treg/Teff towards a less immunosuppressive environment with population of Teffs trained against neoplastic antigens. Changes in peripheral Tregs after cryoablation with transarterial infusion of pembrolizumab were evaluated in patients with melanoma with LM. Tregs decreased 3 weeks after single cycle treatment, while NK cells significantly increased.119

Mechanistically, CD39 expression on Tregs and local adenosine generation are linked to the growth and development of LMs by inhibition of NK cells antitumour immunity.120 Growth of LMs was inhibited in CD39 null mice. CD200R deficient mice receiving CD200+ melanoma cells had massive tumour growth, including LMs, which was not seen in wild-type (WT) mice, whereas CD200− did not produce the same effect.121 Livers of mice with metastatic growth in CD200R deficiency had increased number of Tregs, reduced NK cells and reduced T cell responses compared with WT mice. Lyve-1 deficiency led to reduced growth of LMs of melanoma but not CRC, which was attributed to the more immunogenic nature of melanoma tumours.122 However, both in tumour-free livers, and in livers with metastases, Lyve-1 knockout had increased Treg populations compared with control, suggesting the protective effects of lyve-1 deficiency are not Treg mediated.

Uveal melanoma (UM) is a genetically distinct subset of melanoma that often metastasises to the liver and is usually fatal within 1 year.123 Comparison of peripheral blood of healthy donors with those of patients with UM and LM demonstrated enhanced Treg population in patients with UM as well as presence of Teffs with lower levels of the activation marker human leukocyte antigen DR (HLA-DR), indicating a more immunosuppressive microenvironment.124

Neuroendocrine tumours

The liver is the most common site of metastasis in neuroendocrine tumours, with 82% of patients with metastases developing LMs.125 In one study examining the association between TILs and clinical outcomes for pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours with LMs, decreased infiltration of Tregs was a predictor of prolonged survival.126 Circulating levels of Tregs were also used as a marker for treatment response to transarterial embolisation (TAE) in NETLMs.127 The study included a total of 139 patients with gastropancreatic neuroendocrine tumours, 51 with LMs who underwent TAE to reduce tumour burden. Not only were higher levels of Tregs associated with presence of LMs, but those in the Treg high group (defined as greater than 8.015%) who underwent TAE were also less responsive to therapy and had reduced hepatic PFS. Based on these results, the authors concluded that the level of circulating Tregs may have a strong prognostic impact on gastropancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (online supplemental table 1).

Gastrointestinal stromal tumours

Liver metastases from gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GIST) have been shown to respond well to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as imatinib.128 They are often found synchronously with the primary tumour, or manifest after initial resection of a high-risk GIST.128 Unfortunately, even with TKIs, drug resistance can occur within 2 years of treatment.129 As the primary site of tumour development, GISTs do not harbour a lot of Tregs relatively and correlate negatively with PFS.129 However, they correlate positively with high-risk GIST and have no association with rates of metastasis.129 The TME of GISTs, as well as their peritoneal and liver metastases, was compared with elucidate mechanisms of imatinib resistance, which showed a higher proportion of activated tumour cell to Treg interactions via immune checkpoint T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT)-nectin cell adhesion molecule 2 (NECTIN2).130

Taken together, heterogeneity is evident across the literature even when comparing phenotypic findings on Tregs in the presence of LMs from the same organ. In fact, the systems with less variability tend to have fewer reported studies, highlighting the need for additional work. At the same time, the morbidity and mortality associated with LMs in many organ systems makes it difficult to perform clinical investigations directly studying the hepatic TME. As demonstrated, peripheral Tregs in the presence of LMs have limited prognostic implications, which logically follows that these findings do not reflect the specific interactions between Tregs, tumour antigens and other immune cells within the hepatic TME that produce the systemic immunological changes associated with LMs.

A general trend in comparing the number and function of Tregs in a primary tumour versus the matched LM is a smaller absolute number of Tregs does not necessarily equate to less immunosuppression. Tregs are evaluated as a ratio to the number of CD8+ effector T cells, as a percentage of CD4+ T cells, or by the change in expression of immunosuppressive markers, which makes comparison across studies difficult. Fewer Treg numbers also do not equate to less immunosuppressive function, and as posited in one BC study where Tregs were found to be decreased in LMs, increased immunosuppression can be mediated by innate immunity rather than Tregs. It is also important to contextualise the primary tumour, where Tregs have been demonstrated to be protective in cancers that arise on a background of chronic inflammation, such as CRC. This illustrates the dual role of Tregs based on stage of disease, that is, may prevent formation of metastases, but once developed, can exacerbate progression and drive resistance to immunotherapy.

As understanding of the functionality of immune checkpoints continues to grow alongside drug development, there may be a role for second generation inhibitors such as with TIGIT. Regardless of target, monotherapy is generally not effective in the presence of LM and combination with Treg depleting CTLA-4 or another novel molecule that stimulates the immune system is likely needed to produce sustained effects (table 2).

Therapeutic strategy targeting liver metastases and Tregs

Immunotherapy targeting Tregs in liver metastases

Across different organ systems, a common theme of Tregs in LMs is their contribution to immunotherapy resistance and the mediation of hyperprogressive disease, where specific subsets of Tregs may expand in response to checkpoint blockade. In a mouse model using breast, colorectal and melanoma cell lines, a subpopulation of CD29+ Tregs was found to double in size in response to anti-PD-1 therapy.77 This population was also unique to the liver, as spleen-derived CD29+ Tregs did not exhibit similar levels of suppression. In a study assessing the efficacy of personalised neoantigen hydrogel vaccines (NPT-gel), investigators found that monotherapy successfully increased CD8+ T cells in implanted breast, colorectal and lung subcutaneous tumours in mice.131 However, in the presence of LMs, intratumoural Tregs were also increased in a negative feedback loop that resulted in immune tolerance, and only in combination treatment with anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapy were LMs able to be cured or reduced (table 3).

Overall, dual blockade with a Treg depleting agent such as anti-CTLA-4 therapy while also restoring Teff activity appears to be key to effective treatment in the presence of LM and to preventing immunotherapy resistance. Several agents have been proposed in the above-mentioned organ-specific sections (including novel molecules and vaccines), but it remains to be seen which can be adapted for clinical use to therapeutic benefit.

Tregs in surgery

Surgical resection of stage IV CRLM with isolated hepatic metastases and acceptable performance status is considered standard of care.132 In fact, resection is central to curative treatment, and with advances in liver-directed therapies that can optimise surgery, resection of non-colorectal LM is also beginning to be performed with evidence of acceptable or similar outcomes.133

Unfortunately, while the surgical eradication of remaining tumour tissue remains central to curative treatment, many patients develop postoperative recurrences. There is some evidence that surgical stress itself may cause changes in the TME that promote engraftment.134 Surgical-stress induced metastasis may therefore play a key role not only in recurrence after mastectomy, but also after the resection of the primary tumour itself. In the multistep metastatic cascade, surgical stress both releases tumour cells into circulation while also priming the host organ for engraftment. Tregs, in particular, have been shown to play a role in this process, though the exact mechanisms are not clear.

As this shift occurs with the resection of LM, alongside improvements in quality of life and survival, understanding the immune regulators in surgical-stress induced metastasis will be crucial for the development of future therapeutics.

Tregs in radiotherapy

RT has been shown to induce immune-mediated responses with several different implications. First, radiation-mediated immunosuppression is well documented, with infiltration of Tregs into the TME or into the peripheral blood after treatment, leading to radioresistance.135 In the metastatic setting, studies vary significantly in protocol and findings, though notably the purpose of variation in ‘sub-ablative’ dosing with fractionated radiation is to identify a regimen that does not stimulate Tregs. Second, the abscopal effect of RT has been an area of active research, where the localised treatment of a primary tumour produces immunogenic effects on metastatic deposits outside the area of treatment, usually when performed in combination with immunotherapy.136 Theoretically, combination therapy can harness the pro-immunogenic effects of RT while targeting Tregs for a synergistic effect, shifting mediators within the TME towards an antitumour environment locally and systemically while targeting radioresistance. Promising results have been demonstrated in preclinical models, where CRC mice bearing LMs were subjected to anti-CLTA-4 and anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibodies and RT to leg tumour sites.137 The volume of synchronous LM was reduced, specifically Treg-depleting CTLA-4 treatment+RT reduced LM volume more than RT+IgG control. TME of synchronous LM was also altered, increasing the proportion of CD8+ T cells while reducing the proportion of Tregs. While this appears promising for eradicating micrometastases in the liver that may lead to recurrence, clinical findings on the effects of combination therapy on Tregs in the TME are variable.138 Further work is needed to characterise the abscopal effect in the presence of LMs as well as its effects on the hepatic TME in clinical trials.

Conclusions

As advancements in cancer research have progressed, immunotherapy has shown great promise for many solid tumours; unfortunately, it has not been shown to be effective in the treatment of hepatic metastases. Tregs are key to our understanding and treatment of LMs, from their initial impact on the development of metastases, contribution to worse OS via reshaping of the TME, and finally, to their role in immunotherapy resistance. From the initial observations on Treg infiltration to more recent investigations of agents that can combine targeting of Treg function with traditional immunotherapy, advancements in our understanding of Tregs offer promising insight into targets for future clinical intervention.
