Compositional Attributes of Blue Lupin (Lupinus angustifolius) Seeds for Selection of High-Protein Cultivars
Authors: Laura Roman, Emmanouil Tsochatzis, Kubra Tarin, Eje M. Röndahl, Carl-Otto Ottosen, Milena Corredig
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Lupin is a promising protein source with a high protein concentration. Breeding efforts have resulted in the development of varieties low in quinolizidine alkaloids. The objective of this work was to evaluate 22 different blue lupin genotypes for a high protein concentration and low content of antinutritional alkaloids. These genotypes were grown under uniform controlled environmental and soil conditions, and the harvested seeds were evaluated for their composition. The low phosphorus content confirmed that the phytic acid presence was low in lupin, especially compared to other legumes. Furthermore, some of the varieties had less than 200 ppm alkaloids. Lupin proteins were rich in leucine and lysine, with the lowest amino acid concentration being methionine. There were significant differences in the protein concentration and recovery. This work demonstrated that an approach for selection of genotypes should be based on not only agronomic yields but also nutritional phenotypes, driving better decision making on future varietal selection.

Introduction

Global protein demand is expected to grow steadily in the upcoming years due to the increasing world population and changes in food production and consumers’ diets. In this scenario, increasing the production of high-quality, functional, and sustainable plant protein alternatives is required to overcome the current animal protein overconsumption, allowing us to keep up with the protein demand while preserving the planet. The transition from animal proteins to more sustainable and cheaper plant-based proteins is full of uncertainties and requires a thorough understanding of their functionality and nutritional properties as well as climate and environmental requirements for optimal growth. An increased production of legumes would be beneficial for alleviating some of the environmental issues,1 by contributing to the increased sustainability of agricultural systems through symbiotic nitrogen fixation in the soil.2 Furthermore, their production results in significantly lower greenhouse gas emissions per gram of protein produced compared to other roots and cereal crops and, especially, ruminant meat.3

Lupin is one of such promising protein-rich legumes with high nutritional value that can be grown under different abiotic conditions, including acidic and sandy soils, helping in soil recovery and erosion control.4−6 Lupin is a viable alternative to other legume crops for the temperate zone, with comparable high protein amounts and amino acid profile compared to soy and a higher dietary fiber content.7 Thus, lupins are non-starch leguminous seeds with high protein (∼30–40%), dietary fiber (∼30–40%), and low fat (∼6%) contents, with its composition depending on their genetic differences and the environment in which they are grown.8,9 Globulins are the main storage proteins in lupin, which together with the albumin fraction are known as conglutins. In general, α-conglutin (11S, legumin) and β-conglutin (7S, vicilin) are the most abundant ones, with γ-conglutin (7S, tetramer) and δ-conglutin (2S, albumin) minorly present.10 Furthermore, lupins are also a good dietary source of minerals, whose composition is dependent on species and cultivars.11,12 Lupins are especially rich in potassium, iron, zinc, and copper, with a low content of phosphorus and, hence, phytic acid, which could negatively affect minerals and protein absorption.12,13 However, lupin species are known to contain other antinutritional factors known as quinolizidine alkaloids (QAs), which are responsible for their bitter taste. QAs are toxic secondary metabolites to animals and humans that the lupin plants produce as a resistance to pathogens and herbivores. Some negative health effects linked to QA consumption may include intoxications with arrhythmia, mydriasis, vomiting, stomach aches, labored breathing, trembling, or convulsions.14 When consumed in high quantities, QAs can even result in the death of the animal.15 For this reason, some European countries, New Zealand and Australia, main producers of lupin worldwide, have established a safe limit for the content of QAs in lupin and lupin-derived products to a maximum of 200 mg kg–1 of product.14,16 Thus, the presence of high amounts of QAs in the seed or flour may prevent the direct consumption of fresh lupins as food.

Two approaches are currently shown to lower the level of QAs, either by postharvest processing (i.e., soaking, blanching, boiling of the seeds) or by genetic selection. Breeding efforts have resulted in the development of varieties with a low alkaloid content, also known as “sweet lupins”. Sweet and bitter varieties are often referred to present less than 500 mg kg–1 and more than 10 000 mg kg–1 total QAs, respectively.17 Despite more than 400 species of lupin (genus: Lupinus) found in nature, domestication of only a few sweet lupin species has occurred. In this sense, varieties low in alkaloids are produced from white lupin (Lupinus albus), narrow-leaved or blue lupin (Lupinus angustifolius), yellow lupin (Lupinus luteus), and pearl or Andean lupin (Lupinus mutabilis) species. According to the European Union plant database,18 most of the commercial varieties in Europe are from L. angustifolius (50), followed by L. albus (30) and L. luteus (15), while L. mutabilis is not yet commercialized in Europe. L. angustifolius is the most cultivated species, and in Europe, it is grown primarily in Eastern Europe and Germany,19 as this species is more frost-resistant and has lower temperature tolerance during the vegetative state.4 Despite the wide adaptation potential and tolerance to abiotic stress of sweet lupin compared to other legumes,5 the production and use of lupins as an alternative protein-rich crop are still limited compared to other legumes (i.e., pea, chickpea, soybean).19 However, by identifying more suitable lupin varieties with increased protein yield and low levels of antinutritional alkaloids, sweet lupin may become a legume of choice in the production of protein-rich crops for human food.

Thus, the objective of this work was to evaluate contrasting blue lupin genotypes for protein yield and low content in antinutritional factors, namely, quinolizidine alkaloids. The 22 commercial lupin varieties and prebreeding lines were grown under the same controlled environmental and soil conditions, and the resulting seeds were evaluated for the seed characteristics and proximate composition, including protein content, amino acid and mineral profile, and content of toxic secondary metabolites. Specifically, the study presents, for the first time, an in-depth evaluation of the compositional attributes, including the protein and antinutritional quinolizidine alkaloids, of 22 different blue lupin genotypes, grown under the same controlled environmental and soil conditions (i.e., temperature, irrigation, CO2, light, etc.). We hypothesized that even if grown under similar conditions, the distinct lupin genotypes will result in seeds with different protein concentrations and compositions and therefore processing functionalities. This work is expected to bring value to new lupin cultivars to expand the available choices of sustainable protein-rich legumes.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals

LC-MS-grade acetonitrile, methanol (≥99.8%), formic acid, and ammonium formate were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Hydrochloric acid 37% (w/w) was obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). The ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ) was obtained from a Milli-Q system (Millipore, Bedford, MA). A mixture of 18 l-amino acids (Amino Acid Standard H) was supplied by Thermo Fisher Scientific (Rockford), containing exact amounts of 18 l-amino acid in a concentration of 2.5 (±0.1) μmol mL–1, except for cystine (1.25 ± 0.1 μmol mL–1). Furthermore, phenol (≥99%), 2-mercaptoethanol (≥99%) l-asparagine (≥99%), l-glutamine (≥98%), l-canavanine (≥98%), and 4-hydroxy proline (≥98%) were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Internal standards (IS) were purchased as a “cell-free” amino acid mix of 20 stable isotope-labeled (15N, 13C) amino acids (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories Inc., Andover, MA), excluding l-canavanine and 4-hydroxy proline. (+)-13α-Hydroxylupanine (≥98%) and (−)-angustifoline and isoangustifoline (≥98%) were purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, Canada); (+)-lupanine hydrochloride (>90%) and (+)-sparteine (>98%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). All other chemicals were at least analytical grade.

Methods

Cultivation and Harvesting of Blue Lupin Seeds

A total of 22 cultivars of blue lupin seeds (L. angustifolius) were used. The varieties, either commercially available or originating from different crossbreeding, were provided by the breeding company Nordic Seed (Odder, Denmark). These 22 seeds were selected based on industry interest for specific growth in the Scandinavian region. Thus, the top breeding lines of Nordic Seed together with commercial varieties with interesting traits were provided by the breeder. They were selected first based on agronomic traits such as branching behavior (mainly upright inflorescence), yield potential, plant disease resistance, and no requirement of short-day flower induction. Regarding the commercial lines, “Rumba”, “Samba”, “Bolero”, “Swing”, and “Szot” varieties are listed to Poznanska Hodowla Roslin in Poland. “Haags blaue”, “Mirabor”, “Probor”, and “Boruta” varieties are listed to Saatzucht Steinach Gmbh in Germany. “Sm Orion” and “Furman” varieties are listed to Hodowla Roslin Smolice in Poland, and “Arabella” is listed to Dr. Hans Rolf Spath in Germany. “Primadonna” was originally created and listed at Eghøjgaard in Denmark and has since January 2023 been moved to Nordic Seed as the maintainer. Most of the breeding lines from Nordic Seed include Primadonna or a Primadonna offspring in the pedigree so that the lines become rather unbranched, better adapted for the short growth season of Scandinavia than branched lines, which present more lateral stems and more irregular maturation of the pods, delaying the harvest time.

The 22 varieties of blue lupins were grown in the greenhouse at Agro Food Park 48, Department of Food Science, under the same environmental conditions of temperature, soil, and irrigation, illuminated by FL300 GROW LED lighting (Senmatic, Søndersø, Denmark). The light level was natural light and light-emitting diode (LED) lamps for 14 h. The light level provided by the LED lamps was 250 μmol m–2 s–1. The protocol used for growing the species was the one used for other similar crops grown in the greenhouse facility. Briefly, 15 plants per lupin cultivar were grown in plastic pots (Teku MXC11, Poppelmann, Lohne, Germany) filled with Pindstrup standard peat mixture substrate no. 2 and irrigated with an ebb/flood irrigation method using an AMI Penta irrigation and fertilizer mixer (Senmatic, Søndersø, Denmark) with the nutrient composition shown in Table S1.

The plants were grown under the same temperature profile, CO2 level, and humidity conditions (Table S2), with an average temperature of 18 °C, 627 ppm CO2 level, and 67% relative humidity. The pods were allowed to dry on the plant, and pods were harvested 4 months after planting. The seeds were manually collected from the pods and cleaned by blowing air to remove husk fragments and other contaminants. After cleaning, the seeds were stored at room temperature in sealed bags until further analysis. The 22 varieties of blue lupin have been randomly named with a number ranging from 1 to 22, as listed in Table 1.

Weight of Seeds and Visual Appearance of the Lupin Seeds

The weight (g) of 100 seeds, manually counted, was determined in triplicate for each of the 22 varieties. Furthermore, seeds from each variety were placed on a grid and photographed with a camera to observe visual differences between them, including the color of the seed coat and seed size.

Preparation of the Lupin Flours

To obtain the lupin flours, needed for compositional analyses and extraction of protein, the seeds were milled using a Retsch ZM 200 ultracentrifugal mill (Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany) equipped with a 0.5 mm sieve size and operating at a speed 12,000 rpm. After milling, lupin flours were stored at −18 °C until further analyses.

Preparation of Protein Isolates (PIs) by Alkaline Extraction–Isoelectric Precipitation

For the preparation of blue lupin protein isolates, a tandem alkaline extraction–isoelectric precipitation method was employed. Briefly, 10 g of lupin flour was suspended in ultrapure water (10% w/v) for 1 h at 65 °C to improve hydration. Afterward, the pH was adjusted to 9.0 by adding 1 N NaOH to solubilize the proteins. The suspension was stirred for 30 min at room temperature and centrifuged at 4700g for 30 min. The supernatant was collected, and the pellet was discarded. Then, the pH of the supernatant was adjusted with 0.1 N HCl to pH 4.5, and the suspension was left at 4 °C overnight to allow the proteins to precipitate. After overnight precipitation, the suspensions were centrifuged at 4700g for 20 min at 4 °C and the precipitate was collected, resuspended in 40 mL of water, homogenized, and adjusted to pH 6.5 using 1 M NaOH. The neutralized protein isolates were frozen at −80 °C for 16 h before freeze drying. Freeze-dried protein isolate powders were vacuum-sealed and stored at −20 °C until further analysis.

The recovery of the protein isolate (PI) from each lupin variety after isoelectric precipitation was calculated in duplicate both as a percentage of weight basis and as a percentage of protein basis using the following equations. Protein was based on crude nitrogen measured by Dumas with no distinction between protein and non-protein nitrogen.

Proximate Composition of the Lupin Flours

Proximate compositional analyses were performed at least in duplicate. All results are expressed in grams per 100 grams, as it is. The moisture content of lupin flours was measured using an automated moisture analyzer (Sartorius MA35, Goettingen, Germany) in accordance with the AACC method 44-15.02.20 The protein content was determined using the combustion method according to the AACC method 46-30.01,20 with an automated Dumatherm nitrogen analyzer (C. Gerhardt GmbH & Co. KG, Königswinter, Germany), using a 6.25 factor to convert the measured nitrogen into protein. Crude fat was determined with an automated SOXTHERM rapid extraction system (C. Gerhardt GmbH & Co. KG, Königswinter, Germany), following the AACC 30-10.01 method20 and using HCl and petroleum ether for fat hydrolysis and extraction, respectively.

The ash content was determined using a thermogravimetric analyzer (TGA, TGA-2 STARe system, Mettler Toledo) equipped with STARe software. 12 mg of samples was loaded in Mettler Toledo aluminum crucibles with punctured lids and heated from ambient temperature to 600 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C min–1 under a high-purity nitrogen environment, followed by an isotherm at 600 °C for 45 min under an air environment. The amount of ash in the samples was determined by the weight difference between the remaining weight at the end of the isotherm at 600 °C and the initial weight of the sample and expressed in percentage (g of ash/100 g of sample). Finally, the amount of carbohydrates was estimated by weight difference (i.e., 100 – protein – lipids – ash – moisture).

Mineral Profile of the Lupin Flours

The mineral profile was evaluated in duplicate on the lupin flours. In brief, 0.4 g of lupin flour was microwave-digested (UltraWAVE, Milestone, Sorisole, Italy) in a closed vessel with 3 mL of 69.5% nitric acid solution and brought to volume with 25 mL of ultrapure water. Quantification of the mineral content was performed using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (Varian 725-ES ICP-OES Radial Spectrometer, Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara) using a pneumatic nebulizer. Minerals analyzed, reported in mg/100 g of lupin flour, were calcium, copper, iron, potassium, magnesium, manganese, sodium, phosphorus, sulfur, and zinc.

Color of the Lupin Flours

The color of the lupin flours was determined in four random points of the powders using a Minolta chromameter CR400/410 (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan). The chromameter was calibrated with a blank (L* = 97.60, a* = −0.00 and b* = 1.57) before the measurements. Results were obtained using a D65 standard illuminator and 2° observer and expressed in the CIE L*a*b* color space.

Total Amino Acid Analysis Using Ultrahigh-Performance Liquid Chromatography Tandem Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS)

Stock standard solutions (2.5 μmol mL–1) for l-asparagine, l-glutamine, l-canvanine, and 4-hydroxy proline were prepared in 0.1 N HCl. These stock solutions were further mixed with “amino acid standard H” for the preparation of the final working standard mixtures. From the latter, appropriate serial dilution from the stock solutions using 0.1 N HCl and sonication at 25 °C for 15 min was further used for the preparation of the calibration curve. A concentration of 0.25 μmol mL–1 was reached for all studied amino acids, except for cystine, which had a concentration of 0.125 μmol mL–1. Stock solutions and working standard mixtures were stored at −18 °C until they were used for further analysis. The internal standard (IS) mix solution of the 15N, 13C-isotope-labeled amino acids prepared at a concentration of 1.0 mg mL–1 using 0.1 N HCl as a solvent was used. The IS was added to calibration standards and samples in the ratio of 1:4 v/v (IS/sample) after acidic hydrolysis. All solutions were kept at −80 °C until use.

Acidic hydrolysis was performed with a slightly modified protocol previously reported,21,22 using vacuum hydrolysis tubes (1 mL, Thermo Scientific, IL). In brief, aliquots (10.0 mg) were weighed in triplicate and mixed with 200 μL of hydrolysis liquid to fully cover the sample. Hydrolysis was performed with 6 N HCl, 1% v/v 2-mercaptoethanol in 0.1 N HCl, and 3% w/v phenol in 0.1 N HCl. Phenol and 2-mercaptoethanol were added as antioxidant agents, to prevent the amino acids’ degradation.21,22 Subsequently, the mixture was snap-frozen using liquid nitrogen, and vacuum was applied to remove the air from the tube. The glass tube was carefully closed and placed in a thermostatic oven set at 110 °C for 20 h. The isotope (15N, 13C)-labeled IS mix consisting of 20 amino acids was added after hydrolysis at the specific ratio mentioned above. After hydrolysis, samples were transferred quantitatively to filter vials for HPLC and evaporated to dryness using a vacuum centrifugal evaporator. The dried sample was reconstituted in 200 μL of 0.1 N HCl, filtered with a 0.22 μm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) filter, and finally injected into the LC system as previously reported. Analysis of amino acids was performed on an Agilent 1290 chromatographic system coupled with an Agilent 6495 TQ/triple quadrupole mass spectrometer system (Agilent, Waldbrohn, Germany) operating under MassHunter 10.0 software (Agilent Technologies, Waldrohn, Germany). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (acetonitrile with 0.1% v/v formic acid) and solvent B (100 mM ammonium formate). Elution was based on a linear gradient program of 12 min from 20% B to 100% B, followed by an isocratic elution of 2 min (100% B) and finally a 1 min equilibration step to the initial conditions (20% solvent B) prior to the next injection. The flow rate was 600 μL min–1, and the total analysis time was 15 min, followed by 1 min equilibration time to the initial chromatographic conditions. Chromatographic separation was performed on an Intrada Amino Acid 150 mm × 3.0 mm, 3 μm column (Imtakt), equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HILIC 5.0 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm Van-Guard precolumn (Waters), thermostated at 35 °C. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) with electron spray ionization (ESI) in positive ionization mode was applied with a capillary voltage of 3 kV. The nebulizer temperature was set at 250 °C, a nitrogen gas flow at 14 L min–1 with a pressure of 35 psi, sheath gas (nitrogen) temperature of 250 °C with a flow of 12 L min–1, and a nozzle voltage of 1500 V. The injection volume was 5 μL. Amino acid individual data for molecular formulas, molar masses, precursor/product ions (m/z) and collision energies for the selected MRM along with their retention times are provided in the Supporting Information (Table S3). The method was based on the use of isotope dilution, where isotope-labeled amino acids (15N, 13C) were used for the quantification of the concentration of the amino acids. Details for the isotope-labeled amino acids used in the study are also provided in the Supporting Information (Table S4). Further information about method optimization and validation, including linearity, limits of detection (LOD), and quantification (LOQ) of the analytes,23 is reported in the Supporting Information (Method S1, Figure S1, and Table S5).

Determination of the Quinolizidine Alkaloid Content Using UHPLC-QTOF-MS

(+)-13α-Hydroxylupanine, (−)-angustifoline and isoangustifoline, (+)-lupanine hydrochloride, and (+)-sparteine stock solution at a concentration of 1 ppm were prepared in methanol. Stock solutions were stored at −18 °C. The working standard solutions were prepared in 80% methanol for calibration and quantification of the studied quinolizidine alkaloids (QAs). QAs were extracted and analyzed following the validated method of Hwang et al.24 with some modifications. Briefly, 125 g of lupin flour was extracted in triplicate with 5 mL of 80% (v/v) methanol solution in a sonication water bath for 60 min. Then, the samples were centrifuged at 3200g for 15 min at 20 °C. Finally, the alkaloid-containing solutions were filtered through 0.22 μm nylon filters and transferred to vials. An UHPLC system (Agilent 1290) coupled to a quadrupole time-of-flight (TOF) mass spectrometer (Agilent 6540 UHD Accurate-Mass, Agilent, Germany) was used. Separation of the QAs was performed using a Luna C18 column (i.d. = 100 × 2.1 mm2 and 1.7 μm particle size; Phenomenex) and 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (solvent B) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL min–1. The sample injection volume was 5 μL, the column oven temperature was 25 °C, and the autosampler temperature was set to 10 °C. The following gradient elution program was used: the run started with 95% solvent A, followed by a solvent A decrease to 50% over 6 min, then to 30% A in 3 min, to 12% A over 3 min, and to 1% A over 1 min. The final step was 95% A for 1 min. The column was re-equilibrated to initial conditions for 5 min (95% solvent A) prior to each injection. Dual AJS ESI operating in positive ionization mode with a 4 kV capillary voltage was used. The source was kept at 325 °C, with nitrogen for drying (35 psi) and nebulizing gas (flow rate of 12 L min–1). Drying gas and sheath gas temperatures were 300 and 325 °C, respectively. The fragmentor, skimmer, and octapole 1 RF Vpp were set at 170, 65, and 750 V, respectively. The acquisition mode of the TOF-MS was full scan MS set to acquire data over an m/z range of 40–1500 Da at a rate of 3 spectra s–1. The quantification of the selected QAs was based on extracted ion chromatograms (EIC) of the most abundant ions (m/z). The latter were selected using proper QA analytical standards. For all QAs, the protonated form ([M + H]+) was the most abundant and was selected for quantification (Table S6). Further information on the QA identification and method validation is reported in the Supporting Information (Method S2 and Table S6).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical differences between the parameters for the 22 blue lupin varieties were studied by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with Tukey HSD used to describe means with 95% confidence intervals using Statgraphics Centurion XVI software (Statpoint Technologies, Inc., The Plains, VA). The coefficient of variation among duplicate or triplicate measurements was generally kept below 12%.

Results and Discussion

Seed Size, Color, and Proximate Composition of Lupin Seeds

The weight of the seeds and the proximate composition of the 22 lupin varieties grown under the same environmental conditions are reported in Table 2. The smallest seeds, based on the weight of 100 seeds, were observed for three varieties, Sm Orion and three prebreeding lines (9, 10, and 21), with weights between 5.2 and 5.7 g. Conversely, variety 14 (Mirabor) was the largest, with nearly double the weight of the seeds (9.59 g), followed by varieties 8 and 13 (Primadonna and prebreeding 14-13, respectively). Figure 1 shows the differences in visual appearance of the 22 varieties used, where the differences in the seeds’ color can also be noticed. Although some of the varieties (10 and 21) had the lowest seed weight, no clear trend was observed between commercial and prebreeding lines, as other prebreeding lines (1, 2, 6, and 13) presented bigger seeds. In another study with blue lupin cultivars grown in Mexico, three of the commercial varieties reported here were studied,25 finding similar results for the weight of the seeds, with Haags Blaue having bigger seeds than those of Probor and Boruta.

The color parameters for lightness (L*), from black to white; green (−) to red (+) (a*); and blue (−) to yellow (+) (b*) of the flours obtained from the different lupin cultivars were also analyzed and are reported in Table 2. The color of the flours was related to the color of the pigmented coats (Figure 1), with L* ranging from 79.5 (variety 15, Bolero) to 88.5 (variety 12, Samba). Lower L* values denoted darker flours for varieties 4, 11, 15, 19, and 22, corresponding to the seeds with the darkest (black colored) coats, followed by varieties 1, 9, 20, and 21, i.e., which were characterized by brown seed coats. It was also observed that the flours had a greenish (−a*) and yellowish (+b) color, ranging from −4.1 (variety 15, Bolero) and −0.5 (variety 1, 18-25) for a* and from 26.2 (variety 19, Swing) to 35.6 (variety 16, Probor) for b*. Despite the large differences in the color of the flours, there were no significant relationships found between color parameters and the proximate composition of the seeds (see Section 3.5).

Composition (%, w/w, as is), moisture, protein, fat, ash, and carbohydrates of the 22 lupin cultivars are listed in Table 2. The moisture of the milled lupin seeds ranged from 6.2 to 7.7% (w/w), with no significant differences between most of the cultivars. Only significant differences were observed for this parameter for Probor (variety 16) with some of the other genotypes. Moisture results are in line with the fact that all the seeds were allowed to dry in the pods under the same environmental conditions before harvest. However, the crude protein concentration differed substantially between samples, ranging from 21.2% (w/w) for variety 1 (prebreeding line 18-25) to 27.6% (w/w) for variety 7 (prebreeding line 18-14). The lowest protein content (with no significant differences between them) was found for varieties 1, 6, and 9, while two crossbred varieties denoted the highest values for protein content (varieties 7 and 13). Among the commercial cultivars, the ones that exhibited a significantly lower protein content were Sm Orion (variety 9), Probor (variety 16), and Boruta (variety 18). Overall, the protein content of the blue lupin cultivars was within the range reported by other research studies, in which whole seeds were analyzed.26,27 Of note is that the seed coat in lupins represents ∼25% of the seed weight, which is low in protein compared to the cotyledon tissue,12 and thus, when studying compositional attributes of decorticated seeds, higher protein values are reported in L. angustifolius (up to 40%).28,29

Small differences between the lipid and ash contents of the lupin cultivars were detected. For the total fat content, it ranged between 5.2% (variety 12, Samba) and 7.6% (variety 17, prebreeding line 18-23), while the total ash content varied from 4.6% (variety 9, Sm Orion) to 6.8% (variety 10, prebreeding line 103). The values reported in the present study agreed with literature reports, which stated a lipid content for seeds from L. angustifolius of 5–8% and ash content of 4–6%.12,26,27,29

Finally, total carbohydrates were inversely correlated to the protein content in the seeds and ranged from 53.1% (variety 7) to 60.7% (variety 9), as observed in the literature.25 Carbohydrates in lupin seeds are mostly composed of nonstarch polysaccharides (mostly insoluble dietary fiber, IDF) as well as galacto-oligosaccharides, which account for the soluble dietary fiber fraction.29,12 Dehulling of lupin seeds would lead to a decrease in the amount of IDF as well as to an increase in protein content.28 A higher concentration of carbohydrate and ash in the seed may have an impact on the processability and overall quality of the lupin flour. In this study, all plants were grown under the same environmental and nutrient conditions. However, further work is needed to evaluate how growing conditions may further affect the ratio of these components in the seed.

Mineral Profile of Lupin Seeds

Lupins, similar to other legumes, are a good source of minerals, with their content varying among species.30 The mineral levels have been reported before for L. angustifolius.12,13,29 As suggested by the value of ash in Table 2, although all of the plants were subjected to the same growing and nutrient/irrigation conditions, significant differences were observed for most of the mineral elements in the ground lupin flour (Table 3). Lupin flour was especially rich in K+ (1015–1302 mg/100 g), followed by phosphorus ions (Pi) (500–553 mg/100 g) and Mg2+ (209–261 mg/100 g). The amount of Ca2+ varied between 170 and 494 mg/100 g, while the sulfur ion concentration varied from 179 to 346 g/100 g, showing significant differences among varieties. Interestingly, variety 10 showed the highest Ca2+ (494 mg/100 g) and sulfur contents (346 mg/100 g) among all varieties. Despite the differences in the sulfur content, no statistical correlations were observed between sulfur ions and the presence of sulfur-containing amino acids or nitrogen content. Plants take up sulfur from the soil as sulfate, which is further reduced to sulfite and incorporated into cysteine and then methionine.31 However, if sulfur is in abundant supply in the soil, it accumulates in the seeds, mainly in the oxidized form, rather than the reduced form needed for amino acid synthesis.31 Other than amino acids, the sulfur content may also reflect the presence of other metabolites as sulfur-containing vitamins and cofactors. Smaller amounts of Na+ (<100 mg/100 g) were observed, with significantly different values between varieties, ranging from 8 mg/100 g (variety 17, crossbred 18-23) to 33 mg/100 g (variety 19, Swing). Similarly, Mazumder et al.29 found in other L. angustifolius cultivars that K+ is the most abundant ion, followed by Pi, Mg2+, and Ca2+. No differences in the phosphorus content were found between varieties, while K+ and Mg2+ contents denoted small but significant differences between varieties. Potassium ion concentration showed a positive correlation with both the seed weight (r = 0.56, p < 0.007) and especially the protein content in the seed (r = 0.70, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the presence of P is indicative of the presence of phytic acid, which is the primary source of phosphate storage in cereals and legumes. The phosphorus concentration was low (500–553 mg/100 g) and not different among varieties. Thus, the low P content would confirm that the phytic acid presence in lupin is low and would agree with other studies reporting low values in lupin in comparison with other legumes.32,33

Total Alkaloid Content and Composition

As alkaloid concentrations are critical to the flour nutritional value, the total content in QAs as well as the amount of each individual alkaloid was measured (Figure 2). Total alkaloids (Figure 2A) ranged between 50 and 494 mg kg–1 (for varieties 9 and 17, respectively). These low concentrations were expected since all the studied varieties were sweet lupin varieties. Some varieties were especially low in alkaloids with less than 200 ppm total QA content (i.e., varieties 1, 3, 9, 11, 14, and 20) and then more adequate from a nutritional and safety standpoint. This concentration is considered the safety threshold for human consumption.14 Therefore, these varieties would not need further postprocessing treatment to remove the alkaloid content before being deemed safe for consumption. It is important to note that although other varieties contained >200 ppm alkaloids, their QA content will be significantly reduced during processing of the flour or isolation of the protein.35 The three principal alkaloids detected were 13-α-hydroxylupanine, followed by lupanine and angustifoline (Figure 2B), while sparteine and α-isolupanine were not detected or were under the limit of detection. It should be noted that sparteine is the most toxic alkaloid, which is present in L. luteus and L. mutabilis, compared to lupanine alkaloid, present in L. albus and L. angustifolius.14 Similar to other varieties of L. angustifolius, 13-α-hydroxylupanine and lupanine were the most abundant alkaloids, followed by a lower presence of angustifoline.14,24,36,37

Protein Extraction Recovery and Purity

To evaluate the ease of extraction of lupin protein isolates, all flours were subjected to a standard alkaline extraction procedure, followed by isoelectric precipitation. As expected, the isolates obtained showed a protein concentration (g/100 g, wb) ranging from 70.4% (variety 1, prebreeding line 18-25) to 92.1% (variety 7, prebreeding line 18-14), based on the total nitrogen measured in Dumas. The difference in protein purity may be linked to the presence of ions, carbohydrates, phytates, or other small-molecular weight contaminants interacting with the protein after isoelectric precipitation.38 Lupin mainly contains α-conglutin (11S) and β-conglutin (7S) globulins, which will precipitate by isoelectric precipitation with 2S sulfur-rich albumins remaining in the soluble phase. Therefore, differences in the recovery of the protein should indicate differences in the ratio between globulins and other nitrogen components. For the protein isolates, the recovery was reported as a percentage based on the total seed weight or total seed protein content, as shown in Figure 3. The results showed that varieties 10 and 18 (103 and Boruta) showed the lowest yield of extraction based on solids recovered (i.e., weight of protein isolate/weight of flour used for extraction), while varieties 3 and 14 (Rumba and Mirabor) exhibited the highest recovery values. Similarly, varieties 10 and 18 gave rise to the lowest recovery of protein (as % of protein basis) in the isolates (∼30%), while the highest values were found for varieties 6, 14, and 16 (∼50%).

Recovery percentage of protein after the isoelectric precipitation, based on the weight of protein isolate recovered to the total weight of the flour used for extraction (weight basis, black bars) or corrected to the protein concentration in the protein isolate to the initial protein concentration in the lupin flour (protein basis, gray bars).

These differences in the recovery of the proteins suggest important differences in processing and functional properties between the varieties. Indeed, high protein recovery yields based on total protein, after isoelectric precipitation, would reflect a high ratio in globulins/albumins. Interestingly, the results found for the recovery of protein during isoelectric precipitation were strongly correlated to compositional and size attributes of the seeds (p < 0.01). As one would expect, the yield of the protein isolates (%, weight basis) was positively correlated to the protein content of the seeds (r = 0.62, p < 0.002) and negatively correlated to the total carbohydrate content (r = −0.54, p < 0.009). Furthermore, there was also a positive correlation (r = 0.62, p < 0.002) between the seed weight and the protein isolate yield (%, weight basis). This correlation could be related to the fact that bigger seeds have a lower seed coat-to-cotyledon ratio. In this regard, it is known that protein is preferentially found in the cotyledon of the legumes, while the protein content in the seed coat is lower and presents a higher presence of carbohydrates.31,39 Therefore, bigger seeds seem to be preferential to obtain higher recoveries of the isoelectric precipitated protein isolates, probably due to better protein extraction from the cotyledon tissues. In addition, a higher weight of the seeds was also positively related to the recovery on the protein basis (r = 0.57, p < 0.006). However, the recovery of protein (calculated on the protein basis) in the extracted isolates did not show direct correlations with the total protein content, again demonstrating the presence of important differences in the type of proteins and their proportions among the varieties and, in particular, the proportion of albumins and globulins or the differences in 11S and 7S conglutin fractions.10,40 It is important to mention that while the isoelectric point of α- (11S), β- (7S), and δ- (2S) conglutins is of acidic nature, the tetrameric γ-conglutin (7S) is of basic nature (pH ∼ 7)10 and would not be recovered during isoelectric precipitation. Furthermore, differences in the colloidal state and aggregation of the proteins during extraction and interactions with other components should also affect the protein recovery in the isolates. Interestingly, correlations were also found between the amount of K+ and Ca2+ (first and third most abundant minerals) in the seeds and the recovery of the protein isolates, in terms of both weight and protein basis. While a higher amount of K+ seemed to increase the recovery of the protein isolates (r = 0.72, p < 0.001; r = 0.52, p < 0.01), a higher amount of Ca2+ decreased it (r = 0.67, p < 0.001; r = 0.47, p < 0.03). This suggests a clear effect of the cations in protein solubilization and aggregation,41 with potassium and calcium ions having antagonistic effects in promoting the protein extraction during the alkaline solubilization and isoelectric precipitation procedure. However, these correlations need to be addressed carefully and would need to be better understood, as it is not known which ion species are involved and how these are interacting with proteins or which proteins are better associated with the ions.

To conclude, based on the combined results of seed weight, protein recoveries, alkaloid content, and protein purity in the lupin flours, more suitable cultivars for protein production can be selected (Figure 4). From a breeder perspective (Figure 4A), we can observe that varieties 3 and 14 would result in bigger seeds (>8 g) with a higher protein amount (>25 g/100 g), while keeping low levels of alkaloids (<200 ppm). However, some other varieties, such as 10 and 21, would be less adequate for their cultivation under the growing conditions selected in this study based on their small seed size (<6 g), lower protein (<23 g/100 g), and high alkaloid level (>200 ppm). For extraction of protein isolates using isoelectric precipitation, we can also see that differences are observed based on their recovery. In this regard, it can be observed that for protein recovery, based on total solids (Figure 4B), similar to seed weight, varieties 3, 11, and 14 (Rumba, Furman, and Mirabor) seem to be the most adequate ones to produce protein-rich ingredients with a low alkaloid content (<200 ppm) as well as high protein recovery (>13%). The least desired varieties would be again those with high alkaloids, low protein, and low seed weight, namely 10 and 18 (prebreeding line 103 and Boruta with <10% solids recovered), followed by 21 (crossbred 301-7). The extraction recovery measured as protein to total initial protein also denoted similar results (Figure 4C), 3 and 14 were the best varieties (47–49% protein recovery), followed by 11, while 10, and 18 were the least desired varieties (32−33% protein recovery). However, other varieties such as 2, 4, 8, and 17 (14-10, Haags Blaue, Primadonna, and 18-23), although they possessed a higher protein concentration in the seeds (24–26%) and protein recovery (43–46%), would be less adequate because of their high content in alkaloids (>400 ppm). In Figure 4B,C, we can also observe that despite having lupin varieties with similar protein contents, some varieties present a higher amount of precipitable protein, which may indicate differences in the protein fractions, colloidal state of the protein in the flour, and interactions with other macro- and microcomponents. Similarly, some varieties with an initially lower protein content, such as varieties 6 and 16, presented some of the highest recoveries in protein purity.

In this work, the chemical composition and hence the nutritional quality of lupin seeds from L. angustifolius differed significantly between cultivars grown under the same abiotic conditions. Although no significant differences were found for amino acid composition, significant differences were found regarding the protein content between cultivars, affecting the recovery and purity of the protein isolates. Thus, the results of this research are crucial for selecting lupin genotypes with a high protein value and low toxicity that can be safely introduced into the market to increase the choices for sustainable plant proteins. Further studies are needed to understand how these lupin genotypes can adapt to different abiotic conditions, such as heat, elevated CO2 conditions, and water stress during plant growing. This will allow us to understand which factors control the alkaloid content in lupin as well as identify mechanisms to reduce alkaloids or increase the protein content in lupins to select more robust cultivars that can withstand diverse environmental conditions.
