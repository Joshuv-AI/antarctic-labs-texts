The association between parity, age of first full term pregnancy and invasive lobular breast cancer: a case-only analysis
Authors: Karen Van Baelen, Ha Linh Nguyen, François Richard, Maja Vangoitsenhoven, Giuseppe Floris, Hans Wildiers, Ann Smeets, Ines Nevelsteen, Frederic Amant, Christine Desmedt, Patrick Neven
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Higher parity is associated with an increased prevalence of ILC.•In premenopause, high parity and age 1st FTP are associated with an increase of ILC.•Parity is not associated with the clinicopathological features of ILC.

Introduction

Since the 70s, an ongoing trend is observed to postpone the 1st full-term pregnancy (FTP) [1,2]. In general, women nowadays experience fewer FTPs as compared to a century ago [3]. While it is well known that reproductive factors impact breast cancer (BC) risk, the registration of these reproductive factors is often lacking and often not considered in large series studying prognostic or predictive variables of BC [4].

High parity is often seen as a risk reducing factor of BC risk and development, while occurrence of the 1st FTP at ≥30 yearshas been described as a potential risk factor [5]. The incidence rate for BC as compared to uniparous women, decreased from 0.91 for women with 2 FTPs to 0.50 for women with 5 FTPs [5]. A transient increase of BC risk in the years after pregnancy did only seem to occur after 1st and 2nd FTP and not from 3th FTP onward5. A childbirth before the age of 25 seemed to prevent the transient increase of BC risk in the postpartum years. When compared to nulliparous women, women with 1st FTP at an age <25 years had a lower risk of developing BC, while women with their 1st FTP at >25 years had an increased risk for 10–30 years after delivery [5,6].

Specifically considering development of estrogen receptor positive (ER+) BC, parous women seem to have a lower risk than nulliparous women [7]. There seems to be an agreement, at least in the Western world, that ER+/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 negative (HER2-), is less common in women with a 1st FTP at <25 years [[8], [9], [10]]. Therefore, one might hypothesize that a young age at 1st FTP protects against ER+/HER2- BC or at least delays the onset of it [9].

Besides the impact of reproductive factors on BC risk, studies have also demonstrated poorer prognostic features in multiparous women with BC, with increased tumour size and more axillary involvement leading to a poorer outcome [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. It is unsure if this is caused by a direct effect of parity on the tumour characteristics or by the impact of multiparity on other lifestyle factors like for example body mass index (BMI) which is also associated with poorer tumour characteristics, even in invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) [15]. More recently, it was found that women with 5 or more deliveries had a worse prognosis, especially in case of luminal B-like BC [16]. The hypothesis is that these more aggressive and proliferative luminal B-like BC are biologically affected by high parity. Hormonal changes in the mammary microenvironment induced by multiple pregnancies have been suggested to potentially stimulate the growth of ER-expressing luminal B-like BC [16,17]. It has also been speculated that the growth and progression of luminal B-like BC depends on other pathways like the epidermal growth factor receptor pathway and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Both pathways might be influenced by high parity [16,18].

If parous, young age at motherhood and a recent (<5 years) FTP seem to be associated with a poorer outcome of BC as well [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Some argue that an early 1st FTP mainly prevents BC types associated with good prognosis [23], and higher proportions of triple negative have been described in parous women with an earlier 1st FTP [24]. Others even explain an improved BC outcome (especially in highly educated women) by a trend to postpone pregnancies to a later age [25]. Moreover, it is hypothesized that there is a direct biological effect of a recent pregnancy on the metastatic spread of BC cells [[26], [27], [28]]. It is however still unsure how other reproductivity and lifestyle factors impacts the association between age 1st FTP and prognosis of BC.

ILC affects approximately 15% of all patients with BC [29]. While ILC is mainly characterized by a different histological growth pattern due to the lack of functioning E-cadherin cell-adhesion proteins [30], the differences with invasive BC of no special type (IBC—NST) are much more profound and present on a biological, histological and clinical level [31].

Over 90% of all ILC express ER and/or progesterone receptor (PR) and lack HER2 overexpression or amplification, making them luminal A or B-like [31]. Therefore, one might suspect that the impact of reproductive factors on the development of ILC is comparable to that of luminal IBC—NST. While, it is known that ILC is more common in postmenopausal women than in premenopausal women and in women with a history of menopausal hormonal treatment (MHT) [32], data on the impact of reproductive factors on ILC risk and development is much scarcer.

A population-based control-study, described an 2.4 fold increased risk of ILC when age 1st FTP ≥30 years vs. <20 years. In patients with IBC—NST, this association was less pronounced [33]. These findings were in concordance with findings from the Nurses’ Health Study, where the highest risk for developing ILC was seen with an age 1st FTP between 30 and 34 years as compared to <25, while no significant difference was seen for IBC—NST [34]. In this study, they could not find a significant association between parity and ILC [34]. They concluded that the protective effect of parity is more pronounced for IBC—NST than ILC. To our knowledge, the association between reproductive factors and clinicopathological features of ILC has not been studied before.

Since literature is limited, it remains therefore unsure if ILC development is impacted by parity and age 1st FTP in a similar manner as BC in general and if the prevalence of ILC in a BC cohort is affected by reproductive factors. Little is known about the association of other prognostic factors with reproductive factors in ILC. Within this retrospective analysis, the aim was to investigate whether there is an association between parity and age at 1st FTP and: 1) the prevalence of pure ILC (i.e., not mixed with other histological subtypes) in an ER+/HER2- BC cohort (overall and according to the age at BC diagnosis), 2) standard clinical and pathological features of pure ER+/HER2- ILC.

Patients and methods

Patients

A single center retrospective study was set up at University Hospitals Leuven. After obtaining approval by the ethics committee (S66055), a cohort was assembled of all female patients (n = 7360), diagnosed between January 2000 and December 2020 with stage I-III ER+/HER2- BC. Patient and tumour characteristics were extracted from the medical records: year of birth, age at diagnosis, BMI at diagnosis, menopausal state, gravidity and parity, age at 1st FTP, tumour size, pTN-classification, histological grade, progesterone receptor (PR) status (Supplementary Table 1).

A case-only analysis was performed. To define the proportion of pure ILC in this cohort, it was compared to a reference group that included all other histological subtypes besides pure ILC and consisted mainly of IBC—NST but also included mixed subtypes (i.e., mixed IBC—NST/ILC). The definitions for other histological subtypes including the mixed subtypes has changed over the period of inclusion [35,36], making it impossible to only use patients diagnosed with IBC—NST as a control group. Pure ILC was defined as those cases for which the pathology report did not mention any presence of other histological subtypes, irrespective of the relative proportion of each subtype. Current institutional standards include E-cadherin immunohistochemistry to confirm all cases of ILC. However, this was not the case yet at the beginning of the inclusion period. Following age groups were defined for age at diagnosis: <30 years, 31 – 40 years, 41 – 50 years, 51 – 60 years, 61 – 70 years, >70 years. Parity was both categorized as ‘yes’ versus ‘no’ as well as ‘nulliparous’, ‘1 child’, ‘2 children’ and ‘>2 children’. Age at 1st FTP was considered as a continuous variable as well as a categorical variable (age groups: <21 years, 21 – 25 years, 26 – 30 years and >30 years). BMI at diagnosis was categorized according to the WHO classifications (≤18.5 kg/m2, 18.5 – 24.9 kg/m2, 25.0 – 29.9 kg/m2, and ≥ 30.0 kg/m2).

Historically available immunohistochemistry (IHC) scores were used to define PR status. The Allred score [37] was used to determine PR positivity for patients diagnosed from 2004 onward. A score of 0 – 2 was interpreted as negative, a score of 5 – 8 was interpreted as positive and a score of 3 – 4 as unknown if not otherwise specified. In accordance with ASCO/CAP guidelines the cut-off for PR positivity was set at ≥ 1% of positive tumour cells. For patients diagnosed between 2000 and 2003 the H-score [38] was used in cases were no Allred score was reported. A H-score of ≥1 was considered to be positive.

The patients with pure ILC were further analyzed to define whether parity and age at 1st FTP were associated with the clinico-pathological features of pure ER+/HER2- ILC.

Statistical analyses

The association between the prevalence of pure ILC, as the dependent variable, and parity (yes vs. no) as the primary independent variable of interest was investigated using univariable and multivariable Firth’s logistic regression. Within the parous group of patients, the following independent variables were collectively analyzed in the same multivariable regression models: parity number; age 1st FTP (continuous and per age group); and interval 1st FTP – diagnosis (time in years between moment of 1st full term pregnancy and moment of BC diagnosis; continuous). Additionally, the following variables were included as covariates: age at diagnosis and BMI. Moreover, year of birth was considered as a covariate to correct trends in the general population over time regarding age 1st FTP and parity number. Sub-analyses were performed for the different age groups at diagnosis as described above. Secondly, we combined number of parity and age of 1st FTP in the same 5 groups as for the descriptive analyses. Using these groups, univariable and multivariable logistic regression was performed separately for premenopausal and postmenopausal women. Age at diagnosis, BMI and year of birth were included as covariates in the multivariable regression models.

Only patients with pure ER+/HER2- ILC were considered for further analyses to define whether parity and age at 1st FTP were associated with other clinico-pathological features. Similar data was not available for patients in the reference group. The following standard clinicopathological variables, besides parity, were defined to be included in the association analyses: age, BMI, grade, tumour size, nodal involvement and PR expression. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses with parity ‘yes’ versus ‘no’ and ‘1 child’ versus ≥2 children as dependent variables were performed. Sub-analyses were performed for the different age groups at diagnosis.

For all regression analyses, only cases with complete data for all considered variables were included in the models. Model integrity was verified through several diagnostic checks. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), with all primary independent variables, i.e. parity and age at 1st FTP, showing VIF < 2.0. Age at diagnosis and year of birth showed moderate to high collinearity, as expected. Since these were included as adjustment covariates and did not affect the stability of estimates for our primary independent variables, we retained both in the models. Linearity in the logit for continuous variables, namely age at diagnosis and interval 1st FTP – diagnosis, was evaluated by comparing generalized additive models with and without restricted cubic splines using Akaike information criterion (AIC) and likelihood-ratio tests. Although non-linearity was indicated, it was mainly limited to the extremes of the distribution where data points were relatively sparse, while the overall direction of effect remained consistent across the observed range for both variables. Furthermore, the inclusion of spline terms did not alter the primary associations. Consequently, we only report results from the linear models to facilitate clinical interpretation. Model goodness-of-fit was verified using Firth’s logistic regression with intercept correction (FLIC). The penalized likelihood ratio test confirmed that the fitted models performed significantly better than the null model (p< 0.05).

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.1. All statistical tests were considered statistically significant when p-value <0.05 as a standard evidence criterion.

Results

Patient characteristics

In total, 7360 patients with ER+/HER2- BC were identified, of which 1121 (15.2%) were diagnosed with pure ILC (Fig. 1). Median age at diagnosis of the group with pure ILC was 61 years, while this was 59 years for the reference group. Distribution across age groups differed significantly between the 2 groups(p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in number of parity (p = 0.082). Patients in both groups had a median of 2 FTPs in their medical history. Of all patients with pure ILC, 85.8% were parous in comparison to 83.9% parous women in the reference group. High parity (>2 FTPs) was numerically more frequently seen in the pure ILC group compared to the reference group (27.3% and 24.1% respectively). For both groups, the median age at 1st FTP of parous women was 25 years, no significant difference was observed in distribution across different age at 1st FTP groups (p = 0.151). The proportion of parous patients with pure ILC that had an age >30 years at 1st FTP was 16.1% compared to 13.4% in the reference group. No significant association was seen between parity of 2 vs. 1 and pure ILC prevalence (OR 1.11, 95%CI 0.93–1.32, p-value 0.241). Parity of >2 FTPs was associated with pure ILC (OR 1.28, 95%CI 1.06–1.55, p-value 0.011) as demonstrated in Fig. 2B. This association was mostly driven by the age group of patients diagnosed between 51 and 60 years of age (OR 1.60, 95%CI 1.12–2.28, p-value 0.009). This age group represents 27.7% (311/1121) and 27.3% (1701/6239) of the ILC population of the reference group, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3). Trends, although not reaching significance, were also seen at the age groups 41–50 years (OR 1.47, 95%CI 0.95–2.28, p-value 0.084) and 61–70 years (OR 1.33, 95%CI 0.93–1.90, p-value 0.120). In patients diagnosed at >70 years of age, no difference was seen (OR 0.97, 95%CI 0.64–1.48, p-value 0.884).

In the overall population, no evident association was seen between the prevalence of pure ILC and the different categories of age at 1st FTP in the multivariable analysis (Fig. 3). To address potential shifts in ILC histologic definitions over time, we performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to the sub-cohort diagnosed after 2012 (N= 2722), corresponding to the publication of the WHO 4th Edition classification of breast tumours [39]. In this subset, while the prevalence of ILC stayed the same (428/2722, 15.7%), parity-related associations were attenuated. This is likely attributable to the reduced sample size and shifts in several reproductive factors in the study population. Specifically, compared to the sub-cohort diagnosed in 2012 and earlier, we observed a trend toward a reduction in high parity (> 2 children: 25.5% vs 22.9%, p= 0.053) and later age 1st FTP (age 1st FTP >30: 12.7% vs. 15.7%, p= 0.002) in the post-2012 sub-cohort. The point estimates for non-reproductive variables (e.g., age at diagnosis and BMI), however, remained stable, albeit with wider confidence intervals most likely due to reduced sample size. We speculate that the effect of reproductive risk factors might be obscured in cohorts characterized by lower parity and delayed pregnancy.

Secondly, premenopausal women were categorized by parity and age 1st FTP and compared to nulliparous women. Both the univariable and multivariable regression models demonstrated that women who had ≥2 children and age 1st FTP ≥26 years had significantly higher odds (univariable: OR 1.70 95%CI 1.19–2.46, p-value 0.003; multivariable: OR 1.68 95%CI 1.17–2.46, p-value 0.005), of being diagnosed with ILC in comparison to patients that were nulliparous (Fig. 3). In postmenopausal women, no significant differences were seen in prevalence of ILC between the different groups defined by parity and age 1st FTP and nulliparous women (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Association of parity with clinicopathological features of pure ER+/HER2- ILC

The clinicopathological features of pure ILC did not differ significantly in the parous vs. nulliparous patients in both univariable and multivariable analyses (Fig. 4A). A trend towards a slightly higher although non-significant association of nulliparity with PR expression (OR 0.65, 95%CI 0.32–1.18, p-value 0.166) was seen in multivariable analysis. Subgroup analyses by age at diagnosis category (Supplementary Fig. 4) did not reveal any significant associations between parity and PR expression of the tumour. Regarding tumour grade, a significant association was found between parity and histological grade 3 vs. grade 1 in the age group diagnosed between 61 and 70 years of age (OR 18.13, 95%CI 1.58–298.68, p-value 0.021). In this age group, a trend was seen for histological grade 2 vs. grade 1 tumours as well (OR 7.63, 95%CI 1.02–58.34, p-value 0.048). When comparing uniparous with multiparous patients with pure ER+/HER2- ILC, again no apparent associations with clinicopathological features were observed (Fig. 4B). Subgroup analyses of the different age categories at diagnosis could not reveal any significant association between parity number and clinicopathological features of patients with pure ER+/HER2- ILC (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Discussion

In general, it is believed that high parity, alongside an early 1st FTP and breast feeding, decreases both the risk of developing hormone sensitive BC [5,7] and seems to postpone its onset [9]. Assessment of epidemiological risk is outside the scope of these analyses, as the study design precludes this evaluation. However, in our BC cohort, patients with high parity have a higher prevalence of pure ILC. One might therefore hypothesize that the protective effect of high parity is less pronounced for the risk of developing pure ILC as compared to IBC—NST.

In previous studies, ILC was often associated with a higher age at diagnosis and with a postmenopausal status of the patient at diagnosis [40,41]. Our data point toward the possibility of a subgroup of premenopausal women with BC (those with higher parity and later 1st FTP) who could have higher proportions of ILC. These are results of more exploratory subgroup analyses without multiplicity correction. Since data of age at last childbirth was not available, we could also not consider the effect of the interval between the last pregnancy and the BC diagnosis. Therefore, we cannot exclude that these patients might still be experiencing a transient increased risk of developing ILC after their most recent pregnancy [5,42]. Pregnancy hormones have been described to have a transient promoting effect on the growth hormone sensitive tumours and thus this might bring forward the diagnosis in individuals whose first pregnancy occurs later in life [42].

In our cohort, no significant difference in frequency of pure ILC was seen for parity versus nulliparity in multivariable analysis. In this model, age at 1st FTP could not be considered as an adjustment factor since data would be missing for all nulliparous patients. Thus, additional regression models were performed to compare the prevalence of pure ILC in nulliparous women with several groups defined by parity and age 1st FTP in premenopausal and postmenopausal women.

Nulliparity seems more strongly associated with an increased risk of ILC than IBC—NST in multivariable analyses adjusted for several socio-economic and reproductive factors [33]. There was a 2.4-fold increase in risk of developing ILC with an age at 1st FTP ≥30 years in comparison <20 years in parous women. Our results only demonstrated a significant association between age at 1st FTP >30 and the prevalence of pure ILC in a BC cohort in univariable analyses.

A few studies confirmed stimulation of growth of ILC cells in women with an older age at 1st FTP with a larger effect than in other histological subtypes [33,43]. Another study suggested that an increased interval between menarche and age 1st FTP increases the risk of developing ILC [44]. Others have reported no difference in the association between age at 1st FTP and IBC—NST vs. ILC [[45], [46], [47]]. Previous publications have suggested a role for the time interval between different pregnancies in multiparous women. A larger time interval between pregnancies might increase the risk of ILC, whereas a short interval between births seems to enhance the risk of IBC—NST [48,49]. In large Finnish population studies including women with at least 5 children [16], it was described that when the interval between the 1st and 5th birth was 2–2.99 years the OR for ILC was 2.21 which increased to an OR of 2.98 when the interval was >3 years. Interval between pregnancies as well as the timing of the last pregnancy were not available in our dataset.

With regard to the clinicopathological features, nulliparous patients in our cohort did have a slight, although non-significant trend to more frequently have pure PR+ ILC, especially in patients that were >50 years old at time of diagnosis. No significant associations were seen between parity and the clinicopathological features found at the time of diagnosis in patients with pure ILC. To our knowledge, no other data is available upon the association of reproductive factors and clinicopathological features of ILC. However, conflicting results have been reported for BC in general. While some studies link high parity with poorer clinicopathological features like larger tumour size and more nodal involvement [[11], [12], [13], [14]], others have described a negative correlation between parity and nodal involvement and no correlation with tumour size and grade [50]. The relationship between parity and PR expression is less well studied although an older analysis indicates that tumours of nulliparous premenopausal women have higher expression levels of PR in comparison to those of parous premenopausal women and postmenopausal women [51].

Several underlying biological differences between ILC and IBC—NST have been summarized in a recent review by Flaherty et al., explaining the differences in hormonal risk factors [52]. Preclinical evidence exists that exposing ER to 17ß-estradiol leads to unique gene expression signatures that are only observed in ILC cell lines and not in other BC cell lines [53]. Furthermore, when exposed to 17ß-estradiol, the half-life of ER increased in ILC cell lines, whereas it decreased in IBC—NST cell lines [54]. Both can explain differences in tumour growth caused by exposure to female hormones. Mutational analyses further revealed differences in prevalence of GATA3 and FOXA1 mutations between ILC vs. IBC—NST [55]. Both genes have been linked to ER expression and activity. This indicates that ILC cells vs. IBC—NST cells rely on distinct regulatory mechanisms for ER signaling.

This study presents several limitations. The first is its retrospective and monocentric nature. To generate more insights into the effect of reproductive factors on the risk of developing ILC, larger epidemiological studies are needed. This series has however the advantage that the pathological diagnosis of pure ILC will be more uniform since only a few pathologists were in charge of diagnosing these cases and E-cadherin was used in many cases to confirm the histology. However, no recent histological revision was performed due to the magnitude of the cohort . Previous studies have shown that the interobserver agreement in diagnosing ILC is imperfect but can be aided by the use of IHC for E-cadherin [[56], [57], [58], [59]]. A recent survey pointed out that great differences exist amongst pathologists in the use of E-cadherin to aid in the diagnosis of ILC, in indication of use as well as antibodies and antibody concentrations used to perform the IHC [60]. Recently, the European Lobular Breast Cancer Consortium published several recommendations towards the indication of E-cadherin IHC in ILC diagnosis as well as the best antibodies to use [30]. Diagnostic criteria and definitions of i.e., mixed IBC—NST/ILC have changed over the years [35,36] making it impossible to distinguish which patients would be considered to have a mixed IBC—NST/ILC with current guidelines without performance of central pathology review. The features of mixed IBC—NST/ILC are more comparable to ILC as to IBC—NST, possibly influencing our results [61]. Although the prevalence of mixed IBC—NST/ILC is quite rare.

The second limitation is that not all reproductive factors could be assessed since no data was available on breast feeding and the interval of subsequent pregnancies as well as the age of the last pregnancy. Similarly, previous use of MHT and socio-economic factors which could act as potential bias factors, were not considered due to the lack of data in the entire cohort. The reproductive factors that were assessed were not available for each individual patient, although the amount of missing data for the analyses was quite scarce. Due to the lack of family history and genetic predisposition of patients, the impact of parity and age 1st FTP on prevalence of ILC in higher-risk patients could not be assessed. Lastly, the sensitivity analysis performed for patients diagnosed after 2012 did not reflect the results of the entire cohort. While the full cohort provides the better power, it is unsure if it is representative for the current population. Nevertheless, this is an unprecedented series of patients with pure ILC for whom the effects of several reproductive factors were analyzed. This work therefore contributes to the general knowledge on impact of reproductive factors on the prevalence of ILC in a BC cohort. Only few, often older, publications so far have analyzed the impact of reproductive factors on the risk of developing ILC vs. IBC—NST. This is also the first report, to our knowledge, studying the association between reproductive factors and clinicopathological features of ILC.

In conclusion, a high parity (i.e., >2) was associated with a higher frequency of pure ILC in this ER+/HER2- BC cohort. This could hypothetically indicate a different effect of parity on risk of development between ILC and IBC—NST. Validation in epidemiological studies is, however, needed. Reproductive factors did not seem to impact the clinicopathological features of pure ILC at time of diagnosis, in our analyses. Larger studies of patient cohorts with pure ILC ideally confirmed by central pathology could give more insights into the impact of reproductive factors on ILC.
