Predictive variables analysis for the tongue crib treatment of anterior crossbite in mixed dentition
Authors: Shijie Zhou, Shiqi Sun, Junhan Wan, Shuchang Liu, Xiaoqing Wang, Yuanyuan Zhang, Yulou Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to identify key prognostic variables and to develop and validate a clinical prediction model for pre-treatment assessment of tongue crib applicability.MethodsThis retrospective study included 128 cases with anterior crossbite treated with tongue crib in mixed dentition. The total samples were categorized into applicable (n = 80, corrected within 6 months without relapse) and non-applicable (n = 48) groups. Cephalometric parameters were measured using Dolphin Imaging 11.8, with Python (3.9.12) for statistical analysis. Randomly select 80% as the training set and the remaining 20% as the testing set with 100 iterations to establish logistic regression models incorporating SNB-ANB, Wits, and APDI sagittal parameters as predictors. Three predictive equations P = Exp(L)/1 + Exp(L), with a critical score of 0.5 underwent apparent validation on the training set and internal validation performed on the test set. The model demonstrating optimal validity and accuracy was selected to guide the clinical application.ResultsAs for the total prediction accuracy of apparent validation on the training set and internal validation performed on the test set, SNB-ANB model was 79.9% and 77.0%; Wits model was 80.3% and 78.9%; APDI model was the highest, which was 81.1% and 80.5%. When the three prediction models changed from apparent validation on the training set to internal validation performed on the test set, the total prediction accuracy decreased slightly (-0.6% ~ -2.9%) with the APDI model exhibiting superior stability.ConclusionsThe APDI simplified prediction model expression was P = Exp(L)/1+ Exp(L), L = −0.305(APDI)−0.341(MP-FH)−0.263(Co-Go)+50.496. "P" was the probability predicted to be applicable for tongue crib treatment, with a critical score of 0.5 (that was, p >0.5 was applicable, p < 0.5 was non-applicable). Applying the prediction model was able to effectively predict the results of anterior crossbite in mixed dentition treated with tongue crib.Keywords: Anterior crossbite, Orthodontics, Tongue crib, Prediction model, Machine learning

Introduction

Anterior crossbite is a prevalent malocclusion among children in mixed dentition, with a reported much higher prevalence in East Asian populations [1, 2]. This condition can negatively impact oral function, facial development, and aesthetic appearance, and in severe cases, may even jeopardize psychological health [3, 4]. Moreover, recent studies have suggested that anterior crossbite is not likely to improve spontaneously during growth; rather, it tends to deteriorate over time [5]. Consequently, the concept of early orthodontic intervention for anterior crossbite has gained widespread acceptance among orthodontists [6].

The appropriate orthodontic treatment for anterior crossbite during the mixed dentition phase depends on its etiology and pathogenic mechanisms. In addition to the incorrect sagittal relationship between the maxilla and mandible, low tongue posture and poor tongue habits are two significant functional factors contributing to anterior crossbite. These factors can disrupt the balance of the muscles both inside and outside the oral cavity, leading to a lack of stimulation for the upper arch and maxilla from the tongue muscles, while conversely, the lower dental arch and mandible receive excessive stimulation. This imbalance can result in anterior crossbite and may even lead to a severe skeletal Class III malocclusion [7].

Historically, the utilization of tongue crib treatment has primarily been associated with the management of anterior open bite cases in clinical practice [8], demonstrating efficacy in preventing recurrence [9]. In recent years, some orthodontists have recognized that tongue crib treatment is also effective in managing anterior crossbite [10]. A study conducted by Liu et al. [11] compared the efficacy of orthodontic treatment by analyzing changes in cephalometric measurements before and after anterior crossbite correction. The study concluded that both tongue crib and maxillary protraction appliances can facilitate the development of the maxilla and promote the clockwise rotation of the mandible, thereby effectively correcting anterior crossbite. The study by Showkatbakhsh et al. [12] compared the efficacy of the tongue crib with the maxillary protraction appliance, finding that both devices were effective in promoting forward maxillary movement during craniofacial growth and development. The tongue crib successfully altered tongue behavior by positioning the tongue upward and behind the crib, which encouraged the tongue to exert pressure against the palate, thereby correcting poor tongue posture and habits. Additionally, the tongue’s forward push on the maxilla through the appliance utilized growth potential to promote forward maxillary growth. Concurrently, the abnormal force exerted by the tongue on the mandible was alleviated, contributing to the correction of functional mandibular prognathism. Ultimately, this approach can effectively and stably correct anterior crossbite, harmonize the development of the maxilla and mandible, and address mild jaw deformities [13]. However, in certain cases of anterior crossbite associated with poor tongue postures and habits, the therapeutic efficacy of the tongue crib may be suboptimal, often resulting in prolonged treatment durations and unstable outcomes. This phenomenon is primarily linked to the individual characteristics of maxillofacial development. Additionally, there remains a lack of objective and effective evaluation criteria to inform clinical practice and enhance decision-making regarding which specific patient characteristics are most suitable for tongue crib treatment in cases of anterior crossbite.

Predictive models are developed to assist physicians in estimating the probability that a specific disease or condition is present (diagnostic models) or that a specific event will occur in the future (prognostic models), thereby informing their decision-making [14]. The estimation of such probabilities is rarely based on a single predictor. Instead, predictors are combined into a consensus model, with each predictor weighted according to its relative contribution to derive a final probability [15]. Following model development, internal validation performed on the test set is strongly recommended to evaluate the model’s performance using datasets that were not employed during the development phase. internal validation performed on the test set involves applying the same prediction model to each individual in the new sample and comparing observed outcomes with predicted outcomes [16, 17]. These results indicate that utilizing multiple bioinformatic predictors with varying accuracies, including several weak predictors, can enhance the detection of causal variants. Furthermore, indicators reflecting dental features, along with mandible and maxilla characteristics in cephalometric measurements, are also utilized to develop predictive models.

This study analyzes the skeletal and dental items from initial lateral radiographs, along with related clinical data, in cases of anterior crossbite during mixed dentition treated with a tongue crib. By combining the final treatment outcomes with a retrospective analysis of these cases, the study aims to identify predictive factors and develop a predictive model. This model is designed to assess the applicability of the tongue crib prior to treatment, providing theoretical guidance for its effective clinical application. Furthermore, the predictive model constructed in this study has been practically validated, demonstrating its applicability in clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Patients

A specific collection of lateral cephalogram films, details on dental examination and intraoral photos at first diagnosis was assembled from the Department of Orthodontics, Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of China Medical University. Initial screening identified patients with anterior crossbite in the mixed dentition treated with a tongue crib appliance between February 2019 and February 2024.

The following inclusion criteria were used to define the final sample: (1) presence of anterior crossbite in mixed dentition (anterior overjet < 0 mm and crossbite); (2) poor tongue postures and habits; (3) absence of anterior open bite; (4) absence of posterior crossbite; (5) good compliance; (6) adjunctive use of chin-cup therapy; (7) availability of records from a 6-month follow-up visit (8)no retention appliance was used during the 6-month follow-up. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) with congenitally missing or extracted teeth (2) diagnosed as cleft lip and/or palate, and other craniofacial dysmorphisms (3) with a history of orthopedic or orthodontic treatment.

A total of 128 patients (65 males, 63 females; age range 6–12 years) met the inclusion criteria. Treatment outcomes were assessed based on dental examinations, intraoral photographs, and medical records. Patients were categorized into two groups according to treatment outcome: The applicable group was defined as those in whom the anterior crossbite was corrected within six months and did not relapse during the six-month follow-up after treatment (anterior overjet > 0 mm and overbite > 0 mm), comprising a total of 80 cases (42 males and 38 females). Conversely, the non-applicable group included individuals who’s anterior crossbite was either not corrected within six months or relapsed during the six-month follow-up after treatment (anterior overjet < 0 mm and/or crossbite), totaling 48 cases (23 males and 25 females).

Tongue crib appliance

The tongue crib appliance is a removable device comprising arrowhead clasps, lingual spurs, a base plate, and bilateral posterior occlusal pads. The lingual spurs are fabricated from 0.8 mm stainless steel wire, positioned along the line connecting the upper canines. They are curved inward and extend downward along the lingual side of the lower incisors, with a length of 15–25 mm determined by the floor-of-mouth depth. The spurs should avoid mucosal pressure and clear the lingual frenum. Arrowhead clasps are placed on the maxillary first permanent molars or second deciduous molars, utilizing mesial and distal undercuts for retention. The presence and thickness of the occlusal pads are adjusted based on the severity of anterior crossbite, ideally creating a 2–3 mm separation between the upper and lower anterior teeth (Fig. 1).

General data processing

Age at diagnosis was calculated based on the difference between the birth year and year of initial presentation. Cervical Vertebral Maturation (CVM) was assessed according to Baccetti et al.; stages are reported as CVMS 1–6 [18]. Categorical variables—including sex and the presence of interdental spacing in the mandibular anterior region—were coded numerically for analysis (sex: male = 1, female = 0; spacing: present = 1, absent = 0) (see Table 1).

Cephalometric analysis

All lateral cephalograms were obtained in the Department of Radiology at the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of China Medical University (Shenyang, China), utilizing the same device and digital cephalometric tracing system under strictly standardized conditions to minimize interobserver and inter-device variability. Furthermore, all lateral cephalograms were captured in the intercuspal position (ICP), with the lips naturally relaxed and the Frankfurt horizontal plane aligned parallel to the ground plane.

Dolphin Imaging 11.8 software (May 2015; Patterson Dental Supply, Inc., dba Dolphin Imaging and Management Solutions, Chatsworth, USA) was utilized to measure the skeletal and dental parameters from the initial lateral cephalogram. All measurements were performed and recorded by the same researcher, with the process being repeated two weeks later, after which the results were averaged. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.9.12; Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). The pandas package (version 1.3.5) was used for correlation analysis and descriptive statistics (reported as mean ± standard deviation). The scikit-learn package (version 1.0.2) was employed for random sampling, logistic regression analysis (significance levels: p < 0.05 and p < 0.01), and accuracy validation.

The performance of the prediction models was assessed using sensitivity, specificity, and the Youden index.

Sensitivity (true positive rate) is the proportion of actual ‘applicable’ cases that are correctly identified by the model. It measures the model’s ability to correctly select good candidates for treatment. Specificity (true negative rate) is the proportion of actual ‘non-applicable’ cases that are correctly identified by the model. It measures the model’s ability to correctly rule out poor candidates for treatment. The Youden index (J) is a single statistic that summarizes the model’s overall discriminative ability, calculated as J = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1. Its value ranges from 0 to 1, where a value of 1 indicates a perfect test, and a value of 0 indicates a test with no discriminative power.”

Results of logistic regression analysis

The results of the logistic regression analysis indicate that the core independent variables for each team, SNB, ANB, Wits, and APDI, are all significant factors (p < 0.01) affecting the applicability of tongue crib treatment. These variables reflect the sagittal relationship between the maxilla and the mandible. Furthermore, the regression coefficients suggest that as the ANB and Wits values decrease, the APDI value increases, indicating a stronger tendency toward skeletal Class III malocclusion. This trend implies that the results are increasingly less applicable for tongue crib treatment. Besides, in the SNB-ANB simplified prediction model, SNB and MP-FH are significant factors (p < 0.05), while Co-Go is recognized as a highly significant factor (p < 0.01). In the Wits simplified prediction model, Co-Go is significant (p < 0.05), and MP-FH is classified as a highly significant factor (p < 0.01). Additionally, in the APDI simplified prediction model, Co-Go remains a significant factor (p < 0.05), with MP-FH again being a highly significant factor (p < 0.01).

Comprehensive evaluations of three logistic regression analyses reveal that MP-FH and Co-Go are always significant factors, even highly significant factors with higher stability. Both of them reflect the sagittal relationship between the maxilla and the mandible. Larger MP-FH and Co-Go values indicate a clearer tendency towards a dolichofacial type, suggesting that the results are less applicable for tongue crib treatment. Conversely, OP-FH, Bjork sum, gonial angle, Go-Pog, S-Go/N-Me, U1-SN, L1-MP, overjet, overbite, gender, initial age, CVMS, and lower anterior space were not found to be significant factors (p > 0.05).

The comparison of the Pseudo R2 values among the three groups reveals that group SNB-ANB exhibits a higher value than group APDI, which in turn is greater than that of group Wits. Notably, the Pseudo R2 values for both group SNB-ANB and group APDI are significantly higher than that of group Wits, indicating a superior fitting degree of the regression equations, which would further have made the interpretation of the data stronger (Table 4).

Performance of the models

The ROC curves derived from the test set validation of the three predictive models are displayed in Fig. 4. Each curve corresponds to one randomly selected iteration out of 100 independent validation runs. The SNB-ANB model, Wits model, and APDI model all demonstrated moderate yet consistent discriminatory ability in the test set, as reflected by their respective Brier scores ranging from 0.184 to 0.208. The results indicate that all three models offer comparable and clinically relevant predictive performance.

In the internal validation performed on the test set, the Wits group accurately predicted 88.4% of applicable actual cases (sensitivity) and 63.1% of inapplicable actual cases (specificity), yielding a Youden index of 51.5% and an overall prediction accuracy of 78.9% (Table 9). In addition, in the apparent validation on the training set phase, the APDI simple predictive model demonstrated a sensitivity of 90.0%, accurately predicting applicable actual cases, and a specificity of 66.9% for inapplicable actual cases, resulting in an overall prediction accuracy of 81.1% with a Youden index of 56.9%. In the internal validation performed on the test set, the simple predictive model achieved a sensitivity of 88.4% for applicable actual cases and a specificity of 64.8% for inapplicable actual cases, culminating in a total prediction accuracy of 80.5% with a Youden index of 53.2% (Tables 12 and 13).

Discussion

Compared to conventional approaches such as facemask therapy, the tongue crib offers distinct advantages: it not only corrects functional mandibular prognathism associated with abnormal tongue posture but also actively utilizes natural growth potential to facilitate maxillary advancement to a certain extent. This dual mechanism makes it particularly suitable for managing functional anterior crossbite, including cases with established sagittal discrepancies due to lack of early intervention [19]. As the tongue crib presents a promising alternative for managing anterior crossbite in mixed dentition, it is essential for orthodontists to accurately identify children who are more suited for this treatment option. To date, most predictive studies on early crossbite correction have focused on maxillary expansion and facemask therapy [20, 21], with limited attention given to the tongue crib. This study aims to develop and validate a predictive model to identify ideal candidates for tongue crib treatment prior to initiation, thus enhancing its targeted application. By facilitating patient selection and optimizing outcomes, this model supports the broader adoption of the tongue crib, potentially increasing early treatment success, improving efficiency, and reinforcing long-term stability.

The ANB, Wits, and APDI are cephalometric measurements widely used to evaluate the sagittal relationship between the maxilla and mandible. However, their accuracy remains debated. The ANB angle can be influenced by factors such as the position of the nasal root [22]. Jacobson proposed the Wits analysis as an alternative with potential advantages over ANB [23], though it is also affected by variables like the occlusal plane angle [24]. Ahmed et al. suggested that ANB may be more reliable than Wits in Class III cases with a low occlusal plane angle [25]. Kim argued that a single measurement is insufficient and introduced the APDI as a more comprehensive index for improved accuracy [26]. Although using all three measures may provide a more rigorous assessment, their high collinearity can lead to errors or overfitting if included together in the same logistic regression model.

Therefore, building on the strengths and limitations of each cephalometric variable and considering their intercorrelations, we grouped the core predictors SNB-ANB, Wits, and APDI, and developed three logistic regression models. After comparing and validating their prediction accuracy, the APDI model was selected as the most accurate, represented by the equation P = Exp(L)/1 + Exp(L), L = − 0.305(APDI) − 0.341(MP-FH) − 0.263(Co-Go) + 50.496 with a total prediction accuracy of 81.1% in the apparent validation on the training set and 80.5% in the internal validation performed on the test set indicating that for each new case, the prediction accuracy remains at 80.5%.

In this study, the mandibular plane angle (MP-FH) and ramus length (Go-Co) were significant predictors across all three models, reflecting vertical maxillomandibular relationships. Their regression coefficients were consistently negative (− 0.357 and − 0.258; −0.165 and − 0.250; −0.341 and − 0.263, respectively), indicating that higher values correspond to a dolichofacial growth pattern and reduced suitability for tongue crib treatment. This may be attributed to the challenge of predicting mandibular growth, which varies considerably in direction and morphology in Class III patients. According to Björk [27, 28], mandibular rotation influences the direction and extent of mandibular development. Tahmina [29] and Aristide [30] further identified the mandibular plane angle as a prognostic indicator for facial growth pattern. Vertical growth tendencies are generally associated with poorer stability and outcomes, whereas horizontal patterns correlate with more favorable results. The significant variables in the prediction model of this study primarily consist of indicators that reflect the sagittal and vertical relationships between the maxilla and mandible. Therefore, we can infer that the determining factors for the early treatment of anterior crossbite in mixed dentition involve inferring the degree of forward growth and the rotation of the mandible.

This study is a retrospective analysis in which research subjects are categorized based on their clinical treatment outcomes and treatment duration. While X-ray cephalometric indicators provide valuable insights, orthodontists should also focus on clinical outcomes beyond their treatment. Additionally, the causative factors of anterior overbite are predominantly multifactorial, indicating that the success of orthodontic treatment cannot be solely assessed through cephalometric measurements. Consequently, our study integrated clinical examinations and intraoral photographs to evaluate anterior overjet and overbite as key criteria.

In recent years, machine learning has been an emerging field that effectively integrates computer science, statistics, and medical problems [31]. The application of Python in the field of orthodontics enables data processing and analysis that surpasses the capabilities of traditional methods employed by practitioners. It is important to highlight that the random selection process in this study has never been done in previous prediction studies.

In summary, the study places sufficient emphasis on clinical interpretation and practical implications by highlighting the tongue crib’s dual mechanism—addressing both abnormal tongue posture and sagittal discrepancy through natural growth guidance—and introducing a clinically applicable predictive model based on cephalometric and vertical skeletal factors. The model facilitates patient selection by identifying cases with horizontal growth patterns as more suitable, thereby improving early treatment efficiency, stability, and individualized outcome prediction. Furthermore, the integration of clinical examination findings alongside cephalometric variables underscores the multifactorial nature of treatment success, enhancing the direct relevance to orthodontic practice.

Further research directions

There are several limitations in this study. Most importantly, our findings only reflect short-term stability, as the evaluation period was limited to six months after treatment. Long-term follow-up extending beyond the completion of mandibular growth is necessary to definitively establish the durability of the outcomes. Furthermore, this study focused solely on predicting the short-term effectiveness of early tongue crib therapy and did not incorporate the measurement of mandibular growth during and after treatment. Future studies should include extended follow-up until growth cessation to better assess long-term stability and skeletal changes, although such studies face practical challenges in participant retention and sample size maintenance over extended periods, often spanning 5–10 years.

Anther limitation is the lack of precise overjet and overbite data, which hinders robust statistical analysis and direct comparison with quantitative studies. This underscores the need for standardized data collection in future research. Nonetheless, qualitative findings confirm the treatment’s efficacy and suggest that even minor relapse often represents significant improvement over pretreatment status, potentially reducing the need for more invasive interventions.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that cephalometric indicators reflecting sagittal (ANB, Wits, APDI) and vertical (MP-FH, Co-Go) maxillomandibular relationships are significant predictors of treatment outcome following tongue crib therapy for anterior crossbite in the mixed dentition. Larger MP-FH and Co-Go values indicate a stronger inclination towards dolichofacial type, further suggesting that the results may be less applicable for tongue crib treatment. Among the evaluated models, the APDI simplified prediction model showed superior reliability and accuracy. For each new patient, this model achieves a prediction accuracy of 80.5%. The expression for the APDI simplified prediction model is given by P = Exp(L)/(1 + Exp(L)), where L = − 0.305(APDI) − 0.341(MP-FH) − 0.263(Co-Go) + 50.496. Here, “P” represents the probability of suitability for tongue crib treatment,, with a critical score of 0.5 (i.e., p > 0.5 indicates applicability, while p < 0.5 indicates non-applicability).
