Enhancing neural rehabilitation insights: on the path of bridging artificial and biological neural networks
Authors: Abdullatif Baba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper introduces the conceptual parallel between the ANN training process and the learning mechanisms of the human brain. Then, we briefly discuss a set of recently achieved experimental findings from a prior study that delves into various scenarios, aiding in comprehending the functionality of impaired or damaged neurons within a neural system. The key contribution of this paper is to present a novel variant of the Adam optimizer that incorporates a dynamic momentum adjustment factor, adaptive learning rate, and elastic weight consolidation technique. This enhanced version draws inspiration from biological processes to improve learning stability in artificial neural networks, with conceivable relevance to neural adaptation and rehabilitation research.

Introduction

ANNs simulate the human brain’s neural structure; they are effective for tasks involving large datasets, such as image recognition, natural language processing, or speech recognition. They excel in learning complex relationships between inputs and outputs, making them suitable for tasks with nonlinear mappings [1]. In this context, significant advances have been observed in classifying brain disorders through deep learning; a recent approach involving a multilevel convolutional neural network fusion method has been introduced, which combines various features derived from the neuroimage [2].

Training an ANN involves adjusting weights and biases using an optimization algorithm, typically employing back-propagation, where activation functions like Sigmoid can be used. The backpropagation algorithm computes the error between predicted and desired outputs and adjusts weights to minimize this error. The weights should converge toward values optimized for the task as the training progresses. However, during training, there can be a few issues. For example, the vanishing-gradient problem could be encountered where backpropagated gradient errors become very small, causing the weights of a given neuron to stop adjusting. In such a case, a set of solutions could be presented, including using another training algorithm such as the Adam optimizer [3] or replacing the activation function with ReLU or some other alternatives. This latter activation function may lead to dead neurons with zero output in certain regions due to a zero gradient for negative values. This issue could be avoided by replacing it with LeakyReLU, allowing negative inputs to produce some corresponding output [4, 5]. Behavioral disorders in humans may be correlated with vanishing gradient problems and dead neurons in ANNs. Replacing or modifying activation functions is more feasible in ANNs than in biological neurons with chemical activation functions.

In this context, an alternative approach involves using Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs), which are more biologically realistic than traditional ANNs, incorporating spikes or action potentials seen in biological neurons [6, 7]. Implementing SNNs requires appropriate tools like SpiNNaker or BrainScaleS to enhance their suitability for modeling neural disorders. SNNs are inspired by the communication of biological neurons through electrical spikes or ’action potentials’ [8]. In contrast to traditional neural networks, which process continuous-valued inputs and outputs, SNNs operate on time-varying signals. Each neuron is activated by discrete spikes that represent the timing and strength of incoming signals. SNNs process information more biologically, considering the magnitude, timing, and frequency of incoming spikes. This enables accurate modeling of spatiotemporal dynamics in biological neural networks, addressing challenges involving temporal patterns and event-based data. SNNs excel in tasks such as speech and image recognition and robotic system control, where event timing is critical [9]. SNNs can be trained using various methods, including supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning, with different architectures and variants [10, 11]. A common training algorithm is SpikeProp, an extension of backpropagation used in ANNs. On the other hand, a Synaptic Update Rule (SUR) algorithm could be represented as a biologically plausible learning rule that modifies synaptic weights based on spike timing between pre- and post-synaptic neurons; it strengthens active connections simultaneously and weakens others. The unsupervised learning algorithm, the STDP (Spike-Timing-Dependent Plasticity), also adjusts synaptic weights based on pre- and post-synaptic spike timing, with different rules for updating weights compared to the SUR algorithm. The choice of activation function in SNNs depends on the task and network architecture. Common functions include the Sigmoid Function, which is frequently used in both SNNs and ANNs; the reconstructed linear unit (ReLU), and variants like the spiking reconstructed linear unit (SReLU), which is designed for SNNs, considering their spiking nature. In a parallel context to neuroscience, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) are created to capture sequential dependencies in data by allowing information to persist in the network’s internal state. This architecture is inspired by the feedback loops and recurrent connections observed in the human brain, allowing RNNs to process sequential data [12]. Inspired by the idea that the human brain effectively utilizes memory for learning and reasoning, Turing neural machines and memory-embedded networks integrate external memory structures with neural networks. These architectures enable the model to access and modify memory, enhancing its capacity for tasks that require long-term reliance [13]. Recent neuroscience research has explored how error-driven learning could be implemented in the brain. Lillicrap et al. [14] provide a comprehensive review of biologically plausible alternatives to backpropagation, suggesting potential mechanisms involving feedback alignment and local synaptic plasticity. Similarly, Whittington and Bogacz [15] discuss theories of error backpropagation in the cortex, proposing how the brain may implement credit assignment without symmetric weight transport. These studies highlight the challenges of directly translating ANN training to biological systems and motivate our exploration of conceptual analogies, especially in the context of neural adaptation and rehabilitation.

Elastic weight consolidation (EWC) is also a technique inspired by synaptic consolidation in the brain. It helps neural networks retain previously learned information while learning new tasks. This approach prevents deadly forgetting, a phenomenon in which the network forgets previously learned patterns when trained on new data [16]. This paper clarifies the conceptual parallels between the Adam optimizer’s functionality and the brain’s learning mechanisms. Subsequently, we briefly illustrate a series of previously achieved experimental findings published in a former study to explore various scenarios explaining the impact on the overall performance of a given artificial neural network when a specific neuron unexpectedly loses the weight of its connections during the training phase. These scenarios may contribute to interpreting the function of damaged or impaired neurons in the human brain. Then, we introduce a novel variant of the Adam optimizer incorporating a dynamic momentum adjustment factor and adaptive Learning Rate. This enhanced version of Adam plays a key role in facilitating a deeper understanding of complex neural processes and interactions, thereby offering insight into the functioning of biological neurons and their rehabilitation systems. Consequently, Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) will also be introduced and combined with the Adam optimizer. This combination aims to enhance the capabilities of the trained Artificial Neural Network, enabling it to strike a balance between adapting to new tasks and preserving knowledge acquired from previous ones. Finally, our study ends with a comprehensive conclusion.

Experimental observations on Adam optimizer

Although there are parallels between the learning mechanism of the Adam optimizer and the human brain learning process, their complexity and information-processing methods differ. Reprogramming human neurons directly using the Adam optimizer or any other algorithm is currently unfeasible. However, developing a new reinforced learning algorithm inspired by Adam’s principles could potentially identify patterns or relationships in complex data [17]. The mathematical description of the Adam optimizer does not represent a comprehensive model of biological processes, but conceptual analogies can be drawn, such as moving averages representing changes in synaptic strength. In this context, the choice between using the Adam optimizer with an Artificial Neural Network (ANN) or a Spiking Neural Network (SNN) depends on the specific characteristics of the problem, the nature of the data, and the application’s requirements. Here are some practical considerations for both cases:

Adam with ANN: Adam Optimizer is well-suited for optimizing parameters in continuous domains. Suppose that the data and task are continuous (e.g., image classification). In that case, Adam becomes a common and effective choice, as it is known for its adaptive learning rates, which can lead to faster convergence compared to traditional methods. This can be advantageous in scenarios where training time is a crucial factor. Adam tends to provide stable and robust training for artificial neural networks (ANNs) in various tasks. It is also less sensitive to hyperparameter choices than some other optimizers.

Adam with SNN:

SNNs operate on spiking neurons with a temporal dimension, capturing information over time. The effectiveness of Adam in the context of temporal dynamics may vary, and some customized adaptations may be required.

Within the same framework, to understand the consequences of a neuron losing the weight of connections during the training process, we executed many experimental scenarios employing different activation functions and training algorithms in a prior investigation, where we designed a tailored ANN comprising an input layer (5 neurons), two hidden layers (10 neurons each), and an output layer (single neuron). The data set used contained information on power consumption in a small region spanning five years, with the ANN’s objective being to predict power consumption for the following year. A new variant of adaptive Adam optimizer; (dynamic momentum adjustment, and adaptive learning rate)

Although several optimizers such as SGD, Nadam, and AMSGrad are useful in some situations, Adam was utilized due to its robust parameter-wise adaptive updates and reduced sensitivity to hyperparameters. This property makes it suitable for biologically inspired training dynamics with plasticity and compensation. Our extension leverages this adaptability through the incorporation of reward-driven learning rate and momentum adjustments, which are analogous to characteristics of synaptic plasticity. Moreover, ablation experiments (Section 5.1 and Table 2) demonstrate that dynamic momentum adjustment and adaptive learning rate combined with Adam produce excellent convergence improvement and stability for artificially caused neuron damage.

Recently, several variants of the Adam optimizer were suggested [20–22]. Typically, those approaches exhibit improved performance compared to the initial algorithm. However, our proposed approach involves the development of a novel simplified version that aims to provide a fresh perspective, unveiling the parallels between the Adam optimizer and the learning mechanisms of the human brain. Developing the Adam optimizer to incorporate environmental signals, essentially turning it into a reinforcement learning optimizer, requires some practical considerations. In this context, a dynamic momentum adjustment-based approach could be suggested to adapt the momentum terms according to some criterion related to the performance or reward. The second suggested modification involves introducing an adaptive learning rate based on observed rewards. The idea here is that if the model performs well (lower loss), the learning rate will increase; if the model does not perform well (higher loss), the learning rate will decrease. The biological analogy of modifying the learning rate can also be related to the adaptability of synaptic plasticity, which refers to the ability of synapses (connections between neurons) to change their strength over time in response to different learning conditions or environmental changes, which is crucial for learning and memory in biological brains where the strength of synapses can be modulated according to the significance of the information being processed. In the context of the adaptive Adam optimizer with dynamic learning rates based on rewards, it is like introducing a form of reward-driven synaptic plasticity. Once again, positive outcomes (rewards) could lead to an increase in the learning rate, strengthening the influence of certain connections. In contrast, negative outcomes might lead to a decrease, weakening the influence. On the other hand, just as biological systems seek a balance between stability and adaptability, the adaptive learning rate can help the neural network maintain stability (avoid rapid parameter changes) while still adapting to new information. It is crucial to note that biological neural systems are complex, and the mechanisms of synaptic plasticity are much more intricate than learning rate adjustments in artificial neural networks. Biological systems involve various neuromodulators, multiple forms of plasticity, and complex network interactions.

Elastic weight consolidation (EWC)

Elastic weight consolidation (EWC) is a regularization technique designed to address the challenge of fatal forgetting in neural networks that occurs when the trained model tends to forget data from earlier tasks as it learns new ones. This phenomenon inhibits the ability of neural networks to accumulate knowledge over time and adapt to changing scenarios [23]. It draws inspiration from biological theories of memory consolidation. The key idea behind EWC is to penalize changes in the parameters of a neural network that are crucial for previously learned tasks. By assigning different levels of importance to each parameter, EWC allows the model to prioritize preserving essential knowledge while accepting new information. The regularization term in EWC is determined by Fisher information matrices, which quantify the sensitivity of the model’s output to parameter changes. Parameters associated with higher Fisher Information are considered more important, and their updates are penalized more to prevent forgetting. Merging elastic weight consolidation (EWC) with the adaptive version of the Adam optimizer involves combining the principles of both approaches to enhance the stability and adaptability of the training process, making it a valuable tool for overcoming the challenges posed by dynamic and evolving learning environments. The third implementation

Finally, we suggest a heuristic implementation to simulate a scenario: a neural network learns to control a set of movements that represent a simplified motor rehabilitation task. Whereas the motor task in this deployment uses simulated information, the architecture mimics natural EMG-based muscle activation. Each of the 16 input features can be thought of as comparable to the EMG channel signals showing activity levels of various muscle groups. The analog map is taken from studies on reduced speed and limb movement, where spatiotemporal profiles of muscle activation derived from EMG signals are used for the diagnosis in rehabilitation.

This implementation uses a dataset that represents muscle activation patterns (X) and desired movement outcomes (y). (X) is a matrix composed of 1000 rows (samples) and 16 columns (suggested features), while (y) is a vector composed of 1000 elements.

Although the dataset is synthetic, each of the 16 features in the input matrix X is designed to simulate the activation of a specific muscle or a group of muscles involved in a motor task. We constructed each feature column using modulated sine wave patterns, inspired by common EMG signal patterns observed during locomotion studies. These approximations aim to mimic realistic muscle activations while maintaining full control over signal variability. This abstraction supports the development of algorithms that generalize well to rehabilitation scenarios while remaining computationally manageable.

The semantic interpretation of these features is related to the contribution of each muscle group to the overall motor activity being simulated; that is, the values in each feature indicate the intensity or degree of activation of the corresponding muscle during a motor task, analogous to the activation patterns of neurons in a biological neural network that controls motor functions. In the real world, these features could represent the firing rates or activity levels of motor neurons associated with specific muscles. Combining these 16 features collectively captures the general muscle activation patterns involved in a motor task. We randomly selected hidden neurons and zeroed their weights and biases to simulate neural damage during training. By simulating damage similar to that seen in biological neural circuits, this successfully disabled those neurons. We observed activation levels and gradient flows in nearby neurons before and after damage to observe compensatory behavior. The increased activation in nearby neurons and their updated weight magnitudes over epochs were used to quantify compensation.

The neural network is trained to learn the relationship between these muscle activation patterns and the desired outcome of the movement (y). In this simplified implementation, the 16 features serve as inputs to the neural network, mimicking how muscle activations influence motor control in a biological system. We aim to predict movement outcomes based on muscle activations. The simulation introduces damage to specific neurons during training. The network performance is evaluated in both the original and damaged scenarios.

During the training phase, the weights associated with each feature in the hidden layer capture the model’s learned representation of the importance or contribution of each muscle activation pattern to the desired movement outcome. At first, a classical version of the Adam optimizer is used to train the ANN.

In both cases (original and damaged scenarios), the “loss” is calculated to measure how well the model performs on a particular task. Quantifies the difference between the predicted and actual values for a given input data set. Conclusion

In this paper, we briefly discuss some experimental findings previously documented in a separate study [9], to investigate different scenarios that illustrate the impact on the overall performance of an artificial neural network (ANN) when a specific neuron unexpectedly loses its connection weights during the training phase. The aim is to improve our interpretation of the functionalities of damaged or impaired neurons in the human brain.

Then, we comprehensively explore the applicability of the Adam optimizer in artificial and spiking neural networks. The proposed variant with Dynamic momentum adjustment and adaptive learning rate shows some promise in addressing challenges posed by neurons losing weight during training. Dynamic momentum adjustment was introduced by scaling the momentum term depending on a reward signal. The scaling factor serves as a modulator for the impact of previous gradients on the optimization process. The second development suggested presents an adaptive learning rate based on observed rewards, similar to reward-driven synaptic plasticity in biological neural systems. The learning rate is dynamically adjusted during training, increasing when the model performs well and decreasing when it does not.

Then, elastic weight consolidation (EWC), a regularization technique designed to address the challenge of fatal forgetting in neural networks, was introduced and merged with the new variant of the Adam optimizer. As we aim to evaluate the optimizer’s ability to handle complex relationships in the data and generalize well to situations with nonlinear decision boundaries, the new version of the adaptive Adam optimizer was first tested by training a neural network on XOR logic, which is a nonlinear function. Then, the effect of implementing elastic weight consolidation (EWC) was proved by a new experiment that results in higher accuracy in both training and validation curves compared to scenarios where EWC is not considered. Finally, an alternative implementation is proposed to simulate a scenario in which a neural network learns to control a set of movements, representing a simplified motor rehabilitation task. The adaptation of the Adam optimizer for scenarios involving damaged neurons has potential implications for the field of neural rehabilitation. In addition, our study underscores the importance of continuous improvement and adaptation of optimization algorithms for effective and personalized rehabilitation programs in the context of neurological disorders.
