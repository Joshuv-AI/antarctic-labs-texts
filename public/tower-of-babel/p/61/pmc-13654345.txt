A critical review on the effect of high-velocity ballistic impact loading on the personal armors used by the soldiers
Authors: Suresh Kumar Sundaram, Maalolan Balaji, Harish Saravana Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the field of the defense industry, personal armor is defined as the protective covering designed commonly to withstand a particular type of attack for a soldier. Armors are generally designed to withstand the penetration of a bullet. Behind armor blunt trauma (BABT) is a non-penetrating injury caused by the fast deformation of the body armor. The impact of a bullet or projectile on an armor's front face distorts the surface as it encounters the body. The deformation occurs during the process of capturing the projectile by absorbing its kinetic energy and retarding it. In severe cases, even if the projectile does not penetrate the armor, the BABT can still cause death. As bullet energy increases and armor manufacturers aim to reduce weight, the danger of BABT in military and security personnel rises. BABT results from a combination of pressure waves caused by the rapid movement of the armor's rear and the shear deformation of internal organs due to significant deflection of the body wall. This review assesses the impact of BABT on internal organs and solutions to overcome BABT.

Introduction

Generally, armor plates are employed in battle to shield personnel, tanks, automobiles, portable covers, patrol boats, and helicopter components from projectiles' impacts. Currently, armored vehicles use materials including rolled homogenous armor, iron, titanium, ceramic pads, polymeric composites, and aluminum.1 Body armor can be divided into 2 types: soft and rigid. Soft armor consists of numerous layers of materials (up to 50 layers) and weighs less than 4.5 kg. Corresponding to the National Institute of Justice (NIJ) armor norms, soft armor protects against low to medium energy with a maximum velocity of 500 m/s. When combined with a soft armor vest, hard armor can withstand projectiles with a velocity of over 500 m/s. New ballistic protection concepts have been studied to address wearability, mobility, damage resistance, lightweight, and enhanced energy absorption. In today's borderless world, the inventions of new weaponry, contemporary military operations, and technology-driven combat tactics have created a critical requirement.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 When developing body armor, it is necessary to consider the behind armor blunt trauma (BABT) caused by the projectile's impacts. While armor may not pierce the body, it can cause injuries such as bruises, lacerations, and internal organ damage, as shown in Fig. 1.7,8 As per the NIJ Standard 0101.04, soft armor pads can collapse up to a maximum of 44 mm without causing any kind of penetration. However, this standard does not take into account the impact of pressure waves caused by the projectile's impact, or the energy transferred to the internal organs in the torso, which can result in BABT.9 This paper provides a comparative study of the materials used for armors, various armor standards, and the effects of BABT on vital internal organs.Fig. 1Blunt trauma owing to excessive deformation.7.Fig. 1

Studies on armor materials

Armors have traditionally been made of iron and steel. These materials remain popular for vehicle armor due to their excessive strength, ease of manufacture, and low cost. Although aluminum has a lower density than iron, it also has lower strength.10,11 Nowadays, most combat vehicles are armored with 7075-T6 aluminum alloy. This heat-treatable alloy offers better protection from all directions and has exceptionally strong mechanical properties. 7075-T6 aluminum alloy armor plates are commonly used in defense vehicles due to their ability to provide a high level of protection while minimizing weight, which significantly enhances vehicle mobility. Use of aluminum alloy grade AA7075-T6 has additional benefits, such as increased stiffness and being free from low-temperature embrittlement. Because of its greater rigidity, secondary support structures are not necessary. This alloy is composed of 5.6% zinc, 2.5% magnesium, and 1.6% copper elements that collectively enhance its ultimate strength. However, the copper content makes welding extremely challenging.12 Transparent armor can be made from thermoplastics with high mechanical qualities, such as polycarbonate and acrylic glass. Laminated glass is made up of layers of polymer in both glassy and plastic states, held together by a vinyl adhesion layer. Ceramics were considered potential candidates for armor due to their enhanced mechanical strength compared to glass polymers, but their brittle behavior and poor energy dissipation capacity at high-velocity impact limit their use.13,14 Many other kinds of armor, including composite armor,15 have been proven in recent decades to achieve ever-higher ballistic resistance values. Because woven materials exhibit high strain-to-failure ratios under tension, compression, and impact stresses because of the interlacing of their fiber bundles, woven fabrics are used to create composite armor. When woven into a fabric, the structural characteristics of the individual fibers cause them to react differently to ballistic impact. Since then, a great deal of research has been done to strengthen the materials and boost their resistance to ballistic penetration while retaining their flexibility.16 Examples of high-performance fibers include aramids such as Kevlar (by DuPont) and Twaron (by Teijin), ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene like Spectra (by Allied Signal), and poly-pyridobisimidazole fibers. Advanced materials

The term "functionally gradient bulletproof material" generally refers to a metal/ceramic composite material where the volume content of ceramic particles varies continuously with thickness; that is, the content of ceramic material changes from high to low from the composite armor panel to the back plate, making the panel perform similarly to ceramic material while the back plate performs similarly to metal material.17,18 The impedance shift between the materials is eliminated in this new type of protective material because of its great loading capabilities, due to its structure, and the lack of a visible discrete contact between the ceramic and metal parts. Additionally, there are several benefits, including improved interface shear-coupling properties, ease of adhesion to the material's metal surface, and the ability to quickly resolve interface issues and the impedance matching problem that affect the ballistic performance of conventional ceramic/metal composite armor.19,20 Low-density ceramics and light metals like titanium and aluminum alloy are typically used to create ceramic/metal gradient functional materials, which have the potential to be used in lightweight armor systems. In order to defend objects of caliber armor-piercing incendiary with 7.6 – 14.5 mm, functional gradient materials of armor systems are currently being explored both domestically and internationally. These materials include Ti/TiB2, Al2O3/Al, SiC/Al, B4C/Al, Si3N4/Al, and many more types of material.

One type of biomimetic material is micro-laminated armor.21 Metal intermetallic laminate is devised and applied to this range of materials, drawing inspiration from unique natural structures, specifically the overlapping structure of a high-strength brittle layer and organic layer with good toughness. High specific modulus and specific strength are provided by intermetallic compounds, which also use ductile metals to support the entire laminated structure and give the system toughness. Through small layer spacing and the multi-interface effect, this material not only has a stress field with energy dissipative structure, but it also effectively increases the laminated structure's resistance to transmitted and reflected waves and increases the armor material's fracture toughness.

The development of high-explosive anti-tank technology has made it difficult for conventional armor protection technology to withstand the potent anti-armor technology. Therefore, we need to find new armor protection technology to satisfy the need. At this period in history, electromagnetic armor22 emerges. Electromagnetic armor offers a faster reaction time than explosive armor. Despite the challenges posed by identification and capacitor technology, industrial foundation advancements are making electromagnetic armor technologies more feasible from a development standpoint of over 2 decades. Active electromagnetic armor has so far been successfully tested abroad, and disturbance-type electromagnetic armor is also progressively making its way into real warfare.

Studies on armor standards

The NIJ Standard 0101.04 describes the test criteria used to measure the ballistic resistance of personal body armor. This standard aims to set minimum performance requirements and test methods for evaluating the ballistic resistance of personal body armor, designed to protect the torso from gunfire. This standard specifies 6 different types of body armors, ranked according to the increasing levels of threat protection; however, only the 4 types related to soft armor protection in the absence of inserts were examined in the literature.23 The 4 tiers are categorised as Type I, IIa, II, and IIIa, based on the degree of protection they provide. Each degree of armor is assessed under specific danger conditions, as specified by the standard, considering the bullet's nature, weight, and impact velocity as summarised in Table 2. The soft armor vest is generally tested by covering it with a clay backing and impacting it with the appropriate force. The armor performance is determined by measuring the depth of deformation in the clay caused by the vest's backface signature, assuming that the armor is not penetrated, which would result in vest failure. Studies of injury mechanisms caused by ballistic impact

The injuries that armor is designed to prevent must first be examined to comprehend how armor works. Because deeply penetrating dangers are uncommon, the human body is not built to withstand them; comparatively low-energy penetrating strikes can cause fatal injuries. While military standards suggest that 80 J of kinetic energy are needed to achieve a high probability of incapacitation from a small projectile, English law considers just over 1 J as the minimum threshold for lethality.24,25 The possibility of a big temporary wound cavity with severe tissue injury resulting from the very quick energy transfer following deep penetration is increased with high-velocity threats. On the other hand, the most vulnerable organs are located beneath the rib cage or the skull, meaning that the body is well protected from blunt hits. Even though a football has kinetic energy close to 200 J, it is generally considered to be safe. It is unclear whether energy criteria apply to completely dull collisions because the deadly mechanism is the produced acceleration. The human body has been demonstrated to tolerate up to 40 gravities in falls and auto accidents.25

Observations from the published papers show that bullet penetration must be prevented by the armor, and although trying to lessen the impact energy may have some benefits, it is not strictly required. Because of this, most of the armor is made to be resistant to penetration and does not require energy absorption. It is feared that non-penetrating bullets may injure people wearing soft body armor, leading to a condition known as BABT.26 Fig. 2 shows the plaster casts of backface deformation (BFD) onto a clay backing behind a textile armor showing a (A) pencilled impact and (B) blunt impact.Fig. 2Deformation mechanisms caused by the non-penetrating projectiles, pencilled impact (left) and blunt impact (right).26Fig. 2

Although there is a chance of significant behind blunt trauma, it is rare. It only occurs in a very limited number of exceptional situations where the armor has been hit at its edge or has been subjected to a very high-energy impact. There is some worry that the quest for more flexible and thinner armor could result in a scenario where the armor retains the bullet but is so flexible that it punctures the body, causing an injury known as a pencilling wound. This is treated as a narrow deformation of the armor which may results perforation of the skin and underlying tissue. In BABT experiments, a single "typical" pressure-time history is required.

The armor intends to prevent the penetration of the projectile that hits the wearer at very high velocity. During the impact severe shock wave is generated which travels back and forth within the armor and the body of the wearer. Generally, the projectiles are designed to enhance the kinetic energy delivered to and absorbed by the target. If the target is armored, the projectile will have to strain a lot to defeat the armor. To penetrate the target, the projectile needs higher kinetic energy, which must be concentrated over a small area. The degree of concentration of impact energy is known as kinetic energy density (KED), which is defined as the energy at impact divided by the projected area of the projectile. This is the best single measure of the penetration capability of the projectile. It is noted that a higher KED threat will require more penetration-resistant armor, and thus KED will be an important factor in finalising the nature of armor required to defeat the threat. During the projectile's impact, the impact energy may be spread to the target as a shock wave without causing injury to the wearer. To reduce the backface signature, constructions such as stiffened fabrics, padding, or even steel “shock plates” behind the basic fabric pack are being used. To further reduce the shock loads, a combination of composite plates with a ceramic facing and ballistic fabric backing layers can be used.

Studies on BABT injuries

When on missions in conflict areas, in asymmetric warfare scenarios, or in law enforcement operations like counterterrorism, military personnel face a wide range of threats. Small-calibre ammunition and pieces of high explosive and improvised explosive devices are the most common hazards, and they can be lethal to soldiers who are not protected. On the battlefield, penetrating wounds are the main cause of mortality. During military operations, the head contributes to 34% – 46% of all fatalities, although making up only 9% of the exposed body.27, 28, 29, 30, 31 Carey et al.29 looked at the wounds that soldiers inflicted on a limited group of injured soldiers during Operation Desert Storm in Iraq. He found that just 5% of the victims had been injured by bullets, while 95% had been struck by fragments. The rate of fragmentation (95%) is much higher than that of World War II, Korean War, and Vietnam War studies. During the second Palestinian revolt, known as the "Al-Aqsa Intifada", Lakstein et al.30 investigated Israeli Army deaths. The author revealed that most injuries were caused by bullets (63.5%) and fragments (14.6%). Penetrating trauma accounted for 83.3% of all injury mechanisms, while blunt trauma accounted for 8.3%. With 54.2% of all injuries, the head and neck regions were the most frequently injured body parts. This is detailed as a pie chart in Fig. 3. A retrospective review of combat deaths incurred by the United States (US) military personnel during the rescue operation was carried out by Mabry et al.28Fig. 3Distribution of injured body regions of Israeli soldiers on the second Palestinian revolt.Fig. 3

Carroll and Soderstrom32 conducted several initial studies on non-penetrating ballistic injuries. Research conducted on lab animals revealed that the liver, heart, spleen, and spinal cord remain susceptible to harm even when wearing soft body armor. An outstanding summary of high-velocity impact injury that occurs away from the impact site is given by Cooper and Taylor.9 The authors discovered a lack of discernible rebound velocity using high-speed photography, proving how highly damped or "viscous" the body is. This observation is significant because it shows that the displacement is rate-dependent for a system exhibiting both viscous (viscoelastic) and elastic behavior. A lot of researchers have investigated blunt trauma. Studies show that energy transfer from high velocity projectile was the primary cause of BABT.33 Animals have historically served as ballistic impact test subjects and can help determine the extent of a human being's injuries. The vest lessens the chance of a penetrating torso injury by dissipating impact energy. Even with the protection provided by the vest, there is still a chance of blunt trauma — also known as BABT arising from the collision.34 Cannon35 evaluated BABT in live sedated pigs. Different Kevlar fabric thicknesses were employed to cover the thoraco-abdominal area, and test bullets, including shotgun, 9 mm automatic, and 44 Magnum, were used to replicate ammunition used by both the military and civilian population. Another instance involved extremely serious injuries from a US No. 1 shot vs. 12-ply Kevlar. There were no rib fractures because of the trauma pack, although it did cause crush damage over a greater area. The trauma pack, however, was unable to stop potentially fatal lung injury. The animal was wounded by the shotgun with a more severe injury, but it was still deemed serious when hit by the 44 Magnum and the 36-ply Kevlar. It was conceivable that the animal hit by the 9 mm projectile and shielded by the 36-ply KEVLAR would make it. Thoracic damage caused by BABT has been characterized along with its mechanisms.36 The kind and degree of visceral damage resulting from a blunt hit are contingent on the amount and speed of deflection. An early study examined the pathophysiological effects of pistol ammunition (calibres 22 and 38) on goats that were wearing soft vests for protection. In another series of trials, these observations were associated with the impression created by ballistic plasticine put behind the vest.37

The BFD effects of spherical steel bullets striking dry human heads shielded by flat aluminum plates spaced 10 mm apart and packed with silicone gel — a brain simulator — were examined by Sarron et al.38 The authors observed that localised skull fractures were generated by the impact tests. To evaluate the damage, the silicone material's contact force and pressure were measured. In a different investigation,39 fresh human cadaver heads shielded by monolithic flat plates made of various materials were subjected to impact tests using 9 mm bullets. The authors investigated the injuries, such as fractures to the skull, brain damage, and skin abrasions. They found that the amount of damage to the head is influenced by both the protective material and the space between the skull and the plate. The severity of the skull injuries increased with smaller spacing. A minimum distance of 12.5 mm was suggested by them to avoid serious skull damage. They also found that intracranial pressure (ICP) may be utilized as an injury criterion because it is strongly associated with the likelihood of head injuries. Though this research shed light on behind-the-helmet blunt trauma (BHBT) mechanisms, the protective plates utilized are not at all like actual combat helmets because the impact response of the helmet is affected by its curvature at the impact area. Numerous studies have demonstrated that BFD is impacted by the helmet's curvature at the impact area.40, 41, 42

Studies on the influence of BABT on human organs

This section discusses the influence of BABT on several human organs, such as the skull, heart, lungs, and kidneys.

Skull damage

It is important to remember that bullets and fragments pose the biggest hazard to soldiers on the front lines, that wounds suffered by them can be both non-penetrating and penetration-related, and that head injuries are common. Therefore, head protection is essential. By protecting the head with a battle helmet, impact penetrating injuries will be less likely and more often result in head blunt trauma, or BHBT.43,44 Fig. 3 shows the components of a combat helmet. Sadly, there is a chance that BHBT will result in serious or even deadly injuries. Combat helmets are made to keep military troops safe from serious or fatal head injuries during impact events by neutralising incoming threats (blast, ballistic, or blunt threats). Combat helmet makers use new materials with a higher strength-to-weight ratio to obtain optimum protection for the least amount of weight while still ensuring the soldier's comfort and preventing damage during operations. A kind of blunt trauma known as BHBT is defined as an artificially occurring, non-penetrating head injury brought on by incidents involving helmet impacts. Both in the military and the civilian spheres, blunt trauma is a serious issue.45 Events with a high or low dynamic effect can be the reason.

Damages, such as bone fractures, skin abrasions or contusions, concussions, haemorrhaging, hematomas (clots of blood), diffuse axonal injury, and anoxic injury (low oxygen), which damage brain neurons, are the most common blunt traumas.47 Since the skull serves as the head's protective structure, blunt skull injuries have the potential to harm the brain. Brain injuries, also known as traumatic brain injuries, are caused by direct loads on the skull or by head kinematics because of the contact of the skull and the brain.48, 49, 50 It may result in anatomical trauma, like brain injury, or functional trauma, such as cognitive disability. Either the side of the impact (coup) or the reverse side (contrecoup) may experience it. There are several classification schemes for brain injuries.51,52 The injuries are, it is possible to sustain open or closed wounds, focal or diffuse injuries, or primary or secondary injuries. When the head is physically loaded, primary injuries happen right away, but secondary injuries depend on how severe the original injuries are and manifest themselves minutes to days later. Macroscopic damage that is essentially restricted to a localized and precise area is what defines focal injuries, as opposed to diffuse injuries that are dispersed throughout a larger area.53 Open head injuries are produced by damage to the head brought on by a piercing instrument, as opposed to closed injuries, which are non-penetrating.

Many biomechanical investigations on blunt impacts have investigated the injury criteria and accompanying thresholds (tolerance) to fracture or traumatic brain injury incidence, utilising post-mortem human subjects (PMHS) or animals. Physical variables used to predict brain lesions include impact force or maximal head force, ICP, head accelerations (linear or rotational), strain, stress, and energy.54, 55, 56, 57 Weisenbach et al.57 used the average impact peak force to assess the fracture risk for temporoparietal strikes in their PMHS testing. The maximum fracture-causing force was (8.0 ± 3.2) kN. Using the Bass injury model, Anctil et al.58 conducted impact tests with a headform and discovered that a value of 4.3 kN predicted a 50% chance of fracture damage. In the context of North Atlantic Treaty Organization standardisation procedures for non-lethal impact evaluation, a maximum impact force of 3.6 kN was estimated for a low risk of fracture and 7.4 kN for a high risk of fracture.59 Bass et al.60 developed a skull injury risk model to evaluate the risk of fracture caused by the impact of a 9 mm full metal jacket on a PMHS while wearing a helmet. They discovered that a 50% likelihood of a skull injury is equivalent to an impact pressure of 51.2 MPa. Sarron et al.61 estimated a 50% likelihood of major fracture in silicone-filled skulls at 560 kPa. In contrast, the lowest value of 150 kPa is reported for non-lethal strikes resulting in serious fracture injuries. The absence of the scalp and features in human cadaveric skull subjects could explain the difference. Freitas et al.62 studied the likelihood of skull injuries in studies where a surrogate was shielded by either an appliqué armor helmet or a helmet alone. They found a correlation between the dynamic peak stresses of the skull and cranial fracturing. For values less than 1740 με, no or a small skull fracture was generally seen. Principal stresses and shear strains have been found by Raymond et al.63 to be predictive of skull fracture in their investigation of the skull's resistance to blunt ballistic temporoparietal impact. A primary strain value of 5062 με was reported by them, indicating a 50% chance of skull fractures. This value, even for catastrophic skull trauma, is higher than the values reported.64

For a brain injury, there might be a relationship, for instance, between the impact force and ICP, and many injury criteria that were employed for skull injuries are still applicable to internal injuries.65 One of the most widely utilized injury criteria is the ICP. In their protected human cadaver head impact studies, Sarron et al.61 discovered a relationship between brain injury incidence and the magnachamber's ICP. ICP increased in response to an increase in impact force. The results indicate that when the ICP value was higher than 191 kPa, serious brain injury happened. The Wayne State Tolerance Curve, which is based on forehead impact tests performed on cadavers and animals, was used to establish the most extensively used head injury criterion.66, 67, 68, 69, 70 The head's centre of gravity serves as the evaluation point for the linear acceleration-based head injury criterion. If the helmet has a big back face distortion that is likely to make contact with the head, a head injury may occur due to the limited space (stand-off distance) between the head and the interior of the helmet. Newer helmet models use pad suspension systems that reduce blunt head injuries. Unfortunately, they are not intended to survive ballistic hits. They are commonly used as energy absorption materials in the design of personal protective equipment for falls, parachute impact situations, sports, bicycles, and the automotive industry.71,72

Damage mechanism for the heart

Non-penetrating cardiac injury was first documented in the 17th century, but it would take another 4 centuries for the first effective healing to occur.73 Comprehending blunt cardiac injury (BCI) in its entirety is a difficult clinical task. This problem stems from a lack of precise diagnostic standards and is made worse by an inconsistent grading scheme. To characterise BCI, the American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) developed an injury scale.74 This grading is constrained, according to the AAST, by the range of injuries and the absence of diagnostic testing. This grading scale offers some language to talk about these injuries, but it has not been extensively validated against mortality. The body of literature's best evidence points to a BCI incidence of 3% to 76% for trauma patients.75 Minor injuries cannot show any symptoms. Because severe injuries can present as a very morbid constellation of symptoms, it is impossible to determine the true incidence. According to autopsy findings, prehospital mortality for serious cardiac damage could reach 95%.76 Three mechanisms exist for blunt trauma to cause myocardial lesions: (1) direct energy transfer during the impact on the thorax; (2) fast cardiac deceleration; and (3) squeezing of the heart in between the sternum and the spine.

Myocardial lesions can be classified as "myocardial commotion" or "myocardial contusion," even though there is a continuum between these 2 conditions.77,78 Myocardial contusion79,80 is one of the most frequent lesions; it causes pathological alterations akin to those of acute infarction, with the additional danger of arrhythmias and heart failure.81,82

One of the main coronary arteries83,84 is rarely directly damaged. More severe wounds may result in structural alterations, such as septal rupture and harm to the chordae tendineae or papillary muscles, which would result in valvular insufficiency.84,85

Right ventricular or atrial rips can occasionally result in cardiac tamponade as noted by Patton et al.86 Although they are uncommon, many more cardiac abnormalities can happen as a result of forceful trauma. Less than 1% of cases have valve abnormalities, which primarily affect the aortic and mitral valves. Even less frequently, ventricular and atrial septal abnormalities arise in cases of thoracic blunt trauma. Chirillo et al.87 observed that in fewer than 2% of instances in the autopsy series, coronary artery lesions (laceration, thrombosis, dissection of the ostium) were discovered. Emergency coronary angiography and surgery should follow a clear doubt of myocardial infarction (Troponin I concentration measurement, echo, and electrocardiography) in a young trauma patient. Hemopericardium with or without myocardial lesions and pericardial rupture with potential cardiac herniation can result from pericardial trauma.

As discussed earlier, more than 90% of all cardiac lesions are caused by myocardial trauma, which is elucidated even by ballistic studies. Using high-speed blunt projectiles on anaesthetized pigs, it was discovered that 54% of the animals had areas of contusion, with 29% having ruptures. There were several contusions in regions that appeared random as well as close to the coronary arteries.88 In subsequent articles, the authors conjectured that the distribution of contusions was not caused by direct trauma but rather by the propagation of stress and shear waves.9,89 Blunt chest trauma (BCT) can cause damage to the thoracic aorta and its arch divisions in addition to the coronary arteries. The forces operating on the vessel and its degree of displacement determine the type and location of the damage. Because it connects the somewhat mobile aortic arch to the stationary descending aorta, the aortic isthmus is especially vulnerable to damage. Prêtre et al.90 observed that 80% – 90% of patients with thoracic aortic injuries from BCT have isthmic damage. The most serious and potentially fatal consequence of BCT on the heart, aside from myocardial rupture, is early dysrhythmias. In pig models used in experiments by Link et al.91 ventricular fibrillation and temporary total cardiac block are the 2 most frequent conditions. Variable, brief intervals of "cardiac standstill" are frequently observed, and they don't seem to cause any appreciable morbidity. The more common one, ventricular fibrillation, is also the one that is most likely to be fatal if defibrillation is not tried. A multitude of further rhythm irregularities might occur if the human patient makes it through the initial blow. These consist of sinus bradycardia, bundle branch blockages, atrial fibrillation, and premature ventricular contractions. These side effects always manifest during the first 12 h following the impact. Bass et al.92 investigated the ballistic deformation behaviour of the thorax (Fig. 5) in the presence of armor. High-speed dynamic flash X-ray was used to measure the deformation behaviour. It is noted that, when the projectile penetrated from 2/3 to 3/4 the thickness of the armor, the armor backface was deformed approximately 30 – 40 mm into the subject's thorax. In addition, the authors investigated the single mid-spinal impact using a 7.62 mm round projectile of velocity 700 m/s. Injuries received from the spinal BFD are shown in Fig. 6. Damage mechanism for lungs

While a variety of pulmonary injuries can occur after bone marrow transplantation, lung contusions are the most common, if not pathognomonic, lesion. Before, some reports that surfaced during and after World War II, because of investigations into the impact of blast waves, contusions received little attention.93,94 Localized hemorrhage and edema in the alveoli and interstitial spaces are the typical histological findings.95 Wagner et al.96 employed CT to analyze clinical instances of blunt cardiac trauma, deviating from this histological definition. They discovered that lacerations of the alveoli or alveolar septae were the fundamental component of most lung contusions. Stress waves and tensile pressures across the alveoli have been identified as the causes of this epithelial-endothelial disruption by Liu et al.97 Fung et al.98 observed that up to 26% of instances with substantial blunt cardiac trauma linked to pulmonary contusions are complicated by acute respiratory distress syndrome. Initially identified in World War II,99 the illness has been referred to by several names, such as traumatic wet lung and contusion pneumonitis. In 5% – 10% of instances, pneumonia may complicate blunt cardiac trauma; if a coexisting flail segment is present, this number rises to 85%.100 On the other hand, after substantial chest damage, the incidence is less than 5%. By causing a decreased ability to cough and so clear secretions and by directly lowering the clearance of microorganisms, pulmonary contusions may predispose to lung infection.101

Chest wall injuries, which also include rib fractures, are the most frequent in blunt thoracic trauma cases. Not only should rib fractures be taken into consideration when discussing bone fractures, but also clavicle and sternum fractures. Demirhan et al.102 conducted a study on 4205 trauma patients and showed that chest wall injuries, such as rib, sternum and clavicle fractures, were 36.1% of chest trauma injuries. In 17,308 hospitalized patients with rib fractures, the average death rate was 4%, according to research by Brasel et al.103 The mortality rate is higher when the first 2 ribs are fractured, as it is strongly attached to the musculoskeletal system. First rib fractures carry a significant risk of tracheobronchial, vascular, cardiac, and pulmonary damage as observed by Richardson et al.104

Rib fractures are a significant injury associated with chest trauma, but other thoracic bone structures should also be considered. Dogrul et al.106 noted that the most common cause of sternal fractures in auto accidents is anterior BCT from the steering wheel. A sternal fracture may result in cardiac and pulmonary contusions, as well as vascular damage. When a sternal fracture occurs, cardiac enzymes and electrocardiography should be assessed.107,108 Sternal fractures can be associated with rib fractures, pulmonary contusion, pneumothorax, haemothorax, and even brain injuries, spine fractures, and trauma to the extremities.109 A recent study,110 showed that 2.1% of trauma center admissions involve sternal fractures. Half of patients with sternal fractures are elderly, and their mortality rate is greater. Half of the patients with sternal fractures have concurrent rib fractures, and one-third have vertebral fractures. Increased mortality and pulmonary co-injuries are predicted by combined sternal fractures. Additional considerations for diagnosis and treatment are necessary due to the high frequency of concurrent rib and vertebral fractures.

Additionally, Milisavljević et al.111 inferred that another frequent complication with blunt trauma is tissue contusions in the chest wall. These small occurrences are not frequently reported because they don't result in significant issues. However, by damaging blood vessels in the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and muscles, contusion and abrasion of the chest wall tissue can result in bleeding.112 This kind of bleeding can be troublesome and chronic in people with severe trauma.113

Damage mechanism for the kidney

Renal trauma may induce bleeding or damage to the collecting system, which may result in urine leakage, or it may cause damage to the parenchyma or renal vasculature. In general, genitourinary tract injuries are uncommon (10% of all traumas), with the kidney being the most often impacted organ according to Baghdanian et al.114 Of all trauma patients, 1% – 5% have renal trauma; 80% – 90% of these cases are caused by blunt abdominal trauma.115, 116, 117 Penetrating renal trauma is less common, although depending on the area and population served, it can occur in as many as 20% of cases. The blast impact is more dangerous when it comes to delicate tissues like the renal parenchyma and is primarily observed in parenchymal organs. Still, some variables reduce the effect, like the kidneys' strong ligaments and thick fatty tissue surrounding them. Patients with renal fragmentation require nephrectomy, partial nephrectomy, or renography, depending on the blast effect.118,119 Cass et al.120 discovered that angiograms and CT can show that blunt trauma results in intimal injury and thrombosis in the renal arteries at a rate of 2.3% – 9.3%. Although the exact modifications that take place in the renal arteries are unknown, blast impacts commonly lead to renal fragmentation. Patients with renal fragmentation who have undergone renography may experience hypertension or renal arterial stenosis in the postoperative phase.121

Summary and outlook

Armor plates are employed in battle to shield personnel, military tanks, automobiles, parts of helicopters, patrol boats, and movable shelters from projectiles' impact. In line with the NIJ Standard 0101.04, the greatest deformation a soft armor vest can withstand without penetration is 44 mm. Conversely, this does not account for the influence of the pressure wave or energy transported to the organs of the torso as a result of BABT. BABT is a non-penetrating injury caused by the fast deformation of body armor. The impact of a bullet or projectile on an armor's front face distorts the surface as it encounters the body. The deformation occurs during the process of capturing the projectile by absorbing energy and retarding it. In severe cases, even if the projectile does not penetrate the armor, the BABT can still cause death or injury. The brain, heart, kidney, lung, and spleen are the organs that are most affected by BABT.

The situations discussed highlight the significance of creating innovative methods to reduce this energy transfer, even though a thorough examination of the components and design of protective body armor is outside the purview of this article. Techniques like employing air holes inside the armor,122 adding soft layers,123 and supporting the solid body armor components124 have been discussed.
