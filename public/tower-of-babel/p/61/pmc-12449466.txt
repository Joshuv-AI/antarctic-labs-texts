Response to: Survival after risk-reducing mastectomy in young BRCA1 carriers with breast cancer
Authors: Virginia Delucchi, Matteo Lambertini, Eva Blondeaux
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe address methodological considerations in evaluating risk-reducing mastectomy among young BRCA carriers with prior history of breast cancer, emphasizing time-dependent analysis, realistic assumptions on mortality and contralateral breast cancer risk, and appropriate confounder adjustment as ways to improve accuracy in survival outcome estimates.

We read with interest the comment by Vasily Giannakeas and Steven Narod [1] on our recent article evaluating the association between risk-reducing surgeries and survival outcomes in young BRCA carriers with history of breast cancer at a young age [2].

While simulations such as those suggested by Vasily Giannakeas and Steven Narod [1] provide useful theoretical insights, they are inherently dependent on assumptions regarding risk rates, mortality, and efficacy. These assumptions, particularly a fixed doubling of mortality following a controlateral breast cancer and a 100% reduction in contralateral breast cancer among patients undergoing risk-reducing mastectomy (RRM), may not fully capture the complexity of the clinical trajectories of BRCA carriers. Mortality after contralateral breast cancer is strongly dependent on several factors and the uptake of RRM is not 100% effective in preventing the development of contralateral breast cancer [3]. In our cohort of young BRCA carriers, 42 (1.4%) out of 2910 patients developed contralateral breast cancer following RRM. Furthermore, the described simulated analysis [1] seems to assume that RRM occurs at the same time as breast cancer diagnosis, while we would like to emphasize the importance of treating RRM as a time-dependent variable, given that patients can undergo RRM at any time during the follow-up. While median time from breast cancer diagnosis to RRM was 0.8 (IQR 0.5–2.7) years in our analysis, this interval was longer up to 2.4 years in previous studies [4–6]. Therefore, we believe that it is not appropriate to model two fixed cohorts (RRM vs. no RRM) from the start of the beginning of observation (i.e., breast cancer diagnosis). Indeed, until the moment the patient undergoes RRM, the risk of breast cancer mortality is comparable to that of a patient in the no RRM group.

Importantly, unlike what was stated by Vasily Giannakeas and Steven Narod [1], we would like to clarify that the hazard ratio reported in our study does not refer to breast cancer-specific mortality, but rather to overall survival. Considering the very young age at breast cancer diagnosis in our cohort, breast cancer-related cause of death accounted for the majority of the observed overall survival events. Specifically, out of the 686 death events, only 54 (8%) were from causes other than breast cancer.

The authors pointed out that “women who experience a distant recurrence in the follow-up period are less likely to undergo a subsequent RRM” [1]. This implies that distant recurrence is associated with the exposure (i.e., RRM), without being a consequence of it. Moreover, experiencing a distant recurrence during follow-up is clearly associated with the outcome (i.e., overall survival). Therefore, by definition, distant recurrence meets the criteria for being a confounder [7]. If confounding is not properly controlled for through statistical adjustment, the estimated effect of the exposure on the outcome may be overestimated, underestimated, or even reversed. Therefore, we strongly believe it was appropriate to include distant recurrence (as a time-dependent variable) among the covariates in the adjustment model [2], in order to provide an estimate that is as accurate and unbiased as possible. Nevertheless, to take into account authors’ comment [1], we have re-run the analysis on the association between RRM and OS by removing distant recurrence from the covariates in the multivariable analysis: the hazard ratio further decreased to 0.54 (95% CI 0.45–0.65). However, as previously stated, this estimate is likely to be biased.

From a clinical perspective, it is important to stress that our results, with the limitations of deriving from a retrospective observational study, apply to a special cohort of BRCA carriers, i.e., those that developed breast cancer at age 40 years or less [8, 9]. Considering that both young age at breast cancer diagnosis and carrying germline BRCA pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants are critical risk factors for developing a second primary breast cancer, we believe that, based on the results of our study, the counseling on RRM suggested by the guidelines [10] should be further emphasized in this special cohort of BRCA carriers. Additional prospective research efforts, including also women’s perception, acceptability and quality of life will be critical to optimize the shared decision-making processes.
