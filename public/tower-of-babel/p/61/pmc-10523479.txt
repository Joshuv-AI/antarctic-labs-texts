Photonic crystals built by time in ancient Roman glass
Authors: Giulia Guidetti, Roberta Zanini, Giulia Franceschin, Mauro Moglianetti, Taehoon Kim, Nathaniel Cohan, Lisa Chan, John Treadgold, Arianna Traviglia, Fiorenzo G. Omenetto
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe study of the hierarchical arrangement of a material and its optical properties provides valuable information about its assembly and building blocks. Here, an archaeological glass fragment from the Roman times was analyzed using a correlated optical-morphological and elemental approach. The analysis revealed a highly reflective metallic patina composed of highly ordered nanostructured domains resembling Bragg-stacks. The study of this patina provides insights into the comodulation of self-assembly and pH-driven nanofabrication processes, involving top–down glass corrosion and bottom–up nano-to-micro-scale structuring of silica nanoparticles into ordered lamellae. This allowed for the observation of phenomena across different temporal windows that are not accessible through existing artificial aging methods.

The cultural and economic advancement of a society can often be associated with the technological sophistication of its daily objects. Insights on ancient civilizations can come by studying artifacts that have been, fully or partially, preserved till modern times (1). Glass is an archetype of innovation as its shape, as well as its composition, optical appearance, and use, reflect not only an artisan’s craftmanship but also the level of technological advancements in materials’ production of ancient civilizations (1, 2). The manufacturing of the first glass artifacts represents a paradigm shift in ingenuity and technology, turning a mixture of molten silica, limestone, and soda into a versatile material with both technical and artistic applications that addressed the diverse needs of ancient societies and influenced their evolution (2). The impact of mass production and commodification of glass is considered one of the first examples of globalization (3), affecting the trade and economic development of ancient cities. Today, glass is as essential as it is ubiquitous yet, early in its history, it was an exclusive material that signified status and was often regarded as bling for Kings (2, 4). Ancient glass is still precious because it incorporates essential details about the history and the long-term alteration of the material (5). Glass artifacts recovered from archeological excavations are often transformed into corrosion products in the form of iridescent patinas that cover the unaltered bulk glass (6–10). The composition and the hierarchical arrangement of these characteristic iridescences are driven by the interplay between the glass’ original constituents and the chemical properties of the soil surrounding the glass, modulated by the pH of the local solution in contact with the glass and the amount of groundwater in the soil (11). Therefore, detailed studies of ancient glass fragments open a window on the technological, cultural, and societal legacy of the artifact’s historical era through its interaction with the surrounding environment (1, 12), while also improving the confidence in long-term glass alteration predicting models in geological environments which are useful for parametrizing artificial glass aging (13, 14).

Generally, the corrosion patinas found on glass are formed by slow degradation of the bulk materials through proton leaching (replacement of alkali metal ions with protons from the water the glass is in contact with) (15) and silica dissolution and reprecipitation into nanoparticles (16–18). These pH-dependent transformations induce a restructuring of glass into nano- to micrometer-thick lamellae formed by nanoparticles with regularly alternating packing density, which give ancient glass its characteristic iridescent patina. So far, structural coloration in silica-based ancient artifacts has been documented as 3D opaline structures (19), often with a milky appearance due to scattering, and as thin-film interference patinas with inconspicuous coloration and low-range order (6, 7, 10).

Results and Discussion

We present here the observation and analysis of a Roman glass fragment that exhibits distinctive broadband reflectance and bright iridescence. Analysis of the glass reveals a hierarchical structure (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Figs. S1 and S2) composed of stacks of highly ordered nanostructured domains that individually behave like high-reflectivity Bragg stacks. Their collective behavior results in a remarkable, mirror-like gold reflection that is evidence of the transformation of an amorphous material into a highly ordered photonic crystal by centuries-long glass corrosion processes and silica nanoparticles self-assembly. The observed morphology is consistent with previously reported glass corrosion and reshaping mechanisms observed in historical glass (6, 7, 10), yet it presents a higher order degree and a substantially higher number of nanolayers that result in the brilliant metallic appearance.

The Roman glass fragment was retrieved from the clay-rich plowed soil in the outskirts of the ancient city of Aquileia (Italy) during an archaeological field-walking survey. Chemical analysis obtained from laser ablation mass spectroscopy (LA-ICP-MS) confirms the typical silica–soda–lime composition of glass produced during the Roman period (SI Appendix, Table S1) with dating between the first century B.C. and the first century A.D. Today, the glass fragment exhibits a conspicuous optical appearance due to the presence of a millimeter-thick, strongly reflecting, structurally colored metallic patina that covers the original bulk glass; this suggests that the fragment is in an altered state compared to its optical and morphological properties at the time of manufacturing. The original extremely dark green coloration of the glass is still preserved in the bulk of the fragment (Fig. 1).

The patina conformally adheres to the bulk glass (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) and has a bright metallic appearance characterized by a collection of micrometer-scale concave domains randomly distributed over the glass surface, similar to what is observed in silicate materials weathered under alkaline conditions (6, 17) (Fig. 1). The patina itself is the result of the glass transformation through dissolution and reprecipitation processes that occur at the alteration front which is the interface between the solid and the dissolved glass.

Under mild alkaline conditions (pH varying between 7 and 10) glass weathering induces the transformation of the amorphous glass network into a micro- and nanostructured, hierarchical colloidal material primarily composed of silica (11, 16, 17). Specifically, the local increase in the concentration of basic species at the alteration front induces a series of pH-driven dissolution and reprecipitation processes (16–18) that result in the nucleation of silica nanoparticles on the surface of the glass (Fig. 2). These nanoparticles further grow and coalesce to form nanolamellae that conformally adhere to the glass surface. Cyclic pH conditions direct the nanoparticles’ packing into nanolamellae of alternating high and low density (17).

Macroscopically, the patina portions directly in contact with the bulk glass appear strongly adhered and reflecting blue light, while the outermost portions have a golden appearance and tend to flake off. Cross-sectional imaging of the patina reveals a hierarchical structure composed of the regular arrangement of micrometer-thick layers, which upon further analysis by SEM displays highly regular stacks, each containing tens of nanolamellae (Fig. 1). The overall number of nanolamellae forming the patina is estimated to be well over a thousand. This multilayer structure is composed of vertically stacked nanolamellae with alternating density (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), that create a periodic variation of the refractive index at the nanoscale. The regularity of this multilayer system determines its reflective properties, behaving like a Bragg stack (20) that strongly reflects a narrowband range of wavelengths depending on its periodicity. The vertical stacking of tens of such Bragg reflectors of different periodicity causes the simultaneous reflection of a broad range of wavelengths, resulting in the overall reflective golden appearance of the fragment (Fig. 1).

The Roman glass fragment was then characterized in detail with optical and electron microscopy (Fig. 2). Different portions (i.e., flakes) of the patina that spontaneously exfoliated and that display narrowband reflectance at different center wavelengths (i.e., blue, green, and red) were selected to quantify their optical response. Electron microscopy imaging of the single-colored flakes in cross-section shows a Bragg stack geometry consisting of a highly ordered arrangement of high-density nanolamellae alternating with thin layers of lower-density lamellae (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Figs. S2 andS3).

Reflectance microscopy coupled with spectral analysis shows reflectance values that are exceptionally high for a naturally occurring structure (Raverage ~ 80% with respect to a silver mirror standard). Each single Bragg stack taken individually exhibits a relatively narrowband reflection, with uniform color across each flake (Fig. 2), further supporting the regularity observed in the nanolamellae thickness and in their assembled structure.

The thickness d of the nanolamellae in each Bragg stack increases progressively as the flakes are further from the bulk glass, resulting in a “redshift” of the flakes with increasing patina thickness. The measured thicknesses of the high-density nanolamellae for the different colored flakes are dblue ~ (116 ± 24) nm, dgreen ~ (235 ± 38) nm, and dred ~(290 ± 30) nm (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). The thickness of the lower density lamellae is around 40–60 nm with pores of diameter up to ~ (39 ± 16) nm. The unusually high regularity found in this archaeological fragment can also be appreciated by noting the near-textbook spectral response of several portions of the flakes which have reflection curves with symmetrical interference fringes on the sides of the main peak (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

Spontaneously flaked-off portions of the patina with broadband golden reflectance were then analyzed. On average, all the golden flakes have strong reflectance (R ~ 80–100%) across a broad range of wavelengths (λ > 550 nm) with small differences in intensities and spectral peaks attributed to the natural variability of the silica nanolamellae (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Structurally, gold portions of the patina are made of multiple layers of the narrowband Bragg stacks described above. The observed broadband gold reflective appearance arises from the collective effect of the individual narrowband Bragg reflectors. The high number of Bragg stacks forming the free-standing golden patinas ensures high-intensity reflectance (Fig. 3). The progressive increase in the thickness d of the nanolamellae moving away from the bulk makes the overall structure similar to a chirped mirror. The golden appearance of this weathered glass is not only apparent through measurement (SI Appendix, Figs. S6–S8) but it is also strongly visible by the naked eye (Figs. 1 and 3).

Remarkably, the layers of Bragg stacks can also be identified with optical microscopy. Dark-field reflection microscopy of the gold patinas shows almost colorless layers (SI Appendix, Fig. S8); the limited scattering is caused either by particulate deposited on the outermost layers or by the multilayer edges (Fig. 1). The negligible scattering from the patina further supports the high regularity of the nanolamellar silica assembly.

The photonic crystal nature of the sample is further underscored by the strong angular dependence of the patina’s interaction with light. Free-standing portions of the golden patina appear decisively golden when observed in reflection normally to the multilayer stack (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7), show blue color in transmission (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S7), and have a white appearance when observed from the side, parallel to each layer (Fig. 3). This observed angular dependence (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S9) is consistent with a highly regular photonic structure for which a blueshift of the reflected light is observed when increasing the observation angle with respect to the normal to the sample. Observation in cross-section shows, instead, no structural coloration and the patina appears white because of the predominant effect of scattering from the silica nanoparticles. Both the chirped golden patinas and the metallic blue patina behave like concave mirrors (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S10); positioning an object in front of a concave Bragg stack can result in the formation of a slightly distorted, reduced real image of the object. This optical response was proved by placing a small light bulb (~2 cm in length) in front of the golden patina and by capturing its reflection; the reduced image of the bulb was visible on each smooth concave patina (~100 s of μm in diameter) that contains the collection of narrowband Bragg reflectors described earlier (Fig. 3).

The porous nature of the individual Bragg stacks formed by alternating low- and high-density domains allows infiltration within the structure to tune its optical response. Specifically, upon infiltration of a blue-reflecting flake with water, the reflected light is seen to rapidly redshift, before returning to the initial coloration upon complete water evaporation (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). When the patina fragments are fully immersed in water, the photonic response in the visible range is suppressed, thus revealing the actual color of the stacked nanolamellae without the colors generated by interference with visible light (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). Under these conditions, the fragments appear largely transparent; the lack of the bulk glass green coloration in the patina is attributed to the glass transformation that results in the nanolamellae being composed mostly by silica (16, 17) (SI Appendix, Table S1). Traces of iron and other elements that are found in unaltered portions of the glass fragment as modifiers and/or chromophores are also present within the lamellae (SI Appendix, Table S1) but in such quantities that they do not contribute to their spectral response. This demonstrates that the visible color of flakes is not chromophore-dependent, which would have otherwise resulted in the green appearance of the weathered patina upon water infiltration.

Further analysis of the weathered patina with energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy coupled to SEM and laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA-ICP-MS) allows investigating the composition of the nanostructured portions of the glass compared to the bulk (Fig. 4). The SEM–EDX elemental analysis (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S13) reveals a periodic chemical modulation in the direction normal to the nanolamellae plane as alternating silicon and sodium layers. Stronger silicon signal corresponds to the high-density lamellae and stronger sodium signal to the low-density regions. Overall, each type of weathered glass lamella (either high or low density) has a regular composition within a single Bragg stack (16, 21, 22) (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). This is qualitatively supported by the uniform intensity of the back-scattered electron (BSE) signal where the reduced intensity of the low-density lamellae arises from a combination of an enriched presence of lower atomic number material (i.e., ZNa=11 for low-density nanolamellae compared to ZSi=14 for high-density nanolamellae) and the presence of air nanopores (Fig. 4).

LA-ICP-MS was carried out to monitor the elemental composition of the Roman glass as a function of depth (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Table S1). Three-dimensional elemental maps of selected elements (silicon, sodium, calcium, and iron) were generated to determine the elements’ leaching profiles and their relative concentration across altered and unaltered portions of the glass fragment (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S15). The patina was found to be enriched in silica and depleted of other cations as compared to the bulk glass (Fig. 4 and SI Appendix, Table S1). Silica appears to be more concentrated on the surface of the bulk glass fragment due to the solubilization and depletion of alkali and alkaline-earth ions, while hydrated silica gel forms and accumulates on the surface of the patina. On the contrary, sodium and iron depth distributions suggest different leaching behaviors. Sodium’s map shows that the concentration of alkaline ions decreases moving from the bulk of the glass fragments toward the altered glass, and it appears more homogeneous at higher depths, closer to the bulk (SI Appendix, Fig. S15); this leaching profile is expected, being sodium the most mobile ion in soda–silica–lime glass. Similarly, the iron distribution increases with depth, yet not to the extent observed for sodium. This difference in the modifiers leaching profiles reflects the preferential dissolution of more soluble cations during the leaching process (23) caused by their intrinsic diffusion coefficients. Additionally, the 3D volume reconstructions clearly show the accumulation of silica and the corresponding depletion of modifiers and chromophores at the surface of the patina. Consistently with what was previously reported for glass corrosion (16–18), the patina formation appears to be driven by the depletion of both formers and modifier elements and by the consequent precipitation of hydrated nanosilica at the glass surface.

Conclusion

The unusually high reflection and hierarchical structure of this ancient glass fragment offers an example of the high-order level that can be achieved in natural structures through self-assembly processes. Also, this highly reflective patina consists of a rare example of a self-assembly, pH-driven fabrication process that involves top–down processes (glass network dissolution) followed by bottom–up nano-to-micro-scale structuring (silica nanoparticles nucleation, growth, and assembly in ordered Bragg stacks). The characterization of this Roman glass fragment opens new perspectives on the insights and inspiration provided by analyzing ancient materials at the nanoscale, enabling observations of phenomena occurring across temporal windows that are otherwise challenging to replicate with currently available artificial aging approaches.

Materials and Methods

Materials.

The studied Roman glass fragment (inventory number 591681) belongs to Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Aquileia. The fragment was found on ploughsoil during an archaeological field-walking survey in the outskirts of the ancient city of Aquileia (Italy) in 2012. The LA-ICP-MS measurement was carried out with the permission of Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Aquileia to perform microinvasive analysis. The ablated area on the surface of sample after the analysis is not visible to the naked eye.

Macroscopic Photographs.

Digital images of the glass fragments were taken with a DSLR (digital single-lens reflex) camera (Canon Rebel EOS-SL1 and Canon EOS 5DS). The RAW image files were adjusted for exposure and contrast.

Optical Microscopy and Microspectroscopy.

Optical microscopy and microspectroscopy were performed on a customized Olympus upright Olympus BX5 microscope coupled with a DSLR (digital single-lens reflex) camera (Canon Rebel EOS-T1i) using a halogen lamp (U-LH100L-3) as a light source in Koehler illumination. To perform microspectroscopy, the microscope was coupled to a spectrometer (Ocean Optics, USB2000) using an optical fiber (either 100-µm or 200-µm core size). The reflectance spectra were normalized against a silver mirror (Thorlabs, PF10-03-P01, avg. reflectance >97.5% for λ = 450–2,000 nm). The microscope was set up so that the numerical aperture (NA) in illumination was limited by the NA of the microscope which was set to the minimum. The glass fragments were analyzed in i) bright-field reflection mode using a 20× objective (Olympus, MPlan N, 20x, NA 0.4, WD 1.3 mm) to collect the reflectance signal that originated only from ordered domains; ii) dark-field reflection mode using a 20× objective (Olympus, MPlan N, 20×, NA 0.4, WD 1.3 mm) to collect the light scattered in correspondence of defects; and iii) in bright-field transmission to collect the light transmitted through the multilayer. For the imaging performed at 20× magnification, the NA was determined by the microscope (NA microscope fully closed to minimize scattering), while for the imaging at 5 × (Olympus, MPlan N, 5 ×, NA 0.4, WD 1.3 mm), the NA was limited by the objective (NA microscope fully open to counterbalance the low NA intrinsic to the objective). Refractive index variation studies were performed in bright-field reflection with the same objective (Olympus, MPlan N, 20×, NA 0.1, WD 20 mm) with imaging and microspectroscopy collected as described above. Double-deionized droplets of water were deposited on the glass fragments using a syringe and the evaporation occurred at room temperature (T ~ 20 °C) and humidity (RH ~ 15%). An Olympus SZ STU-2 stereo microscope equipped with a DSLR (digital single-lens reflex) camera (Canon Rebel EOS-T1i) using a ring illuminator (Edmund Optics, MI-150) as a light source was used to capture the concave mirror-like behavior of the flakes.

Microspectroscopy.

The iridescent behavior of the samples was measured using a bespoke goniometer equipped with a lamp (Tungsten-Halogen illuminator, LS-1, Ocean optics) and a spectrometer (USB2000, 200–800 nm, Ocean Optics) (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). The sample was mounted on a rotating stage and illuminated by a 200-μm core fiber (P200-2-VIS-NIR, Ocean Optics) coupled to the lamp at θin incident angle with respect to the normal to the sample. The reflected light was collected by a 600-μm core fiber (P600-2-VIS-NIR, Ocean Optics) coupled into the spectrometer and mounted on a manual rotating detector arm, concentrically aligned to the sample stage. To measure the angular reflection spectrum, the sample stage was manually rotated by θin, (with θin being the angle that the incident ray makes with respect to the normal of the sample stage). The detector arm was then rotated by an angle θout= θin with respect to the normal of the sample (2θin with respect to the incident beam). The angular dependence of the reflection of the sample was measured at increments of 5°, ranging from θin = 20° to θin = 60°. The recorded light intensity was normalized with respect to a silver mirror (PF10-03-P01, Thorlabs). The beam spot size varied between 0.835 mm2 (θin=20°) and 1.57 mm2 (θin=60°). The collected spectra were smoothed using the software OceanView (Ocean Optics, boxcar width = 5) and normalized using the software Origin Pro2019.

Electron Microscopy.

The free-standing glass patinas were mounted on aluminum stubs by using carbon tape. To ensure electrical conductivity, the stubs were sputtered with ~10 nm of gold using an Emitech SC7620 sputter coater. The top view and the cross-sections of the fragments were imaged using a ZEISS EVO MA10 SEM with a SE detector at 10 kV and 6 to 9 mm as the working distance (WD). The SEM images were analyzed with the software ImageJ to determine the lamellae individual thicknesses. The SEM images reported in Figs. 1 and 4, and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 were collected using a Zeiss Sigma 300 VP microscope, equipped with a SE detector, a BSE detector, and an Oxford Instruments UltimMax 65 EDX detector with the Aztec 6.0 software. Specifically, the detector voltage and the WD were set as follows: Fig. 1 H and I at 2 kV, WD=6 mm WD; Fig. 1J at 5 kV, WD=8.4 mm in high vacuum; Fig. 4A at 15 kV, WD = 8.5 mm in VP; and Fig. 4C at 5 kV, WD=8.6 mm in high vacuum. A Safematic CCU-110 (compact coating unit) was used to apply ~10 nm of carbon coating on the samples to increase their conductivity.

Elemental Analysis.

For the line scan reported in Fig. 4 and in SI Appendix, Fig. S13 a free-standing portion of the chirped patina (Sample IV) was carbon coated using a Safematic CCU-110 (compact coating unit) to apply ~10 nm of carbon. The sample was then imaged using Zeiss Sigma 300 VP microscope, equipped with a SE detector, a BSE detector, and an Oxford Instruments UltimMax 65 EDX detector (at 15 kV, 2.3nA beam current and WD = 8.5 mm) with the Aztec 6.0 software. For the area scan reported in SI Appendix, Fig. S14 a small fragment of the roman glass with blue patina was mounted at 90° on an aluminum stub such that the cross-section was perpendicular to the electron beam. To preserve the optical appearance of this archeological sample, no coating was applied. The sample was then imaged using Zeiss Sigma 300 VP microscope, operating in variable pressure (VP) mode and equipped with a SE detector (cascade current detector, C2D), a BSE detector, and an Oxford Instruments UltimMax 65 EDX detector with the Aztec 6.0 software.

Optical Simulations.

The interaction of light with the glass multilayer was simulated using Lumerical FDTD Solutions (Lumerical Inc.), which is based on the three-dimensional finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method (24). For the 1D multilayer, the incident light beam was a plane wave with a broadband spectrum in the range of 400−700 nm, propagating perpendicular to the multilayer. The boundary conditions were set as absorbing in the direction of the light propagation and as periodic in the perpendicular direction. The optical simulations of the Roman glass fragment were structured by simplifying the hierarchical glass structure into a flat multilayer stack, consisting of the regular alternation of high-density and low-density silica nanolamellae, represented, respectively, by alternating layers of glass (nglass = 1.55) and air (nair = 1). The outermost layer was set as having a refractive index of nglass = 1.55. The thicknesses of the glass layers and of the air gaps were inferred from the SEM cross-sections of single-colored flakes and are reported in SI Appendix, Table S2. The reflectance was calculated as the ratio of the light flux incident on the structure and the light flux reflected through the detector plane.

Laser Ablation Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry.

LA-ICP-MS was carried out using an Analyte Excite ArF excimer 193 nm laser (Teledyne CETAC Photon Machines) coupled to an iCAP-RQ quadrupole ICP-MS (Thermo Scientific). The laser ablation device is equipped with a HelEx II two-volume ablation cell mounted on a high-precision xy translation stage. The samples were placed on a standard laser ablation holder and fixed in-place with double-sided tape. A rapid aerosol transfer line (ARIS, Teledyne CETAC Technologies) enables achieving a faster washout time, typically in the range of t = 20 ms, specifically designed for high-speed imaging. Helium was used as carrier gas to transport the ablated materials to the ICP-MS. The glass fragments were analyzed following a previously reported analytical method (23) and the data analysis was carried out using the software HDIP (Teledyne Photon Machines, Bozeman, MT, USA) and Matlab. Specifically, the following parameters were used for the laser ablation: He gas flow cell = 0.25 L min−1, He gas flow cup = 0.25 L min−1, transfer line ARIS, fluence = 4.0 J cm−2, spot size = 20 µm2, washout time = 20 ms, ablation mode set to fixed dosage = 7, repetition rate = 280 Hz, scanning mode = monodirectional raster. The ICP-MS parameters were set as follows: RF power = 1,550 W, cooling gas flow = 14 L min−1, auxiliary gas flow = 0.8 L min−1, Ar makeup gas flow = 0.8 L min−1, monitored isotopes masses = 23—Na, 29—Si, 43—Ca, 57—Fe, total sweep time (duty cycle) = 20 ms (60%).
