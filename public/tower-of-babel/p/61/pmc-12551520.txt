Time to address the drawbacks in estimation and reporting of malaria cases in India and beyond
Authors: Nimita Deora, Richa Singhal, Abhinav Sinha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
India is committed to eliminate malaria by 2030. However, there has been a continuous debate between National Malaria Control Programmes (NMCPs) and World Health rganization (WHO) on the differential perception of malaria burden. The evident gap between the reported cases by NMCPs and WHO estimations, particularly for India, has never been unbiasedly addressed. This work provides a critical understanding of gaps between estimated and reported cases (ERR) of malaria and the possible reasons thereof.We calculated the ratio between estimated and reported cases, called ERR thereof, for highlighting this gap from 2010 to 2022. We also dissected some of the assumptions/adjustments used by WHO to compensate for data inadequacy while estimating malaria cases. The contribution of private sector in reporting malaria cases and contemporary research data on malaria burden in India were correlated with ERR.ERR remained consistently high (WHO-South-East Asia Region >6; India >10) from 2010 to 2022. Certain assumptions/adjustment factors used by WHO for estimation were unclear. Malaria case reporting from private sector in India was virtually non-existent contributing to consistently high ERR. Data from contemporary research in India also reveal a high burden gap with microscopy-positivity being 3 to 55 times higher than that reported by the NMCP.There still remain a few areas where more detailed clarification is needed both from the WHO and the NMCPs reporting malaria burden specifically regarding malaria case reporting from the private health sectors. If these issues are addressed and resolved, the gap between ERR can be narrowed down or even zeroed.

Introduction

Malaria has been associated with significant morbidity and mortality across multiple continents and with an increase of 14 million estimated cases in 2023 is still a major public health burden in parts of Africa and India.1 2 Despite being committed to malaria elimination by 2030, India is a major contributor to the global estimated malaria burden after certain African countries and Pakistan. Despite a reduction of ~1.3 million estimated malaria cases in 2023 over that in 2022, India noticed a 29% increase in the number of reported cases in the same period and remains the leading contributor to the estimated malaria burden in the WHO’s South-East Asia Region (WHO-SEAR).1 It is important here to note and understand that the annual World Malaria Report (WMR) by the WHO mention the country-specific malaria burden in two major formats: the ‘reported burden’ of malaria (number of malaria cases detected by the National Malaria Control Programmes (NMCPs) of respective countries) and the ‘estimated malaria cases’ (obtained by WHO through statistical adjustments to the reported burden) for the country.1 2

The malaria cases reported by the NMCPs are categorised in the WMRs1 2 according to the place of care that reported the cases (public sector, private sector or community level), method of confirmation of malaria (Rapid Diagnostic Tests or RDT and microscopy after correcting for double counting of RDT and microscopy; includes P. knowlesi) and Plasmodium species (P. falciparum, P. vivax and mixed; excludes P. knowlesi).

Estimations in malaria cases are made to compensate or adjust for certain region-specific surveillance lacunae in reporting the anticipated number of malaria cases by various NMCPs.1 2 The WHO estimates malaria cases by adopting two region-specific modelling: method 1 for areas/countries outside of the WHO African region and African countries with low malaria transmission and method 2 for the African countries with high transmission and for specific countries in the WHO Eastern Mediterranean region that have unreliable surveillance data.1 2 Additionally, the data of certain specific countries are also adjusted because of poor quality or non-availability of the data for particular time periods.1 2

The need, method and validity of estimating malaria cases have been debated,3 particularly for India,4–6 which is not only the largest contributor of malaria cases outside of Africa and Pakistan but also has the highest disparity between the reported and estimated cases in the world as discussed and detailed below. India accounted for ~25% of the reported malaria cases in WHO-SEAR in 2022 and 2023, whereas it contributed 66% and 51% of the estimated cases in the same region in 2022 and 2023, respectively.1 2

Gap between estimated and reported cases

There is an evident gap between the estimated (E) and reported (R) cases for different WHO regions as reflected in the ratio between the two, hereinafter termed as the ER ratio (ERR). It is desirable that the ERR is maintained at or around one implying that there is no epidemiologically significant difference between E and R, thus denoting the adequacy of the NMCPs surveillance systems. The higher the ERR, the wider is the surveillance gap implying lesser than expected reporting of malaria burden. The ERR (2010–2023) varies across the WHO regions: lowest for the WHO Region of the Americas (ERR=1 throughout) and highest for the WHO-SEAR (ERR range: 4.1 in 2023 to 11.3 in 2020; Figure 1a).

It is notable from figure 1b that the countries in which the ERR was 1 were able to bring the ERR to <1 (nearing elimination) and finally to zero (Sri Lanka, Timor-Leste, Bhutan), signifying the importance of adequacy of case reporting. Clearly, the number of estimated cases for India appears to be relatively higher than that for other member countries of the WHO-SEAR. For India in 2022, the ERR (gap between estimated and reported cases) was 19.20 that translates into an ‘under-reporting’ by ~19 times, which might risk the targeted malaria elimination by 2030. However, for 2023, the WMR 2024 mentions the ERR declined to ~9, but the data are not comparable with WMR 2023 due to the reasons mentioned below. The ERR across the last 23 years for India shows that the ERR ranged between 10 and 15 from the year 2000 to 2015 with an increasing trend reaching a peak of 29.6 till 2021 and then declining sharply to 19.2 in the year 2022 and further to 8.95 in 2023 (figure 1c).

The gap between estimated and reported cases is also evident when the data were examined by calculating the relative change (percentage) in the estimated and reported cases for all the WHO regions (online supplemental figures 1 and 2) and the countries falling under the WHO-SEAR (online supplemental figures 3 and 4) for the last 6 years (2018–2023). The relative difference between the estimated and reported cases has been significantly higher (online supplemental figures 1 and 2) for the WHO-SEAR (between 77% and 91%) as compared with other WHO regions (ranging between 8% and 61%). Relative difference between the estimated and reported cases plotted from 2018 to 2022 showed that the reported cases have been consistently less by a relative magnitude of at least 77% in the WHO-SEAR. The reported cases in the year 2023 were 77% less than the estimated cases in the year 2022 for the WHO-SEAR, while it was only 56% for the WHO-Eastern Mediterranean Region, 36% for the WHO-Western Pacific Region, 8% for WHO-Region of the Americas and 22.5% for WHO-African Region (online supplemental figures 1 and 2).

Within the WHO-SEAR, it is pertinent to note that India is a major contributor to the higher difference between the reported and estimated malaria cases in the region. Among the WHO-SEAR countries where the reported cases were lesser than the estimated cases (India, Indonesia, Myanmar and Bangladesh) in the last 6 years (2018–2023), the relative difference for India has been between 89% and 97% as compared with 15% and 73% for Indonesia, Myanmar and Bangladesh (online supplemental figures 3 and 4). Nepal and Thailand on the contrary had higher number of reported cases than estimated cases within the same time frame.

It is very clear that the high ERR across the WHO regions is contributed by the WHO-SEAR and within that, by India and therefore understanding this gap is of high importance not only to the NMCPs but also to the malaria epidemiologists and researchers alike. We hereby mention a few insights into the way these estimations are made and plausible reasons for the high(ish) estimations in India and whether this should raise concerns.

The estimate ‘T’ is further adjusted for factoring the differential test-positivity rates, ‘i’ in active versus passive case detection. Because the number of cases reported by the public sector includes both passive (walk-in suspected patients in public healthcare centres) and active (house-to-house surveys by the public sector) case detection, the private sector reporting is adjusted for ‘i’. On top of that, ‘T’ is further adjusted (in some selected states of India) for the reporting period 2020, 2021 and 2022 to accommodate the decrease in malaria testing rates below the expected testing rates (>10% difference in malaria testing between 2019 and 2018) due to the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic-related disruptions in healthcare delivery services. In these regions, ‘T’ is supplemented with the ‘additional’ malaria tests that would have been conducted in absence of the pandemic and multiplying this with the test-positivity ratios for 2019–2022 (table 1).

It is critical to note here that, specifically for India, this additional adjustment ‘i’ was deleted from the way the estimations were made for the cases reported in 2023 in WMR 2024 (1) without citing any reasons thereof. Not only for cases reported in 2023, this additional adjustment was retrospectively removed for previous years, thus changing the number of estimated cases for past several years, leading to discrepancies and incomparability between the number of estimated malaria cases as reported in WMR 2024 and previous such reports (figure 1c). This might have contributed to the India’s recent exit from the list of ‘high burden to high impact’ countries.

Although these adjustments in the reported cases appear to compensate for the deficiencies in case reporting, there remain many concerns, which if addressed, would help understand these estimations better. These include the details of assumptions used for obtaining the values for ‘c’, ‘d’, ‘e’, ‘f’ and ‘g’ and the basis thereof, detailed equations used to estimate ‘i’ and COVID-19-associated adjustments, how exactly is ‘T’ adjusted with ‘i’ and how are the three estimates clubbed together with respect to the weightage of each adjustment. In the absence of these details, the reported cases may be overcompensated or undercompensated to adjust the inadequacies of disease burden reporting.

Critical issues with malaria reporting

An inverse relation between the ERR and proportion of cases reported by the private sector was observed. In the WHO-SEAR, the proportion of cases reported by the private sector plotted against ERR for the last 5 years (2018 to 2022) revealed that as the reporting from the private sector increases, the ERR goes down (figure 2).

The countries in the WHO-SEAR (India, Myanmar, Bangladesh and Indonesia) where the cases from private sector are sub-optimally reported or have not been reported showed that reported cases were much lower than the estimated cases. While the countries like Nepal and Thailand where the reporting from the private sector is more than the above four WHO-SEAR countries, the difference between reported and estimated cases is much lower (reported cases slightly higher than the estimated cases). Thus, the countries within WHO-SEAR that have cases reported from private sector (table 2) have a reduced ERR as compared with countries where private sector reporting is either nil or clubbed with public sector / community level or negligible (figure 2) suggesting that private sector reporting is a critical factor to reduce the ERR. The critical role of private health sector in reporting malaria cases is a well-recognised challenge for a more complete knowledge of malaria burden in India and for malaria elimination.8–10

On the other hand, possible reporting errors by the NMCPs may be identified and addressed. For instance, the number of cases reported by India according to the place of care (Annexure 4G of the WMR 2023 and WMR 2024) raises a flag. Apart from non-availability of data and/or ‘non- applicability’ for the private sector, the figures quoted for the public sector (101,300) and the community level (75,222) needs to be cautiously analysed (table 2, left panel). If the number of reported cases is examined according to the method of confirmation (Annexure 4H of the WMR 2023 and left panel of table 2), it is evident that the number of RDT-confirmed cases (101,300) is same as those reported from the public sector and the microscopy-confirmed cases (75 222) are the same as that reported at the community level. However, this discrepancy in reporting was not observed in the WMR 2024 wherein all malaria cases from 2023 (n=227 564) were classified as being reported by the public sector which included cases detected at community level and in the private sector (table 2, right panel). In both, WMR 2023 and WMR 2024, there were no separate data on cases reported by the private sector (table 2).

The number of reported malaria cases in 2023, according to the place of healthcare provided, revealed that only 100 229 cases were reported by the private sector in the WHO-SEAR as against 726 763 cases reported by the public sector and 84 675 at the community level. However, when the same data was analysed for other countries belonging to the WHO-SEAR, it was found that only four countries (Bangladesh, Indonesia, Nepal and Thailand) in the region had some malaria cases reported from the private sector (table 2). It is important to note that for India and Myanmar, the data for the public sector included cases detected at the community level and in the private sector, which creates a confusion.

The reporting discrepancy (Annexure 4H of the WMR 2023 and left panel of table 2) raises two further concerns: RDT are being used in the public sector and microscopy at the community level (contrary is expected) and the number of RDT-positive cases exceeds the number of microscopy-confirmed cases. The latter is possible if RDT has overtaken microscopy as a ‘gold’ standard and/or RDT-positivity rate (RDTPR) is higher than the microscopy-positivity rate (MSPR). We further examined the trend of RDTPR (2017–2023) and MSPR (2010–2023) as reported in the Annexure 4H of the WMR 2024 and found that the RDTPR was ~13 times higher than MSPR in 2017, which declined gradually up to 2023 but even then, RDTPR was ~6 times that of the MSPR (figure 3a), a finding difficult to believe considering the differentials11–13 in the limits of detection (parasites/µL of blood) for RDT (100–200) and microscopy (10–100).

Additional evidence from contemporary research data

Ques may be obtained from research synthesis of studies reporting the contemporary prevalence of malaria (microscopy-positive febrile cases) for a post-facto analysis to examine the possibilities of cases being missed by the NMCPs. The authors did a preliminary analysis on the pooled data from such studies (from 1982 to 2018; online supplemental table 1) recruited for a different purpose14 and found that MSPR from research was always higher (except in 2004) translating into 3–55 times higher MSPR when compared with that reported by NMCP during the same time period (figure 3b). It might be correctly argued that these data may not be comparable to the NMCP data due to smaller sample sizes and geographic and seasonal differentials in research but may be sufficient enough to raise flags for further probe.

Summary of findings

The ratio of estimated and reported malaria cases (ERR) was consistently high for the WHO’s-SEAR and India, in particular, since 2010. This gap stems from uncertainties in certain assumptions and adjustment factors used by the WHO for malaria estimation and also from incomplete malaria reporting data by NMCPs. Inadequate malaria case reporting from the private healthcare sector is a major contributor to the consistently high ERR from 2010 to 2023. Contemporary research data on malaria burden in India also support the inadequacy of malaria case reporting.

The way forward

With only around 2 years remaining to achieve a zero-malaria case burden and sustain this till 2030 (malaria elimination), India stands on the brink of making this a reality with a significant reduction in the number of malaria cases in the last two decades (figure 1c) and a recent exit from the high burden to high impact (HBHI) countries in 2024.1 This offers a golden chance of eliminating malaria by 2030 provided the intricacies involved in estimation of malaria cases are understood and necessary remedial actions taken, if applicable. Possible remedial steps include improving the quality and completeness of reporting malaria through clear and non-overlapping segregation of the cases by the place of reporting, augmenting the countrywide involvement of the private sector in reporting malaria cases and demonstrated discounting of double counting of cases by RDT and microscopy. As private sector reporting for malaria is almost non-existent and is critical for completeness of reporting, drastic efforts such as harnessing digital platforms of the Government of India (Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission, Ni-kshay, Integrated Health Information Platform) and using appropriate and successful public–private partnership models can fill this reporting gap. Together, these steps will ensure completeness and robustness of malaria case reporting, thus narrowing the gap between estimated and reported cases in a huge way.
