Relationships of triglyceride–glucose index and body mass index with 5-year all-cause mortality in patients with diabetes and comorbid hypertension: Evidence from two prospective cohort studies
Authors: Yilu Liu, Hongzhao You, Yingxuan Zhu, Fang Luo, Yanyan Zhao, Dingyue Zhang, Ying Xiao, Shijie You, Rui Zhang, Yingdong Han, Jing Li, Shouling Wu, Jiansong Yuan, Shuohua Chen, Weixian Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•BMI and TyG predict all-cause mortality in patients with diabetes and hypertension.•TyG mediates 40% of the association between BMI and all-cause mortality.•Significant multiplicative interactions were found between TyG and BMI.•BMI combined TyG provides a simple and stable risk assessment of all-cause mortality.•Weight loss reduce the all-cause mortality risk associated with insulin resistance.

Introduction

Diabetes is a chronic metabolic disorder that seriously affects health and wellbeing, and is one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide [1]. Hypertension is a common comorbidity of diabetes that is associated with an approximately three-times higher risk of all-cause mortality than diabetes alone [2]. However, there is still no stable and robust predictor of all-cause mortality that can be used in such a large population. Obesity, which is defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30.0 kg/m2, is widely recognised as a risk factor for all-cause mortality in individuals with hypertension and diabetes, and is widely referred to in guidelines for medical and surgical use [3,4]. However, BMI is based solely on height and body mass, and therefore it does not provide a comprehensive assessment of the metabolic state of a patient, nor does it permit precise risk stratification for all-cause mortality alone [5]. Therefore, the identification of a new marker that could provide not only a prognosis, but also additional insight into the effect of BMI on the risk of all-cause mortality, would be of great clinical utility.

Insulin resistance (IR) is a state of low sensitivity and responsiveness to insulin and a primary driver of the progression of diabetes and hypertension [6,7]. Inappropriate activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone and sympathetic nervous systems, oxidative stress, abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism and abnormal fat distribution have been demonstrated to be underlying mechanisms [[8], [9], [10]]. The triglyceride–glucose index (TyG) has been proposed as a simple and reliable clinical surrogate marker of IR that is calculated as the logarithmic product of the fasting triglyceride and glucose concentrations. The fasting plasma glucose concentration mainly reflects IR in the liver, whereas the fasting triglyceride concentration mainly reflects IR in adipocytes [11] Consequently, there is a strong physiological basis for the use of TyG to assess diseases related to IR. TyG has previously been reported to perform well in the estimation of IR when compared with direct measurement of IR, such as hyperinsulinaemic–euglycaemic clamp (HIEC) and the Homeostatic Model Assessment of IR (HOMA-IR) in individuals with and without diabetes [12,13]. As a reliable surrogate marker of IR in clinical practice, TyG has previously been demonstrated to be associated with both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality [14,15] Therefore, we speculate that TyG might mediate the association between IR and all-cause mortality in patients with diabetes and comorbid hypertension.

Obesity is a common cause of IR. Both BMI and TyG are potential predictors of the adverse outcomes of patients with diabetes and comorbid hypertension. However, the relationships of TyG and BMI with the all-cause mortality of patients with both diabetes and hypertension remain to be fully characterized. Revealing the potential mediating effect of TyG on the occurrence of all-cause mortality in patients with diabetes with comorbid hypertension may help to identify new therapeutic targets. Furthermore, knowledge of whether the joint effects of abnormal BMI and TyG on the all-cause mortality of such patients are additive or antagonistic would contribute to a better assessment of the risk of adverse events and permit the formulation of corresponding risk stratification strategies.

To address this knowledge gap, we have performed analyses of data obtained during two large-scale, prospective cohort studies performed in different global regions, the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the China Kailuan cohort. We aimed to evaluate the complex relationships of BMI and TyG with 5-year all-cause mortality, as well as to provide a novel risk assessment strategy based on BMI and TyG for use in patients with diabetes and comorbid hypertension, to assist in decision-making in routine clinical practice.

Subjects, materials and methods

Study sample

A flowchart for the study is shown in Fig. S1. The NHANES has a complex, stratified, multi-stage sampling design. All the NHANES protocols have been approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics and all the participants provided their written informed consent. We collected NHANES data for 1999–2014 and enrolled 5,728 participants aged 20–75 years who had both diabetes and hypertension at baseline. In the present study, diabetes included both type 1 and type 2 diabetes mellites. Diabetes was defined as a fasting plasma glucose concentration of ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) > 6.5 %, and a self-reported physician diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes and/or the use of insulin or oral hypoglycaemic medication. Hypertension was defined as self-reported hypertension, systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg (a mean of three recorded measurements), or the reported use of antihypertensive medication [16]. Participants for whom information was missing regarding outcomes and/or key candidate variables were excluded. All-cause mortality during follow-up was determined using the linked National Death Index up to 31st December 2019.

The Kailuan study has a prospective cohort design and is being conducted in Tangshan, China. All the participants are current or retired employees of the Kailuan Group, a coal mining company in Tangshan. Details of the study design and procedures have been provided previously [17]. Between 2005 and 2010, 3,456 patients with both diabetes and hypertension who met the same criteria as that for the NHANES database were enrolled. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kai Luan General Hospital. We tracked the mortality of the participants in the Kailuan cohort by reviewing their medical records, social security records, hospital records and death certificates. Deaths were recorded up to 31st December 2015.

Assessment of BMI, TyG and covariates

The variables recorded were demographic characteristics (age, sex, educational level, marital status and smoking status), comorbidities (ischemic stroke and myocardial infarction), findings on physical examination (body mass index, waist circumference, systolic blood pressure [BP] and diastolic BP) and laboratory data (white blood cell count, haemoglobin concentration, platelet count, albumin, blood urea nitrogen, blood uric acid, estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] and urinary protein concentration). For the NHANES cohort, the triglyceride and glucose concentrations of the participants were measured using a Hitachi 704 Analyzer. Their serum creatinine and uric acid concentrations were measured using a Beckman automated clinical analyser. Haemoglobin concentration, white blood cell count and platelet count were evaluated using automated haematology analysers. Albumin concentration was measured using a bichromatic digital endpoint method and DxC800 modular chemistry. The blood urea nitrogen concentration was determined using an enzymatic conductivity rate method and DxC800 modular chemistry. For the Kailuan cohort, demographic data and information regarding comorbidities were collected from participants using questionnaires completed during face-to-face interviews. Height, body mass, waist circumference and BP were measured by trained physicians using standardised protocols. Participants were instructed to visit the testing site in the morning, after at least 8 h of fasting, then blood samples were collected from a cephalic vein by a trained laboratory technician. An automatic biochemical analyser (7600-020; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) was used to measure the circulating concentrations of other biomarkers. Educational level was categorised as below high school or high school or above); marital status as married, widowed or divorced, or single; and smoking status as never, former or current. TyG was calculated as ln[TG (mg/dl) × FPG (mg/dl)/2]. BMI was calculated as body mass in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the abbreviated 2009 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula [18].

Statistical analyses

To estimate the variances and statistics that would be representative of US adults, the NHANES data were analysed using a weighting of “2/8*WTMEC4YR and 6/8*WTMEC2YR” to avoid oversampling [[19], [20], [21]]. The baseline characteristics of the participants were described for BMI groups, and differences among the groups were identified using analysis of variance adjusted for sampling weights for continuous data and the Rao–Scott χ2 test for categorical data for the NHANES database, and using analysis of variance and the χ2 test for the Kailuan cohort. Continuous datasets from the NHANES database are shown as the weighted mean (standard deviation) and categorical datasets as the number (weighted percentage). Continuous datasets from the Kailuan cohort are shown as the mean (standard deviation) and categorical datasets as the number (percentage).

We explored potential non-linear or linear relationships using 3-knotted restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression. The cut-off values of BMI and TyG were calculated according to current guidelines and the statistical analysis. We used Cox proportional hazard regression models to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) of the outcomes associated with BMI (<30.0 kg/m2 or ≥ 30.0 kg/ m2, based on the outcomes of RCS regression) and TyG (<8.9, in 8.9–10.3 or > 10.3, categorised according to tertiles). Three models were created: Model 1, adjusted for age and sex; Model 2, adjusted for age, sex, marital status, educational level and smoking status; and Model 3, adjusted for the variables in Model 2, plus a history of myocardial infarction or stroke, urinary protein concentration and the CKD-EPI eGFR.

We evaluated the total effect, natural indirect effect (NIE) and natural direct effect (NDE) of BMI (<30.0 kg/m2 or ≥ 30.0 kg/m2) on 5-year all-cause mortality to identify indirect associations mediated by TyG and direct associations not mediated by TyG. NDE reflects the effect of BMI on all-cause mortality independent of TyG, whereas NIE reflects the proportion of BMI that is affected by the changes in TyG over time. NIE was calculated using a multivariable logistic regression model involving TyG (mediator), BMI (exposure) and potential confounders. NDE was calculated using a multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression model involving 5-year all-cause mortality (outcome), BMI, TyG and potential confounders. To evaluate the extent to which TyG mediated the relationship, we calculated the proportion of the association mediated by TyG as the NIE divided by the sum of the NDE and NIE. To quantify the multiplicative interactions, we also included a product term of BMI (<30.0 kg/ m2 or ≥ 30.0 kg/m2) and TyG (tertiles) in the model. The HR and 95 % CI of the product term were used as the measure of the interaction on the multiplicative scale. We assessed additive interactive effects using three distinct metrics: relative excess risk owing to the interaction (RERI), the proportion attributable to the interaction (AP) and the synergy index (SI). These metrics capture different aspects of the interaction, including the component of the effect that is attributable to the interaction, the proportion of the combined effect arising from the interaction and the ratios of the combined effect and the individual effects. We calculated the corresponding 95 % CIs for these three metrics using the delta method. To assess the joint associations, we placed the participants into six groups according to BMI (<30.0 kg/ m2 or ≥ 30.0 kg/m2) and TyG (tertiles) and estimated the HRs for the all-cause mortality of each group versus those with low BMI and in the lowest tertile of TyG. To further evaluate the prognostic value of a combination of BMI and TyG, the patients were placed into three risk groups according to their BMI and TyG. Time-to-event curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences were compared using the log-rank test, to analyse their ability to stratify the participants across risk categories. All analyses were performed using R statistical software version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

We conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, considering the lower overall BMI of the Asian population, we used a BMI cutoff of 27.5 kg/m2 (preferred cutoff for those more prone to developing central obesity in the Asian population) instead of 30 kg/m2 in the Kailuan cohort to evaluate whether the estimated mediating proportions were similar to those in the main analysis [22]. Second, a proportion of the population taking oral antidiabetic medications may be non-diabetic, such as patients diagnosed with heart failure, polycystic ovary syndrome, or simple obesity. Therefore, we reconducted all analyses after removing oral glucose-lowering medications from the definition of diabetes in both cohorts.

To investigate whether TyG mediated the association between BMI and all-cause mortality, sample size calculations were performed using pwrSEM based on Monte Carlo simulations [23]. The data and code for this tool are available from the Open Science Framework at https://osf.io/h8yfk/. The tool has a 10,000 bootstrap bias-corrected 95 % confidence interval. With this approach, the mediation effect is considered statistically significant if the bootstrap 95 % CI does not include the value 0. Direct and indirect effects are expressed as standardized coefficients. R2 represents the explained variance of the variable, expressed as a percentage. A priori sample size calculations indicated that a sample of 480 patients would be sufficiently large to detect a moderate effect size (f2 = 0.15; α = 0.05; 1 − β = 0.8) with two predictor variables in the multiple regression analysis. Considering a 10 % loss to follow-up rate, at least 528 patients would need to be included in the study. Two-sided P-values of <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

A total of 5,728 participants in the NHANES 1999–2014 cycles who had both diabetes and hypertension and were aged 20–75 years were included. After weighting, these participants were representative of almost 196 million non-institutionalised residents of the US. The Kailuan cohort comprised 3,456 participants from the 2010–2015 Kailuan cycles who met the same criteria. During a follow-up period of 5 years, 481 (8.4 %) and 319 (9.2 %) deaths were recorded in the NHANES database and Kailuan cohort, respectively. Of the 5,728 participants in the NHANES database, 2,899 (50.6 %) had low BMI and 2,828 (49.4 %) had high BMI. Analysis of the effect of TyG on the relationships of BMI with 5-year all-cause mortality

Fig. 1 shows the relationships of BMI and TyG with 5-year all-cause mortality in the NHANES database. A U-shaped non-linear association between BMI and the risk of all-cause mortality was identified using RCS regression (for non-linearity, P = 0.014) and a threshold of 29.6 kg/m2. The World Health Organisation defines obesity in adults using a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2. Therefore, 30.0 kg/m2 was used as the cut-off value to define high and low BMI. A positive linear association between TyG and the risk of all-cause death was identified (for non-linearity, P = 0.069; Fig. 2), with a median TyG value of 9.4. After adjustment for age, sex, marital status, educational level, smoking status, a history of coronary artery disease, urinary protein concentration and CKD-EPI eGFR, the HRs for adults with high BMI versus those with low BMI were 1.71 (95 % CI: 1.13–1.95, P < 0.001) for all-cause mortality in the NHANES database, and 1.85 (95 % CI: 1.27–2.03, P = 0.009) for all-cause mortality in the Kailuan cohort (Table S3). For the comparison of the highest tertile of TyG (>10.3) with the lowest tertile (<8.9), the adjusted HRs were 2.45 (95 % CI: 1.27–6.27, P < 0.001) for all-cause mortality in the NHANES database and 2.99 (95 % CI: 1.33–7.26, P < 0.001) for all-cause mortality in the Kailuan cohort (Table S4).Fig. 1(A, B) The TyG and BMI of non-survivors (white) were significantly higher than those of survivors (yellow) in the NHANES database. Fig. S2 showed that the prevalence of all-cause mortality increased across the TyG tertiles as BMI increased, but the differences between groups became smaller, indicating the complex relationship between TyG and BMI with respect to their effects on 5-year all-cause mortality. Prognostic value of the combination of BMI and TyG for 5-year all-cause mortality

To translate these findings into a risk assessment strategy for all-cause mortality, we placed the patients into three subgroups using the combination of BMI and TyG. Participants with BMI < 30 kg/m2 and TyG < 8.9 were defined as low risk (n = 1,049 in the NHANES database, n = 550 in the Kailuan cohort), those with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 and TyG > 10.3 were defined as high risk (n = 1,038 in the NHANES database, n = 681 in the Kailuan cohort) and the remaining participants were placed into an intermediate-risk group. The 5-year mortality rates of the participants increased gradually across these three risk categories. Kaplan–Meier curves for these three risk groups are presented in Fig. 2. The Kaplan–Meier curves showed that the 5-year event-free survival rate decreased across the three risk groups (NHANES database, 98.0 % vs. 91.1 % vs. 86.8 %, log-rank P < 0.001; Kailuan cohort, 96.4 % vs. 90.9 % vs. 85.9 %, log-rank P < 0.001).

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The results of all sensitivity analyses were largely consistent. The mediation proportion decreased slightly when the cutoff BMI was substituted to 27.5 kg/m2 in the Kailuan cohort (41.6 % to 39.2 %, Table S6). The proportion of all-cause mortality mediated by TyG was 39.0 % (24.0 %–43.8 %) in the NHANES population and 40.8 % (35.7 %–53.9 %) in the Kailuan cohort after removing oral glucose-lowering medications from the definition of diabetes (Table S7). The significant multiplicative interaction of TyG and BMI on 5-year all-cause mortality in the NHANES population and the Kailuan cohort remained stable (Table S8).

Discussion

In the present study, we evaluated the mediating, interacting and joint effects of BMI and TyG on all-cause mortality using data from two population-based prospective cohorts from different global regions. We have identified a U-shaped non-linear association between BMI and the risk of all-cause mortality and a positive linear association between TyG and the risk of all-cause mortality using RCS regression. High BMI was associated with a high risk of all-cause mortality in patients with diabetes and comorbid hypertension. Approximately 40 % of the association was found to be mediated by TyG. For both the NHANES database and Kailuan cohort, significant multiplicative interactions were found between TyG and BMI with respect to all-cause mortality, and the associations were closer for patients with high BMI. We have also described a novel risk assessment strategy based on these two key variables that may be a useful means of predicting 5-year all-cause mortality for such patients.

Obesity has become a major health problem worldwide, which amplifies the effects of genetic susceptibility and environmental factors on both diabetes [24] and hypertension [25] through several mechanisms, such as insulin resistance, dysfunctional autophagy, the microbiome–gut–brain axis, inflammation and oxidative stress. In addition, obesity leads to the dysregulation of adipokines, which accelerates the progression of both diabetes and hypertension [26]. BMI is widely used in the clinical evaluation of obesity. In a cohort study, Bhaskaran et al. identified a significant non-linear association between BMI and all-cause mortality, which is consistent with our present findings [27]. An “unhealthy” BMI has been shown to be associated with higher risks of cancer [28], atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [29], chronic kidney disease [30] and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [31], which may account for the higher all-cause mortality of patients with high BMI observed in the present study [32]. However, BMI is an imperfect measure of body composition because this varies according to ethnicity, sex, body shape and muscle mass. Although BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 is closely associated with all-cause mortality in adults [33], it is not a good tool to accurately categorise patients with diabetes and hypertension with respect to their risk of all-cause mortality, and especially for those with relatively low BMI, which demonstrates the importance of identifying additional useful indices.

Insulin resistance, which develops early, alongside impaired glucose metabolism, is a key contributor to the development and progression of diabetes and hypertension, especially in individuals with obesity [34]. The accumulation of fat usually exacerbates insulin resistance, causes a deterioration of metabolic abnormalities and increases the risk of all-cause mortality. TyG is a composite index that is used to evaluate IR and might be a mediator of the relationship between BMI and the risk of all-cause mortality in patients with diabetes and hypertension [7,35].

In the present study, we first showed that TyG had both a mediating effect and a multiplicative interaction with BMI in this population. We found that approximately 40 % of the risk of 5-year all-cause mortality that is associated with BMI is attributable to TyG, indicating that the risk of all-cause mortality associated with a high BMI could be prevented by eliminating the insulin resistance. The identification of TyG as a mediator of the relationship between BMI and all-cause mortality has provided a new potential target for this complex metabolic process. The mechanism by which high BMI promotes insulin resistance is not fully understood, but may involve adipose tissue hypoxia, inflammation, oxidative stress, high free fatty acid concentrations and low lipocalin secretion [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. The present findings have provided valuable insight into the prevention of diabetes and comorbid hypertension, in that early interventions aimed at weight loss would be beneficial for such patients through a reduction in insulin resistance. In addition, we have characterised the interaction between BMI and the TyG with respect to the 5-year all-cause mortality of patients with diabetes and hypertension. Significant multiplicative interactions were identified between BMI and TyG. In addition, the adverse association of TyG with all-cause mortality was closer for participants with high BMI, which highlights the necessity of ameliorating the insulin resistance of such patients, and especially those with obesity, who are more vulnerable to an abnormal TyG.

Patients with diabetes and hypertension are recognised to be at a high risk of all-cause mortality. The identification of patients who are at relatively high risk of all-cause mortality is clinically important in such a large population. DHAM score is a visual risk calculator based on NHANES data, existing for the prediction of 5-year all-cause mortality [40]. This is a complex prediction model that includes eight predictors, but it has not been validated using data from Asian populations, which does not meet the needs of clinical practice. The present findings regarding the joint effects of BMI and TyG imply that individuals with high values of both may be at a higher risk of all-cause mortality than those with high values of only one of these variables. To translate these findings, we constructed a risk assessment strategy based on BMI and TyG in which patients with diabetes and hypertension were classified as being at low-, intermediate- or high-risk. Approximately 19 % of the participants were identified as being at high risk, and these individuals would therefore benefit from early and aggressive interventions. Although there are huge differences in the prevalent lifestyle, medical conditions, level of social support and economic conditions of the United States and China, the prognostic risk assessment was robust and stable for both the US NHANES and China Kailuan cohorts. Thus, the combination of TyG and BMI may represent a simple and convenient screening tool for the precise risk stratification of patients with diabetes and hypertension. This risk assessment strategy, developed using data from two prospective cohorts, one each in East Asia and North America, has the potential for use in clinical practice in various regions worldwide.

The present study has some limitations. First, limited by data collection, the follow-up durations of the two cohorts were relatively short; therefore, the relationships of the all-cause mortality, BMI, and TyG trajectories remain unclear. A large population-based study involving serial measurements and a longer period of follow-up is required. Second, BMI is not an ideal method to evaluate whole-body fat distribution. Alternative metrics, such as waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, body fat composition, or advanced imaging-derived indices that reflect the distribution of body fat, such as those derived from multi-slice spiral computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, might offer a more precise assessment of whole-body fat distribution. Therefore, they may be more sensitive measures of 5-year all-cause mortality risk in patients with diabetes and comorbid hypertension. Third, TyG can be affected by different disease statuses. For example, Bob et al. reported higher TyG values in non-diabetic, non-obese patients with obstructive sleep apnoea, independent of age, sex, and BMI [41,42]. Although our primary analysis and several sensitivity analyses adjusted for baseline coexisting conditions yielded robust results, the possibility of reverse causality and residual confounding (many other conditions were not measured or accounted for) cannot be completely ruled out. Fourth, limited by data acquisition, we did not use HIEC or HOMA-IR. We instead used TyG as a surrogate for IR. Nevertheless, the diagnostic accuracy of TyG using the HIEC and HOMA-IR as reference methods has been tested in several studies. HIEC and HOMA-IR may be more comprehensive methods of assessment to confirm the effects of IR on all-cause mortality in patients with diabetes and comorbid hypertension.

Conclusion

We used the NHANES database and the China Kailuan cohort to demonstrate that BMI was associated with 5-year all-cause mortality in patients with diabetes and hypertension, and that this association was partially mediated by TyG. These findings indicate that weight loss would be beneficial for such patients via reduction of IR. A novel risk assessment strategy combining TyG with BMI could serve as a valuable predictive tool for identifying patients with diabetes with comorbid hypertension who are at an elevated risk of 5-year all-cause mortality.
