Effect of Q-angle symmetry on knee joint kinematics in level walking, upslope walking and landing task
Authors: Zibo Gao, Qingyang Kang, Yang Liu, Zhihong Qiu, Fangzheng Lin, Ji Qi, Yongjin Li, Dingkun Lin, Xiaolong Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe quadriceps angle (Q-angle) is a key biomechanical measure of lower limb alignment. While its asymmetry is linked to knee disorders like patellofemoral pain, its dynamic impact on full six-degree-of-freedom (6DOF) knee kinematics across different tasks remains unclear.MethodsForty healthy university students were categorized into Q-angle symmetric (QS, bilateral difference ≤ 3°) or asymmetric (QA, > 3°) groups. Knee 6DOF kinematics were captured during level walking, 10% upslope walking, and landing task using a 3D motion capture system. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for pain, and Kujala score were assessed at baseline and 18-month follow-up. Statistical parametric mapping (SPM1D) was used for kinematic analysis.ResultsDuring level walking, the QA group showed greater internal rotation at 69–74% gait cycle (GC) (p = 0.042) and altered flexion/extension at 75–90% GC (p = 0.012). During upslope walking, the QA group exhibited different flexion/extension at 74–87% GC (p = 0.018). No kinematic differences were found in any 6DOF parameter during landing task. At 18 months, there were no significant intergroup differences in IPAQ, VAS, or Kujala scores (all p > 0.05).ConclusionQ-angle asymmetry induces task- and phase-specific alterations in knee angular kinematics during walking tasks but does not affect translational kinematics or short-term functional outcomes in healthy young adults. Its biomechanical effect is limited and context-dependent, suggesting caution in using it as a standalone risk marker for knee injury.

Introduction

The quadriceps angle (Q-angle) is defined as the angle between the line connecting the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) to the center of the patella (C.P., midpoint of the patellar transverse diameter) and the line connecting the C.P. to the tibial tuberosity (TT). This angle serves as a key biomechanical indicator reflecting the alignment of the quadriceps muscle force vector relative to the knee joint [1]. During functional activities, an abnormal Q-angle alters the distribution of pressure and shear forces across the patellofemoral joint and is therefore widely considered to be associated with an increased risk of patellofemoral pain syndrome and anterior knee pain [2–4].

However, the biomechanical significance of the Q-angle extends far beyond the patellofemoral joint. As a representation of the quadriceps force line, the Q-angle directly influences the resultant force vector of the extensor mechanism acting on the tibial tuberosity [5]. Theoretically, asymmetry in the bilateral Q-angle implies the presence of asymmetrical lateral pulling forces during knee extension in the lower limbs [6]. During dynamic tasks, this asymmetry may lead to alterations in tibiofemoral joint kinematics across different planes. These alterations could include rotational changes of the tibia in the transverse plane, modifications in adduction and abduction moments at the tibiofemoral joint in the coronal plane, and indirectly, due to changes in patellar tracking and its contact relationship with the femoral trochlea, affect movement coupling in the sagittal plane and other degrees of freedom [7]. Therefore, investigating whether and how Q-angle asymmetry affects the dynamic kinematic characteristics of the knee across all six degrees of freedom (6DOF) is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of its biomechanical consequences.

Previous studies have explored the regulatory effects of the Q-angle on knee kinematics during functional tasks. During single-limb squats, Q-angle magnitude is not significantly associated with peak dynamic knee valgus, while individuals with larger Q-angles exhibit a significantly higher pelvic width-to-femoral length ratio, which is moderately correlated with both static and dynamic knee valgus [8]. During running, the Q-angle does not alter the peak amplitude of lower-extremity angular kinematic parameters including rearfoot eversion and tibial internal rotation, but a larger Q-angle is significantly linked to delayed timing of maximum tibial internal rotation during the stance phase [9]. Further prospective cohort research has demonstrated that individuals with a bilateral Q-angle asymmetry ≥ 4° have a 1.8-fold higher risk of lower extremity running injuries, and a systematic review has synthesized existing evidence on the association between Q-angle abnormalities and lower extremity injury risk [10, 11].

However, current knowledge on the symmetry of the Q-angle still contains several gaps. First, existing dynamic studies have largely been confined to angular kinematic parameters within the 6DOF, neglecting the assessment of translational kinematics [9]. Translational stability, particularly anterior-posterior tibial translation, is of significant importance for evaluating functional knee stability [12]. Second, there is a lack of task-specific analysis. Different dynamic activities impose distinctly varying load patterns on the knee, and pathological or compensatory movement patterns often emerge under specific loading conditions [13–15]. Whether the influence of Q-angle asymmetry is task-dependent remains unclear. Third, baseline data for healthy young populations are scarce. Most studies have enrolled patients or middle-aged individuals, whose kinematic performance may already be influenced by pain or degeneration, making it difficult to isolate the independent effect of Q-angle asymmetry itself [16, 17].

To address these gaps, this study employed a high-precision three-dimensional motion capture system. It aimed to investigate how Q-angle asymmetry affects knee 6DOF kinematics in healthy Chinese university students during three functional tasks: level walking, 10% gradient upslope walking, and landing task, and to assess whether these effects are task-dependent. Furthermore, a prospective observational follow-up was conducted to determine whether Q-angle asymmetry is associated with early adverse changes in knee function outcomes in this healthy young population, thereby providing a basis for targeted injury risk screening and rehabilitation intervention.

Methods

Subjects recruitment

Forty college students (22 males and 18 females) were enrolled in this study, with recruitment commencing in April 2024. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7 to justify the sample size. The analysis was based on an independent two-sample t-test. The effect size (Cohen’s d) was set to 1.1, representing a large effect consistent with prior literatures [18, 19]. Given this effect size, α = 0.05 (two-tailed), and a desired power of 0.90, the analysis indicated that a total sample size of 38 participants was required. To account for potential data attrition and ensure robustness, we aimed to recruit slightly above this threshold, resulting in a final sample of 40 participants.

All participants were healthy Chinese college students. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 18–30 years, BMI < 30 kg/m2; (2) no history of major knee trauma or knee surgery; (3) no musculoskeletal or neurological diseases that affect motor function; (4) no strenuous physical activity within 24 h prior to the experiment. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of knee pain, swelling, or motion limitation at the time of testing; (2) severe lower limb alignment abnormalities; (3) current use of lower limb orthoses or assistive devices that affect gait. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine (approval number: ZE 2024-101-01). After being fully informed of the experimental details, all subjects provided written informed consent, which was documented by the researchers.

Clinical assessment

To determine participants’ eligibility for the experiment, each underwent a knee physical examination to exclude conditions that might impair normal knee motion. Following the examination, the Q-angle was measured according to the standardized protocol proposed by Merchant et al. [20]. Participants assumed a neutral standing posture with knees fully extended and patellae facing forward. First, the examiner marked the ASIS, C.P., and TT with ink dots. Subsequently, measurements were performed using a plastic digital inclinometer with extendable long arms (Gemred, China). During the procedure, the long arm of the inclinometer was first placed on the ASIS, and the participant assisted in stabilizing it to free the examiner’s hands. Next, the examiner held the inclinometer and the patella with one hand, aligning the axis of the inclinometer with the C.P. and ensuring that both this axis and the patella were positioned at the center of the trochlear entrance of the distal femur. Finally, the examiner used the other hand to adjust the short arm of the inclinometer to align with the TT, and the Q-angle value was recorded (Fig. 1). Participants were divided into a Q-angle symmetric group (QS group) and a Q-angle asymmetric group (QA group) based on whether the absolute bilateral Q-angle difference was > 3°. This threshold was determined with reference to the common definition of Q-angle asymmetry used by Hikawa et al. [6] in a large cross-sectional study, which provided important baseline data for assessing Q-angle characteristics in East Asian populations. Additionally, during the baseline data collection, participants completed the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). At the 18-month follow-up, they completed the IPAQ, bilateral Kujala scores, and bilateral visual analogue scale (VAS) scores for knee pain [21, 22].

Functional tasks protocol

Participants were required to complete three functional tasks for kinematic data collection: level walking, upslope walking, and landing task. These three tasks are not only closely related to knee joint kinematics but are also common movements in daily life and sports activities [23–25]. Prior to formal data collection, participants wore well-fitted clothing provided by the laboratory and were barefoot to control the influence of attire on gait mechanics [26]. Before the walking test on the level, bi-directional treadmill, participants underwent a 3-minute acclimatization walk at an initial speed of 1.0 km/h. The treadmill speed was then gradually fine-tuned by the researcher in increments of 0.3 km/h. After each adjustment, participants walked at that speed for 30 s to fully perceive the gait rhythm. Once the participant verbally confirmed that the current speed matched their habitual walking speed, this speed was fixed as the testing speed and used for all subsequent formal tests. During the level walking test, after stable walking was achieved for 10 s, approximately 15 s of knee joint kinematic data were collected, corresponding to 15 walking gait cycles. For the upslope walking test, participants walked on the treadmill set at a 10% gradient for 15 s. For the landing task, participants practiced the movement at least three times beforehand to familiarize themselves with it. During the test, participants stood on a 30-cm-high box with their hands on their hips, then jumped down and landed with both feet simultaneously at a standardized horizontal jump distance of one-third of each participant’s height (Fig. 2).

Devices and experiment procedures

A 3D gait motion capture system (Opti_Knee, Innomotion, Shanghai, China) was used to record 6DOF kinematic data of the bilateral knee joints of the participants [27, 28]. The complete gait system includes a near-infrared tracking and navigation device, a high-speed optical camera, two sets of markers, a handheld digital pointer, and a bidirectional, gradient-adjustable, horizontally moving treadmill. The sampling frequency of the system is 120 Hz. Previous studies have reported that it achieves high accuracy with a root-mean-square precision of 0.3 mm [29], along with a repeatability precision of less than 1.3° for rotation and less than 0.9 mm for displacement [30].

Kinematic data were collected by trained and two experienced gait system operators, who were not involved in the recruitment of participants or pre-experimental evaluation procedures. First, participants were required to stand in a neutral position with hands on their hips, and their lower limbs were exposed to allow attachment of motion capture markers to the thighs and shanks. The operators used the digital pointer to calibrate the participants’ bony landmarks, including the greater trochanter, medial femoral condyle, lateral femoral condyle, medial tibial plateau, and lateral tibial plateau. After the calibration step, a personalized 3D coordinate system of the knee joint was automatically generated by the system software through marker recognition.

During formal data collection, each subject was required to repeat each functional task three times according to the protocol to obtain valid bilateral knee joint kinematic data. A 5-minute rest period was provided between the three functional tasks to avoid fatigue effects in subjects and interference from the previous task. The system automatically averaged the kinematic data of all test cycles into a single cycle containing an average of 101 data points. Knee joint kinematic data were extracted for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to examine the distribution of the data. Independent samples t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used to analyze the statistical differences in demographic data and clinical efficacy scores. The chi-square test was performed to compare gender differences between groups. All these statistical analyses were completed using the statistical software SPSS Version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). For knee kinematic analysis, all knee kinematic data were normalized to the full 0–100% gait cycle for level walking, upslope walking, and landing task. Separately within each group, paired t-tests based on 1D Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM1D) were performed to compare kinematic data between the limb with the larger Q-angle and the limb with the smaller Q-angle, to identify intervals with significant kinematic differences across the 0–100% gait cycle. The SPM1D toolbox (Version m0.4.8, official website: http://www.spm1d.org) running on MATLAB was used [31, 32]. This toolbox enables statistical inference for a set of 1-dimensional measurement data by analyzing the expectations of smooth 1-dimensional Gaussian fields [33]. The statistical significance level was set at 0.05. Gait kinematic curves of both groups for the three tasks were plotted, with the significant difference intervals identified by SPM1D marked accordingly.

Results

Demographic data

There was no statistically significant difference in gender distribution between the two groups (P > 0.05). Specifically, the QS group included 9 males and 9 females, while the QA group comprised 13 males and 9 females. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), IPAQ score, walking speed, left Q-angle, or right Q-angle (all P > 0.05). In contrast, a statistically significant difference was found in the bilateral Q-angle difference between the two groups (P < 0.01) (Table 1).

Kinematic performance during level walking

During a single level walking gait cycle, statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in internal/external rotation of the knee joint during the 69–74% gait cycle phase (P = 0.042). Significant differences in flexion/extension were also detected between the groups during the 75–90% gait cycle phase (P = 0.012) (Fig. 3). In contrast, no significant intergroup difference intervals were found in adduction/abduction, medial/lateral translation, distal/proximal translation, or anterior/posterior translation (all P > 0.05) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study evaluated the task-dependent effects of Q-angle asymmetry on knee 6DOF kinematics in a healthy young population during three tasks using three-dimensional motion capture technology. The main findings can be summarized in three points. First, Q-angle asymmetry induced only task- and phase-specific alterations in angular kinematics, primarily manifesting as differences in rotation and flexion-extension angles during the late stance phases of level and upslope walking, with no significant effects on translational parameters across all tasks. Second, no intergroup differences in any kinematic parameters were observed during the high-impact landing task. Finally, over the 18-month follow-up period, Q-angle asymmetry did not translate into early clinical manifestations of knee pain or functional decline. These results indicate that the biomechanical effect of Q-angle asymmetry in an asymptomatic young population is limited and highly dependent on the task context, suggesting that its clinical value as a standalone risk marker may need re-evaluation.

Interpretation of task-specific kinematic differences

During level walking, the QA group exhibited greater knee internal rotation during the 69–74% phase of the gait cycle, corresponding to the transition from late stance to early swing. This may originate from imbalanced mediolateral pulling forces on the tibia during quadriceps activation due to Q-angle asymmetry, thereby altering the rotational moment arm of the tibia in the transverse plane [5]. Subsequently, during mid-swing (75–90%), where the knee normally extends in preparation for heel strike, the flexion/extension difference observed in the QA group suggests that asymmetrical Q-angle variations affected the coordination of knee extension trajectories between limbs. This aligns with the view proposed by van Eijden et al. that the complex motion of the distal quadriceps can significantly influence biomechanical parameters [34].

In contrast to level walking, 10% gradient upslope walking showed a significant difference only in knee flexion/extension during the 74–87% gait cycle phase. During uphill locomotion, compared to level walking, the knee requires a greater flexion angle during the stance phase to counteract the anteriorly directed ground reaction force [35, 36]. This leads to increased quadriceps force and extensor moment, making control of knee flexion-extension in the sagittal plane the primary task [37, 38]. For healthy individuals with Q-angle asymmetry, this biomechanical adaptation may amplify abnormalities in sagittal plane knee motion.

Furthermore, no differences in any of the 6DOF knee kinematic parameters were found between the two groups during either the preparation or landing phases of the landing task. On the one hand, the landing task protocol adopted in this study is consistent with that of Erdman et al. [39], whose research demonstrated that a horizontal jump distance of one-third and one-half of an individual’s height elicits significantly less task-induced limb asymmetry compared with zero-distance landing, while still maintaining meaningful frontal and transverse plane biomechanical demands. On the other hand, the absence of differences in the 6DOF kinematic parameters may be due to the inherent characteristic of the landing task as a high-impact movement, which relies more on whole-body rapid coordination patterns and pre-activation strategies [39, 40]. For instance, handball athletes in a fatigued state can employ movement pattern adjustments as a compensatory strategy when performing drop vertical jumps [41].

Notably, no significant translational motion in any direction was observed across all three functional movements. This suggests that translational stability of the knee during motion may depend primarily on the integrity of passive restraint structures such as the cruciate ligaments, collateral ligaments and joint capsule, and the congruency of articular surfaces, rather than being solely dictated by the quadriceps force line. This viewpoint is consistent with Zeng et al., who emphasized the importance of translational stability during functional tasks [12].

Early clinical implications of Q-angle symmetry

The findings of this study indicate that, in a healthy young population, isolated Q-angle asymmetry is not a reliable marker for knee injury risk, as its clinical relevance depends on the specific functional task. For example, during daily activities such as level or upslope walking, Q-angle asymmetry may be associated with subtle angular kinematic abnormalities, but these do not translate into short-term functional impairment. Therefore, clinical screening for Q-angle-related knee risks should not rely solely on static Q-angle measurements. Instead, it should incorporate dynamic assessments during medium- or high-demand tasks, such as upslope walking, to identify individuals with persistent kinematic deficits. For individuals with Q-angle asymmetry accompanied by mild knee discomfort, rehabilitation programs should prioritize the control of knee flexion-extension in the sagittal plane during the swing phase. Exercise modalities such as isometric quadriceps activation may help correct the kinematic differences observed in this study [42, 43]. The 18-month follow-up results showed no adverse knee functional outcomes in the QA group, indicating that mild Q-angle asymmetry is well-tolerated in healthy young individuals. However, this does not preclude long-term risks or risks in high-risk populations. Future long-term cohort studies should track the incidence of knee injury or degeneration in individuals with Q-angle asymmetry, with particular attention to those with additional risk factors, such as individuals with obesity and participants in high-impact sports.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was drawn from healthy Chinese university students; therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating the findings to other age groups, ethnicities, or populations with pre-existing pathologies. Second, the lack of synchronized data, such as electromyography, means we were unable to elucidate the neuromuscular control mechanisms underlying the observed kinematic differences. Third, the kinematic characteristics of walking tasks performed on a treadmill in a laboratory setting may differ from those of overground walking. Additionally, while the 18-month follow-up period was sufficient for assessing early functional outcomes, it is inadequate for predicting the long-term risk of degenerative conditions such as osteoarthritis. Finally, the IPAQ, VAS and Kujala scales used may have ceiling effects and limited responsiveness in healthy asymptomatic young adults, potentially restricting the detection of subtle kinematics-related functional changes, and related results are interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that in healthy Chinese university students, Q-angle asymmetry (defined as a bilateral difference > 3°) primarily modulated knee rotation during late stance and flexion/extension during mid-swing in level walking, as well as flexion/extension during late stance in upslope walking, while it had no significant effect on the kinematics of landing task. These findings suggest that the independent biomechanical effect of Q-angle asymmetry in an asymptomatic young population is limited and highly dependent on the type of task performed. Therefore, caution is warranted when considering it as a universal risk marker for knee injury.
