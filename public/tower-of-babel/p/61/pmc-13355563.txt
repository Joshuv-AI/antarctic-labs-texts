Statistical inference for saved time based on disease progression curves in Alzheimer’s disease research
Authors: Guogen Shan, Yahui Zhang, Aidong A Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSaved time is an easy interpretation metric that provides information to patients on how long a new treatment can delay the disease progression in time as compared to the placebo. The frequently used projection approach for saved time estimation utilizes limited information from the available data. To address that limitation, the area above the disease progression curve was proposed to estimate saved time by using more information from data. However, it is a challenge to derive the closed-form statistical inference (e.g., confidence interval of saved time) as area above the curve is quadratic relative to visit time and saved time based on the curves is nonlinearly related to the area above the curve. In this article, we derived the closed-form variance of saved time based on the area above curve approach by using Taylor expansion. We then compared the performance of the closed-form method and the existing simulation-based method to construct the confidence interval for saved time based on the area above curve approach with regard to coverage probability and interval width under various scenarios. The simulation results indicate that the proposed closed-form method has similar performance as compared to the existing method, while the closed-form method can be computationally easy in practice without additional simulations in the existing method. Data from the completed phase 2 donanemab trial were used to illustrate the application of the proposed method.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Closed-form confidence interval, Disease progression trajectory, Restricted mean disease progression scale, Saved time

Introduction

In Alzheimer’s disease (AD) clinical trials, each patient is often scheduled to have multiple visits to investigate the disease progression over time with regard to the commonly used outcome measures, such as clinical dementia rating - sum of boxes (CDR-SB) [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. When patient-level data are available, mixed-effect models and the natural cubic spline models for repeated measures may be used for data analysis with the focus on comparing a new treatment with the placeb at the pre-specified visit (e.g., 18 months in a disease-modifying therapy (DMT) trial) [6], [7]. In general, it is often a challenge to obtain the patient-level data from the recently completed trials [8]. However, the summary statistics for these outcome measures are often available from public resources. Such data can be used to estimate time saving of a new drug as compared to the placebo. Saved time can be viewed as the horizontal difference between the two groups measured in time by months or weeks while the traditional treatment-placebo difference may be considered as the vertical difference at the pre-specified visit from the fitted statistical model.

Saved time is frequently calculated by the projection approach that estimates time saving in the placebo disease progression curve whose disease progression is the same as the disease progression in the treatment group at the pre-specified follow-up time [9], [10], [11]. The projection approach is relatively easy to use in practice. But, this approach utilizes the limited information from the available disease progression curves from both groups. Recently, increasing attention has been given to time-based treatment effect measures and longitudinal disease progression modeling in Alzheimer’s disease and other progressive disorders. In particular, progression model for repeated measures (PMRM) has been proposed to characterize treatment effects through delayed disease progression trajectories using longitudinal repeated-measure data [12], [13]. The concept of area above the disease progression curve was introduced to estimate saved time [13], [14], [15]. In that approach, the disease progression curve of the treatment group is fully used, and the majority of the placebo disease progression curve is also utilized to improve saved time estimation. The area above the curve method can improve the precision of saved time estimation with small standard deviation as more data are used in the approach [13]. As compared to the traditional projection approach, the area above the curve approach is more likely to detect the treatment effectiveness when a new treatment has an early treatment effect or a constant treatment effect.

Saved time based on the area above the curve utilizes more information from the available data than the projection approach. Standard error (SE) of saved time based on the area above the curve is much more complicated than SE of the saved time based on the projection approach. In the projection approach, the calculation of SE involves two adjacent visits measurements [16], [17], [18], while the area above the curve approach involves all prior visits measurements that significantly increases the complexity in deriving the closed-form variance [19]. For that reason, SE of saved time based on the area above the curve is currently estimated by using the simulation-based approach. For each observed data, we first estimate the disease progression curves, and the associated variance–covariance matrix of the outcome. We then conduct simulations to estimate SE of saved time. This approach is relatively time consuming and the results may be affected by simulation errors.

In this article, to address that challenge and limitation from the existing methods, we derive the closed-form SE of saved time based on the area above the curve approach in Section 2. In Section 3, we compared the performance of the closed-form method and the traditional simulation-based method with regard to coverage probability and interval width under various scenarios. Real data from the phase 2 donanemab trial were used in Section 3 to illustrate the application of the closed-form method [20]. Lastly, we provided some comments in Section 4.

Methods

The traditional projection approach calculates time saving of a new drug as compared to the placebo by estimating the time in the placebo disease progression curve that has the same degree of disease progression in the drug group at the pre-specified follow-up visit (e.g., 18 months). Saved time’s SE is estimated by the same projection approach using the SE of treatment effect at the follow-up visit. Recently, we introduced a restricted mean disease progression (RMDP) scale to measure the disease progression from time 0 to the pre-specified time t in the time saving calculation [13]. Simulation studies

We considered three common disease progression patterns: (1): proportional time delay: ΔD(tϕ)=ΔP(t), (2) proportional reduction in decline: ΔD(t)/ΔP(t)=λ, and (3) constant difference: ΔD(t+θ)=ΔP(t). When ϕ=1.20, there is 20% time delayed on average in disease progression for patients in the treatment. For a study with 18 months, it can be delayed by 3.6 months at the end of the trial for patients treated with the new treatment as compared to patients in the placebo group. In the second pattern with λ=0.8, the disease progression in the treatment group is 80% of that in the placebo group during the study time. In the constant difference pattern, the value of θ represents the constant saved time in the treatment group as compared to the placebo group. Suppose θ is 3 months, a new drug is expected to constantly delay disease progression by 3 months as compared to the placebo during the study.

For the disease progression in the placebo group, we used the change score of CDR-SB from the phase 2 donanemab as the first placebo disease progression Δp(t) [20]. We observed that the placebo disease progression may not be a linear function of time with the CDR-SB worsening by 0.38 point from visit 3 to visit 4, 0.12 point between visit 4 and visit 5, and 0.25 point from visit 5 to the last visit (visit 6). From visit 3 to visit 6, the average CDR-SB score was worse by 0.75 point. In addition to the linear pattern with 0.25 point between every two visits during the last four visits, we also considered other placebo disease progression pattern (e.g., reduced disease progression over time, worsening disease progression over time, non-linear disease progression). In total, we studied 9 placebo disease progression patterns, see Fig. 2.

Results

We compare the performance of the proposed closed-form method and the simulation-based method to construct CI for the RMDP saved time under the 9 placebo disease progression patterns, 3 treatment group patterns (proportional time delay, proportional reduction in decline, and constant difference), having five sample sizes from 100 per group to 500 per group (100 to 500 by 100). The overall objective of this article is to investigate whether the closed-form method has similar coverage probability and interval width as the simulation-based method.

We first studied the scenario with the proportional time delayed in the treatment group as compared to the placebo group, having the time delayed parameter ϕ from 1.12 to 1.20. In Fig. 3, we presented the coverage probability and 95% confidence interval width for the two methods as a function of sample size from 100 per group to 500 per group, using the observed placebo disease progression curve from the phase 2 donanemab trial and ϕ=1.16 (top), and the linear curve in the last 4 visits and ϕ=1.20 (bottom). It is as expected that the interval width becomes shorter as the sample size goes up. The interval width difference between the proposed closed-form method and the simulation-based method is negligible when sample size is 200 per group or above. The simulation-based method could have slightly wider intervals than the proposed method when sample size is small (e.g., 100 per group). For these two scenarios, the simulation-based method may have conservative intervals with the actual coverage probability above the nominal level when sample size is small (e.g., 100 per group). As sample size goes up, their coverage probabilities are very close to the nominal level. In Fig. 4, we compared their coverage probability and interval width as a function of ϕ give the sample size of 200 per group. The interval width is independent of the value of ϕ, while the coverage probability could be slightly affected the value of ϕ. Both methods have the CP being close to the nominal level between 94% to 96%.

Example

In the phase 2 donanemab trial [20], the primary outcome measure was the change at week 76 from baseline in the score on the Integrated Alzheimer’s Disease Rating Scale (iADRS). The iARDS is a simple composite score from two components: the 13-item Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale and the Alzheimer’s Disease Cooperative Study–Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Inventory. The range of the iARDS is from 0 to 144 with a lower value representing worse cognitive and daily living performance.

In that trial, each participant had the scheduled follow-up visits at 12, 24, 36, 52, 64, and 76 weeks. The iARDS change at each visit from baseline and the variance of the iARDS change were provided by Mintun et al. [20]. In order to conduct simulation-based approach, we assumed the AR(1) correlation structure with the correlation of ρ|i−j| between visit i and visit j, and value of ρ is assumed to be 0.5 here. The RMDP saved time was estimated as 4.44 months. The closed-form CI was estimated as (2.22, 6.67) months. As compared to the simulation-based CI (2.08, 7.03) months, the lower bound of the closed-form CI is larger. For the computational time, the closed-form method took less than 0.05 s to calculate the CI by using a personal computer while the simulation-based method needed 36 times longer than the closed-form method when 1000 simulations were used.

Conclusions

Based on the results from the simulation section, interval width of the two methods is very close to each other. Their coverage probabilities are often very similar when sample size is 200 per group or above. When sample size is small (e.g., 100 per group), their coverage probability difference could be as large as 1%, and their coverage probabilities could be 1% to 2% less than the nominal level in some cases. We also noticed that when sample size is small, the simulation-based method’s coverage probability is often higher than or equal to that using the closed-form method [21], [22]. When both have conservative CIs, the closed-form method has the coverage probability being closer to the nominal level. When both methods have the CP below the nominal level, the simulation-based method has better performance but their difference in CP is often small.

Through various simulations, we find that the new closed-form method has very similar performance as compared to the commonly used simulation-based method: with similar coverage probability and interval width [23], [24], [25]. Based on that finding, we would recommend using the closed-form method in the future for the statistical inference based on the RMDP saved time.
