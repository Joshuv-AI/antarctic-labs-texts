The Relationship Between Games Missed for Rest or Load Management and Injury in the National Basketball Association, 2014–15 Through 2022–23
Authors: Mackenzie M. Herzog, Alexandra Brink, Rahul Gondalia, John P. DiFiori, Christina D. Mack
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOne often-stated aim of load management in elite athletes is injury reduction. However, current literature on the efficacy and effectiveness of specific load management strategies to reduce injury risk is limited.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between missing regular season games for rest or load management and subsequent injury in the National Basketball Association (NBA).MethodsA 9-year observational analysis of a select group of NBA players participating in each of the 2014–15 through 2022–23 seasons was conducted using the NBA Injury and Illness Database. Standardized, league-wide player participation and health information rules require reporting of any game missed due to injury, illness, rest, or load management. The main outcome was a game injury that resulted in two or more games missed consecutively. Hazard ratios were calculated using the Anderson–Gill modification of the Cox proportional hazards model. Injury rate ratios were calculated using Poisson regression. Analyses were adjusted for age, number of prior injuries, games missed due to injury or other reasons, and average minutes played per regular season.ResultsA total of 1233 player-seasons contributing 1,538,917 player-minutes of NBA game play were included. From 2014–15 to 2022–23, games missed for rest, load management, or 1-game absences increased by 125% (+ 211 games), and 2+ consecutive games missed injuries increased by 43% (+ 61 injuries). Among players who missed games for rest or load management (n = 129,578 player-minutes), there was no significant difference in injury risk compared with players who did not miss games for rest or load management (n = 1,409,339 player-minutes; 1–2 games missed: hazard ratio [HR] = 1.24, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.92–1.65; 3–5 games missed: HR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.39–1.80; 6 or more games missed: HR = 2.16, 95% CI 0.53–5.78).ConclusionsNBA players who missed regular season games for rest or load management did not have a decreased risk of subsequent injury in the same season. Further studies that add assessment of training and internal loads will offer additional insights.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40279-026-02457-w.

Key Points

This study analyzed nine seasons of audited NBA electronic medical record data and found no significant association between missing games for rest or load management and a lower risk of subsequent injuries.This study builds on current sports medicine literature that has assessed load management strategies to improve player health and it encourages further research into additional load-related factors that may impact injury rates, such as off-court training and internal player load.

Introduction

Load management in sport is the practice of monitoring and manipulating training load to maximize performance while minimizing injury risk [1]. Training load consists of external load, which is the physical work performed by the athlete during training, and internal load, which is an athlete’s physiological and psychological response during training [2]. Load management is an increasingly utilized strategy across a wide range of athletic settings and populations, particularly with the expansion of technologies that quantify internal and external athlete load, such as wearable devices and optical tracking systems [3–5]. Although one often-stated aim of the application of load management is injury reduction, current literature is limited on both the association between specific load variables, injury risk, and the efficacy and effectiveness of specific load management strategies for injury reduction [3, 4, 6–20]. An athlete’s response to load after exposure (i.e., training effect) includes changes in performance, physiological, and biomechanical measures following training [2]. These responses are highly individualized, shaped by contextual (e.g., sport-specific training and competition, schedule, and travel) and individual (e.g., age, fatigue, injury history) factors [1, 11, 21–28]. Therefore, monitoring and manipulating load for fitness, performance enhancement, and injury reduction is complex and difficult to generalize across individual athletes, sports, and competition levels [1, 5, 17].

Literature to date does not provide clear evidence on the association between specific load variables and injury risk or the effectiveness of load management techniques. Systematic reviews in multiple athletic populations at various competition levels found a moderate association between training load and injury risk [3–5]. However, reviews in team-based sports (including soccer, rugby, and Australian rules football) [19] and elite soccer were unable to confirm associations between Global Positioning System (GPS)-derived external training load metrics and injury risk due to limited high quality studies and variability in injury definitions, statistical methods, load thresholds, and GPS devices [12]. Similarly, there was not a clear association between higher session frequency (i.e., fewer rest days) or game/match competition and injury risk [4, 12].

Reviews of acute to chronic workload ratio (ACWR) have reported higher injury risk associated with relatively elevated ACWR [4, 6, 10, 14]. However, ACWR has been widely debated due to its statistical limitations and lack of predictive ability [11, 29–31].

Evidence in elite male basketball athletes is similarly limited and has not demonstrated a clear association between load variables and injury risk [15, 18, 28, 32–34]. Load management-related research among National Basketball Association (NBA) players specifically has been limited by use of unvalidated publicly available data [35], lack of player injury history, methodological limitations, and/or small sample sizes [15, 34].

Despite the lack of strong evidence linking load variables and injury or supporting the effectiveness of specific load management practices in reducing injury, missing NBA games for the purposes of rest or load management occurs commonly—internal NBA data showed a significant increase in the number of games players missed per season from the 1990s (10.6 games missed) to 2020s (23.9 games missed) [36]. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between the number of NBA regular season games missed for rest or load management and subsequent injury in the regular season (aim 1) and playoffs (aim 2).

Methods

An observational, retrospective study was conducted to describe the relationship between games missed for rest or load management and player injuries reported in the NBA Injury and Illness Database. Injury data were entered into a league-wide electronic medical record (EMR) and integrated with player game participation information for the 2014–15 through 2022–23 regular seasons (aim 1) and playoffs (aim 2). Data entry was mandated and standardized across all 30 teams. Team medical staff were required to document player participation data in the league-wide EMR for all NBA games, including whether a player participated in the game, and if not, why [37]. If a player was not available to participate in a game, specified reasons included injury/illness, “rest” (available for the 2014–15 through 2018–19 seasons), healthy rest (“rest (healthy)” and “rest—load management (healthy)” options available for the 2019–20 season onward), or load management (“load management (injury/illness associated)” and “load management—return from injury management)” options available for the 2019–20 season onward) (Table 1). Medical staff were required to record injuries that result in a missed NBA game or practice, the issuing of a prescription medication, and/or the need for a physician visit or specific medical care in the EMR [37]. During the study period, EMR data were reviewed and audited against box score data and other public sources (e.g., media reports), with routine data queries sent to teams weekly and monthly to clarify player participation, as needed, to ensure no data were missing [37]. Approvals were obtained from the Hospital for Special Surgery Institutional Review Board (IRB study no. 2024-0077), the NBA, and the Research Committee of the NBA Physicians Association. Study Population

A total of 150 players per NBA season from 2014–15 through 2022–23 were considered for the analysis, defined as those who were (i) an All-Star or All-NBA player in any of the prior three seasons or (ii) a top-10 draft pick in that season’s NBA draft, followed by (iii) the players with the most total minutes played in the prior season who were not already identified in categories (i) and (ii). This population was nearly three times more likely to miss games for rest or load management and played 10 more minutes per game on average versus all other players. This selection criteria ensured a relatively elite and homogeneous player population who play the most minutes, making them more susceptible to in-game injury and more likely to miss games for health-related reasons rather than performance-related decisions (e.g., coach’s decision). Sensitivity analyses restricting to “star players”, defined as those who were an All-Star or All-NBA player in any of the prior three seasons (inclusive of the season of interest), were performed. Players in their rookie season were excluded because they lacked comprehensive EMR-reported injury history, and those without one game of regular season participation were excluded for that particular season since they would not have been at risk for sustaining a game injury for that season.

The 2019–20 through 2021–22 seasons were impacted by the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Games during the NBA Bubble (July to August 2020) were excluded [38]. The 2020–21 regular season was shortened to 72 games per team versus 82 games per team in 2014–15 through 2018–19, 2021–22, and 2022–23. Sensitivity analyses excluding 2020–21 and 2021–22 seasons were performed given that decision making regarding rest or load management differed (e.g., COVID-19 protocols, player availability, game schedule, travel, etc.) in those seasons.

Exposure: Regular Season Games Missed for Rest or Load Management

The primary exposure was a game missed for rest or load management. Analyses used two definitions for quantifying games missed for rest or load management: (i) “Excluding 1-game absences due to injury”, defined as a game missed for EMR-reported reason of “healthy rest” or “load management” and (ii) “Including 1-game absences due to injury”, defined as all games that met criteria for (i) plus EMR-reported injury where the player missed one consecutive game only (was available to participate in both the game preceding and following the missed game).

Outcome: Injuries Resulting in Two or More Consecutive Games Missed

The study outcome was any injury that led to two or more games missed consecutively (“2+ consecutive games missed injuries”), as these injuries are clinically meaningful and one-game absences were included in one version of the exposure definition. Primary analyses included in-game injuries that occurred during the regular season (aim 1) and playoffs (aim 2) from 2014–15 through 2022–23. Sensitivity analyses were performed restricting to noncontact in-game injuries.

Covariates

Potential confounders were defined a priori and included player age at the start of the regular season, injury history in the prior season and current season to date, games missed due to (i) injury or (ii) any reason other than rest or load management in the prior season and current season to date, and average minutes played per game in the prior regular season and current regular season to date.

Sensitivity analyses were performed adjusting for additional in-game optical player tracking metrics, including total load accumulated on court, total time in high speed (defined as time spent on court moving over 14 feet/s), and total time in high acceleration/deceleration states (defined as time spent on court accelerating or decelerating at least 12 ft/s2). Optical player tracking metrics were obtained via motion capture technology, which was in place in each NBA arena since 2013–14 (SportVU [Stats Perform; London, England]: 2013–14 through 2016–17, Second Spectrum [Genius Sports; London, England]: 2017–18 through 2022–23) to collect two-dimensional location data on players 25 times per second during NBA games. Raw data collected by SportVU and Second Spectrum were all harmonized and aggregated by Second Spectrum. Second Spectrum then derived load metrics for each player for each game that were analyzed in this study. Validity of the Second Spectrum technology was assessed and reported by the Federation Internationale de Football Association (FIFA) Quality Programme for Electronic Performance and Tracking Systems [39]. In addition, surgeries and noncontact lower extremity soft tissue (NCLEST) injuries, which may have elevated injury recurrence, in the past year and past 5 years were described.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics (percentages, means, ranges, medians, and interquartile ranges (IQR)) were calculated for all exposures, outcomes, and covariates. For aim 1, survival analysis using the Andersen–Gill modification of the Cox proportional hazards model calculated the risk of in-game injury using hazard ratios for each number of games missed for rest or load management in the regular season to date, compared with players without games missed for rest or load management. Time at risk was calculated using player-minutes played, and players were censored at the occurrence of a nongame injury (e.g., an injury that occurred during practice or other setting) that resulted in two or more consecutive games missed. Analyses were stratified by time to first injury within the season (index injury) versus time to subsequent injury. Player time at risk was calculated using player-minutes of game play, and adjusted analyses accounted for potential confounders.

Comparisons between subgroups were conducted using Wei–Lin robust standard errors accounting for a player’s individual injury risk. In addition, frailty models accounted for heterogeneity of player-level injury risk which may reflect unmeasured confounding factors.

For aim 2, injury rates were calculated as the number of playoff game injuries divided by the total number of player-minutes played (i.e., to account for the different number of minutes each player played in playoff games) and reported as injuries per 10,000 player-minutes. Poisson regressions calculated crude and adjusted injury rates, rate ratios, and 95% confidence intervals. Rate ratios compared the rate of playoff injuries among players with regular season games missed for rest or load management to players without any regular season games missed for rest or load management. Adjusted analyses accounted for player age at the start of the regular season and injury history in the prior season.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between games missed for rest and load management and regular season/playoff injury in the NBA, using nine seasons of high-quality, audited data from the NBA Injury and Illness database [37]. The results demonstrate that while games missed for rest, load management, or one-game absences reported as due to injury have increased, the increase did not coincide with an overall decrease in the risk of injuries, even when adjusting for relevant factors such as age, injury history, prior games missed, and minutes played in the prior season and current season to date.

It is important to note that injury in sports is widely recognized as multifactorial [40, 41], arising from the interplay between biomechanical, physiological, contextual factors, and history of prior injury [17]. Missing games for rest or load management represents only one component of player health management, and when examined as the specific focus, was not shown to meaningfully alter injury risk in this study. Nonetheless, it is important to recognize that the evolving demands of NBA gameplay [42] may have influenced player health-management strategies and contributed to the observed increases in players missing games for rest or load management.

The lack of a protective association between missing games for rest or load management and subsequent injury risk may be partly explained by the “workload–injury paradox”, which suggests that higher chronic workloads can lower injury risk through beneficial physiological adaptations [17]. In the context of the NBA, the regular season schedule typically includes three to four games per week, which along with frequent travel, limits the time available for noncompetition loading. Missing games for rest or load management may moderate fatigue, though reduced exposure to NBA gameplay may affect chronic workload and injury resilience. This hypothesis is consistent with the “workload–training paradox” and supports the concept that missing competitions without maintaining chronic workload may be insufficient to mitigate injury risk.

Prior literature on the association between load and load management strategies for injury reduction is limited [3, 4, 6–20]. There were two prior studies among NBA players that investigated load-related metrics and injury [15, 34]. Both studies used publicly available injury data, which are often limited by lack of standardization, quality control measures, and specificity (e.g., injury mechanism, timing, diagnosis) [35]. Furthermore, those studies did not control for potentially biasing factors such as player age, injury history, and playing time. The first study was an analysis of 34 NBA player injuries that found that more minutes played per game over the cumulative three, five, and ten games preceding the injury was associated with higher occurrence or injuries leading to ten or more games [15]. However, that analysis relied on unadjusted associations and stringent exclusion criteria resulting in a small sample size, thereby limiting generalizability to the broader NBA population. Furthermore, the authors highlighted that the 34 injuries analyzed include contact-related injuries, which may not be applicable to studies of load. The second study analyzed all active NBA players from 2015–16 through 2020–21 seasons and found that minutes played per game was associated with season-ending injuries [34]. That analysis did not account for individual injury history or assess the injury rate. Injury rates account for exposure (i.e., the time at risk for injury), which is essential in analyzing the impact of all factors related to injury incidence, including load measures [34, 43].

Available data on the effect of internal and external load on injury risk in professional basketball outside of the NBA similarly showed mixed results generated from studies that had small sample sizes and/or a variety of methodological concerns. Two studies of internal load, as measured by perceived exertion, found inconsistent results, with the first, conducted among 13 players in Australia/New Zealand, finding moderate (versus low and high) perceived exertion as beneficial [28] and the second study, conducted among 35 players in Italy, found no associations across multiple perceived exertion metrics [33]. Similar inconsistency was observed in two separate studies of objectively captured external load metrics and injury. The first, among 16 players in Australia/New Zealand, found no association between multiple external load metrics and injury [18]. The second study of 33 professional basketball players found that fewer decelerations and less distance covered was associated with higher injury risk. That study utilized publicly available game data and had no measures of individual player injury history (which can affect individual injury rates) [32].

This analysis sought to address several methodological limitations from these prior studies. Specifically, inclusion of important player-level characteristics, such as age, medically reported injury history, and minutes played per game helped adjust for potential biases. In addition, this study used frailty models to account for heterogeneity of player-level injury risk which may reflect unmeasured confounding factors. Such adjustments are important to consider, since decisions to miss games for rest or load management are not randomly assigned. Nonetheless, residual confounding by indication may still persist.

Indeed, unmeasured player-level characteristics and internal workload were unable to be accounted for in analyses. For example, missed games for rest or load management may have been inconsistently applied and/or may have been counterbalanced by unmeasured internal workload. Furthermore, in determining a player’s training and competition schedule, including missing games for rest or load management, teams utilize a wide variety of information, both objective and subjective. This includes tools such as force plate assessments, acceleration and deceleration data, average game speed, game distance covered, scheduling, travel, subjective measures of fatigue, and direct player feedback. The absence of an observed association between missing games for rest or load management and an increase in subsequent injury risk may therefore reflect inadequate adjustment for potential confounding by such factors.

Findings from this study were tested against varying definitions for games missed for rest or load management (including games missed due to “healthy rest” or “load management,” and/or the foregoing definition plus one-game absences due to injury). Adjusting the definition to include one-game absences due to injury likely includes some games that were in fact missed due to injury rather than something more akin to load management. However, including one-game absences due to injury in the definition may have also reduced misclassification of games missed for rest or load management in 2014–15 through 2018–19 seasons, before load management designations were available in the EMR. Therefore, restricting the definition to only games missed for rest or load management likely undercounts the true number of games missed for rest or load management. Nonetheless, analyses performed using each definition (i.e., likely slightly over- or underestimating games missed for rest or load management) delivered the same results.

There are other meaningful sources of heterogeneity in the definitions of games missed for rest or load management. Participation reporting in the EMR from 2019–20 through 2022–23 distinguished between rest among healthy players and various forms of load management associated with injury. Proactive rest in healthy players versus load managing due to injury represent distinct clinical scenarios; however, these distinctions were unable to be directly accounted for across the study period. While findings from this study remained consistent across multiple definitions for games missed for rest or load management, the observed null associations may reflect heterogeneity in exposure across these definitions.

One potential misinterpretation of these null findings could be a suggestion that missing games for rest or load management reduces “high-risk” players’ injury risk to something more similar to other players’ injury risk. However, epidemiological methods were used to take into account potential risk factors that may make a player both more likely to miss games for rest or load management and more likely to sustain an injury. Subsequently, the comparison group consisted of players without any games missed for rest or load management, including “high-risk” players who later went on to miss a game for rest or load management. Also, players were no longer followed after sustaining a 2+ consecutive games missed injury, so players were not missing games for rest or load management for an injury sustained in the regular season. Furthermore, findings were robust to sensitivity analyses among players with and without a prior injury history in the last year and restricted to players with prior injury in the same regular season.

To our knowledge, this study is the first in professional basketball to examine the relationship between missing games for rest or load management and injury incidence over multiple seasons. This study utilized high-quality, medically reported injury and participation data from the NBA Injury and Illness database— a standardized and audited resource that compiles player health and participation information reported by medical staff across all 30 teams.

With these data, important player-level characteristics, such as age, injury history, and minutes played helped to account for potential confounders. Also, the analytic approach used a number of different study designs, statistical approaches, and modeling strategies to methodologically account for potential biases. In addition to using varying definitions for the exposure (games missed for rest or load management), sensitivity analyses using different player populations and injury outcomes were conducted to ensure robustness of the main results. Finally, potential differences in association by timing within a season and differences in competitive play were assessed by analyzing regular season and playoff injuries separately. Across all analyses, there was no association found between missed games for rest or load management and lower injury risk.

Limitations

Any study of rest or load management strategies in elite basketball is subject to important limitations. First, although analyses were conducted using nine seasons of data, the ability to investigate long-term impacts of games missed for rest or load management on, for example, career longevity is limited.

Second, while the study is strengthened by the volume of data, there may be differences over time that cannot be fully addressed. Decision making regarding rest or load management likely evolved over time. For instance, from the 2017–18 through 2022–23 seasons, the NBA instituted its Player Resting Policy, which limited teams from resting healthy players under certain circumstances (e.g., nationally televised games, away games). In addition, team-level circumstances, such team record, roster, schedule, and player health management, were also likely to influence decisions. This was heightened when the COVID-19 pandemic affected player availability, game schedule, and travel during the 2020–21 and 2021–22 seasons.

Third, while nine seasons of data provided sufficient power, the number of injuries included in analyses was small in some subsets. Specifically, when investigating playoff injuries, and in sensitivity analyses involving noncontact injuries and among star players, small sample sizes resulted in limited statistical power and wide confidence intervals. Findings from these analyses should therefore be interpreted with caution. Moreover, comprehensive investigation of a player’s pre-NBA injury history was outside the scope of this study.

Fourth, analyses could not account for or broadly investigate the effect of managing noncompetition load or internal load because detailed data on practice participation, training and internal load were not available on a league-wide basis. In addition, this study did not investigate load management practices outside of missing games, including managing player loads during practice and training, or managing game minutes, player role, or player assignments to manage intensity. As such, this study is focused on the relationship of game participation and injury and does not serve as an analysis of total workload and injury.

Fifth, because one-game absences due to injury are included in the games missed for rest or load management definition for the purposes of comprehensively evaluating all games that may have been missed due to these practices, injury outcomes were restricted to injuries that led to two or more consecutive games missed. Findings described in this study are therefore applicable to more significant, multigame injuries. There may be other injuries that did not result in at least two games missed that may be influenced by frequency of game participation.

Sixth, the rationale for missing games for rest or load management also includes potential fitness or performance enhancements—strategically resting players to optimize performance (e.g., scoring) and team success in subsequent games—which may have influenced the occurrence of missed games for rest and load management during the study period. The impact of missing games to optimize performance was not an aim of this study.

Finally, because these analyses focused on NBA players, the data should not be generalized to other populations, such as youth basketball players, where, owing to various factors, such as musculoskeletal development, additional periods of rest may be recommended [44].

Conclusion

Using nine NBA seasons of medically reported player participation and injury data, this study found players who missed regular season games for rest or load management did not have a lower risk of subsequent injury in the regular season or in the playoffs, even after adjusting for relevant factors such as age and injury history. These findings were consistent across varying definitions of rest or load management and injury outcomes, player subgroups hypothesized to benefit from these practices, and multiple sensitivity analyses.

When considering applying load management strategies for the purpose of injury reduction, further research is needed to better understand the balance between periodically and systemically reducing internal and external load with the benefits of maintaining load stimulus. Future studies should therefore consider assessment of individualized nongame training exposure, internal load, and training effects to gain additional insights into injury etiology and further inform load management strategies.
