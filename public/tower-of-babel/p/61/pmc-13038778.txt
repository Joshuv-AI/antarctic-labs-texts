Fungal immunomodulatory proteins skew macrophages towards a pro-inflammatory and tumour inhibitory phenotype
Authors: Yusi Liu, Paulina A Krzysica, Zhen Li, Harry J Wichers, Shanna Bastiaan-Net, Tamara Hoppenbrouwers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) are associated with poor clinical outcome in cancer patients, making them key targets in immunotherapy. TAMs mainly consist of anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages promoting tumour growth, metastasis and immunosuppression. Fungal immunomodulatory protein from Nectria haematococca (FIP-nha) is a potential anti-tumour agent, notably inhibiting tumour cells and exerting immunomodulatory activity on macrophages. However, the anti-tumour activity of FIP-nha via TAMs modulation is still unclear. Here, the immunomodulatory activity of rFIP-nha on M1 and M2 macrophages was explored, as well as its anti-tumour activity via macrophage modulation, using M2 macrophages as TAM representatives, through an intricate co-culture design with A549 lung cancer cells.MethodsTHP-1-monocyte derived M2 macrophages were treated with rFIP-nha and analysed phenotypically. Subsequently, rFIP-nha treated M2 macrophages were co-cultured with A549 cells to investigate anti-tumour activity via macrophage modulation.ResultsrFIP-nha exposure decreased macrophages phagocytosis activity, enhanced IL-1β, IL-12 and IL-10 cytokine secretion, and modulated specific cell surface markers for M2 (CD163 and CD206) towards those that are typical for M1 (CD68 and CD80) polarization. rFIP-nha treated M2 macrophages polarized towards a tumour inhibitory phenotype which, when co-cultured with A549 cells, resulted in greater reduction of tumour cell proliferation compared to A549 cells co-cultured with M2 or M1 macrophages. Moreover, rFIP-nha exacerbated tumour inhibition and cell death.ConclusionrFIP-nha inhibited tumour growth in two distinct ways, by targeting tumour cells directly and by directing macrophage polarization towards a tumour inhibitory phenotype. This dual bioactivity makes FIP-nha interesting as an additive support in cancer immunotherapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04376-1.Keywords: Fungal immunomodulatory protein (FIP), Macrophages, Polarization, Co-culture, Tumour inhibition, A549 cells

Introduction

Cancer is a disease which is not solely driven by the uncontrolled multiplication and accumulation of tumour cells [1]. Non-cancerous cells in tumours, such as immune cells, also play a critical role in tumour development, progression, and metastasis, and thus also in therapy outcomes [2]. Especially the development of tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs), which are abundant in the tumour microenvironment (TME) of most cancer types, are generally associated with poor prognosis and correlates with chemotherapy resistance in most cancers [3, 4]. As TAMs are one of the dominant innate immune cell populations in tumours (reaching up to 50% of the total cell population) [5], the two-way cell–cell interactions between cancer cells and TAMs shape the tumour immune landscape [2].

Due to the macrophage plasticity, TAMs are typically divided into pro-inflammatory (M1-like phenotype) and anti-inflammatory (M2-like phenotype) macrophages [6]. The M1-like and M2-like TAMs play an important role in tumour progression [7]. M1 macrophages produce pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-12), nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and reactive oxygen species, which are historically regarded as anti-tumour effectors [8]. M2 macrophages express a large number of scavenger receptors and anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-4 and IL-10), suppress immunosurveillance against damaged tissue and tumour cells and contribute to tumour cell proliferation and metastasis [7–9]. In general, TAMs primarily consist of M2-like macrophages and a relatively small number of M1-like macrophages.

TAMs are emerging as a key target in immunotherapy and current research is focused on the discovery of reagents specifically targeting M2-like TAMs [1], including reducing M2-like TAMs already present in the TME; inhibition of macrophage recruitment; and reprogramming/modulation of the M1/M2-like TAM spectrum [10]. The drawback of TAMs clearance and inhibition of macrophage recruitment is the potential decline in their pivotal immune-stimulatory role as primary phagocytes and specialized antigen-presenting cells within the TME [4]. As for the reprogramming/modulation of TAMs, this strategy focuses on redirecting the immune behaviour of existing M2-like TAMs towards phagocytic behaviour and hindering tumour growth by triggering immune responses against the tumour [11]. For instance, administration of Cluster of Differentiation (CD)47 antibodies restored macrophage-mediated phagocytosis within the TME, conferring advantages for cancer patients [12]. In another study, treatment with the Listeria-based HCC vaccine (Lmdd-MPFG) was shown to activate the NF-κB and autophagy pathways in TAMs, switching TAMs from the M2-like state into the M1-like state, which skewed the TME cytokine profile towards a more anti-tumour profile [13, 14]. Thus, a reprogramming/modulation of M2-like TAMs seems to be a preferable immunotherapy strategy.

Fungal immunomodulatory proteins (FIPs), first discovered in medicinal mushrooms, have been studied for their pharmaceutical utilization in recent decades. FIPs show noticeable immunomodulatory activity, especially concerning macrophage activation/modulation by inducing the secretion of cytokines and chemokines, the expression of cell adhesion molecules and enhanced proliferation [15]. Oral administration of FIP-fve (derived from Flammulina velutipe) increased the tumoricidal capacity of peritoneal macrophages in hepatoma-bearing mice and up-regulated cell adhesion molecules (i.e. CD80) on peripheral blood mononuclear cells to inhibit tumour growth and angiogenesis [16]. Li et al. [17] suggested that rLZ-8 (cloned from Ganoderma lucidum) repolarized RAW264.7 macrophages by regulating the transcriptional levels of pro- and anti-inflammatory genes, such as TNF-α, IL-10, iNOS, CCL2 and CXCL10. rFIP-SJ75, a chimera composed of LZ-8, FIP-fve and FIP-vvo (Volvariella volvacea), activated RAW264.7 macrophages by promoting M1 polarization and initiating pro-inflammatory responses [18]. Finally, rFIP-nha, cloned from Nectria haematococca [19], possesses considerable direct anti-tumour activity [20–23]. Although these features make FIPs promising anti-tumour agents, the precise mechanism by which FIPs modulate macrophage activation and polarization, and especially whether they are capable of specifically targeting TAMs, remains unclear.

Therefore, in the current study, we investigated rFIP-nha’s bioactivity on THP-1 derived M1 and M2 polarized macrophages, using M2 macrophages as TAM-representatives, and cultured the rFIP-nha treated macrophages with A549 lung cancer cells in an intricate co-culture design to explore their anti-tumour effects.

Materials and methods

Protein preparation

Mutagenesis construction of rFIP-nha (GeneBank: XM3043608) towards N-glycosylation impairment (mutants N5A, N39A, N5 + 39A), the induction of recombinant protein expression by Pichia pastoris, and protein purification are described previously [24]. Samples were snap frozen by liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further use. Protein concentrations (in 10 mM PBS; pH 7.4) were quantified by BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher, California, USA). LPS content was tested via ENDONEXT™ Kit according to manufacturer’s protocol (Biomerieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). Details of the protein and LPS concentrations are listed in Table S1. Equivalent of the highest LPS content detected in rFIP-nha was used as a negative control (LPS-CN; 3.15 EU/mL = 6.3 ng/mL).

Macrophage differentiation and polarization

THP-1 monocytes (ATCC (#TIB-202™), Massachusetts, USA) were sub-cultured and subsequently differentiated into M1 and M2 macrophages according to previously reported protocols [25]. After macrophage polarisations, the cells (using 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA for cell detachment; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and supernatant were collected for further analyses or treated as described below.

Phagocytosis assay

Following macrophage differentiation and polarization, M1 and M2 macrophages were treated with rFIP-nha at a final concentration of 10 μM for 16 h. As shown in Fig. S1, this did not induce cell death as demonstrated by 1:100 7AAD viability staining (see 2.8 for method description). After supernatant removal, 1 mL culture medium (PMI-1640 containing 25 mM HEPES and 2 mM Glutamax (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland), supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, United States)) containing 4 μg/mL AlexaFluor-conjugated Escherichia coli (K-12 strain) BioParticles® (Molecular Probes, Life Technologies, Eugene, OR, USA) was added to each well. Incubation occurred for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by two washes using 1 mL of DPBS without calcium and magnesium (Sigma, Zwijndrecht, The Netherlands). Macrophages were harvested using 0.3 mL 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA per well, washed, and resuspended with DPBS (without Ca2+/Mg2+; Sigma-Aldrich, Zwijndrecht, The Netherlands). The cells were measured using CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA) with stopping rules of either 180 μL volume or 5000 events in the ‘cells’ gate (Fig. S2). The samples were analysed using CytExpert software (v2.3, Beckman Coulter). The total amount of fluorescent bioparticles absorbed by the cells is presented as mean fluorescent intensity (MFI), while the number of cells that have absorbed fluorescent bioparticles is presented as phagocytosis percentage.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

In the supernatants, IL-12/IL-23(p40) (shown as IL-12 in the following content) and IL-10 were measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol (BioLegend, Koblenz, Germany). All samples were measured using a Tecan Infinite 200PRO (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland).

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

RNA was extracted as reported by Tang et al. [26]. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized using iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, Veenendaal, The Netherlands) with 200 ng RNA per reaction, according to manufacturer’s protocol.

qPCR

qPCR was performed as previously described by Tang et al. [26] using the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). Primers were derived from Harvard Primerbank (http://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank/) and synthesized by Biolegio (Nijmegen, The Netherlands). qPCR was performed in technical duplicate, and samples were normalized to geometric means of reference genes PUM1 and SF3A1 expression (ΔCT) and medium-stimulated macrophages via the ΔΔCT method using the qbase + software (Biogazelle, Gent, Belgium). ΔΔCT normalization was based on either the M1 or M2 medium-stimulated sample, displaying the lowest gene expression. Translated, his means that for the M1-elevated surface markers CD68, CD80, and CD86, normalization occurred via the M2-CN sample, while for the M2 markers CD163 and CD206, normalization was performed using the M1-CN sample. The primers’ information is listed in Table S2.

Cell culture of A549 lung cancer

A549 lung cancer cells were purchased from ATCC (#CCL-185™; Massachusetts, USA) and maintained in RPMI 1640 with HEPES and GlutaMAX™ (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at 37 ℃, 5% CO2 and 95% humidified atmosphere. Subculturing took place in 75 cm2 flasks in which the cell culture concentration was maintained between 6 × 103 and 6 × 104 cells/cm2 according to ATTC culture instructions. An 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA solution was used for detachment of cells during cell passaging.

Macrophage and tumour cell co-culture

To evaluate rFIP-nha’s anti-tumour activity via macrophage activation, a macrophage and tumour cell co-culture system was constructed in which the direct and indirect effects of rFIP-nha-treated macrophages on A549 lung cancer cells was investigated via a parallel contact and contactless co-culture experiment (Fig. 1).

Macorphage-A549 co-culture experiment

To evaluate cell–cell interactive effects of rFIP-nha-treated M2 macrophages on A549 cells, a co-culture experiment was performed. THP-1 monocytes were differentiated and polarized towards M1 or M2 macrophages as described in Sect. “Macrophage differentiation and polarization” (Fig. 1aA and B). Additionally, M2 macrophages were treated with 10 µM rFIP-nha for 16 h, from now on indicated as M2-nha macrophages (Fig. 1aC and D), after which the rFIPs were removed in the next washing step. After polarization, macrophages were harvested by mild centrifugation, washed (PBS; Gibco/Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA), and stained with CellTrace™ CFSE dye (Thermo Fisher, Massachusetts, USA) to allow cell tracking by flow cytometry. A549 cells were stained with CellTrace™ Far Red dye (Thermo Fisher, Massachusetts, USA) according to manufacturer’s protocols. Next, a 12-well tissue culture plate (Greiner Bio-one, Alphen aan den Rijn, The Netherlands) was seeded with stained M1, M2 and M2-nha macrophages at 2 × 106 cells/well, after which stained A549 cells were added at a concentration of 5 × 104 cells/well. Cells were co-cultured for 48 h in the presence (Fig. 1aD) or absence (Fig. 1aA-C) of freshly added rFIP-nha (10 µM). After 48 h, all cells were harvested by 0.3 mL 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA, washed by PBS and stained with 7AAD (1:100; BD Pharmingen, San Jose, CA, USA) to determine cell viability by CytoFlex flow cytometer.

Contactless A549 experiment

To investigate the effect of secreted factors by polarized macrophages on A549 cells, a contactless experiment was performed in which only conditioned culture medium from macrophages was added to A549 cells. As before, THP-1 monocytes were differentiated and polarized towards M1 or M2 macrophages (Fig. 1bE and F) or M2-nha macrophages by adding 10 µM rFIP-nha for 16 h (Fig. 1bG and H). Subsequently, rFIP-nha was removed (Fig. 1bG and H) and fresh culture medium was added. Conditioned culture medium of these macrophages was collected and added to A549 cells that were seeded in 12-well plates at a concentration of 5 × 104 cells/well, in the presence (Fig. 1bH) or absence (Fig. 1bG) of freshly added rFIP-nha (10 µM). After 48 h, all cells were harvested by 0.3 mL 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA, washed with PBS (Gibco/Invitrogen, Massachusetts, USA) and stained with 7AAD (1:100) to analyse cell viability.

Flow cytometer data analysis

Samples were measured by CytoFLEX flow cytometer with stopping rules of either 180 μL volume or 5000 events in the ‘cells’ gate (Figs. S3 and S4). The samples from the contact co-culture experiment were compensated in CytExpert software; then samples from both the contact and contactless co-culture experiments were processed in FlowJo™ Software (v10.10.0, BD Biosciensces).

Data analyses, graphs plotting and statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All data are presented as means ± SD. A One-way ANOVA with a Dunnett’s post-hoc test to correct for multiple comparisons was used to assess the parameters for significance (p < 0.05; 0.05 < p < 0.1 is considered a trend). All groups were either compared to the non-treated medium control (M-CN; Figs. 2, 3) per macrophage type (M1 or M2 polarized), or to the non-treated medium control of M2 polarized macrophages (M2-CN; Fig. 4). Outliers were identified using the ROUT method with Q = 1%.

Results

rFIP-nha reduced macrophage phagocytotic capacity

One of the most important functions of macrophages is their phagocytotic capacity. To evaluate rFIP-nha’s effect on macrophage phagocytosis, the percentage (%) of macrophages that have phagocytosed fluorescent E. coli particles (Fig. 2a), and the amount of E. coli particles consumed per macrophage (mean fluorescent intensity, MFI; Fig. 2b) were investigated. Both M1 and M2 polarized macrophages were treated with rFIP-nha to compare their phagocytic capacity. As shown in Fig. 2, after 16 h rFIP-nha treatment, the phagocytic capacity of both M1 and M2 polarized macrophages decreased significantly. When compared to M1 macrophages, M2 macrophages showed a higher percentage of cells that phagocytosed fluorescent E. coli particles (Fig. 2a), and at the same time a higher number of phagocytosed particles per cell (Fig. 2b). After 16 h rFIP-nha treatment, the phagocytic activity of both M1 and M2 polarized macrophages decreased significantly. Interestingly, M2 macrophages exposed to rFIP-nha showed an equal phagocytosis level as untreated M1 macrophages (Fig. 2a). These results indicate that rFIP-nha enhanced M1-like behaviour and suppressed the M2 phenotype. No obvious difference in phagocytosis activity was observed for the different mutants of rFIP-nha (Fig. S5), indicating that the degree of glycosylation was not relevant for modification of macrophage phagocytosis.

rFIP-nha increased IL-12 and IL-10 cytokine secretion

Reduction of phagocytosis suggests that rFIP-nha might be able to skew M2 polarized macrophages towards an M1-like phenotype. M1 macrophages typically express high levels of IL-12 and low levels of IL-10, while M2 macrophages exhibit the opposite pattern [27]. It is worth noting that the IL-12/IL-10 ratio is crucial, as IL-10 is also secreted by M1 macrophages [28]. To evaluate rFIP-nha’s effect on macrophage polarization and function, we quantified IL-12 and IL-10 secretion levels. rFIP-nha induced overall more IL-12 and IL-10 secretion by both M1 and M2 polarized macrophages (Fig. 3). More importantly, M2 polarized macrophages treated with rFIP-nha showed a higher IL-12/IL-10 ratio compared to nontreated M2 macrophages, indicating as well that rFIP-nha skews M2 macrophages towards a M1-like phenotype.

Interestingly, the glycosylation mutant N39A induced significantly less cytokine secretion in M2 polarized macrophages for both IL-10 and IL-12, although the IL-12/IL10 ratio was unaffected (Fig. S6).

rFIP-nha modulates M1/M2-related genes at transcriptional level

To further evaluate the effect of rFIP-nha on macrophage polarization, we investigated M1-related ( CD80 and CD86) and M2-related cell surface markers (CD163 and CD206) as well as pan-macrophage marker CD68, at transcriptional level (Fig. 4). CD68 gene expression was elevated in M1 compared to M2 macrophages (Fig. 4a). In addition, M2 polarized macrophages showed a trend of up-regulation when exposed to rFIP-nha, which was more similar to the levels of the M1 polarized macrophage phenotype. Moreover, CD80 expression of M2 polarized macrophages was significantly up-regulated by rFIP-nha (Fig. 4b). Although CD86 is a typical M1 marker, we observed no significant difference in expression between M1 and M2, while rFIP-nha significantly down-regulated CD86 in M2 macrophages (Fig. 4c). Both M2 markers were significantly down-regulated at transcriptional level, but not to the level indicative for M1 (Fig. 4d-e). These results seem to indicate that rFIP-nha stimulates the development of an intermediate polarization phenotype, skewed from M2 towards M1-like macrophages, which is consistent with the observed cytokine secretion ratio (IL-12/IL-10) (Fig. 3c) and phagocytosis behaviour (Fig. 2) upon rFIP-nha exposure.

Phenotypic analysis of macrophages after rFIP-nha treatment

To analyse the phenotype of M2 polarized macrophages after rFIP-nha treatment more in depth, a PCA plot was constructed (Fig. 5). M1 and M2 macrophages are separated primarily by the first component based on the transcriptional levels of cell surface markers, secreted cytokines and phagocytosis activity. M1 is found in the down-left corner, and as expected, showing strong correlation with CD68, CD80 and the IL12/IL-10 ratio. M2 is plotted on the right side, and as expected, showing strong correlation with phagocytosis, CD163 and CD206. M2-nha macrophages, distinguished from the M1 and M2 phenotypes by the second principal component, are mainly explained by the IL-12 and IL-10 cytokine profiles and the negative correlation with CD86. As M2-nha macrophages are clearly separated from M1 and M2, but at the same time plotted relatively close to M1 on PC1, they appear to polarize to a unique phenotype with a pro-inflammatory behaviour. This unique polarization phenotype is now referred to in the text as M2-nha.

Macrophages and tumour cell co-culture

To check for anti-tumour activity via macrophage modulation, an intricate macrophage and tumour cell co-culture system was constructed to evaluate direct cell–cell interactions and indirect interactions via secreted mediators in parallel (Fig. 1).

Cell–cell interactions were studied using a co-culture setup in which macrophages (after polarization towards M1, M2 and M2-nha) were cultured with A549 lung cancer cells for 48 h. The different macrophage phenotypes showed distinctive effects on tumour cell growth. Comparing the A549/macrophage cell ratio to the M2-A549 co-culture, exposure to M1 limited the expansion of A549 cells (trend; p = 0.089) (Fig. 6a, Fig. S7b). M2-nha macrophages showed a significant tumour growth inhibition capacity (Fig. 6a). Moreover, when rFIP-nha was added to the M2-nha/A549 co-culture medium, the tumour growth was inhibited even stronger (Fig. 6a), indicating that rFIP-nha itself has an additional effect on A549 cells next to the M2-nha cells. As cell viability of both macrophages and A549 cells were not influenced by the co-culture (Fig. S7a and S5a), the effects seen within 48 h of co-culture can either be attributed to cell doubling or macrophage scavenging. When looking into the macrophage population, the signal associated with A549 (far-red fluorescence) was observed, evidencing A549 uptake (Fig. S3). After 48 h, M2-nha macrophages, especially in the presence of rFIP-nha, phagocytosed a higher number of A549 cells compared to M2 macrophages (Fig. 6b), thereby indicating the importance of modulating M2 macrophages within the tumour micro-environment to prevent tumour growth and subsequent metastasis. In summary, we observed that M2-nha reduced the number of A549 cells, however only M2-nha with the additional presence of rFIP-nha in medium significantly enhanced A549 phagocytosis.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the immunopharmacological potential of rFIP-nha as a cancer chemo-supportive agent, by examining its anti-tumour activity via skewing of macrophage phenotype. M1 macrophages are regarded as anti-tumour effectors, while M2 macrophages are considered contributors to tumour growth. This study revealed that rFIP-nha is capable of repolarizing THP-1 derived M2 macrophages towards a tumour inhibitory phenotype, which was confirmed by the exertion of its direct and indirect anti-tumour activity in an A549 co-culture system.

The TME consists of stromal cells and immune cells, with TAMs and their precursors accounting for the majority of immune cells within the human solid tumour, even up to 50% [5]. With the increasing elucidation of the intricate interplay between TAMs and malignancies, TAMs are a prominent target of immunotherapeutic strategies [1]. In our study, we used THP-1 monocyte-derived M2 polarized macrophages as M2-like TAMs representatives. The use of THP-1 monocyte-derived M2 macrophages as a model for M2-like TAMs is supported by recent evidence showing that recruitment of circulating inflammatory monocytes is essential for TAMs accumulation, rather than tissue-embedded macrophages [7]. Our cytokine and surface markers results obtained from M1 and M2 generated phenotypes are in line with those found in literature, and phagocytic activity was representative for macrophage phenotype, with M2 exhibiting a higher phagocytic activity compared to M1 macrophages [23]. These results confirm that the polarization was successful.

Our data demonstrated that rFIP-nha switched M2 macrophages to a unique phenotype (M2-nha) with M1-like anti-tumour features, indicated by increased IL12/IL10 ratio, decreased phagocytic activity, and altered surface marker expression (CD68, CD80, CD163 and CD206). This emphasizes the potential of rFIP-nha as a TME-targeting chemo-supportive therapeutic, which could modulate the TAM’s tumour-supportive response towards an anti-tumour response.

In vitro co-culture systems involving macrophages and tumour cells have become widely utilized models in research. However, some models are only based on direct or indirect co-culture systems, which just provide an incomplete picture of the working mechanisms [29–31]. In our study, we evaluated both direct cell–cell interactions and indirect anti-tumour activity via secreted mediators in a parallel co-culture design, allowing for a clearer interpretation of observed immunomodulating activities of rFIP-nha. The proliferation of A549 co-cultured with M2 macrophages was higher than with M1, indicating that the polarized M2 macrophages in this study sufficiently simulated the behaviour of TAMs.

M2-nha polarized macrophages significantly decreased the number of A549 cells in a co-culture system, possibly by enhanced macrophage scavenging. This was supported by a higher number of residual far-red fluorescence detected in M2-nha macrophages, indicating phagocytosed A549 cells that were not yet completely degraded. These observations were augmented when additional rFIP-nha protein was present in the co-culture medium (M2-nha + FIP-nha). However, secreted factors from conditioned medium did not reduce A549 cell viability, unless rFIP-nha was present. This indicates a direct anti-tumour effect of the rFIP-nha on the A549 cells rather than a tumour-suppressive effect derived from the factors in the macrophage medium. A possible mechanism explaining the observed effects on A549 cell multiplication may be metabolically related. In a previous study [24], a decrease in metabolic activity was observed in A549 cells exposed to rFIPs for 24 h. In our study, 48 h of exposure was applied, and a prolonged period of metabolic inactivity is inextricably linked to cell death, as discussed by Yang et al. [32]. In a follow-up study, it may be worthwhile to investigate the apoptosis-associated caspase signalling pathways to better understand how cell death is initiated. If rFIP-nha is implemented as a chemo-supportive therapeutic adjuvant, reduced metabolic activity within cancerous cells could be an added benefit, as it could reduce the exploitation of metabolic byproducts by tumour cells to suppress tumour-infiltrating immune cells. For example, the glycolytic by-product lactate has been shown to repolarize M1-like tumour-infiltrating immune cells to an anti-inflammatory (M2-like) phenotype [27]. Thus, checking the decrease in specific metabolic by-products and their effects on TAM phenotype and behaviour would be an interesting direction for further investigation.

Additionally, apoptotic and pyroptotic factors released by M2-nha macrophages could play a role. Pyroptosis is generally mediated by inflammasome activation [28]. In a previous study, we showed that rFIP-nha was able to activate the inflammasome sensor AIM2 in THP-1 derived M0 macrophages, resulting in IL-1β release without cell death [33]. In future research, it would be interesting to look more explicitly at the potency of rFIP-nha to attenuate tumour metastasis, as an extension of the tumour cell growth described in this study. Moving towards a 3D spheroid tumour model would then be a requirement [34, 35].

In summary, rFIP-nha’s immunomodulatory effect on lung cancer cells was evident from the observed polarization-modulating effects on M2 macrophages, and the direct cytotoxic effects on tumour cell growth. Such a dual effect is not always obvious for anti-cancer molecules such as etoposide (a cytostatic compound), which induces cancer cell apoptosis without affecting M1 or M2 polarization [29], indicating that research into FIPs as therapeutic support is well-founded.

Conclusion

In this study, we expressed rFIP-nha and its glycosylation mutants (N5A, N39A, N5 + 39A) to investigate the immunopharmacological potential of rFIP-nha as a cancer chemo-supportive agent. We found that exposure of macrophages to rFIP-nha decreased their phagocytotic activity, enhanced IL-12 and IL-10 secretion, and modulated specific cell surface markers for M2 (CD163 and CD206) towards M1 (CD68 and CD80), independent of rFIP-nha glycosylation state. rFIP-nha treated M2 macrophages polarized towards a tumour inhibitory phenotype that, when subsequently co-cultured with A549 cells, resulted in greater tumour inhibition and cell death compared to A549 cells co-cultured with untreated M2 or M1 macrophages, an effect that was exacerbated in the presence of rFIP-nha. Our findings revealed that rFIP-nha suppressed tumour growth in two distinct ways: by targeting the tumour cells directly and by directing macrophage polarization towards a tumour inhibitory phenotype, which could be an important stepping stone towards future rFIP-nha applications as a medical food ingredient to optimize cancer therapeutic outcomes.
