An Ecological and Processual Understanding of Family Resilience in Dementia: A Qualitative Meta‐Synthesis
Authors: Yoosun Yang, Jun‐Ah Song
Published: 2026

Introduction

Dementia extends beyond individual illness to fundamentally transform entire families over prolonged periods (World Health Organization 2023). Family members, predominantly spouses and adult children, provide most dementia care, accounting for approximately 50% of global dementia‐related costs (USD 1.3 trillion in 2019) through unpaid informal caregiving (World Health Organization 2021). Family caregivers experience multidimensional burdens—physical, emotional, social and economic—in responding to cognitive decline and behavioural symptoms (Aljezawi et al. 2024), with subjectively perceived burden strongly associated with caregiver depression (Del‐Pino‐Casado et al. 2019; Wrede et al. 2025). As dementia is a progressive chronic condition (Oh 2024), families endure these burdens over extended periods under uncertain caregiving timelines. Thus, dementia caregiving represents an ongoing life challenge that reshapes families' daily lives, relationships and futures (Tu et al. 2022).

Recognizing this sustained challenge, research on dementia families is expanding beyond the focus on negative outcomes—burden, depression, burnout (Aljezawi et al. 2024; Del‐Pino‐Casado et al. 2019; Wrede et al. 2025)—toward understanding how families adapt, maintain relationships and sustain functioning despite difficulties (Bannon et al. 2021; Conway et al. 2020). Family resilience has emerged as a key theoretical framework for understanding how families respond to adversity, adapt and reorganize over time (Conway et al. 2020; Yang and Song 2025). Central to this framework, Walsh (2016) stated that ‘a basic systemic premise is that serious crises and persistent life challenges impact the whole family, and in turn, key transactional processes mediate adaptation (or maladaptation) for all members, their relationships, and the family unit’ (p. 617).

The Review

According to Henry et al. (2015), the concept of family resilience has evolved through three distinct waves. Wave 1 conceptualized resilience as family characteristics—cohesion, adaptability, coherence, hardiness—that enable functioning under stress (McCubbin and McCubbin 1988; Olson et al. 1983). From the mid‐1990s, Wave 2 reconceptualized resilience as a dynamic process. Hawley and DeHaan (1996) defined it as ‘the pathway families take as they adapt to and thrive in the face of stress’ (p. 293), whereas Patterson (2002a) emphasized how families reorganize demands, capabilities and meanings in response to adversity.

This shift from characteristics to process is particularly crucial for understanding family resilience in dementia. First, dementia's progressive nature makes long‐term adaptation unpredictable from initial family characteristics alone (Oh 2024). Initially cohesive families may experience burnout as symptoms worsen (La Fontaine et al. 2024), while families with limited resources may develop new strategies over time. This suggests family resilience in dementia is continuously reconstructed within changing circumstances rather than being a fixed trait. Second, dementia families experience continuously changing care demands, reorganize capabilities and repeatedly reconstruct meanings around illness and caregiving (Bannon et al. 2022; Bielsten et al. 2018). This rebalancing reveals dynamic adaptation trajectories difficult to capture through trait‐centered approaches. Third, roles redistribute and cooperation patterns form through interactions across person with dementia‐caregiver dyads and the whole family system (Miller et al. 2025). This relational reorganization unfolds over time with dynamic changes uncapturable at single timepoints. Therefore, family resilience in dementia requires process‐oriented exploration asking ‘How does family resilience emerge and unfold in dementia?’ rather than ‘Is the family resilient?’.

Building on this process‐oriented perspective, qualitative studies examining family resilience in dementia have accumulated, illuminating various processual aspects including role/relationship changes across disease trajectories (Bruinsma et al. 2022), reconstruction oscillating between acknowledging loss and recognizing what remains (Merrick et al. 2013), dynamic balance maintenance (Kuo et al. 2026) and role acceptance through iterative cycles (Nguyen and Levkoff 2020). However, while individual studies excel at depth with small samples, they cannot provide an integrative understanding of common core elements and patterns across diverse contexts.

This limitation points to the value of qualitative meta‐synthesis as an appropriate methodological approach for achieving integrative understanding of family resilience in dementia. Rather than merely listing findings, it generates abstract theoretical understanding by interpretively integrating empirical findings across contexts (Finfgeld‐Connett 2018). Thematic synthesis (Thomas and Harden 2008) inductively derives common patterns and processes from accumulated findings, revealing structural characteristics and developmental dynamics that are difficult to capture in single studies.

To date, qualitative syntheses addressing family resilience in related contexts have emerged, including a meta‐synthesis of family resilience in families of patients requiring long‐term care (Kuang et al. 2023) and a synthesis on the resilient process of families after childhood chronic illness diagnosis (Huang et al. 2022). However, to the best of our knowledge, no qualitative meta‐synthesis has specifically examined family resilience in the context of dementia. The present study addresses this gap by integrating qualitative findings to explore how family resilience emerges and unfolds in dementia caregiving, thereby extending process‐oriented understanding of family resilience in this context.

Aim

This study aims to systematically review qualitative studies addressing family adaptation and coping processes in the context of dementia and integrate them by applying the thematic synthesis method, thereby exploring how family resilience emerges and unfolds within what processes. Through this approach, the present study seeks to develop an in‐depth understanding of family resilience in dementia from a process‐oriented perspective and, furthermore, to provide evidence for the development of family‐centered dementia services and policies.

Methods

Design

This qualitative meta‐synthesis explored how family resilience emerges and unfolds in the context of dementia using thematic synthesis (Thomas and Harden 2008), which inductively derives patterns and interpretations from primary study findings without imposing predetermined theoretical frameworks. The protocol was registered with PROSPERO CRD42023485589.

Search Methods

Based on the perspective that family resilience emerges and unfolds through families' adaptation to dementia, this study explored diverse family adaptation processes. Therefore, our search strategy was intentionally comprehensive. Four databases (PubMed, CINAHL, Web of Science, PsycINFO) were searched from inception through August 26, 2025, using MeSH terms and controlled vocabularies. Two search strings were developed: (1) family‐specific resilience terms (‘family resilience’, ‘family adaptation’, ‘family coping’); (2) combinations of resilience terms (resilience, adaptation, coping) AND family‐related terms (family, spouse, caregiver) to capture studies examining resilience without explicitly using family resilience terminology. Complete search strings are provided in Table S1.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria were established following the PICo framework (Lockwood et al. 2015) with additional methodological criteria as follows: (1) Population comprised family members caring for persons diagnosed with dementia, with family defined inclusively to include not only traditional kinship relations (spouses, adult children, relatives) but also informal caregivers who serve as functional family members. Grounded in Walsh's (2016) systemic premise that individual and dyadic adaptation processes operate as integrated components within the family system, data capturing family resilience from diverse relational vantage points including individual caregivers, spousal dyads, and whole‐family units were treated as complementary perspectives on the same underlying process; (2) Phenomenon of interest focused on family resilience, adaptation, coping, strengths, functioning or relational reorganization, with emphasis on family‐level processes rather than individual experiences. Consistent with qualitative meta‐synthesis principles that the unit of analysis is study findings (Finfgeld‐Connett 2018), studies involving individual caregivers or dyads were eligible provided their findings richly captured relational dynamics, role negotiation or collective meaning‐making processes at the family level; (3) Context was limited to home and community‐based care settings; and (4) Design employed qualitative methods such as phenomenology, grounded theory, narrative inquiry or case study, or mixed‐methods studies with clearly separated qualitative findings.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) quantitative‐only or mixed‐methods studies without distinct qualitative analysis; (2) studies of professional caregivers only or without analytic distinction from other participant groups; (3) studies where dementia was incidental or focused on pre‐dementia stages; (4) studies examining only individual caregiver burden or stress without family‐level dynamics; (5) non‐primary research such as reviews, protocols or abstracts; (6) non‐English publications or inaccessible full texts.

Additionally, studies conducted in ‘institutional care settings’ and ‘COVID‐19‐specific contexts’ were excluded. As family resilience emerges through the family system's collective adaptation (Walsh 2016), capturing these processes requires examining daily interactions among all family members, including the person with dementia. In institutional care settings, family interactions are limited to periodic visits, making it difficult to capture such dynamics. Therefore, this study was limited to home‐based care contexts. Additionally, COVID‐19 pandemic contexts were excluded as they reflect simultaneous responses to dual crises—dementia and social disruption—rather than dementia‐specific adaptation, potentially generating qualitatively distinct resilience mechanisms from typical dementia caregiving.

Two researchers independently screened titles/abstracts and reviewed full texts according to inclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Studies using identical data were included separately when providing independent insights. This study employed a systematic prioritization approach consistent with meta‐synthesis principles emphasizing conceptual depth over numerical comprehensiveness (Bergdahl 2019; Sandelowski and Barroso 2006). Two reviewers independently assessed each eligible study across three dimensions: (1) relevance to family resilience processes in dementia, (2) richness and depth of qualitative data, and (3) theoretical contribution to family‐level dynamics. In applying dimension (3), reviewers specifically examined whether findings captured relational dynamics, structural reorganization or collective adaptation within the family system—regardless of whether participants were individual caregivers, dyads or multi‐member family groups.

For each dimension, studies were rated as high, moderate or low. Studies were then analysed sequentially beginning with those rated high across all three dimensions, continuing iteratively until theoretical sufficiency—when additional studies yielded no new conceptual insights (Finfgeld‐Connett and Johnson 2013). Following synthesis completion, remaining eligible studies were reviewed to verify that no substantially different perspectives had been overlooked.

Search Outcome

A total of 4523 articles were identified through four databases. After removing 2086 duplicate articles, 2437 articles remained for initial screening. Two researchers independently reviewed titles and abstracts, resulting in 961 articles deemed relevant to the research topic. Full texts were carefully reviewed according to inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 244 articles were confirmed to meet the criteria. Conceptual saturation was reached after analysing 18 studies, which were ultimately included in the synthesis. The entire study selection process and reasons for exclusion at each stage are presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

Quality Appraisal

Methodological quality was assessed using the JBI Qualitative Assessment Tool (Lockwood et al. 2015). Two researchers independently assessed studies, resolving disagreements through discussion. Quality results informed interpretation rather than serving as exclusion criteria.

Data Abstraction

Study characteristics (author, year, country, sample, dementia type/severity, methods) and qualitative data were systematically extracted by two researchers using standardized forms. Following Thomas and Harden (2008), qualitative data were extracted verbatim from all ‘results’ or ‘findings’ sections, including participant quotations, researcher interpretations, and thematic analysis results related to family resilience experiences, adaptation processes, coping strategies and family functioning.

Synthesis

An inductive approach was employed, following Thomas and Harden's (2008) three‐stage thematic synthesis. The synthesis was conducted manually without the use of qualitative data analysis software: (1) line‐by‐line coding, (2) descriptive theme development, and (3) analytical theme generation. In the line‐by‐line coding stage, findings from primary studies were coded line‐by‐line to capture their meaning and content. In the descriptive theme development stage, codes were organized into a hierarchical tree structure by identifying similarities and differences between them, resulting in descriptive themes that remained close to the content of primary studies. In the analytical theme generation stage, analytical themes were developed by interpreting descriptive themes in relation to the review question, going beyond the primary studies to generate new interpretive constructs. The process was iterative and cyclical, with continuous verification of connections among coded text, descriptive themes and analytical themes.

Two researchers with extensive research experience in dementia nursing, family caregiving and qualitative methods conducted the synthesis. To ensure reflexivity, the researchers maintained analytic memos documenting interpretive decisions and regularly discussed how their prior experiences with family caregiving research might influence emerging interpretations. To enhance trustworthiness, both researchers independently coded initial studies, with disagreements resolved through discussion until consensus was reached, and iteratively returned to primary study data to verify that interpretations remained grounded in original contexts and participant voices.

Results

Study Characteristics

A total of 18 qualitative studies were ultimately included in this meta‐synthesis (Table 1). Studies represented diverse geographic and cultural contexts across North America (n = 5), Europe (n = 7), Asia (n = 5) and Africa (n = 1). Study designs comprised person with dementia‐family caregiver dyad studies (n = 6), studies including families with persons with dementia (n = 3), and studies focusing solely on family caregivers (n = 9), with sample sizes ranging from 4 to 72 participants. Participants included persons with dementia, spousal or partner caregivers and adult child caregivers, and extended family members such as daughters‐in‐law, sons‐in‐law and friends. Although most studies focused on Alzheimer's disease, various dementia subtypes were represented, including frontotemporal dementia, Lewy body dementia and vascular dementia, with five studies addressing young‐onset or rare dementias. Severity predominantly ranged from mild to moderate stages, though two studies included persons with severe dementia. Data collection methods included individual interviews (n = 9), dyadic interviews (n = 6), family interviews (n = 1), diary recordings (n = 1) and peer support group sessions (n = 1). Analytical approaches encompassed grounded theory (n = 6), thematic analysis (n = 4), content analysis (n = 5), interpretative phenomenological analysis (n = 2) and phenomenology (n = 1). Study aims varied, including exploration of couple and family relational dynamics, understanding adaptation and changes in caregiving processes, discovering coping strategies and positive experiences, and identifying family resilience factors; however, all studies contributed to revealing processual aspects of family resilience by exploring family experiences in the context of dementia. Quality Assessment

All included studies were assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. All studies demonstrated appropriate methodological quality for inclusion. Most adequately reported congruence among objectives, methodology, and data collection/analysis and reflected participants' voices, though some lacked clear reporting on researcher reflexivity. Nevertheless, all presented logical, evidence‐based conclusions and met ethical standards. Detailed quality assessments are presented in Table S2.

Main Findings of Meta‐Synthesis

Based on 18 included studies, this study derived 68 initial codes through line‐by‐line coding (Table S3) and organized them into 28 descriptive themes (Table S4). Thematic synthesis inductively revealed that family resilience in dementia manifests three characteristics: ecological multidimensionality, iterative and cyclical progression of processual stages and integrative manifestation of multiple analytical themes.

Within this synthesis, family resilience in dementia is understood as unfolding along two intersecting axes (Figure 2). The ecological axis comprises three levels (intrapersonal, interpersonal, societal), while the processual axis encompasses five iterative‐cyclical stages (emergence of adaptive resources and caregiving motivation, acceptance and reconstruction of reality, establishment and implementation of adaptive strategies, restructuring for family system maintenance, and transcendence and meaning‐making in the journey).

Descriptive themes of family resilience in dementia across ecological and processual axes. This figure systematically maps descriptive themes of family resilience in dementia caregiving families along two axes: The processual axis (x‐axis) and the ecological axis (y‐axis). It illustrates how individual descriptive themes are integrated and synthesized into higher‐order analytical themes. The processual stages on the x‐axis are depicted using a spring‐like spiral configuration to represent their iterative and cyclical nature, rather than a linear progression, emphasizing that families may revisit earlier stages as contexts change.

Six analytical themes were derived from this framework: (1) Emergence of Multilevel Adaptive Resources and Caregiving Motivation, (2) Individual Active Adaptation and Coping, (3) Redefining and Deepening the Dyadic Relationship through Dementia‐Personhood Separation, (4) Family‐Wide Collaboration and Structural Reconstruction to Overcome Crises, (5) Construction and Integration of Social Support Networks, (6) Transcendent Expansion of Multilevel Meaning and Value. These themes were manifested integratively rather than independently. Based on these themes and characteristics, an interpretive framework was constructed visualizing how family resilience unfolds following dementia diagnosis (Figure 3).

An interpretive framework of family resilience in dementia. This model visualizes how family resilience in dementia unfolds iteratively and cyclically across intrapersonal, interpersonal and societal levels. The three concentric semicircles represent ecological levels, while the fan‐shaped structure depicts five processual stages unfolding clockwise from the time of dementia diagnosis. Six analytical themes are positioned at the intersections of ecological levels and processual stages. The counterclockwise arrows and the return arrow at the bottom signify the iterative and cyclical characteristics of resilience. Bidirectional arrows between themes indicate that they manifest integratively rather than independently. The gradient connecting Themes 3 and 4 at the interpersonal level illustrates how the locus of resilience shifts between the dyadic relationship and family system levels depending on stage and context.

Theme 1: Emergence of Multilevel Adaptive Resources and Caregiving Motivation

This analytical theme captured the pattern of how adaptive resources and caregiving motivation emerged following dementia diagnosis during the family resilience manifestation process. This theme manifested across intrapersonal, interpersonal and societal levels.

At the intrapersonal level, caregivers' prior experiences and capabilities (Conway et al. 2020; Miller et al. 2025; Nguyen and Levkoff 2020), as well as the remaining sense of efficacy and self‐esteem of persons with dementia, emerged as resources (Bannon et al. 2022; Merrick et al. 2013; Yang and Song 2025). Caregivers' personal motivations included a firm commitment to provide care until the end, existential urgency that cherished remaining time together, and determination to do one's best (Cheng et al. 2016; Nguyen and Levkoff 2020; O'Donnell 2000).

I just kind of say ‘we're going to get you through it.’ I mean it happens. I have a bad day, but we kind of just move on from it. (Bannon et al. 2022)

Additionally, spiritual and religious belief systems manifested as important resources for enduring hardship and finding meaning.

If I feel things are too much, I just open the Bible and read a passage and somehow you just get a little bit of strength. (Deist and Greeff 2015)

At the interpersonal level, positive pre‐dementia relationships—love, devotion, years shared together and shared experiences—functioned as psychological and emotional resources for sustaining caregiving (Deist and Greeff 2015; Rapley et al. 2023; Wong et al. 2015). The logic of reciprocity lay at the core of relational caregiving motivation.

She had a rough time with me, I had a serious accident, so it's my turn to look after her. (Merrick et al. 2013)

Continued intimacy with the person with dementia and family identity also became relational resources (Cheng et al. 2016; Swall et al. 2020; Wong et al. 2015).

The most important thing is the love between us. I worship Betty and I know she worships me. (Merrick et al. 2013)

At the societal level, moral obligation based on social values and norms—marriage vows, spousal duties, filial piety and cultural expectations—manifested (Bruinsma et al. 2022; Conway et al. 2020; Rapley et al. 2023). Normative motivations and avoidance of institutional placement stigma were also included (Kuo et al. 2026; Ploeg et al. 2019).

I married Mark, in sickness and in health and now he's really sick, but it's for life. (Merrick et al. 2013)

Simultaneously, social support networks—friends, neighbours, religious communities and professionals—began to emerge as important resources (Bannon et al. 2022; Bruinsma et al. 2022; Nguyen and Levkoff 2020).

I take my mother to the day care. It gives me a break so I can go shopping… It helps. (Deist and Greeff 2015)

Theme 2: Individual Active Adaptation and Coping

This analytical theme captured active adaptation and coping patterns at the intrapersonal level during the family resilience process in dementia care. This theme unfolded through the active responses of both family caregivers and persons with dementia.

Family caregivers accepted and positively reinterpreted the changed reality of dementia diagnosis (Bannon et al. 2021; Deist and Greeff 2015; Yang and Song 2025). Although this process was accompanied by grief over losses (Bannon et al. 2022; Miller et al. 2025), acceptance became not resignation but a starting point for confronting reality and exploring possibilities for response. They employed active cognitive transformation strategies such as focusing on the present moment, strategically using humour and maintaining an optimistic mindset (Conway et al. 2020; Rapley et al. 2023), while some attributed transcendent meaning through spiritual and existential frameworks (Deist and Greeff 2015; Nguyen and Levkoff 2020).

I think that God believes I am capable, so He chose me, thinking I can handle this task (caregiving). I keep a positive mindset and constantly tell myself I can do this. (Kuo et al. 2026)

Family caregivers developed creative strategies tailored to the individual characteristics of persons with dementia (Bruinsma et al. 2022; Conway et al. 2020). They practiced supporting reminiscence and memory, maintaining daily routines, learning effective communication methods and managing safety (Bielsten et al. 2018; Swall et al. 2020; Yang and Song 2025), and established strategic plans for information seeking and resource mobilization (Deist and Greeff 2015; Rapley et al. 2023; Van Hout et al. 2024). They enhanced their caregiving competence through trial and error (Nguyen and Levkoff 2020).

Before, I couldn't control my emotions. But looking back, I started to realize ways to deal with this illness, helping me release my tension. (Cheng et al. 2016)

I've been experimenting with stuff, what works best. (Van Hout et al. 2024)

Family caregivers recognized and regulated negative emotions, and continuously reflected on and adjusted their emotions and behaviours (Conway et al. 2020; Nguyen and Levkoff 2020; Rapley et al. 2023). They practiced self‐care and established their identity as caregivers, while simultaneously pursuing balance by intentionally maintaining their pre‐caregiving self and activities (Conway et al. 2020; Kuo et al. 2026; Rapley et al. 2023).

I try to do my normal things as well, to keep me balanced. Of course, I had to give up most of them. I still got a few, which I kind of called ‘my non‐negotiables’. (Van Hout et al. 2024)

Furthermore, persons with dementia acted not as passive recipients of diagnosis but as agents who actively responded to their circumstances. They accepted their diagnosis and strived to maintain remaining efficacy and self‐esteem (Bannon et al. 2022; Conway et al. 2020; Yang and Song 2025), and sought to slow disease progression and maintain quality of life through maintaining daily routines, exercise and nutrition management, cognitive training and social activity participation (Bielsten et al. 2018; Conway et al. 2020).

I feel it is important to try to behave as usual and not be thinking, ‘I can't do this, and I can't do that…not just sit at home and withdraw into oneself’. (Bielsten et al. 2018)

Theme 3: Redefining and Deepening the Dyadic Relationship Through Dementia‐Personhood Separation

This theme captured how relationships are redefined and deepened through separating dementia symptoms from personhood during the family resilience process. This theme manifested at the interpersonal level, particularly focusing on the dyadic relationship between the person with dementia and the family caregiver.

Family caregivers reduced frustration and anger by attributing problematic behaviours to the disease rather than to personality or intentions (Cheng et al. 2016; Deist and Greeff 2015; Yang and Song 2025). Some caregivers experienced relief upon diagnosis as previously incomprehensible behaviours became explained by the disease, resolving relational conflicts (Bruinsma et al. 2022; Kuo et al. 2026).

After I understood that her behaviors were due to the disease…. there were fewer misunderstandings and less miserable feelings. (Cheng et al. 2016)

Based on this, caregivers shifted their perspective to view the person not as ‘a dementia patient’ but as ‘my spouse (or parent) who has dementia’ (Bielsten et al. 2018; Merrick et al. 2013; Swall et al. 2020). This meant re‐recognizing them as a relational being with shared history, beyond simply acknowledging dignity. Caregivers adopted respectful attitudes, focused on remaining rather than lost abilities, and acknowledged identity continuity between past and present (Conway et al. 2020; Miller et al. 2025; Yang and Song 2025).

Although recognizing dementia‐caused limitations and adjusting expectations, caregivers respected autonomy by involving persons with dementia in everyday decision‐making and supporting activities within their capabilities (Bielsten et al. 2018; Nguyen and Levkoff 2020; Van Hout et al. 2024). Caregivers continuously expressed affection and respect (Kuo et al. 2026; Nguyen and Levkoff 2020; O'Donnell 2000).

Persons with dementia also emerged as active contributors to the dyadic relationship. They recognized caregiving efforts, expressed gratitude and sometimes showed consideration for caregivers (Bielsten et al. 2018; Merrick et al. 2013; Swall et al. 2020). Caregivers recognized and valued this affection and consideration (Van Hout et al. 2024; Wong et al. 2015).

My mom was nervous about not getting cooking done in time for dinner…. It kind of bothered me. But when I thought about it from a different angle, I began to appreciate what she had done. She wanted to prepare food because she cared for us. (Cheng et al. 2016)

Theme 4: Family‐Wide Collaboration and Structural Reconstruction to Overcome Crises

This analytical theme captured how families maintain and strengthen their system by enhancing communication and collaboration and reorganizing family structure—roles, hierarchies, boundaries, decision‐making—in response to dementia crisis during the family resilience process. This theme manifested at the interpersonal level, particularly focusing on interactions and structural dynamics across the entire family system.

Family caregivers increased dialogue and openly shared feelings and difficulties in the face of the common task of dementia caregiving (Deist and Greeff 2015; Rapley et al. 2023; Yang and Song 2025). Conflicts inevitably arose around dementia symptom management, caregiving approaches, responsibility distribution and decision‐making, but rather than avoiding them, families coordinated and sought resolution (Bannon et al. 2022; Cheng et al. 2016; Wong et al. 2015).

In the phase following the diagnosis everything changed for the positive. We started to talk about what we were going to do with the family business. If you talk about it, you go for it together. This gave us energy. (Bruinsma et al. 2022)

Family members such as adult children, siblings and daughters/sons‐in‐law formed emotional solidarity by supporting and comforting each other, and developed collaborative problem‐solving patterns while flexibly adapting to changing circumstances (Conway et al. 2020; Rapley et al. 2023; Swall et al. 2020).

Along with strengthened communication and collaboration at the family system level, families readjusted existing role and authority distributions. Traditional hierarchies and gender roles changed flexibly according to caregiving needs, and authority over who decided what among family caregivers was redistributed (Bruinsma et al. 2022; Miller et al. 2025; O'Donnell 2000). Particularly, a structure was established in which important decisions—medical decisions, financial management, care planning, institutional placement and others—were performed jointly by the entire family rather than individual caregivers (Conway et al. 2020; Nguyen and Levkoff 2020; Ploeg et al. 2019). Important decisions were discussed and made together, and each member's roles and responsibilities were clarified (Bannon et al. 2021; Bruinsma et al. 2022; Yang and Song 2025).

Each one has a role to play. During the day, I am not there. My dad's niece actually comes in to help my wife look after him. And as far as his medication is concerned – that is where my sister comes in; she will take care of the medical side of things. I am on the financial side of everything. (Deist and Greeff 2015)

Theme 5: Construction and Integration of Social Support Networks

This analytical theme captured how families construct social support networks and integrate social support into their caregiving systems during the family resilience process. This theme manifested at the societal level.

Initially, families experienced embarrassment and discomfort due to social stigma, but gradually became more open about the dementia diagnosis. Families talked openly about the illness and became more comfortable with others' perceptions (Bielsten et al. 2018; Cheng et al. 2016). Caregivers acted as active advocates for persons with dementia, negotiating with healthcare professionals, serving as intermediaries when communicating with others and demanding appropriate care (Bruinsma et al. 2022; O'Donnell 2000; Van Hout et al. 2024).

Families actively sought and utilized diverse forms of social support. Caregivers sought emotional support from family and friends, shared experiences with fellow caregivers and communicated openly with healthcare professionals. This support included formal support (dementia centers, day care, respite services, hired caregivers) and informal support (friends, neighbours, community) (Bruinsma et al. 2022; Cheng et al. 2016; Rapley et al. 2023). Even with access to the same resources, families varied considerably in their utilization and satisfaction levels (Deist and Greeff 2015; Nguyen and Levkoff 2020; Yang and Song 2025). Beyond the mere existence of resources, how families utilized and integrated them into their caregiving system played a crucial role in family resilience. Families did not indiscriminately accept support but selectively accepted what was helpful while rejecting intrusive or burdensome assistance (Bielsten et al. 2018; Rapley et al. 2023).

You might read up a bit about it and take in a bit. It gets you down. You take what you need from that bit. Sometimes that helps you get a bit stronger and other times it doesn't. (Rapley et al. 2023)

Theme 6: Transcendent Expansion of Multilevel Meaning and Value

This analytical theme captured how personal suffering was expanded into personal growth, relationship deepening and social contribution through the dementia caregiving journey and sublimated into transcendent meaning during the family resilience process. This theme manifested across intrapersonal, interpersonal and societal levels.

At the intrapersonal level, persons with dementia and family caregivers experienced personal transformation and growth. Persons with dementia discovered opportunities for meaningful roles and contribution (Bannon et al. 2022; Merrick et al. 2013). Family caregivers developed patience and emotional intelligence, acquired new skills and enhanced their coping abilities (Cheng et al. 2016; Yang and Song 2025). Some found spiritual and existential meaning in their caregiving role and perceived hardship as an opportunity for growth (Deist and Greeff 2015; Rapley et al. 2023).

Studying metaphysics helped me complain less and adopt a calmer perspective toward fate … I believe that crises can also be opportunities. (Kuo et al. 2026)

At the interpersonal level, caregivers experienced satisfaction and reward when persons with dementia responded positively, and found joy in small achievements during daily caregiving (Bielsten et al. 2018; Swall et al. 2020; Wong et al. 2015). Paradoxically, the crisis of dementia strengthened family bonds, deepening couple relationships, increasing intimacy between siblings, and renewing parent–child relationships (Conway et al. 2020; Nguyen and Levkoff 2020; Yang and Song 2025).

If not for my wife's illness, I would not have spent so much time taking care of her personally. We have known each other a lot more. (Cheng et al. 2016)

At the societal level, caregivers found a larger purpose by using their experiences to comfort and help other caregivers. This engendered feelings of empowerment and usefulness. Some participated in research or advocacy to be helpful to others facing similar situations (Bannon et al. 2022; Rapley et al. 2023).

I have mastered these issues when the other [caregivers] had not. So I tried to comfort them… I don't know if I'm becoming a teacher myself but it feels good to be able to relieve them of their tension. At least, I helped someone! (Cheng et al. 2016)

Discussion

This meta‐synthesis analysed 18 studies to demonstrate how family resilience emerges and unfolds in the context of dementia. Rather than simply listing descriptive themes, this study applied an ecological‐processual framework to capture patterns not explicitly revealed in primary studies and expressed them as analytical themes (Thomas and Harden 2008).

Three Characteristics of Family Resilience in Dementia

This study's thematic synthesis revealed that family resilience in dementia manifests three characteristics. First, ecological multidimensionality: family resilience in the context of dementia unfolds across ecological levels. At the intrapersonal level, active adaptation and coping by both persons with dementia and caregivers are observed, while at the interpersonal level relationship redefinition and deepening occur, and at the societal level support system construction and meaning expansion emerge.

Second, iterative and cyclical progression: this process unfolded in iterative and cyclical patterns rather than linear progression. Across the included studies, families consistently demonstrated a pattern of repeatedly modifying and readjusting their adaptive strategies in response to changing circumstances, sometimes returning to earlier stages as contexts shifted or restarting the entire process in the face of new crises. This iterative and cyclical characteristic is understood to stem from the progressive nature of dementia, in which symptoms gradually worsen over time.

Third, integrative manifestation of analytical themes: the analytical themes derived from this study appeared to coexist frequently rather than manifest independently. Individual data excerpts often addressed multiple themes simultaneously, demonstrating that the analytical themes actually manifest in an integrated manner.

These characteristics suggest that family resilience is variably constructed depending on context and timing rather than following fixed developmental stages. Therefore, family resilience in dementia should be understood not as an outcome achieved once, but as a dynamic process continuously maintained and reconstructed within changing environments. By emphasizing trajectories and cascading changes from a multilevel, multi‐systemic perspective, this study aligns with Wave 3 directions in family resilience research (Henry et al. 2015), which advocates for examining resilience as a dynamic process operating across multiple family system levels and domains over time, moving beyond static descriptions of protective factors toward understanding how resilience unfolds through complex interactions within families and ecosystems.

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first qualitative meta‐synthesis specifically examining family resilience in the context of dementia. The interpretive framework derived in this study is consistent with Walsh's (2016) family systems theory and Bronfenbrenner's (1979) ecological systems theory, and the analytical themes converge with well‐established constructs in stress–coping and family adaptation literature, lending support to the inductively derived findings. Rather than proposing new concepts, this study contributes by integrating, through qualitative meta‐synthesis, the processes of family resilience that individual studies have only partially captured from varying levels and perspectives in the context of dementia caregiving, thereby illuminating how these processes operate together within a coherent interpretive lens. This framework should therefore be understood not as presupposing a linear or universal developmental trajectory, but as an interpretive framework designed to organize and make sense of patterns repeatedly observed across diverse contexts.

Six Analytic Themes of Family Resilience of Dementia

This study revealed six analytical themes capturing how family resilience emerges and unfolds in the context of dementia. Rather than listing factors or determinants, these themes depict distinct aspects of family resilience manifestation—from the emergence of resources and motivations through individual and relational adaptations to family‐wide reorganization and meaning‐making processes.

Theme 1: Emergence of Multilevel Adaptive Resources and Caregiving Motivation

The first analytical theme captures that family resilience manifestation in dementia unfolds beyond simple resource presence or absence, through resources and motivations working together across multiple levels. While existing theories focus on ‘how’ families utilize resources (McCubbin and McCubbin 2013; Patterson 2002b; Walsh 2016), this study further revealed ‘why’ caregivers persist. Multilevel resources—individual capabilities, relational history, spiritual beliefs, social support networks—were found together with caregiving motivations—personal commitment (‘care until the end’), relational reciprocity (‘you cared for me, now I care for you’) and normative obligation (‘it's expected as spouses/children’)—at intrapersonal, interpersonal and sociocultural levels (Cheng et al. 2016; Deist and Greeff 2015; Merrick et al. 2013). Given dementia's chronic nature with symptom unpredictability and progressive deterioration, resources secured early cannot sustain long‐term caregiving alone (Connors et al. 2019). This study demonstrates that caregiving in dementia requires not only available resources but also motivations, both operating across multiple levels.

The specific ways in which multilevel resources and caregiving motivations emerge may vary across cultural and structural contexts. For instance, societal‐level factors such as filial piety norms or the availability of formal support systems may shape which ecological levels are more prominently engaged in particular cultural settings (Jia et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2023).

Theme 2: Individual Active Adaptation and Coping

The second analytical theme captures active adaptation and coping patterns at the individual level during family resilience manifestation. While much of dementia research has framed the agency of persons with dementia as diminished due to cognitive decline (Mentzou et al. 2023; Xanthopoulou and McCabe 2019), the included studies revealed that persons with dementia actively engaged in accepting their diagnosis, maintaining daily activities and preserving self‐esteem (Bannon et al. 2022; Bielsten et al. 2018; Merrick et al. 2013; Yang and Song 2025), demonstrating that they remain active agents who adapt within limited capacities. Caregivers also demonstrated active adaptation by positively reinterpreting diagnosis, learning caregiving skills, acquiring competence through repeated practice (Cheng et al. 2016; Rapley et al. 2023), and pursuing balance between caregiver identity and pre‐existing self through self‐care.

These individual efforts, however, gain fuller meaning when interpreted through Walsh's (2016) perspective, which views families as functional units beyond individual resources. In this study, persons with dementia's diagnosis acceptance contributes to family meaning‐making and connectedness, while caregivers' reinterpretation and practice implement a positive outlook, flexibility and collaborative problem‐solving. Individual efforts thus constitute and activate family resilience at the micro level, interacting within the family system to become integrated into the overall process of family resilience. The finding that persons with dementia actively contribute aligns with recent discussions (Mentzou et al. 2025) that agency persists within relational contexts in dementia, suggesting family resilience research should not overlook vulnerable members' agency.

While Theme 2 addresses individual‐level adaptation, this should not be conflated with resilience in family caregivers. Individual resilience, including that of family caregivers, refers to the process of effectively negotiating, managing or adapting to significant sources of stress or trauma (Windle 2011), whereas family resilience refers to a collective process in which key transactional processes mediate adaptation for all members, their relationships, and the family unit as a whole (Walsh 2016). Individual‐level adaptations captured in this theme, including the person with dementia's diagnosis acceptance and the caregiver's active coping, are micro‐level constituents that become integrated into the overall process of family resilience through interaction within the family system.

Theme 3: Redefining and Deepening the Dyadic Relationship Through Dementia‐Personhood Separation

The third analytical theme captures how relationships are redefined and deepened through separating dementia symptoms from personhood during family resilience manifestation. While personhood and person‐centered care discourse (Kitwood 1997) primarily developed within formal care systems (Lee et al. 2020; Saragih et al. 2024), family caregivers differ fundamentally: they share decades of relational history, with caregiving rooted in emotional bonds and family identity rather than professional roles (Dellafiore et al. 2025). This necessitates understanding person‐centered care's particular meaning within family systems.

This study shows family caregivers separate dementia from personhood, moving beyond recognizing ‘a person with dementia’ to accepting ‘my family member with dementia’ (Bannon et al. 2021; Cheng et al. 2016; Swall et al. 2020). Critically, this separation manifests through relational historicity—memories attributing current symptoms to disease (‘this person was not originally like this’) (Ploeg et al. 2019)—representing a family‐specific mode distinct from formal care. This suggests interventions may prove more effective by facilitating families' autonomous construction of illness‐personhood separation using existing relational resources rather than directly teaching person‐centered care techniques.

Furthermore, illness‐personhood separation manifested not merely as cognitive reconstruction but as relational identity reaffirmation and bidirectional affection circulation (Nguyen and Levkoff 2020; O'Donnell 2000). This aligns with relational selfhood (Tieu and Matthews 2024), which posits that identity is co‐constructed through interactions, demonstrating that family person‐centered care constitutes a relational process rather than unilateral provision.

Theme 4: Family‐Wide Collaboration and Structural Reconstruction to Overcome Crises

The fourth analytical theme captures how family members collaborate and reconstruct family structure in response to dementia crisis, demonstrating flexibility and connectedness. Flexibility represents families' ability to reorganize and restabilize systems through adaptive changes, while connectedness involves forming collaborative caregiving teams through mutual support, teamwork and commitment (Walsh 2016). This study's findings of role redistribution, caregiving sharing and decision‐making sharing exemplify connectedness, whereas changes in daily routines, environmental rearrangement and external resource utilization exemplify flexibility (Bruinsma et al. 2022; Kuo et al. 2026).

Furthermore, this study shows that such reconstruction of family structure is not simply functional rearrangement but is accompanied by relational renegotiation of roles and authority among family members and strengthening of collaborative solidarity (Bruinsma et al. 2022; Deist and Greeff 2015). This suggests that flexibility and connectedness are not separate processes but operate in mutually reinforcing ways, representing the process of achieving the balance that Walsh (2016) emphasized of ‘maintaining family cohesion while respecting individual needs and differences.’

Theme 5: Construction and Integration of Social Support Networks

The fifth analytical theme captures how families construct and integrate social support networks into caregiving systems during resilience unfolding. Social support construction by dementia families involves not simply ‘receiving external help’ but strategically resetting family boundaries. Included studies revealed participants coordinated external relationships by accepting useful support while blocking interference, and accepting practical help while rejecting pity (Bielsten et al. 2018; Rapley et al. 2023). This suggests family resilience manifestation is characterized by selective permeability—family members regulating boundaries according to their values (Broderick 1993; White and Klein 2008)—rather than unconditional openness. This finding demonstrates that support system construction must transcend quantitative resource expansion. Effective support requires ensuring families' agency and autonomy to choose support type, timing, and providers themselves.

Theme 6: Transcendent Expansion of Multilevel Meaning and Value

The final analytical theme captures how family resilience extends beyond functional maintenance or burden management to reconstruct caregiving meaning and expand into personal, relational and social domains. Walsh (2016) proposed ‘transcendence and spirituality’—finding greater values from adversity and achieving positive growth through learning and transformation—as a key family resilience domain. At the intrapersonal level, caregivers discovered existential meaning by redefining themselves from ‘sacrificing persons’ to ‘growing caregivers’ (Ploeg et al. 2019; Van Hout et al. 2024). At the interpersonal level, caregiving acts were reinterpreted as expressions of love and commitment, deepening family bonds (Conway et al. 2020; Wong et al. 2015). At the societal level, some families extended their experiences to peer support, research participation and community awareness‐raising (Bannon et al. 2022; Cheng et al. 2016). This suggests that transcendence and growth in family resilience unfolds not as an isolated intrapersonal level but as an integrated phenomenon across all ecological levels.

Interpretive Framework of Family Resilience in Dementia

This study developed an interpretive framework visualizing the three characteristics and six analytical themes derived through Thomas and Harden's (2008) three‐stage thematic synthesis (Figure 3). Three concentric semicircles represent intrapersonal, interpersonal and societal levels from innermost to outermost, while the fan‐shaped structure visualizes five processual stages unfolding clockwise from dementia diagnosis. The six analytical themes are positioned at intersections of ecological levels and processual stages.

Counterclockwise arrows against the fan's expansion represent iterative progression, and the return arrow at the model's bottom indicates the cyclical characteristic where families return to initial adaptation stages to build new resilience as disease progresses. Small bidirectional arrows between themes indicate that they manifest integratively rather than independently. The gradient connecting Themes 3 and 4 at the interpersonal level represents shifting resilience locus between dyad and family system levels. In the ‘acceptance and reframing’ stage, the dyadic relationship predominates; in ‘formulation and enactment,’ both levels activate; in ‘restructuring,’ family‐wide collaboration is emphasized. However, this locus shifts according to circumstances, reflecting the iterative nature.

Implication

Building on these findings, this study offers implications for both practice and policy. From a practical perspective, mental health nurses and dementia care practitioners should shift from cross‐sectional burden assessment at discrete timepoints (Chu et al. 2022; Jia et al. 2025) to longitudinal, process‐oriented continuous support exploring how family resilience emerges and unfolds. Practice should understand the entire family, including the person with dementia, as agents of resilience, and move beyond burden measurement‐centered approaches (Aljezawi et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2022) to capture how resilience actually unfolds. Interventions should employ flexible, context‐sensitive approaches that support currently weakened mechanisms and facilitate resource connections. For instance, promoting illness‐personhood separation or helping families reinterpret subtle responses from persons with dementia as meaningful relational acts may be worth exploring. Furthermore, considering the iterative and cyclical characteristics of resilience, practitioners should serve as facilitators supporting the reactivation of resilience mechanisms while understanding temporary weakening not as family failure but as natural adaptive fluctuation.

From a policy perspective, policymakers should recognize that dementia care is families' long‐term adaptation process operating across individual, relational and societal levels. Given family resilience's nonlinear, context‐dependent nature (Henry et al. 2015), one‐time assessments or fixed service packages cannot adequately reflect changing needs. Policy‐level structural foundations should enable longitudinal identification of family system adaptation patterns and resilience mechanisms (Chen et al. 2025) through family‐unit assessment systems. Reflecting this study's finding of selective permeability in social support networks, family‐centered service design should simultaneously address support accessibility and boundary setting, enabling families to accept needed support while blocking unnecessary interference.

Limitations

First, although included studies span diverse cultural contexts across North America, Europe, Asia and Africa, their regional distribution is uneven, with limited representation from low‐ and middle‐income countries. As family resilience may manifest differently depending on sociocultural contexts such as cultural expectations regarding caregiving, family structures and social support systems, the findings of this study require careful interpretation when applied to specific cultural settings. Future research should prioritize diverse cultural contexts to examine cross‐cultural variations in family resilience processes.

Second, qualitative meta‐synthesis methodology lacks consensus on study selection, particularly regarding comprehensive versus purposive sampling (Nye et al. 2016; Sandelowski and Barroso 2006). Facing 244 eligible studies, we prioritized conceptual richness to enable theoretical depth consistent with interpretivist principles (Bergdahl 2019). While we reviewed all remaining eligible studies after completing synthesis to verify no major divergent perspectives were overlooked, we acknowledge this approach as one among multiple legitimate strategies, and studies not included in our analysis may have revealed additional patterns, exceptions or contextual variations. In particular, prioritizing conceptually rich studies may have selectively reinforced patterns that are more theoretically coherent, while relatively underrepresenting more fragmented, context‐specific or atypical experiences.

Third, grounded in Walsh's (2016) family systems perspective, this study treated findings from individual caregiver and dyadic studies as capturing different vantage points of the same family resilience process, and accordingly included not only studies involving whole‐family interactions but also those focusing on individual caregivers or dyads. Future accumulation of longitudinal qualitative studies of family resilience targeting multi‐member families would allow for more direct examination of how family resilience unfolds through collective family dynamics, further strengthening the empirical basis of the interpretive framework proposed in this study.

Last, this study focused on home and community‐based care settings to capture family interactions in daily contexts and excluded institutional care transitions and the COVID‐19 pandemic situation. Future research should explore family resilience processes in dementia across these excluded contexts to examine how family resilience manifests under different care settings and crisis conditions.

Conclusions

This qualitative meta‐synthesis systematically explored how family resilience emerges and unfolds in the context of dementia by analysing 18 studies. The findings reveal that family resilience unfolds as a dynamic process characterized by multilevel ecological dimensions—intrapersonal, interpersonal and societal—and iterative and cyclical progression. The six analytical themes derived from this study manifested integratively, demonstrating that family resilience emerges through the integrated operation of these themes. Building on these findings, this study supports the understanding that family resilience is not a static outcome achieved once but rather a dynamic process continuously maintained and reconstructed in response to disease progression and environmental changes.

These insights have implications for practice and policy. Family support must shift from episodic resource provision or burden measurement toward longitudinal, process‐oriented approaches that recognize families' ongoing adaptation. Services should facilitate families' capacity to activate and reconstruct their own resilience mechanisms rather than imposing standardized interventions. This study provides both a theoretical foundation and practical direction for developing family‐centered dementia care systems that support families across the entire dementia trajectory.
