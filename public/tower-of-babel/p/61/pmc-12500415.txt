Recurrent Huge Aortic Aneurysm, Myocardial Infarction, and Aortic Insufficiency in a Patient With Longstanding Behcet's Disease: A Case Report of a Rare Triple Presentation
Authors: Mohammad Ali Nazarinia, Ahmad Ali Amirghofran, Helia Bazroodi, Samaneh Hasanzadeh, Afagh Atrian, Sadra Soveid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBehçet's disease is a systemic inflammatory disorder with an incompletely understood pathophysiology. Its clinical manifestations are highly diverse, affecting multiple organs throughout the body. In some cases, Behçet's disease presents with large arterial involvement, as aneurysms, which resulted from perivascular and endovascular inflammation, if not identified and treated promptly, can be life‐threatening. Cardiac valve and coronary artery involvement are other manifestations of Behçet's. They are very rare and are often associated with poor prognosis. The case presented in this report illustrates a combination of strange and severe manifestations of Behçet's disease over time, including extensive aortic aneurysm, coronary artery aneurysm leading to myocardial infarction, and aortic valve insufficiency. In addition to appropriate pharmacological treatment, the patient underwent two open‐heart surgeries and one percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), with successful outcomes.

Summary Systemic manifestations of Behçet's disease can occasionally result in rare yet highly dangerous complications.Therefore, particular attention should be given to these life‐threatening conditions. Among these complications are coronary artery involvement, heart valve involvement, and widespread aortic involvement, all of which can be fatal if not identified in a timely manner.As such, in a patient with Behçet's disease, any signs of cardiac symptoms, including shortness of breath, reduced ability to perform daily activities, chest pain, and so on, should be taken seriously and thoroughly investigated.Additionally, any sharp, radiating pain to the back, which may indicate an aortic aneurysm, should also be assessed.

Background

Behcet's disease (BD) is a rare chronic systemic vasculitis that is more common in countries that are placed on the Silk Road, especially in Middle Eastern countries, Mediterranean regions, and also East Asian countries [1]. The symptoms of BD vary from patient to patient, but it usually presents as recurrent oral ulcers, genital ulcers, and ocular, musculoskeletal, neurological, cardiac, pulmonary, and gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms [2]. The principal pathogenic mechanisms are as below: genetic factors (including specific human leukocyte antigens [HLA] and non‐HLA genes), disruptions in the host microbial composition, dysregulation of the innate immune function, alterations in hematopoietic cell populations and the cytokines they produce, immune complexes and autoantibodies, activation of the vascular endothelium and a state of hypercoagulability, neutrophil activation, and epigenetic modifications [3]. The prognosis of BD is clinically determined by the existence of typical symptoms or several other symptoms associated with organ damage. Consequently, a diverse array of symptoms may be present. Some patients manifest only skin and mucosal lesions, but others may manifest dangerous central nervous system (CNS), GI, and pulmonary artery aneurysms [4]. Heart may be involved with BD, which is called cardiac BD. Thrombosis inside the heart, endocarditis, myocarditis, pericarditis, endomyocardial fibrosis, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, or valvular disease can all happen in this case [5]. We report a case of a 56‐year‐old male who had a recurrent severe aortic aneurysm together with myocardial infarction and aortic insufficiency.

Case History and Examination

Herein, we present a 55‐year‐old male known case of ulcerative colitis (which has been controlled with mesalamine over the past decade) and Behçet's disease. Sixteen years ago, for the first time, he developed dyspnea on exertion and progressive chest and back pain. Upon comprehensive assessment, in addition to an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), he was diagnosed with a substantial aneurysm of the ascending aorta extending to the aortic arch (with maximum diameter of 6 cm), accompanied by aortic regurgitation. So, he underwent open surgery for aortic root, aortic arch, and aortic valve replacement (AVR) by a bi‐leaflet prosthetic heart valve at the same time. The histopathology confirmed Behçet's disease. He was diagnosed with Behçet's disease a year before he started having shortness of breath when he worked out. He met the criteria set by the “International Study Group for Behçet's Disease” [6], which included recurrent herpetiform oral ulceration, major oral aphthous lesions (five episodes within 12 months), a positive pathergy test, and erythema nodosum. He also had experienced episodes of amaurosis fugax, raising the possibility of BD ocular involvement. However, he did not take the ophthalmologic follow‐up seriously and failed to undergo an eye examination. Cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, prednisolone, and colchicine were prescribed as his rheumatologic medications over various temporal phases. Although he was not taking drugs constantly, the symptoms had been relieved. Warfarin was also prescribed for his AVR. Additionally, 15 years ago, he developed ischemic heart disease for which he underwent coronary angiography, which revealed an aneurysm in the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery with a 70% stenosis. So, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) on LAD was done, and ticagrelor, aspirin, bisoprolol, and rosuvastatin were administered to him. However, ticagrelor was switched to clopidogrel a few months later due to multiple epistaxis occurrences and a small amount of blood in sputum. He was in good condition until he experienced an episode of massive hemoptysis (2 glasses of fresh bleeding) about 6 months ago. So, he referred us to our tertiary center for better evaluation. Upon admission, his vital signs were as follows: respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min, pulse rate of 100 beats/min, blood pressure of 130/88 mm Hg, and temperature of 36.8°C. Except for hemoptysis, the rest of his physical examination was normal. At the time of admission, laboratory studies revealed ESR of 49 mm/h, C‐reactive protein (CRP) of 44 mg/deciliter, prothrombin time (PT) of 33.9 s, and international normalized ratio (INR) of 2.56.

Investigation

After multidisciplinary evaluation and ruling out other probable reasons for massive hemoptysis, such as warfarin toxicity or pulmonary thromboembolism, the impression of severe aortic aneurysm caused by Behcet's flare‐up was entertained. In other words, he was experiencing another episode of aortic aneurysm, like what occurred 16 years ago. But this episode was much more severe. Chest x‐ray showed mediastinal widening suggestive of aortic aneurysm (Figure 1) and computed tomography (CT) (Figure 2) revealed severe aortic arch pseudoaneurysm (with a maximum diameter of 45 mm) at the site of previous surgery, severe aneurysmal dilation of the total course of the descending aorta (maximum diameter was 67 mm and length of aneurysmal involvement was 130 mm), and abdominal aorta intimal flap. The aortic arch pseudoaneurysm extended to the aortopulmonary window, measuring approximately 67 × 36 mm with a neck width of about 3 mm. In addition to the findings from CT, two‐dimensional echocardiography revealed an ejection fraction of 40%, dilated left atrium, bi‐leaflet aortic valve with proper position of the graft without leakage, and dilated inferior vena cava. For the patient, cyclophosphamide 750 mg intravenously (IV) and methylprednisolone 1000 mg IV for 3 days were prescribed. One week later, he was stable enough to be transferred for surgery. At that point, laboratory study showed, ESR of 20 and CRP of 14. During operation, the branched aortic arch was resected and debranched, thoracic aorta was repaired by the Thoraflex stent‐graft, the innominate artery and left carotid artery were anastomosed on the Thoraflex, and also exploration and cannulation of the right femoral artery and vein was done.

Preoperative chest X‐ray showed mediastinal widening suggestive of aortic aneurysm.

Axial (left) and coronal (right) views of spiral computed tomography revealed significant aneurysmal dilatation with a maximum diameter of 67 mm.

Treatment

The patient was discharged 1 month later and continued taking 0.6 mg of colchicine orally two times a day, 50 mg of prednisone per day (which was subsequently tapered to 5 mg/day), and 1 g of cyclophosphamide administered intravenously each month. The patient's aneurysms have remained stable for 6 months after beginning medicinal and surgical therapy.

Discussion

The pathogenesis of BD, a systemic inflammatory disease, is uncertain. The “Revised Chapel Hill Consensus Conference” classified BD as a variable‐vessel vasculitis since it affects venous and arterial vessels of all diameters [7]. Recurrent mucocutaneous lesions and significant organ involvement, including gastrointestinal, ophthalmic, neurologic, and vascular symptoms, are its most distinctive characteristics [8]. BD often begins in the second or third decade of life and progresses in a remitting–relapsing manner, with a decline in disease activity as one becomes older. Male BD patients, especially those under 25, have a more severe course of the disease [9]. BD is diagnosed mostly on the basis of clinical symptoms following the exclusion of other possible causes. For the diagnosis of BD, there are no particular laboratory, histopathologic, or genetic findings [8]. The cardiovascular complications caused by BD have garnered an abundance of interest nowadays. One well‐known BD consequence that affects up to 40% of patients is vascular involvement, which is believed to be a negative prognostic indicator [10, 11]. Because endarteritis obliterans is caused by occlusions of the vasa vasorum of the large and medium arteries, aneurysm formation occurs [12]. Large arteries' involvement can result in aneurysms, occlusions, and thromboses, and when an aneurysm ruptures, it can be extremely dangerous [13]. Compared to venous involvement, arterial involvement occurs less frequently, and arterial aneurysms are recorded more often than arterial occlusions [14]. Vasculo‐Behcet's disease is treated with both medication and surgery. The use of immunosuppressants and corticosteroid therapy has begun to alter BD treatment [15]. A serious problem with vasculo‐Behcet's disease is aneurysm recurrence following surgery; in this case, a diagnostic angiography may reveal a false aneurysm even near the puncture site [16]. The operation should be avoided during the active period of BD in order to minimize mortality and aneurysm recurrence after the procedure [17]. So, immunosuppressant usage, both before and after surgery, to control inflammation in patients with BD could reduce the risk of surgical complications [18]. In our case, he reported poor compliance for taking medication. Thus, after 16 years, aortic aneurysm and pseudoaneurysm returned, although of increased severity this time. On the other hand, the life‐threatening cardiac involvement of BD has been described in 1% to 16% of cases [19]. Herein, aortic insufficiency and coronary artery vasculitis (LAD) were the two types of cardiac involvement that our case exhibited, occurring 16 years apart. Aortic valve involvement has a significant mortality rate (44%), making it a poor prognostic indicator based on a study by Choong won et al. [20]. Ando et al. [21] illustrated that after undergoing surgical therapy for aortic valve disease, the rates of mortality and prosthetic valve detachment were 20% and 40%, respectively. Even so, the aortic valve in our case's echocardiogram was positioned correctly up to that moment. Conversely, coronary vasculitis can manifest as coronary stenosis, occlusion, aneurysm, or rupture [22]. Features associated with early, severe, or atypical coronary artery disease may suggest an inflammatory origin, even when no specific finding at coronary angiography offers a safe diagnosis [22]. Our case has experienced coronary artery aneurysm and stenosis, and revascularization was done by PCI. For all patients with coronary aneurysms, lifelong antiplatelet medication is advised, without regard to the type of revascularization [23]. Still, immunosuppressive treatment remains a cornerstone of treatment [24]. Herein, we detailed a long‐standing BD with three rare presentations of BD: myocardial infarction followed by recurrent aortic aneurysm and valvular disease (aortic insufficiency). Finally, immunosuppressive medication and suitable surgical techniques were performed for the management of this challenging patient.
