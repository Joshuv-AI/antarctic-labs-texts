Hemolysis-related acute kidney injury after pulsed field ablation: An emerging concern in patients with hematologic disorders
Authors: Reisuke Yoshizawa
Published: 2025

The case report by Sawada et al1 illuminates a critical intersection between emerging ablation technology and hematologic vulnerability, documenting hemolysis-induced acute kidney injury (AKI) after pulsed field ablation (PFA) in a patient with Waldenström’s macroglobulinemia (WM). This confluence of factors, including an innovative ablation modality, an underlying paraproteinemia, and pharmacologic endothelial modification, creates a convergence of risk factors for renal complications that demands our attention as PFA rapidly enters mainstream clinical practice.

The double-edged sword of electroporation

PFA represents a significant advancement in cardiac ablation, using irreversible electroporation to achieve myocardial-selective tissue destruction while sparing adjacent structures. However, the same electrical fields that selectively target cardiomyocytes can inadvertently affect circulating erythrocytes. Recent systematic data reveal substantial heterogeneity in AKI incidence after PFA, ranging from 0.03% in large registries2 to 75% in nonhydrated patients receiving high application numbers.3 Notably, although Dello Russo et al4 reported a low overall complication rate of 3.0% in a large multicenter cohort including elderly patients, Popa et al5 systematically demonstrated hemolysis in 94.3% of PFA patients with associated bilirubin elevation. This substantial variability underscores the critical importance of identifying vulnerable populations.

The mechanism of PFA-induced hemolysis involves direct electrical trauma to red blood cell membranes during energy delivery. Under normal circumstances, healthy erythrocytes tolerate this stress with minimal clinical consequences. However, in patients with WM, multiple factors converge to amplify this risk. The elevated immunoglobulin M (IgM) levels (2663 mg/dL in this case1) increase plasma viscosity, prolonging erythrocyte exposure to electrical fields and creating rheological conditions that impair microcirculatory flow. In addition, WM-associated erythrocyte deformability defects render red cells more susceptible to membrane disruption under the shear stress conditions generated during PFA.

The compounding effect of Bruton’s tyrosine kinase inhibition

The concurrent use of ibrutinib introduces another layer of complexity that warrants careful consideration. Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitors, while representing a major therapeutic advance in WM, induce endothelial dysfunction and platelet abnormalities that may synergistically increase hemolysis risk. The vascular endothelium, already compromised by hyperviscosity syndrome, faces additional insult from both pharmacologic modification and electrical trauma. This triple hit of rheological disturbance, pharmacologic endothelial dysfunction, and electroporation-mediated injury creates significant vulnerability to hemolysis and subsequent renal injury.

Expanding our understanding of risk

The MANIFEST-17K study’s2 identification of chronic kidney disease and high application numbers as primary risk factors represents only the beginning of our understanding. This case1 suggests that our risk assessment framework must evolve to encompass the complex interplay among hematologic disorders, pharmacologic therapies, and procedural factors.

The markedly elevated IgM level (2663 mg/dL) in this case1 raises important questions about the role of paraproteinemias in PFA-related complications. Although systematic evidence linking specific IgM thresholds to AKI risk remains lacking,6 the rheological alterations associated with hyperviscosity syndrome clearly warrant consideration. The absence of established predictive models underscores the need for heightened vigilance when evaluating patients with known or suspected paraproteinemias.

Furthermore, the interaction between BTK inhibitors and PFA-induced hemolysis deserves prospective investigation. As these agents become increasingly prevalent in WM management, understanding their impact on procedural risk becomes essential. The endothelial dysfunction and platelet abnormalities induced by BTK inhibitors may create a particularly vulnerable phenotype when combined with the electrical stress of PFA.

The relationship between application number and hemolysis seems dose dependent, although optimal thresholds for vulnerable populations remain undefined. Recent reports of hemolysis-related complications5 suggest that patients with hematologic disorders may require modified procedural approaches, although specific protocols await validation through prospective studies.

Implications for clinical practice

This case highlights the importance of aggressive periprocedural hydration, which has emerged as a cornerstone of AKI prevention. The stark contrast in outcomes between hydrated and nonhydrated patients3 underscores this intervention’s critical importance, particularly for those with hyperviscosity syndromes. Preprocedural optimization, including consideration of plasmapheresis in selected cases, may also merit evaluation, although optimal timing and target parameters require further investigation.

The potential for real-time monitoring of hemolysis markers during procedures represents an intriguing avenue for future development. Such capabilities could enable dynamic protocol modification, allowing operators to balance procedural efficacy with safety in vulnerable populations.

Balancing innovation with caution

Although this case1 raises important safety concerns, abandoning PFA in all hematologic patients would be premature. The technology’s unique advantages of tissue selectivity, reduced collateral damage, and potentially superior long-term outcomes remain compelling. Instead, this case should catalyze the development of nuanced approaches that preserve PFA’s benefits while mitigating risks in vulnerable populations.

The comparative safety of PFA vs thermal ablation modalities in patients with hematologic disorders remains uncertain. Until specific evidence emerges, individualized risk-benefit assessment, informed by the growing understanding of patient-specific vulnerabilities, must guide procedural planning.

Looking forward

This case1 illuminates critical knowledge gaps that merit systematic investigation. Prospective registries specifically tracking PFA outcomes in patients with hematologic disorders could provide essential insights into risk stratification and optimal management strategies. The development of predictive models incorporating rheological parameters, medication effects, and baseline laboratory values represents another important research priority.

Conclusion

The case presented by Sawada et al1 represents more than an isolated complication; it exemplifies the challenges of implementing transformative technologies in heterogeneous patient populations. As PFA technology advances and its adoption accelerates, our understanding of its interaction with complex medical comorbidities must evolve correspondingly. The combination of hyperviscosity syndrome, BTK inhibitor use, and PFA seems to create a particularly high-risk phenotype. However, the relative contribution of each factor remains to be elucidated.

This case reminds us that, in the era of precision medicine, careful patient selection and individualized procedural planning remain paramount. The development of evidence-based approaches addressing the unique vulnerabilities of hematologic patients will be essential to ensuring that PFA’s remarkable promise can be safely extended to all who might benefit. Future research must focus not only on identifying at-risk populations but also on developing tailored strategies that preserve the benefits of this innovative technology while minimizing its risks.
