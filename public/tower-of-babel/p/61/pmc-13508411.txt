HSV-1 reactivation as an emergent property of neuronal stress: implications for traumatic brain injury
Authors: Alyson R Stewart, Amalia Bustillos Saucedo, Lindsey L Beauregard, Christopher D Link, Christy S Niemeyer, Rachel K Rowe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHerpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) establishes lifelong latency in neurons, with reactivation driven by multiple molecular, cellular, and systemic stressors. While several individual mechanisms of reactivation have been well characterized, there are potentially other unresolved vulnerabilities that drive HSV-1 reactivation. Traumatic brain injury (TBI) has emerged as a potential trigger of HSV-1 reactivation and represents a complex and clinically relevant perturbation that disrupts neuronal homeostasis, immune surveillance, and inflammatory signaling, processes that are also central to HSV-1 latency and reactivation. However, the mechanisms linking TBI to HSV-1 reactivation remain poorly understood.In this review, we examine whether known mechanisms of TBI-induced cellular stress overlap with pathways implicated in HSV-1 latency and reactivation. We synthesize shared mechanisms, including stress signaling, neuroinflammation, and immune dysregulation during TBI that may create conditions permissive for HSV-1 reactivation in the injured brain. This integrated perspective reframes TBI as a context in which established drivers of HSV-1 reactivation converge and may increase reactivation susceptibility.We propose that HSV-1 reactivation is an emergent property of dysregulated neural systems and that TBI may engage many of these processes. In this framework, viral reactivation is inseparable from the broader neuronal and systemic context in which it occurs. This perspective highlights the importance of integrating neural state, injury, and immune dynamics into models of HSV-1 latency and reactivation. Advancing this multidimensional view will be critical for developing therapeutic strategies that not only suppress viral reactivation but also address neuroinflammation following brain injury.Keywords: Viral infection, Traumatic brain injury, Neuronal vulnerability, Mechanisms of reactivation, Viral latency, Herpes simplex virus type 1, Immune activation, Sleep disturbances

Introduction

Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) belongs to the Alphaherpesvirinae subfamily and primarily causes the common cold sore [1]. Approximately two-thirds of the world’s population is infected with asymptomatic HSV-1, while one-third experience symptomatic HSV-1 at some point in their lifetime [1, 2]. Unlike many viruses that remain confined to peripheral tissues, HSV-1 is highly neurotropic. By establishing latency in the sensory ganglia, the virus retains the capacity to access, infect, and establish latent reservoirs within the central nervous system (CNS). Although often considered a relatively benign infection, HSV-1 is clinically significant due to its lifelong persistence and its ability to cause severe neurological complications, including herpes simplex encephalitis [3, 4]. However, despite the widespread prevalence of HSV-1 and its neuroinvasive capacity, remarkably little is known about the long-term consequences of latent HSV-1 infection within the CNS, the frequency and drivers of viral reactivation in brain parenchyma, or how these events may influence neurological health over time.

A substantial body of literature has identified and summarized a range of stimuli that can induce HSV-1 reactivation, including immunosuppression, ultraviolet (UV) radiation, psychological and physiological stress, fever, and tissue damage [5–8] (Fig. 1). While specific triggers of HSV-1 reactivation have often been examined through focused mechanistic studies, each linked to defined molecular pathways that disrupt latency, these pathways are unlikely to operate in isolation in vivo. Instead, multiple biological and environmental stressors can converge on shared and complementary mechanisms, collectively lowering the threshold for reactivation. Therefore, HSV-1 reactivation likely reflects the integrated effects of converging signals that engage multiple reactivation pathways simultaneously.

This framework integrates neuronal vulnerability, neuroinflammatory signaling, neurotrauma, and systemic physiological disruptions, including glucocorticoid activation, sleep and circadian disturbances, and cytokine-induced hyperexcitability, as interconnected processes [2, 5–8]. These factors are hypothesized to converge on shared molecular pathways, including dual leucine zipper kinase/c-Jun N-terminal kinase (DLK/JNK) signaling, chromatin remodeling of the viral genome, heat shock responses, mitochondrial dysfunction, and suppression of antiviral immune surveillance. In this context, HSV-1 reactivation can emerge from a network of stress-responsive processes.

Importantly, TBI has also been reported to trigger HSV-1 reactivation and engages many of the same biological pathways, including neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, blood-brain barrier dysfunction, glucocorticoid signaling, and induction of heat shock proteins (HSPs) [9–11]. Emerging evidence from experimental models and case reports suggests that TBI may create physiological conditions permissive for viral reactivation [12–15]. Despite this reported association and substantial mechanistic overlap, very little is known about how neuronal injuries, such as TBI, interact with latent HSV-1 infection or influence viral reactivation within the CNS. This knowledge gap is particularly important because TBI represents a complex systems-level perturbation that simultaneously engages multiple pathways known to regulate HSV-1 latency and reactivation. It is therefore plausible that neuronal injury may create a permissive environment through convergent stress, immune, and neuronal signaling pathways that facilitate viral reactivation. However, the mechanisms underlying this relationship remain poorly defined.

In this review, we aim to synthesize existing literature on HSV-1 reactivation and integrate these diverse pathways into a broader framework focused on neuronal stress and injury. By examining the diversity of mechanisms that drive HSV-1 reactivation, we seek to identify novel or underexplored triggers arising from neuronal injury and physiological stress that may converge on established reactivation pathways. Furthermore, we use TBI to illustrate how neuronal injury can engage these pathways and may lower the threshold for HSV-1 reactivation. Importantly, we highlight that subclinical HSV-1 reactivation within the CNS remains poorly characterized and likely underdetected. This gap is significant, as repeated low-level or asymptomatic reactivation events may contribute to chronic neuroinflammatory signaling and cumulative neuronal stress. The potential consequences of these interactions for long-term neurological dysfunction and neurodegenerative disease are discussed later in the review.

Biology of HSV-1 latency and reactivation

HSV-1 latency and reactivation

HSV-1 initially infects and replicates in the mucosal epithelial cells through a tightly regulated lytic transcriptional cascade consisting of immediate-early (IE), early (E), and late (L) viral genes before entering neuritic extensions via fusion of the viral envelope with the plasma membrane [16]. Viral entry is mediated by enveloped glycoproteins (e.g., gB, gC, gD), which facilitate attachment and membrane fusion. Viral nucleocapsids are then transported retrogradely along axons to the neuronal cell body, where HSV-1 establishes latency in the sensory and autonomic ganglia. During this process, virion production and lytic gene expression are suppressed, including the silencing of key viral transactivators, such as infected cell protein (ICP) and ICP0, allowing the virus to enter a latent, noninfectious state. Additionally, the viral genome becomes associated with repressive chromatin modifications, including H3K9me3, and H3K27me3, that restrict transcription from lytic promoters [17–19]. The viral genome persists in neuronal nuclei as a circular double-stranded DNA episome. The latency-associated transcript (LAT) is the primary viral RNA expressed during this phase and plays a key role in maintaining latency and inhibiting pro-apoptotic processes [20–24]. LAT is also thought to promote viral genome silencing and suppress lytic gene expression, thereby contributing to long-term stability of latency. In conjunction with host signaling pathways, including high levels of Akt and Wnt/β-catenin, LAT contributes to the regulation of apoptosis, immune responses, and neuronal survival and differentiation [25–27]. Collectively, these mechanisms establish latency as an actively maintained transcriptionally repressed state rather than complete viral dormancy.

Importantly, the maintenance of latency reflects a dynamic balance between pro-latency and pro-reactivation signals within neurons. Rather than being passively maintained, latency is actively stabilized by host signaling pathways that counteract stress-induced transcriptional activation of the viral genome. At the molecular level, latency is characterized by a chromatin-repressed viral genome in which lytic promoters remain largely inaccessible, while the LAT locus retains a comparatively permissive transcriptional state. Disruption of this balance, through the integration of neuroendocrine, immune, and neuronal stress signals, can shift the system toward reactivation. Reactivation is believed to begin with the derepression of IE viral genes, particularly ICP0 and ICP4, followed by re-engagement of the canonical IE-E-L transcriptional program, culminating in viral DNA replication and the production of infectious virions. Although many mechanisms regulating HSV-1 latency and reactivation have been extensively reviewed elsewhere, our discussion is intentionally centered on pathways that intersect with known TBI-associated biological processes. Consequently, not all established reactivation pathways are covered in this review, and additional, as-yet-unidentified mechanisms may further link TBI pathology with HSV-1 reactivation. A broader overview of HSV-1 latency and reactivation is directed to several excellent reviews on the topic [28–31].

Although HSV-1 latency is classically associated with sensory and autonomic ganglia, accumulating evidence suggests that the virus can also establish long-term persistence within the CNS [32]. Following primary infection, HSV-1 may access the brain through neuronal pathways, including the olfactory and trigeminal systems, where viral genomes have been detected in multiple brain regions [33]. Compared with peripheral ganglia, latent viral gene expression within the CNS appears to be more limited and less consistent, which may contribute to the relative rarity of clinically apparent CNS reactivation. Nevertheless, experimental studies demonstrate that latent CNS infection can re-enter the lytic cycle under conditions that disrupt immune surveillance or alter neuronal homeostasis [34–36]. Importantly, even low levels of viral reactivation within the CNS may provoke substantial neuroinflammatory responses involving microglia, astrocytes, and blood-brain barrier dysfunction, suggesting that recurrent CNS viral activity could contribute to neurological pathology even in the absence of overt encephalitis.

The pathways described below represent interconnected components of a broader stress-responsive network that collectively determine whether latency is preserved or destabilized. Additionally, we evaluate how converging mechanisms associated with brain injury may exploit each of these pathways to promote viral reactivation.

Transcriptional regulation of reactivation

Reactivation of HSV-1 from latency is ultimately governed at the level of viral gene expression, where transcriptional access to the viral genome determines whether latency is maintained or disrupted. During latency, lytic gene promoters are tightly repressed through heterochromatinization, limiting transcriptional activity despite the continued presence of the viral genome within neurons [17, 19, 23]. Reactivation, therefore, requires a shift in chromatin state that permits transcription of immediate early viral genes, initiating the lytic cascade.

Importantly, this process is not a binary switch but a graded and dynamic response. Early transcriptional activation, often referred to as Phase I reactivation, can occur in the absence of full viral replication, reflecting partial or transient engagement of stress-responsive pathways. Progression to full reactivation requires sustained transcriptional activation mediated by host transcription factors, including c-Jun and cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB), which amplify and stabilize viral gene expression [37, 38].

c-Jun, a stress-responsive transcription factor capable of binding closed chromatin, promotes chromatin remodeling and contributes to neuronal processes, including apoptosis and axon pruning. Similarly, CREB, a neuronal activity and stress-responsive transcription factor, supports full reactivation, as blocking CREB restricts progression beyond Phase I without preventing its initiation [38, 39]. Together, these findings show that reactivation is a multistep process in which initial stress signaling creates permissive conditions, while downstream transcriptional regulators determine the extent and persistence of viral gene expression.

In addition to acute signaling events, prior stress exposure may leave lasting effects on neuronal state that influence future reactivation risk. Neurons can experience “molecular scarring”, referring to stable changes in the gene-regulatory landscape that bias those neurons toward altered function or increased susceptibility to reactivation [40, 41]. Within the broader stress-responsive network, these changes can shift the baseline balance between pro-latency and pro-reactivation pathways, effectively lowering or raising the threshold for future reactivation events. The Akt pathway generally promotes cell survival and can support chromatin states and signaling that suppress stress-induced reactivation [42, 43]. Wnt/β-catenin can contribute to transcriptional programs that maintain a more stable [44, 45], less-stressed neuronal state and may support latency-favoring chromatin. DNA repair machinery counters the genotoxic signals that activate the DLK/JNK pathway and helps restore a more repressive chromatin structure on the viral genome, pushing the system back toward latency. These pro-latency mechanisms operate in parallel with stress-activated pathways, creating a dynamic and continuously adjusting balance within neurons.

While transcription factors, such as c-Jun and CREB, regulate the progression and amplification of HSV-1 reactivation, they do not function in isolation. Instead, their activity is governed by upstream stress-responsive signaling pathways that integrate diverse physiological inputs into a coordinated neuronal response. Among these, the DLK/JNK pathway emerges as a central regulator linking neuronal stress to transcriptional activation of the viral genome [46, 47].

Traumatic brain injury as an emerging trigger

TBI is a clinically relevant example of how multiple stress-responsive systems might converge to influence HSV-1 reactivation. Injury induces both acute and chronic changes in neuronal physiology, including DNA damage, disruptions in axonal integrity, and alterations in chromatin regulation that interfere with neuronal homeostasis [10, 48] (Fig. 1). These changes recapitulate many of the intracellular conditions known to promote HSV-1 reactivation, suggesting potential mechanistic links between injury-induced stress and viral regulatory pathways.

Increasing evidence from clinical observations and an experimental model suggests that these injury-induced stress states may promote HSV-1 reactivation in the CNS [12, 13, 49]. In a human induced neural stem cell (hiNSC)-derived brain organoid model, repetitive impact modeled using closed head injury (CHI) triggered reactivation of latent HSV-1 when assessed four days after injury [12]. Although organoid systems lack full immune and vascular complexity, these findings demonstrate that neuronal stress alone may be sufficient to disrupt latency. Notably, the persistence of injury-induced stress responses following TBI may create a prolonged window of neuronal vulnerability, during which cumulative signaling continues to shift the system toward reactivation. The effects of sustained injury-induced stress and cumulative signaling would likely be best addressed in animal models.

At a mechanistic level, TBI engages multiple interconnected pathways that converge on previously described reactivation signaling cascades. Rather than acting in isolation, these injury-induced signals converge on shared intracellular pathways, including DLK/JNK and cAMP-dependent signaling. Mechanical injury and Ca²⁺ influx, together with widespread glutamate release, drive sustained neuronal hyperexcitability, while simultaneously engaging stress kinase pathways (Fig. 1). In latently infected neurons, this convergence of excitability and stress signaling is predicted to favor pathways associated with the histone methyl/phospho switch and early lytic gene expression [28, 37]. Thus, TBI represents a physiological context in which multiple upstream stressors are integrated into a unified intracellular response that governs viral reactivation.

Clinical observations further support the link between TBI and HSV-1 reactivation, though the relationship remains complex. In a large prospective study, mechanically ventilated patients with severe TBI, defined by a Glasgow Coma Scale score ≤ 12 and an abnormal CT scan, were monitored for viral reactivation with HSV-1 DNA quantified in blood and tracheal aspirates two weeks after injury [50]. These findings suggest that TBI may create physiological conditions permissive for viral reactivation in humans. A key strength of this study is the prospective design and the controlled clinical setting, as patients were hospitalized and continuously monitored following severe injury. However, the study focused exclusively on patients with severe TBI and mechanical ventilation, limiting the generalizability of the findings to milder injuries. Additionally, while the association between viral reactivation and poorer outcomes is compelling, the observational nature of the study prevents determination of causality. Consistent with these findings, a limited number of case reports document HSV-1 reactivation following TBI and related central nervous system insults [13–15, 49, 51]. Importantly, several of these reports describe viral reactivation following traumatic injuries in the absence of neurosurgical intervention [14, 15]. Although anecdotal, these observations reduce the likelihood that surgical manipulation or perioperative factors alone are responsible for reactivation and instead suggest that injury-associated physiological stress may itself be sufficient to disrupt viral latency. Although rare, these cases provide clinical evidence that disruption of neural tissue can coincide with loss of latency control. Together, these reports extend the evidence for TBI-associated HSV-1 reactivation beyond surgically managed injuries and support the hypothesis that trauma itself may contribute to reactivation susceptibility. Notably, how HSV-1 reactivation following injury influences neurological outcomes remains largely unexplored.

Importantly, TBI does not engage these pathways in isolation but instead represents a systems-level convergence point for multiple interacting stress responses. Endocrine stress signaling, neuroinflammatory activation, and injury-induced stress kinase pathways are simultaneously engaged within vulnerable neuronal populations. The combined activation of these pathways shifts the balance of the stress-responsive network, promoting transcriptional accessibility of the viral genome while impairing antiviral immune surveillance. We propose that TBI may lower the threshold required for HSV-1 reactivation by coordinating multiple upstream signals into a unified intracellular response; however, direct in vivo evidence demonstrating this mechanism within the CNS remains limited. Rather than acting as a singular trigger, injury represents a sustained perturbation that alters network dynamics over time, prolonging periods of neuronal vulnerability. Thus, viral reactivation may serve as a marker of network-level perturbation rather than a singular pathological event.

Collectively, these findings position TBI as a profound neuronal vulnerability state in which sleep disturbances, immune dysregulation, and persistent stress signaling converge to shape the likelihood and timing of HSV-1 reactivation. This provides a basis for understanding how diverse physiological and pathological conditions may interact to influence viral behavior within the CNS.

Integrated neuroimmune and stress pathways regulating HSV-1 reactivation

Stress as a regulator of HSV-1 reactivation

Throughout this review, the term “stress” refers broadly to physiological, neuroendocrine, inflammatory, metabolic, oxidative, and neuronal perturbations capable of influencing the balance between HSV-1 latency and reactivation. Although these stressors differ in origin, many converge on shared intracellular signaling pathways that regulate chromatin accessibility, neuronal excitability, immune surveillance, and viral gene expression. Consequently, HSV-1 reactivation is increasingly viewed not as a response to a single trigger, but as the integrated outcome of multiple converging stress-responsive pathways.

Within this framework, stress acts as a systems-level regulator of latency. Diverse stressors engage overlapping molecular mechanisms that collectively influence whether latency is maintained or disrupted. The pathways discussed below represent key nodes within this broader stress-responsive network and provide a mechanistic link between external physiological perturbations and viral gene expression.

DLK/JNK as a central stress integrator

The DLK signaling pathway regulates synaptic function, neural regeneration, and neurodegeneration and was originally discovered in invertebrate models [52, 53], then subsequently characterized in peripheral neurons in the context of mechanical axonal injury [54–57]. Upon activation, DLK signals through JNK to initiate a DLK/JNK stress response cascade. The outcome of HSV-1 latency versus reactivation is determined by the dynamic balance between pro-reactivation and pro-latency pathways within this integrated network.

Consistent with the broader stress-responsive framework described above, real-world stressors such as injury, inflammation, loss of trophic support, hormonal signaling, and oxidative stress can transiently push latently infected neurons toward DLK/JNK activation, while opposing pathways act to stabilize latency. Rather than functioning as independent switches, these signals are continuously integrated, and the net balance of their activity determines whether latency is maintained, becomes “leaky,” or progresses to full lytic reactivation. A major contributor to DLK/JNK activation is neuroendocrine stress signaling, particularly through activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA axis) and subsequent glucocorticoid release. These signals provide a key link between systemic physiological stress and neuronal pathways that influence HSV-1 latency and reactivation.

Stress and glucocorticoid signaling

Stress represents one of several upstream inputs into the stress-responsive network that governs HSV-1 latency and reactivation [2, 58]. Acute or chronic stress activates the HPA axis, leading to glucocorticoid release. These hormones bind intracellular glucocorticoid receptors (GR), which dimerize, translocate to the nucleus, and bind glucocorticoid response elements (GREs) to regulate genes involved in inflammatory and immune responses [25, 34].

Glucocorticoid signaling can shift the balance between latency and reactivation by simultaneously altering immune function and the transcriptional programs of both viruses and neurons. HSV-1 is highly responsive to stress-mediated signaling, and GR activation enhances viral lytic gene expression, thereby promoting reactivation. The HSV-1 genome contains multiple GREs and half-GREs to which activated GR can bind. GRs also cooperate with other stress-activated host transcription factors to activate key early viral promoters, driving expression of critical regulatory proteins, including ICP0, ICP4, and ICP27. At the same time, glucocorticoids suppress inflammatory signaling and weaken immune responses, which facilitates the transition of HSV-1 from latency to active replication [25].

In addition to the magnitude of stress signaling, the timing of these signals further shapes their impact on HSV-1 latency. The activity of the HPA axis, and consequently glucocorticoid release, is shaped by circadian rhythmicity. Circadian activation of the HPA axis toward the end of the sleep period (early morning) increases glucocorticoid levels, which exert anti-inflammatory effects and counterbalance earlier pro-inflammatory activity [59, 60]. This daily oscillation provides an additional layer of regulation that may influence the timing and likelihood of HSV-1 reactivation.

TBI represents a potent and clinically relevant physiological stressor capable of engaging this same glucocorticoid-driven regulatory network. Following TBI, rapid activation of the HPA axis drives a surge in circulating cortisol (or corticosterone in rodents), with levels rising within hours of injury and often remaining elevated for days depending on injury severity and systemic stress [9, 61, 62].

This acute glucocorticoid response is initially adaptive, functioning to limit inflammation; however, sustained or dysregulated signaling can alter neuronal transcriptional programs, disrupt hippocampal circuitry, and reshape neuroimmune responses. Importantly, glucocorticoid receptor activation in this context mirrors canonical stress-induced pathways known to promote HSV-1 reactivation, including transcriptional activation of viral immediate early genes and suppression of antiviral immunity. The magnitude and trajectory of cortisol responses after TBI are highly variable, with some patients exhibiting prolonged elevations associated with worse neurological outcomes.

While glucocorticoid signaling represents a well-defined neuroendocrine pathway linking systemic stress to HSV-1 reactivation, many physiological stressors engage multiple components of the stress-responsive network simultaneously. Systems-level stressors such as heat and fever exemplify this complexity, as they co-activate inflammatory signaling, hormonal responses, and neuronal stress pathways in parallel.

Systems-level stressors: heat and fever

Heat and fever represent system-wide stressors that simultaneously activate multiple components of the stress-responsive network. Thermal stress is a well-established trigger for reactivation of HSV-1 [8, 63]. Rather than acting through a single pathway, these conditions co-activate heat shock responses, inflammatory signaling, glucocorticoid pathways, and neuronal stress mechanisms, collectively creating a physiological environment that can promote HSV-1 reactivation. In this context, heat- and fever-associated signals converge with cytokine production and neuronal hyperexcitability to engage downstream pathways such as DLK/JNK (as mentioned above).

Heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) and HSPs are co-activated with inflammatory cytokines and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) during thermal stress [64, 65]. These responses shape the magnitude and duration of inflammatory signaling, indirectly influencing neuronal excitability and downstream stress pathway engagement. HSPs also support protein folding and cellular stress tolerance, potentially enabling both host cells and the virus to persist through stress conditions. Importantly, HSPs do not initiate reactivation but instead act as modulators and facilitators once stress-responsive pathways are engaged [66].

Hsp90 localizes to HSV-1 replication compartments and virus-induced chaperone-enriched (VICE) foci, where it supports viral replication processes. Inhibition of Hsp90 alters viral DNA polymerase function and localization and disrupts nuclear transport of capsid proteins such as ICP5 [67, 68]. HSV-1 infection also induces accumulation and re-localization of heat-shock-responsive proteins, including p57, consistent with the virus exploiting host stress responses during productive infection [69]. Together, these findings position heat shock responses as amplifiers of an already activated network, rather than primary drivers of HSV-1 reactivation.

TBI similarly engages the heat shock response as part of a broader cellular stress program that overlaps with pathways implicated in HSV-1 reactivation. Following injury, heat shock proteins, such as HSP70 and small HSPs (e.g., HSPB2), are rapidly upregulated in neurons and glial cells, where they function to restore proteostasis, limit protein aggregation, and mitigate secondary injury cascades including excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and apoptosis [64]. While these responses are primarily neuroprotective, they also reflect a cellular environment characterized by heightened stress signaling, protein turnover, and activation of chaperone systems that are similarly exploited during viral infection. As observed in HSV-1 infection, these chaperone systems may also facilitate viral processes once reactivation is initiated, including protein folding, trafficking, and replication efficiency. Thus, TBI-induced heat shock responses, like those triggered by thermal stress, are unlikely to act as primary drivers of HSV-1 reactivation but instead serve as amplifiers of an already engaged stress-responsive network, reinforcing conditions that support viral progression once latency is destabilized.

Neuroimmune and sleep contributions

Cytokine signaling provides a key interface between immune activation and neuronal stress pathways within this integrated network. Interleukin (IL)-1β is released during physiological stress, fever, and UV exposure and can act on both immune cells and neurons, including sympathetic neurons in which HSV-1 often lies latent [46]. Through these actions, IL-1β links systemic inflammatory states to changes in neuronal function that influence viral reactivation.

IL-1β exposure increases neuronal excitability by enhancing the expression and function of voltage-gated sodium channels, thereby promoting sustained neuronal firing [37, 70]. Thus, cytokine signaling converges with other stress-induced inputs to engage shared intracellular pathways, including DLK/JNK activation and chromatin remodeling of the viral genome. HSV-1 has evolved to interpret a specific combination of neuronal stress and chromatin modification as a signal to exit latency and initiate replication.

Importantly, the in vivo context is likely shaped by the composition, magnitude, and duration of cytokine signaling rather than the presence of a single mediator. While IL-1β provides a well-characterized example, the specific combinations of cytokines, tissues, and neuronal populations that most strongly influence reactivation remain incompletely defined. Experimental models using primary neuronal cultures demonstrate that stimuli increasing excitability, such as forskolin-induced cAMP elevation, require DLK/JNK activation and produce more robust reactivation under sustained conditions [37]. These findings further support the concept that immune-derived signals contribute to reactivation by integrating into broader neuronal stress pathways rather than acting as independent triggers.

Beyond cytokine signaling, innate immune pathways further integrate environmental and physiological stress signals with neuronal responses relevant to HSV-1 reactivation. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) detect viral and damage-associated signals and initiate cytokine and interferon responses. TLR3, TLR9, TLR2, and TLR4 recognize viral intermediates and components, including those of HSV [71, 72], with TLR2 strongly linked to HSV-1 immunopathology [71, 73]. Microglia, the resident innate immune cells of the CNS, express these receptors [74], positioning them as key mediators linking immune activation to neuronal stress states [75–77].

Sleep supports coordination of innate and adaptive immune responses [78, 79]. During sleep, particularly slow-wave sleep, neuroendocrine conditions favor immune cell trafficking, antigen presentation, and T cell activation [59]. In contrast, sleep disturbances shift the immune system toward a pro-inflammatory state, characterized by elevated cytokine production, impaired interferon-mediated antiviral responses, and increased risk for infection and inflammatory disease [59, 80, 81]. Sleep disturbances serve as physiological stressors that amplify inflammatory signaling within the stress-responsive network and alter immune function [82]. Excessive sleep loss heightens vulnerability to infection [83], exacerbates inflammatory responses [84], and reduces vaccine-induced antibody production and immunological memory [85, 86]. Additionally, sleep loss increases circulating and central levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and IL-1β, thereby reinforcing inflammatory pathways and neuronal stress signaling. This bidirectional relationship between sleep and inflammation further destabilizes the balance between pro-latency and pro-reactivation processes [87].

Consistent with these effects, acute sleep deprivation increases circulating TNF-α [88]. Sleep deprivation also contributes to elevated cytokine levels in plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with prolonged wakefulness [80, 89]. Through these effects, sleep loss amplifies inflammatory signaling that can feed into neuronal stress pathways and may increase susceptibility to HSV-1 reactivation. In addition to promoting inflammatory signaling, sleep deprivation disrupts interferon-mediated antiviral responses [82] and increases prostaglandin production following microglial activation [90], indicating broad dysregulation of neuroimmune signaling during extended wakefulness. Conversely, normal sleep supports immune recalibration and antiviral defense, maintaining a balance that stabilizes neuronal and viral states [91–94].

At the cellular level, microglia adopt a more reactive phenotype during wakefulness and return to surveillance during sleep; chronic sleep loss promotes sustained microglial activation, altered morphology, and increased synaptic remodeling [95–101]. Through these effects, prolonged wakefulness biases the system toward a pro-inflammatory, less antiviral state that may lower the threshold for HSV-1 reactivation. This interaction is supported by the observation that insomnia strongly increases the risk of HSV [102].

TBI has been shown to disrupt both sleep-wake regulation and immune homeostasis [61, 103–105], thereby provoking the simultaneous engagement of stress-responsive networks as described above. In preclinical models, TBI is well established as a disruptor of sleep, with both mild and moderate injury associated with increases in total sleep time and injury severity predicting the degree of sleep fragmentation [103, 104, 106]. TBI acutely increases sleep while simultaneously increasing sleep fragmentation [103, 104].

Together, these alterations reflect potential disruptions in neural circuits regulating arousal and sleep continuity, as well as broader changes in neuroimmune signaling. Within the context of HSV-1 latency, these changes are significant because sleep disruption and fragmentation directly influence the balance of inflammatory and antiviral signaling. By altering immune function and neural circuit stability, TBI-induced sleep disturbances may shift the system toward a pro-inflammatory and less antiviral state, potentially increasing susceptibility to viral reactivation.

Circuit-level integration and reactivation dynamics

HSV-1 infection has been characterized in key brainstem nuclei, including the locus coeruleus and the nucleus of the solitary tract [33]. However, detection of viral presence in these regions does not necessarily indicate that they serve as primary sites of latency, as the precise anatomical distribution of latent HSV-1 within the brain remains incompletely defined. Notably, these same regions were recently identified as central components of the brainstem circuitry that mediates sickness-induced sleep [107], positioning HSV-1 within neural systems that both regulate and are dynamically reshaped by inflammatory brain states. This anatomical and functional overlap suggests that viral latency may be embedded within circuits governing arousal, autonomic integration, and immune signaling, placing HSV-1 in a position to be influenced by state-dependent changes in brain activity.

This convergence provides a mechanism through which established triggers of HSV‑1 reactivation interact within shared anatomical and functional brain regions over time. Such a framework also accommodates bidirectional feedback, whereby HSV‑1 reactivation may reshape neural circuits involved in sleep and autonomic regulation, further modifying the brain states that influence subsequent viral behavior. In this way, reactivation history itself may become embedded within circuit dynamics, reinforcing vulnerability through repeated cycles of inflammation and state disruption.

TBI represents one example of a systems-level perturbation that synchronizes multiple reactivation‑associated pathways across vulnerable neuronal populations. Initial reactivation events may further destabilize this network by amplifying inflammatory signaling and altering sleep regulation, thereby increasing susceptibility to subsequent episodes. Critically, these interactions unfold across multiple temporal scales, from acute inflammatory and sleep disturbances to chronic alterations in circuit function, suggesting that brief perturbations may be integrated over time to bias long‑term reactivation risk.

Converging risk factors of dementia

Increasing evidence implicates HSV-1 reactivation in broader neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative processes, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and related dementias [12, 108–110]. Viral DNA and transcripts have been detected in postmortem human AD brains, and epidemiological and experimental studies suggest associations between HSV-1 infection and pathological hallmarks of neurodegeneration, including amyloid-β accumulation, tau pathology, neuroinflammation, and synaptic dysfunction [43, 111–115]. Despite these associations, the mechanisms by which latent or recurrent HSV-1 infection contributes to CNS dysfunction remain poorly understood.

TBI and HSV-1 infection have independently been implicated as risk factors for neurodegenerative disease, including AD [12, 116–119]. TBI is associated with chronic neuroinflammation, disrupted proteostasis, and long-term alterations in neuronal excitability, while HSV-1 has been linked to amyloidogenic processing, tau pathology, and recurrent inflammatory activation within the CNS [112, 114]. Given that TBI has been associated with HSV-1 reactivation in experimental and clinical studies and that both conditions engage many of the same pathways involved in neuronal stress and inflammation, their interaction may represent an underappreciated mechanism of neurological vulnerability.

Within the framework proposed here, TBI may act not only as an acute trigger of HSV-1 reactivation but also as a factor that amplifies the long-term consequences of recurrent viral activity. Repeated cycles of injury-induced stress, viral reactivation, and neuroimmune activation could create a feed-forward process characterized by persistent inflammation, circuit disruption, and cumulative neuronal stress. Although direct evidence remains limited, the co-occurrence of TBI and HSV-1 infection may confer greater risk than either factor alone and represents an important area for future investigation.

Conclusions

HSV-1 latency and reactivation cannot be fully understood through isolated triggers or single molecular pathways. Instead, the work synthesized here supports a model in which reactivation emerges from the integration of physiological, immunological, and neuronal stress signals that collectively destabilize latency. Neuronal injury, hyperexcitability, immune dysregulation, and systemic disruptions, such as sleep loss or TBI, converge on shared molecular mechanisms regulating chromatin accessibility, transcriptional competence, and cellular vulnerability. HSV-1 reactivation is best viewed as a threshold-dependent outcome of cumulative stress rather than a binary switch initiated by a single factor.

Central to this model is the concept of neuronal vulnerability states. Pathways such as DLK/JNK signaling, calcium- and cAMP-dependent cascades, and stress-responsive transcription factors, including c-Jun and CREB, are essential for neuronal maintenance and repair, yet HSV-1 has evolved to exploit these same pathways as indicators of host stress. When stress exceeds the capacity of pro-latency mechanisms, such as chromatin silencing, Akt and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, and DNA repair–associated repression, latent viral genomes gain transcriptional access. The resulting outcome, ranging from abortive transcription to full lytic reactivation, depends on the magnitude, duration, and context of these signals.

Immune status and systemic physiology further shape these neuronal outcomes. Disruptions such as sleep and circadian misalignment bias the system toward inflammation and reduced antiviral capacity, lowering the threshold for reactivation. TBI represents a key convergence point, combining endocrine stress, neuroinflammation, and axonal injury, each linked to pathways implicated in HSV-1 reactivation. We propose that these converging processes may create prolonged windows of neuronal vulnerability that favor viral emergence, although direct evidence remains limited.

This perspective also reframes the clinical relevance of subclinical reactivation. Repeated, low-level events that do not produce overt symptoms may nonetheless contribute to chronic neuroinflammation, neuronal dysfunction, or cumulative damage over time, potentially intersecting with neurodegenerative disease or impaired recovery following injury. Although causality remains unresolved, HSV-1 reactivation may act as both a consequence and an amplifier of neurological stress.

Recognizing reactivation as a network-level phenomenon has important therapeutic implications. Approaches that target viral replication alone may be insufficient if upstream host states remain permissive. Interventions aimed at neuronal hyperexcitability, stress-responsive signaling pathways (e.g., DLK/JNK), chromatin remodeling, or maladaptive immune activation may offer more durable strategies. Similarly, improving physiological resilience, through management of sleep, inflammation, and injury recovery, may help stabilize latency. The potential benefit of antiviral therapy following TBI warrants further investigation.

Despite the mechanistic overlap and emerging clinical observations discussed throughout this review, direct evidence linking TBI to HSV-1 reactivation within the CNS remains limited. Much of the current evidence derives from experimental models, observational clinical studies, and isolated case reports, each of which carries important limitations. Organoid models do not fully recapitulate the immune and vascular complexity of the intact CNS, clinical studies are often restricted to severely injured patient populations and cannot establish causality, and case reports provide only anecdotal support for injury-associated reactivation. Consequently, the framework proposed here should be viewed as a hypothesis-generating model that integrates existing evidence and identifies mechanistic convergence points rather than a definitive explanation of TBI-induced HSV-1 reactivation. Future studies capable of directly measuring latent viral activity before and after injury will be essential for establishing causality and determining whether TBI meaningfully alters HSV-1 reactivation dynamics within the CNS.

Overall, HSV-1 reactivation reflects an emergent property of stressed neural systems rather than a purely virus-centric event. This framework integrates mechanisms traditionally studied in isolation into a systems-level model linking neuronal state to viral behavior.

Importantly, key gaps remain. The role of brain-state dynamics, particularly sleep, in shaping HSV-1 latency and reactivation is poorly defined. Characterizing how HSV-1 alters sleep architecture across infection stages and how these changes influence viral transcription may provide new insights into pathogenesis and identify accessible biomarkers. Additionally, the frequency and impact of subclinical CNS reactivation, particularly following mild TBI, remain underexplored and likely underdetected. Furthermore, it is unclear whether HSV-1 reactivation in the CNS is uncoupled from peripheral reactivation and therefore can go undetected by traditional measures of viral presence in saliva or skin. Addressing these questions will require longitudinal, translational approaches capable of capturing dynamic host-virus interactions.
