An uncommon cause of epigastric pain in a child: falciform ligament appendigitis
Authors: Raja Tamiru Muleta, Nibretu Bekele Kassa, Tazebew Tilahun Alamneh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFalciform ligament is a double fold of the peritoneum which has fat attached to it. The torsion of this fat leads to appendigitis which is a rare cause of acute severe epigastric pain. A 7-year-old boy presented with severe epigastric pain and vomiting of 3 days duration with associated epigastric tenderness. Ultrasound showed echogenic mass lesion adjacent to falciform ligament without color flow with associated thickening of the ligament which resolved after 01 week on follow up visit. Falciform ligament appendigitis is a rare cause of epigastric pain in the general spopulation and in pediatrics age group which should be considered in appropriate clinical setup. Early suggestion of falciform ligament appendigitis by a radiologist can help to avoid unnecessary surgeries as the clinical picture of this benign process overlaps with other surgical emergencies.Keywords: Falciform ligament, Appendigitis, Epigastric pain, Fat infarction

Introduction

Falciform ligament is a double fold of the peritoneum which anatomically divides the liver into right and left lobes [1]. The falciform ligament contains extraperitoneal fat [2]. Intraperitoneal focal fat infarction is the umbrella term referring to self-limiting group of diseases as a result of primary inflammatory or ischemic change of peritoneal fat. It includes epiploic appendigitis, omental infarction and perigastric appendagitis. Among these perigastric appendigitis is the rarest. Perigastric appendigitis in turn involves the gastrohepatic, gastrosplenic and falciform ligament [[1], [3]]. Patient with falciform ligament appendigitis presents with epigastric pain which is non specific and laboratory tests are usually nonrevealing. So, imaging plays crucial role in suggesting the diagnosis. Falciform ligament appendigitis is uncommon and there is very little documentation on the current literature [2]. Here we present a case of falciform ligament appendigitis in a 7-year-old boy who presented with epigastric pain of 03 days duration.

Case presentation

We report a case of 7-year-old boy who presented with severe epigastric pain and vomiting of 03 days duration. No history of trauma, no history of change in bowel habit, no history of fever. Physical examination showed epigastric tenderness. The vital signs were in the normal range. The treating physician ordered laboratory tests and abdominal ultrasound. The CBC result revealed all parameters are in the normal range (WBC = 5.6 × 109/L, Hgb = 14 g/dL, Platelet= 240 × 109/L). Liver enzymes (ALT = 32 IU, AST = 33 IU, ALP = 50 IU) and Renal function test (Creatinine = 0.6mg/dL, BUN = 16 mg/dL) are also nonrevealing.

Abdominal ultrasound revealed thickened and edematous falciform ligament with associated well defined mass lesion in-continuity with the ligament. The lesion is echogenic with rim of hypoechogenicity. On color doppler interrogation no color flow detected. It measures 2.7 cm x 1.4 cm (Fig. 1). The appendix was visualized at the RLQ and had normal transverse diameter of 4.3 mm with compressible lumen. With these findings the diagnosis of falciform ligament appendigitis was suggested and follow up ultrasound scan after 01 week recommended. Treating physician counseled the parents, prescribed po antipain (Ibuprofen 100 mg/5 mL,7.5 mL, po, PRN) and appointed the patient after 01 week. Up on follow up visit after 01 week the epigastric pain gone and the boy presented to the ultrasound room with cheerful well looking appearance. Follow up ultrasound scan revealed normal appearing nonedematous falciform ligament with the adjacent lesion resolved not visualized on the scan (Fig. 2). This confirmed the diagnosis and the patient was sent home.

Trans-abdominal scan of the patient showed thickened and edematous falciform ligament as shown by the arrow (A). At the epigastric region just adjacent to the falciform ligament there is a well-defined echogenic lesion with hypoechoic rim (shown by the arrow). The lesion has no internal color flow (B). The vermiform appendix is visualized at the RLQ with normal diameter and compressible lumen (indicated by the arrow) (C).

Trans abdominal scan at the epigastric region after 01 week showed normalization of the falciform ligament (indicated by the arrows) (A and B) and resolution of the previously observed lesion.

Discussion

Intra-abdominal focal fat infarction usually affects the larger omentum or epiploic appendages. It rarely affects the perigastric ligaments which includes gastrohepatic, gastrosplenic, and falciform ligaments [4]. These ligaments may experience ischemia and inflammation due to torsion of the fat. Patients typically appear with severe abdominal pain. The location of the involved peritoneal fat determines the location of pain. In falciform ligament appendigitis patients usually present with acute epigastric pain and tenderness [5]. Pediatric patients with focal epigastric discomfort are considerably more likely to have inflammatory disorders such acute gastritis, cholecystitis, or pancreatitis as a cause. As the clinical presentation overlaps with more common illnesses, high index of suspicion is required during imaging these patients in order to suggest the diagnosis of falciform ligament appendigitis. This avoids unnecessary surgery as the main stay of treatment is conservative [[1], [5], [6]].

CT scan or ultrasound can be used to diagnose toted falciform ligament fatty appendage. Ultrasound has several advantages over CT scan [[5], [7]]. The first and most being because it is radiation free which is a big concern especially in children. The other is its easy accessibility and it its capability of real-time imaging. The ultrasound finding of falciform ligament appendigitis is hyperechoic mass lesion at the falciform ligament region at the location of maximal epigastric tenderness and showing no internal color flow. The lesion usually disappears during follow up as the inflammatory process subside and the nonviable appendage is absorbed [[5], [6]].

Conservative management is the preferred treatment option given the benign self-resolving nature of the disease. In that regard, the choice of treatment is using NSAID antipains. Surgical resection of the fatty ligament is preserved for cases with unresolving and persistent abdominal pain [[1], [5]].

Conclusion

Falciform ligament appendigitis is an uncommon cause of epigastric pain in children. As its clinical presentation overlaps with more common other causes of acute abdomen imaging plays crucial role in diagnosing the condition. Radiologists should have understanding and high index of suspicion for fat infarctions in order to avoid unnecessary surgeries because conservative management is the first line of treatment.
