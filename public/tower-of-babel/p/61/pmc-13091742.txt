Toxoplasmic granuloma within an epiretinal membrane in a patient with recurrent ocular toxoplasmosis
Authors: Marcelo Rudzinski, Juan Cortalezzi, Margarita Erhard, Lourdes Racca, Cristóbal Couto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Ocular toxoplasmosis (OT) is the leading cause of posterior uveitis worldwide.1 It is highly prevalent in the southern states of Brazil2 and the neighboring Misiones province in Argentina.3 Clinically, OT presents as retinochoroiditis with vitritis,4 often accompanied by retinal vasculitis and papillitis.4 Complications include cataract, glaucoma, macular edema, retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, epiretinal membrane (ERM), and band keratopathy.5

ERM formation results from preretinal proliferation of myofibroblastic cells and extracellular matrix deposition.6 ERMs are a recognized complication of posterior and intermediate uveitis, with an incidence of up to 40%.7 In a cohort of 5450 eyes with uveitis, 216 presented with ERM at diagnosis; of these, 15 (9.6%) had OT.8

Granulomas are organized aggregates of activated immune cells formed in response to persistent inflammatory stimuli. Histologically, they consist of epithelioid histiocytes surrounding a central core, with a peripheral cuff of lymphocytes and plasma cells, and occasionally a necrotic center.9 Histopathologic analysis of toxoplasmic retinochoroiditis reveals intense inflammation in the retina, overlying vitreous, and underlying choroid, with granulomatous infiltration adjacent to the lesion.10

In this report, we describe the occurrence of granuloma within an ERM in a patient with recurrent toxoplasmic retinochoroiditis.

Case description

A 54-year-old female patient presented to a uveitis specialist (MR) with a one-year history of decreased visual acuity (VA) and myodesopsia (floaters) in the right eye (OD). The patient reported a history of recurrent ocular toxoplasmosis (OT) in the OD, for which she had been previously treated by outside ophthalmologists with sequential courses of systemic anti-parasitic and anti-inflammatory therapy: first, pyrimethamine, sulfadiazine, meprednisone, and folinic acid, and subsequently, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX), meprednisone, and folinic acid. Both regimens resulted in only partial clinical response. She denied any significant chronic systemic health issues or long-term medication use prior to the onset of ocular symptoms. She denied any intraocular drug treatment.

A comprehensive ophthalmic examination was performed. Laboratory investigations included polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) types I and II, Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV), and Cytomegalovirus (CMV) from an aqueous humor tap; and serology for Treponema pallidum, Toxoplasma gondii, and Bartonella henselae. Systemic workup included a purified protein derivative (PPD) skin test, chest X-ray, and a Central Nervous System (CNS) Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI). Further laboratory tests included antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA), antibodies anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA-c and ANCA-p), and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) levels.

Results

Upon initial examination, the Best Corrected Visual Acuity (BCVA) was 20/400 in the OD and 20/20 in the left eye (OS). Ocular findings in the OD included granulomatous keratic precipitates in the central and inferior paracentral cornea. Intraocular pressure (IOP) was elevated at 35 mmHg (OD) versus 16 mmHg (OS). Anterior chamber inflammation was graded as flare 2+ and cells 2+ (SUN).11

Fundus examination revealed multiple retinal lesions. The active lesions, located along the superior temporal vessels, appeared white and well-defined, while others temporal to the macula had “ground-glass” appearance, suggesting different stages of activity (long thin yellow arrow, Fig. 1A). Vitreous inflammation was 2+. Ultra-widefield digital retinal imaging (Optos, California) showed areas of retinal fibrosis, retinochoroideal scarring around the superior temporal vessels and in the inferior retinal quadrants, and a pigmented, inactive retinochoroideal scar in the inferonasal quadrant (asterisk, Fig. 1B). Optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the macular area demonstrated cystoid macular edema (CME) and a thick ERM causing disruption of the retinal layers and absence of the foveal pit. This corresponded to a grade 2 classification of ERM in patients with uveitis, according to Aaron Yap 8 (Fig. 1C). OCT through the active lesions revealed hyper-reflective changes in the choroid, decreased retinal thickness, altered retinal architecture, and an overlying thick ERM (thick yellow arrow, Fig. 1D).

While awaiting laboratory results, the patient was initiated on TMP-SMX (160 mg/800 mg/day), azithromycin (250 mg/day), meprednisone (40 mg/day) and folinic acid. The BCVA improved to 20/40 after 45 days of treatment. All infectious workups (PCR for HSV/VZV/CMV; serology for syphilis/Bartonella; PPD/chest X-ray) were negative. Anti-Toxoplasma gondii IgG titters were 182 IU/mL (positive), and IgM was below the cutoff. The CNS MRI with gadolinium did not show abnormalities. Systemic inflammatory markers were all negative or within normal limits. After 60 days of treatment with TMP-SMX + azithromycin and meprednisone, the treatment was suspended. The BCVA decreased to 20/70 after four weeks of treatment suspension. An active area of retinitis was observed temporal to the macula; vitreal inflammation was graded as 2+ according to SUN.11 Treatment was re-initiated with TMP-SMX + azithromycin + meprednisone, and oral clindamycin (300 mg four times daily) was added. Clindamycin was suspended after 4 weeks to avoid severe secondary effects such as pseudomembranous colitis. The patient completed a second round of 2 months of TMP-SMX + azithromycin + meprednisone followed by a preventive OT reactivation treatment with TMP-SMX 3 times per week. Despite the systemic therapy, there was no resolution of the retinochoroiditis area temporal to the macula with persistence of mild vitritis. Further progression of tractional forces on OCT was recorded, so the patient was scheduled for vitrectomy to relieve retinal traction. Due to socioeconomic constraints, the surgery was delayed for 60 days, during which time retinal fibrosis worsened. The report of the examination performed by the vitreoretinal surgeon immediately before surgery revealed a BCVA of 20/100. Nor retinal or preretinal granuloma was observed during the indirect binocular ophthalmoscopy nor in the OCT images of the examination performed immediately before the vitrectomy by the vitreoretinal surgeon. A retinal detachment was detected pre-operatively.

During vitrectomy, ERMs were successfully peeled from the macula and temporal arcades. Due to the extensive glial proliferation and the associated detachment, silicone tamponade was performed. The peeled membranes were sent for pathological analysis. After four months of post-surgery, the patient's BCVA remained 20/60. Pathology results confirmed the presence of a granuloma within the fibroglial tissue of the ERM (yellow arrow on Fig. 2A). Toxoplasma gondii tachyzoites were microscopically identified within the cells of the granuloma (red arrows on Fig. 2B, blue arrows on Fig. 2C).

Discussion

Granuloma formation within retinal tissue is well-documented in enucleated eyes from patients with infectious uveitis caused by parasites such as Toxocara canis12 and Toxoplasma gondii.13 Granulomas induced by Toxocara canis are readily visible during fundoscopy, as T. canis larvae measures approximately 404 μM in length and 20 μM in width14 and because optical coherence tomography (OCT) typically shows these lesions involving the inner retinal layers.15 In contrast, T. gondii induces retinal necrosis with lymphocytic and mononuclear infiltration of the retina and the choroid, clinically translated into an area of retinochoroiditis and easily observed during indirect ophthalmoscopy examination.16 Histopathologically the area of retinochoroiditis may comprise one or more retinal granulomas. Consequently, a Toxoplasmic retinal granuloma has traditionally been defined by histopathological criteria.16 Recently, the term “granuloma” has been used to describe inflammatory deposits visualized via multimodal imaging of the retina in patients with active OT.17,18

In this report, we describe the histopathological diagnosis of a toxoplasmic granuloma within an epiretinal membrane (ERM) in a patient with recurrent OT. ERMs are known to be stimulated by the release of inflammatory mediators (IL-6, PDGF, TGF-β) into the vitreoretinal interface.19 The intraocular milieu during active toxoplasmic retinochoroiditis presents molecules involved in the activation and transformation of macrophages such as IFN-γ and TNF-α20,21 implicated in the formation of granuloma as well as those associated with myofibrocytes and glial cell stimulation such as IL-6, PDGF, VEGF,20 involved in ERM formation. Elevated IL-6 levels in aqueous humor have been associated with severe OT in South American patients,20,21 while high VEGF levels correlate with increased OT reactivation frequency in the same population.22

Previously, Tolou C et al.17 and Guagnini AP et al.23 reported preretinal nodules and spherical deposits visualized by OCT in association with retinochoroiditis. Similar deposits were also described in a patient with probable sarcoidosis.24 In OT patients, these deposits were located at the vitreoretinal interface and pre-hyaloid space, along temporal vessels, and over the macula. In those cases, the lesions resolved within two months following the addition of systemic corticosteroids to antiparasitic therapy. In our patient, however, the granuloma within the ERM was not detectable via indirect ophthalmoscopy or OCT, despite longitudinal imaging. Consequently, we could not document the exact timing of its formation or its response to various therapeutic regimens. Notably, the patient exhibited areas of retinal and preretinal fibrosis. The former appeared as “ground-glass” opacities on ophthalmoscopy, distinct from active retinochoroiditis and well-defined retinochoroideal scars.

Given the presence of retinal and preretinal fibrosis and granulomatous keratic precipitates at the initial exam, the toxoplasmic granuloma likely predated our observation. Furthermore, the lesion persisted despite aggressive management, including two consecutive plans of two-month courses of antifolates, macrolides, and corticosteroids, followed by preventive OT treatment with TMP-SMX. The decline in best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) may be attributed to progressive distortion of the macular architecture by the ERM and persistent vitritis refractory to systemic therapy.

The presence of T. gondii organisms within a granuloma confined to the ERM may have biological and therapeutic implications. ERM are avascular structures in nature. Given that this patient received only systemic anti-parasitic and corticosteroid treatment (without intravitreal administration), the ERM may act as a barrier, protecting the parasite from systemically delivered drugs that rely on vascular access to the lesion. Ophthalmologists should consider intravitreal treatment with clindamycin after partial response to oral anti-toxoplasmic drugs or vitrectomy with ERM peeling in South American patients that have extensive pre-retinal fibrosis and successive reactivations of OT.
