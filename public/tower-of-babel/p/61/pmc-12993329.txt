CD79A/CD40 intracellular domain uses a 4-1BB-like metabolic pathway driven by cholesterol biosynthesis
Authors: Yuki Takeuchi, Seitaro Terakura, Kohei Ishigiwa, Jakrawadee Julamanee, Shiho Hirano, Hirofumi Yokota, Shihomi Kuwano, Ryo Hanajiri, Hitoshi Kiyoi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy targeting CD19 has transformed the treatment of hematologic malignancies. The costimulatory domain (CSD) of CAR constructs plays a crucial role in determining T cell metabolism, persistence, and antitumor function. We previously developed a novel CSD combining CD79A and CD40, which conferred superior proliferation and antitumor efficacy compared with CD28-based or 4-1BB-based CAR-T cells. The metabolic mechanisms underlying these effects remain to be elucidated.MethodsWe compared CD28, 4-1BB, and CD79A/CD40 CAR-T cells using transcriptomic analysis, metabolic flux assays, and metabolomics. Patient samples treated with 4-1BB-based or CD28-based CAR-T therapies were analyzed to assess correlations between serum lipids and CAR-T expansion.ResultsTranscriptomic profiling revealed that CD79A/CD40 CAR-T cells shared gene expression patterns with 4-1BB CAR-T cells, particularly in pathways related to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and T cell memory differentiation, but were distinct from CD28 CAR-T cells. Both 4-1BB and CD79A/CD40 CAR-T cells relied on OXPHOS and exhibited greater mitochondrial fitness, as evidenced by higher spare respiratory capacity and mitochondrial mass. Notably, CD79A/CD40 CAR-T cells displayed significantly enhanced glycolysis during the early phase following antigen stimulation, distinguishing them from 4-1BB CAR-T cells and supporting rapid initial expansion. Metabolomic profiling showed upregulation of cholesterol biosynthesis enzymes in both CD79A/CD40 and 4-1BB CAR-T cells, suggesting a shared reliance on cholesterol metabolism. Importantly, in patients treated with 4-1BB-based CAR-T therapy, higher serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels positively correlated with CAR-T expansion in the late phase, particularly within CD4+T cell subsets. This relationship was not observed in patients receiving CD28-based CAR-T therapy. These findings indicate that cholesterol availability may influence CAR-T persistence in vivo, and that the metabolic phenotype of CD79A/CD40 CAR-T cells is optimized for both early proliferation and long-term survival.ConclusionsCD79A/CD40 CAR-T cells exhibit unique metabolic adaptations, including early glycolytic activation, sustained OXPHOS, and upregulated cholesterol metabolism, which together may underpin their enhanced proliferation and persistence. Targeting cholesterol metabolism may represent a novel strategy to optimize CAR-T cell function and improve therapeutic outcomes.

The metabolic programming of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells, shaped by their costimulatory domains (CSDs), influences their persistence and therapeutic efficacy. CD28-based CAR-T cells primarily rely on glycolysis, whereas 4-1BB-based CAR-T cells favor oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). However, the metabolic mechanisms underlying enhanced function in alternative CSD designs such as CD79A/CD40 remain unexplored.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy targeting CD19 and B cell maturation antigen has demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy against various hematologic malignancies, including B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia, B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, and multiple myeloma.15 This therapy has revolutionized the field by harnessing and modifying the fundamental principles of T cell receptor signaling.6 Optimization of CAR signaling through structural modulation enables T cells to be rapidly activated and effectively target cancer cells, highlighting the importance of refining the co-stimulatory domain (CSD) to enhance CAR-T cell therapeutic efficacy.79 These advancements have driven the development of second-generation and third-generation CARs, incorporating additional costimulatory molecules such as CD28, 4-1BB, ICOS, OX40, and CD27.10 11 The inclusion of different CSDs significantly influences the differentiation status of CAR-T cell progeny following antigen stimulation (Ag-stim). For instance, integrating the ICOS intracellular domain promotes a Th17-like differentiation profile.12 13

To enhance CAR-T cell proliferation, we developed and reported a novel CSD incorporating a composite B-cell signaling module, CD79A/CD40 (CD19.CD79a.40z). This construct demonstrated enhanced NF-κB signaling and improved CAR-T cell proliferation.14 Moreover, CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells exhibited superior anti-tumor efficacy in murine models of both B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma compared with CD19.CAR-T cells incorporating either CD28 (CD19.CD28z) or 4-1BB (CD19.BBz).14 15 However, the precise mechanisms underlying the enhanced anti-tumor efficacy observed in these murine models, as well as the predominant role of the NF-κB signaling pathway, remain unclear.

Previous studies have demonstrated distinct metabolic pathways among T cell subtypes: effector T cells predominantly utilize glycolytic pathways, whereas memory T cells rely on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS).16 17 Regarding the metabolism of CD19 CAR-T cells, CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells have been reported to exhibit a glycolytic metabolic profile, while CD19.BBz CAR-T cells preferentially rely on OXPHOS. Furthermore, spare respiratory capacity (SRC), an indicator of mitochondrial function, was significantly higher in CD19.BBz cells compared to CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells.8 18 These findings suggest that CAR-T cell metabolic programming varies significantly depending on the CSD incorporated. A comparative metabolic analysis of CD19.CD79a.40z and conventional CD19 CAR-T cells could provide valuable insights for optimizing CSD selection. Therefore, we focused on T cell metabolism to investigate the mechanisms underlying the superior performance of CD79a.40z CSD compared to 4-1BB and CD28 CSDs. Our findings reveal that cholesterol biosynthesis plays a pivotal role in CD19 CAR-T cell differentiation during the late phase following Ag-stim.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Phoenix-Ampho, Nalm6 cell lines were maintained in our laboratory. The authenticity of all cell lines was routinely confirmed by analyzing their immunophenotypes using flow cytometry. Cells were cultured for up to 2 months prior to use. Adherent cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cell line suspensions were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute Medium (RPMI) 1640 containing 10% fetal bovine serum, L-glutamine (0.8 mM), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

CD19 CAR retroviral vector construction

CD19 CAR constructs, including CD28 (CD28z), 4-1BB (BBz), and CD79A/40 (CD79a.40z), were developed by fusing the CSD with an anti-CD19 single-chain variable fragment-short hinge (12 amino acids)-CD28 transmembrane domain, followed by the CD3ζ CSD.14 19 The CD19 used in all CAR constructs was derived from the FMC63 clone.20 To facilitate transduction and selection, the CD19 CAR constructs were fused with a truncated epidermal growth factor receptor (tEGFR) lacking epidermal growth factor binding and intracellular signaling domains, positioned downstream of the self-cleaving T2A sequence. Biotinylated Erbitux monoclonal antibody (Bristol-Myers Squibb) was used to detect tEGFR expression.8 21 The CAR constructs were subcloned into the LZRS-pBMN-Z vector, and their sequence was validated via direct sequencing. Gamma-retroviral supernatants were produced using the Phoenix-Ampho system (Orbigen). The sequences of the second-generation CAR-T cells used in this study were identical to those previously reported and clinically applied.22

Generation, expansion, and selection of CD19 CAR-T cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from healthy donors by centrifuging whole blood with Ficoll-Hypaque solution (GE Healthcare). CD8+ lymphocytes were then purified using immunomagnetic beads (Miltenyi Biotec) and stimulated with anti-CD3/CD28 beads (Invitrogen). On day 3, CD19 CAR-T cells were retrovirally transduced using recombinant human RetroNectin-coated plates (Takara Bio), preloaded with CD19 CAR-encoding retrovirus, and centrifuged at 2100 rpm for 2 hours at 32°C. Transduced T cells were expanded in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% human serum, L-glutamine (0.8 mM), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 2-mercaptoethanol (0.5 µM), and recombinant human IL-2 (50 IU/mL). On day 7, the CAR-positive T cells were enriched using biotin-conjugated anti-EGFR monoclonal antibody and streptavidin beads (Miltenyi Biotec). On day 10, purified CD19 CAR-T cells were cryopreserved and stored. For the in vitro experiment, stored CD19 CAR-T cells were thawed on day −1, and on days 0, 7, and 14, the enriched CAR-positive cells were activated with γ-irradiated Nalm6 at a 1:5 responder-to-stimulator ratio, with IL-2 supplementation (50 IU/mL) every 3 days.19 For proteomics analyses, stored CD19 CAR-T cells were thawed on day 1, and on days 0, 3, and 7, enriched CAR-positive cells were activated with γ-irradiated Nalm6 at a 1:5 responder-to-stimulator ratio, with IL-2 supplementation (50 IU/mL) every 3 days. For the cholesterol biosynthesis inhibition assay, CAR-T cells were co-cultured with γ-irradiated Nalm6 cells at a 1:5 responder-to-stimulator ratio in the presence of 10 µM lovastatin (Selleck).

Flow cytometry and immunophenotyping

All samples were examined using flow cytometry on a FACSAria II and LSR Fortessa (BD Biosciences), and the data were evaluated using FlowJo software (Tree Star). Human T cells were stained with combinations of the following mAb conjugated to fluorophores: CD8; CD62L; lymphocyte activation gene 3 (LAG-3) (BD Biosciences); CD45RA; programmed cell death 1 (PD-1); cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4); T cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 3 (TIM-3) (BioLegend); and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor (Thermo Fisher). tEGFR+cells were identified using biotinylated Erbitux and streptavidin-Phycoerythrin (BD Biosciences). For the assessment of cholesterol uptake, BODIPY-cholesterol (5 µg/mL, Avanti Research) was used.

Extracellular flux analysis

Glycolysis and mitochondrial function were assessed using an extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent). Individual wells of XF24 cell culture microplates were coated with CellTak (Corning) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The coating matrix was adsorbed overnight at 37°C, aspirated, air-dried, and stored at 4°C until use. Mitochondrial function was assessed on days 7 and 21. To assay CAR-T cell mitochondrial function, CAR-T cells were centrifuged at 1200×g for 5 min. Cell pellets were resuspended in XF assay medium containing 5.5 mM glucose, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1 mM sodium pyruvate and seeded at 1×106 cells per well. The microplate was then centrifuged at 200 g for 1 min and incubated under standard culture conditions for 60 min. During instrument calibration (30 min), the cells were switched to a CO2-free (37°C) incubator. The XF24 assay cartridge was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cellular oxygen consumption rates (OCR) were measured under basal conditions and after treatment with 1.5 µM oligomycin, 1.5 µM carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and 40 nM rotenone, followed by 1 µM antimycin A (XF Cell Mito Stress kit, Seahorse Bioscience). The SRC was calculated as the ratio between the maximum OCR and basal OCR. The results were analyzed using Seahorse Wave V.2.4.

Continuous measurement of glucose, lactate, and their metabolic rate

The glucose and lactate levels in the medium were measured using LiCellMo (PHC Corporation) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, electrochemical sensors, equipped with enzymes that react with glucose and lactate, detect their concentrations by measuring the current generated during these enzymatic reactions. The metabolic rate was calculated from the continuous concentration data, which were smoothed using the smoothing spline method, and quantified as glucose consumption and lactate production rates.

RNA-seq analysis

Each CD19 CAR-T cell was induced with γ-irradiated Nalm6 cells at a 1:5 E:T ratio, and the CAR positive fraction was sorted by FACSAria II on day 7 and 21 poststimulation. To isolate CAR-T cells, CD8+ and tEGFR+ populations were gated. Total RNA was isolated and purified from their cells using the QIAamp RNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen). RNA-Seq libraries were prepared employing the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) and the NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (New England Biolabs) following the manufacturer’s instructions and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeqX with 2×150 base paired-end reads. First, the raw read data quality was assessed for each sample using FastQC (V.0.11.9) for RNA-seq analysis. Transcript abundances were then directly quantified from the raw RNA-seq FASTQ files using the Kallisto V.0.44.0 pseudoalignment method. For rapid and accurate quantification, a transcriptome index constructed from the Ensembl project’s transcriptome v91 with a 100 bootstrap value based on the pseudoalignment was used. Gene-by-gene expression matrices were created using the R package tximport. Consequently, differential expression was analyzed using an integrated web application for differential expression and pathway analysis of RNA-seq data (iDEP).23 Functional analyses like enrichment and pathway analyses were conducted using Metascape (http://metascape.org/).24 25

Evaluation of mitochondrial function by flow cytometry

1 mM stocks of Mitotracker Red and Green (MR and MG) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were prepared using DMSO. 1 µL stock Mitotracker solutions were added to a 10 mL culture medium containing 1×106 cells, giving final concentration of 100 nM, and incubated at 37°C for 5 min and proceeded to flow cytometry analysis.

ATP production analysis

The ATP assay was conducted using an ATP Assay Kit (Dojindo). Briefly, CAR-T cells (1×104 per well) were cultured in 96-well culture plates and added 100 µL of ATP working solution and further incubated for 10 min. Ultimately, the ATP levels were quantified using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega).

Sample preparation for iMPAQT

This study employed the iMPAQT assay to perform simultaneous global analysis for the absolute quantification of protein expression. The analysis was performed as previously described.26 In brief, frozen tissue was crushed with a bead shocker and lysed in 100 µL lysis buffer per 10 mg of tissue powder. The samples were then diluted with equal amounts of water. Protein concentrations in the samples were determined using a Bicinchoninic Acid assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories). To block the cysteine/cysteine residues, portions of the samples (200 µg of protein) were treated with 5.0 mM Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 37°C, followed by alkylation with 10 mM 2-iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 20°C. The samples were then subjected to acetone precipitation, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in 100 µL digestion buffer (0.5 M triethylammonium bicarbonate). Each sample was digested with lysyl-endopeptidase (2 µg, Wako) for 3 hour at 37°C, followed by further digestion with trypsin (4 µg, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 14 hours at 37°C. The resulting digests were freeze-dried and labeled with the mTRAQ Δ0 (light) reagent. Each sample was spiked with synthetic peptides (Funakoshi and GenScript) for the internal standard, which underwent reductive alkylation and mTRAQ Δ4 (heavy) labeling. The samples were subjected to reversed-phase liquid chromatography, followed by multiple reaction monitoring analysis. Pretreatment and mass spectrometry experiments were performed by an external service provider (Kyushu Pro Search).

Metabolomics data analyses

All metabolomics analyses were performed via MetaboAnalyst V.4.0,27 28 whereby raw peak intensities were filtered to remove low-detection features (>50% missing values across samples), imputed to replace missing values (half of minimum value across samples), then filtered to remove near-constant values (5% of features with lowest IQR). Next, the data were sample normalized to the pooled control group, log2-transformed, and scaled to z-score. Differential metabolite levels were then calculated by unpaired two-tailed t test, assuming equal variance between groups. Metabolomics Pathway Analyses (MetPA) were performed with default settings: global enrichment test with pathway topologies calculated as relative-betweenness centrality, and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Homo sapiens pathway library (October 2019). Integrated transcriptome-metabolome MetPA was performed with log2-fold-changes (LFC) of significantly changed metabolites (q<0.10) and the beta-value of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (q<0.01, abs(b) > 1) as input, using hypergeometric tests for enrichment with KEGG Homo sapiens metabolic pathways (October 2019), pathway topologies calculated as degree centrality, and unweighted integration of gene-metabolite p values. Significantly enriched pathways were those with a false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p<0.05, and a pathway impact score >0.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are presented as the mean±SEM or SD. Student’s t-test and one-way or two-way analysis of variance with Bonferroni’s or Tukey’s post-test correction were used to determine statistical differences between results. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. GraphPad Prism V.9.3.1 software (GraphPad Software) was used for the statistical analysis.

Results

Superior expansion capacity of CD19 CAR-T Cells with CD79a.40z CSD

We generated CD19 CAR-T cells with three distinct CSDs: CD28 (CD19.CD28z), 4-1BB (CD19.BBz), and CD79A/40 (CD19.CD79a.40z) (figure 1A). Primary human CD8+ T cells were transduced with CAR constructs and purified to achieve >95% purity (online supplemental figure S1A,B). To clarify the metabolic differences among each CD19 CAR-T cell on repetitive antigen exposure, we performed a multi-challenge assay in which CAR-T cells were co-cultured with tumor cells in multiple sequential rounds (figure 1B). CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells exhibited significantly greater expansion than CD19.CD28z and CD19.BBz CAR-T cells on days 14 and 21 (figure 1C; online supplemental figure S1B). Subsequent sorting based on CD45RA and CD62L markers defined central memory T (TCM, CD45RA⁻CD62L+) and effector memory T (TEM, CD45RA⁻CD62L⁻) subsets (online supplemental figure S1C).29 CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells demonstrated superior expansion compared with CD19.BBz CAR-T cells in TCM fractions on day 28 (figure 1D) and in TEM fractions on day 21 (figure 1E). In both bulk and sorted cultures, CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells showed superior proliferation post-Ag-stim, consistent with previous findings.14

Transcriptomic analyses reveal similarities between CD79a.40z and BBz

To explore the transcriptomic differences among CD19 CAR-T cells, RNA was extracted on days 7 and 21 following the first and third Ag-stim with NALM6 cells (figure 1C). DEGs were assessed in CD19.CD28z, CD19.BBz, and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells. Notably, the Venn diagrams indicate that the number of DEGs between CD19.CD79a.40z and CD19.BBz CAR-T cells was very limited, suggesting that their overall transcriptional profiles were highly similar. Only CD79A and CD40 were uniquely upregulated in CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells compared to CD19.BBz CAR-T cells on days 7 and 21 (figure 2A,B). In contrast, numerous DEGs were observed when comparing CD19.BBz or CD19.CD79a.40z with CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells (day 7: 66–82 genes; day 21: 138–156 genes). A complete list of DEGs is provided in online supplemental table S2. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed two distinct transcriptomic groups, CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells and CD19.BBz/CD79a.40z CAR-T cells on both time points (figure 2C,D), although donor variability may have contributed to this. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on day 7 highlighted significant upregulation of glycolysis and OXPHOS pathways in CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells compared with CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells (figure 2E,F). Gene ontology (GO) analysis on day 21 identified the enrichment of NF-κB signaling pathways in both CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells (figure 2G,H; online supplemental figure S2A,B). Additionally, GO-biological process analysis showed upregulation of T cell activation pathways and downregulation of apoptotic pathways in CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells (online supplemental figure S2C,D). These findings indicate that CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells share metabolic and NF-κB pathway similarities, distinguishing them from CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells. Collectively, CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells exhibit highly similar metabolic profiles while sharing a distinct transcriptomic signature compared with CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells.

CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells exhibit reduced T cell exhaustion after repeated Ag-stim

We evaluated exhaustion marker dynamics in CD19 CAR-T cells following repeated Ag-stim. CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells demonstrated significantly lower expression of exhaustion markers, including PD-1 (figure 3A,B) and TIM-3 (figure 3C,D) compared to CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells on days 14 and 21. No significant differences were observed in the expression of CTLA-4 (online supplemental figure S3A) or LAG-3 (online supplemental figure S3B). To further assess T cell exhaustion, we analyzed terminally exhausted T cells (TEX; PD-1+TIM-3+) and precursor-exhausted T cells (TPEX; PD-1+TIM-3⁻) (figure 3E).30 31 On day 14, CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells exhibited a significantly lower proportion of TEX compared with CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells (figure 3F), whereas TPEX proportions remained comparable across all groups (figure 3G). Overall, following repeated Ag-stim, both CD19.CD79a.40z and CD19.BBz CAR-T cells exhibited reduced expression of exhaustion markers (PD-1 and TIM-3) compared with CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells. Notably, CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells displayed a lower proportion of TEX, which represents irreversibly exhausted T cells, suggesting that this CSD structure confers less exhausted phenotype and preserves functional capacity.32 33

Early glycolysis and sustained OXPHOS characterize CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T Cells

To investigate cellular metabolism, we measured the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and OCR in a coculture assay.34 CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells exhibited significantly higher ECAR and glycolysis levels compared with CD19.CD28z and CD19.BBz CAR-T cells on day 7 (figure 4A,B). On day 21, both CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells displayed higher ECAR and glycolysis levels than CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells (figure 4C,D). OCR measurements were conducted on days 7 and 21 after Ag-stim to assess mitochondrial respiration. On day 7, basal and maximal OCR levels were the highest in CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells (figure 4E,G,H). However, by day 21, both CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells exhibited significantly higher basal and maximal OCR compared with CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells (figure 4F,H). Additionally, mitochondrial SRC, a measure of mitochondrial fitness,35 36 was significantly higher in CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells on day 21 (figure 4I). These findings indicate that CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells initially favor a glycolytic-dominant metabolic pathway, supporting effector T cell differentiation. Furthermore, both CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells transitioned to an OXPHOS-dominant pathway with enhanced mitochondrial fitness, as evidenced by elevated SRC on day 21. This suggests that CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells may offer metabolic advantages through both increased flexibility and greater mitochondrial resilience.

CD19.BBz and CD79a.40z CAR-T cells maintain high mitochondrial functionality

Given the strong correlation between SRC and mitochondrial function, we assessed the mitochondrial membrane potential and mass in CD19 CAR-T cells using MR and MG.3739 On day 14, the MR median fluorescence intensity (MFI) ratio of CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells was significantly higher than that of CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells (figure 5A,B). Additionally, the MG MFI ratios of CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells were significantly higher than those of CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells on days 7 and 21 (figure 5C,D). To further evaluate mitochondrial function in relation to T cell differentiation, CD19 CAR-T cells were sorted into TCM and TEM subsets on day 7. After additional Ag-stim, functional mitochondria (MR high and MG high population) were assessed.40 Both CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells retained significantly higher levels of functional mitochondria compared to CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells in TCM (figure 5E,F) and TEM subsets (figure 5G,H). We further analyzed mitochondrial biogenesis-associated gene expression using bulk RNA-seq data. Key nuclear-encoded transcription factors involved in mitochondrial biogenesis and function as well as their influence on the expression of mitochondrial-encoded genes were evaluated (online supplemental figure S4A). The expression levels of mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase 1 (online supplemental figure S4B) and mitochondrial transcription factor A (online supplemental figure S4C) were not significantly different among CAR-T cell types. However, CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells exhibited a significantly higher expression of nuclear respiratory factor 1 (online supplemental figure S4D) and nuclear factor erythroid-derived 2 (online supplemental figure S4E) on day 21 compared to CD19.BBz CAR-T cells. Despite these differences in mitochondrial dynamics, ATP production levels remained comparable across CD19 CAR-T cells (online supplemental figure S4F), suggesting that alternative metabolic pathways may contribute to supporting CAR-T cell function.

Proteomics reveals upregulation of the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway in CD19.CD79a.40z and CD19.BBz CAR-T Cells

To investigate metabolic differences among CD19 CAR-T cells, we conducted a proteomic analysis using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Prior to this, the glucose and lactate concentrations in the culture medium were monitored using a live-cell metabolic analyzer (LiCellmo) to determine the timing of metabolic differences. CD19 CAR-T cells were stimulated with NALM6 cells every 3–4 days, and live-cell metabolic analysis was performed 48–120 hours after the third Ag-stim (figure 6A). Metabolic differences in glucose and lactate levels were most pronounced between 80 and 100 hours post-Ag-stim (figure 6B), leading to sample collection at 96 hours for proteomic analysis. Consistent with the RNA-seq findings, PCA of the metabolic enzyme profiles identified two distinct proteomic groups: CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells and CD19.BBz/CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells (figure 6C). KEGG pathway analysis identified upregulated pathways, particularly steroid biosynthesis and fatty acid metabolism, in CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells compared to those in CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells (figure 6D; Online supplemental figure S5A,B). Steroid biosynthesis was the most significantly enriched pathway identified in this study. Volcano plots further highlighted the upregulated metabolic enzymes in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, which were shared by both CD19.BBz (online supplemental figure S5C) and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells (online supplemental figure S5D). Compared with CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells, the KEGG pathway map illustrating significantly upregulated metabolic enzymes in CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells is shown in online supplemental figure S5E. Specifically, enzymes involved in steroid biosynthesis—squalene epoxidase (SQLE; EC 1.14.14.17), lanosterol synthase (LSS; EC 5.4.99.7), NAD(P) dependent steroid dehydrogenase-like protein (NSDHL; EC 1.1.1.170), 24-dehydrocholesterol reductase (DHCR24; EC 1.3.1.72), and DHCR7 (EC 1.3.1.21)—were upregulated in both CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells. Metabolic enzymes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis, including hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA synthase 1, farnesyl-diphosphate farnesyltransferase 1, SQLE, LSS, NSDHL, DHCR24, and DHCR7, were significantly upregulated in CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells compared to CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells, as shown in the heatmap and quantitative analysis (figure 6E,F). In summary, proteomic analysis revealed that the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway was significantly upregulated in CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells compared to that in CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells, highlighting distinct metabolic adaptations after Ag-stim.

Serum LDL cholesterol levels correlate with CAR-T cell proliferation in the late phase of CAR-T therapy

To evaluate the potential correlation between serum cholesterol levels and CAR-T cell proliferation in vivo, we analyzed peripheral blood samples from patients who had undergone CAR-T cell therapy. A total of 38 recipients of 4-1BB-based CAR-T cells (tisagenlecleucel and lisocabtagene maraleucel) and 25 recipients of CD28-based CAR-T cells (axicabtagene ciloleucel) were included in this analysis (online supplemental table S1). No significant correlation was observed between statin use and CAR-T cell expansion. In patients treated with 4-1BB-based CAR-T cell therapy, serum LDL cholesterol levels showed a significant correlation with CAR-T cell counts, particularly within the CD3+ and CD4+ cell fractions, during the later phases of treatment (figure 7A,B,D,E). This correlation was stronger for LDL cholesterol than for total cholesterol and was more pronounced in CD4+ than in CD8+ T cells. Additionally, in CD8+ CAR-T cells, a modest correlation with LDL cholesterol levels was observed during the early phase after stimulation (figure 7C,F). In contrast, no significant correlation was observed in patients treated with CD28-based CAR-T therapy. These findings suggest that in patients who received 4-1BB-based CAR-T cell therapy, serum LDL cholesterol levels exhibited a weak but notable correlation with CAR-T cell expansion, particularly in the CD4+ subset.

CAR-T cell dependence on both endogenous and exogenous cholesterol

To functionally validate the role of cholesterol biosynthesis in CAR-T cell expansion, we treated CAR-T cells with lovastatin, an HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor. Lovastatin reduced expansion of CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells in a dose-dependent manner, while CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells showed minimal sensitivity (figure 7G). Next, to assess whether CAR-T cells use extracellular cholesterol, we performed a BODIPY-cholesterol uptake assay. Although statistical significance was not observed, CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells exhibited a trend toward increased cholesterol uptake compared to CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells (figure 7H). Consistently, flow cytometric analysis revealed a similar trend of increased LDL receptor expression on the cell surface of CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells relative to CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells, though the differences did not reach statistical significance (figure 7I). Together, these findings demonstrate that CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells rely at least in part on endogenous cholesterol biosynthesis for their proliferation and may possess enhanced capacity for exogenous cholesterol uptake compared to CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically compared the metabolic characteristics of CD19 CAR-T cells incorporating CD28, 4-1BB, and a novel CD79A/CD40 CSD. To comprehensively investigate metabolic differences, we employed a multifaceted approach, including RNA-seq, metabolic flux analysis, mitochondrial function assays, continuous glucose consumption and lactate production rate measurements, and metabolomic profiling. Our findings demonstrated that CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells exhibit highly similar metabolic signatures, distinct from those observed in CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells.8 15 Notably, CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells display unique metabolic adaptations that may contribute to their superior persistence and functionality.15

Using flux analysis, we observed that CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells initially adopted a glycolysis-dominant metabolic state, followed by a transition to an OXPHOS-dominant state at later time points. This early-phase glycolytic upregulation was more pronounced than that observed in CD19.CD28z and CD19.BBz CAR-T cells. However, after repeated Ag-stim, both CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells exhibited similar metabolic properties, characterized by a shift toward OXPHOS dependence. These findings suggest that CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells not only exhibit enhanced glycolysis and effector function in the early phase but may also maintain a long-term survival advantage by relying on OXPHOS during the memory phase.8 10 Early reliance on glycolysis can enhance CAR-T expansion and effector function; however, sustained glycolytic dependence has been associated with metabolic stress and T-cell exhaustion. In line with this paradigm, CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells in our study demonstrated metabolic flexibility, transitioning from glycolysis toward OXPHOS over time. Such adaptive reprogramming may help preserve mitochondrial fitness, thereby supporting both persistence and long-term functional durability. Although we hypothesized that CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells might exhibit higher ATP production due to their metabolic profile, no significant differences in ATP levels were observed among CAR-T constructs. This suggests that effector function may not be directly linked to ATP abundance but rather to metabolic reprogramming and flexibility.41 42 The ability of CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells to maintain mitochondrial fitness and transition to OXPHOS dominance may confer advantages in persistence and memory formation, even in the absence of increased ATP production. This apparent discrepancy may reflect mitochondrial inefficiency or uncoupling, or alternatively, a preferential shift toward maintaining mitochondrial reserve capacity rather than maximizing immediate ATP output. Such a reserve may enhance metabolic adaptability under stress and support long-term persistence and memory formation, even without elevated baseline ATP levels.

CD28-based CAR-T cells are generally characterized by glycolysis-dominant metabolism and limited mitochondrial persistence. However, recent studies indicate that CD28 signaling transiently enhances mitochondrial activity during early activation. CD28 engagement promotes PGC-1α–dependent mitochondrial biogenesis, cristae remodeling, and OXPHOS, which together support rapid effector expansion before the metabolic shift toward sustained glycolysis.43 44 Consistent with these findings, our Seahorse analysis showed elevated OCR in CD28 CAR-T cells at day 7 post-stimulation, suggesting that this time point captured an early activation phase characterized by transient mitochondrial engagement preceding glycolytic predominance.

After the third Ag-stim, both CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells demonstrated an OXPHOS-dominant metabolic profile, accompanied by significantly higher mitochondrial SRC, a key marker of mitochondrial resilience under metabolic stress.8 36 Mitochondrial function assessments confirmed that CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells maintained functional mitochondria across both the total CAR-T cell population and various T cell differentiation subsets. These data suggest that CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells possess an enhanced capacity for long-term mitochondrial fitness, a characteristic that has been previously associated with improved in vivo persistence and antitumor efficacy in CAR-T cell therapies.4547 Furthermore, increased mitochondrial mass in infused CD8+ CD19 CAR-T cells has been correlated with enhanced expansion and persistence in patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia.48 This further supports the clinical relevance of mitochondrial fitness in CAR-T cell therapy. Given these findings, strategies aimed at enhancing mitochondrial function may serve as promising avenues for improving the efficacy of CAR T-cell therapies.

To further elucidate these metabolic differences, we conducted metabolomic profiling using LC-MS/MS,26 which revealed a striking upregulation of the steroid biosynthetic pathway in CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells. Among the upregulated pathways, the enzymes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis were significantly enriched, suggesting that this metabolic adaptation may be critical for CAR-T cell function. Previous studies have demonstrated that cholesterol biosynthesis is primarily regulated by transcription factors such as sterol regulatory element-binding protein-2 and liver X receptor (LXR).49 50 Notably, LXR plays a crucial role in T cell cholesterol metabolism within the tumor microenvironment. Genetic ablation of LXR in CAR-T cells increases membrane cholesterol levels and enhances antitumor efficacy in murine solid tumor models.50 These findings suggest that cholesterol biosynthesis is a key metabolic determinant of CAR-T cell persistence and function. Moreover, previous reports have indicated that statin use in the context of allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is associated with graft-versus-host disease incidence, highlighting the broader immunological implications of cholesterol metabolism.51 52 Additionally, metabolomic analyses have suggested that cholesterol-related pathways may play a role in predicting immune-related toxicities, such as cytokine release syndrome and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome.53 Lipid metabolism has also been shown to play a critical role in the function of other immune effector cells, such as NK cells. Altered lipid metabolism in the tumor microenvironment has been reported to impair NK cell anti-tumor responses in multiple myeloma.54 Taken together, these findings indicate that cholesterol biosynthesis represents a potential metabolic target for optimizing CD19 CAR-T cell function.

A positive correlation between serum LDL cholesterol levels and CAR-T cell expansion was observed in CD4+ cells but not in CD8+ CAR-T cells. One possible explanation is that 4-1BB CAR-T cells favor CD4+ persistence,55 56 which relies more on OXPHOS and may be better able to utilize cholesterol metabolism. In contrast, CD8+ CAR-T cells, which are more glycolysis-dependent, may be less affected by cholesterol availability. Notably, the effect of cholesterol on CD8+ T cell antitumor function remains controversial, with reports suggesting both positive and negative effects. Some studies indicate that cholesterol enhances T cell expansion and persistence,50 while others suggest that it promotes exhaustion and dysfunction.57 Thus, the impact of cholesterol on CAR-T cell immunity appears to be highly context-dependent. Biosynthetic cholesterol upregulation may provide early metabolic support that enhances CAR-T activation and persistence, whereas prolonged accumulation under chronic stimulation has been linked to exhaustion. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting cholesterol metabolism will require precise timing and regulation to optimize CAR-T efficacy while minimizing dysfunctional outcomes. Further investigation is needed to determine how cholesterol metabolism differentially affects CD4+ and CD8+ CAR-T cells and its implications for optimizing CAR-T therapy. These observations further suggest that modulation of cholesterol metabolism—particularly LDL-cholesterol pathways—may influence CAR-T cell persistence and exhaustion dynamics. Thus, therapeutic strategies aimed at controlling cholesterol availability could represent a novel approach to enhance CAR-T cell efficacy. However, this concept remains hypothetical and will require validation through dedicated translational and clinical studies.

This study had several limitations. First, although we identified metabolic differences, direct functional validation of mitochondrial and cholesterol metabolism was not performed. Second, single-cell metabolic profiling was not conducted, limiting insights into cellular heterogeneity. Bulk transcriptomic and proteomic analyses may obscure heterogeneity within CAR-T populations, including the presence of exhausted or metabolically impaired subsets. Future studies using single-cell profiling will better resolve metabolic differences among subpopulations. Third, we did not evaluate whether the metabolic differences observed among CD28-, 4-1BB-, and CD79A/CD40-based CARs are generalizable to CARs targeting other antigens. Finally, the correlation between LDL cholesterol and CAR-T cell expansion requires validation in a larger prospective cohort. While the present study provides mechanistic evidence linking CAR construct-specific signaling to cholesterol biosynthesis and CAR-T cell persistence, future studies incorporating direct lipidomics profiling and in vivo modulation of cholesterol metabolism will be important to further delineate the causal role of cholesterol metabolic pathways in CAR-T cell function. Despite these limitations, our study provides key insights into CAR-T cell metabolism and highlights potential strategies for optimizing therapy.

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive metabolic profile of CD19 CAR-T cells incorporating different CSDs. Our findings highlight that CD19.BBz and CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cells share similar metabolic features, whereas CD19.CD28z CAR-T cells exhibit markedly distinct metabolic characteristics. The CD19.CD79a.40z CAR-T cell construct is expected to exert potent antitumor activity, not only through its early glycolysis-driven effector function but also through its long-term OXPHOS-dominant memory phenotype. Cholesterol biosynthesis is a key metabolic pathway that regulates CAR-T cell differentiation and persistence. Future investigations targeting cholesterol metabolism may provide novel strategies to enhance the efficacy and durability of CAR-T cells.
