Physical exercise moderates the mediating effect of sleep quality between internet addiction and college students’ academic burnout
Authors: Qingkun Feng, Yao Tong, Guihua Huang, Yanying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Internet addiction is a significant contributing factor to academic burnout among college students, though the underlying psychological and behavioral mechanisms warrant further investigation. This study investigated the mediating role of sleep quality between internet addiction and college students’ academic burnout, as well as the moderating role of physical exercise on this mediation pathway. A study was conducted to assess internet addiction, sleep quality, academic burnout, and physical exercise among college students. Descriptive statistics and correlation analyses were performed on these variables, and a moderated mediation model was developed. Internet addiction was positively correlated with sleep quality and academic burnout, and sleep quality was positively associated with academic burnout. Additionally, physical exercise demonstrated negative associations with internet addiction, sleep quality, and academic burnout. Furthermore, physical exercise moderated the relationship between internet addiction and sleep quality. This study provides further insights into the psychological mechanisms underlying the relationship between internet addiction and academic burnout among college students. Sleep quality serves as a mediating factor, while physical exercise act as a moderating factor in the relationship between internet addiction and sleep quality. This study highlights the role of sleep quality and physical exercise in the relationship between internet addiction and academic burnout, offering novel perspectives and strategies for addressing these challenges among college students.

Introduction

The rapid development of internet technology in recent years has made internet addiction a significant social problem, negatively impacting college students’ psychological well-being and learning efficacy1. Research shows that when college students overuse digital media (e.g., social networks, online games, short videos), they often experience cognitive deficits, emotional instability, and poor impulse control, resulting in academic burnout2. Academic burnout refers to students’ negative attitudes and behaviors resulting from prolonged exposure to academic stress or loss of learning motivation. It manifests as emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced personal accomplishment3, which significantly impair students’ learning motivation and academic performance4. Currently, academic burnout has become a prevalent phenomenon in universities, with more than half of students experiencing academic burnout5. Academic burnout not only significantly diminishes individuals’ self-esteem and academic self-efficacy but also reduces their learning engagement, ultimately impairing subjective well-being and overall quality of life6. It is noteworthy that the prevalence of internet addiction among Chinese college students has shown a consistent upward trend7, with the consequent issue of academic burnout becoming increasingly prominent. Against this background, an in-depth exploration of the intrinsic relationship and underlying mechanisms between internet addiction and academic burnout among college students is not only conducive to improving the mental health status of college students but also has important practical significance for enhancing the quality of talent cultivation for college students.

Internet addiction and academic burnout

Internet addiction refers to an intense sense of dependence on the internet in the absence of addictive substances, accompanied by a loss of control over usage impulses8. It primarily encompasses compulsive online activities such as social networking, gambling, gaming, cybersex, and shopping9. Internet addiction demonstrates significant associations with academic impairments, including increased academic stress10 and declining grade point averages11. Furthermore, internet addiction heightens the risk of psychological and social dysfunction, manifesting as depression, anxiety, and impairments in academic performance, occupational functioning, and interpersonal relationships12. Conservation of resources theory provides a robust framework for understanding these relationships. The theory posits that individuals strive to protect their valued resources (e.g., time, energy, self-esteem), and stress occurs when resources are lost or threatened13. Critically, conservation of resources theory introduces the concept of resource loss spirals, where initial loss leads to further losses, creating a vicious cycle. In the context of internet addiction, college students may initially use the internet as a substitute coping strategy to escape academic pressure or compensate for depleted psychological resources14. However, excessive and compulsive internet use consumes time, attention, and cognitive energy without replenishing lost resources. Instead, it exacerbates resource depletion by displacing sleep, reducing study time, and increasing guilt or anxiety. Thus, internet addiction traps students in a resource loss spiral: academic stress → escapist internet use → further resource loss → heightened stress and burnout. Therefore, we propose Hypothesis 1: Internet addiction positively predicts academic burnout among college students.

The mediating effect of sleep quality

Sleep quality is a core indicator for assessing an individual’s sleep status, encompassing multiple dimensions such as difficulty falling asleep, sleep maintenance disturbances, frequency of nighttime awakenings, and morning refreshments15. Research indicates a significant relationship between sleep quality and internet addiction16. Sleep problems and poor sleep quality were positively related to high internet use among college students17. One systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed a significant negative correlation between internet addiction and sleep quality among college students18. Furthermore, a negative association was found between the degree of internet addiction and sleep quality among college students19. These findings suggest that internet addiction may lead to sleep problems, while poor sleep quality may also result in excessive internet use. Sleep quality also demonstrates a robust association with academic burnout. High-quality sleep quality exerts positive effects on academic performance20, whereas excessive smartphone dependence adversely impacts learning outcomes and exacerbates academic burnout severity21. College students with better sleep quality generally exhibit superior academic adaptation and performance22. From the perspective of conservation of resources theory, sleep quality functions as a key resource reservoir. Sleep is a fundamental recovery resource that replenishes energy, restores self-regulatory capacity, and consolidates memory23. According to the theory, resource loss is more impactful than resource gain, and individuals must invest resources to prevent further loss. Internet addiction depletes sleep quality through multiple mechanisms: late-night screen time displaces sleep hours, blue light disrupts circadian rhythms, and cognitive arousal delays sleep onset. Poor sleep quality, in turn, represents a critical resource deficit that reduces students’ ability to cope with academic demands, impairs emotion regulation, and accelerates depletion of remaining cognitive resources. This creates a mediated resource loss pathway: internet addiction → deterioration of sleep quality → increased vulnerability to academic burnout. Sleep quality thus acts as the “resource bridge” through which internet addiction propagates its negative effects. Consequently, deteriorated sleep quality may serve as a potential risk factor for academic burnout among college students. Studies demonstrate that smartphone addiction significantly impairs students’ self-regulatory capacity and time-management efficiency, consequently triggering academic procrastination and predisposing them to burnout syndrome24. Based on the aforementioned research, this study proposes Hypothesis 2: Sleep quality mediates the relationship between internet addiction and academic burnout among college students.

The moderating effect of physical exercise

The effect of internet addiction on academic burnout may be moderated by behavioral interventions such as physical exercise. Physical exercise is any bodily movement that is planned, structured, repetitive, and purposefully focused on the improvement or maintenance of one or more components of physical fitness25. Regular physical exercise can help regulate circadian rhythms and promote normal melatonin secretion26, thereby counteracting the disruption of biological rhythms caused by nighttime blue light exposure in individuals with internet addiction27. Meanwhile, physical exercise has been demonstrated to effectively alleviate cognitive load and psychological stress induced by Internet use28, reduce bedtime procrastination, and improve sleep quality29. Furthermore, physical exercise can enhance self-regulatory capacity, enabling individuals with internet addiction to better manage their online time and thereby reducing the negative impacts of excessive Internet use on both academic performance and sleep quality30,31. Conservation of resources theory provides a powerful explanation for why physical exercise acts as a moderator. According to the theory, individuals can invest resources to prevent future loss or initiate gain spirals. Physical exercise serves as a resource investment strategy that buffers the resource loss spiral triggered by internet addiction. Specifically, exercise promotes resource replenishment by improving sleep quality through regulating circadian rhythms and reducing stress, directly counteracting the sleep-depleting effect of internet addiction. It also facilitates resource generation by enhancing self-regulatory capacity and psychological resilience—“resource caravans” that help students control internet use and protect sleep time. By protecting sleep quality from the detrimental effects of internet addiction, exercise weakens the mediating pathway. In conservation of resources terms, exercise acts as a resource gain strategy that disrupts the loss spiral: students who exercise can maintain or even improve sleep quality, reducing the translation of internet addiction into academic burnout. Thus, physical exercise moderates the relationship between internet addiction and sleep quality by providing healthy resource replenishment that competes with maladaptive internet use, thereby disrupting the indirect pathway to academic burnout. Based on this, the present study proposes Hypothesis 3: Physical exercise moderates the relationship between internet addiction and sleep quality among college students.

Under the guidance of the conservation of resources theory, this study attempts to construct a moderated mediation model with internet addiction as the independent variable, academic burnout as the dependent variable, sleep quality as the mediating variable, and physical exercise as the moderating variable. The research aims to further elucidate the underlying psychological and behavioral mechanisms between internet addiction and academic burnout among college students.

The proposed model is illustrated in Fig. 1. In this proposed model, the three hypotheses form a clear hierarchical structure. Specifically, H1 establishes the direct predictive relationship between internet addiction and academic burnout, serving as the total effect in the model. H2 proposes a mediation mechanism, in which sleep quality acts as an intervening variable that transmits the effect of internet addiction to academic burnout. H3 further extends this mediation model by proposing a moderated mediation effect, wherein physical exercise moderates the relationship between internet addiction and sleep quality, thereby influencing the strength of the indirect pathway. Collectively, these hypotheses move from a direct effect (H1) to a mediation model (H2) and finally to a moderated mediation model (H3), offering a progressively deeper understanding of how and under what conditions internet addiction leads to academic burnout among college students.Fig. 1Moderated mediation model.

Participants and methods

Participants

The survey was conducted among college students from six universities in Guangdong Province using a stratified cluster random sampling method from October to December 2025. Participants were first stratified by academic discipline (Engineering and Technology, Natural Sciences, Humanities and Literature, Social Sciences and Management, and Arts), and cluster sampling was then conducted by academic year (Year 1 through Year 4) within each disciplinary stratum. A total of 1,060 questionnaires were collected in this survey. After excluding 53 invalid responses (including incomplete, contradictory, or patterned answers), 1,007 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 95.0%. To ensure the sample size was adequate to detect the hypothesized effect, an a priori power analysis was conducted. Using G*Power software, the analysis was configured with an effect size of f2 = 0.15, α = 0.05, and power (1 − β) = 0.80. The choice of a medium effect size follows Cohen’s guidelines (f2 ≥ 0.15) for multiple regression and structural equation modeling32, a standard consistently adopted in behavioral and educational research33,34. Statistical power analysis was performed using GPower 3.1. Available from: https://www.psychologie.hhu.de/arbeitsgruppen/allgemeine-psychologie-und-arbeitspsychologie/gpower? Prior studies on internet addiction, sleep quality, and academic burnout have also anticipated medium effect sizes. In addition, following the rule of thumb that sample size should be 5 to 10 times the number of items, the 62 items in this study required a minimum of 620 participants. The actual sample size of 1,007 meets and exceeds this criterion. All participants engaged in this study voluntarily after signing informed consent forms. A comprehensive pre-survey briefing covered the research objectives, data protection measures, and specific applications of the collected data. The study protocol was approved by the Academic Ethics Committee of Zhaoqing University in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Measurement tools

Internet addiction scale

The Revised Chinese Internet Addiction Scale (CIAS-R), revised by Bai and Fan35 based on Young’s Internet Addiction Test (IAT)8, was utilized to assess Internet addiction among college students. The scale consists of 19 items across four dimensions: compulsive internet use and withdrawal symptoms, internet addiction tolerance, interpersonal and health problems, and time management issues. A 4-point Likert scale was used, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 19 to 76, with higher scores reflecting more severe Internet addiction tendencies. This measurement tool has been widely verified and applied in a population of Chinese college students31. In this study, the scale’s Cronbach’s α was 0.920, and the coefficients for its four dimensions were 0.870, 0.926, 0.936, and 0.920, respectively. The confirmatory factor analysis revealed acceptable model fit: χ2/df = 4.361; GFI = 0.941; AGFI = 0.918; IFI = 0.977; TLI = 0.971; CFI = 0.977; RMSEA = 0.057.

Academic burnout scale

The Chinese version of the Learning Burnout Scale (LBS-C), originally developed by Lian et al.36 based on the Maslach Burnout Inventory-Student Survey37, was employed to assess college students’ academic burnout. The scale consists of 20 items categorized into three dimensions: emotional exhaustion, improper behavior, and low accomplishment. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher total scores indicate more severe academic burnout. This measurement tool has been widely verified and applied in a population of Chinese college students38. In this study, the full scale achieved a Cronbach’s α of 0.942, and its three subscales obtained coefficients of 0.947, 0.918, and 0.949. The confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated a good model fit: χ2/df = 3.588; GFI = 0.945; AGFI = 0.931; IFI = 0.976; TLI = 0.972; CFI = 0.976; RMSEA = 0.050.

Pittsburgh sleep quality Index

The Pittsburgh sleep quality Index (PSQI) was developed by Buysse et al.39 and revised by Liu et al.40 to assess the sleep quality of college students. It consists of 18 items measuring seven distinct components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep efficiency, sleep duration, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medications, and daytime dysfunction. The scale consists of 4 open-ended questions and 14 multiple-choice items, with the multiple-choice questions scored from 1 to 3. The total score ranges from 0 to 21. The higher the score, the poorer the sleep quality. The Cronbach’s α coefficient of this scale was 0.776.

Physical activity rating scale

Physical exercise was assessed using the physical activity rating scale (PARS-3) developed by Hashimoto41 and compiled by Liang42. The scale consisted of three items: intensity, time, and frequency. Amount of physical exercise = intensity × time × frequency. Each item is rated on a five-point scale, with intensity and frequency scored from 1 to 5, and time scored from 0 to 4. The higher score represents a higher level of physical exercise. This measurement tool has been widely verified and applied in a population of Chinese college students38. In this study, Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.809.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and the PROCESS macro (version 4.1) for mediation and moderation analysis. Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias. Descriptive statistical analyses were carried out for variables. Pearson correlation analysis was then employed to assess the correlations among variables. Hayes’ PROCESS macro (version 4.1, Model 4) was utilized to test the mediating effect of sleep quality between Internet addiction and academic burnout. Further, Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Version 4.1, Model 7) was employed to examine the moderating effect of physical exercise on the mediating role of sleep quality in the relationship between Internet addiction and academic burnout. Additionally, the study employed a Bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples to obtain the 95% confidence interval (95% CI), with a non-inclusion of zero in the interval indicating significance. To ensure the robustness and replicability of the bootstrap results, we performed 5,000 bootstrap resamples with a random seed fixed at 12,345. For associations involving categorical variables, the analytical approach was tailored to the variable type: point-biserial correlations were used for binary variables (e.g., gender), and ANOVA was conducted for unordered categorical variables (e.g., family socioeconomic status). Pearson correlation analysis was reported solely for continuous-continuous pairs.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

Participants comprised 498 males (49.5%) and 509 females (50.5%). The distribution across academic years was as follows: 223 freshmen (22.2%), 392 sophomores (38.9%), 240 juniors (23.8%), and 152 seniors (15.1%). Descriptive statistics for key continuous variables were as follows: age (20.00 ± 1.12), family socioeconomic status (5.44 ± 2.88), self-rated health (3.68 ± 1.13), neuroticism (6.05 ± 3.02), and deprehssive symptoms (2.10 ± 1.59).

Common method bias test

Common method bias refers to systematic bias or error that occurs when all variables are influenced by the same measurement method or source, typically assessed using Harman’s single-factor test. In this study, the variance explained by the first factor was only 32.23%, which is below the 40% threshold recommended by scholars. Therefore, it can be concluded that the survey results do not exhibit severe common method bias.

Diagnosis of multicollinearity

To prevent the impact of multicollinearity on the regression results, variance inflation factor (VIF) tests were conducted on the independent variables in this study. The results indicated that the VIF values for all independent variables ranged from 1.009 to 1.422, all well below the critical threshold of 5. This suggests that there is no severe multicollinearity issue among the variables, confirming their appropriateness for subsequent regression analysis.

The mediating role of sleep quality between internet addiction and academic burnout was further examined using the Bootstrap method across different levels of physical exercise. The results (see Table 5) showed that when the level of physical exercise was low (M − 1SD), the mediation effect of sleep quality was 0.095 (95% CI [0.062, 0.133]), and the confidence interval did not include zero, indicating a significant mediation effect. Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between internet addiction and academic burnout among college students, examining the mediating role of sleep quality and the moderating role of physical exercise. Firstly, correlational analysis revealed a significant positive association between internet addiction and academic burnout among college students. Secondly, mediation analysis demonstrated that sleep quality played a mediating role between internet addiction and academic burnout among college students. Thirdly, the moderated mediation analysis revealed that physical exercise significantly moderated the relationship between internet addiction and sleep quality, thereby attenuating the negative effect of internet addiction on academic burnout. Based on the aforementioned findings, this study further elucidates the psychological and behavioral mechanisms underlying the relationship between internet addiction and academic burnout among college students.

The effect of internet addiction on academic burnout

This study found that internet addiction is positively correlated with poor sleep quality among college students, supporting Hypothesis 1 and aligning with previous research15,44. Internet addiction could serve as a potential predictor of academic burnout among college students15. Conservation of Resources Theory posits that individuals experience burnout when they undergo persistent loss of psychological resources (e.g., time, attention, and emotion regulation capacity)13. Internet addiction exacerbates academic burnout among college students by depleting psychological capital through three pathways: time displacement, cognitive resource depletion, and diminished social support45. The study revealed that college students with internet addiction exhibit diminished self-control, predisposing them to a vicious cycle of "late-night smartphone overuse → next-day academic inefficiency"46,47. From a neurobiological perspective, internet addiction induces significant structural alterations in the brain, manifesting as reduced gray matter volume in specific regions and abnormal white matter integrity, with some changes demonstrating duration-dependent correlations48,49. Furthermore, neurobiological alterations collectively form the biological basis for the impact of internet addiction on academic burnout through multiple pathways that impair motivational regulation (dopaminergic pathways), impair cognitive resources (prefrontal cortex deficits), exacerbate emotional depletion (HPA-amygdala system hyperactivity), and decrease neuroplasticity (hippocampal and white matter deterioration)50. In summary, internet addiction predicts and exacerbates academic burnout among college students through multiple pathways, including psychological resource depletion, diminished self-regulation, reduced academic self-efficacy, and neurobiological mechanisms.

The mediating effect of sleep quality

This study found that sleep quality mediates the relationship between internet addiction and academic burnout among college students, confirming Hypothesis 2. Consistent with prior research20, the present study revealed a significant positive correlation between internet addiction and poor sleep quality among college students. Firstly, excessive internet use (particularly at night) can delay sleep onset, reduce total sleep duration, and disrupt circadian rhythms, leading to increased insomnia symptoms51. Individuals with internet addiction tend to allocate excessive time to online activities, particularly during nighttime. This excessive internet use directly displaces sleep time, thereby impairing sleep quality52. Studies found that college students with internet addiction commonly exhibit bedtime procrastination and poorer sleep quality53. Secondly, the psychological stimulation from online activities (such as gaming and social media) can increase brain arousal and suppress melatonin secretion, thereby prolonging sleep latency54. The blue light emitted by electronic devices suppresses melatonin secretion, delays circadian rhythms, and consequently impairs sleep quality55. Furthermore, internet addiction is often accompanied by anxiety and depressive symptoms, which can further impair sleep maintenance capacity, leading to nocturnal awakenings and early morning awakenings56,57.

This study also found that poor sleep quality was associated with academic burnout among college students, consistent with previous research17. Firstly, sleep plays an important role in learning processes and memory consolidation58,59. Both sleep quality and sleep duration are closely associated with students’ learning ability and academic performance60. Sleep loss is typically linked to poorer learning capacity, whereas sleep optimization can enhance academic achievement60. Sleep deprivation impairs cognitive performance (e.g., attention and memory), leading to reduced learning efficiency. This compels students to invest more time in completing academic tasks, consequently exacerbating emotional exhaustion and fatigue61. Furthermore, sleep disturbances disrupt emotional regulation, exacerbate mood disorders, and trigger anxiety and depressive symptoms, thereby further diminishing academic motivation62. Moreover, chronic poor sleep quality diminishes self-efficacy, reducing the motivation and confidence required to cope with daily challenges, ultimately impairing academic performance63. In conclusion, behavioral interventions (e.g., reducing screen time) and cognitive interventions (e.g., decreasing psychological dependence on the internet) may effectively improve sleep quality, thereby alleviating academic burnout.

The moderating effect of physical exercise

This study found that physical exercise moderated the relationship between internet addiction and sleep quality, thereby validating Hypothesis 3. Notably, all three levels of physical exercise (low, moderate, and high) demonstrated significant moderating effects, albeit with distinct patterns and practical implications.

Physical exercise can improve sleep quality without notable adverse effects64,65. From a psychological mechanism perspective, physical exercise effectively alleviates anxiety and depressive symptoms associated with Internet addiction by enhancing self-efficacy and emotion regulation capacity, thereby reducing bedtime procrastination66,67. Exercise-induced release of endogenous opioid peptides not only enhances positive emotional states but also directly modulates the sleep–wake cycle68. Physiologically, physical exercise enhances sleep quality by boosting melatonin to regulate circadian rhythms and offset blue light effects69, and activating the endogenous opioid system to modulate sleep–wake cycles68. Research indicates that physical exercise can reduce Internet addiction levels70. Indeed, physical exercise can not only alleviate Internet addiction but also mitigate the negative effects associated with Internet addiction70,71.

Elaborating on the practical significance of different exercise levels, low-level physical exercise (e.g., occasional walking or stretching, < 3 times per week, < 30 min per session) primarily offers a low-threshold entry point for physically inactive or severely addicted students. Even such minimal activity can initiate the disruption of sedentary behavior and the vicious cycle of “addiction-poor sleep”—for instance, by briefly reducing evening screen time and alleviating immediate state anxiety66. The moderation effect at this level is significant but relatively modest, suggesting that while any amount of exercise is beneficial, merely meeting minimal standards may be insufficient to fully counteract the negative consequences of internet addiction. Medium-level physical exercise (e.g., 3–5 times per week, 30–60 min per session of moderate-intensity activities such as jogging or swimming) appears to produce optimal moderation effects. This level is realistically achievable for most college students and can be integrated into campus routines (e.g., as credit-bearing physical education courses or extracurricular sports clubs). It likely facilitates more consistent circadian rhythm entrainment and sustained elevation of brain-derived neurotrophic factors, thereby enhancing executive control and self-regulatory capacity over internet use behaviors73. High-level physical exercise (e.g., daily vigorous training, > 60 min per session) demonstrated the strongest moderating effect, although its feasibility for the general student population is limited due to time commitment and risk of overtraining. For student athletes or exercise enthusiasts, however, this high level provides the most robust protection, possibly via more profound modulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis function and greater neuroplasticity in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus72,73. From a neurobiological perspective, exercise can alleviate internet addiction by modulating neurobiological mechanisms in both the central and autonomic nervous systems72. Physical exercise enhances neuroplasticity in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, thereby strengthening executive control functions and improving self-regulatory capacity over internet use behaviors73. Regular physical exercise also modulates HPA axis function, reduces cortisol levels, and alleviates stress responses associated with internet addiction72. Physical exercise also enhances the secretion of brain-derived neurotrophic factors, improves emotional regulation capacity, and reduces anxiety and depressive symptoms associated with internet addiction, thereby promoting better sleep quality74.

Furthermore, physical exercise is also associated with reduced academic burnout38. Physical exercise (both aerobic and strength) was effective in reducing academic burnout levels in college students75. College students who engage in regular physical exercise exhibit lower levels of academic burnout, which may be attributed to improved sleep quality, enhanced self-regulation, and reduced physiological susceptibility to stress38,76. Research indicates that higher levels of exercise participation correlate with lower degrees of academic burnout in college students38. In summary, physical exercise may reduce academic burnout levels among college students by modulating the impact of internet addiction on sleep quality. From a practical standpoint, these findings suggest that campus-based exercise interventions should be tiered: promoting simple, accessible activities (e.g., standing stretches or brief walks) to engage sedentary students; encouraging moderate, structured physical activity through physical education and recreational sports for the general population; and providing specialized support for student athletes to maintain high-intensity training without overuse injuries. Such a stratified approach can maximize the protective role of physical exercise in mitigating the adverse effects of internet addiction on sleep quality and, consequently, academic burnout.

Limitations

First, this study employed a cross-sectional design. Given the ambiguity of temporal sequence, single-source bias, and cultural/contextual specificity issues, it is recommended that future research adopt longitudinal or experimental designs for validation. Second, the sample in this study was limited to college students from a single province in China. Since internet usage patterns, academic stress, sleep habits, and physical exercise practices may vary significantly across different regions, types of institutions, or cultural backgrounds, future studies should expand the sample size and enhance its diversity and representativeness. Third, the research data collection primarily relied on self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce social desirability bias, recall bias, and subjective bias. Future research could consider incorporating more objective measurement methods (e.g., device-recorded screen time, fitness trackers monitoring sleep and activity, academic performance records, etc.) to supplement or validate self-reported data. Fourth, although we implemented procedural remedies and Harman’s single-factor test did not indicate severe common method bias, we acknowledge the inherent limitations of cross-sectional, self-report data and the diagnostic constraints of Harman’s test. Future research could employ longitudinal designs or incorporate marker variables to more robustly control for this potential bias. Finally, certain psychosocial confounders were not measured. Other potential confounding variables, such as social support, academic motivation, and disciplinary characteristics, should be adequately controlled or investigated in future study. Moreover, additional mediating or moderating mechanisms (e.g., self-control ability, coping strategies, time management skills) should be further explored.

Conclusions

This study investigated the relationship between college students’ internet addiction and academic burnout, revealing sleep quality as a mediating factor and physical exercise as a moderator. Specifically, sleep quality mediated the relationship between internet addiction and academic burnout. The mediating effect of sleep quality was significantly moderated by physical exercise, with higher levels of physical exercise attenuated the adverse effects of internet addiction on sleep quality. Furthermore, higher levels of physical exercise attenuated the negative impact of internet addiction on sleep quality. These findings elucidate the effect of internet addiction on academic burnout among college students and its underlying mechanisms, underscoring the moderating role of physical exercise. This study highlights the distinctive role of physical exercise in mitigating the effect of internet addiction on academic burnout, offering novel perspectives and strategies for addressing these challenges among college students.
