Evaluation of piezosurgery combined with L-PRF, A-PRF, and photobiomodulation for optimizing MRONJ treatment: an animal study
Authors: Mohammad Reza Jamalpour, Shiva Shahabi, Abbas Shokri, Shokoofeh Jamshidi, Salman Khazaei, Atefeh Hashemi, Mehdi Baghestani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This research evaluates the complementary roles of piezosurgery, photobiomodulation (PBM), leukocyte- and platelet-rich fibrin (L-PRF), and advanced platelet-rich fibrin (A-PRF) in the management of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ). First, we induced MRONJ lesions in rats. All rats were inspected for signs of MRONJ eight weeks post-extraction. Forty-nine rats having positive signs of MRONJ were selected and categorized into seven different groups: C (control), PS (piezosurgery), P (piezosurgery + PBM), A (piezosurgery + A-PRF), L (piezosurgery + L-PRF), A + P (piezosurgery + A-PRF + PBM), and L + P (piezosurgery + L-PRF + PBM). Thirty days after the last treatment session, euthanasia was carried out. Additionally, the healing of the lesions was evaluated radiographically, clinically, and histologically. The data were analyzed using STATA software version 14, with a significance level set at 5%. Based on the current research, L-PRF and A-PRF treatments led to significant improvements in radiographical, clinical, and histological parameters compared to the C group (P ˂ 0.05). The PBM, compared to the C group (P ˂ 0.05), decreased the number of empty lacunae and bone and wound exposure dimensions. 6 out of 7 cases showed complete MRONJ resolution in L + P and A + P (P ˂ 0.05). Consequently, no considerable difference between A-PRF and L-PRF treatment groups was observed (P ˃ 0.05). Considering the limitations of this animal study, we concluded that piezosurgery and photobiomodulation alone, unlike PRF, are insufficient for MRONJ treatment. Importantly, the combination of piezosurgery, PBM, and PRF therapy yielded the most promising results in addressing MRONJ. Further clinical investigations are required to confirm these findings.

Introduction

The American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons (AAOMS) defines medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) as the simultaneous presence of three features: exposed bone or intraoral or extraoral fistula linking bone to mucosa in the maxillofacial region that persists for more than eight weeks; a history of current or prior antiangiogenic or antiresorptive therapy; and) no history of radiotherapy to the jaws or metastatic disease involving the jaws [1].

Antiresorptive and antiangiogenic drugs have been broadly prescribed for oncologic and metabolic diseases affecting the skeletal system, including osteoporosis, Paget’s disease, multiple myeloma, and bone metastases of malignant neoplasms [2]. While these medications are essential for managing these conditions, they establish the background risk for MRONJ lesions, particularly following aggressive dental procedures such as tooth extractions [3].

The pathophysiology of MRONJ is believed to be multifactorial. Under the influence of the mentioned medications, severe suppression of bone remodeling, immunological failure, and inhibition of angiogenesis may prevent complete healing of the oral mucosa and bone remodeling [4, 5].

Bisphosphonates are the most common antiresorptive drugs and were associated with the first reported case of MRONJ in 2003 [6]. Since then, the optimal management strategy for MRONJ has remained controversial. Current approaches primarily focus on pain relief, infection prevention, and halting the progression of necrotic areas. Conventional MRONJ treatments, including both surgical and non-surgical conservative therapies, have shown variable success in resolving lesions and preventing recurrence.

Therefore, recent efforts have focused on enhancing conventional methods by incorporating new devices or combining them with adjuvant therapies such as autologous platelet concentrates (APCs), parathyroid hormone, photobiomodulation (PBM), hyperbaric oxygen therapy, and ozone therapy [7–9]. However, none of these approaches has yet been established as the most effective, and in practice, combinations are often used empirically.

Employing multiple treatment modalities can increase costs and extend the overall treatment duration. Meanwhile, these patients are often debilitated due to the chronic underlying disease and its challenging complication, MRONJ. Consequently, it is crucial to identify which combination of treatments can significantly enhance clinical outcomes.

Conventional osteotomy tools often cause marginal osteonecrosis due to significant heat generation, which can impair subsequent bone regeneration. However, modern osteotomy devices, such as piezosurgery, allow bone removal while promoting, rather than inhibiting, bone regeneration. Piezosurgery selectively removes bone using ultrasonic vibrations without raising the bone temperature. As a result, the cellular structures of the bone, soft tissues, and blood supply are preserved [10, 11]. Additionally, piezosurgery may help decontaminate necrotic bone by directly killing bacteria through its cavitation effect [12].

PBM is a safe method widely available for reducing pain and enhancing wound healing. It has also been reported that PBM promotes osteoblast proliferation and differentiation, collagen type 1 formation, calcium deposition, ATP synthesis, and secretion of growth factors, thereby supporting bone regeneration [13, 14].

The elimination of additives in the centrifugation process has led to the development of a new generation of APCs, such as Leukocyte and platelet-rich fibrin (L-PRF) and advanced platelet-rich fibrin (A-PRF).

Studies have shown that modestly increasing centrifugation time while reducing speed leads to the formation of A-PRF, a variant of standard PRF that contains a higher concentration of leukocytes and exhibits enhanced regenerative potential and anti-inflammatory properties [15–17]. The distinction in regenerative potential motivated us to evaluate both PRF types in our study. Considering the limitations of conventional MRONJ treatments, this study was designed to assess healing outcomes following adjunctive therapies—photobiomodulation (PBM), Leukocyte- and Platelet-R-rich Fibrin (L-PRF), and advanced PRF (A-PRF)—in combination with piezosurgery, to evaluate the effectiveness of these conservative modalities, individually or in combination.

Materials and methods

Animals

Ethical approval was obtained from the Hamadan University of Medical Science Ethics Committee (IR.UMSHA.REC.1399.447), and the study followed relevant regulations. All methods were conducted according to the ARRIVE guidelines [18].

Sampling

In our study, an a priori sample size calculation was performed based on data reported by Weber et al. [19], assuming a significance level of 0.05 and a power of 90%. The estimated sample size was 49 rats with MRONJ, which we adopted for all study groups. The calculation was carried out using STATA software version 14.

To induce MRONJ in rats, we implemented the protocol presented by Zandi et al. [20]. According to this protocol, approximately 83% of healthy rats would develop MRONJ following Zoledronic acid injection. Based on this incidence rate, we selected 62 healthy rats and administered Zoledronic acid as per Zandi’s protocol, expecting that about 83%—or roughly 50 rats—would develop MRONJ (60 × 0.83 ≈ 49).

MRONJ induction

The first step of this study induced MRONJ lesions according to the Zandi et al. protocol [20]. We obtained 62 male Wistar Albino rats weighing 300–350 g from the Hamadan Medical Sciences University’s Animal House. The animals were acclimated to laboratory conditions, including temperature and humidity, for ten days prior to the start of the study. They were provided with a standard diet of rat pellets and water ad libitum.

The animals underwent an intraperitoneal injection of 0.06 mg/kg zoledronate (Zolena, Ronak Pharmaceutical, Saveh, Iran) once a week for six weeks. One week after the last injection, the first and second molars of the left mandible were extracted using dental forceps under general anesthesia induced by an intraperitoneal injection of 75 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride (Ketamine Hydrochloride, Laboratoires Sterop, Brussels, Belgium) and 7.5 mg/kg midazolam (Midazolam, Chemidarou Industrial Company, Tehran, Iran). Rats were subsequently administered a subcutaneous injection of 2 mg/kg ketorolac (Ketorolac, Caspian Tamin Pharmaceutical, Gilan, Iran) for analgesic purposes. For three days following the extractions, they were fed a soft diet consisting of crushed pellets and provided with 300 ml of drinking water containing 450 mg of amoxicillin (50 mg/ml, Tehran Chemie, Tehran, Iran). Zoledronic acid injections, administered at the same dose and interval as previously described, were continued for an additional six weeks following the teeth extractions.

Study groups

Eight weeks after extraction, all rats were examined for signs of MRONJ, defined by AAOMS [1]. Forty-nine rats had one or more signs of this disease. In intraoral examinations, buccolingual (BL) and mesiodistal (MD) dimensions of the wound area and the exposed bone area were measured using a surgical graduated caliper. The wound area involved exposed bone and surrounding mucosa that lacked normal color and consistency. Furthermore, the presence of intraoral and extraoral fistulas was determined. Following the confirmation of MRONJ signs in 49 rats, they were randomly allocated into seven groups (n = 7 per group) using a computer-generated simple randomization sequence created in Microsoft Excel (Table 1). Outcome assessments, including clinical measurements, radiographic evaluations, and histological scoring, were performed by independent examiners who were blinded to group allocation. To ensure blinding, all specimens were labeled with anonymized codes unrelated to treatment groups. Only one researcher, who was not involved in the assessments, had access to the treatment allocation file to maintain proper separation of roles.

Surgical procedure

General anesthesia was induced via intraperitoneal injection of 75 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride (Ketamine Hydrochloride, Laboratoires Sterop, Brussels, Belgium) and 7.5 mg/kg midazolam (Midazolam, Chemidarou Industrial Company, Tehran, Iran). A mucoperiosteal flap was then elevated and mobilized to expose the necrotic bone areas. Inflamed wound margins were excised, and necrotic bone was removed using a piezosurgery unit (Surgeystar, Dmetec, South Korea). The surgical procedure was guided intraoperatively by observing bleeding bone margins and ensuring the elimination of any sharp edges. In the A, L, A + P, and L + P groups, the surgical defect was first covered with a PRF membrane, followed by two simple interrupted 4 − 0 nylon (Nylon, Supa, Tehran, Iran) sutures to achieve tension-free primary closure. These sutures served to immobilize the membrane and prevent it from being washed away by saliva. In the other groups, primary closure was achieved with 4 − 0 nylon (Nylon, Supa, Tehran, Iran) sutures alone. Sutures were removed on the 10th postoperative day. Postoperative care was provided as previously described for tooth extractions.

Photobiomodulation

For P, A + P, and L + P groups, the PBM was performed by an 808 nm diode laser (3 L-IR, Hamerz, Tehran, Iran) on the 1 st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, and 10th postoperative days, according to the settings described in Table 2 [13]. The device was calibrated before each use to ensure accuracy and safety.

A-PRF and L-PRF preparation

Following induction of complete anesthesia, approximately 2 mL of blood was collected from the retro-orbital sinus using a capillary pipette and transferred into sterile tubes. Although the retro-orbital technique yields sufficient blood volume, it carries a risk of tissue damage and therefore requires careful execution by experienced personnel. To minimize potential complications, blood collection was limited to one eye per session; if unsuccessful, the procedure was postponed for at least one week before attempting the other eye. For post-procedural pain relief, topical ophthalmic anesthesia (Tetracaine HCL 0.5%, 5 mL, Darupakhsh Company, Iran) was applied at the conclusion of blood collection.

Plain glass-coated plastic tubes were used for L-PRF, while A-PRF was prepared using glass-based vacuum tubes [21]. Centrifugation was performed immediately at 2700 rpm for 12 min to prepare L-PRF and at 1500 rpm for 14 min to prepare A-PRF. After centrifugation, the tube contents were expelled, and the PRF layer was separated from the acellular plasma above and the red blood cell layer below [17, 22]. Subsequently, the PRF layer was compressed to form a membrane suitable for placement over the defect [23]. We determined the membrane dimensions from the extent of the surgical defect.

Clinical evaluation

Thirty days after the last session of PBM therapy, euthanasia was performed using an overdose of anesthesia via an intraperitoneal injection of 150 mg/kg ketamine hydrochloride (Ketamine Hydrochloride, Laboratoires Sterop, Brussels, Belgium) and 15 mg/kg midazolam (Midazolam, Chemidarou Industrial Company, Tehran, Iran). The dimensions of the MD and BL wounds, the presence of intraoral and extraoral fistulas, and the bone exposure areas in the MD and BL regions were recorded. Two observers, blinded to the treatment allocation, graded mucosal healing into highly satisfactory, satisfactory, and unsatisfactory (Fig. 1). The highly satisfactory group showed fully healed mucosal tissue that appeared healthy, with smooth and intact coverage over the area. In the satisfactory group, the mucosal coverage had healed but showed noticeable differences from healthy tissue. The color might be slightly altered—more reddish or pinkish—and the texture could feel different, such as being thicker, thinner, or more fragile. The unsatisfactory group demonstrated inadequate healing, evidenced by the exposure of bone or other underlying tissues, reflecting incomplete recovery.

Radiologic evaluation

After harvesting the hemimandibles, the soft tissues were detached, and plain radiographs were obtained. The lesion site on each hemimandible was marked with opaque pointers. Digital periapical radiographs were then acquired using a MinRay (Soredex, Tuusula, Finland) at 60 kVp, 6 mA, and a 0.12 s exposure time. Images were evaluated by an Oral and Maxillofacial radiologist with over 20 years of experience who was blinded to group assignment. For each image, identical 20 × 20 pixel squares were cropped from the center of the surgical site, with the upper edge starting 5 pixels below the pointers. The cropped images were imported into MATLAB software v7.11 (MathWorks, Cambridge, MA, USA) to determine the gray values of all pixels. Gray values ranged from 0 to 255, with 255 indicating the greatest attenuation of X-rays. The mean and standard deviation of the gray values for each image were calculated, yielding a value between 0 and 255 that reflects the bone density of the scanned region.

Histological evaluation

The hemimandibles were fixed in 10% formalin solution for approximately 72 h. Subsequently, they were decalcified using ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 30 days. After demineralization and embedding in paraffin, all samples were sectioned buccolingually at the experimental sites (5 μm thick, four sections per sample); then, they were stained with eosin and hematoxylin. Two blinded pathologists carried out the histological evaluations.

The slides were mounted on a light microscope and examined using a 10 × 10 mm eyepiece grid reticle under 400× magnification. The average number of empty lacunae and osteocytes per 25 mm² was calculated across five fields. Descriptive evaluations of the inflammatory infiltration, vascularization, and epithelial tissue integrity were performed in five areas. The vascularization was reported using a four-point scale (0: absent, 1: slight, 2: moderate, and 3: marked), similar to the inflammatory infiltration (0: absent, 1: 1–100 cells showing slight inflammation, 2: 100–250 cells showing moderate inflammation, and 3: >250 cells showing severe inflammation). The epithelial tissue integrity was graded as highly satisfactory (complete epithelial tissue coverage), satisfactory (interrupted epithelial tissue formation), and unsatisfactory (no epithelial tissue formation) [19].

Statistical analysis

Data displayed as absolute frequency, percentage, and mean ± standard deviation were analyzed via STATA 14 (Stata Corp LP, Lakeway, Texas, USA). Moreover, using the kappa coefficient (κ-values ˃ 0.75: excellent, κ-values ˂ 0.40: low, and 0.40 ˂ κ-values ˂ 0.75: moderate agreement), the interobserver agreement was interpreted. Pearson’s chi-square or Fisher’s exact test (when the projected values in table cells were less than five) determined the association among qualitative variables. Using the one-way ANOVA test, the quantitative variables were compared. In addition, Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. A significance level of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall, the rats tolerated the experimental period well. Excessive weight loss caused by zoledronate administration resulted in the loss of four rats before tooth extraction. The remaining animals maintained their health, achieved satisfactory hemostasis, and recovered from anesthesia without incident.

Clinical findings

There were no statistically significant differences between study groups in the baseline mean MD and BL wound dimensions, the mean MD and BL exposed bone areas, or the number of fistulas (P > 0.05). Accordingly, the control group data were used as the pre-treatment reference for comparisons of outcomes before and after treatment.

Clinical examinations revealed an excellent interobserver agreement (κ = 0.90, P < 0.001). Post-treatment clinical examinations demonstrated significant reductions in mean MD and BL wound dimensions across all treatment groups (PS, P, A, L, A + P, L + P) compared with the control group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the mean MD and BL bone exposure area dimensions in A + P and L + P were significantly reduced compared to the PS group (P < 0.05). Specimens from A, L, A + P, and L + P reached intraoral fistula healing, and the extraoral fistula was healed in L, A + P, and L + P. The best mucosal healing was observed in A + P and L + P, with six cases in each group showing highly satisfactory mucosal healing. Notably, satisfactory mucosal healing was observed frequently in the P (n = 3) and L (n = 4) groups. Discussion

To optimize the MRONJ healing process, adjuvant therapies should support conventional treatments and accelerate the suppressed bone and soft tissue regeneration impaired by antiresorptive and antiangiogenic medications. Various adjuvant therapies have been introduced so far. However, it is still unclear which treatment has the most impact and whether the simultaneous application of these therapies is more effective than single therapy [7, 8].

In our previous study, we assessed the complementarity of surgical therapy (removal of necrotic bone with a conventional round surgical bur), PBM, A-PRF, and L-PRF for managing MRONJ lesions, and we observed that following surgical resection, the combined application of PBM and PRF treatment resulted in optimal healing of MRONJ lesions [24]. Consequently, we decided to evaluate the effect of a less invasive surgical method (piezosurgery) in combination with these adjuvant therapies on the outcomes. Although necrotic bone removal by piezosurgery is time-consuming, it has proven to be an easy-handling method that provides superior intraoperative visibility in an antimicrobial field [25].

The lack of mucosal coverage leads to frequent infections that delay healing; therefore, the elimination of necrotic bone is critical [26, 27].

Based on our findings, piezoelectric surgical treatment (PS group) resulted in smaller mean wound and bone exposure dimensions compared to the control group. Similarly, Blus et al. reported that 18 MRONJ patients achieved complete soft-tissue closure after necrotic bone removal with a piezoelectric device at one-month follow-up [28]. Conversely, we observed no substantial improvements in MRONJ healing with respect to other clinical, radiographic, and histological parameters. Thus, necrotic bone removal is essential for preparing healthy margins to facilitate healing, but it appears insufficient to enhance overall tissue regeneration.

According to prior reports, the piezoelectric device can produce a smooth-cut bony surface without damaging osteocytes at the site or the surrounding soft tissues [29, 30]. Accordingly, has the potential to be preferable for achieving the primary goal of removing necrotic bone.

Adding PBM therapy resulted in slightly better outcomes compared to surgery alone. PBM utilizes red or infrared light to modulate cellular function [31]. It enhances adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production and promotes controlled generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitric oxide (NO). The release of NO facilitates vasodilation, improves microcirculation, and accelerates tissue repair processes [32].

The inflammation and vascularization of the specimens in the P group were relatively reduced, which could be attributed to the maturation of granulation tissue. Despite the positive effects of PBM on bone regeneration, our results showed inconclusive evidence of bone formation progress, which might be explained by the suppressive effect of zoledronate on bone remodeling [33]. Similarly, Ervolino et al. [34] stated that PBM therapy in the extraction sites of zoledronate-treated rats is ineffective in promoting bone remodeling.

Nica et al. [35] used a combination of antibiotic therapy, piezosurgery, and PBM to prevent or treat MRONJ lesions. The PBM helped prevent MRONJ occurrence in 84 at-risk cases. In 49 patients without exposed necrotic bone or need for surgery, PBM therapy plus antibiotics reduced pain and mucosal inflammation in all cases. They also treated 108 patients with combined perioperative antibiotics, preoperative PBM, and piezosurgery, achieving a healing rate of 91.66%.

Latifyan et al. [14] reviewed seven published studies on the effects of PBM in treating MRONJ. They reported that the overall complete remission rate in studies using PBM was higher than in the control groups (55% vs. 30%). They also confirmed that PBM enhances tissue regeneration by promoting cell proliferation, calcium deposition, angiogenesis, and anti-inflammatory effects. Regarding the efficiency of laser therapy, a narrative review by Del Vecchio et al. [36] revealed that laser applications significantly support MRONJ management due to pain reduction, antibacterial effects, and stimulatory impacts on both bone and the hemoderivatives used to boost healing at the surgical site.

However, PBM outcomes may vary according to several factors, including laser parameters, the number of treatment sessions required, and the pre-treatment stage of MRONJ.

Regarding the effects of PRF therapy on MRONJ healing, we observed that PRF therapy (A and L groups) improved healing and bone regeneration compared to the previous groups. All extraoral and intraoral fistulas were resolved except for a single case. These groups also exhibited greater bone remodeling, as indicated by a higher average number of osteocytes and fewer empty lacunae. This improvement aligns with reduced inflammation, decreased vascularization, and the maturation of granulation tissue into mineralized bone.

Consistent with our findings, Bilimoria et al. [37] concluded that combining piezosurgery with L-PRF treatment promotes the healing of MRONJ and osteoradionecrosis, demonstrating 67% complete mucosal coverage within one year in 12 treated cases. Similarly, a 2016 systematic review by Lopez-Jornet et al. [38] found that 85.98% of MRONJ cases were fully resolved when APC was used in combination with surgery. However, a systematic review by Fortunato et al. [39] reported that although the healing rate was higher when APC was combined with surgery compared to surgery alone (87.8% vs. 63.8%), this difference was not statistically significant. Due to their fibrin structure, PRFs act as scaffolds that trap platelets and prevent direct contact between bone-released medications and the oral mucosa [40]. Stored platelets contribute by releasing growth factors, upregulating osteoprotegerin and alkaline phosphatase, providing inflammatory protection, and promoting proliferation of osteoblasts [41]. The properties of PRFs are durable (lasting 7–28 days) and reduce the need for multiple therapy sessions, in contrast to PBM [21].Furthermore, PRFs are chemical-free, cost-effective, and straightforward to prepare. These advantages, along with our findings, establish PRF therapy as a highly effective adjuvant treatment for MRONJ lesions.

We also investigated the efficacy of combined PRF and PBM therapies (A + P and L + P groups). The improved bone remodeling observed in these groups was significant compared to PBM therapy alone, confirming the determinative role of adding PRFs. Clinical examinations revealed complete healing in 6 out of 7 specimens from these groups. The concurrent adjuvant therapies also reduced inflammatory infiltration and improved epithelial tissue integrity.

Based on our findings, applying PBM and PRF together enhances their effect on MRONJ healing. The exact cellular interactions between PBM and PRF are still unclear. However, it is believed that the synergy may involve PBM boosting cellular activity, promoting growth factor activity, stimulating angiogenesis, and reducing inflammation. These combined effects likely strengthen the regenerative potential of PRF. Additionally, this combination not only promotes increased cell migration and proliferation but also supports greater calcium deposition, which is vital for bone mineralization and strength. Overall, this integrated approach may lead to faster and more effective healing compared to using either treatment alone [42, 43]. There are a few reports of positive outcomes with combined adjuvant therapies. Tenore et al. [44] reported that combining antibiotics, surgery, L-PRF, and photobiomodulation could effectively help manage MRONJ. All 13 patients treated with this combination showed complete healing at both three and six months of follow-up. Şahin et al. [45] treated 21 patients with stage 2–3 MRONJ using ultrasonic piezoelectric bone surgery to remove necrotic bone, L-PRF from the patient’s blood, and Nd: YAG laser for biostimulation. They observed complete mucosal healing in all patients by the third-month follow-up. In another study, Merigo et al. [46] applied the combination of platelet-rich plasma (PRP), 808 nm laser for PBM, piezosurgery, and Er: YAG laser ablation to treat 21 MRONJ patients. At the six-month follow-up, 92.85% showed complete healing. They recommended using different high-tech approaches sequentially during MRONJ treatment. Lastly, while two different PRF types (A-PRF and L-PRF) were used, there were no statistically significant differences between them (P >0.05).

Bone density measurements obtained from periapical radiographs are considered semi-quantitative because these radiographs provide two-dimensional images of a three-dimensional structure. The gray values in such radiographs are affected by various factors, including exposure settings, X-ray tube angulation, absence of calibration, anatomical overlap and superimposition, sensor sensitivity, and image processing. These variables prevent the images from accurately representing absolute bone mineral density, allowing only for relative comparisons (e.g., before vs. after treatment within the same patient), rather than precise quantitative values.

In contrast, CBCT and CT images calibrated in Hounsfield units can offer true quantitative bone density values under controlled protocols. However, they also have limitations, such as inherent noise, metal artifacts, streak artifacts, and other factors that can reduce image quality.

Due to several reasons—including lower radiation exposure compared to CT and CBCT, lower cost, and easier accessibility—periapical radiographs are commonly used for the initial evaluation of bone healing and bone density. Many studies have employed periapical radiography for evaluating bone density and healing [47–49]. For a more detailed assessment, especially in complex and advanced cases or for procedures like implant placement and bone augmentation, using CT or CBCT images is generally preferable.

Although this animal study employed a statistically adequate sample size of rats based on power analysis, it is essential to consider the differences between rat mandibles and human jaws—such as size, bone turnover, and biomechanics—when interpreting and applying the results, particularly in actual clinical cases.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this animal study, we concluded that piezosurgery and PBM therapy alone are insufficient to enhance MRONJ healing. PRF therapy, however, resulted in significant bone regeneration and soft tissue healing. Notably, the combination of piezosurgery, PBM, and PRF therapy produced the most favorable outcomes in MRONJ treatment. Further clinical trials are necessary to validate these findings.
