Bridge technique self-traction improves outcomes over conventional endoscopic submucosal dissection for semicircumferential gastric cardia neoplasms in a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Junwei Xie, Yaowu Cai, Linyun Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo compare the efficacy and safety of the novel “Bridge Technique” self-traction endoscopic submucosal dissection (BT-ESD) versus conventional ESD (C-ESD) for treating semi-circumferential or larger superficial neoplasms of the gastric cardia. A single-center, randomized controlled trial. The First Hospital of Putian City of Fujian Province, China for period of July 2020 to December 2023. Eighty patients with gastric cardia adenoma or early carcinoma were enrolled and randomly allocated to either the BT-ESD group or the C-ESD group. Outcome measures included lesion size (longest diameter), procedure time, resection speed, submucosal injection volume, en bloc resection rate, frequency of intraprocedural bleeding, hospitalization duration, and complications (perforation, postoperative bleeding, infection). The mean lesion long diameter was comparable between groups (BT-ESD: 55.7 ± 15.6 mm; C-ESD: 55.2 ± 10.6 mm). Compared to the C-ESD group, the BT-ESD group demonstrated a significantly shorter procedure time, lower submucosal injection volume, and reduced frequency of intraprocedural bleeding (all p < 0.05). Resection speed was significantly faster in the BT-ESD group. The en bloc resection rate was higher and the postoperative infection rate was lower in the BT-ESD group (p < 0.05). Overall complication rates were lower with BT-ESD, but the difference was not statistically significant(p > 0.05). For large (≥ semi-circumferential) superficial lesions of the gastric cardia, the “Bridge Technique” self-traction ESD is a safe, efficient, and rapid approach. The BT-ESD technique demonstrated favorable outcomes in terms of procedure time, en bloc resection rate, and intraprocedural bleeding compared with conventional ESD. Introduction

There were over 968,000 new cases of gastric cancer in 2022 and close to 660,000 deaths, ranking the disease as fifth in terms of both incidence and mortality worldwide1. Although gastric cancer is often reported as a single entity, it can be classified as two epidemiologically distinct entities of the gastric cardia (upper stomach) and noncardia (lower stomach). Gastric cardia neoplasm locates in the gastric cardia beneath the gastroesophageal junction. A recent study from China put that proportion of gastric cardia cancer at over 60%2. Compared to 7.0% in Japan and 11.9% in western cohort3,4, gastric cardia carcinoma was much higher of early gastric cancer (EGC) in China, which reached to 23.3%5. Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is an advanced therapeutic technique for en bloc resection of superficial gastrointestinal (GI) Neoplasms, providing minimal invasiveness and favorable outcomes. However, to safely and effectively perform gastric cardia ESD, clear visualization of the submucosal layer is crucial. Data has suggested that ESD management of gastric neoplasms located in the upper and middle thirds of the stomach represented higher-risk6. Compared to ESD performed in the gastric body, antrum, or even the oesophagus, lesions located in the gastric cardia are particularly difficult to manage because of the unique anatomical location and structure of the cardia.

The main challenges of gastric cardia ESD are as follows: (1) Restricted operating space and difficult angulation; (2) Unstable visual field for performing ESD; (3) Complex submucosal architecture and increased perforation risk; (4) Rich vascularity and anatomical Variation make high risk of bleeding; (5) Elevated risk of post-Procedural stricture; (6) Difficulty with submucosal injection, especially in large curve of cardia. The traction-assisted approach represents a solution to overcome some of the technical difficulties associated with gastric and colonic ESD7–13. However, reported existing traction maneuvers are often device-dependent, which can be operationally cumbersome to some extent. Furthermore, there is a lack of research on traction techniques for gastric cardia lesions exceeding a semi-circumferential extent. In this case series study, we aim to evaluate whether the “Bridge Technique” self-traction ESD improves clinical outcomes in treating semi-circumferential or larger gastric cardia neoplasm, compared with the conventional ESD.

Objects and methods

Patients

This prospective, randomized controlled study initially estimated the required sample size based on the primary endpoint of en bloc resection rate. Assuming an en bloc resection rate of 85% in the conventional ESD group and 95% in the BT-ESD group, with a two-sided alpha error of 0.05 and a beta error of 0.20 (statistical power of 80%), a minimum of 35 patients per group was calculated. Anticipating a potential dropout rate of 10%, we planned to enroll 40 patients per group, totaling 80 patients. Between July 2020 and December 2023, patients diagnosed with gastric adenoma or early adenocarcinoma located in the gastric cardia at our hospital were screened for eligibility. Eligible patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the conventional ESD (CESD) group or the “Bridge Technique” self-traction ESD (BT- ESD) group using a computer-generated random number sequence. Concealed allocation was maintained until the start of the endoscopic procedure. Due to the inherent technical nature of the interventions, blinding of the performing endoscopist and the patients was not feasible in this study. However, the pathologist assessing the specimens (e.g., for margin status evaluation) was blinded to the group allocation of the patients. All procedures were performed by the same senior endoscopist. The endoscopist had performed over 800 gastric ESD procedures prior to the start of this study, including approximately 120 cases involving the gastric cardia. The BT-ESD technique was developed and practiced in approximately 15 pilot cases (not included in the trial) before the formal randomized study to overcome the learning curve. Lesion staging was confirmed preoperatively by endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS) and magnifying endoscopy (ME) to ensure the depth of invasion met ESD criteria. Contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) scans of the chest and abdomen were performed to exclude lymph node or distant metastasis. The final analysis included all randomized patients (intention-to-treat population). The data lock for this interim analysis was June 2025. Follow-up duration was calculated from the date of the ESD procedure to the date of the last endoscopic surveillance or the data lock date, whichever came first. The trial was retrospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2600119809; Date of Registration: March 4, 2026) after completion of patient enrollment. The delay in registration was due to the investigators’ insufficient awareness of mandatory prospective registration requirements at the study initiation. The study protocol, including primary and secondary endpoints, was finalized prior to the first patient enrollment and remained unchanged throughout the trial.The study protocol is available as Supplementary Information. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of First hospital of Putian City. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Inclusion and exclusion standards

The indications for ESD were based on the Japanese Gastric Cancer Treatment Guidelines (absolute and expanded criteria) for early gastric cancer and adenoma with high-grade dysplasia: (1) intramucosal (cT1a) intestinal-type adenocarcinoma of any size without ulceration; (2) ulcerated, cT1a intestinal-type adenocarcinoma with a diameter ≤ 3 cm; or (3) non-ulcerated, cT1a diffuse-type adenocarcinoma with a diameter ≤ 2 cm14,15. Specifically for this study, eligible lesions were required to be located within the gastric cardia region (defined as the area within 2 cm distal to the gastroesophageal junction) and to involve ≥ 50% of the luminal circumference (semi-circumferential) or have a maximum diameter ≥ 3 cm (large neoplasm). Exclusion criteria included: evidence of lymph node or distant metastasis on CECT; conditions precluding safe anesthesia (e.g., severe heart failure NYHA Class III/IV, respiratory insufficiency); coagulation disorders; or prior gastric surgery involving the cardia.

Endoscopic procedure

Conventional ESD(CESD) : The procedure was regularly performed with a single-channel endoscope (GIF-Q260J; Olympus Optical, Tokyo, Japan) with a transparent cap attached. The conventional ESD techniques were previously detailed. Briefly, following the placement of circumferential marking dots outside the lesion margins, a submucosal injection containing Methylene Blue and Epinephrine was administered. Subsequently, a circumferential mucosal incision was performed, followed by submucosal dissection. Both steps utilized a high-frequency electrosurgical knife (MFK; Anrei Medical Co., Ltd., China). Hemostasis during the procedure was achieved endoscopically using either the knife tip itself or hemostatic forceps.

The “Bridge Technique” self-traction ESD (BT-ESD): The procedure was regularly performed with a single-channel endoscope (GIF-Q260J; Olympus Optical, Tokyo, Japan) with a transparent cap attached.The mucosal incision around the lesion is performed in the same manner as in conventional ESD. During submucosal dissection in the forward view, a bridge of submucosal tissue at the lesser curvature side of the gastric cardia (approximately 3–5 mm in width and maintaining adequate thickness for mechanical support) is deliberately preserved. This creates a pivotal self-traction effect. The preserved tissue band, under inherent tension, lifts the dissected mucosal flap away from the dissection plane, effectively preventing its sagging or collapse into the operative field. The central and greater curvature aspects of the lesion are then progressively dissected until the anal margin is reached and connected. This results in a ‘bridge-like’ structure providing bilateral traction with a fully dissected central corridor. The efficacy of the traction was directly assessed visually during the procedure: effective traction was confirmed by the sustained elevation of the mucosal flap and the maintenance of a clear, unobstructed submucosal dissection plane. Only after the major part of the lesion was removed under this stable view were the remaining traction tissue bands at the lesser curvature side finally and safely transected (Figs. 1 and 2). Both steps utilized a high-frequency electrosurgical knife (MFK; Anrei Medical Co., Ltd., China). Hemostasis during the procedure was achieved endoscopically using either the knife tip itself or hemostatic forceps. A representative video demonstrating the key steps of the BT-ESD procedure is provided as Supplementary Video 1.

The observation indexes

The primary observation indicators included intraoperative parameters such as procedure duration, resection speed, complete resection rate, as well as complications occurring during and after the procedure, including hemorrhage, perforation, and stricture and so on. The following data were prospectively collected: histopathological diagnosis, lesion dimensions, length of hospital stay, and procedural metrics. These metrics included operative time, resection speed, frequency of intraprocedural bleeding, volume of submucosal injectate, en bloc resection rate, and procedure-related adverse events (e.g., perforation, delayed bleeding, post-procedural infection, and stricture formation). Definition: Incisions made intentionally within the lesion for therapeutic purposes (e.g., for dissection or hemostasis) were not considered specimen damage. In contrast, any unintentional transection of the resection specimen by the electrosurgical knife was classified as an inadvertent incision. En bloc resection was defined as the removal of the entire neoplastic lesion in one piece, resulting in a single, macroscopically continuous specimen. R0 resection was achieved when en bloc resection was accomplished, and subsequent histopathological examination demonstrated no evidence of neoplasia at both the lateral (horizontal) and deep vertical margins. Tumor size was determined by measuring the maximum gross diameter of the fresh, unfixed resection specimen. Instances of hemostasis were tallied specifically when targeted coagulation with hemostatic forceps was required to secure hemostasis in the setting of active bleeding. The intraoperative injection volume was defined as the total submucosally injected fluid volume during ESD procedure. Operative time was defined as the interval from the initial mucosal incision to the complete resection of the specimen. The speed of ESD was calculated as the resected specimen area (in mm2) divided by the total procedure time (in minutes). Perforation was diagnosed either by the direct visualization of mesenteric fat or intra-abdominal organs during the procedure, or by the detection of pneumoperitoneum on plain radiographs of the chest and abdomen postoperatively16. Delayed bleeding was defined as clinical evidence of gastrointestinal hemorrhage following ESD that necessitated a blood transfusion or an endoscopic/surgical hemostatic intervention. The primary infections were pulmonary and abdominal infections. Pulmonary infection presented with postoperative fever, elevated inflammatory markers on blood tests, cough and expectoration, and evidence of pulmonary inflammation on chest CT or X-ray17. Abdominal infection manifested as abdominal pain, physical examination findings of tenderness and rebound tenderness, and abdominal CT showing inflammatory exudates in the peritoneal cavity. Postprocedural stricture is defined as a luminal narrowing to a diameter of less than 10 mm, caused by excessive fibrotic tissue formation and scar contracture during healing, which impedes the passage of a standard endoscope.

Histopathological evaluation

Histological assessment of the endoscopically resected specimen was performed to determine the following: tumor histotype, vertical invasion depth, lateral and deep margin involvement (R0/R1), and evidence of lymphovascular invasion.

Follow-up

Surveillance endoscopy is performed at 3, 6, and 12 months post-procedure, followed by annual examinations thereafter. For tumors with invasion limited to the mucosa (T1a), annual computed tomography (CT) of the chest and upper abdomen is recommended. In cases with submucosal invasion (T1b), CT surveillance should be performed every six months for the first year, then annually thereafter.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by SPSS22.0, count data was tested by Chi-square test, showed as n %; measurement data was tested by t test, showed as x ± s; P < 0.05 was defined as significant difference.

The complications of the two group

Intraoperative perforation occurred in 2 patients, both in the CESD group. These perforations were immediately closed with titanium clips during the ESD procedure, followed by nasogastric tube placement for decompression after the whole ESD performance. Postoperatively, intensive antibiotic therapy was administered. Both patients resumed a gradual oral diet after 5 days of active treatment. Both patients did not develop mediastinitis, and manifested only mild signs of peritonitis. No postoperative bleeding occurred in either group. Stenosis occurred in 4 patients (5.0% overall): 3 (7.5%) in the CESD group and 1 (2.5%) in the BT-ESD group. All of the 4 lesions all involved greater than three-quarters of the lumen. Two of the 4 patients were administered oral budesonide (640 µg) plus alginate-magnesium suspension three times daily, initiated on the first postoperative day, for stricture prophylaxis. However, the two cases still developed significant dysphagia-causing strictures by postoperative day 14. Stenosis resolved after a single endoscopic dilation(The bougie was dilated to 13 mm) in 3 patients. The remaining patient achieved resolution after four sessions of endoscopic dilation and three sessions of endoscopic stricturotomy. The risk factors for post-ESD stenosis at the gastric cardia are considered to be associated with lesion extent and intraoperative muscle layer injury. The stenosis rate was lower in the BTESD group than in the CBD group. Positive vertical margins were identified in two patients, with depth of submucosal invasion greater than 500 μm. Subsequent surgical intervention was performed in both cases.The details see in the Table 2. All patients were followed up for a mean duration of 37.5 months, with no recurrence or metastasis observed.

Discussion

Clinical data has confirmed that ESD is a safe and effective technique for the treatment of early gastric cancer including gastric cardia caner. However, the unique anatomical location and structure of the gastric cardia make the ESD for gastric cardia neoplasm more difficult than other location of the upper gastrointestinal cancer. The unique anatomy of the gastric cardia poses substantial technical challenges for ESD, especially for large semi-circumferential lesion.

To reduce procedural difficulty and complication rates, digestive endoscopy experts have explored various approaches. The most effective modality involves optimizing the surgical field through traction techniques, thereby facilitating precise dissection and achieving reliable hemostasis during endoscopic procedures7,9,11–13. Currently employed traction techniques, including clip-assisted traction, spring-and-loop with clip (S–O clip) traction, rubber band-based traction, and magnetic traction, all necessitate the placement of a foreign body or prosthesis14,15,18−20.

A multicenter prospective randomized controlled trial confirmed that the clip-assisted traction method did not reduce the procedure time for gastric ESD15. The main limitation of the clip-assisted traction method is the direction toward the endoscope in gastric ESD. In gastric ESD procedures, distal traction may lead to distal displacement or collapse of the submucosal dissection plane, thereby increasing technical difficulty and potentially prolonging procedure time in certain cases. If the tip of the endoscope cannot be positioned parallel to the gastric wall, proximal traction may cause proximal sagging of the mucosal flap, further complicating submucosal dissection. Furthermore, for lesions located along the greater curvature of the stomach, the clip-with-line method can provide vertical traction that facilitates submucosal dissection. However, this traction technique may not be equally suitable for lesions in other regions of the stomach due to anatomical constraints.

Study has demonstrated that the S–O clip-assisted method reduced the median gastric ESD procedure time, especially the direction vertical to the gastric wall18. The principle for generating traction is the same between S–O clip traction and rubber band-based traction. The procedure involves the following sequential steps: First, a specialized traction-generating clip is deployed on the target tissue. Next, a standard hemostatic clip is used to anchor the distal end of the traction device to the gastrointestinal wall. Finally, controlled extension of the traction mechanism enables precise tissue traction, thereby facilitating endoscopic submucosal dissection19. The S–O clip-assisted ESD is especially recommended for lesions in the upper and middle third of the stomach.The disadvantage is that a certain distance between the anchor clip and the clip attached to the lesion is required to generate the traction force.

Magnetic traction is a sophisticated method allowing dynamic tissue retraction with a rotatable external magnet (permanent magnet and electromagnet are the 2 available types)20. Several studies have confirmed that the technique is feasible and safe while offering excellent visualization for gastric ESD21–22. The key advantage of this method over other traction approaches is its preservation of unrestricted endoscopic maneuverability throughout ESD. Concurrently, the continuous adjustment of the external magnet generates a dynamic traction force, which optimally directs the retraction to facilitate submucosal dissection. However, the unique anatomical configuration of the gastroesophageal junction (gastric cardia region) imposes significant constraints on the operational workspace.

Although the introduction of additional devices may temporarily improve visual field exposure during certain procedural phases, the risk of instrument entanglement often compounds the complexity of endoscopic manipulation.

In conventional ESD, the procedure involves making circumferential markings around the lesion, followed by a complete circumferential incision along the margin. Submucosal dissection is then initiated from one end (typically the oral or anal side) and progressively extended toward the opposite side until en bloc resection is achieved23–24. To achieve optimal visualization during the procedure, some experts have introduced technical refinements to the conventional ESD technique, leading to the development of tunnel-ESD (T- ESD) and the pocket-creation method ESD (PCM - ESD)25–27. T-ESD creates a narrow submucosal tunnel by making an initial incision at one side of the lesion and dissecting toward the opposite side. The overlying intact mucosa acts as a “roof”, providing natural traction, preventing lesion sagging, and reducing fluid cushion loss. However, the confined tunnel space limits lateral visualization, delays management of lateral margins, and makes hemostasis more difficult compared to conventional ESD28. Although T- ESD can provide improved visualization, this technique is more suitable for resecting large lesions in relatively straight and non-angled anatomical regions such as the esophagus and intestines29,30. In contrast, the gastric cardia presents technical challenges for T- ESD when managing lesions on either side due to its unique anatomical architecture characterized by a significant angulation.

PCM-ESD creates a wide submucosal pocket by incising approximately 180–270 degrees of the oral side and dissecting within it, leaving the anal margin for final incision. This intact oral flap (“pocket base”) supports the lesion and provides better visualization than conventional ESD26. Compared with T-ESD, PCM-ESD offers a more open field, especially for lesions predominantly on the oral or central aspect. However, after the anal mucosa is incised, that portion may still sag, and bleeding from the incised edge can obscure the field. Moreover, late anal incision risks imprecise positioning. In the gastric cardia, the acute angulation makes completion of the anal marginal incision particularly challenging, rendering the final lateral dissection difficult.

We acknowledge that in contemporary expert practice, conventional ESD without any traction assistance is rarely used for large semi-circumferential gastric cardia lesions. The present study therefore primarily demonstrates the benefit of incorporating a self-traction strategy over a non-traction approach, rather than establishing the superiority of the BT-ESD technique over existing traction-assisted methods (e.g., clip-with-line, S-O clip, rubber band traction). The observed improvements in procedure time, bleeding, and en bloc resection rate should be interpreted as reflecting the general advantage of providing traction, to which the bridge technique contributes as one feasible and device-free option.

Notwithstanding this limitation, a comparison with existing traction-assisted methods is informative to position the bridge technique within the current landscape. Compared with external traction devices, the BT-ESD technique offers the advantage of rearticular benefit in the conquiring no additional foreign body or dedicated equipment, thereby avoiding device entanglement or interference with endoscopic maneuverability – a pfined gastric cardia space. However, unlike clip-based methods that allow dynamic adjustment of traction direction, the BT-ESD provides a fixed, unidirectional self-traction (from the lesser curvature side). This makes it most suitable for lesions where the major portion extends toward the greater curvature. For lesions involving the entire circumference or those predominantly located on the lesser curvature, alternative techniques (e.g., pocket-creation method or clip-with-line) may be more appropriate. Thus, the bridge technique should be viewed as a complementary option within the armamentarium of traction-assisted ESD, rather than a universal replacement for all existing methods.The present study aimed to evaluate this complementary technique.

We note that Jawaid et al. previously described a “mucosal bridge technique” for ESD at the gastroesophageal junction (VideoGIE, 2019)31. Their method preserves a small bridge of intact mucosa primarily to prevent specimen slippage during dissection. In contrast, our BT-ESD technique deliberately preserves a strip of submucosal tissue (3–5 mm in width) at the lesser curvature side. This submucosal bridge generates sustained elastic traction that actively elevates the dissected mucosal flap, thereby improving visualization of the submucosal plane—a function distinct from passive anchoring. Furthermore, our technique is specifically designed for large semi-circumferential lesions in the cardia, where gravity and angulation compromise the visual field. Thus, despite the shared term “bridge”, the two techniques differ fundamentally in the tissue layer preserved, mechanism of action, and clinical application.

In this study, the BT-ESD technique was developed by comprehensively considering the advantages and limitations of the aforementioned methods. Based on the conventional ESD with complete circumferential incision, for extensive lesions involving more than half of the gastric cardia circumference, the BT-ESD method intentionally preserves portions of the submucosal tissue at the margins during submucosal dissection of the high gastric cardia. This approach not only mitigates the difficulty of marginal incision at later stages but also provides effective traction, prevents lesion sagging, maintains a clear visual field, and reduces fluid loss during the procedure. Our results suggested that, compared to conventional ESD, the self-traction ESD technique significantly reduced procedure time, total volume of fluid injection, intraprocedural bleeding, these all contributed to enhanced visualization during the procedure. Furthermore, the improved surgical field of view also contributes to a reduction in muscularis propria injury, leading to lower rates of complications such as perforation and stenosis, as well as shorter hospital stays and reduced hospitalization costs.

Regarding the en bloc resection rate of 77.5% in the conventional ESD (CESD) group, which the reviewers noted as comparatively lower than rates often reported for gastric ESD in general, we offer the following explanation based on our experience and the specific context of this study. This study exclusively focused on large lesions (involving ≥ 50% of the circumference or ≥ 3 cm in diameter) located in the cardia. The cardia’s acute angle and restricted space pose exceptional technical challenges, particularly for achieving en bloc resection of extensive lesions, especially those extending to the greater curvature where instrument maneuverability is severely limited. In such demanding cases within the CESD group, piecemeal resection was occasionally necessitated to ensure procedural safety and completeness, directly impacting the en bloc rate.

Compared to the CESD group, the BTESD group demonstrated a lower incidence of infection. The observed postoperative pneumonia rate of 22.5% in the CESD cohort requires specific contextualization. All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. The mean operative time in the CESD group was significantly longer than in the BTESD group. Prolonged anesthesia and operative time are well-established independent risk factors for postoperative pulmonary complications, including atelectasis and aspiration-related pneumonia. The higher incidence in the CESD group is strongly correlated with its longer procedure duration. Furthermore, the gastric cardia region’s proximity to the diaphragm and the potential for micro-aspiration of irrigant fluid during prolonged dissection in a challenging position may have contributed. It is important to note that the majority of these cases were mild, diagnosed based on postoperative fever, cough, and radiologic findings, and resolved promptly with standard antibiotic therapy. This rate underscores one of the significant clinical benefits of reducing operative time, as achieved by the BTESD technique.

The development of post-ESD stenosis is primarily associated with the lesion size and the extent of muscular layer injury. This is analogous to the mechanism of stenosis following esophageal ESD32. Compared to other gastric regions, perforations during ESD at the gastric cardia are associated with a lower incidence of peritonitis and relatively milder symptoms. This is likely attributable to the unique anatomy of the gastric cardia. The gastric cardia’s anatomical position high in the stomach minimizes acid exposure and consequent chemical injury. Moreover, its thick sphincteric musculature facilitates perforation closure via contraction, while the overlying gastro-phrenic ligament restricts the dissemination of fluid and air.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the trial was retrospectively registered after patient enrollment had begun, due to insufficient awareness of prospective registration requirements; however, the protocol was finalized before enrollment and all endpoints were objective. Second, all procedures were performed by a single experienced endoscopist at one center, limiting generalizability. Third, the comparator was conventional ESD without traction, which is rarely used in contemporary practice for large cardia lesions; thus, our findings primarily demonstrate the benefit of traction itself rather than superiority over established traction-assisted methods. Fourth, the sample size was relatively small, and larger multi-center trials are needed to confirm the results.

Conclusions

Compared with conventional ESD, the “Bridge Technique” self-traction ESD (BT-ESD) significantly shortened procedure time, improved en bloc resection rate, and reduced intraprocedural bleeding for large semi-circumferential gastric cardia neoplasms. This device-free self-traction method represents a simple and effective option for challenging gastric cardia lesions.
