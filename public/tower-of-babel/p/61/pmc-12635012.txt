Clinical Burden and Healthcare Resource Use Among Adults Living with Hemophilia A: An Observational Study
Authors: Lisa Young, Lu Ban, Yong Chen, Patrick F Fogarty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPeople with hemophilia A (PwHA) may suffer from repeated bleeding in muscles and joints. However, few studies have reported disease burden and healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) in PwHA experiencing bleeding events and/or joint disorders in a real-world setting. The aim of this study was to examine disease burden and HCRU in PwHA via two different categorizations: PwHA with and without medically recorded bleeding (MRB), and PwHA with and without joint disorders.MethodsThis was an observational cross-sectional study of adult male PwHA treated with factor VIII replacement therapy, identified from PharMetrics Plus® claims data between 2010 and 2019. Comorbidities prevalence and HCRU rates were described. Rate ratios (RRs) of HCRU between PwHA with and without MRB, and PwHA with and without joint disorders were estimated using multivariate adjusted Poisson or negative binomial regressions.ResultsThere were 1961 PwHA identified. Of those, 1045 had MRB and 352 had arthropathy. PwHA with MRB showed a higher prevalence of comorbidities and HCRU compared with those without MRB, including inpatient admissions, emergency department visits, outpatient visits, factor VIII replacement therapy, and pain relief medications use. PwHA with MRB also had high bleeding-related HCRU; bleeding-related events accounted for 46% of inpatient hospitalizations. Similarly, PwHA with joint disorders had a higher comorbidity burden and HCRU than those without joint disorders.ConclusionsPwHA with concurrent bleeding/joint disorders had higher clinical burden compared with those without MRB or joint disorders. Treatment approaches to reduce bleeding episodes, and consequently joint damage, in PwHA may reduce clinical burden.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40801-025-00516-8.

Key Points

The study reports a high clinical burden among people with hemophilia A, especially those with concurrent bleeding/joint disorders.The study indicates a lower quality of life among people with hemophilia A and revealed unmet needs for treatments of adult people with hemophilia A.

Background

Hemophilia A is a rare, X-linked genetic disease that leads to deficiency in clotting factor VIII and mainly affects males. The World Federation of Hemophilia’s Annual Global Survey 2020 reported 165,379 people with hemophilia A (PwHA) worldwide [1]. The prevalence of hemophilia A is 17.1 per 100,000 males in high-income countries [2]. The prevalence for severe hemophilia A is 6.0 per 100,000 males [2].

Hemophilia A is characterized by bleeding manifestations and spontaneous bleeding episodes in muscles and joints, which may occur in people with moderate-to-severe disease. The standard of care in PwHA is factor VIII replacement treatment, either on demand for acute bleeding or as prophylaxis to prevent bleeding and related health problems [3, 4]. Although effective, this approach is expensive and involves frequent, lifelong injections. Furthermore, PwHA can develop inhibitors to factor VIII, which prevents the treatment from working [5]. In addition to factor VIII replacement treatment, emicizumab was approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2017 and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in 2018 for PwHA with factor VIII inhibitors. On 4 October 2018, the FDA and EMA approved a label expansion of emicizumab to include PwHA without factor VIII inhibitors.

Although hemophilia A affects only a small portion of the worldwide population, it accounts for substantial economic burden on individuals, healthcare systems, and society [6–10]. Croteau and colleagues reported that the mean total healthcare costs for PwHA in their study was $287,055 (standard deviation [SD] $306,933) per patient per year, and the majority (90%) was attributable to costs related to clotting factor replacement therapy [7]. Factors such as age, presence of inhibitors, and severity impact the cost of hemophilia care [6, 9, 10]. Hemophilia-related complications, such as joint bleeding episodes and joint damage, may also lead to high utilization of medical services and a further cost increase [8].

Currently, real-world evidence on clinical burden and healthcare resource utilization (HCRU) for people with moderate-to-severe hemophilia A is limited, especially in adult PwHA with concurrent bleeding events or with joint damage. To understand the clinical and economic burden on these people (as well as the healthcare system), this study aimed to examine PwHA via two different categorizations: (1) all-cause, bleeding-related, and arthropathy-related HCRU and clinical burden, in PwHA with medically recorded bleeding (MRB) compared with those without bleeding; and (2) all-cause and arthropathy-related HCRU and clinical burden, in PwHA with joint disorders, compared with those without joint disorders. This study provides useful information on the potential benefits of treatment options in development that could prevent bleeding events and/or further joint damage.

Methods

Data Source and Study Population

PwHA were selected from the PharMetrics Plus database [11]. It is a medical insurance claims database that includes pharmacy, hospital, and medical claims for approximately 150 million patients between 18 and 65 years old enrolled across the USA since 2007 [11]. It includes claims for 95 million patients and represents a broad geographic distribution, with data from 90% of USA hospitals and 80% of doctors. The database is largely reflective of the under-65, commercially insured population, with some representation of Medicaid and Medicare patients, though it primarily consists of preferred provider organization (PPO) plans. Relevant information on diagnosis, medication, and comorbidities used International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9/10-CM); National Drug Code; ICD-10-Procedure Coding System (ICD-10-PCS); HealthCare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS); or Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes (identified in Supplementary Table S1). The PharMetrics Plus database was chosen for its larger cohort size after a thorough comparison with other major claims databases available to us, which offer similar population and variable coverage.

The study population consisted of adult men with hemophilia A without factor VIII inhibitors. A cross-sectional study design was utilized, with the study period spanning from January 2010 to December 2019. Patients were selected if they had at least one inpatient or outpatient claim for the diagnosis of hemophilia A and had either one or more claims for clotting factor VIII replacement therapy during the study period, or one or more claims for emicizumab treatment after it was approved for PwHA without factor VIII inhibitors (i.e., on or after 4 October 2018). PwHA were continuously enrolled in the study until death, disenrollment, or 31 December 2019, whichever came earliest (i.e., continuous enrollment coverage). Each patient was required to have at least 1 day of continuous enrollment. Patient age was defined as age at the first claim for hemophilia A diagnosis or treatment during the study period. Patients were excluded from the study if, at any time during the study period, they had claims related to acquired hemophilia, liver cirrhosis (one inpatient claim or two outpatient claims within 30 days), or used any bypassing agent (e.g., Feiba and/or NovoSeven) or factor IX replacement therapy. Moreover, people who used emicizumab before 4 October 2018 were excluded, since emicizumab use during this timeframe was likely for patients who had developed factor VIII inhibitors.

Definition of Bleeding Episodes and Joint Disorders

The presence of bleeding during the study period was defined as hemarthrosis, intracerebral hemorrhage, gastrointestinal bleeding, and other symptomatic bleeds, as listed in Supplementary Table S1. To avoid double-counting, bleeding-related medical claims (either inpatient or outpatient) within 7 days of another claim were clustered into the same bleeding episode. This approach ensured that multiple healthcare encounters for the same bleeding event were considered as a single bleeding episode. [3, 12]. PwHA who experienced at least one bleeding episode during the study period were categorized as PwHA with concurrent bleeding, also referred to as PwHA with MRB.

Joint disorders (i.e., arthropathy) were defined as at least one inpatient claim or at least two outpatient claims at least 30 days apart using ICD codes, as listed in Supplementary Table S1. In addition, information on joint replacement/arthroplasty and synovectomy procedures on the ankle, knee, elbow, and hip were obtained using ICD-9/10-PCS and CPT codes. PwHA who experienced at least one arthropathy-related event during the study period were categorized as PwHA with joint disorders.

Comorbidities

Specific comorbidities were included if they occurred at any time during the study period. Comorbidities of interest were myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, dementia, chronic pulmonary heart disease, connective tissue disease/rheumatic disease, peptic ulcer disease, mild chronic liver disease including chronic hepatitis and fatty liver, diabetes with or without chronic complications, hemiplegia or paraplegia, chronic renal disease, metastatic or non-metastatic cancer, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), hepatitis B or C virus (HBV or HCV), depression, and anxiety. Individual comorbidities from the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) were also considered. CCI conditions with a prevalence greater than 5% within the study population (e.g., mild chronic liver disease) were selected for inclusion, as those with lower prevalence are less likely to account for differences between groups. Relevant ICD-9/10-CM diagnosis codes used to identify these conditions are included in Supplementary Table S1. The number of comorbidities per patient (categorized as 0, 1, 2, or ≥ 3) are also reported.

HCRU Measures

HCRU was measured as all-cause, arthropathy-related, and bleeding-related. All-cause HCRU included healthcare services used for any cause during each patient’s continuous enrollment. In this study, we described healthcare services as inpatient hospitalizations—both admission frequency and the average length of stay (LOS), emergency department (ED) visits, outpatient visits, all-cause pharmacy claims, and pain-relief medication claims.

Arthropathy-related HCRU included healthcare services claims (i.e., inpatient hospitalizations, ED visits, and outpatient visits) with a diagnosis of joint disorders/arthropathy in any diagnosis position in the inpatient or outpatient setting, and/or with joint surgery claims during each patient’s continuous enrollment. Furthermore, medical claims for joint surgery and physical therapy service visits were identified. Relevant ICD, HCPCS, and CPT codes are listed in the Supplementary Table S1.

Bleeding-related HCRU included healthcare service claims (i.e., inpatient hospitalizations, ED visits, outpatient visits, and pharmacy claims) that occurred during the bleeding episodes of each patient’s continuous enrollment, as well as red blood cell transfusions and clotting factor VIII tests. Relevant ICD, HCPCS, and CPT codes are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

All results were presented using two different categories: (1) results among PwHA with and without MRB; (2) results among PwHA with and without joint disorders.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of PwHA. Continuous variables were described using means with SDs, and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Categorical variables were described using frequency counts and percentages.

HCRU was estimated using rate per 100 person-years. In addition, HCRU rates were compared among PwHA with versus without MRB, and among PwHA with versus without joint disorders using multivariable adjusted models. Poisson or negative binomial regression models (with log link function and natural log of follow-up time in years as an offset variable) were applied. Poisson regression was used for rate of inpatient admission and clotting factor tests. Negative binomial regression was used for the rest. Rate ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. When comparing PwHA with and without MRB, multivariable analyses were adjusted for age, geographic region, calendar year, payer type, number of comorbidities, continuous enrollment coverage, and comorbidities (i.e., chronic pulmonary heart disease, mild chronic liver disease, diabetes without chronic complications, nonmetastatic cancer, hepatitis C, depression, and anxiety). Owing to a smaller number of PwHA with joint disorder, only comorbidities that occurred in at least 5% of patients were included in the multivariable adjusted models when comparing PwHA with and without joint disorders.

In subgroup analyses, demographics, clinical burden, and HCRU were also described by age groups: young (19–30 years old), middle-aged (31–44 years old), and older (45–64 years old).

All analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1.

Results

The final study population included 1961 PwHA without factor VIII inhibitors. Of these, 1045 (53.3%) had MRB, and 352 (18.0%) had joint disorders (Fig. 1).

Demographics and Comorbidities of PwHA

Categorized by MRB

The median age of PwHA with and without MRB was comparable (31.0 [IQR: 24.0–43.0] versus 30.0 [IQR: 24.0–38.0]). PwHA with MRB had longer continuous enrollment coverage compared with PwHA without MRB (33.0 [IQR: 16.1–56.0] versus 16.0 [IQR: 9.0–30.0] months).

Overall, PwHA with MRB had a higher comorbidity burden than those without MRB (Fig. 2, Table 1): 21.8% versus 16.9% had one comorbidity, 17.2% versus 9.6% had two comorbidities, and 15.4% versus 6.3% had three or more comorbidities, respectively. HCRU Among PwHA with and Without MRB

HCRU among PwHA with and without MRB are presented in Table 2. PwHA with MRB had higher all-cause HCRU compared with PwHA without MRB. The inpatient hospitalization rate was more than two times higher in PwHA with MRB than those without MRB (adjusted RR: 2.6 [95% CI 2.0–3.3]), while average LOS per inpatient admission was comparable (4.2 [SD: 5.0] versus 4.1 [SD: 3.0] days). Similarly, the rate of ED visits was three times higher for those with bleeding (adjusted RR: 3.1 (95% CI 2.6–3.7) and the outpatient visit rate was 1.5 times higher (adjusted RR: 1.4 [95% CI 1.3–1.6]). Furthermore, the use of pain-relief medications was also more than two times higher among PwHA with MRB than those without MRB (adjusted RR 2.2 [95% CI 1.7–3.0]).

Arthropathy-related HCRU was much more common among PwHA with MRB than without MRB as assessed by inpatient hospitalizations (adjusted RR: 49.3 [95% CI 11.9–203.5]), ED visits (adjusted RR: 64.7 [95% CI 25.8–161.8]), joint surgery (adjusted RR: 3.7 [95% CI 2.5–5.4]), and physical therapy visits (adjusted RR: 28.7 [95% CI 10.2–81.3]) (Table 2).

MRB-related HCRU was observed among 1045 PwHA with MRB during their bleeding episodes (Table 2). The rate of bleeding episodes was 1.2 per person-year (95% CI 1.2–1.3). The rate of bleeding-related inpatient hospitalizations was 6.8 per 100 person-years (95% CI 6.0–7.7), and average LOS was 4.7 days (SD: 5.5; Table 2). Bleeding-related inpatient hospitalization accounted for approximately 46% of the all-cause inpatient hospitalizations. The rate of bleeding-related ED visits was 24.8 per 100 person-years (95% CI 23.2–26.5; Table 2) and bleeding-related ED visits accounted for 40% of the all-cause ED visits.

When stratified by age, all-cause and arthropathy-related HCRU was consistently higher among PwHA with MRB than those without MRB (Supplementary Table S3a, Supplementary Table S3b) in each age group. Furthermore, the rates of all-cause/arthropathy/bleeding-related inpatient admissions, outpatient visits, and medication (as all-cause and pain-relief) increased by age (Supplementary Table S3a, Supplementary Table S3b, and Supplementary Table S3c). However, the rates of all-cause/arthropathy/bleeding-related ED visits had no clear pattern across different age groups (Supplementary Table S3a, Supplementary Table S3b, and Supplementary Table S3c).

HCRU Among PwHA with and without Joint Disorders

HCRU among PwHA with and without joint disorders are presented in Table 3. Overall, all-cause HCRU were higher for PwHA with joint disorders than those without as assessed by rates of inpatient hospitalizations, ED visits, outpatient visits, all-cause and pain-relief medication dispensing. PwHA with joint disorders had an inpatient admission rate twice as high as those without joint disorders (adjusted RR: 2.33 [95% CI 1.82–2.97]), although the average LOS was similar between groups. Rates of other all-cause healthcare services were also higher for PwHA with joint disorders than without them, including ED visits (adjusted RR: 1.65 [95% CI 1.35–2.00]), outpatient visits (adjusted RR: 1.60 [95% CI 1.46–1.75]), all-cause pharmacy dispensing (adjusted RR: 1.47 [95% CI 1.29–1.68]), and pain-relief medication dispensing (adjusted RR: 5.82 [95% CI 4.18–8.11]).

Arthropathy-related HCRU was reported among 352 PwHA with joint disorders. The rate of arthropathy-related inpatient admissions in PwHA with joint disorders was 9.2 per 100 person-years (95% CI 7.8–11.0), and the average LOS was 4.8 days (SD: 4.7). When stratified by age, all-cause HCRU was higher among PwHA with joint disorders than without them in each age group (Supplementary Table S4a). Furthermore, in PwHA both with and without joint disorders, we observed an increased HCRU requirement with increasing age with regards to all-cause and arthropathy-related inpatient admission, outpatient visits, medications (as well as pain-relief medicine), joint surgeries, and physical therapy visits (Supplementary Table S4a, Supplementary Table S4b). In contrast, the rates of all-cause and arthropathy-related ED visits were comparable across different age groups (Supplementary Table S4a, Supplementary Table S4b).

Discussion

The current study reported clinical burden and HCRU in adult PwHA with factor VIII replacement therapy. To better understand the impact of moderate-to-severe hemophilia A (e.g., patients with concurrent bleeding or subsequent joint damage), all results were presented by two categories for PwHA with and without MRB, and for PwHA with and without joint disorders. Among 1961 PwHA, we observed 1045 (53.3%) with concurrent bleeding episodes and 352 (18.0%) with arthropathy during the study period. PwHA with MRB had a higher comorbidity burden and required more HCRU and pain-relief medication compared with PwHA without MRB. A similar pattern was observed among PwHA with joint disorders compared with PwHA without joint disorders. Our findings suggest that there remain unmet needs in treatment of PwHA to reduce bleeding episodes, HCRU, and pain.

Our findings of high healthcare burden among PwHA experiencing bleeding or joint damage aligned with previous studies. Zhou et al. reported that total medical cost in PwHA increased with severity of hemophilia A, with costs over three times higher in severe hemophilia using on-demand treatment, and around five times higher in severe hemophilia receiving prophylaxis, compared with mild hemophilia [6]. In addition, Croteau et al. reported a substantial economic burden associated with managing adults with hemophilia A receiving prophylaxis, with over 90% of total annual healthcare costs attributable to FVIII concentrate dispensed [7].

Our study found that more than half of PwHA (53%) experienced concurrent bleeding during the study period. Among these people, bleeding-related inpatient admissions and ED visits accounted for approximately 46% and 40% of the all-cause inpatient hospitalizations and ED visits, respectively. It suggests a heavy bleeding-related HCRU burden for people with moderate-to-severe hemophilia A, maybe even among those receiving prophylaxis treatment and had good adherence as shown by Warren et al. [13] and Caplan et al. [4]. Furthermore, although we did not explore the patient group with both MRB and joint disorders during the study period, arthropathy-related HCRU was observed much more commonly among PwHA with MRB than people without MRB (Table 2). For example, rates (with 95% CI) of arthropathy-related inpatient admissions, ED visits and joint surgery among PwHA with MRB were 4.9 (4.3–5.7), 12.0 (10.9–13.3), and 7.4 (6.5–8.3) per 100 person years, respectively. In contrast, the rates among PwHA without MRB were 0.1 (0.0–0.4), 0.3 (0.1–0.7), and 2.0 (1.5–2.8) per 100 person years, respectively. It suggested that arthropathy-related HCRU could likely be a consequence of joint bleeding due to hemophilia A.

In the current study, we observed a high prevalence of depression, anxiety, and pain-relief medication use among PwHA with MRB and PwHA with joint disorders. Together with high overall HCRU burden, this evidence may indicate a low quality of life among people with moderate-to-severe hemophilia A, which is in line with previous patient-reported studies [14–22]. In a survey of people with hemophilia in Sweden, Finland, and Denmark, Steen Carlson and colleagues reported that 50% of respondents experienced pain in the past 6 months, and 28% of the respondents had anxiety/depression [22]. O’Hara and colleagues showed that PwHA with joint disorders had worse health-related quality of life than those without joint disorders [19]. In addition, a number of previous studies also reported a high prevalence of anxiety and depression in PwHA [14, 15, 20, 21, 23].

In this study, we also observed HIV and HCV infections among PwHA with joint disorders (13.9% and 12.5%, respectively) and among PwHA with concurrent bleeding (8.5% and 8.0%, respectively). This was in line with the findings from other studies [4, 24, 25]. Caplan et al. reported the prevalence of HIV and HCV to be 9.2% and 19.4% among PwHA, respectively in the USA between 2007 and 2018 [4]. Recht et al. reported the prevalence of HIV and HCV to be 13.7% and 24.1%, respectively among severe PwHA [25].

The study should be interpreted within the context of certain limitations. PharMetrics Plus® is a commercial claims database that only captures clinical events that resulted in medical service use, and HCRU directly related to the covered interactions between enrollees and the third-party payer. For example, the infusion of factor VIII at home or in some specialist care centers or over-the-counter pain-relief products might not be recorded in the database. It may lead to underestimated factor VIII replacement therapy use or pain-relief medication. Although comorbid conditions were identified throughout the continuous enrollment period to comprehensively capture patient claims data and minimize missing information, it is possible that some data gaps persist, particularly regarding nonbillable clinical signs and symptoms. Moreover, as the current study focused on moderate-to-severe hemophilia A, we applied factor VIII replacement therapy as an inclusion criterion. In this case, PwHA missing recorded factor VIII infusion, or those who did not take factor VIII would not be involved. The generalizability of the study may therefore be affected. In addition, we observed that PwHA with MRB and joint disorders had longer average continuous enrollment coverage compared with those without, likely owing to the high cost of switching plans for severe PwHA with complex treatment needs [26] and it may contribute to the higher prevalence of comorbidities in PwHA with MRB and joint disorders. Despite this, O’Hara and colleagues showed that the high prevalence of comorbidities in PwHA with joint disorders was not driven by longer continuous enrollment coverage [19].

Conclusions

In summary, PwHA seeking medical care for bleeding events and/or joint disorders had substantially higher disease burden, as measured by all-cause and disease specific HCRU and higher prevalence of comorbidities compared with those without bleeds and/or joint disorders. Better disease and comorbidities management and improved treatment is needed to help reduce the disease burden, especially in those who experience bleeding and joint damage.
