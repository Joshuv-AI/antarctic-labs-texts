Unresolved questions in pediatric dengue: from disease mechanisms to prevention
Authors: Eduardo Lopez-Medina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of reviewDespite recent advances in understanding dengue infection, substantial gaps remain in its epidemiology, pathogenesis, diagnosis, and prevention, including vaccine development and its long-term impact. This review summarizes recent findings and highlights the most pressing research needs to advance the field and reduce individual and public health burden.Recent findingsDengue burden has increased, particularly among children and adolescents, and it has expanded into previously unaffected regions. Still, current data likely underestimates the true burden due to suboptimal surveillance systems. Improving control will require a better understanding of severe dengue pathogenesis, where key gaps remain, alongside reassessment of diagnostic limitations in the context of increasing vaccine availability. Vaccines represent an important complementary tool, with several candidates under study and two currently in use, each with distinct strengths, limitations, and unresolved questions.SummaryMore extensive research and investment are needed to address the growing burden of dengue, including severe outbreaks and geographic spread. Meanwhile, stronger regulatory engagement in the development and evaluation of innovative therapeutic and preventive measures is essential, particularly in low-income and middle-income regions that bear the highest burden. Clear communication and education on dengue, its impact, and available preventive measures proven to be well tolerated and effective are also critical to strengthen trust, improve uptake, and maximize public health impact.

INTRODUCTION

Despite being recognized by the WHO as one of the top 10 global public health threats [1], dengue remains a neglected tropical disease – endemic across tropical and subtropical regions, disproportionately affecting resource-limited populations, and lacking highly effective treatments and universally accessible preventive measures. While recent work to develop vaccine candidates has advanced the dengue field, unresolved questions remain. Herein, this review summarizes current understanding of dengue epidemiology, pathogenesis, diagnosis, and prevention, describing recent developments, remaining knowledge gaps and research priorities.

EPIDEMIOLOGY

The global burden of dengue has increased dramatically in recent years [2]. Climate change, rapid urbanization, and population growth has expanded the at-risk population, in regions with long-standing climatic suitability as well as in previously unfavorable areas [3]. Data from the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2021, estimated that dengue burden doubled between 1990 and 2021, with increases in annual incidence (26.45–58.96 million cases), deaths (14 315–29 075) and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs; 1.25–2.08 million) [4▪▪]. Under different emissions scenarios, incidence in Asia and the Americas is projected to increase by 49–76% by 2050 [5▪▪].

Globally, the burden of dengue is higher among children and adolescents than in the overall population, highlighted by a recent meta-analysis providing global estimates of dengue incidence and seroprevalence in this population [6]. GBD 2021 study data for children and adolescents (<20 years) showed the highest burden in middle and low–middle sociodemographic regions, with highest incidence in tropical Latin America, greatest DALY burden in Southeast Asia, and a pronounced increase in incidence in high-income North America [7]. While most dengue cases reported in the United States in 2024 were travel-associated [8▪▪], sporadic local transmission has been documented in several states [9], and the risk of further spread may increase with climate warming, urbanization, and global travel [10▪▪].

A clinical trial involving active surveillance of healthy 4 to 16-year-olds in endemic regions of Asia and Latin America, found highest dengue incidence among 4–5-year-olds, with around a quarter (25.4%) of all pediatric dengue cases requiring hospitalization [11▪▪].

In 2023, WHO declared a global emergency, launching a dengue surveillance dashboard [6,12]. Reported cases increased two-fold from 2023 to 2024, reaching 14.1 million [11▪▪]. However, the true burden (as indicated by the GBD 2021 study) is likely much higher due to gaps in surveillance and health data reporting [13▪]. These epidemiological gaps hinder accurate burden estimation and evidence-based decision-making (Table 1). Existing surveillance systems are often fragmented, with insufficient standardization of laboratory methods, reporting frameworks, and data-sharing practices. As a result, they frequently fail to capture the full clinical spectrum of disease, particularly inapparent and mildly symptomatic infections. These limitations restrict the availability of high-quality, age-stratified, population-based data and longitudinal datasets needed to assess temporal changes in incidence and seroprevalence. Thus, our ability to characterize transmission dynamics and the determinants of epidemic severity across diverse settings is limited, which has important implications for the implementation and evaluation of prevention and treatment strategies, as well as for policy development.

DENGUE CLASSIFICATION AND MECHANISMS OF PROGRESSION TO SEVERE DISEASE

Dengue is characterized by a heterogeneous clinical spectrum and unpredictable course. The WHO classifies symptomatic dengue into three categories: ‘dengue without warning signs’, ‘dengue with warning signs’, and ‘severe dengue’ [14]. While useful for identifying cases at risk of progression [15], it oversimplifies disease mechanisms and management needs. Detailed evaluation of patients with ‘dengue with warning signs’ revealed substantial heterogeneity in severity and outcomes, supporting the need for refined categorization [16▪].

Although 60–80% of cases are asymptomatic and only 2–5% progress to severe dengue [2], the absolute number of severe cases is substantial and rising with the global incidence. Severe dengue is characterized by plasma leakage that may lead to shock and can be associated with hemorrhagic manifestations [2]. While the factors driving severe dengue development have not been fully elucidated, antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) is a key immunopathogenic mechanism in which antibodies from a prior infection facilitate viral entry into phagocytes via Fc gamma receptor-mediated endocytosis during secondary heterologous infection, increasing infection efficiency, suppressing interferon signaling and chemokine transcription, and inducing mitochondrial dysfunction [17▪,18▪,19▪▪]. Cross-flavivirus immunity also impacts severity. In a long-term pediatric cohort study in Nicaragua, cross-reactive antinonstructural protein 1 (NS1) immunoglobulin A (IgA) antibodies elicited by primary Zika infection enhanced neutrophil activation, endothelial injury, and vascular damage, increasing the risk of subsequent severe dengue virus (DENV)-2 disease [20▪▪].

Mechanistic studies indicate that severe dengue results from interactions between viral factors, immune dysregulation, and host susceptibility [21]. Infection of multiple immune cell types [e.g. monocytes, B cells, natural killer cells (NK)] drives aberrant cytokine responses, altered differentiation patterns, and impaired viral clearance. Excessive inflammation, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and dysregulated vasoactive mediators disrupt endothelial integrity, promoting capillary leakage [22▪▪], while extracellular vesicles further amplify inflammation and suppress antiviral responses [23▪].

Transcriptional and proteomic analyses show that severe dengue is associated with reduced type-I interferon responses and increased co-inhibitory receptor expression on CD4+/CD8+ T cells and NK cells, reducing cytotoxic capacity. These early T/NK-cell signatures predict severe dengue progression and are more pronounced in overweight/obese individuals [24▪]. Other comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, renal, and cardiovascular disease also increase severe dengue risk in adults [15,25,26▪], while older age is associated with worse outcomes owing to age-related changes in immune function and vascular integrity [27▪]. Increased risk is also observed in high-density living conditions [2] and among children, particularly infants due to maternal antibodies and ADE [28].

DENV serotype can influence disease severity with geographic variation [29▪▪,30]. In a long-term Nicaraguan cohort study, DENV-3 was associated with severe primary infections [29▪▪], while DENV-2 predominated among hospitalized severe cases during Colombia's 2023–2024 epidemic [26▪]. Prior infection is a recognized risk factor for sever [15], particularly with longer intervals between infections [31▪▪], although some pediatric studies report similar severity in primary and secondary infections [32]. These inconsistencies highlight the need to better understand interactions between infection timing, serotype sequence, and serotype-specific immunity and how these impact disease transmission and severity (Table 1).

DIAGNOSIS

Dengue's clinical spectrum [26▪,33▪] and geographic distribution overlap with numerous viral, bacterial, and parasitic infections [34▪,35▪,36,37▪], complicating diagnosis and public health responses. Cross-reaction of antibodies from prior ortho flavivirus exposure can hinder serological assays [38] (Table 1) making robust diagnostic strategies essential for differentiating DENV from co-circulating pathogens [39], particularly in pregnancy during co-circulation of dengue–Zika due to the risk of adverse neonatal outcomes [40–42].

Accurate diagnosis requires appropriate laboratory methods, which vary by infection stage, local epidemiology, co-infections, prior dengue/ortho flavivirus infections, vaccination history, and sample type. While serum is the preferred sample, DENV can also be detected in plasma, whole blood, capillary blood, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, and tissue. During the acute phase (0–7 days), DENV can be detected using reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR), which is highly specific and enables serotype differentiation. Although access remains limited, some multiplex molecular assays now offer near point-of-care alternatives with rapid turnaround [43]. Multiplex fever panels can detect DENV, chikungunya virus, Leptospira spp., and Plasmodium spp. from whole blood, supporting clinical care and surveillance in co-endemic regions [44]. NS1 antigen detection also provides an early diagnostic option, typically up to 9 days after symptom onset in primary infections and up to 6 days in secondary infections. Beyond day 7, serological assays detecting anti-DENV IgM and IgG antibodies can be used, with improved accuracy when paired acute and convalescent samples are tested [45]. Additional tools for surveillance and during outbreak investigations include plaque reduction neutralization tests, multiplex PCR for serotype differentiation, and next-generation sequencing.

Primary and secondary infections show distinct diagnostic profiles. In primary infection, viral RNA and NS1 are detectable early, IgM peaks around 2 weeks and IgG appears later and persists long-term. In secondary infection, NS1 detection may be reduced due to immune complex formation with preexisting antibodies, IgM responses are diminished, and IgG rises rapidly. High IgG titers with detectable viral RNA, NS1, or IgM support secondary infection, and IgM/IgG ratios assist differentiation of primary/secondary infections [39] (Table 2). No universal diagnostic algorithm exists. In resource-limited settings, distinguishing dengue from other febrile illnesses often relies on clinical features and routine laboratory tests. Bleeding, anorexia, skin flushing, and low-platelet and white blood cell counts are associated with dengue, with diagnostic performance improving between days 2 and 5, highlighting the importance of serial assessment [46].

Diagnostic strategies should reflect local dengue and ortho flavivirus epidemiology, vaccination programs, and laboratory capacity. Recent dengue vaccination (TAK-003, Qdenga) may affect test interpretation. From the first dose to 30 days after the second dose, serologic testing is unreliable, particularly in previously exposed individuals. Although IgG, IgM, and NS1 retain high negative predictive value, IgM/IgG profiles have low positive predictive value, and NS1 sensitivity and positive predictive value may remain reduced for up to 18 months postvaccination [47].

DENGUE PREVENTION THROUGH VACCINATION

Vector control, surveillance, early warning systems, and dengue vaccines are complementary tools for dengue control. A variety of vaccine technologies have been investigated, including live-attenuated, recombinant vectors, recombinant proteins, mRNA, and DNA vaccines [54]. The need for a tetravalent formulation that provides balanced protection against all four serotypes remains a major challenge, especially as partial or unbalanced immunity may increase the risk of severe dengue through ADE [2,55]. Although multiple vaccine platforms show promise [56▪,57▪], only live attenuated vaccines have reached advanced clinical development and licensure, and important unresolved questions remain for both current and next-generation dengue vaccines (Table 3). Ultimately, the population-level impact of dengue vaccination depends on public acceptance and uptake. Even highly efficacious vaccines will have limited impact without clear regulatory guidance, targeted education, and strong communication to support provider engagement and community trust [58▪].

Unresolved questions for current and future dengue vaccines

DENV, dengue virus.

Dengvaxia, developed by Sanofi Pasteur, was the first approved vaccine [59]. However, subsequent analyses identified increased risk of severe dengue and hospitalization in vaccinated individuals without prior dengue exposure [60]. Accordingly, WHO recommends prevaccination screening, restricting use of this vaccine to individuals with evidence of prior infection [61]. Logistical hurdles and limited access to rapid testing have reduced its uptake [62].

Clinical development of the Takeda dengue vaccine (TAK-003, Qdenga; Table 4), a live attenuated tetravalent vaccine was extensive, and it remains the only widely available dengue vaccine [63–66]. In the pivotal phase 3 trial, two doses administered 3 months apart with ~4.5 years follow-up showed cumulative vaccine efficacy of 61.2% [95% confidence interval (CI) 56.0–65.8] against virologically confirmed dengue (VCD) and 84.1% (95% CI 77.8–88.6) against hospitalized VCD [67▪]. Vaccine efficacy varied according to both dengue serotype and baseline serostatus. Among seronegative individuals, vaccine efficacy against VCD was 53.5% (95% CI 41.6–62.9) and against hospitalized VCD was 79.3% (95% CI 63.5–88.2). Corresponding estimates among seropositive participants were 64.2% (95% CI 58.4–69.2) and 85.9% (95% CI 78.7–90.7), respectively. Protection was observed against all serotypes in seropositive participants, while in seronegative individuals’ efficacy was demonstrated against DENV-1 and DENV-2 but not DENV-3, and the low incidence of DENV-4 precluded assessment.

Although no major safety concerns emerged, several factors – including the limited number of severe dengue cases, serotype-specific epidemiological variation, and heterogeneity in clinical standards across study sites – constrained outcome evaluation. Specifically, a higher number of hospitalized VCD cases due to DENV-3 was observed among seronegative vaccine recipients (11 in the TAK-003 arm versus 3 in the placebo arm), although this difference was not statistically significant. For DENV-4, only four cases were reported in the placebo group and none in the vaccine group, precluding reliable estimation of vaccine efficacy against hospitalization for this serotype.

Accordingly, a potential risk of disease enhancement associated with DENV-3 and DENV-4 among baseline seronegative TAK-003 recipients cannot be definitively excluded, and the efficacy and safety profiles of TAK-003 against these serotypes remain inconclusive.

The imbalance in hospitalized DENV-3 cases among seronegative vaccine recipients (11 versus 3) was largely driven by higher hospitalization rates at sites in Sri Lanka, likely reflecting local clinical practices. Sensitivity analyses excluding Sri Lanka eliminated this imbalance without affecting vaccine efficacy estimates for other serotypes.

A decline in vaccine efficacy from year 1 (80.9%) [60] to year 2 (56.2%) [65] prompted evaluation of a booster dose administered 4–4.5 years after primary vaccination. At 4.5 years, two doses provided 61.2% vaccine efficacy against VCD [67▪], which increased modestly to 74.3% over 2 years following the booster. Vaccine efficacy against hospitalization remained high, at 84.1% at 4.5 years and 90.6% after the booster dose [68]. Given the limited incremental benefit and programmatic considerations, booster doses are not anticipated.

Based on this evidence, WHO recommends TAK-003 in routine immunization programs in high-transmission settings (regardless of prior exposure), and it is currently licensed in more than 40 countries as a two-dose, subcutaneous schedule administered 3 months apart [69]. While many countries authorize use from age 4, WHO recommends vaccination in children aged 6–16 years. Bridging studies have shown comparable immunogenicity in adults, supporting potential efficacy. However, there is some variation in approved age-range, with some countries authorizing use up to 60 years of age and others with no upper limit [70–75].

Since its first approval in Indonesia in 2022, 18.6 million doses have been distributed in 11 endemic countries [76]. Brazil launched a mass campaign in 2024 for children aged 10–14 years with enhanced pharmacovigilance. Anaphylaxis rates were higher than expected (36.4 per million doses), prompting recommendations for in-facility administration and authorities emphasizing that vaccination benefits outweigh risks when safe practices are followed [77]. Real-world effectiveness in a test-negative study during the 2024 São Paulo outbreak showed vaccine efficacy of 50.2% (95% CI 45–54.9) after one dose and 61.7% (95% CI 39.9–75.6) after two doses, and 67.5% (95% CI 43.4–81.3) against hospitalization after one dose, supporting its role in outbreak mitigation and potential for travelers to endemic regions [78▪▪].

Butantan-DV, the other licensed vaccine, and V181 (Merck), currently under evaluation in a phase 3 trial, are based on the NIH TV003 formulation (Table 4). In a phase 3 trial in Brazil involving 16 235 participants aged 2–59 years, a single dose of Butantan-DV showed acceptable safety, although systemic adverse events were more frequent than with placebo (58.3 versus 45.6%, respectively) [79▪]. Over 5 years of follow-up, vaccine efficacy was 65% (95% CI 57.8–71.0) overall; 58.9% (95% CI 48–67.6) in dengue-naive individuals and 77.1% (95% CI 67.6–83.9) in those with prior dengue exposure. Serotype-specific vaccine efficacy was 73% (95% CI 64.3–79.7) against DENV-1 and 55.7% (95% CI 42.3–66.1) against DENV-2; DENV-3 and DENV-4 were not identified. Given the infrequent occurrence of severe dengue, vaccine efficacy against this outcome by serostatus or serotype has not been described; however, efficacy against a combined outcome of dengue with warning signs or severe dengue was 80.5% (95% CI 50.8–92.4%) [80▪▪]. In November 2025, Butantan-DV was authorized for use within the Brazilian public health system for individuals aged 12–59 years, followed by a pilot campaign in three cities with planned assessment of effectiveness and identification of any unusual adverse events [81,82].Beyond vaccine efficacy and safety, other important challenges must be addressed to maximize the public health impact of dengue vaccination. These include greater standardization in clinical trial endpoint assessment [83▪▪], stronger regulatory engagement – particularly in low-income and middle-income regions that bear the highest disease burden – and targeted educational and behavioral strategies to strengthen physician and community confidence and improve vaccine uptake [58▪]. Harmonizing outcome definitions and strengthening regulatory alignment are essential to improve comparability across studies, support robust evidence generation, and facilitate equitable implementation of new preventive strategies. In parallel, physician-focused initiatives and community-centered communication approaches are needed to address barriers to recommending dengue vaccines, strengthen confidence, and support peer-to-peer advocacy, ultimately maximizing the population-level impact of vaccination.

CONCLUSION

The accelerating global burden of dengue – driven by climate change, urbanization, and shifting serotypes – underscores the need to address knowledge gaps in immunopathogenesis, correlates of protection, risk stratification, diagnostics, vaccination, and long-term impact. The lack of effective antiviral therapies for acute dengue further emphasizes the importance of prevention through vaccination, vector control, and early detection. While an ideal vaccine providing durable, balanced tetravalent protection, irrespective of age or serostatus, is not yet available, promising candidates are under investigation. Meanwhile, maximizing uptake of currently licensed dengue vaccines is an immediate global priority and, when implemented within integrated programs combining surveillance, vector control, and climate-informed early warning systems [84▪], will offer a practical path to reducing severe dengue, hospitalizations, and outbreak intensity worldwide.
