Risk Prediction in Cardio-Oncology: Conceptual and Methodological Considerations
Authors: Jonathan Sen, Eitan Amir, Peter C Austin, Thomas H Marwick, Chris McIntosh, Paaladinesh Thavendiranathan, Husam Abdel-Qadir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRisk prediction models can guide prevention, monitoring, and treatment decisions in cardio-oncology, but their development requires careful attention to methodological challenges unique to cardiovascular disease arising in the context of cancer. In this state-of-the-art review, the authors use case scenarios to illustrate how prediction objectives, index dates, and time horizons must align with specific clinical decisions across the cancer trajectory. They summarize key considerations for data source selection, population definition, sample size, and variable selection and discuss challenges in incorporating treatment data, including immortal time bias and confounding by indication. The authors review regression-based, competing risk, dynamic, and machine learning approaches for model development, along with best practices for evaluating discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Finally, they outline principles for implementation, including workflow integration, transparency, and ongoing model updating. Together, these concepts provide a framework to support the development and adoption of rigorous, clinically meaningful risk prediction tools tailored to cardio-oncology.Key Words: clinical implementation, competing risks, epidemiology, machine learning, model validation, outcomes, prognostic models, risk prediction, treatment exposure, treatment planning

Risk prediction is central to cardio-oncology because cardiovascular risk varies widely among individuals with cancer.1 This heterogeneity arises from traditional cardiovascular risk factors (eg, age, hypertension, smoking, diabetes) and shared pathophysiology between cancer and cardiovascular disease. Moreover, many cancer treatments carry diverse cardiotoxic effects, further compounding cardiovascular risk.

A core task in cardio-oncology is to evaluate the risks and benefits of potentially cardiotoxic therapies to guide personalized treatment decisions. This involves estimating both the likelihood of cardiovascular toxicity and the potential clinical consequences if it occurs (ie, development of overt cardiovascular disease or cardiovascular disease–related mortality conditional on the occurrence of cardiovascular toxicity). These risks must be weighed against the anticipated oncologic benefit, which is often defined by the baseline risk for cancer progression and the expected treatment effect. Cardiovascular risk estimates also inform surveillance strategies and guide the use of preventive interventions before, during, and after treatment with potentially cardiotoxic agents.

A unique challenge in cardio-oncology is determining how cancer therapies under consideration modify the risk for adverse cardiovascular outcomes. The range of cancer treatments currently available requires that prediction models account for factors such as cancer type and stage, treatment regimen, and patient-specific risk factors. Moreover, risk prediction is needed at multiple time points across the cancer trajectory, creating a need for dynamic risk assessment that evolves as therapy progresses.

Despite shared statistical foundations with other areas of medicine, cardio-oncology presents distinctive methodological and clinical challenges that warrant dedicated approaches. Conventional cardiovascular risk models may perform poorly in patients with cancer because they were developed in populations without competing oncologic mortality and do not account for cancer therapies. Moreover, periodic cardiovascular surveillance in some treatment settings (such as HER2-targeted therapies) can bias event detection; the incidence of subclinical cardiovascular events and the prognosis after their recognition may therefore differ from those in a general cardiology population. In the absence of validated risk models, guideline-recommended surveillance strategies often default to uniform, intensive cardiac imaging, even among asymptomatic, low-risk patients. This approach consumes health care resources, increases patient anxiety, and raises the likelihood of false positives without necessarily improving outcomes. Existing general guidance on risk prediction provides a robust statistical foundation but does not address the complexities of cardio-oncology. Consequently, developing and implementing cardio-oncology-specific prediction tools is essential to personalize prevention and surveillance strategies and move beyond current “one-size-fits-all” approaches.

This state-of-the-art review outlines key conceptual and methodological considerations for developing risk prediction tools for cardio-oncology. We begin with clinical case scenarios to illustrate how prediction objectives, index dates, and time horizons vary across the cancer treatment continuum. We then discuss foundational principles, including defining a model’s purpose, selecting appropriate outcomes, and handling competing risks. Subsequent sections address key components of model development, including data sources, candidate predictors, population selection, sample size requirements, and the challenges of incorporating treatment variables while minimizing bias. We then review analytical approaches ranging from regression-based and competing risk methods to dynamic prediction frameworks and machine learning (ML) techniques. We examine model evaluation in terms of discrimination, calibration, internal and external validation, and clinical utility, followed by consideration for implementation. Because the scope of a single review precludes detailed discussion of every relevant statistical concept, we focus on core principles and refer interested readers to the cited references for additional methodological detail.

In case 1, a 38-year-old woman with stage II HER2-positive breast cancer was treated with anthracyclines followed by trastuzumab, both of which are known to cause cancer therapy–related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD). She had a complete response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, corresponding to a low risk for cancer recurrence and cancer-related mortality. Four time points for cardiovascular risk prediction are relevant to this clinical scenario: 1) before initiating anthracyclines (to estimate CTRCD risk from anthracyclines); 2) after completing anthracyclines but before initiating trastuzumab (to assess CTRCD risk specific to trastuzumab and/or longer term CTRCD risk from anthracyclines); 3) during trastuzumab treatment (to assess CTRCD risk with future trastuzumab cycles using data from prior cycles); and 4) after completing trastuzumab (to assess longer term CTRCD risk after treatment).

In case 2, a 62-year-old man with a 4-pack-year smoking history, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia was diagnosed with left-sided stage IIIA epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)–mutant non–small cell lung cancer. Staging computed tomography revealed incidental severe coronary artery calcification. He underwent left upper lobectomy with mediastinal lymph node dissection, followed by adjuvant osimertinib and platinum-based chemotherapy. Locoregional disease recurrence without evidence of distant metastases occurred 18 months after completing initial therapy. Molecular testing confirmed persistence of the original sensitizing EGFR mutation without emergent resistance. He was subsequently enrolled in a clinical trial evaluating dual EGFR-directed therapy with amivantamab and lazertinib, with plans to receive radiation for local control.

This clinical scenario illustrates the need for cardiovascular risk prediction at multiple time points: 1) before surgery (to estimate perioperative cardiac risk); 2) before initiating adjuvant osimertinib, which is associated with cardiomyopathy and QT interval prolongation2; 3) before initiating dual EGFR blockade; and 4) before initiating left-sided radiation therapy. This case also highlights the importance of appropriate cardiovascular risk stratification to preclude inappropriate exclusion from cancer clinical trials.

These scenarios exemplify key choices and challenges in cardiovascular risk prediction in cardio-oncology. In both cases, the data available to estimate cardiovascular risk increase as the patient progresses through cancer treatment. Furthermore, incorporating treatment response into prediction models may improve the estimation of competing risks from cancer-related death before a cardiovascular event, particularly at later time points. Case 2 also highlights that the relevance of cardiovascular risk prediction shifts as competing oncologic risks evolve. Because prognosis in lung cancer is dominated by oncologic outcomes, cardiovascular surveillance may focus more on minimizing treatment interruptions by mitigating drug-induced cardiotoxicity.

Purpose of the Model

A clearly defined prediction objective is one of the most critical design components in risk modeling. Foundational elements that should be specified include: 1) the target population; 2) the outcome(s) of interest; 3) the index date; and 4) the prediction time horizon (including the risk window) (Figure 2). These decisions shape all downstream aspects of model development, from data source selection to variable inclusion, analytical approaches, and eventual implementation.

In cardio-oncology, prediction objectives can vary widely. Predicting the risk for therapy-specific cardiotoxicities is conceptually distinct from predicting broader cardiovascular risk; the relevant predictors, time horizons, and clinical implications differ. The model’s purpose and outcome definitions also determine its clinical application. For example, models designed to guide interventions that directly mitigate treatment-related cardiotoxicity (such as dexrazoxane use, cardioprotective pharmacotherapy, or imaging surveillance during active therapy) typically require shorter time horizons and narrower outcome definitions aligned with the anticipated mechanism of injury (eg, left ventricular dysfunction for anthracyclines rather than atherosclerotic events). Conversely, models intended to inform long-term cardiovascular prevention (eg, blood pressure or lipid management) or post-treatment surveillance in survivors may benefit from broader composite outcomes encompassing multiple modifiable events (eg, myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure [HF]).

Ideally, the outcome definition should align with the intervention it is intended to guide. The pooled cohort equations (PCEs), for example, were designed to guide statin initiation by predicting 10-year atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk (nonfatal myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease death, nonfatal stroke, and fatal stroke).3 Similarly, models developed for long-term cardiovascular risk assessment in cancer survivors may use composite outcomes analogous to established cardiovascular risk scores, allowing the application of familiar thresholds to guide prevention and follow-up. For instance, the BRISK (Breast Cancer Risk) model for women with breast cancer4 used a composite cardiovascular outcome similar to that in the 2008 Framingham risk score, enabling classification into low-, medium-, and high-risk categories using established cutoffs for guiding cardiovascular care.5

It is also essential to consider whether the outcome of interest is subject to detection bias, that is, whether the likelihood of detecting the outcome differs according to patient characteristics or treatments included in the model. This is particularly relevant for subclinical cardiovascular disease that requires diagnostic testing for detection and for which testing frequency may vary by treatment protocols or patient profiles. For example, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration recommends routine cardiac monitoring (eg, echocardiography) every 3 months for patients receiving HER2-targeted therapies such as trastuzumab. This degree of surveillance is not routinely applied to other breast cancer regimens, increasing the likelihood of detecting asymptomatic HF (stage B HF) in patients receiving HER2-targeted therapies. Similarly, patients treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors or other agents known to prolong the QT interval may undergo more frequent electrocardiographic examinations,6 which may increase the detection of low-burden asymptomatic atrial fibrillation. Differences in surveillance intensity can introduce detection bias and may inflate estimates of cardiovascular risk associated with certain therapies if not properly accounted for in model development. Ideally, outcomes should be defined to ensure comparable opportunities for detection across patient subgroups. This may require restricting outcomes to higher threshold clinical events that are less susceptible to variation in surveillance practices, such as unplanned hospitalizations or cardiovascular death.

Temporal patterns also shape model purpose. Cancer therapeutics have distinct early, intermediate, and late cardiovascular risk trajectories, which inform selection of the index date and prediction horizon. Early risks (eg, atrial fibrillation after stem cell transplantation, myocarditis after immune checkpoint inhibitors) call for short-term prediction, whereas late toxicities (eg, radiation-associated valvular heart disease) require long-term estimation. Aligning outcomes and time horizons with the underlying risk trajectory is therefore essential. In patients with favorable long-term prognoses (such as case 1), both short-term therapy-specific and long-term composite risk predictions are clinically relevant. Conversely, near-term outcomes and treatment-specific toxicities may be more applicable in patients with limited long-term survival (such as case 2).

Finally, a clear purpose helps determine whether to build a new model or recalibrate an existing one. Many risk prediction models are never used in clinical practice despite substantial resources invested in their development.7,8 Recalibrating, adapting, or updating existing models to reflect the characteristics of a target population may be more efficient than developing an entirely new model. For example, the Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation 2 model, endorsed by the European Society of Cardiology, was developed using contemporary European cohorts and provides region-specific recalibration across 4 European risk regions (low, moderate, high, and very high risk).9 The Framingham Heart Study and the PCE risk scores have also been recalibrated for use in the Canadian province of Ontario, which had a lower baseline cardiovascular risk than the populations in which the models were developed.10 If a suitable model exists, it may be more pragmatic to recalibrate that model than to develop and validate a new one. That said, it is important to avoid indication drift expanding a risk prediction model beyond its originally intended use.

Competing Risks

Competing risks occur when an event during follow-up precludes the primary outcome of interest from occurring.11,12 This is highly relevant in cardio-oncology because patients who die of cancer cannot subsequently experience cardiovascular disease. This concept was covered in detail in a prior state-of-the-art review in JACC: Cardio-Oncology.13

Consider the natural progression of HF, which typically advances from risk factors alone (stage A) through asymptomatic structural abnormalities (stage B) to symptomatic HF (stage C).14 Many general cardiology patients will follow this trajectory if untreated. In cardio-oncology, however, early cancer-related deaths may truncate this trajectory. Traditional survival analysis methods that censor deaths before the cardiovascular outcome can overestimate the cumulative incidence of cardiovascular disease because censoring treats these patients as if they could still experience the outcome later. Such approaches fail to acknowledge that patients are no longer at risk once a cancer-related death has occurred. In case 2, treating cancer deaths as censoring events rather than competing risks will inflate the estimated incidence of cardiovascular disease by implicitly assuming that some patients who died of cancer would have developed cardiovascular disease after the censoring time. Clinically, accounting for competing risks provides important context for cardiovascular risk estimates and supports a holistic approach that incorporates oncologic prognosis and treatment response. Importantly, a patient with a poor prognosis at diagnosis may become a long-term survivor, making cardiovascular outcomes more clinically relevant. This dynamic reinforces the need to align risk prediction models with appropriate index dates and evolving cancer trajectories.

Data Sources

Risk prediction models in cardio-oncology rely on diverse data that may span multiple institutions and require integration from sources such as administrative databases, clinical registries, and electronic health records. Careful scrutiny of data sources is essential to ensure quality, completeness, validity, and accuracy because these factors affect the generalizability and precision of risk prediction models.

A key distinction is between randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and clinical practice data, often referred to as real-world data (RWD). Well-designed RCTs mitigate channeling bias and confounding by indication through randomization and blinding, improving comparability between patients exposed and unexposed to cardiotoxic therapies. They often include prospectively measured predictors, have fewer missing data, and include adjudicated outcomes, although cardiovascular endpoint adjudication in oncology trials remains inconsistent.15,16 Conversely, RWD include patients with broader comorbidity profiles and greater diversity, making them more relevant for risk prediction in routine practice. RWD can be derived from retrospective or prospective sources. Retrospective data sets (eg, Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results–Medicare in the United States, the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences in Ontario) are large, cost efficient, and broadly representative, making them well suited for models intended for application in routine care.

RCT populations are often healthier than those seen in practice because patients with important comorbidities or pre-existing cardiovascular disease are frequently excluded. As a result, cardiovascular event rates reported in RCTs may underestimate the disease burden observed in RWD. For example, registry analyses of breast cancer patients treated with anthracyclines or trastuzumab have reported higher rates of HF than oncology trials of the same therapies.17,18 However, RWD are more vulnerable to missing or misclassified data, which may particularly affect variables important for risk prediction in cardio-oncology, such as smoking status, exercise, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), or cumulative chemotherapy doses. Prospective cohorts can provide greater clinical detail, including serial imaging and biomarkers, but are usually smaller and more costly and may have limited generalizability.

An emerging and highly promising category includes large-scale data sets (eg, the UK Biobank) that combine prospective data collection with linkage to administrative, genomic, and imaging data. These resources bridge traditional cohort and RWD paradigms by offering deep phenotyping and long-term follow-up at scale. They are increasingly used to study treatment-related cardiovascular risk and to develop multimodal prediction models in oncology. Finally, pooled or harmonized cohorts integrate data across institutions or countries to increase sample size, power, and generalizability. A classic example is the PCEs,3 which were developed from multiple prospective U.S. cohorts and recalibrated for specific populations. Analogously, pooled cardio-oncology cohorts could be particularly valuable for studying rare toxicities or validating models across diverse populations.

A related decision is the population in which the risk prediction model should be developed. The study population should align with the intended target population defined in the prediction objective. For example, the 2 cases presented could be addressed by models developed for patients with all cancers, for the specific cancer under consideration (breast cancer in case 1, lung cancer in case 2), or for the treatment regimen of interest (anthracyclines or trastuzumab in case 1, EGFR inhibitors in case 2). Models derived from heterogeneous populations may improve generalizability but can reduce predictive accuracy, whereas models developed in more homogeneous groups may have more limited applicability in practice.

Choosing Candidate Predictors

Risk prediction models in cardio-oncology rely on diverse data (see Supplemental Table 1).19 Core predictors include demographic and clinical factors (age, sex, race/ethnicity, cardiovascular risk factors, prior cardiovascular disease), cancer-specific variables (cancer type and stage, specific agents and cumulative doses, radiation fields), and cardiac assessment data (LVEF, biomarkers, medications). Emerging predictors include advanced imaging (eg, global longitudinal strain, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging) and circulating biomarkers (troponin, natriuretic peptides, novel protein panels). Future models will likely incorporate genomic or other omics data, although ancestry-specific calibration may be required.20

Although many potential predictors may be available, parsimony and usability are essential for clinical adoption. Each additional variable increases measurement burden, cost, and risk for missing data. Highly complex models with numerous specialized predictors may offer only marginal gains in discrimination or calibration while reducing feasibility in practice. Parsimonious models built from a small set of clinically intuitive, routinely available predictors (eg, age, sex, prior cardiovascular disease, cancer type, functional status) are more likely to be adopted in routine care. Variables that require specialized testing or nonstandard data extraction or that are inconsistently recorded should be de-emphasized (or reserved for secondary analysis). For example, coronary calcification from staging computed tomographic scans may be readily available for patients with lung cancer (as in case 2) but is often unavailable for women with early breast cancer (as in case 1).

Predictor selection should be guided by prior literature, clinical expertise, and the specific purpose of the model. Variables consistently associated with outcomes, or those with strong biologic plausibility, are strong candidates for inclusion. In contrast, predictors selected using automated, data-driven approaches (eg, stepwise regression or P value thresholds) may yield unstable models that perform poorly on external validation.21 Exploratory analyses, literature review, and expert input should form the foundation of predictor selection.22,23 Although initial candidate selection should prioritize biological plausibility, data-driven techniques for dimensionality reduction and weighting can be used to refine the model among these prespecified, clinically meaningful variables. Multicollinearity should be minimized because it undermines interpretability and can lead to unstable estimates in regression models. ML models generally tolerate correlated predictors better, but correlated inputs can still reduce the stability of measures of variable importance.

Statistical methods for variable reduction can complement this process by refining predictor sets and reducing overfitting in high-dimensional settings. Ridge regression shrinks coefficients toward zero and is useful when predictors are highly correlated, although it does not perform variable selection. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator performs both shrinkage and variable selection, improving interpretability in high-dimensional data sets but performing suboptimally when predictors are strongly correlated. Elastic net penalties combine ridge and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator to balance these trade-offs. Penalized regression and ML methods can efficiently handle large predictor sets, but model usability and clinical plausibility should ultimately determine which variables are retained.

Sample Size

When developing and validating risk prediction models, appropriate sample size is essential to ensure statistical reliability. Unlike conventional hypothesis testing, in which sample size calculations are based on the power to detect differences between groups, sample size for risk prediction modeling depends primarily on the number of outcome events and the amount of information per parameter estimated rather than on the sample size alone.

A widely used criterion recommends 10 to 20 outcome events per predictor variable to reduce the risk for overfitting.24 For instance, a model with 10 predictors should ideally be supported by at least 100 to 200 observed events (eg, cases of symptomatic HF). However, simulation studies suggest that the required number of events per predictor variable depends on multiple factors, including the strength of association between predictors and outcomes, the proportion of missing data, outcome prevalence, and model complexity (eg, inclusion of nonlinear terms or interactions).25, 26, 27 Accordingly, researchers are encouraged to use simulation-based approaches or shrinkage-informed sample size calculations when planning model development, especially for high-dimensional models.

Many outcomes relevant to cardio-oncology, such as myocarditis or symptomatic HF, are infrequent, necessitating large administrative data sets, registries, or pooled individual patient data from multiple studies and/or sites.28 The total sample size also matters, particularly for assessing calibration. Models developed in small or demographically homogeneous cohorts may appear well calibrated internally but perform poorly in external populations with different case mixes. A commonly suggested threshold is at least 100 events and 100 nonevents to estimate discrimination and calibrate reliably, although this approach may be overly simplistic.29, 30, 31 Thus, simulation-based approaches tailored to the specific model, desired CIs, and other contextual details are recommended wherever possible.29, 30, 31

A fundamental limitation of conventional risk models is that data generated after the index date cannot be incorporated without appropriate methods. For example, a model intended to predict risk before starting treatment should not use post-treatment biomarkers or imaging. Doing so introduces immortal time bias because only patients who survive and remain event free accumulate these measurements.32 Including these variables can bias estimated associations (often toward underestimation) for predictors measured after the index date.

Setting the index date at treatment initiation allows the inclusion of baseline characteristics and intended treatment plans but does not capture dose modifications, interruptions, or cumulative exposure. Conversely, choosing an index date after treatment initiation (or after treatment completion) allows the incorporation of emergent data (eg, cumulative dose, biomarkers, serial LVEF values), which may improve the prediction of long-term outcomes but cannot guide initial treatment decisions or predict early toxicity. Thus, index date selection should be aligned with the model’s intended clinical purpose: informing pretreatment decisions, guiding surveillance during therapy, or forecasting long-term outcomes (Figure 3). These considerations also underscore the value of dynamic prediction approaches, which are described in the “Analytical Approach” section.

Incorporating cancer treatment as a predictor variable can also introduce confounding by indication. Treatment decisions are influenced by cardiovascular risk, cancer risk, comorbidities, and clinician preferences; these factors can bias the observed association between treatment and cardiovascular outcomes. Including cardiotoxic treatments as predictors, without accounting for the context in which they were prescribed, can underestimate their true cardiovascular risk. Statistical approaches such as multivariable adjustment or propensity scores can partially address these biases but do not eliminate residual confounding from unmeasured variables (eg, frailty, functional status).33 Because it is unlikely that any model can fully account for all determinants of treatment selection, incorporating cancer treatments as predictors often yields biased measures of association.

Consequently, treatment variables should be incorporated cautiously, with thoughtful selection of the index date and careful attention to the clinical context surrounding treatment decisions. If treatment variables are included, the plausibility of the estimated relationships should be examined closely. Conversely, omitting treatment variables may reduce face validity and limit clinical uptake. A reasonable compromise is to develop separate models stratified by treatment regimen, which can yield more valid and interpretable risk estimates within more homogeneous groups. This approach was used for the International Cardio-Oncology Society–Heart Failure Association risk score.34 However, this strategy precludes comparison of cardiovascular risk across therapies.

Analytical Approach

Once variables have been selected and key conceptual considerations addressed, the next step is to choose appropriate analytical methods. The optimal approach depends on the outcome type, whether time-to-event data are available, whether competing risks must be addressed, and whether the goal is static or dynamic prediction. This section outlines analytical techniques used in risk modeling, including their relevance, strengths, and limitations in cardio-oncology.

Before modeling begins, it is essential to perform a thorough review of the baseline data set. This step informs model design, variable selection, and assessment of data integrity. It begins with descriptive statistics to characterize the distribution of candidate predictors, outcomes, and follow-up time. Assessing skewness, outliers, and nonlinear relationships between predictors and outcomes can inform the need for transformation or categorization of variables or the use of cubic splines for model relationships between continuous predictors and outcomes.35 Fractional polynomials can also be used to model nonlinear effects.36 It is also important to assess collinearity (intervariable correlation) to avoid redundancy and instability in coefficient estimates.37

An often overlooked but critical step in model development is assessing the extent and pattern of missing data. There is no formal approach to test whether data are missing at random (or not), so the assessment should be informed by clinical knowledge.38 Where feasible, multiple imputation is recommended to preserve statistical power, improve precision, and reduce bias. The decision to use imputation should be guided by the proportion of missingness, the clinical importance of the variable, and the availability of auxiliary information. Some have suggested using multiple imputation when more than 5% to 10% of values are missing for a key predictor.39

The most widely used approach is multivariate imputation by chained equations,40 although random forest–based and Bayesian hierarchical methods can also be applied depending on the data structure. However, imputation does not recover the missing information, and downstream models may still detect patterns indicating which values were imputed (eg, a mean-derived floating-point age among otherwise integer ages). Interpretation should therefore be cautious, acknowledging that results reflect both observed and inferred data. For variables with high rates of missingness or for which data are likely missing not at random (eg, cardiac biomarkers ordered selectively according to clinical suspicion), sensitivity analyses should be performed to evaluate the impact on model performance and generalizability.

Regression-Based Prediction Models

Regression models are widely used to quantify associations between predictor variables and outcomes. They are well understood and offer transparency, interpretability, and established validation frameworks. Logistic regression is suited to binary outcomes with a fixed time frame (eg, 1-year incidence of symptomatic HF) when censoring is not present, but it does not account for event timing (ie, in a model examining predictors of HF within 10 years, an event occurring at 9 months is handled similarly to an event occurring at 9 years). Time-to-event models quantify associations between predictors and time to an event while accounting for censoring (ie, not all participants are followed through the desired time horizon). Some commonly used time-to-event models assume proportional hazards; this assumption should be assessed, although modest violations may not meaningfully affect predictive performance.41

In the setting of competing risks, cause-specific hazard models estimate covariate effects on the instantaneous hazard of the event of interest among individuals who are currently event free. For absolute risk prediction with competing risks, cause-specific hazards can be used to estimate the cumulative incidence function, but this requires modeling the cause-specific hazard for both the event of interest and competing events and then combining these hazards to compute cumulative incidence. Fine-Gray models directly target the cumulative incidence function for the event of interest within a single modeling framework. Also known as subdistribution hazard models, they are designed to account for competing risks when estimating the association between covariates on the cumulative incidence of the outcome of interest. This enables estimation of absolute risk in the presence of competing events, which is important for informing clinical decisions in cardio-oncology.41

However, if cumulative incidence is to be estimated simultaneously for all event types, cause-specific hazard models may be preferable because the sum of predicted probabilities from Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models may exceed 1.42 Moreover, HRs and CIs from Fine-Gray models can be less straightforward to interpret. This limitation is more relevant for etiologic research questions than for risk prediction, in which the primary goal is accurate estimation of absolute risk rather than causal inference.

Despite these limitations, Fine-Gray models remain well suited to generate clinically relevant cumulative incidence predictions for a primary event of interest (such as HF). Although variable selection for Fine-Gray models remains an area of ongoing research, a pragmatic strategy is to use cause-specific models to explore predictor-outcome associations and then fit a Fine-Gray model to generate absolute risk predictions.

Dynamic Prediction Models

As illustrated in the 2 case examples, the evolving nature of cardiotoxicity makes dynamic prediction particularly relevant in cardio-oncology.43 Rather than relying solely on baseline variables at a fixed index date, these approaches incorporate time-updated information to refine risk estimates as treatment progresses. The most basic application of this concept models covariates in cause-specific hazard models as time dependent. This allows the hazard of an event to be updated as new biomarker or imaging values become available. Although this approach is relatively straightforward, it generally cannot be used to estimate absolute risk that accounts for covariate changes over time. It also assumes that the updated covariate value exerts its effect instantaneously.

More sophisticated approaches are needed to incorporate information after the index date. Landmarking defines new “time zero” points at prespecified intervals (eg, after every 2 trastuzumab cycles). Risk models are reestimated at each landmark date using covariate information up to that time (such as the most recent LVEF value). Landmarking is simple to implement and computationally efficient, but its predictions are conditional on having remained event free up to the landmark; patients who experience events before a given landmark are excluded from analyses at that landmark.

Joint models integrate longitudinal data with time-to-event outcomes.44 The most common joint model approach simultaneously fits a mixed-effects submodel for a longitudinal biomarker (eg, LVEF decline, global longitudinal strain trajectory, troponin rise) and a survival submodel for the event of interest (eg, CTRCD), linking them through shared random effects. This framework can capture both the trajectory and the cumulative burden of change in the predictor over time. Unlike landmarking, joint models do not exclude early events. Because of the assumptions underlying mixed-effects submodels, joint models are particularly useful when predictors are measured intermittently, as is common in cardio-oncology (eg, LVEF, biomarkers). Clinicians can therefore update a patient’s cardiovascular risk at follow-up visits using a unified model, rather than separate baseline and time-varying models.

Multistate models extend survival models by allowing patients to transition between multiple states (eg, from normal cardiac function to subclinical injury to overt HF).45 Transition intensities (hazards) can be estimated for each pathway, enabling the prediction of intermediate as well as terminal outcomes. Multistate approaches can be useful in cardio-oncology settings in which subclinical injury leads to clinically overt CTRCD in some patients and recovery of cardiac function in others. They are also well suited to modeling competing risks.

Machine Learning

ML techniques can model complex, nonlinear relationships and interactions without requiring explicit prespecification.46 ML holds promise for improving predictive accuracy beyond traditional regression models in cardio-oncology, in which patient characteristics, cancer types, and cardiovascular risk factors intersect in multifaceted ways.47 Multiple algorithms can support cardio-oncology risk assessment (Supplemental Table 2). For binary outcomes (eg, incident chemotherapy-induced HF), tree-based ensemble methods such as random forests and extreme gradient boosting can provide a practical baseline because of their robustness and relative interpretability.48,49 Deep learning algorithms, including attention-based transformers and graph neural networks, can achieve state-of-the-art performance when longitudinal or multimodal data are available. Time-to-event prediction can be addressed using random survival forests50 or deep learning–based survival models such as DeepSurv.51,52

Despite their potential, ML approaches face technical, clinical, and methodological challenges. As with conventional models, ML performance depends on the availability of high-quality, comprehensive training data.47 A key issue is outcome imbalance (also called class imbalance), which arises when 1 outcome (eg, absence of cardiotoxicity) occurs much more frequently than the other (cardiotoxicity present). Without correction, ML algorithms trained on imbalanced data tend to predict the majority class for most patients, achieving high overall accuracy while failing to identify the minority class of clinical interest.

Strategies to address outcome imbalance include resampling techniques (eg, oversampling the minority class with synthetic minority oversampling technique, undersampling the majority class), cost-sensitive learning (assigning a greater penalty for misclassifying minority-class examples), specialized loss functions (eg, focal loss, which down-weights easy examples), and ensemble methods that combine multiple weak classifiers to improve performance.53,54 Each of these approaches carries trade-offs in generalizability and interpretability. For example, oversampling may lead to overfitting to synthetic examples, whereas aggressive undersampling discards potentially informative data. The optimal strategy depends on the clinical context and the model’s purpose. For instance, a model designed to flag patients at high risk for cardiotoxicity may prioritize sensitivity (reducing false negatives), whereas models used to guide cardiovascular surveillance in survivors may prioritize specificity (reducing unnecessary testing). These choices should reflect clinical priorities, patient safety, and resource constraints, underscoring the need for close collaboration between data scientists and frontline clinicians.55

Most institutions currently lack the infrastructure to support the level of data integration needed to fully realize the potential of ML. Emerging strategies may partially address these limitations, including natural language processing to extract clinical variables from unstructured notes, transfer learning approaches that leverage models pretrained on large external data sets, multitask networks that jointly predict related cardiac endpoints, and federated learning frameworks that enable cross-institutional model development without data sharing.56 External validation across institutions with different documentation practices, patient populations, and treatment protocols remains essential to ensure model generalizability and clinical utility. ML and conventional statistical methods are not mutually exclusive and can be synergistic. For example, ML can be used to identify or distill high-dimensional features into simplified, interpretable scores that can then be incorporated into traditional regression frameworks to generate calibrated, clinically meaningful risk predictions.

Model Evaluation

Once a risk prediction model has been developed, it must be evaluated to determine the accuracy of its predictions, whether it provides clinically meaningful information, and whether it is applicable to the target population. Evaluation includes internal validation (performance within the development data set) and external validation (performance in a distinct data set or setting).57 Key criteria include discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility (see Figure 4). Evaluations should be tailored to challenges common in cardio-oncology, including competing risks for cancer-related mortality, low event rates, and the dual goals of preserving cardiovascular health while supporting effective cancer therapy. Ultimately, success hinges not only on statistical performance but also on clinical impact: does the model meaningfully inform decisions and improve outcomes for patients with cancer at risk for cardiovascular complications?

Internal Validation

Internal validation assesses model performance in the same data set used for model development.57 This can help identify overfitting, when a model performs well on training data but poorly in new data. A common but suboptimal approach is split-sample validation, which divides the data set into training and testing subsets (eg, 70:30).58 The model is developed on the training set and then evaluated on the hold-out testing set using discrimination and calibration metrics.

Although conceptually straightforward, split-sample validation is statistically inefficient because it reduces the number of events available for both model development and validation, leading to unstable and potentially biased estimates of model performance. Accordingly, this approach is discouraged for small or medium-sized data sets or when event numbers are limited.26 This applies to many cardio-oncology settings, in which outcomes may be rare. In the 2 case scenarios presented, symptomatic HF is expected to occur in <5% of patients in the year after treatment.

Resampling methods are therefore preferred for internal validation. Bootstrapping repeatedly draws samples with replacement from the original data set, fitting and evaluating the model in each iteration.58 Another common approach is k-fold cross-validation, in which the data set is partitioned into k subsets. The model is trained on k − 1 subsets and tested on the remaining subset, cycling through all partitions.59

External Validation

External validation evaluates a model’s performance in an independent data set, ideally with both independent patients and independent data acquisition.57 The validation data set is ideally drawn from a different institution, time period, or geographic region. External validation is particularly important in cardio-oncology because there is substantial heterogeneity in cancer types and stages, treatment protocols, supportive care practices, cardiovascular surveillance strategies,60 tolerance for permissive cardiotoxicity,61 and use of primary prevention interventions for cardiovascular toxicity (eg, dexrazoxane).62 As a result, a model that performs well in one setting may not perform well in another. Validation across diverse cohorts enhances credibility, supports broader implementation, and can identify where local adaptation is needed. Despite its importance, external validation is often omitted because of the logistical and collaborative demands of accessing independent data sets.

A related consideration is model transportability, that is, the extent to which a model maintains its performance across different time periods, health care systems, patient demographics, and clinical settings. Evaluating transportability through external validation can help determine whether a model is fit for broader dissemination or requires local recalibration prior to clinical integration.

Model Performance

Evaluation of model performance requires assessment across 3 key domains: discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility.63 When competing risks are present, traditional performance metrics often require adaptation.

Discrimination refers to a model’s ability to distinguish between individuals who experience the outcome and those who do not. For binary outcomes, discrimination is typically summarized using the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve, which is equivalent to the C statistic. This metric reflects the probability that a randomly selected individual who experienced the outcome has a higher predicted risk than a randomly selected individual who did not.63 For time-to-event outcomes, censoring complicates this comparison, and specialized metrics are needed. The Harrell C index estimates the probability that, among all comparable pairs of individuals, the patient with the higher predicted risk experiences the event earlier.64 In competing risk settings, discrimination should be computed using methods that account for competing risks. Time-dependent area under the curve is another option for time-to-event discrimination, evaluating discriminative accuracy at specific time points.65 Following common conventions, C indexes of 0.60 or less are considered poor, 0.61 to 0.70 moderate, and >0.70 good.63,66 Models should not be compared solely on the basis of these discrimination metrics; likelihood-based measures such as the model chi-square statistic should also be considered.66

These metrics should also be interpreted alongside event rates because models with high discrimination may have limited clinical utility when the event rate is very low. Measures of discrimination are influenced not only by predictive performance but also by the heterogeneity of the test population. For example, a model evaluated in a population of men and women 18 to 100 years of age will typically show higher discrimination than the same model tested in a cohort of women 65 to 90 years of age. This occurs because greater variation in baseline risk increases the ability of the model to differentiate between individuals who do and do not experience events. Consequently, lower discrimination during external validation does not necessarily indicate poor model quality and may instead reflect differences in the underlying population spectrum.

Calibration evaluates agreement between predicted event probability and observed event incidence.63 Calibration plots are commonly used to visualize this agreement. One approach is to divide the sample into categories of predicted risk (eg, quintiles or deciles), calculate the observed incidence within each category and compare it with the mean predicted risk for that category. In the presence of competing risks, observed probabilities should be estimated using the Aalen-Johansen estimator of the cumulative incidence function rather than the complement of the Kaplan-Meier estimate. Smoothed calibration curves generated using flexible methods such as locally weighted scatterplot smoothing can provide continuous assessment across the risk spectrum.63,67

Other measures can supplement calibration plots. Calibration-in-the-large assesses whether predictions are systematically too high or too low overall.68 The calibration slope measures whether predicted risks are overdispersed or underdispersed, with a slope of 1 indicating perfect calibration. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test, although historically common, is not recommended for modern risk prediction because it depends on arbitrary grouping and has poor behavior in large data sets (in which it often rejects well-calibrated models).69

Clinical Utility

Assessing clinical utility often requires selecting risk thresholds at which specific actions are warranted. This can be challenging in cardio-oncology because intervention thresholds may be uncertain and may vary with patient preferences and competing clinical priorities, such as cancer prognosis. In the case scenarios provided, case 1 may benefit from a model that identifies even modest cardiovascular risk to guide early preventive strategies, whereas in case 2, changes in treatment may be justified only at very high predicted risks. In the absence of trial-based thresholds, decision curve analysis (DCA) and expert consensus may be used to inform cutpoints. DCA evaluates the net benefit of a prediction model across a range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities.70 It weighs true positives against false positives and illustrates whether a model provides greater clinical utility than default strategies such as “treat all” or “treat none.” Unlike single-number summary metrics, DCA is interpreted as a curve because different institutions or clinicians may prioritize different thresholds (eg, high sensitivity vs high specificity), and no single summary statistic captures these varying preferences.

The net reclassification index and integrated discrimination improvement are commonly used to assess whether a new model or predictor improves risk classification relative to an existing model.71 The net reclassification index quantifies how many individuals are reclassified into more appropriate risk categories with a new model compared with an existing one, whereas integrated discrimination improvement captures the overall improvement in average predicted risk for cases vs noncases. Although these metrics are intuitive to clinicians, their use has been increasingly discouraged by methodologists because of concerns about instability, inflation of performance gains, and dependence on arbitrary cutpoints.72, 73, 74 Nevertheless, they may provide complementary insights when used judiciously and interpreted alongside, rather than in place of, discrimination and calibration metrics.

Ultimately, the true utility of a model depends on its ability to guide clinical decisions and improve outcomes, ideally demonstrated through prospective validation or pragmatic trials.75 Prospective validation can demonstrate that a model retains its predictive performance when applied to new patients under routine clinical conditions and can reveal biases that may not be apparent during development.76 Pragmatic trials go a step further by embedding the model into actual care pathways, randomizing clinicians or sites to use the algorithm vs standard practice, and measuring outcomes such as mortality, complications, or resource use.77 These studies also capture implementation factors, such as workflow integration, clinician adherence, and patient acceptability, that are not available in retrospective analyses. By showing that the model not only predicts risk but also translates into measurable improvements in decision-making and health outcomes, prospective and pragmatic evaluations provide the strongest evidence for its real-world value.

Model Implementation

Despite the proliferation of risk prediction models in cardio-oncology,4,34 a substantial implementation gap persists between model development and routine clinical use. Dissemination often relies on conventional knowledge translation approaches, including publication in peer-reviewed journals and presentation at medical conferences. Transparent reporting is critical to support reproducibility and adoption. The Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis guidelines69 provide a framework for reporting model development, validation, and performance, and the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool is used to assess risk for bias and applicability of risk prediction models.78

Effective integration into clinical workflows requires thoughtful planning and stakeholder engagement (Table 3). Because many institutions lack dedicated cardio-oncology services, implementation strategies should be adaptable to local resources and incorporated into existing cardiovascular and oncology care structures. Ideally, implementation begins with a pilot phase, enabling iterative refinement of protocols on the basis of institutional experience. Medicolegal considerations also warrant attention to clearly define the model’s intended role, limitations, and clinician accountability.

Seamless implementation would involve embedding risk algorithms into electronic health records, triggered at key clinical touchpoints, such as pretreatment consultations, baseline assessments, and surveillance visits. Model inputs would ideally be autopopulated from existing data, with prompts to collect missing data. However, this level of integration remains aspirational in many settings, and stand-alone calculators or semiautomated tools remain the most common mode of implementation, despite being less efficient and harder to sustain.

A common approach is to convert coefficients from a fitted regression-based risk prediction model into a point-based system.4,79 Similar approaches can use risk ratios or assign weights on the basis of clinical judgment (eg, when empirical data are limited or expert consensus is prioritized), although regression coefficient–based weights are preferred for mathematical accuracy.

Patient engagement is essential for successful implementation. Risk prediction outputs should be communicated in clear, accessible language, such as “Your estimated 5-year risk for HF with this treatment is X%.” This can help patients understand potential cardiovascular risks and support shared decision-making around the complex trade-offs between cancer control and cardiovascular safety.

Over time, evolving cancer therapies and preventive strategies may lead to calibration drift, in which predicted risks no longer reflect current outcomes. Periodic reevaluation is therefore necessary, and models should support ongoing updating where feasible. Implementation also requires attention to ethical and equity implications. Models trained on data sets that underrepresent women, ethnic minority groups, and older adults may underperform in these populations, potentially exacerbating existing health care disparities. Furthermore, opaque models (eg, ML-based models) raise concerns regarding accountability, liability, and informed consent. This is particularly relevant in high-stakes decisions, such as withholding optimal cancer treatment on the basis of model predictions of cardiovascular disease risk.

Regardless of how they are developed, models should report measures of uncertainty (eg, CIs for discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility metrics) to convey the precision of model performance estimates. Making uncertainty explicit helps clinicians interpret predictions appropriately and supports more nuanced, individualized treatment and survivorship decisions.

Conclusions

Risk prediction in cardio-oncology presents unique challenges at the intersection of 2 complex specialties. Developing models that dynamically reflect evolving risk across the cancer treatment continuum, account for competing risks, and remain applicable to diverse clinical environments has substantial potential to improve practice. This requires careful attention to the conceptual and methodological considerations for model development outlined in this review and summarized in the Central Illustration. Ultimately, risk prediction tools should be designed to support, rather than replace, clinical judgment, enabling clinicians and patients to make informed decisions that balance cardiovascular safety with optimal cancer outcomes and align with individual values and preferences. Achieving this vision will require sustained collaboration among experts in oncology, cardiology, data science, and implementation science to ensure that these tools are scientifically rigorous and practical.
