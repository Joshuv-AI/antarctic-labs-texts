Static cold perfusion solutions in clinical limb transplantation: a systematic review
Authors: Yiji Tu, Huizhi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:To summarize the application of perfusion solutions in clinical limb transplantation procedures, to analyze the outcomes between using different perfusion solutions, and to provide recommendations for future research.Methods:Our current study was carried out in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement. Articles were acquired from multiple databases (EMBASE, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus) by using key words or terms such as “perfusion solution,” “preservation solution,” “allograft,” “allotransplantation,” “transplantation,” “extremity,” and “limb.” Clinical studies with the description of perfusion solutions for donor limb procurement were included. We evaluated our systematic review compliance by using A Measurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews 2 criteria and fell in moderate quality. Perfusion solution parameters and outcomes were extracted and analyzed. Data analyses were performed using SPSS version 19.0. This study was conducted without using artificial intelligence tools in accordance with the TITAN Guidelines 2025.Results:Nineteen clinical studies encompassing 47 patients were enrolled. In clinical practices, the application rate of cold University of Wisconsin (UW), histidine–tryptophan–ketoglutarate (HTK), St. Thomas, and Institute Georges Lopez-1 (IGL-1) solutions for limb preservation was 70.21% (33/47), 14.90% (7/47), 8.51% (4/47), and 6.38% (3/47), respectively. Postoperatively, the occurrence of at least one episode of immunological rejection was reported in 9 studies (7 with UW perfusion, 1 with UW&IGL-1 perfusion, and 1 with HTK perfusion) encompassing 25 patients (25/47, 53.19%). Amputation was reported in one hand transplantation case, and death was reported in one quadruple limb transplantation case.Conclusion:Despite the predominant use of cold UW solution in clinical limb transplantation, robust comparative evidence demonstrating its superiority over alternative solutions (HTK, St. Thomas, and IGL-1) remains lacking. The current literature suffers from insufficient sample sizes and non-standardized outcome measures, precluding definitive conclusions about optimal perfusion strategies. Only through large-scale, multicenter clinical trials with standardized protocols can we establish evidence-based guidelines for future limb preservation.

Introduction

Since the first successful hand transplantation performed by Dubernard et al in 1998[1], hundreds of vascularized composite allotransplantations (VCAs) involving upper and lower limbs have been conducted worldwide[2]. According to a recent study by Milek D et al, acute and chronic immunological rejection occurred in 89% and 11% of patients receiving facial and upper extremity VCAs, respectively[3]. Another challenge to VCA is ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), a consequence of multiple pathophysiological processes such as endothelium damage, inflammatory response, and oxidative stress upon the restoration of blood flow to the allograft after ischemia[4].

Static cold storage remains the predominant preservation method for organs and limbs by suppressing cell metabolism and oxidative stress under hypothermia conditions[5]. Various cold preservation solutions have been developed and clinically used, with the University of Wisconsin (UW) and histidine–tryptophan–ketoglutarate (HTK) solutions being the most widely used[6,7]. Initially developed by Wahlberg et al in the late 1980s for pancreas preservation[8], the UW solution is now extensively used for organ and limb preservation. Unlike the UW solution – an intracellular-type solution with high potassium and low sodium concentrations, the HTK solution is an extracellular-type solution characterized by low potassium and high sodium levels[9]. Additionally, the normothermic perfusion has been explored in numerous clinical and experimental transplantation studies, demonstrating benefits such as maintaining physiological cell metabolism and mitigating hypoxia- and hypothermia-induced deleterious effects[10–12].

Recently, Muss TE et al conducted a systematic review of preclinical studies on perfusion protocols in limb VCA[13]; however, the clinical selection of perfusion solutions during the donor limb procurement remains poorly documented. The present study aims to summarize the application of perfusion solutions in clinical limb transplantation, to compare the outcomes with different perfusion solutions and to provide evidence-based recommendations for future research.

Methods

Literature search and article selection

Our current study was carried out in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement[14]. Methodological quality was evaluated using A Measurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews 2 criteria, with our review rated as moderate quality[15]. This study was conducted without using artificial intelligence tools in accordance with the TITAN Guidelines 2025[16].

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Web of Science using key words or terms such as “perfusion solution,” “preservation solution,” “allograft,” “allotransplantation,” “transplantation,” “extremity,” and “limb.” The final search was performed in February 2025, with no restrictions on publication date. Exclusion criteria were (1) non-English publications, (2) experimental studies, reviews, comments, conference abstracts, articles with duplicated data, and (3) studies lacking clear documentation of perfusion solution protocols.

Data extraction

The following data were extracted: date of transplantation, sex and age of recipient and donor, transplanted limb, perfusion and storage of allograft, ischemia time, immunological rejection, and transplantation failure. Study quality was appraised using the methodological tool developed by Moga et al[17]. Two reviewers independently extracted the data of interest from each included study. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through consensus-based discussion.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all the variables. Continuous variables were reported as mean values and standard deviations and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages. A P-value of <0.05 indicated statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 19.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Perfusion solutions and clinical outcomes

In clinical practices, most teams used cold UW solution (4°C) for donor limb perfusion before transplantation. UW was used in 33 patients (33/47, 70.21%), followed by HTK in 7 patients (7/47, 14.90%), St. Thomas in 4 patients (4/47, 8.51%), and cold Institute Georges Lopez-1 (IGL-1) in 3 patients (3/47, 6.38%).

No clinical evidence of immunological rejection was reported in 5 studies (all with UW perfusion) encompassing 5 patients (5/47, 10.64%). Occurrence of at least one episode of immunological rejection was reported in 9 studies (7 with UW perfusion, 1 with UW&IGL-1 perfusion, and 1 with HTK perfusion) encompassing 25 patients (25/47, 53.19%).

Postoperative complications were reported in only three studies[18,21,29], including two cases (with UW perfusion) of skin necrosis, which healed uneventfully and one case (with HTK perfusion) of a nonhealing wound, which led to amputation. In the study of Nasir et al[31], the patient who received quadruple limb transplantations (with UW perfusion) died on the fourth day postoperatively due to severe metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, and hyperphosphatemia.

Discussion

Our systematic review highlights that static cold perfusion with UW solution (4°C) remains the predominant method in clinical limb transplantation, accounting for about 70% of cases, while HTK, St. Thomas, and IGL-1 solutions collectively represent the remaining 30%. Postoperative immunological rejections and surgical complications were reported in several studies. Unfortunately, pooled analyses based on those data were unreliable due to the lack of unified evaluation criteria among studies.

IRI in limb transplantation

IRI occurs in a wide range of disorders such as myocardial infarction, stroke, traumatic shock, and diabetic retinopathy[36–39]. Besides, IRI is inevitable in some surgeries such as coronary artery bypass, solid organ transplantation, and VCA. In recent decades, VCA has been carried out in various types of vascularized tissues, including limbs, face, uterus, and tongue[14]. Unlike solid organ transplantation, VCA contains a wide range of tissues with very different resistance to ischemia. Ischemia time within hours will lead to irreversible injury in muscle, while skin can be grafted after weeks of storage at 4°C[40]. Prolonged ischemia and hypoxia in amputated limbs lead to a wide range of intracellular metabolic disturbances, while the reintroduction of blood and oxygen after limb transplantation further aggravates those metabolic disturbances and causes additional tissue injuries. The cell biology of IRI, which is beyond the scope of our current review, has been extensively described in the study of Kalogeris et al[41].

Immunological rejection in limb transplantation

Immunological rejection remains to be the major postoperative concern in transplantation individuals. It is known that skin is the critical target of alloimmune responses in VCA, and the incidence of acute immunological rejection in hand and face transplantation exceeds 80%[42,43]. Pathologically, the occurrence of immunological rejection is closely associated with IRI. Studies show that during IRI, the tissue damage-induced release of endogenous molecules was recognized by the cells of innate as well as adaptive immune response, followed by the manifestations of acute and chronic rejection[4]. The use of immunosuppression drugs and the occurrence of immunological rejection were described in detail in every human limb transplantation study. Current strategies to prevent immunological rejection in VCA, which is also outside the scope of our current review, have been discussed elsewhere[3].

UW solution and alternatives for cold perfusion (CP)

It is known that every 10°C temperature drop slows down the cellular metabolism by 1.5–3 folds, which indirectly decreases the ischemia-induced tissue damage. While cooling alone or perfusion with saline or Ringer’s solution is insufficient for maintaining the electrolyte balance of the allograft and preventing cell acidosis and swelling with prolonged ischemia duration. Thus, hypothermic storage combined with intravascular perfusion using preservation solution has been the standard protocol in transplantation procedures.

The UW solution has been widely used in clinical settings for organ preservation, providing a certain foundation for its use in limb transplantation[44]. However, the UW solution is not without limitations. First, the UW solution is expensive, which can be a barrier in some settings. Besides, it is primarily designed for cold storage and may not be ideal for normothermic extracorporeal machine perfusion (EMP). Third, the UW solution is a “one-size-fits-all” approach and may not fully address the unique metabolic demands of all limb tissues, such as peripheral nerves. HTK, St. Thomas, and IGL-1 solutions each offer unique advantages, such as lower viscosity, cost-effectiveness, and improved buffering capacity, making them viable alternatives to the UW solution in certain contexts. However, their use in limb transplantation requires further research and validation to ensure optimal outcomes.

EMP in limb transplantation

EMP has been recently used for the clinical preservation of heart, lung, liver, and kidney in transplantation practices[45]. Different from static CP, EMP provides real-time functional and clinical assessments of the perfused organs. Besides, the continuous perfusion flow helps to improve the cellular ion and signal homeostasis and remove the blood cells and leukocytes from the microcirculation of donor organs[46]. EMP can be maintained with normothermia or hypothermia. It is thought that near-normothermic perfusion might be beneficial to the functional recovery of transplanted limbs compared to hypothermic perfusion by minimizing peripheral vasoconstriction and shunting[47]. A variety of experimental studies investigating the use of EMP in limb transplantation have been conducted[48–51]. Recently, Figueroa BA and Rezaei M et al investigated the role of normothermic red blood cell-based perfusion in limb transplantation with Yucatan minipig and human cadaver forelimbs[52,53], indicating that normothermic EMP could be a promising technology in future clinical limb transplantation.

Limitations and perspectives

We found several limitations existed in those enrolled studies, as well as in our current review. First, our literature search was conducted with the assumption that all relevant studies would be collected via the selected databases (EMBASE, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus) and a predetermined set of search terms. Besides, we excluded studies published in non-English languages. The selection bias of our current review decreased the comprehensiveness and reliability of the pooled data analysis. Third, we failed to compare the limb preservation effect of different perfusion solutions due to a lack of relevant comparative data. Moreover, limb transplantation is performed by a limited number of institutions, and on a significantly smaller scale than solid organ transplants. Most of the relevant clinical studies were published as case reports or series with significant heterogeneity, making the comparison results at a low level of evidence.

Based on our findings from the enrolled studies, the reasons why no optimal perfusion solution exists for limb transplantation might be the following: (1) Limbs consist of multiple tissue types (skin, muscle, bone, nerves, blood vessels, etc.), each with distinct metabolic and perfusion needs. (2) Animal models for limb transplantation are less developed compared to those for solid organs, making it challenging to study perfusion solutions systematically. (3) Many studies focus on tissue survival rather than functional recovery, which is critical for limb transplantation success. (4) The inflammatory cascade in limb transplantation is more severe and multifaceted than in solid organs, complicating the development of perfusion solutions that also modulate immune responses.

Thus, future research should focus on the development and standardization of animal models that closely mimic human limb transplantation, detailed mechanistic studies on IRI in limb tissues, and the development of limb-specific machine perfusion devices that can maintain near-physiological conditions (temperature, pressure, and flow rates) during preservation. Besides, further large-scale, multi-center clinical trials in wider regions are warranted to identify the best perfusion solutions for future clinical transplantation.

Conclusion

Despite the predominant use of cold UW solution in clinical limb transplantation, robust comparative evidence demonstrating its superiority over alternative solutions (HTK, St. Thomas, and IGL-1) remains lacking. The current literature suffers from insufficient sample sizes and non-standardized outcome measures, precluding definitive conclusions about optimal perfusion strategies. Only through large-scale, multicenter clinical trials with standardized protocols can we establish evidence-based guidelines for future limb preservation.
