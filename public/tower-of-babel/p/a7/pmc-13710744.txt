In-situ enhancement of autotrophic nitrogen removal in coking wastewater using staged diatomite and pyrite strategy
Authors: Zhiqi Ren, Jiaying Ma, Pei Ding, Huaqiang Chu, Xuefei Zhou, Yalei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDue to toxicity inhibition and high external carbon costs, anaerobic/anoxic/oxic technologies for coking wastewater require efficient, low-carbon alternatives. Here we develop a strategy to effectively enhance the autotrophic nitrogen removal process in coking wastewater through a phased addition of diatomite porous material and an inorganic electron donor. Under long-term operation, diatomite increased biomass by 86% and induced micro-granular sludge with an average size of 196 μm. Nitrogen removal pathway analysis showed a shift from heterotrophic to autotrophic mode. Ultimately, SCN−- and FeS2-driven autotrophic denitrification contributed 25.6% and 27.9%, respectively. Enrichment of autotrophic sulfur-oxidizing nitrate-reducing bacteria, such as Sulfuritalea and Sulfurisoma, provided strong evidence. Additional pyrite supplementation ultimately increased total nitrogen removal from 77.7% to over 94.0% without external carbon. This study enhanced the in-situ autotrophic process of conventional systems for coking wastewater, offering a cost-effective pathway for energy-saving and carbon-reduction goals.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Water resources

Introduction

The acceleration of industrialization has led to a sharp increase in wastewater discharge, with its complex pollutants posing severe challenges to both ecological environments and human health. Coking wastewater, characterized by high concentrations of nitrogenous compounds, refractory organic matter, and specific toxic substances, is recognized as one of the most challenging industrial wastewaters to treat1,2. Faced with increasingly stringent discharge standards, traditional biological treatment processes have revealed multiple issues in handling coking wastewater. First, the scarcity of biodegradable carbon sources necessitates the reliance on external carbon sources, leading to increased operating costs2. In addition, excessive addition of soluble organic carbon sources might cause secondary pollution, and the promotion of excessive heterotrophic sludge production increases the burden of hazardous sludge disposal. Meanwhile, specific toxic substances, such as phenols and cyanides, significantly inhibit the function of microorganisms. Although phenolic compounds are potential carbon sources, their complex degradation processes and inhibitory effects limit their direct use for denitrification, which highlights the need to explore alternative pathways. Furthermore, the inherent drawbacks of the conventional activated sludge process, such as sludge bulking and biomass washout, further hinder the process’s efficient and stable operation3. Therefore, exploring novel nitrogen removal technologies is crucial for achieving efficient and economical treatment of coking wastewater.

The conventional anaerobic/anoxic/oxic (A/A/O) process or its variants have been widely applied in wastewater treatment. However, for coking wastewater, the A/A/O process has limited pollutant removal capacity due to toxicity inhibition and biomass washout under toxic stress conditions, often necessitating substantial supplementation of the carbon source4,5. Given the overall goal of energy saving and carbon reduction, sulfur-based autotrophic denitrification using reduced sulfur compounds as electron donors has attracted increasing attention due to its advantages in reducing dependence on external carbon sources and lowering sludge production6,7. Studies showed that SCN−, a characteristic pollutant in coking wastewater, can be biologically degraded to release reduced sulfur intermediates such as sulfide and elemental sulfur8. These degradation products can effectively serve as endogenous electron donors to drive denitrification. This process strongly depends on sulfur-oxidizing denitrifying bacteria7,8. Therefore, SCN− in coking wastewater provides in situ conditions for sulfur-based autotrophic denitrification. To further reduce energy consumption in coking wastewater treatment, it is possible to manage the distribution of carbon sources in the raw wastewater and optimize multi-modal nitrogen removal pathways. Pyrite (FeS2), an abundant and inexpensive natural mineral, offers an economical option as an inorganic electron donor9. It offers advantages such as low sulfate production, a stable pH, and the simultaneous removal of nitrogen and phosphorus, which can further reduce system energy consumption9,10. Compared with organic carbon sources, pyrite, as an inorganic slow-release electron donor, sustains autotrophic denitrification with lower sludge yield and without the risk of secondary organic pollution. Thus, the combined effect of endogenous and exogenous electron donors might provide a solution for the low-carbon treatment of coking wastewater.

Relying solely on electron donors is insufficient to address the issues of microbial loss and activity inhibition under high-load and high-toxicity conditions. To overcome this challenge, the addition of microbial carriers has emerged as an effective strategy for enhancing bioreactor performance. Diatomite has the advantages of a natural porous structure, high specific surface area, good biocompatibility, and chemical stability11. A pilot-scale study confirmed that combining a powder carrier with a hydrocyclone separator could resolve the sludge age conflict by constructing a composite sludge system with spatial differentiation of functional microbial groups12. Other research has integrated micro-granular sludge induced by powder carriers with a membrane bioreactor to enhance anti-fouling capacity, thereby improving overall process performance13. Therefore, diatomite can rapidly increase microbial concentration and optimize sludge structure, laying the groundwork for achieving efficient pollutant removal when used in conjunction with other functional materials. However, previous studies mainly focused on municipal wastewater treatment and primarily used powder carriers to enhance biomass and improve reactor performance. Their potential roles under complex and highly toxic wastewater conditions remain insufficiently explored. In particular, combining two economical inorganic electron donors with efficient microbial carriers for treating complex coking wastewater remains a gap in the current research field. Therefore, this study proposed a staged coupling strategy that integrated diatomite powder carriers and pyrite to enhance in situ autotrophic nitrogen removal and reduce the dependence on external organic carbon sources. The feasibility, enhancement mechanisms, and long-term stability of this synergistic regulation strategy require further investigation.

Based on this, a continuous-flow reactor was constructed and operated for 140 days to evaluate the in situ enhancement effect of a phased carrier-electron donor synergistic enhancement strategy on the autotrophic nitrogen removal process in the coking wastewater treatment process. The core objectives were to: (1) systematically evaluate the long-term nutrient removal efficiency and stability of this diatomite-pyrite composite system for coking wastewater; (2) reveal the transformation mechanisms and removal pathways of major pollutants by analyzing the contributions of different denitrification pathways; (3) comprehensively investigate the combined effects of diatomite and pyrite on the macroscopic characteristics, microscopic physicochemical properties, and microbial physiological activity indicators of the sludge within the reactor; and (4) elucidate the succession patterns of the microbial community structure under gradient addition conditions. This study provides theoretical and technical support for the coking wastewater treatment using an autotrophic nitrogen removal enhancement technology based on powder carriers and inorganic electron donors.

Results and discussion

Enhancement of system operational performance by the staged-enhancement strategy

The reactor was operated continuously for 140 days, with its performance as shown in Fig. 1. In the initial phase (P0, days 1–20), the system gradually showed a stable nitrogen removal capability. At the end of this phase, the total nitrogen (TN) removal efficiency (NRE) and NH4+-N removal efficiency (ARE) were 77.7 ± 1.4% and 87.0 ± 2.8%, respectively (Fig. 1a, b). In P1 (days 21–50), 5 g·L−1 diatomite powder carrier was added to the reactor. The addition of diatomite increased the biomass in the reactor. Compared with conventional centimeter-scale carriers, diatomite powder carriers markedly accelerated microbial aggregation and colonization11,14. By the end of P1, the system achieved rapid enrichment of microorganisms within 30 days, with mixed liquor suspended solids (MLSS) and mixed liquor volatile suspended solids (MLVSS) were improved. The effluent TN and NH4+-N concentrations had further decreased to 31.1 ± 2.1 mg·L−1 and 8.8 ± 2.0 mg·L−1, respectively. These results indicated that diatomite enhanced microbial enrichment and functional activity. Microbial assimilation during growth and adsorption by diatomite might also jointly promote the reduction of NH4+-N. In P2 (days 51–80), 20 mg·L−1 pyrite was added to the reactor. At the end of this phase, the addition of pyrite led to an improvement in NRE and ARE, reaching 90.2 ± 1.0% and 95.2 ± 0.2%, respectively. In P3 (days 81–110), the pyrite dosage was increased to 50 mg·L−1. The NO3−-N removal efficiency further increased to 83.6 ± 4.1% by the end of P3. The increase in NO3−-N removal efficiency strongly demonstrates the critical role of pyrite in alleviating the electron donor deficiency for the denitrification process, thereby promoting denitrification. Ultimately, during the P4 (days 111–140), with the addition of 100 mg·L−1 of pyrite, the system exhibited stable and highly efficient nitrogen removal performance. The effluent TN, NH4+-N, and NO3−-N concentrations stabilized at 13.1 ± 0.1, 6.4 ± 0.3, and 5.3 ± 0.4 mg·L−1, respectively. This indicated that by the end of P4, the levels of TN and NH4+-N in the treated effluent met the wastewater discharge standards (GB 16171-2012, TN < 20 mg·L−1, NH4+-N < 10 mg·L−1) without the need for additional carbon source addition15.

From the perspective of reactor units, nitrogen transformation exhibited phased functional changes (Fig. 1a–d). A decrease in NH4+-N concentration was observed in the anaerobic stage. In the A/A/O system, the nitrified liquor from the aerobic stage was returned to the anoxic tank at a 300% recirculation rate, and 100% of the sludge from the sedimentation tank was returned to the anaerobic tank. The dilution effect caused by recirculation also influenced the NH4+-N concentration. In addition, residual and refluxed NO2−-N and NO3−-N provided electron acceptors for anaerobic ammonia oxidation and promoting further consumption of NH4+-N. In the anoxic stage, NO3−-N recycled from the aerobic tank was effectively removed. Particularly during P2-P4, pyrite addition transformed this stage into a coupled autotrophic and heterotrophic denitrification zone, thereby maintaining NO3−-N at low levels. In the oxic stage, nitrifying bacteria effectively oxidized residual NH4+-N to NO2−-N and NO3−-N, supplying essential electron acceptors for the anaerobic and anoxic stages, which was reflected by the concurrent increase in NO2−-N and NO3−-N. Overall, the staged addition of diatomite and pyrite enhanced nitrogen removal in each unit and ultimately achieved stable and efficient performance in P4.

The system also showed a good removal effect on organic matter (Fig. 1e, f). The anaerobic stage contributed most to the reduction of chemical oxygen demand (COD) and total organic carbon (TOC) concentrations, which was attributed not only to dilution caused by recirculation, but also to adsorption of organic matter by sludge and its utilization as an electron donor for heterotrophic denitrification. Upon entering the anoxic stage, a portion of readily biodegradable organic matter was consumed as an internal carbon source to drive heterotrophic denitrification. In the oxic stage, sufficient dissolved oxygen promoted the biodegradation of residual refractory organic matter, enabling efficient removal without the addition of external carbon sources. Specifically, in the initial P0, the effluent COD and TOC concentrations were 246.0 ± 13.0 and 47.8 ± 7.4 mg·L−1, with corresponding removal efficiency of 77.4 ± 1.0% and 87.5 ± 2.2%, respectively. After the addition of diatomite in P1, the COD and TOC removal efficiency showed some improvement due to the enrichment of microorganisms. The increase in total biomass induced by diatomite enhanced the degradation of organic matter11,14. With the addition of pyrite in P2-P4, the COD and TOC removal efficiency was further enhanced. By the end of P4, the effluent COD and TOC concentrations had decreased to 132.1 ± 19.0 and 18.8 ± 0.5 mg·L−1, respectively. Removal efficiency reached 88.8 ± 0.5% and 95.4 ± 0.1%, respectively. In summary, this study exhibited high pollutant removal efficiency without the external addition of conventional organic carbon sources, which demonstrates the potential and advantages of the composite powder carrier phased enhancement system for treating coking wastewater.

Pollutant transformation pathways: increased contribution of the autotrophic denitrification

The contributions of the endogenous electron donor SCN− and the exogenous electron donor pyrite to denitrification were calculated to evaluate the changes in denitrification pathways. Thiocyanate is a characteristic pollutant in coking wastewater. The reduced sulfur in coking wastewater is oxidized into sulfate to provide electrons for nitrate and nitrite8. The system exhibited high SCN− removal efficiency throughout the operational phase. SCN− was mainly removed in the anaerobic phase, and the removal efficiency was always maintained above 92% (Fig. 1g). This indicated a microbial population in the reactor that can efficiently degrade SCN−. The degradation of SCN− not only removed toxic substances, but its oxidation products could also serve as endogenous electron donors for subsequent sulfur-based autotrophic denitrification, contributing to the autotrophic denitrification of the system16. The transformation of sulfide is a key indicator for evaluating the autotrophic denitrification process. As one of the products of sulfur oxidation, the concentration changes of SO42− directly reflect the degree of sulfur transformation17. At the end of P1, the SO42− concentration in the effluent from the anoxic phase increased from an influent concentration of 439.9 ± 8.8 to 715.0 ± 12.9 mg·L−1 (Fig. 1h). In addition, under the near-neutral and anaerobic conditions of the reactor, the contribution of abiotic pyrite dissolution to sulfate production remained minimal and could be neglected18. This indicated that sulfides derived from SCN− were oxidized, contributing electrons for denitrification in the process. After the addition of pyrite, the sulfur oxidation process was further intensified. By the end of P4, the effluent SO42− concentration further increased to 861.5 ± 15.7 mg·L−1. The continuous increase in SO42− concentration indicated heightened oxidation of sulfur elements derived from SCN− and pyrite within the system, which is closely linked to the electron demand of denitrification processes. In P0 and P1, the actual sulfate production in the anaerobic and anoxic zones was close to the theoretical yield from SCN− degradation. However, for P2 to P4 with pyrite addition, the stoichiometric estimation of pyrite contribution based on net sulfate increase contains uncertainty. A systematic quantification of intermediate sulfur species and biological sulfur assimilation is still required to distinguish these sulfur-driven oxidation pathways.

By analyzing the contribution of different nitrogen removal pathways, the sources of electron donors and their impact on nitrogen removal pathways can be clearly identified (Fig. 2). In P0, heterotrophic denitrification was dominant, contributing 62.8% to TN removal. SCN−-driven autotrophic denitrification contributed 27.4% (Fig. 2a). In P1, the addition of diatomite altered the system’s nitrogen removal pathways. The contribution of SCN−-driven autotrophic denitrification increased to 39.3%, while the contribution of heterotrophic denitrification decreased to 47.6% (Fig. 2b). P1 increased the proportion of autotrophic denitrification by 13.2% compared to P0. This was attributed to the role of the diatomite powder carrier, which effectively enriched denitrifying functional microorganisms13,19. The addition of pyrite further changed the system’s nitrogen removal pathways. In P4, the contribution of FeS2-driven autotrophic denitrification reached 27.9%, while SCN−-driven autotrophic denitrification contributed 25.6%, and the contribution of heterotrophic denitrification was further reduced to 31% (Fig. 2e). The contribution of FeS2-driven denitrification pathways suggested that pyrite acts as a unique electron donor to support specific microbial metabolic pathways. This might involve the enrichment or enhanced activity of microorganisms capable of directly or indirectly utilizing the electrons released from pyrite oxidation. The system demonstrated a strong ability to utilize multiple electron donors. The reduced dependence on heterotrophic pathways also implied that the cost of external carbon source addition can be effectively saved when treating coking wastewater. Furthermore, the contribution of wasted sludge gradually increased during operation, which can be attributed to the promotion of biomass accumulation by the addition of diatomite. As shown in Fig. 3a, the increase in MLVSS indicated that microbial growth led to more nitrogen assimilation into biomass. Although the staged addition of diatomite and pyrite effectively enhanced autotrophic nitrogen removal, the absence of a pyrite-only control limited the quantitative differentiation of their individual roles. Future studies should include a continuous-flow reactor with only pyrite addition to further clarify its independent contribution and better understand the synergistic mechanism.

Synergistic enhancement of micro-granular sludge formation, surface properties, and metabolic activity mediated by composite carriers

The effects of diatomite and pyrite addition on sludge aggregation and surface properties are key to enhancing reactor performance. After the addition of diatomite in P1, MLSS increased from 2022 mg L−1 and stabilized above 4000 mg L−1, which included the inorganic mass of diatomite (Fig. 3a). At the same time, the concentration of mixed liquor volatile suspended solids, which represented biomass, showed a corresponding increasing trend, rising from 1049 mg L−1 at the end of P0 to 2141 mg L−1 at the end of P1. This indicated an increase in system biomass. The SV30 value continuously decreased from 57 ± 1% to 30 ± 2%, indicating a denser sludge structure20. More importantly, the system exhibited a clear granulation trend. Under the synergistic effect of diatomite and pyrite, the average sludge particle size (d0.5) increased from 127 μm in P0 to 196 μm by the end of P4 (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 1). Scanning electron microscope images visually confirmed that microorganisms attach to the diatomite surface to form complex aggregates (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 2). This structure was beneficial for resisting hydraulic shear and forming multiple metabolic microenvironments21.

The aggregation behavior of sludge is closely related to changes in extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and surface chemical properties. At the end of P3, the contents of key proteins and humic acids increased, leading to enhanced sludge surface hydrophobicity (contact angle increased from 51.1 ± 2.5° in P0 to 62.9 ± 8.0°) (Fig. 3d, e)22. This enhanced hydrophobicity helps overcome the electrostatic repulsion between granules, promoting adhesion and aggregation between microorganisms and between microorganisms and carriers23. Concurrently, the electrostatic repulsion between micro-granular sludge weakened, as evidenced by the change in zeta potential from −15.9 ± 1.9 mV to −9.6 ± 0.9 mV. This means that the reduction in the negative charge density on the surface of micro-granular sludge promotes flocculation and aggregation of sludge24. Therefore, the optimization of EPS components, the enhancement of surface hydrophobicity, and the reduction of electrostatic repulsion synergistically and fundamentally promoted the adhesion between microorganisms and carriers, as well as sludge granulation.

The analysis of the sludge’s surface functional groups and elemental composition provided direct evidence for the action mechanism of the composite carrier. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis showed that the functional group structure of the sludge underwent distinct changes at different stages. Typical absorption peaks such as O–H, C–H, and C=O were identified23,25,26. Beginning from P1, a strong absorption peak appeared at 1087 cm−1, which was attributed to the Si-O-Si stretching vibration in diatomite (Supplementary Fig. 3)27. This new characteristic peak demonstrated that microorganisms had successfully attached to the diatomite carrier, forming composite aggregates. Meanwhile, subtle changes in the peak intensities of the Amide I and II bands and the polysaccharide region also implied changes in EPS components, consistent with the previously observed changes in sludge surface properties28. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis revealed the chemical transformation process of pyrite (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). In P0, the sludge already contained Fe (2.7%) and S (1.1%), which rose to 3.0% and 8.3% by the end of P4. The Fe species transformed from Fe (II)/Fe (III) to be dominated by Fe (III)-O, indicating that an FeS2 oxidation reaction occurred. The enhancement of Fe (III)-O indicated the oxidation of pyrite and the release of electrons, which sulfur-oxidizing and iron-oxidizing bacteria used to drive denitrification29.

Intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) content and coenzyme I redox ratio (NAD+/NADH) are essential indicators reflecting microorganisms’ overall physiological activity and energy status. NAD+ accepts electrons and H+ to form NADH. These electrons are transferred through the subsequent respiration chain, which drives the pumping of protons to create a proton motive force that ultimately powers ATP synthesis30,31. As shown in Fig. 3g, compared with P0, the intracellular ATP content of microorganisms showed a significant increasing trend in both P1 and P4, reaching 674.2 ± 57.9 and 933.3 ± 81.6µmol·g prot−1, respectively (p < 0.05). An increase in ATP content usually indicates an improved energy level within microbial cells and enhanced overall metabolic activity. The NAD+/NADH ratio also remained at a high level in P3 and P4, being 2.2 ± 0.2 at the end of P4. An elevated NAD+/NADH ratio indicated increased activity of oxidative metabolic pathways within the cells and enhanced electron transport chain function. This might be related to pyrite acting as an electron donor, promoting the oxidative phosphorylation process in autotrophic microorganisms, thereby producing more ATP. The improvement of these physiological indicators was consistent with the enhancement of the overall reactor performance.

Microbial activity tests elucidated the evolution of the system’s metabolic functions (Fig. 3h). Throughout the entire operational period, the heterotrophic denitrification activity increased from 60.3 ± 6.7 to 83.5 ± 3.0 mg N·g−1 VSS·d−1. After adding pyrite, the sulfur-based autotrophic denitrification activity increased to 68.4 ± 9.6 mg N·g−1 VSS·d−1 at the end of P2. It reached 78.0 ± 0.6 mg N·g−1 VSS·d−1 at the end of P4. This directly confirmed that pyrite acted as an electron donor to enhance the autotrophic denitrification process, which was highly consistent with the analysis results of the contribution rate of the denitrification pathway (Fig. 2c–f). The specific oxygen uptake rate tests provided direct evidence for the nitrification process (Fig. 3i). The results showed that the specific oxygen uptake rate of ammonia oxidizing bacteria was higher than that of nitrite oxidizing bacteria. This indicated that the ammonia oxidation capacity in the system was more substantial than the nitrite oxidation capacity. This imbalance could lead to nitrite accumulation, which reasonably explains the transient NO2−-N accumulation shown in P3 (Fig. 1c). In summary, the addition of the composite carrier synergistically enhanced both the nitrification and autotrophic denitrification pathways by enriching microorganisms and providing an additional electron donor, thereby effectively improving the system’s total nitrogen removal efficiency without relying on external carbon sources.

Microbial community succession and enrichment of functional genera

To deeply reveal the impact mechanism of diatomite and pyrite addition on the reactor’s microbial system, this study used metagenomic sequencing technology to systematically analyze the succession patterns of key functional groups in the microbial community to clarify its inherent ecological mechanism. Alpha diversity analysis results showed that from P2 to P4, both the richness and evenness of the microbial community increased (Supplementary Fig. 6). This indicated that the addition of pyrite not only altered the composition of the microbial community but also created favorable conditions for the growth of functional microorganisms. At the phylum level, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Myxococcota, Actinomycetota, and Nitrospirota were the main dominant phyla in the reactor (Fig. 4a). These phyla are common core members in activated sludge systems, widely involved in key biogeochemical cycle processes such as organic matter degradation, nitrification, and denitrification. At the genus level, the composition and relative abundance of the microbial community showed more dynamic changes at different phases. In the initial P0, the main dominant genera included Thiobacillus, Chloracidobacterium, Nitrospira, and Azohydromonas. After the addition of diatomite, Candidatus Nitricoxidivorans, Sulfuritalea, Sulfurisoma, and Denitratisoma became the new dominant genera. The relative abundance of Candidatus Nitricoxidivorans increased from 0.18 ± 0.04% and 0.15 ± 0.01% at the end of P0 to 11.0 ± 0.6% and 11.1 ± 0.7% at the end of P1, respectively, during the anaerobic and anoxic phases. Candidatus Nitricoxidivorans is a novel microorganism capable of directly reducing NO to N2 with minimal production of the greenhouse gas N2O. The enrichment of this genus indicates its potential to reduce N2O accumulation32.

Sulfuritalea and Sulfurisoma were identified as autotrophic sulfur-oxidizing nitrate-reducing bacteria33,34. They can utilize reduced sulfur compounds as electron donors and nitrate or nitrite as electron acceptors for denitrification34. Autotrophic denitrification driven by sulfur-oxidizing nitrate-reducing bacteria is an effective way to save carbon source consumption in wastewater treatment35. At the end of P0, the relative abundance of Sulfuritalea and Sulfurisoma in the anaerobic phase was only 1.2 ± 0.1% and 0.1 ± 0.01%, respectively. After diatomite addition, their relative abundance at the end of P1 increased to 6.1 ± 0.3% and 3.2 ± 0.2%, respectively. This indicated that the addition of diatomite played a key role in promoting the enrichment of autotrophic sulfur-oxidizing nitrate-reducing bacteria. This is also consistent with the phenomenon of an increase in the contribution of SCN−-driven autotrophic denitrification in P1 (Fig. 2b). This indicated that sulfur-oxidizing nitrate-reducing bacteria could not only utilize the degradation products of SCN−, but also effectively utilize the electrons released by pyrite oxidation or its intermediates for autotrophic denitrification. However, the relative abundance of Thiobacillus, the dominant genus in P0, decreased after pyrite addition. This might be due to differences in adaptability to environmental conditions and niche competition among different autotrophic sulfur-oxidizing nitrate-reducing bacteria. The addition of pyrite might have created a more competitive ecological niche for Sulfuritalea and Sulfurisoma, while possibly having some inhibitory or competitive disadvantage for the growth of Thiobacillus. The substrate ratio is a key factor influencing the abundance of autotrophic sulfur-oxidizing nitrate-reducing bacteria7. As a solid-phase electron donor, pyrite has relatively low bioavailability, which might reduce the competitive advantage of Thiobacillus. In addition, pyrite oxidation might alter local pH and microenvironmental conditions, thereby further affecting the microbial community structure.

Furthermore, the relative abundance of Sphingosinicella, Sphingopyxis, and Candidatus Desulfobacillus also increased in P2-P4 compared with P1. Sphingosinicella usually possesses strong organic matter biodegradation capabilities36. Its relative abundance in the anaerobic and anoxic phases reached 4.5 ± 0.4% and 5.9 ± 0.4%, respectively, which is consistent with the improvement in COD removal efficiency during the pyrite addition phase (Fig. 1e). This suggested that the addition of pyrite might indirectly promote the growth of heterotrophic organic matter-degrading bacteria. Sphingopyxis bacteria are known for their ability to secrete EPS37,38. The increase in the relative abundance of Sphingopyxis in P2-P4 might be related to the rise in EPS content and the improvement in sludge granulation. It might play a role in maintaining the biofilm structure and promoting sludge aggregation. The changes in microbial community composition clearly reflected the adaptive response of the system to diatomite and pyrite addition. They supported the observed changes in reactor performance and alterations in denitrification pathways.

Expression and regulation of nitrogen and sulfur metabolic functions

Microorganisms regulate their metabolic processes to adapt to environmental changes, and the understanding of microbial metabolic pathways, functional genes, and enzymes is crucial for system optimization39. To reveal the functional potential of the microbial community at the gene level, KEGG functional classification analysis was performed on metagenomic data from different stages. In the KEGG level 3 metabolic pathways, purine metabolism (1.09–1.15%), pyruvate metabolism (1.05–1.12%), and oxidative phosphorylation (0.94–1.04%) were the most abundant functional modules. These are essential core metabolic pathways for microorganisms to maintain basic life activities (Supplementary Fig. 7). Specifically, the total relative abundance of nitrogen metabolism pathways at different stages was relatively stable. However, the relative abundance of sulfur metabolism pathways showed changes, increasing from 0.63% in P0 to 0.81% in P1. The increase in the relative abundance of sulfur metabolic pathways was consistent with the enrichment of autotrophic sulfur-oxidizing nitrate-reducing bacteria in P1 and the increase in the contribution of SCN−-driven autotrophic denitrification, indicating that the addition of diatomite and pyrite enhanced the sulfur transformation capacity of the community. KEGG annotation also showed that carbon metabolism, biosynthesis of cofactors, valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation, peroxisome and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism were enriched pathways (Fig. 4e). These pathways are crucial for maintaining bacterial metabolic activity.

To further explore the relative abundance changes of key enzyme genes driving nitrogen and sulfur cycles, core enzymes involved in nitrogen metabolism (KO00910) and sulfur metabolism (KO00920) were analyzed (Fig. 4d). In nitrogen metabolism, nitrite reductase (NO-forming) (EC 1.7.2.1), hydroxylamine reductase (EC 1.7.99.1), nitrous-oxide reductase (EC 1.7.2.4), and nitric-oxide reductase (cytochrome c) (EC 1.7.2.5) were key enzymes involved in nitrogen metabolism. From the end of P0 to P1, the average relative abundance of nitrite reductase (EC 1.7.2.1) and hydroxylamine reductase (EC 1.7.99.1) increased from 0.12% and 0.06% to 0.23% and 0.11%, respectively. This indicated that adding diatomite promoted the enrichment of microorganisms encoding these key denitrification enzymes, thereby enhancing the system’s ability to reduce intermediates such as nitrite and hydroxylamine further. This might also be related to the enrichment of NO-reducing bacteria such as Candidatus Nitricoxidivorans.

In sulfur metabolism, sulfate adenylyltransferase (EC 2.7.7.4), adenylyl-sulfate kinase (EC 2.7.1.25), assimilatory sulfite reductase (NADPH) (EC 1.8.1.2), and adenylyl-sulfate reductase (EC 1.8.99.2) were key enzymes involved in sulfur metabolism. In the dissimilatory sulfate reduction pathway, sulfate adenylyltransferases and adenylyl-sulfate reductases were responsible for reducing sulfate to sulfite, which is then reduced by sulfite reductases40. The results showed that from P0 to P1, the relative abundance of sulfate adenylyltransferase (EC 2.7.7.4) increased from 0.15% to 0.28%, and remained at a relatively high level of 0.23% at the end of P4. Similarly, the relative abundance of adenylate sulfate kinase (EC 2.7.1.25) also began to increase starting from P1, rising gradually from 0.046 ± 0.001% at the end of P0 to 0.069 ± 0.002%. The increased relative abundance of enzymes involved in sulfur activation and preliminary reduction further confirmed the enhanced potential for systemic sulfur metabolism at the molecular level. Diatomite-pyrite composite powder carriers might improve system performance primarily by enriching specific, metabolically efficient functional microorganisms or regulating specific enzymes’ expression and activity.

Metagenomic analysis revealed that although the total relative abundance of genes related to nitrogen metabolism pathways did not vary significantly across different phases, the relative abundance of key functional genes changed (Fig. 5). In the denitrification pathway, the relative abundance of narG, responsible for the reduction of nitrate to nitrite, and nirK/nirS, responsible for the reduction of nitrite to NO, remained at high levels during P2-P4. Among them, the relative abundance of nirS and narH/nary/nxrB increased after adding diatomite and pyrite. At the end of P0, the relative abundances of nirS and narH/nary/nxrB were 0.030% and 0.003%, respectively. By the end of P1, they increased to 0.064% and 0.024%, respectively. In addition, the nirK/nirS ratio decreased from 1.74 in P0 to 0.96 in P1 and remained around 1 in the subsequent phases. Fe(II) tends to enrich nirS-type rather than nirK-type denitrifiers41. This indicated that the addition of diatomite and pyrite promoted a shift of the denitrifying community toward nirS-type denitrifiers. In the later operational phases, the complementarity between nirK-type and nirS-type denitrifiers also maintained the overall stability of the denitrification process. The relative abundance of several key genes involved in sulfur metabolism increased during P2-P4. Specifically, the relative abundance of scnC increased from P0 to P1. The relative abundance of dsrB, soxAB, and soxXYZ increased after adding diatomite and pyrite. This suggested that the system’s pathways for converting S2− to SO32− and S2O32− to SO42− were further promoted. The core of this process involved microorganisms utilizing reduced sulfur compounds as electron donors to reduce nitrate or nitrite. The enhancement of this pathway indicated that after pyrite addition, the system could improve nitrogen removal efficiency even when organic carbon sources were potentially limited. Furthermore, the relative abundance of key genes involved in the dissimilatory sulfate reduction pathway also increased during P2-P4. This indicated the formation of a sulfur cycle involving both sulfur oxidation and sulfate reduction within the system.

Driving mechanisms of operational strategies on microbial community succession

Microbial ecological analysis revealed the synergistic mechanisms and driving factors of the system. Microbial co-occurrence network analysis indicated that the autotrophic sulfur-oxidizing nitrate-reducing bacteria Sulfuritalea and Sulfurisoma had a positive correlation with the sulfide oxidizing bacterium Candidatus Desulfobacillus (Supplementary Fig. 8). This suggested the existence of a nitrogen-sulfur coupled cycle within the system that enhanced electron transfer efficiency. Redundancy analysis revealed the influence of environmental factors on microbial community structure succession (Fig. 4c). The addition of diatomite was significantly and positively correlated with the relative abundance of sulfur-oxidizing nitrate-reducing bacteria Sulfurisoma, Sulfuritalea, and NO-reducing bacteria Candidatus Nitricoxidivorans, indicating the physical enrichment effect of diatomite. Adding pyrite was a key factor influencing the community structure in P2-P4 and was strongly positively correlated with the relative abundance of Sphingosinicella. Mantel test confirmed that environmental factors were the main driving force for community succession (Supplementary Fig. 9). The addition of diatomite increased the MLSS. The strong positive correlation of Candidatus Nitricoxidivorans, Sulfuritalea, Sulfurisoma, and Denitratisoma with MLSS indicated that the addition of diatomite effectively enriched these functional microorganisms, significantly altering the composition and function of the microbial community. Meanwhile, adding pyrite was also highly correlated with the community structure and the enhancement of system function. This demonstrated that diatomite’s physical enrichment effect and pyrite’s chemical driving force collectively shaped an efficient and stable microbial system.

Engineering and economic perspectives on full-scale application

Although the staged diatomite and pyrite strategy demonstrates efficient nitrogen removal, its application in full-scale coking wastewater treatment systems still requires careful consideration of economic feasibility and scalability factors. From an economic perspective, the main advantage lies in the significant reduction of operational costs associated with chemical dosing. Coking wastewater typically has a low C/N ratio and therefore requires expensive external organic carbon sources such as sodium acetate or methanol for deep nitrogen removal. This strategy replaces these organic carbon sources with low-cost and abundant natural minerals, thereby enabling a cost-effective autotrophic denitrification pathway. In addition, since the powdered carriers and minerals can be directly introduced into the existing anaerobic and anoxic zones of conventional A/A/O processes, no additional infrastructure is required. However, several challenges remain for large-scale implementation. The increase in inorganic components in the mixed liquor might moderately increase the cost of excess sludge disposal. Although diatomite and pyrite addition increased the inorganic fraction in sludge, the MLVSS/MLSS ratio decreased only from 0.581 to 0.547, indicating that the accumulation of inorganic minerals remains relatively limited and that the sludge system was still dominated by biologically active components. In addition, since coking sludge is enriched with toxic substances such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and cyanides, it is classified as hazardous waste and is strictly prohibited from agricultural use. Therefore, the moderate increase in inorganic components might only influence the ash load during final incineration disposal. Moreover, the high density of pyrite requires enhanced mechanical mixing in large-scale systems to prevent mineral accumulation in dead zones. In practical applications, the hydrocyclone separator can be considered to selectively remove denser, iron-rich sludge while retaining carrier and biomass, thereby effectively controlling inorganic content. In addition, the potential loss of powdered carriers and the optimization of dosing strategies should be carefully evaluated, and further pilot-scale studies are required to assess economic feasibility and operational stability.

Conclusion

This study successfully constructed a synergistic bioaugmentation system using diatomite powder carrier and pyrite as an electron donor, effectively enhancing the autotrophic nitrogen removal process in coking wastewater. First, this study successfully utilized the synergistic effect of diatomite powder carrier and EPS to construct a high-biomass, highly stable micro-granular sludge system. Second, by adding pyrite, this study successfully increased autotrophic sulfur-oxidizing nitrate-reducing bacteria, such as Sulfuritalea, and Sulfurisoma, achieving efficient autotrophic nitrogen removal. Finally, this study revealed the underlying mechanisms of the synergistic action between diatomite and pyrite from molecular and microbial ecological perspectives. The system enriched key functional consortia and constructed a more complex and robust microbial ecosystem by promoting synergy among microorganisms and optimizing metabolic networks. This study provides an innovative path with economic and environmental benefits for treating high-concentration, high-toxicity coking wastewater.

Methods

Experimental setup and operation

The study set up a single continuous flow A/A/O reactor, which was operated in five phases. The effective volume of the reactor was 27 L, and the dimensions of the rectangular reactor were 60 cm in length, 15 cm in width, and 40 cm in height, with a freeboard of 10 cm. The sedimentation tank was a vertical-flow type with a diameter of 14 cm and a height of 35 cm. The effective volume ratio of each unit in the reactor was anaerobic:anoxic:oxic = 1:1:2. The aeration method in the oxic zone utilized microporous aeration, where dissolved oxygen was monitored to control the dissolved oxygen in the anaerobic tank below 0.2 mg·L−1, the anoxic tank below 0.5 mg·L−1, and the oxic zone above 2 mg·L−1. The sludge was collected from the primary sedimentation tank of a coking wastewater anoxic/aerobic/anoxic/aerobic treatment system at Baosteel in Shanghai. The mixed liquor reflux ratio of this process was 300%, the sludge reflux ratio was 100%, the hydraulic retention time was 80 h, the sludge retention time was 50 days, and the temperature was maintained at approximately 25 °C. The study set up P0 as the control (20 days). 100-mesh powdered diatomite carriers were added to the reactor at a dose of 5 g·L−1 starting at P1 (30 days), and no further additions were made during long-term operation. In P2-P4, 325-mesh pyrite was added to promote stable reactor operation (with dosages of 20, 50, and 100 mg·L−1, respectively, for 30 days each).

The experimental influent was composed of simulated coking wastewater components, with the concentrations set at 378 mg·L−1 for phenol, 72 mg·L−1 for pyridine, 54 mg·L−1 for quinoline, and 20 mg·L−1 for indole, resulting in a theoretical COD of approximately 1200 mg·L−1. KSCN, (NH4)2SO4, KNO₃, pyridine, quinoline, and indole were used as nitrogen sources to achieve a TN concentration of approximately 215 mg·L−1. KH₂PO₄ served as the phosphorus source, and Na₂CO₃ was used to adjust the influent pH. The influent SO42− was mainly supplied by (NH4)2SO4 and MgSO4·7H2O, with a concentration of approximately 445 mg L−1. Throughout the experimental process, the TN, NH4+-N, NO2−-N, NO3−-N, COD, TOC, SO42−, SCN−, DO, and pH of the reactor influent and effluent were monitored every other day. The water quality samples were collected from the stable operating coking wastewater biological treatment systems, including influent, effluent, and various biological treatment stages. To ensure the accuracy of water quality parameters, the water quality data for the end of each phase were the average of the data from the last five days of that phase.

Physicochemical analysis methods

The pH of the water samples was measured using a pH meter (FE28; Mettler, USA). TOC and TN were determined using an organic carbon analyzer (TOC-L CPH; Shimadzu, JPN). COD, NH4+-N, NO2−-N, NO3−-N, SCN−, MLSS, and MLVSS were measured according to standard methods42. The calculation method for nitrogen removal pathways was presented in Section S1 of the Supplementary Information43–45. The contribution of each pathway was calculated using the average water quality data from the last five days of each phase. This method relied on water quality parameters and included assumptions such as complete substrate conversion, independent pathways, and negligible side reactions. Due to the lack of direct measurement of gaseous nitrogen products, this method had inherent limitations and might introduce uncertainty into pathway contribution estimation. Specifically, the occurrence of dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium might lead to an underestimation of the contribution of anammox. In addition, unmeasured processes, including incomplete denitrification and sulfur intermediate transformation, might also introduce uncertainty into the nitrogen and sulfur mass balance calculations.

The particle size distribution of the mixed sludge samples was determined by the laser particle size analyzer (Partica LA-960, HORIBA, JPN). Sludge samples were collected from the reactors and pretreated for analysis of surface characteristics and morphology. The specific steps for sample preparation were described in Section S2 of the Supplementary Information. The morphology and elemental distribution of the diatomite powder carriers and the sludge were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (Zeiss Sigma 360, GER) and an energy dispersive spectrometer (Oxford Xplore30, UK). The structure and functional groups of sludge samples were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Thermo Scientific Nexsa, USA) and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (Nicolet 5700, USA).

Specific sludge activity was measured to assess nitrogen removal activity. The specific procedures were described in Section S3 of Supplementary Information. EPS were extracted using the thermal extraction method, and three main components, namely protein, humic substance, and polysaccharide contents, were quantified by the modified Lowry method and the anthrone method, respectively46. At the end of each operation phase, sludge samples were collected, washed with phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 8), and homogenized in an ice-water bath. After sonication treatment (130 W, 20 min), the homogenized mixture in an ice-water bath was denoted for enzyme activity testing. ATP and NAD+/NADH were biomarkers for cell viability, cell death, and membrane damage47. They were analyzed using assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), followed by the manufacturer’s instructions. Each analysis was conducted in triplicate, and the results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27 (IBM, USA), with significance set at p < 0.05.

Microbial analysis

Sludge samples from different biological treatment stages were collected at the end of each phase for metagenomic analysis. Each sludge sample was collected three times. Metagenomic sequencing technology was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq platform. Raw reads were filtered using fastp (version 0.20.0) to remove low-quality bases. Clean reads from each sample were assembled using MEGAHIT (version 1.2.9), and contigs with lengths less than 300 bp were discarded. Open reading frames were predicted from the assembled sequences using Prodigal (version 2.6.3), and sequences with lengths ≥100 bp were retained. Subsequently, CD-HIT (version 4.6.1) was used to cluster the predicted gene sequences from all samples with identity ≥90.0% and coverage ≥90.0% to remove redundant genes. SOAPaligner (version soap2.21 release) was then used to align high-quality reads from each sample to the non-redundant gene set with identity ≥95.0%, and the gene abundance in each sample was calculated. To obtain taxonomic and functional information, Diamond (version 2.0.13) was used to align the amino acid sequences of the non-redundant gene set against the NR, KEGG, ARDB, CARD, and VFDB databases, with BLASTP parameters set to an e-value ≤ 1e−5. Gene abundance was calculated using the RPKM (Reads Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads) method to normalize for gene length (based on predicted open reading frames) and sequencing depth. The α-diversity indices and β-diversity analysis were calculated using R. Redundancy analysis was conducted using the vegan package (version 4.3.2) in R, and the network analysis results were visualized using Gephi (version 0.9.1).
