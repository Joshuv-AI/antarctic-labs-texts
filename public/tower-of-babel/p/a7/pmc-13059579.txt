Age-Period-Cohort analysis of gonorrhea surveillance data in Eastern China from 2005 to 2024
Authors: Ziyang Fang, Mingkuan Fan, Shuangqing Wang, Haocheng Wu, Qinbao Lu, Zheyuan Ding, Xinyi Wang, Junjie Li, Tianyin Fu, Kui Liu, Chen Wu, Jianmin Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGonorrhea poses a major public health challenge. This study analyzed the independent effects of age, period, and birth cohort on gonorrhea incidence in Eastern China to inform targeted interventions.MethodsCase data from Zhejiang Province (2005–2024) were obtained from the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention. Age-Period-Cohort and Joinpoint regression models were used to evaluate trends and effects.ResultsA total of 347,868 gonorrhea cases were reported, with an average annual incidence of 31.87 per 100,000 and a male-to-female ratio of 3.66. Joinpoint analysis identified three distinct trends: a significant decrease during 2005–2012 (Annual Percentage Change=-10.56%; P < 0.01), followed by an increase from 2012 to 2017 (Annual Percentage Change = 5.90%; P = 0.03), and a subsequent decline from 2017 to 2024 (Annual Percentage Change=-10.24%; P < 0.01). Overall, the net drift showed an average annual decrease of 4.80%. However, local drift revealed a significant increasing incidence in the 15–19 age group (Annual Percentage Change = 4.88%). Age-effect analysis identified the highest incidence in the 20–29 age group, with a minor peak in children aged 0–4; this latter group also exhibited the most rapid decline among all age categories. Males had a consistently higher risk than females after age 15. The period effect showed the highest risk in 2005–2009 (RR = 1.62) and the lowest in 2020–2024 (RR = 0.69). Cohort effect analysis indicated that later birth cohorts had lower risks, with the highest risk in the 1920–1924 cohort (RR = 36.51) and the lowest in the 2020–2024 cohort (RR = 0.04).ConclusionsThe overall incidence rate of gonorrhea in Zhejiang Province exhibited a downward trend, underscoring the effectiveness of existing prevention and control strategies. The period effect showed an overall declining risk over time, while the cohort effect was marked by lower incidence risk among those born in later years. In terms of the age effect, the 20–29 years age group was a high-incidence population, which should be controlled through a combination of school-based health education integrated with digital platforms and the expansion of affordable and accessible gonorrhea testing services, while also exploring novel interventions such as expedited partner therapy. Introduction

Gonorrhea is a highly contagious sexually transmitted disease (STD) caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae. It primarily manifests as a purulent infection of the genitourinary tract. While some infected individuals develop symptoms such as dysuria and urethral discharge, the majority remain asymptomatic [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates, there were 82.4 million new cases of gonorrhea globally in 2020, making it the second most common STD worldwide, with consistently high notification rates in the Western Pacific Region [3, 4]. In China, 104,160 gonorrhea cases were reported in 2024, ranking it among the top five legally notifiable infectious diseases [5]. If left untreated, gonorrhea can lead to serious complications including epididymitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and salpingitis, conditions that may progress to infertility or ectopic pregnancy, while also increasing the risk of HIV acquisition. Additionally, vertical transmission of Neisseria gonorrhoeae from mother-to-newborn can cause neonatal conjunctivitis, potentially resulting in blindness in severe cases. Furthermore, the growing challenge of antimicrobial resistance globally complicates gonorrhea control efforts, presenting a persistent and critical public health threat [1].

The epidemiological profile of gonorrhea exhibits considerable global diversity, with marked variations in incidence across countries and over time. Overall, reported gonorrhea incidence showed an upward trend in regions like the United States and EU/EEA countries in recent years, while incidence in some Asian countries showed a declining trend. In terms of age distribution, individuals aged 15–34 years were the main affected population in most regions [6–8]. Unfortunately, the scarcity of studies that concurrently examine the effects of age, period, and birth cohort limits a comprehensive understanding of the gonorrhea epidemic in China, including its eastern regions.

This study utilized data from Zhejiang Province and applied an Age-Period-Cohort (APC) model alongside a Joinpoint regression model to analyze the epidemiological characteristics of gonorrhea between 2005 and 2024. The objective was to disentangle the independent effects of age, period, and birth cohort, and to explore potential high-risk populations and related epidemiological trends. The findings are expected to provide a scientific basis for formulating targeted prevention and intervention strategies, thereby offering valuable insights for future public health policy-making on gonorrhea.

Methods

Study areas

Zhejiang Province, located in eastern China, experienced sustained demographic expansion over the past two decades: its resident population increased from approximately 49.8 million in 2005 to 66.7 million in 2024, according to the 2025 Zhejiang Statistical Yearbook published by the Zhejiang Provincial Bureau of Statistics [9]. In the case of Zhejiang Province, data from the 2020 Seventh National Population Census documented distinct age distributions within the core 15–29-year-old cohort: 2,809,150 individuals fell into the 15–19 age group, 3,689,026 into the 20–24 age group, and 4,842,431 into the 25–29 age group. The aggregation of these three subgroups resulted in a total 15–29-year-old population of 11,340,607, which constituted approximately 17.56% of Zhejiang’s overall resident population (64,567,588) in 2020 [9]. Data collection and definitions

Gonorrhea case data were obtained from the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention, which mandated that all suspected and confirmed gonorrhea cases be reported within 24 h. All gonorrhea cases were diagnosed in accordance with the corresponding national standards. Specifically, the Diagnostic Criteria and Management of Gonorrhea (GB 15975 − 1995) was applied before 2007 [10]; the Diagnostic Criteria of Gonorrhea (WS 268–2007) was used from 2007 to 2019 [11]; and the Diagnostic Criteria of Gonorrhea (WS 268–2019) has been implemented since 2019 [12]. All cases were diagnosed through a comprehensive assessment of patient history, physical examination, and laboratory findings. In this study, both clinically and laboratory-confirmed cases were included, while only the latter were documented from 2012 to 2024. Clinically confirmed cases referred to patients who met the epidemiological history and typical clinical manifestations without positive laboratory results, and clinical diagnosis for such cases was based on epidemiological history—including unprotected sexual intercourse, multiple sexual partners, or contact with an infected partner—along with typical clinical manifestations; those manifestations included dysuria and urethral discharge in men, and increased vaginal discharge accompanied by purulent cervical secretions in women. In contrast, laboratory-confirmed cases required direct detection of Neisseria gonorrhoeae via validated methods, primarily Gram-stained smear microscopy, culture, or nucleic acid amplification testing [10–12].

Case information was extracted based on disease onset date, ranging from January 1, 2005, to December 31, 2024, and restricted to individuals with a current residential address in Zhejiang Province. Corresponding demographic data were also sourced from the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention.

Statistical analysis

Joinpoint regression model

The Joinpoint regression model analyzes temporal trends in disease distribution by fitting segmented linear regressions to the natural logarithms of incidence rates. It identifies points in time where trends change significantly, calculates the annual percentage change with its 95% confidence interval (CI) for each resulting segment, and thus characterizes distinct periods of disease progression [13]. In this study, we utilized the Joinpoint Regression Program, version 5.4.0.0 (released in April 2025; Statistical Research and Applications Branch, US National Cancer Institute, https://surveillance.cancer.gov/joinpoint/download), to analyze the temporal trends in the reported gonorrhea incidence.

Age-Period-Cohort model

The APC model is widely employed in epidemiological research. Based on a log-linear model, it quantifies the independent effects of age, period, and birth cohort by decomposing temporal trends in disease rates, thereby offering a more accurate reflection of the influence of these three factors on gonorrhea incidence [14]. The age effect captures variations in disease risk across different stages of life. The period effect reflects the impact of external factors—such as economic conditions, diagnostic capabilities, and treatment or intervention strategies—on disease incidence over calendar time. The cohort effect represents the influence of risk factors specific to different birth cohorts on the risk of developing the disease [15]. Within the APC framework, net drift and local drift are key parameters. Net drift estimates the overall average annual percentage change in incidence over time, while local drift estimates the annual percentage change specific to each age group, capturing age-specific temporal trends. Values greater than zero indicate an increasing trend in incidence, and values less than zero indicate a decreasing trend. Cross-sectional and longitudinal age curves were adjusted for cohort and period biases, respectively, to better represent cross-sectional and longitudinal age-specific rates. The period/cohort rate ratio (RR) indicates the relative risk of a specific period or birth cohort compared to the reference group. An RR > 1 signifies a higher risk than the reference, whereas an RR < 1 indicates a lower risk [14].

Notably, the APC model is inherently subject to the identification problem arising from the linear dependency between age, period, and cohort (i.e., cohort=period − age). To address this issue, we adopted the default method of the APC Model Web Tool (http://analysistools.nci.nih.gov/apc/), the intrinsic estimator—a widely recognized approach for resolving the linear dependency issue in APC analysis by constraining the sum of weighted linear trends of age, period, and cohort effects to zero [17]. In this study, we used this tool to estimate and visualize model parameters, with age and period intervals set to equal lengths of 5 years in accordance with its requirements; the reference groups for period and birth cohort were designated as the central intervals (2010–2014 for period and 1970–1974 for cohort, with RR = 1), and the significance of estimable parameters and functions was assessed using two-sided Wald χ² tests.

Results

General characteristics of reported gonorrhea in Zhejiang Province

Between 2005 and 2024, a total of 347,868 gonorrhea cases were reported in Zhejiang Province, corresponding to an average annual incidence rate of 31.87 per 100,000 population and a male-to-female ratio of 3.66. The epidemiological trend exhibited distinct phase-specific characteristics: the reported incidence decreased continuously from 53.46 to 25.51 per 100,000 between 2005 and 2012, rebounded beginning in 2012, and reached 39.19 per 100,000 by 2017. Thereafter, aside from a brief increase in 2021, the rate showed a general decline, falling to the study-period low of 15.23 per 100,000 in 2024. Analysis by age group indicated that the 25–29 age group had the highest reported incidence, while a minor peak was also observed among children aged 0–4 years.

In terms of sex distribution, the incidence rate was consistently higher in males than in females, and trends over time were generally consistent between both sexes and the overall population. By age group, males exhibited higher incidence rates than females in all groups except the 5–14 age group. The age-specific distribution among males showed a single peak in the 25–29 age group, whereas a bimodal pattern was observed among females, with distinct peaks in the 20–24 and 55–59 age groups (see Fig. 2 for details).

Joinpoint regression model

The reported incidence of gonorrhea in Zhejiang Province exhibited an overall declining trend from 2005 to 2024, with two inflection points identified in 2012 and 2017. Joinpoint analysis revealed three distinct phases: a significant decrease during 2005–2012, with an annual percentage change of − 10.56% (95% CI: − 15.63%, − 7.95%; P < 0.01); a significant increase during 2012–2017, with an annual percentage change of 5.90% (95% CI: 0.71%, 17.03%; P = 0.03); and a significant decreasing trend during 2017–2024, with an annual percentage change of − 10.24% (95% CI: − 13.70%, − 7.79%; P < 0.01). This overall trend was consistent for both males and females (Fig. 3).

Age-Period-Cohort model

Net drift and local drift of notification rate of gonorrhea

The APC model indicated a net drift of − 4.80% per year (95% CI: − 5.74%, − 3.86%), reflecting an average annual decline in the reported gonorrhea incidence over the study period. Local drift values revealed an increasing trend in the 15–19 age group, with an annual percentage change of 4.88% (95% CI: 3.17%, 6.62%). In contrast, significant decreasing trends were observed in the 0–9 and 25–69 age groups. Notably, the 0–4 age group showed the most rapid decline, with a local drift of − 17.00% (95% CI: − 27.33%, − 5.21%).

In subgroup analyses, the net drift was − 4.98% (95% CI: − 5.96%, − 3.97%) for males and − 4.70% (95% CI: − 5.87%, − 3.52%) for females, with no statistically significant difference between sexes. Similarly, local drift patterns across age groups did not differ significantly between males and females, and were consistent with the overall population trend (Fig. 4).

The shaded areas around each curve represent the 95% confidence intervals for the local drift estimates; curves correspond to females, males, and the total population

Age effect

Given that the absolute value of the net drift exceeded 1%, the longitudinal age curves were analyzed to illustrate age-specific effects. After adjustment for the period and cohort effects, the curves revealed a major peak in gonorrhea incidence risk in the 20–24 age group. A minor, earlier peak was also observed in the 0–4 age group, with a rate relatively higher than that in the subsequent 5–14 age group, although the absolute risk remained low. The incidence risk was 11.99 per 100,000 (95% CI: 6.98, 20.58) in the 0–4 age group. Following a brief decline in the 5–14 age group, the risk increased markedly beginning in the 15–19 age group and peaked at 229.58 per 100,000 (95% CI: 193.59, 272.25) in the 20–24 age group, after which it gradually declined in older age groups. Notably, the incidence rate in the 20–29 age group was significantly higher than that in all other age groups.

In terms of sex-specific patterns, the age effect curves for males and females closely resembled that of the overall population. The incidence risk was lower in males than in females in the 0–14 age group, whereas males exhibited a significantly higher risk than females in the 15–54 age group (Fig. 5A).

Period effect

After adjusting for age and cohort effects with 2010–2014 as the reference period, the period RR was calculated to compare gonorrhea incidence risks across different time intervals. The results indicated that the risk was highest in the total population during 2005–2009 (RR = 1.62; 95% CI: 1.49, 1.77), which was significantly elevated compared to the reference period, while the lowest risk was observed in 2020–2024 (RR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.61, 0.78). The temporal pattern of period RR was consistent between males and females and aligned with that of the overall population (Fig. 5B).

Cohort effect

The birth cohorts in Zhejiang Province from 1920 to 2024 were divided into 5-year intervals, and an APC model analysis was performed using the 1970–1974 cohort as the reference. Results indicated that the risk of gonorrhea incidence in the overall population generally decreased with later birth cohorts. However, a slight increase in risk was observed among those born between 2000 and 2009. The RR declined from 36.51 (95% CI: 1.07, 1243.19) in the 1920–1924 cohort to 0.04 (95% CI: 0.00, 0.34) in the 2020–2024 cohort. In both males and females, trends in gonorrhea risk across birth cohorts were consistent with those observed in the overall population (Fig. 5C).

Discussions

To investigate the long-term trends and driving factors of gonorrhea incidence in eastern China, this study employed an integrated approach using the APC model and Joinpoint regression analysis based on reported case data from Zhejiang Province. The APC model, through its inherent mathematical structure, effectively addresses the issue of linear dependency among age, period, and cohort effects, enabling the quantification of their independent net influences on changes in gonorrhea incidence. This analytical framework helps accurately identify potential high-risk populations, evaluate the impact of historical intervention policies, and predict potential future disease burden, thereby providing high-quality evidence to inform and optimize public health strategies.

Descriptive epidemiological findings showed that the average annual reported incidence of gonorrhea in Zhejiang Province during the study period was 31.87 per 100,000 population. By comparison, the average reported incidence in China from 2004 to 2021 was 9.46 per 100,000 [18], indicating a relatively higher disease burden of gonorrhea in Zhejiang. These findings suggest that gonorrhea control remains a serious challenge in the province, necessitating the establishment of a multi-tiered prevention and control system. Key priorities should include enhancing surveillance and screening among high-risk populations (e.g., commercial sex workers, men who have sex with men), optimizing diagnostic and drug resistance testing, implementing tailored health education, and establishing standardized procedures for partner notification and treatment. In addition, the use of digital tools—such as virtual simulation platforms—should be strengthened to improve training for healthcare workers and expand health education among high-risk groups. Finally, cross-sector collaboration among education, healthcare, and community management agencies is essential for building a comprehensive gonorrhea prevention and control framework.

Joinpoint regression analysis indicated an overall declining trend in the reported incidence of gonorrhea, which was consistent with the net drift estimate showing an average annual decrease of approximately 4.80%. A more detailed stage-specific analysis revealed that the reported incidence declined continuously from 2005 to 2012, increased gradually from 2012 to 2017, and resumed a downward trend from 2017 to 2024. The increase observed after 2012 might be attributable to a combination of potential epidemiological and surveillance-related factors: on the one hand, possible epidemiological drivers—such as the growing prevalence of antimicrobial-resistant strains, increased transmission potential among asymptomatic carriers, and the evolution of social networks—might contribute to a genuine uptick in gonorrhea infections [19]. On the other hand, developments in surveillance and diagnostic capacity during this period might also play a role: the wider adoption of highly sensitive nucleic acid amplification tests and intensified targeted screening efforts in high-risk populations (e.g., sexually active young adults) might enhance case detection capabilities, which might in turn lead to the identification of more previously under-reported infections that contributed to the observed upward trend in reported incidence [20]. After 2017, however, Zhejiang Province gradually strengthened its gonorrhea prevention and control system by actively implementing the national action plan to contain antimicrobial resistance and enhancing the surveillance network, which might contribute to the subsequent decline in reported incidence [21]. Globally, gonorrhea trends vary across countries. For example, South Korea experienced an overall decline in the notification rate from 39.76 per 100,000 in 2010 to 22.96 per 100,000 in 2021 [8], a trend similar to that observed in our study. In contrast, the United States saw a rise in infection rates by 60.6% among males and 43.6% among females from 2015 to 2019, reaching 188.4 per 100,000 in 2019 [6]. Similarly, in EU/EEA countries, the notification rate increased from 6.0 per 100,000 in 2012 to 13.7 per 100,000 in 2021 [7]. This alignment between Zhejiang and South Korea might be attributed to shared regional similarities: firstly, both implemented rigorous sexually transmitted disease surveillance systems and targeted intervention strategies (e.g., enhanced screening for high-risk populations and standardized diagnostic protocols) that facilitated timely case detection and control; secondly, cultural factors such as relatively conservative sexual attitudes and higher compliance with public health guidelines might reduce transmission risks compared to Western countries; thirdly, both regions might be in a similar stage of epidemic transition, with mature public health infrastructure supporting sustained prevention efforts [8]. These variations across regions were closely related to differences in economic development levels, gonorrhea prevention and intervention policies, climatic and environmental factors, sexual attitudes, access to healthcare resources, and national laws and regulations [22].

The period effect analysis showed that the risk of gonorrhea was highest during 2005–2009, rebounded moderately during 2015–2019, and fell to its lowest level in the study period during 2020–2024. After 2005, China’s efforts to standardize the prevention and control of notifiable infectious diseases, combined with Zhejiang’s targeted measures—such as enhanced monitoring of entertainment venues and mobile populations, expanded testing coverage in primary care institutions, promotion of condom use among sexually active groups, and public awareness campaigns on sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [23, 24]—might contribute to an overall decline in incidence. The revision of the national Administrative Measures for the Prevention and Control of STDs in 2012 strengthened case reporting requirements [25]. This led to more standardized gonorrhea management and a reduction in underdiagnosis and underreporting, which might consequently result in an increase in the number of reported cases. In addition, with rapid socioeconomic development and the proliferation of social media, young people might gain easier access to sexually explicit content, which might lead to increased sexual activity and higher-risk sexual behaviors. Greater mobility among youth and generally insufficient sexuality education in colleges might further accelerate disease transmission [26, 27]. After 2020, gonorrhea risk began to decline again, which might be attributed to a combination of targeted interventions and technological innovations, strengthened outreach to key populations, deepened public health education, and improved policy coordination. In particular, the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic and associated containment measures—such as lockdowns, social distancing, temporary adjustments in health service delivery, changes in sexual behavior, and reduced international travel—might also play a significant role in curbing gonorrhea transmission [28, 29].

The age effect analysis revealed a distinct age-stratified pattern in gonorrhea incidence. The incidence rate in the 20–29 age group was significantly higher than that in other age groups, and the 15–19 age group showed an upward trend, suggesting a shift toward younger age groups in gonorrhea occurrence. This finding is consistent with domestic studies in China [30–32] and aligns with patterns observed in Europe and the United States [6, 7]. This phenomenon may be explained by two main factors. Firstly, this demographic is at the peak of sexual activity and exhibits a higher incidence of high-risk sexual behaviors [33, 34]. Secondly, adolescents and young adults are in a developmental stage characterized by psychological susceptibility to peer and media influences, including exposure to content through electronic devices and adverse environments, which might contribute to emotional and cognitive biases that increase vulnerability to gonorrhea infection [35, 36]. Notably, a small but distinct peak in incidence was observed in the 0–4 age group, which might be associated with perinatal mother-to-child transmission. However, local drift estimates from this study indicated that the 0–4 age group experienced the largest decline in incidence among all age groups. This strongly suggests that Zhejiang Province has achieved tangible success in preventing and controlling vertical transmission of gonorrhea, particularly through early screening and intervention strategies for neonatal infection, leading to effective curtailment of mother-to-child transmission. Specifically, China’s Measures for the Administration of Sexually Transmitted Diseases Prevention and Control mandated standardized prevention of gonococcal mother-to-child transmission when it was first promulgated on January 1, 2013 [37]. Prior to this regulatory update, foundational protocols for neonatal gonorrhea prevention had already been established in national clinical guidelines, including the Diagnostic Criteria for Gonorrhea (WS 268–2007) issued by the Ministry of Health in 2007 [10]. Zhejiang Province consistently adopted and enforced these national standards throughout the entire study period. Key interventions included routine prenatal gonorrhea screening for pregnant women, timely treatment of infected mothers, and prophylactic measures for newborns (e.g., instillation of 1% silver nitrate eye drops or application of 0.5% tetracycline eye ointment immediately after birth) [10, 37], which might collectively contribute to the effective curtailment of mother-to-child transmission. In terms of sex distribution, this study found that after age 15, the reported incidence of gonorrhea was consistently higher in males than in females. This pattern may be explained by several factors. Firstly, clinical manifestations of gonorrhea in men are predominantly urological and more apparent, leading to higher healthcare-seeking behavior. In contrast, symptoms in women are often subtle or asymptomatic, resulting in lower detection rates [2]. Secondly, men generally exhibit broader social networks and higher mobility, which may increase the probability of infection. Furthermore, among female STI patients, barriers such as social stigma or traditional attitudes may reduce their likelihood of seeking care, contributing to relatively lower reporting rates [38–40].

The cohort effect analysis indicated that the risk of gonorrhea generally decreased among later birth cohorts. This trend is closely associated with ongoing public health interventions in China, including improvements in public awareness and preventive behaviors, enhanced access to and comprehension of health information, and a strengthened healthcare delivery system. China established a national infectious disease reporting system as early as the 1950s and implemented numerous initiatives since then to reduce the infectious disease burden through robust surveillance and reporting mechanisms [41]. Subsequent strategies to interrupt STD transmission—including the prohibition and crackdown on all forms of commercial sex, large-scale health education campaigns, universal screening, voluntary confidential contact tracing, and active treatment for infected individuals—might effectively reduce gonorrhea risk by 1964 [42]. However, a relative increase in gonorrhea risk was observed in the 1995–2009 birth cohort, which might be attributed to sweeping socioeconomic and lifestyle changes resulting from economic reforms and the open-door policy initiated in the early 1980s. These changes contributed to more open sexual attitudes and an increase in commercial and extramarital sex, thereby raising the risk of STIs including gonorrhea [43]. In response to the increasing risk of gonorrhea, the Chinese government initiated a series of measures after 2010 to standardize the diagnosis and treatment of STDs, establish specialized STD clinics, and promote first-line treatment regimens for gonorrhea. Gonorrhea medications were included in the National Essential Medicines List, and testing and treatment costs were made reimbursable through personal medical insurance accounts, with strict protections for patient privacy [25]. In 2016, the State Council issued the National Action Plan for Curbing Bacterial Drug Resistance, which led to the gradual establishment of a Gonococcal Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network spanning 19 provinces [44]. At the provincial level, Zhejiang released its first multi-sector mid- to long-term plan, the Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of the Spread of STDs in Zhejiang Province (2023–2027), in 2023 [45]. As the first provincial-level multi-sector joint mid- to long-term plan for STD prevention and control nationwide, this plan was jointly implemented by 7 departments including health commission, education, and public security authorities, marking Zhejiang’s shift from single-disease management to a “multi-disease co-prevention” model. This plan aims to improve the STD prevention and control system and enhance comprehensive capacity through eight major actions, including health education, integrated surveillance, and standardized diagnosis and treatment (specifically involving unifying and standardizing the diagnostic procedures for gonorrhea and establishing more standardized testing laboratories across the province), with clearly defined departmental responsibilities and priority areas. Collectively, these measures—deployed at both national and local levels—might facilitate patient cure and reduce transmission, while they might also help prevent new infections through surveillance and awareness campaigns, potentially playing a critical and positive role in containing the spread of gonorrhea and other STDs.

Limitations

Some limitations of the present analysis must be considered. Firstly, as the data were derived primarily from the passive surveillance system, inherent limitations—including under-reporting (especially of asymptomatic infections), inconsistent diagnostic standards across healthcare settings, and potential reporting delays—alongside reporting biases, might compromise data completeness and representativeness, and the data were also unable to identify infection sources. Secondly, in the assessment of cohort effects, certain estimates were associated with relatively wide confidence intervals, indicating limited precision. The surveillance data did not cover the high-risk periods of these early cohorts, thus compelling the model to rely on uncertain extrapolation. Additionally, factors such as very low case numbers in the early years, changes in diagnostic sensitivity, and differences in clinical practices during that era might further compromise the precision and comparability of these estimates. Thirdly, the lengthy study period witnessed notable evolution in the clinical and laboratory diagnostic standards for gonorrhea. Specifically, the clinically diagnosed cases were included in the early stage of the study, while only laboratory-confirmed cases were documented beginning in 2012. Such temporal variations could introduce inconsistencies in case definitions and confirmation methods over time, and might consequently introduce the bias.

Conclusions

The overall incidence rate of gonorrhea in Zhejiang Province exhibited a downward trend, underscoring the effectiveness of existing prevention and control strategies. The period effect showed an overall declining risk over time, while the cohort effect was marked by lower incidence risk among those born in later years. In terms of the age effect, the 20–29 years age group was a high-incidence population, which should be controlled through a combination of school-based health education integrated with digital platforms and the expansion of affordable and accessible gonorrhea testing services, while also exploring novel interventions such as expedited partner therapy (EPT). Furthermore, future targeted interventions for males should include incorporating gonorrhea testing into relevant occupational health check-ups and establishing confidential STI testing services.
