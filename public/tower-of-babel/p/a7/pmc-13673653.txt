Communication and educational gaps in end-of-life care in Italian internal medicine wards: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mario Stabile, Maria Ghinatti, Elena Migliore, Fabrizio Elia, Francesca Riccardi, Lorenzo Ferrando, Nicolò Gilardi, Francesco Ravera, Benedetta Cigolini, Martina Penso, Daniele Dotta, Elena Barbagelata, Gabriella Biffa, Franco Aprà, Luigi Fenoglio, Nicola Girtler, Roberto Pontremoli, Christian Bracco, Alberto Ballestrero, Gabriele Zoppoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnd-of-life (EOL) care is a frequent and complex aspect of internal medicine, yet data on physicians’ training, clinical practices, and perceived challenges are limited. This study aimed to explore current practices, knowledge gaps, and unmet needs in EOL care among Italian internal medicine physicians and residents.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted under the auspices of the Piedmont-Liguria-Aosta Valley Section of the Italian Society of Internal Medicine (SIMI). The survey included 25 closed-ended questions addressing five domains: epidemiological, pharmacological, practical, psychological, and educational aspects of EOL care. Physicians actively involved in clinical care from multiple internal medicine wards participated voluntarily. Responses were collected anonymously via paper and online formats. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize data. Differences between groups (specialists vs. residents, oncology/hematology vs. non-oncology wards, low vs. medium vs. high clinical intensity wards) were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests. Associations between ordinal variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.ResultsOf approximately 7,400 invited physicians, 619 completed the survey (response rate ≈ 8.4%; median age 38 years; 60.5% female; 70.8% board-certified internists). More than 88% reported facing terminal patients at least weekly, yet only 2.5% were aware of Advance Directives for > 25% of inpatients. Use of sedatives and analgesics during terminal sedation varied, while discontinuation of active therapies, total parenteral nutrition (TPN), and transfusions was widely accepted. Internal protocols and dedicated multidisciplinary discussions were infrequently used. Most respondents reported difficulties communicating with patients and families at EOL. Formal training in EOL care was perceived as largely inadequate during undergraduate, residency, and postgraduate education. Compared with residents, board-certified internists more frequently reported postgraduate/Master training, whereas residents more frequently reported undergraduate and residency training in EOL. Exploratory correlation analyses generated hypotheses linking communication difficulties with patients to those with family members and training experiences across educational stages.ConclusionsThe study highlights substantial gaps in training, communication, and organizational support, independent of clinical setting. Structured education and integration of psychological support are urgently needed. These findings can inform curriculum development, hospital policies, and future research to improve physician preparedness and patient-centered EOL care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-026-02241-7.

Background

The care of patients at the end of life (EOL) represents a common and significant challenge for internists, carrying important social, ethical, and psychological implications [1]. Worldwide, the leading causes of death include cardiovascular diseases, respiratory conditions, dementia, and infections [2]. In Italy, as in Europe and North America, cancer remains one of the leading causes of death [3, 4]. Each of these diseases follows a distinct clinical trajectory and requires timely and appropriate palliative care interventions [5]. Although differences exist among prevalent conditions—such as oncological and cardiovascular diseases [6]—healthcare institutions frequently represent the final setting in which terminally ill patients receive care.

Palliative care may be delivered at home [7, 8] or within healthcare institutions such as hospitals and hospices, particularly at the EOL [9]. In Italy, most deaths occur in healthcare settings [3]. High-intensity care environments, including intensive care units, may represent the final stage of the disease trajectory [10], often becoming places where patients die [11] and where ethical dilemmas are more frequently encountered [12, 13]. Internal medicine wards also provide care for a substantial number of patients who die during hospitalization, although the exact epidemiological burden is not fully documented. Nevertheless, existing literature suggests that medical wards play a central role in EOL care for patients with a wide range of conditions, including infectious, cardiovascular, and oncological diseases [14–16].

In recent years, several guidelines and consensus documents have been published to promote good clinical practice in EOL management [17–19]. Beyond technical and pharmacological expertise, high-quality EOL care requires the establishment of a strong therapeutic alliance with patients and their families. In this context, effective and compassionate communication between physicians, patients, and caregivers is essential [20, 21]. Moreover, physicians are frequently required to face complex ethical decisions, such as treatment limitation, palliative sedation, and advance care planning [22].

Adequate physician training in EOL care, starting in undergraduate medical education [23], continuing through residency [24, 25], and extending into continuing professional development [26], is essential to ensure appropriate patient-centered care. The COVID-19 pandemic further highlighted pre-existing gaps in EOL training among both specialists and residents in internal medicine [27, 28]. In Italy, the introduction of Law 219/2017 on “Informed Consent and Advance Treatment Provisions” (Disposizioni Anticipate di Trattamento, DAT) established a legal framework for advance care planning and shared decision-making at the end of life, making it crucial to assess how Italian internists translate this framework into daily clinical practice. Despite the clinical relevance of EOL care in internal medicine [29], data describing how EOL issues are managed by hospital-based internists remain limited [30, 31].

Against this background, the present study had the following specific aims: (i) to describe how Italian internal medicine physicians and residents manage EOL care in daily clinical practice across pharmacological, practical, psychological and organizational domains; (ii) to quantify perceived gaps in EOL-related education at the undergraduate, residency and postgraduate levels; (iii) to explore whether perceptions of EOL care differ according to professional role (specialist vs. resident), clinical intensity of the working unit, or the oncology/hematology orientation of the department; and (iv) to identify associations between communication difficulties, pharmacological practices and educational experience that may inform targeted educational and organizational interventions.

Methods

Study design and participants

The EOLO initiative was launched in 2021 under the auspices of the Piedmont–Liguria–Aosta Valley Section of the Italian Society of Internal Medicine (SIMI) [32] and involved a multidisciplinary working group composed of eight board-certified internists, eight internal medicine residents, and three psychologists drawn from multiple internal medicine departments in Liguria and Piedmont. The aim of the initiative was to explore unmet needs in end-of-life (EOL) care within internal medicine wards and to identify potential areas for improvement. After three hybrid (in-person and online) consensus meetings, this collaborative effort led to the development of the EOLO (End-Of-Life Opinions) survey, designed for use in internal medicine wards and structured to investigate five key domains—epidemiological, pharmacological, practical, psychological, and educational—while limiting the total number of items to 25 to maximize compliance.

This study was designed as a cross-sectional survey targeting physicians actively involved in clinical care. Participation was voluntary, and no incentives were offered. Survey responses were collected anonymously. Only healthcare professionals engaged in direct clinical activity were required to complete the full questionnaire.

Survey development

The development of the EOLO survey followed a structured, stepwise process. First, the working group conducted an assessment of the current state of the art in EOL care and reviewed existing literature and guidelines. This was followed by the sharing of clinical experiences among participants and the identification of critical areas requiring further investigation. Based on this process, a questionnaire was developed through iterative discussion and consensus within the multidisciplinary group, with content validity grounded in the clinical expertise of the panel and in existing literature, to reflect real-world clinical practice and perceived needs in EOL management. The questionnaire was not subjected to a formal psychometric validation or a separate pilot study before its diffusion; this is acknowledged as a limitation. Item wording and response options were, however, reviewed and refined in multiple rounds by the working group, and the survey was administered first in a small group of internal medicine attendees at the SIMI Interregional Congress (paper version) before broader online dissemination, which provided an opportunity to verify clarity and completion time.

The final questionnaire consisted of 25 closed-ended questions and was designed to be user-friendly, with an estimated completion time of approximately 10 min. The survey began with a brief introduction outlining the objectives of the study, the estimated time commitment, and information about the research team.

The questionnaire used in this study was specifically developed by the authors for the purposes of the present research and has not been previously published. The full English-language version of the questionnaire is provided in the Supplementary Material.

All questions were closed-ended. Most items included five response options, while education-related questions included three response options. Responses were ordinal or categorical in nature. Frequency items used a five-point scale ranging from “Never” to “Almost always”; agreement items used a five-point Likert scale from “Completely disagree” to “Completely agree”; items on the percentage of patients used five categories from “No patients” to “>75% of patients”; education-related items used three response options (“No, not at all”, “Yes, but insufficient”, “Yes, adequately”). The complete list of response options for each item is provided in Supplementary Material 1 (Questionnaire).

Survey administration

The survey was administered in both paper-based and online formats. The paper version was first distributed on 27 May 2022 during the Interregional Congress of the Piedmont–Liguria–Aosta Valley Section of SIMI. Subsequently, the online version was emailed to SIMI members on 27 January 2023, with two reminder emails, and to members of the Italian Federation of Associations of Hospital Doctors on Internal Medicine (FADOI) on 3 June 2024 through their respective secretariats.

All questions and response options were originally provided in Italian.

Statistical analysis

Survey responses were initially analyzed using descriptive statistics. For quantitative variables, median values along with minimum and maximum ranges were reported. For categorical variables, absolute frequencies and percentages of the total sample were calculated.

Likert-scale and other ordinal responses were recoded into numeric variables reflecting the rank order of the response categories prior to analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using R (R version 4.4.x; analyses run within RStudio 2025.09.0 + 387). Given the ordinal nature of the survey data and their non-normal distribution, non-parametric statistical methods were applied.

Comparisons between two independent groups—professional role (board-certified internists vs. residents) and presence of an oncology/hematology-oriented department (yes vs. no)—were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Comparisons involving more than two groups—specifically, clinical intensity of the working unit (low vs. medium vs. high)—were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When applicable, the direction of group differences was explored by comparing mean ranks. Effect sizes were quantified as the rank-biserial correlation for two-group comparisons and as epsilon-squared (ε²) for Kruskal–Wallis tests.

Associations between survey items were assessed using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient (rho), which was preferred over the chi-squared test of independence to better capture the strength and direction (positive or negative) of associations between ordinal variables. Items 12b and 24a–c were excluded from the Spearman correlation analysis because they were nominal (qualitative) rather than ordinal variables.

For all analyses, a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To reduce the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. 95% confidence intervals for Spearman’s ρ were obtained using the Fisher z-transformation.

Characteristics of the interviewees (N = 616)

The median age of respondents was 38 years (range 25–81 years). Overall, 373 participants (60.5%) were female and 243 (39.5%) were male. Most respondents were board-certified internists (437; 70.8%), while 180 (29.2%) were internal medicine residents.

Regarding workplace characteristics, 307 participants (49.8%) worked in low-intensity internal medicine wards, 182 (29.5%) in medium-intensity units, and 128 (20.7%) in high-intensity care units. Eighty respondents (13.0%) reported working in an oncology/hematology-oriented internal medicine department, while the majority (537; 87.0%) did not.

All response tables are provided in the Supplementary Material. Full item-level frequency distributions are reported in Supplementary Tables S1–S5 (one table per domain), and detailed subgroup comparisons and correlation matrices in Supplementary Tables S6–S8.

Epidemiological domain

The majority of respondents reported facing terminality and terminal sedation in routine clinical practice: 548/619 (88.5%) did so at least several times per month (“sometimes” to “almost always”), while only 71 (11.5%) reported “never” or “rarely” (Fig. 1a). Conversely, awareness of Advance Directives was strikingly limited: 426/617 respondents (69.0%) reported not being aware of any patient with formalised directives in their ward, and only 15 (2.5%) reported awareness for more than 25% of admitted patients (Fig. 1b). Full frequency distributions are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Pharmacological domain

Reported use of midazolam in terminal sedation was heterogeneous, ranging from “never” (62/619, 10.0%) to “almost always” (161, 26.0%), with no single response category exceeding one third of respondents (Fig. 2a).

Fig. 2Heterogeneity in the pharmacological management of terminal sedation

Heterogeneity in the pharmacological management of terminal sedation

Opinions on restricting morphine to symptomatic relief (pain, dyspnea, discomfort) during terminal sedation were similarly heterogeneous: 188/619 (30.3%) disagreed or completely disagreed, 201 (32.5%) slightly agreed, and 230 (37.2%) mostly or completely agreed (Fig. 2b). This distribution indicates a marked lack of consensus regarding the appropriate role of opioids in palliative sedation, an issue addressed in the Discussion.

In contrast, agreement on discontinuing active therapies during terminal sedation was high: 462/619 respondents (74.6%) mostly or completely agreed, while only 46 (7.4%) disagreed.

Agreement was even stronger for the discontinuation of total parenteral nutrition and blood product transfusions, endorsed (mostly or completely) by 485/619 respondents (78.3%). Full item-level distributions for the pharmacological domain are reported in Supplementary Table S2.

Practical domain

Use of internal protocols or guidelines for EOL patient assessment and management was infrequent: 429/619 respondents (69.3%) reported using them “never” or “rarely”, and only 97 (15.7%) used them “often” or “almost always”.

Dedicated institutional moments for EOL discussion (among colleagues or in multidisciplinary teams) were also uncommon, being reported as “never” or “rarely” available by 411/619 respondents (66.4%) and as “often” or “almost always” available by only 95 (15.4%) (Fig. 3a). Full item-level distributions are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

Psychological domain

Most respondents reported difficulties communicating with terminally ill patients: 478/619 (77.2%) acknowledged at least some difficulty (“slightly” to “completely agree”), while only 141 (22.8%) disagreed or completely disagreed (Fig. 3b).

Difficulties in communicating with family members were also commonly reported, although less marked: 344/619 respondents (55.6%) agreed at least slightly with this statement, whereas 275 (44.4%) disagreed or completely disagreed. Full item-level distributions are reported in Supplementary Table S4.

Educational domain

Across all stages of medical education, fewer than 10% of respondents reported having received adequate dedicated training in EOL care. During undergraduate medical education (medical degree), 497/619 (80.3%) reported “no training at all” and only 11 (1.8%) considered it adequate (Fig. 4a).

Fig. 4End-of-life training and self-perceived preparedness

End-of-life training and self-perceived preparedness

Reports of EOL training during residency were similarly limited: 418/619 (67.5%) received none, and only 52 (8.4%) considered it adequate.

Postgraduate/Master courses dedicated to EOL care were attended by a minority of respondents, with 498/619 (80.4%) reporting no such training and only 58 (9.4%) considering it adequate.

Consistently, when asked about overall self-perceived preparation in EOL management, only 10/619 respondents (1.6%) felt “completely adequate”, while 609 (98.4%) rated their preparation as either “partially adequate” or “overall inadequate” (Fig. 4b). Full item-level distributions for the educational domain are reported in Supplementary Table S5.

Comparisons of mean responses between physicians working in oncology/hematology-oriented departments and those working in other internal medicine wards revealed no statistically significant differences across survey items. Across clinical intensity levels (low-, medium-, and high-intensity care), only three items reached statistical significance, all with negligible effect sizes (ε² ≤ 0.02). Detailed results are reported in the Supplementary Material.

In contrast, several items differed significantly between board-certified internists and internal medicine residents (Supplementary Table S6a). The differences most relevant to the educational focus of this study concerned training exposure: specialists more frequently reported having attended dedicated Master/Postgraduate courses in EOL care (Q23; adjusted p < 0.001), whereas residents reported higher levels of formal EOL training during undergraduate medical education (Q21; adjusted p < 0.001) and during residency (Q22; adjusted p < 0.001). Discussion

This cross-sectional survey, endorsed by professionals from multiple centres, was designed to describe how Italian internal medicine physicians and residents manage EOL care in daily clinical practice, to quantify perceived gaps in EOL-related education, to explore whether perceptions of EOL care differ according to professional role, clinical intensity or oncology/hematology orientation of the working unit, and to identify associations that may inform targeted interventions. Three findings stand out: EOL situations are part of weekly clinical practice for almost nine out of ten respondents, yet awareness of patient-formalised Advance Directives is exceedingly rare; structured EOL teaching is largely absent across all stages of medical education, with self-perceived preparation rated as adequate by only 1.6% of respondents; and dedicated multidisciplinary moments and internal protocols supporting EOL decisions are uncommon. Below we discuss these findings in light of the existing literature and consider their implications.

The finding that EOL situations are encountered routinely by Italian internists supports the recognition of palliative and end-of-life competencies as a core component of the internist’s profile. Mortality data for Italian internal medicine wards converge on a similar picture, with high in-hospital mortality reported across the leading causes of admission [14–16], confirming that medical wards are a central setting for end-of-life care. Against this background, the very low awareness of Advance Directives is particularly noteworthy. The Italian Law 219/2017 on “Informed Consent and Advance Treatment Provisions” (DAT) has been in force for several years, yet only 2.5% of respondents were aware of formalised directives for more than 25% of their patients, and almost seven out of ten reported not being aware of any. This gap is consistent with reports of limited implementation of advance care planning in hospital settings across Europe and Latin America [22, 34–41] and points to a need for systematic integration of advance care planning into routine clinical workflows rather than relying on individual initiative.

In the pharmacological domain, two patterns deserve specific comment. The first is the heterogeneous use of midazolam, which is the agent of choice for palliative sedation in international guidelines and consensus documents [17–19]. Only about half of respondents reported using it “often” or “almost always”, whereas roughly one third used it “never” or “rarely”. The second, and more concerning, is the persisting endorsement of morphine within terminal sedation: nearly 70% of respondents agreed, at least slightly, that morphine use in terminal sedation should be reserved for pain, dyspnea or discomfort — an assertion that conflates two distinct interventions. Current ESICM and ESMO guidelines are explicit that the goal of palliative sedation is the relief of refractory suffering through reduction of consciousness, and that opioids should not be used as sedating agents; they remain indicated for the symptomatic management of pain and dyspnea, but on a separate clinical pathway from sedation itself [17–19]. The pattern observed in our sample therefore points to a misalignment between routine practice and current guidance that has direct implications for both training and the development of internal protocols. By contrast, broad agreement on discontinuing active therapies, total parenteral nutrition and blood product transfusions at the end of life suggests that the underlying palliative principle — prioritising comfort over disease-directed interventions — is widely accepted; what appears to be lacking is the operational translation of this principle into pharmacologically coherent sedation protocols.

The organisational picture is consistent with this gap between principles and practice. The very limited use of internal protocols and the rarity of dedicated multidisciplinary moments for EOL discussion suggest that decisions in this domain are largely individual rather than team-based and protocol-supported. This is in line with previous European reports of substantial inter-hospital variability in palliative care provision within medical wards [9, 14–16] and contrasts with the team-based model recommended for EOL care in critical and high-intensity settings [10–13, 17]. In our sample, this pattern did not differ significantly between oncology/hematology-oriented departments and non-oncology wards, nor across levels of clinical intensity, suggesting that organisational vulnerability in EOL care is broadly shared across hospital internal medicine and is not confined to lower-acuity settings.

The psychological and relational dimension emerged as one of the most fragile areas in our sample. More than three quarters of respondents reported difficulties communicating with terminally ill patients, and more than half reported difficulties communicating with their family members. These figures are in line with international literature, which has repeatedly highlighted communication at the end of life as a high-stakes, under-trained clinical skill [20, 21]. Two related findings deserve particular attention. First, the role of spiritual and existential support, which is recognised as a relevant determinant of quality of care for patients with advanced disease [42, 43] but is generally not embedded in internal medicine practice or training. Second, spiritual and existential support remained limited in our sample, which is relevant given the growing rationale for integrating psychologists within multidisciplinary teams, an approach for which there is increasing evidence in oncology and palliative settings [44].

The educational findings are arguably the most actionable. Less than 10% of respondents reported having received adequate dedicated training in EOL care at any stage of medical education, and only 1.6% rated their overall preparation as adequate — a remarkably homogeneous signal across a heterogeneous national sample. This is in line with previous reports from medical school curricula and from residency programmes in various specialties [23–28, 45–47] and supports the call to embed palliative and EOL competencies systematically throughout undergraduate, residency and continuing medical education [48–51]. The comparison between residents and specialists adds a tentative temporal dimension to this picture. Residents reported significantly more EOL training during medical school and residency than specialists did when reflecting on their own training; specialists, conversely, more frequently reported having attended dedicated Master/Postgraduate courses. Taken together, these findings are compatible with two non-exclusive interpretations: a slow but real penetration of EOL content into Italian medical curricula over the past two decades, and a reliance among more experienced clinicians on experiential learning and post-hoc structured training.

The correlation analyses reported in Table 3 should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, given the cross-sectional, post-hoc nature of the analysis and the absence of confirmatory testing. Within these limits, three patterns appear coherent with the rest of the survey and worth mentioning. Difficulties in communicating with patients and with family members co-occurred, suggesting that EOL communication may be best conceptualised as a single underlying skill rather than two separate ones, with implications for the design of communication-training programmes. Reported EOL training experiences co-occurred across educational stages, in line with the idea that earlier exposure shapes attitudes towards later opportunities.

Recognising and addressing the spiritual and existential dimensions of EOL care remains important, yet these aspects are often insufficiently embedded in internal medicine practice and training.

The patterns reported here are consistent with findings from other healthcare systems. Surveys conducted in Spain, Argentina, Mexico, Brazil and France have similarly identified EOL care as a major source of ethical complexity and professional uncertainty for hospital-based physicians, with substantial inter-institutional variability in practice and persistent gaps in training [34–41]. Differences in advance care planning across specialties and conditions further underscore the need for context-specific yet standardised strategies to support clinicians in EOL decision-making [52, 53]. A specific reflection is, however, warranted on the transferability of our findings outside internal medicine. Some unmet needs identified here — particularly in communication, in the integration of psychological and spiritual support, and in the embedding of EOL competencies in formal curricula — are likely to be even more relevant in disciplines that are increasingly confronted with palliative-care issues but whose training framework has not historically focused on them, such as several surgical and procedural specialties. Conversely, oncology has developed dedicated palliative-care pathways for longer, and may face different rather than absent needs. The EOLO questionnaire, with appropriate adaptation, may therefore be of interest as a model for cross-specialty assessment of EOL practice and educational gaps.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the survey was limited to internal medicine physicians and residents, representing a single-specialty perspective. Second, the relatively small number of questions may have constrained the depth of exploration of complex issues related to end-of-life (EOL) care. Third, cultural and legislative references are specific to Italy—for example, questions regarding Advance Directives under Law 219/2017—potentially limiting generalizability to other countries.

Participation bias is also possible. Although the survey was disseminated to approximately 7400 internists, the exact number of individual recipients is unknown. A total of 619 physicians responded, and those with a particular interest in EOL care may have been more likely to participate. Additionally, a proportion of respondents completed the survey on paper while the remainder completed it online, which led to incomplete responses for some questions (e.g., only 616 respondents for questions 24b and 24c, and 617 for question 24a).

Statistically significant correlations between survey items were identified using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. However, as this was an exploratory, post hoc analysis that was not originally planned, the results may be subject to bias and should therefore be interpreted with caution. These findings should be considered hypothesis-generating only and warrant confirmation in future, prospectively designed studies.

Finally, the survey was conducted in Italian, which may complicate direct comparison with studies in other languages. The study did not include objective measures or external evaluation of actual clinical practice; it captures perceived gaps rather than verified performance. While this is a limitation, it also highlights areas for targeted intervention to improve education, organizational support, and psychological preparedness among healthcare providers.

However, our study has several notable strengths. It represents a large collaborative effort endorsed by professionals from multiple centres, providing a broad perspective on real-world clinical practice. The survey captured responses from a diverse range of departments and clinical intensity levels, offering insights into patterns of practice and perceived needs across different hospital settings. The comprehensive evaluation across five domains—epidemiological, pharmacological, practical, psychological, and educational—provides a holistic understanding of EOL care from the perspective of front-line physicians.

Furthermore, the results confirm previously reported gaps in training, communication and standardisation of EOL care, while suggesting exploratory associations between pharmacological practices, communication difficulties and educational experiences that may guide further research. Within these limits, the findings provide actionable information for institutions seeking to improve training, develop protocols and foster a more humanistic approach to EOL care.

Conclusions

In response to the specific aims set out for this study, the EOLO survey shows that (i) Italian internal medicine physicians and residents encounter EOL situations as part of routine clinical practice, but manage them within a largely unstructured organisational framework, with limited use of internal protocols, infrequent multidisciplinary discussions and rare awareness of formalised Advance Directives; (ii) perceived gaps in formal EOL training are pervasive at all levels of medical education, with fewer than 10% of respondents reporting adequate training at any single stage and only 1.6% reporting overall adequate preparation; (iii) perceptions of EOL care did not differ significantly according to clinical intensity of the working unit or oncology/hematology orientation of the department, whereas systematic differences emerged between residents and specialists, with residents reporting more undergraduate and residency training and specialists reporting more postgraduate training; (iv) exploratory analyses generated hypotheses linking EOL communication difficulties with patients to those with family members, training experiences across educational stages.

Taken together, these findings provide a description of perceived practice and unmet needs that can inform curricular planning, hospital policies on EOL care and the design of future, prospectively planned studies.

Extension of the EOLO instrument to nursing staff, allied health professionals and other medical specialties confronted with EOL care would allow these gaps to be characterised across the whole multidisciplinary team and across hospital settings beyond internal medicine.
