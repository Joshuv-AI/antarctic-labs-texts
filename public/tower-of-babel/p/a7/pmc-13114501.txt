Intrinsic and Water‐Triggered Hydrophilicity in Tween 20‐PDMS Composites for Dynamic and Long‐Term Wettability Control
Authors: Haotian Cha, Quang Thang Trinh, Fariba Malekpour Galogahi, Sharda Yadav, Hoang Huy Vu, Du Tuan Tran, Jun Zhang, Hongjie An, Nam‐Trung Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAchieving stable and tunable hydrophilicity in soft elastomers such as polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) remains a long‐standing challenge due to hydrophobic recovery and surface reorientation. Here, we report a one‐step bulk integration of the non‐ionic surfactant Tween 20 into the PDMS matrix to create intrinsically and water‐triggered hydrophilic composites with long‐term stability. First, density functional theory (DFT) analysis demonstrates that the oligo(ethylene glycol) (OEG) chains of Tween 20 could physically coordinate with the siloxane backbone, establishing intrinsic surface polarity. Experiments confirm the intrinsic hydrophilicity and further found a time‐dependent wetting transition, which may arise from unbound or semi‐bound Tween 20 molecules at the interface that reorient upon water contact, exposing additional hydrophilic groups and forming hydrogen‐bond networks. Meantime, the Tween 20‐PDMS composites exhibit concentration‐dependent wetting dynamics, maintaining hydrophilicity for over 100 days, preserving surface functionality under mechanical stretching. Finally, versatile applications were developed based on Tween 20‐PDMS, including (i) self‐driven unidirectional liquid transport; (ii) oil‐in‐water (O/W) droplet generation; (iii) and spatially selective cell culture. The present work demonstrates a simple, quick and stable way to make hydrophilic PDMS by bulk modification with Tween 20, offering a universal strategy for adaptive, energy‐free, and biocompatible microfluidic systems.Keywords: DFT calculations, dynamic wettability, hydrophilic PDMS, liquid transport, microfluidics

Introduction

Controlling the wettability of soft materials is vital for designing adaptive and functional interfaces [1, 2, 3]. The surface–liquid interface determines not only how droplets spread, merge, or evaporate, but also how materials exchange mass, energy, and biological information across the solid–liquid boundary [4, 5]. Such interfacial control underpins technologies ranging from flexible electronics or wearable sensors [6, 7], to lab‐on‐chip, and bio‐integrated systems [8, 9]. However, achieving stable, and programmable wettability in soft polymers remains a challenge, because their molecular networks easily reconstruct under time, humidity, or mechanical strain, leading to loss of hydrophilicity, and unreliable liquid handling [10, 11, 12]. Therefore, developing elastomeric materials that can maintain or even adapt their hydrophilic surface properties over time is of both scientific and technological significance.

Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) has remained the dominant material for microfluidic devices because of its optical transparency, simple‐fabrication, chemical stability, elasticity, and biocompatibility [13, 14, 15]. However, the inherent hydrophobicity of PDMS has presented challenges and restricted the applications requiring controlled wettability [16]. To reduce this hydrophobicity, conventional strategies such as plasma oxidation, ultraviolet (UV) activation, or chemical coating can temporarily introduce silanol (Si─OH) groups or polar layers to increase the surface energy [17]. However, this hydrophilic effect rapidly decays within hours or days through surface reconstruction or hydrocarbon migration [18, 19]. On the other hand, these treatments highly rely on vacuum systems or reactive chemicals that limit scalability and compatibility with flexible architectures. Therefore, maintaining long‐term hydrophilicity under realistic environmental or mechanical conditions is a long‐standing bottleneck.

Bulk‐additive modification, mixing hydrophilic moieties within the PDMS matrix, offers an attractive alternative to make PDMS hydrophilic. This approach can provide persistent surface polarity without post‐treatment [15]. Hydrophilic additives segregate toward the surface, imparting stable surface polarity through molecular compatibility rather than external activation. Several additives, such as poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) [19, 20, 21]. and poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) [22], have been incorporated to suppress hydrophobic recovery, achieving partial success. Nevertheless, these approaches still leave several unresolved issues, including a limited understanding of their long‐term stability under mechanical strain, the fundamental mechanism underlying their hydrophilicity, and their applicability in practical microfluidic systems. In particular, the molecular mechanism governing how hydrophilic additives interact with the siloxane network and respond to liquid contact remains poorly understood. This gap highlights the need for a molecular‐level framework that connects additive structure with time‐dependent surface behavior.

To address the above gaps, we introduce Tween 20, a biocompatible non‐ionic surfactant and widely used, as a distinctive additive to provide an intrinsic and water‐triggered wetting behavior in PDMS. Tween 20 is an amphiphilic molecule containing a hydrophobic C12 alkyl chain and multiple hydrophilic oligo(ethylene glycol) (OEG) branches, holding massive potential for both compatibility with the PDMS backbone and potential affinity toward polar liquids [23]. One previous study demonstrated the phenomenon that non‐ionic surfactants, such as Tween 20, could be incorporated into PDMS to enhance its hydrophilicity [24]. While this study primarily focused on the phenomenological wetting behavior, the underlying molecular interactions, and the long‐term stability of Tween 20‐modified PDMS, particularly under different applications, remain largely unexplored. Unlike conventional surface coatings or chemical grafting, this approach of bulk‐integration with Tween 20 could enable molecular‐level blending between PDMS and amphiphilic additives, which may yield a surface with long‐lasting or even adaptive hydrophilic behavior.

We explored the one‐step fabrication of hydrophilic Tween 20‐PDMS composites that exhibit intrinsic, long‐term, and stretch‐tunable hydrophilicity. First, we applied the Density‐functional‐theory (DFT) calculation to analyze the interaction between Tween 20 molecules and PDMS surfaces, and their subsequent affinity toward water. Next, we systematically experimentally investigated the dynamic wetting behavior across various Tween 20 concentrations, analyzing surface free energy (SFE) and evaluating long‐term stability (over 100 days) and mechanical stretching response. Finally, we demonstrated three diverse applications using the hybrid material, including (i) self‐driven and unidirectional liquid transport in an open microfluidic platform; (ii) oil‐in‐water (O/W) droplet generation in a flow‐focusing channel without post‐treatment; (iii) spatially selective cell culture on patterned Tween‐20 PDMS substrates. We believe that this bulk modification strategy, coupling molecular‐level interactions with dynamic surface reorganization, bridges the long‐standing gap between static surface modification and adaptive wetting control.

Materials and Methods

Tween 20‐PDMS Mixture Preparation

We used polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Sylgard 184, Dow Corning, Australia) as the base polymer throughout this study. Bare PDMS substrates were prepared following the standard protocol by thoroughly mixing the base and curing agent at a 5:1 weight ratio, pouring the mixture into molds, and curing in a levelled oven at 80°C for 2 h [25]. To prepare hydrophilic PDMS substrates, Tween 20 (P1379, Sigma–Aldrich, Australia) was incorporated into the PDMS prepolymer at concentrations ranging from 1 to 5 wt.%. The weight percentage was calculated relative to the total mass of the PDMS base and curing agent. Owing to the viscoelastic nature of Tween 20, the mass ratio was employed and precisely measured using an analytical balance (BCE224‐1S, Entris II Essential Balance, Sartorius, Germany). When PDMS was prepared at the conventional 10:1 base‐to‐curing‐agent ratio, incorporation of Tween 20 resulted in pronounced sticky and poor mechanical integrity. Similar softening and handling difficulties have been reported for PEG‐modified PDMS systems [19]. To mitigate this problem, the curing agent content was increased to a 5:1 ratio, which enhanced the crosslinking density and produced a mechanically robust, non‐tacky substrate. After thorough mixing and degassing, the Tween 20‐PDMS mixtures were cast into molds and thermally cured at 80°C for 3 h. It should be noted that increasing Tween 20 concentration led to a noticeable reduction in optical transparency. More details are provided in Figure S1. In addition, we performed tensile tests to evaluate the effect of Tween 20 concentration on the mechanical properties of Tween 20‐PDMS. The corresponding results are provided in Figure S2. For long‐term durability testing, all PDMS and Tween 20‐PDMS samples were stored in a laboratory drawer under ambient conditions (20°C–25°C, relative humidity 30%–60%).

Contact Angle Measurement and Surface Free Energy Measurement

The wettability of each substrate was characterized by static and time‐resolved contact angle (CA) measurements with an optical tensiometer (Theta Flex, Biolin Scientific, Finland). A 5‐µL droplet of Milli‐Q water (pH 7; Milli‐Q Direct 8, Merck Millipore, France) was automatically dispensed onto the sample surface, and the apparent contact angle was recorded using the integrated camera of the instrument. Because the contact angle of hydrophilic PDMS tends to change over time due to liquid spreading and surface reconfiguration, the dynamic wetting behavior was recorded for 900 s at 1 frame per second (FPS). The ambient temperature and relative humidity were continuously recorded using a calibrated digital thermo‐hygrometer (EL‐USB‐2‐LCD+, EasyLog, UK) during all measurements. Long‐term contact angle measurements (∼3000 s) were first performed to capture the complete wetting‐evaporation process for both pure PDMS and Tween 20‐PDMS substrates, Figure 1B,C. Based on these measurements, we identified the most pronounced wetting transition within the first 900 s. Therefore, the subsequent measurements were conducted within this time window to minimize the influence of the environmental fluctuations.

To further analyze the surface property, we calculated the surface free energy (SFE) of the substrates from the CA values using the Owens–Wendt–Rabel–Kaelble (OWRK) method [26, 27]. The SFE represents an intrinsic surface property of an ideally smooth surface, unaffected by topographical roughness. According to the OWRK approach, two probe liquids with known polar and dispersive components are required.

The values of polar, dispersive, and total surface tensions of DI water are 51.0, 21.8, and 72.8 mN m−1. On the other hand, the corresponding values of glycerol (Sigma–Aldrich, Australia) are 30.0, 34.0, and 64.0 mN m−1 [27, 28, 29]. These values were used to calculate the SFE of the Tween‐PDMS substrate. The total SFE value of the PDMS and Tween 20‐PDMS substrates was determined from the measured CAs of these two liquids. At least three independent measurements were performed for each sample, resulting in the mean values with standard deviations.

DFT Calculation Procedures

All Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna ab‐initio simulation program (VASP) package, version 5.4.4 [30, 31]. All DFT calculations employed the Perdew‐Burke‐Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange‐correlation functional [32], with the cut‐off energy of 520 eV and the Gaussian smearing method with a smearing width of 0.01 eV. The dispersion‐correction was described using the Becke‐Johnson (BJ) damping variant option of the DFT‐D3 scheme [33, 34]. We adopted the bulk structure of PDMS proposed by Schilling et al. [35] and later refined by fitting the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns and wide‐angle X‐ray diffraction (WAXD) patterns in Tosaka et al. [36]. The surface structure of PDMS was modeled by the (100) facet with the three layers of the (2 × 2) unit cell and a vacuum thickness of 35Å was applied above the top PDMS surface. The simulation unit cell to calculate gas phase molecules (water, Tween‐20, oxygen) was 20Å × 20Å × 20Å. The structural optimization of all systems (PDMS and gas phase molecules) was carried out using the conjugate‐gradient method, while the 1 × 1 × 1 KPOINT k‐point grid density was used to sample the Brillouin zones in those calculations. These simulations provided the equilibrium configurations and adsorption energetics required to understand the molecular interactions between PDMS, Tween 20, and water, serving as the theoretical foundation for the wetting mechanism discussed in Section 3.2.

Device Design and Preparation

Next, we demonstrated potential applications of the Tween 20‐PDMS composite. We fabricated two types of microfluidic devices with this material: a self‐driven unidirectional flow device and an oil‐in‐water (O/W) droplet generation channel.

For the self‐driven flow device, we employed a CO2 laser cutter (Rayjet 300, Trotec) to engrave diode‐shaped microchannel molds onto polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) sheets. The channel geometry consisted of an asymmetric arrow‐like structure inspired by our previous design [37], featuring a narrow neck width of 0.7 mm, a wide outlet of 6.87 mm, and an arrow length of 10.35 mm. The channel depth was 0.9 mm, Figure 5B. Following the fabrication of the PMMA mould, the Tween 20‐PDMS mixture was poured, degassed, and cured to obtain the patterned substrate.

Construction of the Liquid‐Electric Circuit Platform

We constructed a simple fluidic‐electronic circuit to demonstrate the potential of the self‐driven liquid diode for practical applications. The system consisted of a standard breadboard, an LED light, connecting wires, a 3 V battery, and our Tween 20‐PDMS‐based platform. The two pins of the LED light were positioned to make direct contact with the inlet and outlet ends of the Tween 20‐PDMS liquid diode channel. A 30‐µL droplet of 3 wt.% NaCl aqueous solution, dyed for visualization, was dispensed onto one pin. Driven by the self and unidirectional flow behavior of the Tween 20‐PDMS substrate, the electrolyte droplet autonomously propagated along the channel and bridged the gap to the opposite pins. When the liquid flows and connects both pins, the circuit is completed, and the LED is illuminated, demonstrating the liquid‐triggered electrical switch function. All experiments were recorded using a digital camera (D7500, Nikon, Japan) operating at 60 FPS for both images and video capture.

Fabrication of Patterned PDMS Substrates for Spatially Selective Cell Culture

To explore the biological application of the Tween 20‐PDMS material, spatially patterned substrates were fabricated for selective cell adhesion. The pattern consists of defined cell‐adhesive pure PDMS regions and cell‐repellent Tween 20‐PDMS regions, produced using a simple stamping approach. Briefly, a polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) stamp containing circular features (diameter: 6 mm) was fabricated in‐house. We coated the PMMA stamp lightly with uncured pure PDMS and gently pressed it onto a thin 3 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS substrate (thickness of 500 µm). The composite assembly was cured in an oven at 80°C for 2 h, resulting in a discrete pure PDMS island embedded within a continuous Tween 20‐PDMS background. For comparison, inverse‐patterned substrates (Tween 20‐PDMS island on pure PDMS), as well as uniform pure PDMS and uniform 3 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS films with identical thickness, were also prepared as control samples.

MDA‐MB‐231 human breast cancer cells were used for this study. The cells were maintained in DMEM/F‐12 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Life Technologies, USA). Cultures were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The patterned composite PDMS samples were sterilized with 80% ethanol, washed three times with sterile 1 X Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS), and exposed to UV radiation for 30 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of culture medium was added to each sample and incubated for 1 h to enhance the biocompatibility. For seeding, cells at approximately 80% confluence were harvested and counted using a hemocytometer. A total of 50 000 cells were seeded onto each sample and incubated for 3 days to allow optimal attachment and proliferation. The cells were then washed three times with HBSS and prepared for staining.

For immunofluorescence staining, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min and washed three times with HBSS. The cells were then incubated with Actin Green (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Nuc Blue Ready Probe reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min. The stained cells were washed three times with HBSS. The fluorescent images were taken with a fluorescence microscope (BX53M, Olympus, Japan).

Experimental Setup

It should be noted that evaporation inevitably contributes to droplet evaporation at an extended timescale. Therefore, we conducted all experiments under controlled laboratory conditions (temperature 20°C–25°C; relative humidity 40%–60%), which the comparative wettability trends between different samples remain reliable. Concurrently, we used a calibrated digital thermo‐hygrometer (EL‐USB‐2‐LCD+, EasyLog, UK) to continuously record ambient temperature and humidity, particularly during contact‐angle and dynamic wetting measurements. To investigate the effect of mechanical stretch, we employed a custom stretching platform, comprising a manually controlled linear stage mounted on a PMMA stage, following our previous design [39]. The Tween 20‐PDMS substrates were stretched from 0 to 7 mm elongation while simultaneous contact‐angle measurements were performed to examine the strain‐dependent wetting behavior.

We observed the droplet‐generation process using a high‐speed camera (VEO, Photron, USA) mounted on an inverted optical microscope (IX73, Olympus, Japan). Microchannels were fabricated from pure PDMS and 1 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS for comparison. The 1 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS device was bonded to a glass slide using a plasma cleaner (PDC‐002‐CE, Harrick Plasma, USA). We should note that plasma bonding becomes increasingly difficult at higher Tween 20 contents, consistent with previous similar works [19, 20]. This may come from the PDMS configured with Tween 20, reducing the density of the PDMS part available for oxidation. The continuous phase was DI water containing 0.05 vol% Tween 20, and the dispersed phase was mineral oil. Image sequences and videos were analyzed using the open‐source software ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA). The contact line length was normalized by the minimum contact line length observed during the entire measurement period. This normalization helps to highlight the relative expansion and recession of the contact line during wetting and evaporation.

Results and Discussion

Dynamic Wettability Mechanism

Based on experimental observations, we propose a working hypothesis to rationalize the dynamic wettability evolution of Tween 20‐PDMS composites. The molecular‐level interactions underlying this behavior are further examined by DFT calculations in Section 3.2.

The Tween 20‐PDMS composites were fabricated via a simple one‐step bulk‐mixing process, Figure 1A (i). During curing, we hypothesized that a portion of the Tween 20 molecules may physically coordinate with the PDMS siloxane network through their oligo(ethylene glycol) (OEG) chains, potentially leading to a more stable configuration and contributing to surface polarity, Figure 1A (ii). Meanwhile, a fraction of unbound or semi‐bound Tween 20 molecules could remain mobile and are dispersed near the surface. Upon contact with water, these mobile molecules may gradually reorient toward the liquid interface, exposing their hydrophilic OEG groups and forming hydrogen‐bond networks with water molecules. This proposed surface reorientation is consistent with previous observations for PEG‐PDMS systems, in which polymer chains undergo interfacial rearrangement under aqueous conditions [20]. As a result, the surface transitions from an initially weakly hydrophobic to a hydrophilic state, as shown in Figure 1A (ii). The cooperative effect between the bound and free Tween 20 molecules is proposed to contribute to the experimentally observed time‐dependent wetting behavior of the Tween 20‐PDMS substrates. To demonstrate the dynamic wettability of the Tween 20‐PDMS surfaces, the time‐dependent contact angles were measured to evaluate the hydrophilic behavior of the substrates. A 3 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS substrate was selected as the representative sample and compared with pure PDMS as the control (Figure 1B–E).

For the pure PDMS substrate, which is intrinsically hydrophobic, the droplet was pinned. The contact angle change was dominated by the droplet evaporation. The contact‐angle change during droplet evaporation could be divided into three characteristic regions (Figure 1B). In Region (i) (0–1500 s), the contact radius remained pinned while both contact angle and droplet height gradually decreased with time, indicating an evaporation‐dominated process. In Region (ii), the contact angle remained nearly constant while the droplet contact radius decreased significantly, suggesting that evaporation was now governed by the receding of the contact radius. In Region (iii), both contact angle and base diameter decreased simultaneously until the droplet completely evaporated, reflecting a mixed evaporation regime. Figure 1D shows representative images of the droplet morphology at each stage.

In contrast, the Tween 20‐PDMS substrate exhibited a markedly different wetting‐evaporation profile (Figure 1C,E). At the initial wetting transition stage, in Region (I), the surface expressed a weak hydrophobicity with minimal spreading, which came from the intrinsic Tween 20 configuration. This initial state typically continued for less than a 100 s, depending on the Tween 20 concentration. Subsequently, a wetting/spreading stage occurred in Region (II). In this region, the droplet rapidly expanded, accompanied by a sharp decrease in contact angle. This dynamic transition may arise from the gradual migration and orientation of Tween 20 molecules toward the surface, exposing hydrophilic head groups and potentially enhancing interfacial energy. The spreading also increased the liquid‐air interface area, which in turn accelerated evaporation. After approximately 1100 s, the system reached Region (III), where the contact radius stabilized and the contact angle continued to decline slowly due to evaporation, similar to the first stage of pure PDMS. Notably, due to the enlarged surface area after wetting, the overall evaporation time on Tween 20‐PDMS was approximately 20% shorter than on pure PDMS.

These results demonstrate that the bulk modification of Tween 20‐PDMS can alter the balance between wetting and evaporation progress compared to unmodified PDMS, demonstrating a time‐dependent hydrophilization. To investigate the molecular origin of this behavior, especially how Tween 20 interacts with the PDMS network to generate surface hydrophilicity, we conducted DFT calculations, as described in Section 3.2.

DFT Calculation Results and Discussion

The Tween 20‐PDMS composites exhibited a weakly hydrophobic behavior in Region I, as previously discussed, suggesting the presence of partial or transient hydrophilicity at the surface. To gain a deeper insight into this phenomenon, we performed DFT calculations to investigate the molecular origin of the hydrophilicity in Tween 20‐PDMS composites, and their subsequent affinity toward water, Figure 2. Figure 2A shows the optimized molecular structure. The material consists of one long linear C12‐hydrocarbon chain and three oligo(ethylene glycol) side chains, giving the molecule an amphiphilic character: the C12‐hydrocarbon chain induces hydrophobicity, whereas the oligo(ethylene glycol) branches provide hydrophilicity. The distances between the terminal oxygen atoms of the hydrophilic chains are 6.3, 8.3, and 11.5 Å, respectively.

(C) and (D) represent the adsorption sites (highlighted by blue circles) and interaction energies of a single water molecule on (C) PDMS and (D) Tween 20‐PDMS surfaces. The adsorption energies (in kJ mol−1) are labeled at each site. (E,F) represent the adsorption configurations and energies (ΔEads) of a water bilayer on (E) PDMS and (F) Tween 20‐PDMS surfaces. The inset image in (F) shows a zoomed view of hydrogen‐bond formation between water molecules and hydrophilic groups of Tween 20, indicated by dashed black lines. Color code: yellow, gray, red, and white balls represent Silicon (Si), Carbon (C), Oxygen (O), and Hydrogen (H) atoms, respectively. Incorporation of Tween 20 introduces hydrophilic domains capable of forming multiple hydrogen bonds with water, enhancing the overall surface wettability.

The DFT optimized PDMS surface model, in a top‐view, was constructed based on the (100) facet of the crosslinked siloxane network, Figure 2B. During PDMS curing, the platinum catalyst in Sylgard 184 promotes hydrosilylation, producing Si─O─Si backbones with surface siloxane sites that can interact with the hydroxyl terminals of Tween 20. In the PDMS fabrication, a curing agent in Sylgard 184 (which contains a platinum‐based catalyst) accelerates a hydrosilylation process, creating the strong siloxane Si─O─Si backbone of the cured elastomer. Therefore, to be incorporated into the PDMS structure during the preparation of Tween 20‐PDMS, two terminal hydroxyl groups of the oligo(ethylene glycol) chains of Tween 20 have to coordinate with the Si─O─ sites. Two possible configurations were optimized for the incorporation of Tween 20 into the PDMS surface. In configuration 1, the terminal oxygens of two oligo(ethylene glycol) chains (separated by 6.3 Å) align with adjacent Si─O─ sites spaced 5.2 Å apart on the same PDMS chain. In configuration 2, the terminal oxygens (8.3 Å apart) align with Si─O─ sites on neighboring rows (9.7 Å distance).

Due to the large mismatch, the oxygen pairs of Tween 20 with a distance of 11.5 Å could not be integrated into the PDMS structure. DFT optimization and energetic analysis exhibited that configuration 1 is more stable and thermodynamically feasible under the experimental curing conditions. Figure S3 provides the detailed explanation. Furthermore, the side view of configuration 1 shows that, due to steric hindrance from the bulky C12‐hydrocarbon tail, Tween 20 molecules predominantly accumulate near the PDMS surface rather than embedding deeply within the cross‐link structure of PDMS, Figure 2D. These calculation results are consistent with the prior experimental observations for C12E4‐PDMS systems [40]. More importantly, those studies further reported that surfactant molecules remain homogeneously distributed within the PDMS bulk at the PDMS‐air interface, and that surface segregation is triggered only upon contact with water, resulting in surfactant enrichment at the PDMS‐water interface. This water‐induced interfacial reorganization is consistent with the dynamic wetting transition observed in our Tween 20‐PDMS system.

A single H2O molecule served as a probe to compute adsorption energies at different surface sites (Figure 2C,D) to investigate the surface‐water interactions. On the PDMS surface, two sites that could interact with water are the exposed CH3─ group and the O─ site in the Si─O─Si siloxane backbone (Figure 2C). The CH3‐terminated groups show a positive binding energy of +11.7 kJ mol−1 (non‐wetting), while adsorption at siloxane Si─O─Si siloxane site yields a small negative energy of −18.3 kJ mol−1. These data reflect the intrinsic hydrophobicity of PDMS, consistent with the experimentally measured contact angle of around 119°, Figure 1. In contrast, incorporation of Tween 20 introduces multiple hydrophilic moieties (─OH, ─C═O, ─C─O─C─) capable of forming strong hydrogen bonds with water, Figure 2D. The calculated adsorption energies for these sites range from −19.2 to −38.4 kJ mol−1, while the hydrocarbon tail remains hydrophobic (+16.8 kJ mol−1). This duality explains the overall amphiphilic surface character of Tween 20‐PDMS and the enhanced affinity toward water observed experimentally, Figure 1. The interaction of a single water probe molecule with different sites on PDMS and Tween 20‐PDMS, and their structures are presented in the Figure S4–1 and 4–2.

Water bilayers were placed on both PDMS and Tween 20‐PDMS surfaces (Figure 2E,F) to further evaluate the overall wetting behavior. This water bilayer model was popularly used to investigate the wetting behavior and the spread of water droplets on solid surfaces in the literature [34, 41, 42, 43]. The calculated adsorption energy of the water bilayer on pure PDMS was −18.9 kJ mol−1, confirming weak surface‐water interactions. In contrast, the adsorption on Tween 20‐PDMS was significantly stronger (−191.6 kJ mol−1), consistent with enhanced surface hydrophilicity. Detailed zooming image (Figure 2F) reveals the formation of approximately six hydrogen bonds between water molecules and the hydrophilic groups of Tween 20, each contributing roughly 30 kJ mol−1 to the total adsorption energy. These hydrogen bonds collectively create a stable hydration layer that mediates strong water adhesion. Because the Tween 20 content in the composite is relatively low (1–5 wt.%) in experiments, the surface coverage maybe discontinuous. The Tween 20 molecules could also disperse within the PDMS matrix rather than forming a continuous surface film. Consequently, when a water droplet contacts the Tween 20‐PDMS surface, a wetting transition period is required for discrete Tween 20 molecules to reorient to expose more polar groups and form hydrogen bonds. Once sufficient interfacial hydrogen bonding develops, water spreading is rapidly triggered. This behavior could explain the presence of an initial wetting transition stage (Region I) in Figure 1D for the wetting of water on Tween 20‐PDMS. The contact angle remained unchanged during this time before sharply decreased in the subsequent wetting/spreading stage (Region II).

In summary, the DFT results reveal that the incorporation of Tween 20 introduces localized hydrophilic domains capable of forming strong hydrogen‐bond networks with water. This interfacial reorganization provides a molecular‐level explanation for the experimentally observed time‐dependent hydrophilization and dynamic wetting behavior of Tween 20‐PDMS substrates. Based on this molecular insight, we hypothesize that the degree of hydrophilicity and the dynamic wetting response of the Tween 20‐PDMS substrate should depend strongly on the concentration of Tween 20 and its surface migration behavior. To experimentally validate these predictions, we systematically investigated the surface wettability and surface free energy (SFE) of Tween 20‐PDMS composites with varying Tween 20 concentrations in the next section.

Hydrophilic Properties of the Tween 20‐PDMS

The wettability and surface energetics were systematically characterized as a function of Tween 20 concentration (1‐5 wt.%) and storage time (up to 113 days), to evaluate the key factors affecting the hydrophilic behavior of Tween 20‐PDMS substrates. The surface free energy (SFE) values were derived from contact‐angle measurements using the OWRK method (Figure 3A). For pure PDMS, the total SFE remained ∼15 mN m−1, consistent with data reported in literature [39]. In contrast, Tween 20‐PDMS exhibited significantly higher SFE, and the values increased with increasing Tween 20 concentration, confirming the enhancement of surface polarity. Moreover, the SFE over time revealed a sharp increase corresponding to the onset of dynamic wetting. This SFE rise correlates with the transition region observed in the contact angle‐time and contact line curves, Figure 3B,C, where the decreasing slope of the contact angle became sharper at higher Tween 20 concentrations. The wetting transition time shortened from ∼300 s for 1 wt.% to ∼75 s for 5 wt.%, indicating that higher Tween 20 loading accelerates the exposure of hydrophilic moieties and facilitates faster wetting transitions.

Long‐term stability was evaluated by measuring the contact angles of each sample over 113 days of ambient storage (the represent data shown in Figure 3D–F was 5 wt.%). For the long‐term stability tests, contact angle measurements were performed on fresh, spatially separated locations of samples with identical dimensions, prepared from the same master mould, to avoid previous pre‐wetting or surface history effects. From the results, the Tween 20‐PDMS substrates retained their hydrophilic characteristics for nearly three months after curing. With aging, the initial contact angle gets smaller, possibly due to the increased PDMS network rigidity, the dispersed Tween 20 molecules may be squeezed to the surface. This can also be validated from the wetting duration (Region‐II), Figure 3F. Despite minor temporal changes, all Tween 20‐PDMS samples maintained contact angles below 60° after 100 days, confirming robust and durable hydrophilicity.

In summary, the above results demonstrate that the hydrophilicity of Tween 20‐PDMS is tunable by concentration and stable over extended periods, making it suitable for long‐term microfluidic or sensing applications that require persistent surface wettability. This provides an alternative to plasma treatment, although widely used, suffers from poor long‐term stability due to hydrophobic recovery.

Effect of Mechanical Stretching on the Surface Wettability of Tween 20‐PDMS

Conventional approaches for rendering PDMS hydrophilic, such as plasma activation, UV irradiation, or surface coatings, are inherently unstable under mechanical strain. For instance, plasma‐treated PDMS surfaces rapidly lose hydrophilicity upon stretching or bending due to cracking and exposure of the underlying hydrophobic matrix [44]. In contrast, bulk modification using Tween 20 could provide an intrinsically hydrophilic material, maintaining surface functionality even under strain.

We used a custom‐built stretching platform with a tensile stage (Figure 4A) to evaluate the influence of mechanical stretching on the wetting behavior of Tween 20‐PDMS. The representative 5 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS substrate was stretched incrementally from 0 to 6 mm while recording time‐resolved contact angles and normalized contact‐line positions, Figure 4B,C. Across all elongation distances, the substrate remained hydrophilic, as evidenced by a consistently low steady‐state contact angle below 60° (Figure 4B). A slight increase in the initial contact angle was observed with increasing elongation, indicating a modest modification of the interfacial state induced by mechanical stretching. More importantly, this change did not compromise the overall wetting capability of the surface, suggesting that mechanical deformation primarily perturbs the early‐stage interfacial condition rather than fundamentally altering surface wettability.

(A) Experimental setup of the custom stretching platform integrated with an optical tensiometer, consisting of a PMMA stretching stage, a manually controlled linear stage, and a clamped Tween 20‐PDMS sample. (B) Time‐resolved contact angle curves at different elongation distances (0–6 mm) on 5 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS substrates. (C) Normalized contact line displacement vs. time under various elongations. (D) The duration time for 5% Tween 20‐PDMS at different wetting regions under stretching including (I) Wetting transition stage, (II) wetting spreading, and (III) steady evaporation, respectively. Overall, Tween 20‐PDMS retains its hydrophilicity under strain while allowing tunable modulation of wetting kinetics through mechanical stretching.

On the other hand, the normalized contact‐line dynamics exhibited pronounced elongation‐dependent differences, Figure 4C. Under all stretching conditions, the contact line advanced monotonically. However, the evolution of this advance differed substantially with applied elongation. We also found that mechanical stretching primarily redistributed the duration among different wetting and transport regimes, Figure 4D. In particular, the duration of Region II increased systematically with elongation, indicating that stretching modifies the balance between driving and dissipative processes during the spreading stage, thereby delaying the transition to the late‐stage regime. Additional contact angle and contact‐line data for Tween 20‐PDMS substrates with other concentrations are provided in Figure S5. We should note that dynamic wetting behavior in surfactant‐modified elastomers is governed by multiple coupled factors. Consequently, transport dynamics exhibited different behaviors, cannot be fully characterized by static or dynamic contact angle measurements alone.

In summary, our findings confirm that Tween 20‐PDMS preserves its hydrophilic functionality under mechanical stretching while allowing strain‐tunable modulation of wetting dynamics. This capability provides a practical route for designing flexible microfluidic or sensing systems, where liquid motion can be mechanically controlled without additional surface treatment.

Applications 1‐Self‐Driven Flow in Liquid Diode Channel

We explored the Tween 20‐PDMS substrate in open‐channel microfluidics to evaluate whether it exhibits sufficient and stable wettability for practical applications. Surface‐driven liquid transport without external pumping is a key strategy for low‐power and wearable microfluidic systems. PDMS has been widely used for such devices, but its intrinsic hydrophobicity severely restricts spontaneous flow in open channels. Alternative porous or paper‐based materials enable capillary transport, but lack geometric precision, mechanical flexibility, and the ability to support complex flow architectures [45, 46]. Therefore, the Tween‐20 PDMS substrate could serve as an alternative for making the open channel more hydrophilic, controlling liquid delivery.

We fabricated an open channel with liquid diode structures using 5 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS material, which could provide long‐term surface hydrophilicity without plasma treatment, Figure 5A,B. When a droplet was placed at the inlet, no immediate motion was observed, which was consistent with the wetting transition stage (Region I) identified earlier. After this transition period, the droplet spontaneously advanced and filled the channel, driven by the intrinsic hydrophilicity of Tween 20‐PDMS. Owing to the asymmetric geometry of the liquid diode structures, the liquid propagated unidirectionally and arrested at the designed end region, demonstrating self‐driven, directional liquid transport. Under identical conditions, the total travel distance gradually decreased with time due to evaporation, but the unidirectional flow remained consistent. These results confirm that Tween 20‐PDMS holds a massive potential to enable robust, self‐propelled flow in open channels. This could offer an effective materials‐based route toward energy‐free fluid transport for wearable sensors, lab‐on‐chip devices, water harvesting, and passive cooling systems.

Beyond fluid transport, the self‐driven flow can also be coupled with electrical functionality to create fluidic‐electronic circuits, Figure 5C. The unidirectional propagation of conductive liquid effectively serves as a logical “on–off” switch. To demonstrate this concept, we integrated the Tween 20‐PDMS liquid‐diode channel with a simple circuit consisting of a 3 V power supply, connecting wires, and an LED, Figure 5C. A 30 µL droplet of 3 wt.% NaCl solution was dispensed on the one‐sided channel. As the droplet self‐propelled through the diode channel and bridged the opposite pins, the circuit closed and the LED illuminated, Figure 5D. This proof‐of‐concept device demonstrates that Tween 20‐PDMS material can enable reconfigurable, self‐driven liquid circuits without solid conductors. Such liquid‐electronic integration holds great promise for adaptive bio‐microfluidic systems, logic gates, and wearable soft electronics.

Applications 2–Droplet Generation in Tween 20‐PDMS Channel

PDMS is one of the most widely used materials for droplet‐based microfluidics due to its elasticity, optical transparency, and ease of fabrication. However, its intrinsic hydrophobicity restricts droplet generation to water‐in‐oil (W/O) systems, making the production of oil‐in‐water (O/W) or core–shell droplets difficult [47, 48, 49]. To overcome this limitation, numerous surface modification methods, such as plasma treatment, UV irradiation, or coatings using poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) have been intensively investigated [22, 50, 51]. However, these approaches are time‐consuming, require additional equipment, and often suffer from poor long‐term stability as PDMS rapidly recovers its hydrophobicity. In contrast, the Tween 20‐PDMS composite could provide a simple, one‐step bulk modification and long‐term approach for a hydrophilic surface, enabling O/W droplet generation without post‐treatment.

To test the above hypothesis, we used a flow‐focusing microchannel for O/W droplet generation, where deionized (DI) water served as the continuous phase and mineral oil as the dispersed phase, Figure 6A. As discussed previously in Section 3.3, a higher Tween 20 concentration increases opacity. We also found that Tween 20‐PDMS substrates containing more than 1 wt.% Tween 20 exhibited insufficient bonding strength after plasma treatment. Therefore, considering both bonding strength and visible requirement, we used 1 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS channel to demonstrate the function for generating O/W droplet. For higher Tween 20 concentration, a reversible clamping method provided an alternative bonding approach, enabling device reuse and easy cleaning.

In pure PDMS channels, O/W droplet formation was not possible because the hydrophobic walls prevented water from wetting the surface, Figure 6B. Instead, the oil phase preferentially wetted the channel, resulting in the phenomenon that both fluids flow parallelly within the channel. In contrast, in the hydrophilic Tween 20‐PDMS channels, oil tends to form discrete droplets rather than continuous streams. This behavior arises because the aqueous continuous phase preferentially wets the hydrophilic channel walls, minimizing oil‐wall contact and generating interfacial tension that promotes the breakup of the oil phase into droplets. When water flowed through the side inlets, it imposed strong shear on the oil stream at the flow‐focusing junction. Combined with the interfacial tension between the two immiscible phases, this led to periodic necking and the formation of monodisperse O/W droplets, Figure 6C.

These results demonstrate that Tween 20‐PDMS provides a simple way to achieve O/W droplet generation in the flow focusing microfluidic channel without complex surface modification. Moreover, by spatially patterning the PDMS and Tween 20‐PDMS regions within a single chip, it would be possible to design hybrid microchannels capable of generating double emulsions or core–shell droplets, integrating hydrophobic and hydrophilic domains on the same substrate.

Applications 3–Spatially Selective Cell Culture on Patterned Tween 20‐PDMS Substrates

The intrinsic biocompatibility and elasticity of PDMS have made it a popular material for cell‐culture and organ‐on‐chip systems [52, 53]. However, controlling where cells adhere and grow on such soft materials remains a long‐standing challenge. Spatial confinement of cell growth not only allows fundamental studies of cell‐material interactions but also provides a route to high‐throughput, localized drug testing [54, 55]. To achieve this without complex surface treatments or lithography, we exploited the natural contrast between the cell‐adhesive pure PDMS and the cell‐repellent Tween 20‐PDMS to construct patterned substrates with well‐defined adhesion regions, Figure 7A.

Application of spatially selective cell culture on patterned Tween 20‐PDMS substrates. (A) Schematic illustration of the stamping process and the expected spatial confinement of cell growth on PDMS (cell‐adhesive) and Tween 20‐PDMS (cell‐repellent) regions. (B) Fluorescence‐field images of MDA‐MB‐231 cell culture after three days for four different substrate configurations: (i) pure PDMS, (ii) pure PDMS island on a 3 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS substrate, (iii) 3 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS island on a pure PDMS substrate and (iv) 3 wt.% Tween 20‐PDMS. Overall, cells adhered and proliferated only on the pure PDMS regions, whereas the Tween 20‐PDMS areas remained cell‐free, demonstrating a simple, material‐based strategy for high‐contrast, and spatially defined cell patterning.

Four types of substrates were prepared including (i) pure PDMS substrate; (ii) 3% Tween 20‐PDMS group; (iii) pure PDMS islands stamped on a Tween 20‐PDMS background; (iv) Tween 20‐PDMS islands stamped on a pure PDMS background. MDA‐MB‐231 breast cancer cells were cultured for three days on these substrates. The results revealed clear selectivity in cell attachment, Figure 7B (i,iv). On pure PDMS, cells attached and spread uniformly, exhibiting the characteristic elongated morphology typical of adherent epithelial‐like cells. In contrast, almost no cell adhesion was observed on Tween 20‐PDMS surfaces, indicating that the presence of Tween 20 strongly suppresses nonspecific cell attachment.

The patterned substrates demonstrated striking spatial confinement of cell growth, Figure 7B (ii,iii). For both PDMS‐island on Tween‐20‐PDMS and Tween‐20‐PDMS‐island on PDMS, cells proliferated exclusively within the PDMS regions, with well‐defined sharp boundaries separating the cell‐adhesive and cell‐repellent areas. This clear adhesion contrast between the two materials highlights the potential of this simple material‐based patterning approach. The underlying mechanism of this selective adhesion is consistent with the antifouling nature of Tween 20. Its hydrophilic head groups form a hydrated surface layer that suppresses protein adsorption and prevents integrin‐mediated attachment [23]. Because cell adhesion on polymer surfaces generally requires initial serum‐protein adsorption, this reduction in nonspecific adsorption inhibits stable focal‐adhesion formation and subsequent spreading. As a result, we can choose a spatially selective cell growing on the patterned Tween 20‐PDMS substrates without surface modification.

This simple patterning strategy provides a direct and reproducible route to create cell arrays or localized drug‐testing platforms. Each PDMS region could be exposed to different drug concentrations or stimuli, enabling parallel testing on a single chip while minimizing cross‐contamination. Moreover, since the patterning is achieved through intrinsic material composition, it is inherently compatible with soft, reconfigurable microfluidic systems based on the same Tween 20‐PDMS platform.

Conclusion

In this work, we developed a simple one‐step strategy to fabricate bulk hydrophilic PDMS substrates by incorporating Tween 20 into the polymer matrix. The Tween 20‐PDMS composites exhibit a long‐term, self‐activating, and stretching‐tunable hydrophilicity without requiring plasma treatment or surface coating. The resulting Tween 20‐PDMS composites exhibit both intrinsic and water‐triggered wetting characteristics. Density functional theory calculations revealed that the oligo(ethylene glycol) (OEG) segments of Tween 20 can interact with the siloxane network through physical coordination, providing an intrinsically polar surface. Meanwhile, unbound or semi‐bound Tween 20 molecules near the interface may retain molecular mobility, which enables water‐induced reorientation of OEG chains toward the liquid interface. This dual mechanism, combining intrinsic polarity, and dynamic interfacial activation, accounts for the time‐dependent wetting behavior and long‐term stability observed experimentally. Finally, the Tween 20‐PDMS material further enabled diverse applications, including self‐driven unidirectional liquid transport, oil‐in‐water (O/W) droplet generation without surface modification, and spatially selective cell culture. In conclusion, our bulk modification method can provide a simple, fast, and stable strategy to make the PDMS substrate hydrophilic. This concept may offer a general framework for designing adaptive and energy‐free interfacial materials, with potential applications in flexible microfluidics, wearable sensors, and bio‐integrated devices.
