Torture and healthcare service utilization in Syrian refugees resettled in Norway – a longitudinal, registry-based study
Authors: Alexander Nissen, Katharina Haag
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Torture, banned under international treaties such as the UN Convention Against Torture, remains a widespread violation with profound health consequences. The Istanbul Protocol (IP) sets global standards for the medical documentation of torture highlighting the important role of healthcare providers. A limitation of existing research on torture's health impacts is that studies are largely cross-sectional and reliant on self-reported clinical data. Norway's detailed healthcare registry data offers a robust opportunity to conduct longitudinal, population-based studies, advancing our understanding of torture's long-term effects on refugees and its public health implications.Objectives: This study has two main aims: (1) to examine the frequency of torture-related diagnostic codes and the factors associated with their use in primary and specialized care among adult Syrian refugees resettled in Norway, and (2) to link self-report data on torture exposure in adult refugees from Syria with data on HCSU over a 6-year follow-up to explore group differences in utilization patterns.Methods: Study participants include the RBMI cohort (N = 14,350), comprised of all adult refugees from Syria resettled in Norway in 2015–2017; and the REFUGE cohort – a subsample of the RBMI cohort – comprised of those in the RBMI cohort who participated in a nationwide survey study in 2018. Aim 1 will be addressed using data (2015–2024) from the Norwegian Registry for Primary Health Care (KPR) and the Norwegian Patient Registry (NPR), which contain information on all contacts with primary- and specialized healthcare services throughout Norway (e.g. date of contact, diagnostic code given). To address aim 2, we will link 2018 survey data on torture exposure to the abovementioned registry data on HCSU. In addition to descriptive statistics, multivariable, two-part hurdle regression models will be used to analyse data since we expect zero inflation and overdispersion of the outcomes (HCSU).Stage of study: This manuscript reports Stage 1 of a Registered Report; analyses will be conducted after in-principle acceptance.

Introduction

Torture is internationally recognized as one of the gravest human rights violations and prohibited without exception under the UN Convention Against Torture (UNCAT) and the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR, Article 3) (Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment; European Convention on Human Rights - Article 3, 2018). Despite these international human rights treaties, torture remains widespread (Milewski et al., 2023). The estimated prevalence of torture across conflicts and groups of refugees and asylum seekers (RAS) varies notably (Sigvardsdotter et al., 2016b), and scholars point to substantial heterogeneity with respect to how torture is defined and operationalized across prevalence studies (Kalt et al., 2013).

The Manual on Effective Investigation and Documentation of Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment, commonly referred to as the Istanbul protocol (IP), establishes global standards for the medical documentation of torture. The IP was created through a collaborative effort involving experts from multiple disciplines and organizations over several years. It was developed as a ground-breaking resource before being adopted as a United Nations standard in 1999. The protocol underwent a major revision in 2022, which added two new sections: one focusing specifically on the role of healthcare professionals in documenting torture and ill-treatment (Chapter VII); the other containing recommendations for States and civil society on how to effectively implement the protocol (Chapter VIII) (Istanbul Protocol: Manual on the Effective Investigation and Documentation of Torture and Other Cruel, 2022). Although the Istanbul Protocol has existed for nearly three decades, the degree to which its recommendations are adhered to in practice differs between countries (Lønning et al., 2021; Ostergaard et al., 2020; Rossolatou, 2019).

Two recent reports in Norway – a country committed to implementing the IP – have cast a critical light on its adherence to the protocol (Torturert og glemt: Identifisering og rehabilitering av torturutsatte i Norge, 2020; Weiss, 2020). The reports identify several shortcomings and recommend sector-specific mitigation measures, including better communication between immigration and health services and the creation of reimbursement codes for medical examinations of potential torture survivors. One of the reports was commissioned and funded by the Norwegian Directorate of Immigration (UDI) and formed part of the directorate’s ongoing efforts to establish more rigorous strategies for addressing torture in the asylum process (Weiss, 2020). In November 2024, this culminated in the launch of new guidelines and procedures for use by UDI staff to facilitate the identification and documentation of newly arrived torture survivors (UDI, 2024). From a health perspective, one important feature of the new guidelines was the inclusion of concrete advice for how UDI staff should connect suspected torture survivors with healthcare providers and the broader healthcare system to help ensure rehabilitation in line with international obligations. The present study is part of a wider research project that will also examine the implementation and potential effects of the new UDI guidelines.

As mentioned, the role of medical professionals in documenting torture is an important element of the IP, especially following the major revision in 2022. The IP outlines the clinicians’ duty to carefully document torture-related injuries, not only as part of treatment but also as a form of rehabilitation and legal redress. In Norway, there are two diagnostic coding schemes employed by healthcare providers: the International Classification of Primary Care, Second edition (ICPC-2) in primary care; and the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) in specialized care. The ICPC-2, which was first introduced in 1987, has been translated into close to 20 languages and is accepted by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a member of the Family of International Classifications (International Classification of Primary Care, 2nd edition (ICPC-2), 2026). Both coding schemes provide diagnostic codes intended to document torture exposure: Z25.0012 (Victim of torture) in ICPC-2; and Z65.4 (Victim of crime or terrorism, including victims of torture) in ICD-10. There are, however, no clear guidelines or official recommendations on how these specific diagnoses should be used, for example to supplement or contextualize a PTSD diagnosis in clinical settings. As with most ICD-10 Z-codes related to social determinants of health, they tend to be underutilized in clinical practice (Jacobs, 2021; Luke & Scribano, 2023). We are unaware of any study that has investigated how frequent these torture-specific diagnoses are used in Norway or elsewhere.

The health consequences of torture are complex, long-lasting and multi-dimensional (de C Williams & van der Merwe, 2013). Numerous studies have documented strong links between torture exposure and mental health problems (de Jong et al., 2001; Steel et al., 2009) as well as physical health problems, particularly pain (Kaur, 2017; Olsen et al., 2007; Williams et al., 2010). Co-occurring psychiatric and somatic conditions tend to exacerbate disability and complicate recovery and addressing both the psychological and somatic effects of torture may be necessary for effective treatment of either (Crosby, 2013). A limitation of the available evidence on the health consequences of torture is that many studies are based on small, clinical samples of refugees and/or rely on cross-sectional designs and self-report questionnaire data (Steel et al., 2009). Given the high prevalence of torture exposure among RAS documented in the literature (Sigvardsdotter et al., 2016b), and the well-established role of torture as a driver of health problems, adding longitudinal evidence from population-based samples is important. This would contribute to documenting the overall public health burden related to torture exposure in RAS populations.

The extensive registry data on healthcare service utilization (HCSU) that exists in the Nordic countries offers a unique opportunity to explore health in a longitudinal perspective, and several registry-based studies on RAS have been published from this region in the last decade. The aggregate evidence from these studies points towards increased HCSU among refugee populations compared to the general populations (Abebe et al., 2017; Manhica et al., 2017; Rahman et al., 2022). Importantly, though, none of the studies have incorporated data on torture, and we are not aware of other studies that have explored the health consequences of torture using longitudinal data from population-based, non-clinical samples.

The present study will utilize and expand on data from the REFUGE project (Nissen et al., 2020), which was initiated in 2016 in the wake of the high numbers of Syrian refugees arriving in Norway following the outbreak of the Syrian civil war. The study has two main aims: the first is to explore how frequent torture-related diagnostic codes – i.e. Z25 (ICPC-2) and Z65.4 (ICD-10) – are used by Norwegian healthcare providers in primary- and specialized care during consultations with adult refugees from Syria and what important predictors of use are. The second aim is to compare HCSU in the years after arrival in Norway among refugees who self-reported torture exposure in a survey questionnaire with those who did not report torture exposure. Comparisons will be made with respect to: (i) overall HCSU both at primary and specialized care level; (ii) HCSU related to mental health problems at primary and specialized care level (including sub analysis focusing specifically on PTSD); (iii) HCSU related to musculoskeletal problems and pain, as well as deliberate self-harm/suicide behaviour; (iv) differences in HCSU across years after resettlement (i.e. exploring time-trends). In addition, we will explore the correspondence between self-reported torture exposure and the presence of torture-related diagnostic codes in registry data – specifically, we will compare the proportion of refugees with a torture-related diagnostic code among those who self-reported exposure to torture with those who did not.

Methods

Detailed plans for the first aim of the study were preregistered in the Open Science Framework (OSF) prior to submission of the Stage 1 manuscript. The OSF preregistration can be found here: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/EJ5DQ. Segments of the preregistration are reproduced verbatim below for methodological consistency.

Design and participants

The present study is an extension of the 2018 REFUGE study – a cross-sectional, nationwide, survey study focusing on migration related stress and mental health among Syrian refugees resettled in Norway. The REFUGE study’s sampling frame was defined by the following three criteria: (1) age over 18 years; (2) refugee from Syria; and (3) granted residency rights and resettled in Norway between 01/01/2015 and 31/12/2017. These criteria were provided to the Norwegian population registry which identified N = 14,350 eligible individuals as of August 2018. Of these eligible participants, 10,000 were selected to receive study invitations via simple random sampling, of which 902 ultimately participated. We refer to prior publications for extensive discussions on recruitment and data collection procedures, potential selection bias (Nissen et al., 2020) and the estimated burden of mental health problems (Nissen et al., 2021) and chronic pain (Nissen et al., 2022) in this sample.

The present study consists of two cohorts: the RBMI cohort (Refugee Burden of Mental Illness cohort; N = 14,350), comprised of all adult refugees from Syria that were resettled in Norway between 2015 and 2017. The RBMI cohort constituted the sampling frame for the 2018/19 REFUGE survey study (Nissen et al., 2020). The second cohort, the REFUGE-cohort (N = 902), is a subsample of the RBMI cohort, comprised of those refugees in the RBMI cohort who participated in the REFUGE survey study (i.e. 10,000 of the 14,350 in RBMI cohort were randomly selected and invited to participate in the REFUGE survey, of which 902 actually participated – for details, please see (Nissen et al., 2020)). Aim 1 of the study will be explored using registry data from primary- and specialized HCSU and Statistics Norway for the RBMI cohort for the period 2015–2024 (i.e. N = 14,350). Aim 2 of the study will only involve the REFUGE cohort and will in addition to the registry data listed in Aim 1 also utilize survey data on exposure to torture from the 2018 REFUGE study. The survey data will be linked with the registry data on an individual level.

Aim 1 of the study will exclusively use the above registry data.

Aim 2 of the study will link the above registry data at an individual level with data from the 2018 REFUGE survey study. Specifically, the present study will use survey data on exposure to potentially traumatic experiences (PTEs) prior to or during the flight to Norway, which includes one item specifically asking about torture exposure. The survey questions on PTEs were adapted from the Refugee Trauma History Checklist (RTHC), developed by Sigvardsdotter et al. (Sigvardsdotter et al., 2016a, 2017). The RTHC comprises eight PTE items referring to experiences both pre-flight and during flight (e.g. ‘war at close quarters’, ‘sexual violence’). All items are answered dichotomously (Yes/No). The stem of the RTHC reads: ‘Before you left your home [/After you left your home, during your flight], have you personally faced any of the following situations … ’ Item 6 of the RTHC asks about ‘torture’ and is the main focus in this study, although other PTEs will be included to account for potential confounding.

Sample size and power estimations

There is limited prior evidence available regarding HCSU in torture-exposed refugees to guide assumptions in power estimations. The power calculations below for Aim 2 of the study rest on the landmark review study by Steel et al. from 2009 (Steel et al., 2009), where the odds for PTSD were compared across samples of refugees with different levels of torture exposure. Specifically, the odds ratio (OR) of PTSD in samples where more than 40% of refugees had been exposed to torture compared to samples where <20% had been exposed was estimated at OR = 4.03 (2.31-7.04). The estimated OR was 2.00 (1.18-3,37) when comparing samples with torture exposure in the range 20-40% with those <20%. In terms of HCSU in Norway, Abebe et al. gives valuable information on specialist mental healthcare utilization in immigrants and refugees, even if torture is not explored per se (Abebe et al., 2017). Specifically, based on table 2 in the Abebe et al. article, assuming that 10% of refugees will use specialist services over the 6-year follow-up in the present study, would be a conservative assumption (i.e. a higher percentage will likely use services). Thus, with roughly 210 out of 902 participating refugees reporting torture exposure in the sample (Nissen et al., 2020), an assumed OR of 2.5 comparing the odds of HCSU in torture exposed vs. non-exposed based on Steel et. al. and a baseline HCSU of 10% in non-exposed refugees, the study will have over 95% power to detect a group difference with significance level set at 5%. If the OR is set at 2.00, the study will have around 84% power. Given that a notably higher proportion of refugees are expected to have HCSU at primary care level (Straiton et al., 2017; 2014), the study will most likely have a statistical power >90% for all research questions when using KPR data.

Statistical analysis

All planned statistical analyses and details regarding variable handling for Aim 1 of the study were preregistered in the OSF platform (Nissen & Haag, 2025). This preregistration is included in the supplementary material (with minor language modification). We have also created a detailed statistical analysis plan for Aim 2 that is included in the supplementary material. The sections below outline plans for statistical analysis on a more conceptual level. Please see supplementary material for details on variable handling and regression model building.

Aim 1 of the study will mostly report descriptive statistics with 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) of the frequency of torture-related ICPC-2 and ICD-10 diagnoses in primary and specialized care. The frequency (95%CI) will be reported for the sample overall, and for the sample stratified by key sociodemographic and background variables (e.g. sex, age-groups, education, household income). If statistical power allows, we will build a multivariable logistic regression model of the odds of HCSU with a torture-specific diagnosis, with sociodemographic and background variables included as predictors. Different models will be run for primary and specialized care.

For aim 2 of the study. We will report descriptive statistics on the frequency of HCSU (95%CI) stratified by the variable of key interest: torture-exposed vs. non-exposed. We will also report on and compare the number and proportion with a torture-related diagnostic code among torture-exposed vs. non-exposed refugees, done separately for primary care and specialized care. We will present descriptive statistics for all outcomes – i.e. overall HCSU as well as HCSU related to mental health problems; musculoskeletal problems and pain; and deliberate self-harm/suicidal behaviour – separately for primary and specialized care. We will then move on to explore how torture-exposure is associated with HCSU across outcomes within a multivariable framework, presenting both crude, partially adjusted and fully adjusted models. Given that all outcome variables are count variables with likely zero inflation (i.e. many have no HCSU) and overdispersion, we will use two-part regression models: one logistic part modelling the odds of having at least one HCSU with the relevant outcome diagnoses, and one count part where the count/rate of HCSU is modelled in those with at least one HCSU. Because we believe one could argue that the process by which zeros and ones are generated in the first binary model is qualitatively different from the process impacting the number of healthcare contacts in the count model (i.e. whether a refugee uses healthcare services depends on a different process than how often services are used), we will use Hurdle models. The crude models will include only the main predictor of interest (i.e. torture-exposure), the partially adjusted models will add sociodemographic and background variables, and the fully adjusted models will further add the other PTEs. Exploration of time-trends will be done by fitting an interaction term between torture exposure and time (please see supplementary material for details).

We will adjust for multiple testing using Benjamini Hochberg's procedure with a threshold for false positive rate set at 0.05 (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). Please see supplementary material for details on how variables will be coded and handled and how missing will be dealt with.
