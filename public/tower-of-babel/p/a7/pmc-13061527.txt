Strategies for Malar Implant Placement: A Review of Access, Planning, and Outcomes
Authors: Martin Kauke-Navarro, Leonard Knoedler, Felix J. Klimitz, Fortunay Diatta, Victoria Kong, Simon Bigus, Michael Alperovich, Alexandre G. Lellouch, Ali-Farid Safi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The absence of standardized planning and placement strategies for malar implants contributes to variable aesthetic outcomes.Methods:A systematic review following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, and Google Scholar for clinical studies on aesthetic malar implantation. Surgical access, implant positioning, planning methods, and complications were extracted.Results:Fifteen studies including 796 patients were analyzed. Intraoral (n = 370) and preauricular (n = 350) approaches were most common, followed by subciliary (n = 54) and endonasal (n = 20). Intraoral access showed higher infection rates (2.0%), revisions (4.3%), removals (2.0%), and malposition (1.3%). Preauricular access was associated with asymmetry (20.3%) and contour irregularities. Subciliary and endonasal approaches showed few reported complications. Most implants were placed over the anterolateral zygoma to enhance malar projection. Only 3 studies used patient-specific implants, and 6 incorporated computed tomography or 3-dimensional planning.Conclusions:Intraoral access carries a higher infection risk. Targeting the central malar eminence may optimize projection while avoiding lateral overcorrection. Broader use of patient-specific implants and image-guided planning may improve precision and aesthetic outcomes.

Takeaways

Question: What surgical access routes, planning strategies, and implant types have been reported for malar augmentation, and how do these affect complications and revisions?

Findings: In this systematic review of 15 studies (796 patients), intraoral access accounted for most infections (17 cases, 2.0%), whereas rhytidectomy cases showed high rates of asymmetry (71 patients, 20.3%). Subciliary access produced only minor aesthetic complaints, and endonasal access had no reported complications. Patient-specific implants and computed tomography–based planning were rarely used.

Meaning: The choice of access route strongly influences complication patterns, and wider adoption of patient-specific planning may improve outcomes in malar augmentation.

INTRODUCTION

Age-related changes contribute to midfacial volume loss through a combination of bone resorption, soft tissue descent, and fat atrophy. These changes include significant maxillary retrusion and loss of midfacial skeletal volume, whereas the bony orbit remains relatively stable. The result is overall midface flattening, accentuated by atrophy of the facial fat pads.1–3 Malar prominences are considered attractive, and their augmentation is a cornerstone of facial rejuvenation and aesthetic surgery.4

One of the earliest reported uses of malar implants was by Hinderer5 and Spadafora6 in 1971, who emphasized the zygomatic region as a key determinant of facial shape in frontal and oblique views, advocating skeletal enhancement to restore facial contour.7 González-Ulloa8 similarly described the malar region as essential for maintaining youthful facial contours. Yaremchuk9 and Yaremchuk and Kahn10 later introduced the concept of the “negative vector face,” in which midfacial retrusion relative to the corneal plane results in loss of soft tissue support, contributing to an aged appearance.

Malar implants are the second most used facial implants, after chin implants.11 Despite their popularity, revision rates remain high, with 10%–14% of implants removed due to aesthetic dissatisfaction.10 However, factors contributing to suboptimal outcomes remain poorly defined in the literature. Thus, the objective of this systematic review was to critically evaluate the current evidence on malar implant placement, with a focus on surgical access routes, preoperative planning strategies, implant positioning techniques, and complications, with the ultimate goal of identifying strategies that optimize outcomes and reduce revision rates.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

This systematic review was conducted in July 2025 in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses PRISMA) 2020 guidelines, as previously described by our workgroup.11–13 This review should be considered a descriptive systematic review of the existing literature, as we did not perform a meta-analysis due to the observed heterogeneity in outcome parameters and study designs.

The systematic review was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO identifier: CRD420251121676). A structured search of the PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane CENTRAL, and Google Scholar databases was performed using a combination of medical subject headings terms and keywords related to the zygomatic region, facial implants, and alloplastic augmentation. The search string included terms such as “zygomatic bone,” “malar,” “cheek,” “implant,” “onlay,” “augmentation,” and specific implant materials including silicone, porous polyethylene, polyether ether ketone, titanium, and Medpor. (See Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the search strategy, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E713.)

We screened titles and abstracts to identify studies with a primary focus on the elective use of malar implants for aesthetic purposes. This included procedures performed to correct congenital hypoplasia, age-related volume loss, or posttraumatic midfacial contour irregularities, provided the indication was elective and not part of acute trauma reconstruction. Articles were excluded if they described dental implants, acute fracture repair, primary reconstruction of zygomatic defects, or if they reported on mixed facial implants without clear delineation of outcomes for malar augmentation. Review articles, editorials, and studies lacking original clinical data were also excluded.

Full-text review was performed on eligible studies to extract data on implant type; material; fixation method; surgical access; anatomical placement strategy; adjunctive procedures; and complications (including clearly defined complications: implant revision/removal, exposure, infection, malposition, nerve injury, implant removal, aesthetic complaints [asymmetries], hematoma, seroma). Furthermore, data on implant positioning, preoperative planning, including the use of aesthetic landmarks, and intraoperative assessment were recorded. Studies were synthesized qualitatively, with particular attention paid to how implant location was chosen, how surgical access was determined, and what outcomes were observed across different techniques and materials. Only studies that explicitly presented clinical results related to malar implants were included in the final analysis. Two independent reviewers (M.K.-N. and L.K.) performed literature screening and data extraction. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus with the senior author (A.-F.S).

RESULTS

This initial search returned 310 results. After screening and eligibility assessment, 15 studies were included, comprising a total of 1089 patients (Fig. 1). A detailed overview of patient demographics and study characteristics is shown in Table 1.10,14–27 Among these, 796 patients underwent malar implant augmentation. Sample sizes ranged from single-patient case reports to large retrospective series, with variable indications including aesthetic midface enhancement, reconstruction of congenital or posttraumatic deformities, and adjunctive volume restoration during rhytidectomy. Patient ages ranged from 13 to 82 years; follow-up ranged from 5 months to 15 years. The most used implant materials were silicone and porous polyethylene (Medpor). Patient-specific implants fabricated from polyether ether ketone or custom silicone were used in a small number of cases (see Table 1).

Surgical Details

All malar implants were placed subperiosteally (Table 2). An intraoral approach was used in 11 of the 15 studies. A subciliary approach was described by Pelle-Ceravolo and Angelini,25 Niechajev,23 and Yim et al.17 Transconjunctival access was used in the series by Yaremchuk4, primarily for infraorbital rim augmentation, with occasional extension into the malar region. An endonasal mucosal approach was used by de la Peña-Salcedo et al,27 involving a vertical 2-cm incision inside the nasal vestibule adjacent to the piriform aperture. The majority of implants were secured using titanium screws, with some studies reporting the use of bolsters27 or relying on precise pocket dissection for implant placement without any additional method of implant stabilization.

Complications

Complications were reported in 10 of the 15 included studies (Table 3, Fig. 2). Briefly, infections occurred exclusively in studies that used intraoral access, either alone or in combination with other approaches. A total of 17 infections were reported across 5 studies.9,19,20,26 Implant removal or revision due to infection or dissatisfaction was performed in 31 patients. Malposition was reported in 10 patients, most commonly in the series by Metzinger et al22 and Yaremchuk,4 both of which used intraoral access. Transient nerve injury was documented in 3 patients,22 with no long-term deficits reported. Subciliary (transcutaneous) access was used in 3 studies and was not associated with infection or implant removal. However, aesthetic dissatisfaction, including implant visibility or asymmetry, was reported in 2 patients. The preauricular approach at the time of rhytidectomy was used in 1 study.25 Asymmetry (71 patients, 20.3%), unnatural appearance (5.8%), and over- or undercorrection (4.3%), with 5 patients undergoing revision for aesthetic reasons, were reported in this series. One patient requested implant removal due to dissatisfaction. One study described an endonasal mucosal approach, which was not associated with infection or malposition.

Implant Planning and Target Zone for Malar Augmentation

Computed tomography (CT)–based imaging was used in 6 of the 15 studies. Ramieri et al,14 Narciso et al,15 and Eski et al16 used preoperative CT or cone-beam CT with 3-dimensional (3D) planning to preview implant positioning, volume, and screw trajectory. Yim et al17 used cone-beam computed tomography with rapid prototyping for hands-on clay modeling of implant contours. Metzinger et al22 and Niechajev23 used photograph-based simulation and direct visualization methods, whereas Hopping et al19 incorporated preoperative computer imaging combined with patient feedback to guide malar implant placement. In contrast, several studies, including those by Atherton and Haers,18 French et al,24 and Pelle-Ceravolo and Angelini,25 relied on visual inspection, intraoperative judgment, or surface markings for implant selection and placement.24,25

Three studies report the use of anatomical classification systems or structured methodologies to guide implant positioning. The Hinderer method was used in 2 studies19,22 to define the malar apex (most prominent point), often in combination with patient-specific modifications such as mirror-assisted visualization. The zonal division of the midface (malar, paranasal, submalar) was described in the study conducted by de la Peña-Salcedo et al,27 who divided the midface into malar (zone 1), paranasal (zone 2), and submalar (zone 3) regions. The authors intended to augment zone 1 and avoided the zygomatic arch to prevent excessive transverse widening of the midface.27 Five studies did not clearly specify a target zone for implant placement.14–16,18,24 Yaremchuk4 emphasized placement below and lateral to the infraorbital rim, explicitly targeting the most prominent malar point and avoiding both the zygomatic arch and submalar hollow. Pelle-Ceravolo and Angelini25 uniquely differentiated between malar and submalar hypoplasia in 350 patients, customizing implant location based on surface contour analysis and patient-specific asymmetry.

DISCUSSION

This review summarizes the current clinical literature on malar implant augmentation, focusing on implant type, surgical access, complications, planning strategies, and implant positioning. Malar midface complex augmentation using implants is a challenging procedure, which is associated with a high percentage of patient dissatisfaction and revisional surgery.9,29 Such asymmetries often become apparent over time after the initial surgery, when swelling subsides, potentially requiring revisional surgery.30 Due to these inherent challenges with implant placement, nonpermanent hyaluronic acid fillers have become increasingly popular alternatives, offering predictable, reversible, and well-tolerated midface augmentation with high short-term stability—maintaining approximately 80% of injected volume in the malar region and consistently improving patient-reported satisfaction.31

Four main surgical access routes were identified in this systematic review: intraoral, subciliary (transcutaneous), endonasal, and preauricular (combined with rhytidectomy). Intraoral access was the most frequently reported approach. Notably, all infections reported in the reviewed studies occurred in cases where an intraoral approach was used, either alone or in combination with other techniques. No infections were reported with subciliary, endonasal, or preauricular access. This finding is expected and emphasizes that intraoral placement carries a higher infection risk due to exposure to the oral bacterial flora.4 When combined with rhytidectomy, surgeons should anticipate a higher asymmetry risk. Persistent asymmetry affected more than 20% of patients in the rhytidectomy cohort, with some requiring revision. This may reflect the complexity of combining implant placement with soft tissue redraping or heightened patient expectations in such complex aesthetic procedures. Subciliary, endonasal, and preauricular approaches could be considered for patients at high infection risk or undergoing combined facial procedures. Across the included studies, fixation of malar implants was achieved with titanium screws in the vast majority of cases, whereas a minority relied on nylon sutures, bolster fixation, or precise subperiosteal pocket dissection without hardware, particularly when silicone implants were used (Table 2). The findings of this review indicate that rigid screw fixation represents the standard of care for malar implant stabilization, likely offering superior reliability in maintaining implant position and minimizing the risk of malposition or migration, which can be a common cause for revision surgery.11

Given the limited number of studies and patients specifically addressing malar implants, it is difficult to determine the impact of implant material on complication rates. However, a recent systematic review pooling data from a larger cohort of midface implant procedures found that complication rates varied notably by material.11 Polyethylene implants demonstrated a lower overall complication rate (~4.2%) compared with silicone implants (~8.3%), whereas glass-ceramic implants exhibited the highest rates (~23%). The most frequently reported complications included infection and implant exposure, with polyethylene showing a lower incidence of exposure relative to silicone. Notably, the majority of polyethylene midface implant series were complication-free, contributing to its more favorable overall profile.

Augmentation of the malar region can be challenging given the absence of a standard point of reference on the face that precisely defines the point of maximum malar projection.4,9 According to Yaremchuk,4 it is exactly the absence of defining anthropometric or cephalometric landmarks that makes malar augmentation challenging and introduces variability in outcomes. The malar–midface complex can generally be divided into 3 regions: medial, middle, and lateral thirds.30 The medial third lies just lateral to the nasolabial fold and borders the malar eminence. The lateral third corresponds to the zygomatic arch. Augmentation should be focused on the middle third to enhance malar projection effectively.4,29 Other zonal divisions have been proposed by Terino32,33 who divided the midface into 5 distinct zones (1–5) for the purpose of conducting facial analysis and implant placement planning. Terino32 outlines the importance of augmenting zones 1 (the major part of the malar bone and the first third of the arch) and 5 (the submalar zone). Zone 1 should be targeted to produce a strong anteroposterior projection of the malar prominence, which leads to volumization of the upper cheek and thus produces a high, prominent contour of the cheekbones.32 Sometimes, zone 2 (the middle third of the zygomatic arch) can also be augmented in addition to zone 1; however, lateral overcorrection can be a complication.33 When targeting zone 5 (the submalar triangle), this can restore a rounder and more youthful contour of the midface by restoring midfacial volume.33 Terino33 also emphasizes that zone 3 (paranasal) and 4 (posterior third of the zygomatic arch) should generally be avoided for augmentation.

Several strategies to define the point of projection and best placement have been described in the studies presented herein. Across these studies, the central malar eminence (the most anterior and convex point of the zygomatic body) has been highlighted as the preferred target. The authors of the included studies emphasized that lateral overcorrection (ie, towards the zygomatic arch, corresponding to Terino zone 4) can lead to midface widening and an unnatural appearance, stressing the importance of enhancing anteroposterior projection (Terino zone 1).29 In his work on malar implant revision, Yaremchuk4 underscores the value of augmenting the malar eminence while cautioning against excessive lateral augmentation toward the zygomatic arch, particularly in patients with midface hypoplasia. Too far lateral implant placement can exacerbate the appearance of prominent eyes. Instead, augmentation of the deficient infraorbital rim and medial malar region—including the malar eminence—is often more appropriate.29

In recent years, technological advancements in the planning and execution of surgical aesthetic procedures have led to an increasing emphasis on gender-specific approaches. It is essential to account for the fundamental anatomical differences between sexes, particularly when performing midfacial augmentation. Although prominent cheekbones are considered an aesthetic ideal in many parts of the world, the underlying concepts of masculinity and femininity differ significantly. This concept was alluded to in the article by de la Peña-Salcedo et al.27 Feminizing procedures often involve volume augmentation in the medial or central midface, whereas such an approach may be counterproductive in masculinizing treatments. In the latter context, the lateral aspect of the zygomatic arch plays a critical role in achieving the desired facial definition and harmony. Therefore, it is of utmost importance to thoroughly assess the patient’s individual goals during the preoperative planning phase. This includes not only the selection of augmentation sites but also the choice of materials and surgical techniques. As the field of aesthetic surgery continues to evolve, gender-sensitive and individualized planning and execution are expected to become increasingly relevant in achieving optimal outcomes.

Planning strategies varied widely across studies, and our study highlights a lack of detail on the exact preferred implant placement location. Only a few incorporated CT imaging, 3D modeling, or patient-specific fabrication to guide implant selection and positioning. Most relied on visual inspection, surface markings, or intraoperative assessment. Despite their potential benefits, custom implants remain uncommon and are mostly used in case reports.14,15,17 The most commonly targeted location for implant placement was the anterolateral malar region over the zygomatic body. Some authors used structured frameworks such as the Hinderer method or midfacial zone classification. Although most aimed to augment projection beneath the infraorbital rim, others intentionally avoided the zygomatic arch to prevent excessive transverse widening. A few studies reported tailoring implant position based on surface contour or asymmetry.

LIMITATIONS

This systematic review has several limitations. The included studies were heterogeneous in design, sample size, implant material, and follow-up duration, which limited the ability to perform quantitative analysis or meta-analysis. Most publications were retrospective in nature, and only a few provided detailed or standardized reporting of complications, fixation methods, or patient-reported outcomes. Single-patient case reports were included when they described novel approaches or the use of patient-specific implants; however, their inclusion introduces potential selection bias and limits comparative strength. Given the small number of available studies in this field, these reports were retained to provide a comprehensive overview of malar implant placement and to capture the full spectrum of techniques currently described. Additionally, the absence of validated outcome measures (or standardized outcome reporting), such as the FACE-Q, prevented direct comparison of patient-reported results across studies. Finally, publication bias cannot be excluded, as studies with favorable aesthetic outcomes are more likely to be published, especially in the field of aesthetic plastic surgery. Despite these limitations, this review provides the most comprehensive synthesis to date of surgical access, planning strategies, and complication patterns in malar implant augmentation.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, although malar implants are a well-established tool for midfacial augmentation, the substantial variability in access routes, planning methods, and anatomical targeting contributes to inconsistent outcomes. As expected, intraoral access seems to be associated with a higher risk of infection, whereas aesthetically unsatisfactory outcomes are common in rhytidectomy cases. An increase in the use of custom-fabricated implants based on 3D preoperative scans may provide an opportunity to improve precision and aesthetic outcomes, and reduce revision rates in future practice.
