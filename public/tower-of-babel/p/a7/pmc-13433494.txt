Relationship between ileocolic arterial stump length and lymph node yield – a retrospective study using machine learning
Authors: Rogelio Zayas-Borquez, María Sofía Arizaga-Ramírez, Jorge Canto-Losa, Ana C Gándara-Lois, Noel Salgado-Nesme, Francisco Alvarez-Bautista, Oscar Santes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIn right-sided colon cancer surgery, ileocolic artery stump length may reflect the extent of mesenteric resection and lymph node harvest. This study evaluated the association between postoperative stump length measured on CT, lymph node yield, and recurrence or progression within 24 months.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 64 patients who underwent right or extended right hemicolectomy for right-sided colon adenocarcinoma between 2016 and 2024. Right-sided tumors were defined as those located in the cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, or proximal transverse colon. Only primary surgical cases were included. Ileocolic artery stump length was measured on contrast-enhanced CT scans performed approximately 12 months postoperatively (± 1 month) by two independent radiologists. A Random Forest regression model using stump length as the only predictor variable was developed to estimate lymph node yield. Model performance was assessed using 10-fold cross-validation with R² and mean squared error (MSE). Exploratory recurrence/progression analysis used logistic regression with natural cubic splines.ResultsMean stump length was 2.03 cm (range 1.0–4.5 cm), and median lymph node yield was 17 (range 10–55). Adequate nodal evaluation (≥ 12 nodes) was achieved in 85.9% of patients. The model demonstrated a consistent inverse relationship between stump length and lymph node yield (R² = 0.78; MSE = 0.16). Exploratory analysis suggested a non-significant trend toward higher recurrence or progression with longer stumps.ConclusionLonger ileocolic artery stumps were associated with reduced lymph node yield, suggesting stump length may reflect the extent of mesenteric resection. External validation is required before clinical application.Keywords: Right hemicolectomy, Lymph node retrieval, Complete mesocolic excision, Central vascular ligation, Machine learning

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common malignancy worldwide and one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths, with more than 1.9 million new cases and an estimated 935,000 deaths reported in 2020 according to GLOBOCAN [1]. Despite advances in surgical techniques and adjuvant therapies, tumor recurrence remains a major clinical challenge, significantly affecting survival and quality of life [2]. Accurate lymph node evaluation is a cornerstone of pathological staging and therapeutic decision-making in CRC. International guidelines from the AJCC and NCCN recommend the assessment of at least 12 lymph nodes to ensure reliable staging and optimize oncologic outcomes [3, 4], and multiple studies have demonstrated that higher lymph node yield is associated with reduced recurrence risk and improved overall survival [5, 6].

In right-sided colectomies, complete mesocolic excision (CME) with central ligation of the ileocolic pedicle has been shown to increase lymph node retrieval and improve oncologic outcomes [7]. Within this surgical framework, the residual length of the ileocolic arterial stump has emerged as a potential indirect marker of surgical radicality and the level of vascular ligation. Previous studies have explored the relationship between arterial stump length and lymph node yield, as well as its potential association with locoregional recurrence [8, 9]. However, discrepancies between radiological and intraoperative assessments of ligation level persist, limiting the standardization and objective comparison of surgical quality across institutions [9].

These limitations highlight the need to explore ileocolic arterial stump length as an objective biomarker of surgical quality and to clarify its relationship with lymph node yield using modern predictive approaches such as machine learning. Accordingly, this study seeks to develop a univariable Random Forest–based model to estimate expected lymph node counts based on stump length and to explore its potential association with tumor recurrence/progression.

Methods

Study design

This was a single-center, retrospective observational study conducted at the National Institute of Medical Sciences and Nutrition Salvador Zubirán (Mexico City, Mexico). The report follows the STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

The primary aim was to evaluate the relationship between the length of the ileocolic arterial stump and lymph node yield. A secondary objective was to explore whether stump length might also be associated with cancer recurrence/progression, using predictive modeling and exploratory statistical analysis.

Definition of Right-Sided Colon Cancer and Patient Selection. Right-sided colon cancer was defined as adenocarcinoma located in the cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, or transverse colon proximal to the splenic flexure. The study population consisted of patients older than 18 years with a histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of right-sided colon adenocarcinoma. Only primary surgical cases were included; patients with prior colorectal resections for cancer were excluded. Eligible patients were those who underwent elective or emergency right or extended right hemicolectomy, had postoperative computed tomography scans available, and had complete histopathological reports from the surgical specimens. A total of 64 consecutive patients were evaluated during the study period (January 2016 to December 2024), all of whom met the inclusion criteria.

Surgical considerations

All surgeries were performed by the institutional colorectal surgery team comprising three experienced colorectal surgeons. Surgeries were performed without a standardized protocol for complete mesocolic excision (CME); however, all resections followed current international oncologic guidelines for right-sided colectomies. The surgical technique involved medial-to-lateral dissection with ligation of the ileocolic vessels along the superior mesenteric axis according to oncologic principles, This technical variability reflects real-world practice and was documented as part of the study limitations.

Variables and outcomes

Primary predictor variable

The main independent variable was the length of the ileocolic arterial stump, measured in centimeters on postoperative CT scans. This was the sole predictor variable included in the Random Forest model.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was lymph node yield, defined as the total number of lymph nodes examined in the surgical specimen

Secondary outcome

The secondary outcome was cancer recurrence/progression within 24 months, defined radiologically based on postoperative imaging follow-up. Recurrence/progression was defined as the appearance of new metastatic lesions or local tumor recurrence on contrast-enhanced CT, PET-CT, or MRI, as documented in the clinical records. All patients had at least 24 months of available clinical follow-up data. Recurrence/progression was ascertained radiologically throughout routine surveillance during the 24-month follow-up window (contrast-enhanced CT, PET-CT, or MRI as clinically indicated and documented in the records). The postoperative CT performed approximately 12 months after surgery (± 1 month) was used specifically for arterial stump length measurement.

Descriptive variables

Additional variables were collected for descriptive purposes and to characterize the study population: patient age and sex, pathological tumor stage (pT), pathological nodal stage (pN), presence of distant metastasis (M category), histologic grade, lymphovascular invasion (LVI), perineural invasion (PNI), and margin status. These variables were not included in the Random Forest model, which was designed as a univariable proof-of-concept analysis focusing exclusively on the stump length–lymph node yield relationship.

Variables not available

Several potentially relevant variables were not available in the retrospective database and therefore could not be included: surgical approach (open versus laparoscopic), exact segmental tumor location within the right colon, and individual adjuvant chemotherapy data. These limitations are acknowledged in the Discussion.

Measurement of arterial stump length

The length of the ileocolic arterial stump was measured on contrast-enhanced CT scans with multiplanar reconstruction, performed approximately 12 months after surgery (± 1 month), using the institution’s radiology system (PACS). Two experienced abdominal radiologists independently measured the stump length. Discrepancies greater than 2 mm were resolved by consensus. Although individual numeric readings were not recorded to formally calculate an intraclass correlation coefficient, the double-reading process with consensus was intended to reduce measurement error and improve reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Python 3.11 (Anaconda), employing libraries such as scikit-learn, statsmodels, and pandas. Data preprocessing included distribution assessments and outlier detection. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR), depending on distribution.

To model the relationship between stump length and lymph node yield, we used a univariable Random Forest model with stump length as the sole predictor. The model was chosen for its ability to capture nonlinear patterns without assuming normality. The model was trained with 500 trees and validated via 10-fold cross-validation. To improve clinical interpretability, a second-degree polynomial fit was applied to the Random Forest predictions. This fit was used solely for descriptive and visualization purposes—not for statistical inference. Model performance was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R²) and mean squared error (MSE). Confidence intervals (95%) for both metrics were calculated using 1,000 bootstrap resamples.

Sensitivity analysis for measurement error

Because stump length was measured via CT, we assumed a plausible error margin of ± 2 mm, consistent with CT resolution and the threshold used for resolving discrepancies. We explored how robust the observed relationship was within this margin.

Exploratory analysis of recurrence

For exploratory analysis of cancer recurrence/progression within 24 months, we used logistic regression with natural cubic splines (3 degrees of freedom) to allow for potential nonlinear relationships. Given the limited number of recurrence/progression events (n = 15), no multivariable adjustment was performed for this analysis, as the sample size would not support stable model estimation with additional covariates. We also conducted a mediation analysis using the classical Baron and Kenny framework, with the intention of generating hypotheses rather than drawing conclusions. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and national research regulations. Since it involved retrospective analysis of previously collected, anonymized data, informed consent was not required.

Results

Between January 2016 and December 2024, a total of 64 consecutive patients underwent standard or extended right hemicolectomy for right-sided colon adenocarcinoma and met the study inclusion criteria. No patients were excluded after initial selection, as all cases had complete postoperative computed tomography scans and full histopathological reports.

The baseline clinical and pathological characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 67.4 years (range, 39–93 years), with a slight male predominance (53.1%). The median number of lymph nodes retrieved was 17 (range, 10–55), and 85.9% of patients had 12 or more lymph nodes examined. The mean length of the ileocolic arterial stump was 2.03 cm (range, 1.0–4.5 cm).

Regarding tumor stage, most tumors were classified as pT3 (40.6%) or pT4 (37.5%). Pathological nodal status was N0 in 51.6%, N1 in 28.1%, and N2 in 20.3% of patients. Metastatic disease (M1) was present in 12 patients (18.8%) at the time of surgery; these patients were included in the analysis, with some having undergone R0 resection. Histologic grade distribution was: G1 (well-differentiated) in 6.2%, G2 (moderately differentiated) in 71.9%, and G3 (poorly differentiated) in 21.9%. Lymphovascular invasion was present in 56.2% of patients, and perineural invasion was observed in 28.1%. Negative margins were achieved in 95.3% of cases.

At two-year follow-up, cancer recurrence/progression was observed in 15 patients (23.4%), including 13 cases of distant metastases and 5 cases of local recurrence; two patients experienced both local and distant recurrence. Among patients with M1 disease at presentation, recurrence/progression was documented in 3 of 12 patients (25.0%).

Relationship between stump length and lymph node yield

A consistent inverse relationship was observed between ileocolic stump length and lymph node yield: the longer the stump, the fewer lymph nodes were retrieved from the surgical specimen (Fig. 1).

The univariable Random Forest model captured a consistent non-linear relationship, demonstrating a clear inverse trend between stump length and lymph node yield (Fig. 1), with a coefficient of determination (R²) of 0.78 [95% CI: 0.65–0.88] and a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.16 [95% CI: 0.08–0.24].

The inverse relationship remained stable even when accounting for a ± 2 mm margin of measurement error in stump length, suggesting the model is robust to minor inaccuracies.

Exploratory analysis of cancer recurrence

In the exploratory recurrence/progression analysis, a trend was observed: patients with longer arterial stumps—especially those above 3.5 cm—seemed to have higher rates of recurrence/progression. However, this trend did not reach statistical significance in the cubic spline-adjusted models (p > 0.2). A sensitivity analysis excluding stage IV patients (n = 12) showed that the trend toward higher recurrence with longer stumps remained similar, though still non-significant.

A descriptive mediation analysis suggested that the effect of stump length on recurrence/progression might be partially mediated by lymph node yield. Given the limited number of events and the absence of multivariable adjustment due to sample size constraints, these findings should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.

Post hoc power analysis

Given the total of 15 observed recurrence/progression events, a post hoc power analysis was performed to assess the study’s ability to detect associations between stump length and recurrence/progression. Assuming an approximate 1:1 distribution between short and long stumps, only large effect sizes (odds ratio ≥ 4.5) would achieve around 80% statistical power. Moderate effect sizes (OR ≈ 2) would have less than 25% power (Table 3). These results confirm that the study was underpowered to detect small or moderate effects regarding recurrence/progression, limiting the inferential strength of these findings.

Discussion

This study found a consistent inverse relationship between the length of the ileocolic arterial stump and lymph node yield in patients undergoing right hemicolectomy for colon cancer. In simpler terms, the longer the residual arterial stump, the fewer lymph nodes were retrieved. This finding suggests that arterial stump length, as measured on postoperative imaging, may serve as an indirect indicator of the extent of mesenteric resection and the level of vascular ligation achieved during surgery.

These findings align with the core principles of complete mesocolic excision (CME) and central vascular ligation, surgical techniques consistently shown to yield a higher number of lymph nodes in oncologic specimens compared with conventional colectomy, reflecting more extensive lymphadenectomy along the vascular pedicles [10]. Previous anatomic and surgical studies emphasize precise dissection along embryologically defined planes and central ligation of feeding vessels—approaches that overlap with D3 lymphadenectomy and are associated with increased lymph node harvest and improved pathological staging [11]. However, much of the prior research has focused on technical descriptions or categorical comparisons between surgical templates, and few studies to date have quantified the relationship between vascular stump length and lymph node yield as a continuous variable [10].

Our study adds to the field by modeling this relationship using a univariable machine learning approach, which allows for a more nuanced understanding. Although a univariable relationship could theoretically be modeled using traditional non-linear regression (e.g., polynomial or splines), a Random Forest approach was initially selected as a non-parametric framework. Unlike traditional linear analyses, the use of a Random Forest model captured a non-linear association between stump length and node count without imposing rigid statistical assumptions. The model’s strong performance (R² = 0.78) is biologically plausible: the length of the arterial stump is directly tied to the extent of mesenteric tissue resected—and therefore to the volume of lymphatic tissue available for evaluation. It is important to emphasize that this was a proof-of-concept technical demonstration using a univariable model, and additional covariates were not incorporated due to the limited sample size and the proof-of-concept nature of the model and the primary aim of establishing the stump length–lymph node relationship.

Several imaging-based studies have validated the feasibility of measuring arterial stump length using postoperative CT scans, showing it to be a reasonable estimate of vascular ligation level. Munkedal et al. demonstrated that routine CT scans performed one year after surgery can reliably estimate the level of central ligation in colon cancer surgery [9, 12–14]. Our findings extend this work by directly linking stump length to pathological outcomes, positioning it as a potential objective marker of surgical quality.

The work of Livadaru et al. is relevant to this discussion. Their study proposed a postoperative scoring system based on stump length and demonstrated an association with locoregional recurrence risk [8]. Importantly, Livadaru et al. also reported a direct relationship between arterial stump length and lymph node yield—a finding that aligns with our results but was not emphasized in the initial reference to their work. Additionally, their study addressed the role of postoperative CT in detecting insufficient CME and guiding potential surgical re-intervention, providing a balanced perspective on the clinical utility of stump length assessment. The study by Munkedal et al. [9] provides complementary evidence for the validity of CT-based stump length measurement, although it focused primarily on the correlation between radiological and intraoperative findings rather than pathological outcomes.

While our study was not designed or powered to make definitive conclusions about recurrence/progression, exploratory analyses did reveal a trend toward higher recurrence/progression rates in patients with longer arterial stumps. Descriptive mediation analysis further suggested that this relationship might be indirectly driven by lower lymph node yields—a biologically plausible mechanism, given that a higher node count is linked to more accurate staging and, potentially, better oncologic outcomes. That said, the recurrence/progression findings must be interpreted with caution. The limited number of events in our cohort significantly restricts statistical power, especially for detecting small or moderate effects. Moreover, the study was not designed to assess causality in recurrence outcomes, and no multivariable adjustment was possible due to the limited sample size. Therefore, these observations should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged. First, its retrospective, single-center design limits the generalizability of the findings to other institutional settings with different surgical practices and patient populations. No external validation was performed; therefore, the predictive model and reference table should not be applied clinically until independent validation in separate cohorts is available. Second, the univariable nature of the Random Forest model—while appropriate for a proof-of-concept analysis—precludes adjustment for potential confounders. Variables such as surgical approach (open versus laparoscopic), exact tumor location, and adjuvant chemotherapy were not available in the retrospective database and could not be incorporated into the analysis. Third, the inclusion of patients with stage IV disease (M1, 18.8% of the cohort) introduces heterogeneity, although these patients were included to reflect real-world practice and all underwent surgical resection with curative intent where feasible. Fourth, although stump length measurements were obtained via double independent review with consensus resolution, we were unable to calculate a formal intraclass correlation coefficient, as individual readings were not recorded. Fifth, the absence of a standardized CME protocol introduces technical variability—though this heterogeneity may reflect real-world practice and potentially enhance external applicability. Finally, the recurrence/progression analysis was significantly underpowered, and the lack of multivariable adjustment limits the interpretability of these exploratory findings.

From a clinical perspective, the findings of this study should be interpreted cautiously. While the predictive reference table derived from our model allows expected lymph node yields to be contextualized based on stump length, this tool is not intended for immediate clinical implementation. Any potential application in surgical quality auditing, institutional benchmarking, or surgical training settings would require prior external validation. The tool is not intended for intraoperative decision-making or as a replacement for standard histopathologic evaluation.

Conclusion

Ileocolic arterial stump length measured on postoperative CT showed a measurable association with lymph node yield in this cohort. However, given the exploratory nature of the analysis and the absence of external validation, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further validation in independent cohorts is required before any clinical application can be considered.
