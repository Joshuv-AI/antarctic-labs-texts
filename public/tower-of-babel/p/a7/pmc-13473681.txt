Parental experience and professional training shape the perception of comfort and discomfort in preterm and term newborns
Authors: M Filippa, F Barcos-Munoz, E Rabatel, S Baertchy, N Garcia-Herrero, C Zores, D Grandjean, P Kuhn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the comfort or discomfort expressions of preterm and term newborns represents a challenge for both parents and nurses, particularly in hospitalization settings. While parents rely mainly on intuitive parenting abilities and parental experience, pediatric nurses receive specific training to recognize newborns’ expressions. This study compared the perception of preterm and term infants’ facial emotion expressions across five observer groups: 63 parents of hospitalized preterm infants, 53 parents of hospitalized term infants, 55 general parents, 91 naïve non-parents and 61 professional pediatric nurses (total N = 323). Observers from Strasbourg (France) and Geneva (Switzerland) rated 30 video extracts of preterm infants at 32- and 40-weeks PMA and of term infants, balanced across positive, negative, and neutral conditions. They judged the comfort, discomfort, or neutrality of expressions using a graduated scale. The objective was to assess the influence of training, professional, and parental experience on evaluations of newborns’ emotional expressions. The effect of parenting emerges in valence judgement, with parents and non-parents’ tendency to attribute discomfort to the newborn’s expression, and nurses being more likely than all other groups to judge infant expressions as comfort. Across groups, preterm infants at 32 weeks were mainly perceived as uncomfortable, but ratings shifted toward comfort at 40 weeks, while term infants showed a more balanced profile. Finally, accuracy analyses showed that nurses were most accurate in recognizing comfort, parents performed best for discomfort, and non-parents achieved the highest accuracy for neutral expressions, with significant contrasts across all groups (p < .05). These findings highlight that the recognizability of infant emotional expressions depends on the infants’ developmental maturation, while the accuracy and interpretive patterns of ratings are shaped by the observers’ parental or professional experience.Keywords: Comfort, Discomfort, Term newborns, Preterm newborns, Facial expressions, Emotion expression, Parental perceptionSubject terms: Health care, Medical research, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Interpreting and responding to newborns’ expressions of comfort and discomfort is a central aspect of early caregiving and crucial for supporting their well-being. Accurate evaluation of infant signals supports both the immediate regulation of distress and the establishment of interaction patterns that shape long-term cognitive and socio-emotional development1,2. From the first days of life, caregivers rely on subtle behavioral and facial cues to infer infants’ internal states, modulate arousal, and scaffold early social exchanges3–5.

In the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), these interpretive demands are particularly critical, as preterm infants are frequently exposed to repeated painful and stressful procedures6,7. These cumulative exposures are associated with immediate physiological instability8,9 and may lead to a state of ongoing discomfort due to immature sensory and regulatory systems10. Importantly, repeated pain and stress in the NICU have been linked to long-term alterations in brain development, stress regulation, and neurobehavioral outcomes11–13. In hospitalization contexts, where newborns, especially preterms, often display immature and ambiguous behavioral signals, accurate assessment is essential to guide pain management and caregiving and to mitigate the short- and long-term consequences of untreated pain and stress14,15. Parents in these contexts frequently depend on intuitive caregiving abilities and may feel uncertain or stressed when interpreting their newborn’s cues, particularly in the case of first-time parents16. In contrast, pediatric nurses receive structured training designed to enhance observational accuracy and to support clinical decisions based on subtle indicators of infant well-being or distress17–19 and including NIDCAP-based education in observing infant approach and withdrawal behaviors14.

Preterm infants pose specific challenges, as their behavioral expressions tend to be less stable and more ambiguous than those of term infants20. Even clinically stable preterm infants exhibit greater disorganization in autonomic, motor, attentional, and self-regulatory systems compared to term peers21, and, with decreasing gestational age, the affective states and expressions are attenuated22.

Facial expressions are widely considered a key indicator of pain and distress in neonatal assessment. Even in extremely preterm infants and in newborns with cerebral lesions, facial cues remain a reliable marker of pain during noxious procedures23,24. Behavioral signs are alaso not fully specific to pain and can also be elicited by non-painful but stressful environmental stimuli25. The ability to discriminate noxious from innocuous stimuli improves with brain maturation, with more distinct facial expressions emerging from around 33 weeks’ gestation26.

Despite the clinical relevance of interpreting these subtle states, available assessment tools are not equally developed for all emotional states. Numerous validated scales exist for decoding pain in preterm infants (e.g., PIPP-R, NIPS, NFCS). In contrast, expression of comfort and mild discomfort lack dedicated, facial-expression–based assessment frameworks. Tools such as COMFORTneo27 or EDIN28 assess infant distress and prolonged discomfort, but they tend to define comfort primarily as the absence of negative signals rather than as a distinct positive affective state. Consequently, the identification of expressions of comfort or discomfort often rely on observers’ interpretations, shaped by professional training, clinical experience, and parental familiarity with infants.

Another unresolved issue concerns how developmental maturation affects the recognizability of preterm infants’ expressions. As neuromuscular control and behavioral organization improve with age, facial cues are expected to become clearer, yet it remains unknown whether observers with different levels of experience detect these changes similarly, or whether some groups, such as pediatric nurses, are particularly sensitive to them.

The present study addresses these gaps by comparing how five observer groups, pediatric nurses, parents of hospitalized preterm infants, parents of hospitalized term infants, general parents, and naïve non-parents, interpret facial expressions of preterm and term infants across ages and affective contexts. Using standardized video excerpts recorded at 32 and 40 weeks postmenstrual age and at term, the study examines how experience influences comfort and discomfort judgments, how maturation shapes expression clarity, and which groups achieve the highest accuracy in identifying positive, negative, and neutral states.

Methods

Population

A total of 323 participants were recruited from five groups: (1) parents from the general population, defined as adults with parenting experience; (2) non-parents; (3) primiparous parents of preterm hospitalized newborns; (4) primiparous parents of term hospitalized newborns; and (5) pediatric nurses working in neonatology units at the University Hospitals of Geneva and the University Hospital Regional Center of Strasbourg.

Parents of hospitalized infants were recruited during the immediate postnatal hospitalization period, while participants from the general population and non-parents were recruited through community-based approaches. Nurses were recruited from neonatology units at the participating centers. A detailed description of the participants has been reported in the Result section.

Parents of hospitalized preterm and term infants were approached during their infant’s stay in the neonatal units by trained research staff. All eligible parents present during the recruitment period were informed about the study and invited to participate. Similarly, all eligible nursing staff working in the participating units during the recruitment period were invited to take part. This approach aimed to reduce potential selection bias in hospital-based samples.

Participants from the general population, including parents and non-parents, were recruited through community-based dissemination, including the student community of the University of Geneva. Participation was voluntary across all groups.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were adults with sufficient proficiency in spoken and comprehended French to ensure reliable participation in the experimental tasks. For the subsamples of parents recruited during hospitalization, only primiparous mothers and fathers were considered, to specifically target novice parenting experience. Exclusion criteria encompassed any current psychiatric disorders, substance use problems, or other medical conditions likely to interfere with participation or the interpretation of results.

Procedure

In this cross-sectional observational study, we compared emotion judgments across five observer groups using a standardized video-based experimental paradigm.

After completing the demographic questionnaire, participants proceeded to the main task, which consisted of viewing 15 video clips of preterm and term newborns, each lasting approximately 45 s. Starting from the 10th second of each clip, they were asked to evaluate the infant’s facial expressions by rating the perceived degree of comfort, discomfort, and neutrality on three distinct continuous scales ranging from 0 to 100. All videos were presented without sound.

Participants rated comfort, discomfort, and neutrality using three independent continuous scales ranging from 0 to 100. Each dimension was evaluated independently to capture variations in perceived affective cues across emotional categories. This approach allows participants to assign graded values to more than one dimension when expressions are ambiguous or contain mixed signals, thereby accommodating multiple and potentially overlapping judgments. In this framework, neutrality reflects the perceived absence or ambiguity of affective cues rather than a midpoint between comfort and discomfort.

The video database was developed by Dr. P. Kuhn and his team at the University Hospital of Strasbourg as part of a project on preterm infants’ auditory capacities and received ethical approval.

The study protocol received approval from two ethics committees: the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Psychology and Educational Sciences at the University of Geneva (Decision No. PSE.20200502.04, March 29, 2021) and the Ethics Committee of the Faculties of Medicine, Dentistry, Pharmacy, Schools of Nursing, Physiotherapy, Midwifery, and the University Hospitals of Strasbourg (Ref. CE-2021-156, February 28, 2022).

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The original database comprised 30 recordings of newborns (10 preterm at 32 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA), 10 preterm at 40 weeks PMA, 10 full-term). The clips were pre-coded by expert coders (for details see Data analysis section) balanced across conditions (9 comfort, 8 discomfort, 13 neutral). Detailed description is presented in Table 1.

Each 45-s clip included a 10-s baseline, a 5-s auditory stimulus (500 Hz noise or maternal voice), and 30 s post-stimulus. Data analysis

The accuracy index

To assess the accuracy of the collected judgement in the total population, a reference for video judgment has been established, and three expert coders independently evaluated each video. The expert panel consisted of two neonatologists and one pediatric nurse, all with more than 10 years of clinical experience in neonatal care; one neonatologist had over 30 years of experience. Two of the experts were trained in the NIDCAP program, and one had specialized training in infant sensorimotor development.

Experts classified each video into one of three categories (comfort, discomfort, or neutrality), without rating intensity, and were blind to the experimental conditions. Coding was based on the observation of facial and behavioral indicators commonly used in neonatal pain assessment, including facial activity, skin color changes, head movements, and hand-to-face movements.

Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Fleiss’ Kappa (κ = 0.473), indicating moderate agreement. This level of agreement likely reflects the inherent ambiguity and variability of newborns’ behavioral expressions, particularly in preterm infants.

Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, during which experts reviewed the videos repeatedly and reached a consensus based on shared clinical judgment; when full agreement could not be achieved, the majority classification was retained. This consensus served as the reference for the accuracy index. In addition to accuracy relative to this reference, the analyses also examined response tendencies across observer groups, allowing a complementary characterization of systematic differences in perceptual judgments, independent from the expert reference.

For each video, participants’ continuous ratings were converted into a categorical response (“best rating”) by assigning the category (comfort, discomfort, or neutrality) corresponding to the highest score among the three scales as the participant’s classification. Accuracy was defined as the proportion of correct classifications within each category relative to the number of video evaluations in which that category was the expert reference. Because each participant evaluated multiple video clips, the number of observations reflects the total number of video evaluations (i.e., video × participant) rather than the number of unique videos. As an illustration, in the non-parent group for the comfort category, there were 1,107 video evaluations in which the expert reference was comfort. Of these, 459 were correctly classified, yielding an accuracy of 41.5%.

Misclassification patterns were further examined using a confusion matrix, which provided the proportion of responses assigned to each of the non-target categories when a specific category was correct (e.g., comfort stimuli misclassified as neutral or discomfort). This procedure allowed for quantifying group differences in accuracy while also identifying systematic biases in classification (e.g., a tendency to overestimate neutral or discomfort).

Statistical analysis

Group differences in emotion judgments were analyzed using generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) with a binomial error structure and logit link function, implemented with the lme4 package in R29. Although participants provided continuous ratings (0–100) for comfort, discomfort, and neutrality, categorical outcomes were derived for inferential analyses: for each video, the category with the highest rating was retained as the participant’s classification (“best rating”). Separate models were specified for comfort versus all other ratings and for discomfort versus all other ratings. To account for repeated measures within participants, random intercepts were included at the participant level.

Fixed effects comprised group (nurses, parents, parents of preterm hospitalized infants, parents of term hospitalized infants, non-parents) as the main predictor, with participant’s age and sex included as covariates to adjust for demographic differences across groups. Center (Geneva vs. Strasbourg) was initially tested but excluded from the final model given the absence of significant effects on age or sex distribution across sites. Post-hoc pairwise contrasts were performed with Bonferroni correction (α = 0.05).

Model fit was assessed via likelihood-ratio tests, comparing each fitted model to a null (intercept-only) model, and effect sizes are reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. This analytic strategy follows established recommendations for categorical data analysis with mixed-effects models30.

Participants were distributed across five groups: nurses (n = 61), parents (n = 55), parents of preterm hospitalized infants (n = 63), parents of term hospitalized infants (n = 53), and non-parents (n = 91). The majority of participants were recruited in Geneva (70.0%), with the remainder from Strasbourg (29.4%). Detailed demographic characteristics are presented in Table 2.

Response tendencies across observer groups

To provide an overview of rating tendencies across groups, we examined the best rating, defined as the highest intensity value attributed by each participant to one of the three scales (comfort, discomfort, or neutral) for every video.

Across groups, nurses showed the strongest tendency to attribute comfort as the best rating (64.6%), while non-parents displayed the lowest proportion of comfort (37.5%) and the highest proportion of neutral judgments (31.1%). Parents of preterm hospitalized infants assigned comfort most frequently among parent groups (51.5%), whereas parents of term hospitalized infants showed a more balanced distribution across comfort (43.4%), discomfort (27.2%), and neutral (29.4%). Parents of non-hospitalized infants (hereafter parents) presented a higher proportion of discomfort ratings (32.0%) compared to other parent groups. These patterns highlight systematic differences in rating tendencies, with nurses consistently privileging comfort and non-parents showing greater uncertainty reflected in neutral judgments (Fig. 1).

Infant age effects on comfort and discomfort recognition across groups

Based on our longitudinal analysis of expression ratings across gestational ages, we observed distinct developmental trajectories for each rating category, with several statistically significant contrasts. Comfort classification showed a marked increase from 33.7% in the videos of preterm infants at 32 weeks PMA to 59.8% in the videos of preterm infants at 40 weeks PMA (OR = 1.59, p < 0.001), followed by a significant decrease to 48.4% in the videos of term infants. Discomfort classification displayed an inverse pattern, starting significantly higher at 45.2% in preterm infants at 32 weeks PMA (OR = 2.04, p < 0.001 compared to term), then dramatically decreasing to 10.4% at 40 weeks PMA (OR = 0.29, p < 0.001), before settling at 28.8% in term infants, indicating a substantial reduction in discomfort expressions as preterm infants mature. Neutral classification, while showing less dramatic changes, demonstrated statistically significant variations, starting at 21.1% at 32 weeks PMA, significantly increasing to 29.8% at 40 weeks PMA (OR = 1.44, p < 0.001), and then decreasing to 22.9% in term infants. This pattern reveals a clear developmental trajectory in adult observers’ classification of neonatal video recordings: preterm infants are judged as predominantly displaying recognisable discomfort at 32 weeks PMA, but significantly more identifiable comfort at 40 weeks PMA. In contrast, term infants receive more balanced ratings across behavioral expressions. All these transitions are statistically significant in our regression model (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Finally, interaction effects between observer group and infant’s age revealed distinct patterns. In the videos of preterms at 32 weeks, parents of preterm hospitalized infants rated comfort the highest (41.4%), while non-parents and term hospitalized infants had the lowest ratings (31.4%); discomfort ratings were highest among general parents (55.3%). At 40 weeks, all groups exhibited increased comfort ratings, with parents showing the highest levels (64.5%), followed by preterm hospitalized infants (59.6%) and parents of term hospitalized infants (52.6%), while discomfort ratings dropped across all groups (10–12%). For term newborns, comfort ratings were more balanced, with parents (51.6%) and parents of preterm hospitalized infants (52.5%) maintaining similar levels, whereas parents of term hospitalized infants exhibited the lowest ratings (44.5%), and discomfort ratings were distributed more evenly across groups (25–32%).

Accuracy analyses revealed consistent differences depending on the infant group depicted in the videos. Facial expressions of term infants were recognized with significantly higher accuracy than those of preterm infants at 32 weeks across all response categories: comfort (65.1% vs. 33.2%, p < 0.001), discomfort (56.2% vs. 51.6%, p = 0.003), and neutral (32.0% vs. 28.4%, p = 0.047). Comparisons between term infants and preterms at 40 weeks showed a more nuanced pattern: accuracy remained higher for term infant videos in the comfort condition (65.1% vs. 60.9%, p = 0.029), whereas no significant difference was observed for neutral expressions (32.0% vs. 33.7%, p = 0.212).

Participant group × age effect on recognition accuracy

Analyses of developmental trajectories indicated distinct patterns for the three recognition categories. For comfort recognition, accuracy was higher for videos of infants at 40 weeks PMA compared to 32 weeks across most observer groups, with nurses maintaining the highest level of performance throughout. Non-parents exhibited the steepest increase from a very low baseline, whereas parents of preterm and term hospitalized infants showed more moderate improvements. By 40 weeks, the gap between groups had narrowed, and at term age (41 weeks) further gains were observed across all groups, with nurses reaching peak performance and the other groups continuing to progress, consistent with a general maturation of comfort recognition abilities.

Discomfort recognition followed a different course. During the preterm period (32–40 weeks), non-parents and general parents displayed relatively strong performance from the outset, although group trajectories were more variable than for comfort. Parents of term hospitalized infants showed only a slight improvement of 0.8% from early to late preterm, followed by a small decline of 1.0% between 40 and 41 weeks, while other groups remained relatively stable.

Neutral recognition showed a more gradual developmental profile. Between 32 and 40 weeks, non-parents demonstrated the largest increase in accuracy, while the other groups exhibited moderate improvements. From 40 weeks to term newborns, improvements continued, albeit at a slower pace compared to the preterm period (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The present study examined how parental experience, professional training, and infant developmental age shape the decoding of comfort and discomfort facial expressions in preterm and term newborns.

These findings highlight that the perception of newborn facial expressions is shaped by both observer experience and infant developmental stage. Systematic differences across groups indicate that professional experience influences interpretation tendencies, while recognition accuracy varies across emotional categories, with distinct group-specific advantages. Infant maturation also played a key role, as early preterm expressions were more often interpreted as discomfort and were less accurately decoded, whereas term-age expressions were more accurately recognized. In the context of infant comfort/discomfort recognition, distinguishing between response tendencies and perceptual accuracy is crucial. Response tendencies reflect systematic differences in how groups label infant expressions, whereas perceptual accuracy refers to the ability to correctly identify these expressions. Prior research shows that such tendencies could be shaped by individual factors including mood or attachment, leading to consistent patterns such as positivity biases, overattributing positive affect, or negativity biases among participants experiencing anxiety or depression31–34.

Response tendencies do not necessarily reflect true perceptual ability. Perceptual accuracy, by contrast, refers to the actual capacity to detect and correctly identify infants’ emotional expressions and is typically measured through behavioral accuracy or neural discrimination markers35.

Consistent with this literature, in the present study our accuracy analyses revealed meaningful differences across groups that did not simply mirror their rating biases or tendencies. Across all parental groups, several common features appear to shape how parents perceive newborn expressions. First, parents showed higher accuracy in recognizing discomfort expressions than clinicians, consistent with prior evidence that caregiving experience is associated with improved detection of infant distress signals, potentially due to their biological relevance for protection and caregiving motivation36,37. Prior research has shown that parents and caregivers tend to adopt more protective or affectively engaged decision criteria, especially when evaluating discomfort or distress36. In contrast, trained clinicians generally exhibit more calibrated perceptual thresholds, enabling them to detect positive and negative affective cues with greater precision38.

In the present study, when considered as a single category, parents attributed discomfort more frequently than nurses and non-parents and showed the highest overall accuracy for discomfort recognition. However, this pattern did not characterize all parent subgroups equally, as parents of preterm infants showed patterns closer to nurses, whereas parents of term infants displayed higher discomfort ratings, suggesting that contextual factors associated with hospitalization could influence how these parents interpret infants’ expressions of discomfort.

It is important to note that hospitalized parents are exposed to their preterm infant’s medical and painful procedures while also receiving specific training from medical staff to recognize signs of comfort or discomfort39, which may bias or align their interpretations more closely40. Indeed, very preterm infants are highly exposed to painful stimuli and stressful caring procedures41.

Parents are increasingly present with their infants in the neonatal units following the zero-separation concept and the implementation of infant and family centered developmental care programs42,43 such as the NIDCAP program which is currently used in one of the participating hospital44. Parents are also increasingly encouraged to take an active role in their infants’ care to support their well-being45.

Consistent with our results, a large body of research shows that parents tend to attribute discomfort or pain to infants and children more frequently than nurses or non-parents, and that their assessments generally demonstrate higher accuracy. Across diverse clinical contexts, parents’ ratings of discomfort align more closely with children’s self-reports than those of nurses or physicians, who often underestimate pain or prioritize cues related to clinical stability46–50. Meta-analytic evidence similarly indicates stronger agreement in child–parent dyads than in child–nurse dyads51. Importantly, parental accuracy tends to be strongest in naturalistic or pre-treatment contexts, whereas nurses’ accuracy may be reduced in demanding clinical environments or following analgesic administration50,52.

Non-parents showed lower comfort, higher discomfort, and more neutral responses, suggesting greater uncertainty in interpreting infant expressions. This pattern aligns with evidence that parentality enhances responsiveness to infant cues53–55. Finally, preterm infants at 32 weeks were consistently rated as less comfortable and more distressed, a pattern that likely reflects both their physiological immaturity14 and observer perceptions of fragility56. At this stage, underdeveloped regulatory mechanisms and heightened sensitivity to external stimuli contribute to more frequent discomfort cues and ambiguous signals, which observers may interpret cautiously as signs of distress or pain57.

By 40 weeks, comfort ratings increased markedly, and discomfort ratings declined across all observer groups, suggesting the perception of increased neurobehavioral regulation and greater clarity of behavioral expressions58,59. Neutral ratings also peaked at 40 weeks, consistent with a transitional stage in which expressions became more organized but not yet fully differentiated. At term age, ratings stabilized, with comfort remaining higher and discomfort more evenly distributed, indicating a more balanced and recognizable repertoire of infant behaviors. We interpreted the distribution of perceived expressions as balanced as it was more evenly spread across categories (comfort, discomfort, neutral), with no single category dominating responses, in contrast to the skewed pattern observed at earlier gestational ages.

Observer experience further shaped these patterns. Parents of preterm hospitalized infants were more likely than other groups to recognize subtle signs of comfort at 32 weeks, suggesting that direct caregiving exposure in the NICU and parental supporting interventions could enhance sensitivity to positive cues, although these effects have primarily been observed in parent–own-infant contexts39. In contrast, non-parents and parents of term hospitalized infants reported the lowest comfort ratings, indicating an intuitive tendency to perceive preterm infants as less comfortable. This pattern may partly reflect a broader representational bias in the general population, whereby prematurity is commonly associated with vulnerability, medical fragility, and potential suffering. Such societal perceptions of preterm birth may shape observers’ expectations and future work could benefit from considering how cultural and emotional representations of prematurity influence adult judgments56. By 40 weeks, differences between parental subgroups diminished, and for newborns at term, ratings converged further, with both parents and nurses showing relatively high comfort recognition. These findings suggest that as infants mature, their behavioral signals become more structured and universally interpretable, reducing variability across observer groups.

These results underscore the importance of structured parental education in the NICU and in early caregiving more broadly. Interpreting newborn behavioral signals requires learning to distinguish between different affective states and to calibrate responses accordingly, rather than relying solely on intuitive judgments. In clinical settings, in particular, guided support from healthcare professionals can help parents develop more accurate and balanced interpretations of their infant’s signals. Beyond the NICU, early educational interventions could promote caregivers’ ability to recognize both distress and emerging signs of comfort and regulation, supporting more adaptive and responsive parent–infant interactions.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the sample size was sufficient to detect the main effects reported, it remained relatively limited and geographically restricted, as participants were recruited exclusively in Geneva and Strasbourg. A larger and more diverse sample would have allowed more detailed examination of individual differences (e.g., psychological and demographic factors) and would strengthen the generalizability of the findings across cultural and clinical contexts. Second, the reference framework for comfort and discomfort judgments relied on evaluations provided by experienced clinicians; while this approach ensures clinical relevance, it may not fully capture the diversity of interpretive strategies used across different caregiving contexts. Third, the absence of complementary behavioral data, such as eye-tracking measures, limits our ability to determine which infant cues participants attended to when making their judgments. Future research would benefit from incorporating eye-tracking methods to clarify what aspects of infant behavior are visually prioritized during comfort and discomfort assessments. Finally, judgments were based on simulated observations using video recordings and thus based only on visual cues, rather than direct, in-person assessment of infants, which may differ from live clinical interactions where multisensory cues and contextual information play a critical role. Addressing these limitations will be essential to strengthen the ecological validity and translational relevance of future research on hedonic ratings in neonatal care contexts.

Finally, although categorical classifications were derived from the highest-rated dimension, comfort, discomfort, and neutrality remain conceptually interdependent. Future studies could use multinomial or joint modeling approaches to model these response categories simultaneously and better account for their dependency structure.

Despite these limitations, the present study also has several important strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically examine how different observer groups, varying in professional training and parental experience, decode comfort, discomfort, and neutral facial expressions in preterm and term newborns across developmental ages. A major strength lies in the ecological validity of the stimuli: the video excerpts were selected from real neonatal contexts and closely reflect the everyday behavioral expressions of preterm infants in hospital settings. In addition, the randomized presentation of videos helped minimize order effects and potential response biases. Finally, the combination of response tendency analyses, accuracy measures, and developmental comparisons provides a comprehensive methodological framework.

Conclusions

Overall, nurse training and clinical experience do not guarantee higher accuracy, as clinicians show enhanced accuracy primarily for comfort, while potentially underestimating infant discomfort. In contrast, parentality is associated with a stronger attunement to infant distress, likely reflecting an adaptive, protective orientation that prioritizes the detection of negative affect. Together with the role of infant maturational stage in shaping the clarity of behavioral expressions, these results support the theoretical framework of Infant and Family Centered Developmentally supportive Care in the NICU60. This holistic approach recognizes parents as co-regulators of infant well-being and active contributors to the interpretation of infant behavioral states61. Integrating parental perceptual sensitivity into clinical decision-making, while simultaneously supporting parental ability to recognise comfort cues, may promote more balanced, developmentally attuned, and accurate assessments of infant comfort and distress, ultimately enhancing developmental care outcomes for preterm infants.
