Brain structure abnormalities in neurodevelopmental synaptopathies
Authors: Júlia Romagosa-Perez, Juliana Ribeiro-Constante, Natalia Alexandra Julià-Palacios, Alejandra Darling, Rosanna Marí-Vico, Arnau Valls-Esteve, Mireia Olivella, Xavier Altafaj, Synaptopathies-Working Group, Christian Stephan-Otto, Angeles García-Cazorla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvanced brain imaging studies have been scarcely reported in neurodevelopmental encephalopathies. In this study we assess structural brain alterations in three rare neurogenetic disorders primarily affecting glutamatergic neurotransmission, aiming to identify shared and disease-specific neuroanatomical patterns and their clinical correlations. A cohort of patients with SYNGAP1 (n = 19), GRIN gene family (n = 19), and STXBP1 (n = 10) mutations underwent magnetic resonance imaging. Advanced segmentation tools were used to extract regional brain volumes. Volumetric differences between patient and age-matched normative reference templates were assessed using parametric and non-parametric tests, depending on data distribution, and ANCOVA was used to adjust between covariates. Associations with clinical symptoms were evaluated using the appropriate correlation tests. In our cohort, we found that patients exhibited statistically significant and consistent shared differences in brain tissue volumes compared to age-matched templates, including larger volumes in the basal ganglia, thalamus, ventricles, and certain cortical regions, alongside reductions in total white matter, cerebellar, and limbic structures (amygdala and parahippocampal gyrus). Clinically, ventricular enlargement correlated positively with the severity of intellectual disability, language impairment, and motor dysfunction, while total intracranial volume showed negative correlations with these same domains. Distinctive trends included supplementary motor cortex enlargement and cerebellar volume deficit in STXBP1, while amygdala volume deficit was most prominent in SYNGAP1 and GRINpathies. In conclusion, the study suggests the presence of shared and disease-specific brain alterations in SYNGAP1, GRINpathies, and STXBP1 disorders. Overall, brain volumetry may represent a useful exploratory tool, contributing to a more detailed characterization of these diseases while offering insights beyond conventional radiological assessment.Keywords: Synaptopathies, Glutamatergic transmission, Neurodevelopmental disorders, Magnetic resonance imaging, Volumetric analysis, Neuroimaging

Introduction

Synaptic dysfunction has emerged as a hallmark feature across a diverse spectrum of neurodegenerative, neurodevelopmental, and psychiatric disorders. These conditions share disruptions in synaptic processes that are critical for neuronal communication and network integrity (Grant, 2012).

Among these, the GRIN gene family, together with STXBP1 and SYNGAP1 are three key synaptic genes encoding proteins essential for glutamatergic synaptic transmission. Mutations in the GRIN genes result in dysfunction of the NMDA type of ionotropic glutamate receptors, which play a critical role in synaptic plasticity and cognitive processes (Cha et al., 2025). SYNGAP1 mutations disrupt postsynaptic signaling pathways essential for proper synaptic maturation and neuronal communication, particularly involving AMPA receptor regulation (Clement et al., 2012). While both GRIN gene family and SYNGAP1 genes encode for postsynaptic proteins, STXBP1 gene product primarily affects presynaptic glutamatergic transmission by impairing synaptic vesicle release and recycling, thus impacting neurotransmitter release dynamics. However, STXBP1 gene product also significantly impacts other neurotransmitter systems, particularly the GABAergic system, leading to decreased inhibitory control over excitatory neurons (Kim et al., 2024).

Pathophysiologically, STXBP1 mutations have been generally associated with hypoglutamatergic function (Kim et al., 2024). In contrast, SYNGAP1 mutations are linked to hyperglutamatergic activity (Clement et al., 2012). GRIN-related encephalopathies can present with both loss-of-function (LOF) mutations, resulting in reduced glutamatergic transmission, and gain-of-function (GOF) mutations, leading to excessive glutamatergic signaling (Cha et al., 2025).

Mutations in these genes result in a spectrum of neurodevelopmental conditions, including intellectual disability (ID), epilepsy, language impairment, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and movement disorders. However, although the three disorders exhibit some comorbidities, each gene condition displays clinical specificities. SYNGAP1 and GRIN gene family mutations are typically associated with moderate to severe ID (Lemke et al., 2016; Platzer et al., 2017; Vlaskamp et al., 2019), while STXBP1 mutations often lead to severe and profound ID (Xian et al., 2022). Behavioral abnormalities, such as ASD traits, anxiety and impulsivity, are more prominent in SYNGAP1 compared to their lower prevalence in GRINpathies and rarity in STXBP1 (Xian et al., 2022). Epilepsy is common across all groups but tends to be particularly severe and refractory in SYNGAP1 (Ribeiro-Constante et al., 2024) and STXBP1 (Xian et al., 2022), whereas GRINpathies presents with variable severity (Cha et al., 2025). Motor impairments also vary across gene mutations. SYNGAP1 patients frequently experience ataxia (Vlaskamp et al., 2019), while GRIN mutations are in general associated with milder motor symptoms, although some patients can exhibit severe hypotonia and general movement impairment (Platzer et al., 2017). STXBP1 mutations often result in severe hypotonic and hyperkinetic movements (Xian et al., 2022).

Regarding brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), although nonspecific alterations have been reported across disorders (Lemke et al., 2016; Platzer et al., 2017; Vlaskamp et al., 2019; Balagura et al., 2022), patients typically do not exhibit specific brain abnormalities directly linked to each disease. In this context, volumetric MRI techniques, which are not widely used in clinical practice, show promise in identifying common alterations that could serve as biomarkers for understanding the neurodevelopmental impact of these mutations. From this perspective, several studies have identified common structural patterns in the brains of individuals with autism (Lee et al., 2022), and more recent work has linked specific volumetric changes to clinical severity in neurotransmitter disorders (Alfonsi et al., 2022). Cerebellar involvement has also been reported across several neurological and neurodevelopmental conditions (Sathyanesan et al., 2019). These findings suggest that brain volume metrics could serve as candidate markers of the neurodevelopmental impact of gene mutations primarily affecting glutamatergic transmission.

The aim of this work is to explore possible common and specific volumetric features that could be identified in conventional brain MRI of patients with three different glutamatergic neurodevelopmental synaptopathies, with the goal of contributing to a more nuanced neuroanatomical characterization of these genetic conditions. We hypothesized that the shared disruption of glutamatergic synaptic function, together with the distinct molecular roles of each gene, would result in both overlapping and gene-specific patterns of volumetric alteration, with potential associations between regional volumes and clinical severity.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study included 48 MRI scans from patients diagnosed with GRIN-related disorders (GRIN2B (N = 8), GRIN2A (N = 5), GRIN1 (N = 6); 17 LoF and 2 GoF), STXBP1 (N = 10), or SYNGAP1 (N = 19) disorders. The patients were referred through a Spanish network of collaborating child neurologists, geneticists, and psychiatrists. Inclusion criteria were as follows: i) a confirmed diagnosis with pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants of GRIN1, GRIN2A, GRIN2B, SYNGAP1, or STXBP1 genes; ii) at least one MRI study, including a T1-weighted 3D sequence; iii) sufficient brain image quality to meet the study's analytical standards. After applying these criteria, 19 patients with GRIN variants, 19 with SYNGAP1 variants, and 10 with STXBP1 variants were included (Supplementary Table 1). A standardized phenotypic questionnaire was provided to referring physicians to assess clinical features, genetic pathogenic variants, developmental milestones, and epilepsy features.

Regarding clinical symptoms, the severity of intellectual disability (ID) was established with Intelligence Quotient (IQ) scores or information on the level of functioning in accordance with the fifth edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), American Psychiatric Association 2023. Behavioral abnormalities level was established based on clinical evaluations performed by neuropediatricians or psychiatrists. Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) was assessed according to DSM-5 autism criteria. Global motor function was scored using the Gross Motor Function (GMF) scale (Palisano et al., 1997). In order to compare with normative reference brains, we used age-specific atlases from the Neurodevelopmental MRI database (Richards et al., 2016), which provides templates representative of different developmental stages. Given the retrospective multicenter design and the rarity of the conditions studied, individually scanned aged-matched healthy controls were not available; findings should therefore be interpreted as deviations from normative references rather than differences relative to matched healthy controls. All participants included in these templates were screened as typically developing, with MRIs acquired either as normative data or as controls in neurodevelopmental disorder studies. The database includes approximately equal numbers of males and females across age ranges. More information regarding the reference templates can be found in Supplementary Table 2.

Image acquisition

Out of the 48 images selected, 16 were from patients followed at 11 different hospitals across Spain, and 33 were from patients at Sant Joan de Déu Hospital Barcelona (HSJD). Most scanners were made by General Electric and had the same 1.5 T field strength. For most of the patients, T1-weighted 3D FSPGR IR acquisitions from a clinical protocol were obtained, using the following parameters: pixel spacing = 0.43 mm × 0.43 mm, slice thickness = 2 mm, TR = 12.4 ms, TE = 5.2 ms and FOV = 22 cm. Our volume calculation methodology was specifically designed to provide reliable volumetric measurements independently of image acquisition parameters.

Image processing

Image processing and analysis were completed in the following steps. First, a representative template was generated using the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) (https://stnava.github.io/ANTs/) template construction tool, considering high-resolution MRI scans from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study (ABCD) (Casey et al., 2018) database of healthy individuals. The computational anatomy toolbox CAT12 (http://www.neuro.uni-jena.de/cat/) was then used to perform tissue and atlas segmentation, integrating anatomical information from the Neuromorphometrics Inc. parcellated brain (http://www.neuromorphometrics.com/) for cortical and subcortical structures, and cerebellar segmentation following the SUIT atlas (Diedrichsen et al., 2009). As a result, brain volumes are divided into 161 parcellations, 60 per cerebral hemisphere, 13 per cerebellar hemisphere and 15 central structures. Next, the elastic registration tool from ANTS software was applied to align the labels from template to individual subject anatomical spaces. This process enabled the extraction of volumes for each segmented region. The output anatomical regions were visually inspected for slice-wise segmentation quality.

The volumes for each segmented region were converted to a proportion of total intracranial volume (TIV), comprising gray matter (GM), white matter (WM), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Volumes are reported as proportional values (fractions) relative to TIV, reflecting the relative contribution of each region to total brain volume and accounting for brain size differences across participants and age. TIV was additionally examined as an independent variable of interest in correlational analyses.

Segmentation quality control was performed through systematic visual inspection of all outputs on a slice-by-slice basis by two experienced reviewers. Each scan was assessed for motion artifacts, image blurring, field-of-view truncation, and signal noise. When multiple MRI acquisitions were available for a given patient, which was the case for most participants, the scan of highest quality was selected according to the criteria above. No patients were excluded due to segmentation failure or insufficient image quality.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

This study was approved by the local institutional Ethics Committee (Children's Hospital Sant Joan de Déu ID number: PIC-135-23), following the Declaration of Helsinki and all relevant guidelines and regulations.

Statistical analysis

To assess volumetric differences between patients and age-matched normative templates, difference scores were computed for each patient as the deviation between the observed and the corresponding age-specific normative template regional volume. These difference scores were then evaluated using one-sample t-tests against a null hypothesis of zero. When the assumption of normality was not met (Shapiro-Wilk test), a one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied instead. A sensitivity analysis restricting these comparisons to images acquired at the main center only (n = 33) was conducted to assess the potential impact of site-related variance. Type II ANCOVA was used to compare the region volumes between groups, with age, sex and disease category as covariates. Effect sizes for ANCOVA group comparisons were estimated using partial eta-squared (η2). Prior to correlational analyses, Spearman correlations between clinical measures and age were computed to assess potential age-related confounding; no significant associations were found. Therefore, the strength and direction of associations between volumetric measures and symptoms were evaluated using point-biserial correlation for dichotomous symptom variables and the Spearman's ρ correlation coefficient for graded symptoms. For categorical data, frequency tables are generated. An additional analysis examined the effect of total intracranial volume (TIV) normalization on the results.

Quantitative data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), or as medians with interquartile range (IQR) when the data showed a non-normal distribution. Statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p < 0.05. P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Besides, if the data followed a normal distribution, Cohen's d was employed for pairwise comparisons. For non-normally distributed data, the rank-biserial correlation (r) was calculated. All statistical analyses were conducted using Python libraries, primarily scipy and statsmodels.

Demographic and phenotypic characterization of the cohort.

ID was a prominent feature across all groups, affecting 97.9% of the cohort. A chi-square analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in the distribution of ID severity levels among the groups, with severe ID being the most common severity level among the three groups.

Language impairments were observed in 95.8% of the cohort. STXBP1 patients had the highest prevalence of profound language impairment, without it being statistically significant (60%, p = 0.20), while severe impairments were more frequent in SYNGAP1 patients (42.1, p = 0.16). GRINpathies patients showed a broader range of impairments.

Hypotonia was observed in 68.4% of GRINpathies patients, 73.7% of SYNGAP1 patients, and 40% of STXBP1 patients, with no statistically significant differences between groups. Similarly, hypertonia was reported in 26.3%, 26.3%, and 20% of patients, respectively, without statistical significance. The age differences between patients with hypotonia and hypertonia were not statistically significant (p = 0.41).

Movement disorders were observed in 85.4% of the cohort, with no statistically significant differences between groups (p = 0.13). Among the types of movement disorders, SYNGAP1 patients showed the highest prevalence of ataxia (78.9%, p = 0.0002) and all STXBP1 patients exhibited hyperkinetic movements, although the overall prevalence of hyperkinetic and hypokinetic movements did not differ significantly across groups. Similarly, gross motor function levels differed significantly in their distribution, with SYNGAP1 patients more frequently at Level I (p = 0.023) and STXBP1 at Level II (p = 0.0008), reflecting qualitatively different motor profiles despite comparable overall motor impairment rates.

Psychiatric symptoms were observed in 91.6% of the cohort, with no statistically significant differences between groups. Behavioral abnormalities were notably more frequent in SYNGAP1 patients, including diagnosed ASD at 89.5% (p = 0.0001), aggressiveness at 78.9% (p = 0.0004), impulsivity at 83.3% (p = 0.002), and inattention (94.7%, p = 0.007). Other behavioral traits such as hyperactivity and self-injurious behavior were also more prevalent but did not show statistically significant differences.

Epilepsy was diagnosed in 66.7% of the cohort, with SYNGAP1 patients showing the highest prevalence (73.7%, p = 0.57). Refractory epilepsy was present in 23.3% of all patients, without significant differences between groups.

In addition, a bilateral white matter volume deficit was observed in patients (p < 0.00001, d = 0.97). However, the corpus callosum (CC) total volume does not show significant differences compared to reference templates, although specific segments exhibit distinct changes: the anterior body is slightly enlarged (p = 0.014), while the posterior body shows a smaller volume than reference templates (p = 0.00014).

To assess the potential influence of scanner variability on the primary findings, a sensitivity analysis was conducted restricting the normative volumetric comparisons to images acquired at the main center only (n = 33) (Supplementary Table 3). Results were largely consistent with the full-sample analysis, with only three clinically relevant regions failing to reach statistical significance in the restricted sample, but maintaining the same effect size direction, likely reflecting the reduced statistical power. An additional sensitivity analysis using absolute volumes with TIV and age as covariates confirmed the robustness of the primary findings (Supplementary Table 4), further supporting the rationale for TIV normalization as a strategy to account for brain size differences across individuals and developmental stages.

Age-related findings

Age-dependent volumetric changes were observed across the dataset, identified through ANCOVA analysis. The analysis compared patient volumes relative to age-matched normative templates while accounting for the effects of age and sex.

Key findings include distinct developmental trajectories in specific regions. While the amygdala consistently shows a smaller volume compared to age-matched templates, it follows a similar linear growth trajectory to the one observed in healthy individuals (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the posterior insula exhibits an initially larger volume during early developmental stages, but by age 12, its volume becomes more similar to that of healthy individuals (Fig. 4B).

Similarly, some regions of the occipital, frontal, and parietal gyri display larger volumes during early developmental stages. However, by late childhood, their volumes become more comparable to those of normative reference templates(Supplementary Fig. 3A–E).

Cerebellar regions in normative reference templates do not show significant differences across neurodevelopmental stages. In our patient cohort, however, specific cerebellar regions demonstrate marked smaller volumes between the ages of 6 and 12 years, particularly in the right and left VIIIa and VIIIb cerebellar lobes (Supplementary Fig. 4A–B). Additionally, vermis IX exhibits a distinct age-related pattern. During early development, its volume is larger, but between 9 and 12 years of age, it becomes more aligned with reference volumes (Supplementary Fig. 4C).

Correlation with phenotypes

Some associations between symptom severity or frequency and specific brain regions were observed across the whole patient cohort. Results are reported in Supplementary Table 5, including nominal and FDR-corrected p-values. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, only associations with |ρ| ≥ 0.35 and nominal p < 0.05 are discussed, corresponding to moderate-to-large effect sizes. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, as most did not survive FDR correction, consistent with the limited statistical power relative to the number of regions examined.

Prior to the main correlational analyses, Spearman correlations between each clinical measure and age were computed across the full patient cohort. No significant associations between age and symptom severity were found for any of the clinical variables examined (all p > 0.05).

Nominally significant findings with clinical relevance include a positive correlation between ventricular volume and the severity of language impairment (ρ = 0.38, p = 0.023), ID (ρ = 0.389, p = 0.022), and gross motor function (GMF) impairments (ρ = 0.559, p = 0.0005, p-adjusted = 0.012) (Fig. 5A). Conversely, total intracranial volume (TIV) exhibited a negative correlation with the severity of language impairment (ρ = −0.396, p = 0.02), GMF impairments (ρ = −0.46, p = 0.005), and ID (ρ = −0.55, p = 0.0006, p-adjusted = 0.014) (Fig. 5B). Correlations were also found for specific brain regions (Supplementary Table 5). For example, the volume of certain gray matter structures showed negative correlations with GMF impairments, namely the right middle temporal gyrus (ρ = −0.43, p = 0.0011), the superior temporal gyrus (ρ = −0.45, p = 0.007), the left superior frontal gyrus (medial segment) (ρ = −0.48, p = 0.003), the left orbital part of the inferior frontal gyrus (ρ = −0.38, p = 0.002), and the right angular gyrus (ρ = −0.399, p = 0.0019). Similarly, the severity of ID was negatively correlated with the left superior frontal gyrus (medial segment) (corr = −0.45, p = 0.007), and language impairment with left superior parietal lobule (ρ = −0.45, p = 0.007) and left superior frontal gyrus medial segment (ρ = −0.39, p = 0.02). Frontal, temporal and parietal regions negatively correlating with GMF, language impairment and ID are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Nominally significant correlations with some other of the studied brain regions are detailed in Supplementary Table 5. Findings also revealed a positive correlation between basal ganglia volume and GMF impairments, indicating that enlargement of structures such as the pallidum and putamen were associated with more severe motor impairments. Additional correlations included the hippocampus (ρ = 0.44, p = 0.0008) and basal forebrain (ρ = 0.38, p = 0.02), also linked to greater GMF impairment severity.

On the other hand, negative correlations were observed between epilepsy and regional brain volumes in the Left Subcallosal Area (corr = −0.61, p = 0.00013), Right Subcallosal Area (corr = −0.59, p = 0.00022), Right Cuneus (corr = −0.60, p = 0.00015), Left Cuneus (corr = −0.53, p = 0.0013), and Left Anterior Cingulate Gyrus (corr = −0.52, p = 0.0015). Moreover, corpus callosum volume was positively correlated with inattention (corr = 0.42, p = 0.042), while the right frontal pole volume showed a strong positive correlation with the presence of psychiatric symptoms (corr = 0.53, p = 0.001).

The cerebellum demonstrated a positive correlation with sleep abnormalities (corr = 0.43, p = 0.013), particularly in regions including lobules V and VI, the fastigial cerebellar nuclei, and vermis IX, suggesting that structural changes in these areas may contribute to the observed sleep disturbances.

Finally, the left subcallosal area emerged as a key region negatively correlated with language impairment (corr = −0.44, p = 0.009) and ID (corr = −0.4, p = 0.018).

Disease-specific volumetric alterations

We further explored potential disease-specific volumetric differences at an exploratory level. Aligning with current evidence that GRIN-GoF and GRIN-LoF present with different characteristics (Bayrak et al., 2021), we treated them as separate groups. For robustness of the analysis, GRIN GoF patients were excluded (N = 2). Analysis of covariance revealed distinct differences across five developmentally relevant brain regions, with p < 0.05 and (Supplementary Table 6 and Supplementary Fig. 6) among the genetic groups, controlling for age and reference template volumes as covariates. However, none of these disease-specific effects remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons, likely due to the limited sample size and the number of regions assessed, although consistent tendencies were observed.

The cerebellum exhibited a more pronounced volume deficit in STXBP1 compared to both GRIN-LoF and SYNGAP1 patients (p = 0.034). When comparing STXBP1 to GRIN-LoF and SYNGAP1, the following structures presented nominally significantly smaller volumes: left dentate (p = 0.047) and interposed (p = 0.012) nucleus, left lobule VIIIa (p = 0.0039), VIIb (p = 0.041) and bilateral lobules I-IV (p < 0.01). Notably, STXBP1 patients differentially exhibited volumetric deficit in vermis lobule X (p = 0.032). Conversely, a larger volume of the right lobule V was observed only of SYNGAP1 patients (p = 0.035).

The supplementary motor cortex exhibited enlargement in STXBP1 patients compared to normative templates, GRIN-LoF and SYNGAP1 groups.

The right amygdala showed a nominally significantly smaller volume in SYNGAP1 and GRIN-LoF patients, compared to both normative templates and STXBP1 patients, in whom no significant reduction was observed.

We observed selective insula enlargement, as the right posterior insula appeared to be nominally significantly enlarged in the GRIN-LoF group (p = 0.005). No differences were observed in the SYNGAP1 group.

The corpus callosum (CC) volume varied significantly among the disease groups. STXBP1 patients exhibited a markedly smaller volume in total CC volume (−11.77%, p = 0.0088), particularly in the genu (−17.44%, p = 0.004) and posterior body (−19.2%, p < 0.000001). The volume of the posterior body was also observed to be smaller in GRIN-LoF patients (−11%, p = 0.017). In contrast, SYNGAP1 patients displayed an enlargement of the anterior body of the CC (22.87%, p = 0.0004), as well as a slightly enlarged total CC volume (8%, p = 0.039).

An additional Age × Diagnostic Group interaction analysis revealed no significant effects after FDR correction, with considerable within-group variability across all regions (Supplementary Fig. 7).

A sensitivity analysis restricting the ANCOVA to images acquired at the main center only was conducted to further assess the robustness of these findings (Supplementary Table 7). In addition, two complementary levels of analysis were performed. First, we examined if there were differences in the whole cohort, comparing patients with LoF and GoF mutations (See Supplementary information section “Cohort-wide volumetric differences between GoF and LoF mutations”). Hypoglutamatergic variants appeared to be associated with reduced cerebellar volumes and increased volumes in the supplementary motor cortex compared to reference volumes. In contrast, hyperglutamatergic variants showed larger volumes in the corpus callosum (particularly the anterior body), and a tendency towards increased thalamic and pons volumes. Nonetheless, direct GoF vs. LoF comparisons did not reach statistical significance after correction (Supplementary Table 8). Some of these effects are likely driven by disease-specific volumetric characteristics, although new patterns also emerged, including enlarged thalamic and pontine volumes in hyperglutamatergic patients. Given the limited data available, it is not possible to determine whether the observed results stem from hyper- or hypoglutamatergic transmission or simply reflect disease-specific features.

We then restricted the analysis to the GRIN gene family (Supplementary information section “Volumetric differences between GRIN gene family GoF and LoF mutations”). Although only limited data were available (2 GoF and 17 LoF cases), we observed a trend towards larger basal ganglia volumes in the hyperglutamatergic group compared with the hypoglutamatergic group (Supplementary Table 9). The small sample size precludes the identification of meaningful or consistent patterns, but the individual volumetric profiles of GoF patients are reported relative to the LoF group as a preliminary reference for future investigations.

Discussion

This study presents the first volumetric characterization of brain structure abnormalities in three genetic neurodevelopmental disorders involving glutamatergic dysfunction: GRIN-, SYNGAP1-, and STXBP1-related encephalopathies. Using advanced neuroimaging techniques, we observed consistent patterns of volumetric alterations when comparing the patient group to age-matched normative templates, as well as potential disease-specific patterns. Previous neuroimaging studies in these disorders have largely relied on conventional MRI, typically reporting normal findings or nonspecific structural abnormalities without consistent region-specific patterns (Lemke et al., 2016; Platzer et al., 2017; Vlaskamp et al., 2019; Xian et al., 2022). In some cases, more specific features have been described, including diffuse cerebral atrophy and corpus callosum thinning in a subset of STXBP1 patients (Balagura et al., 2022), as well as structural brain abnormalities in subsets of GRIN-related encephalopathies (Platzer et al., 2017). Within this context, our findings extend the existing literature by providing a quantitative characterization of brain structure across these conditions,

For the whole patient cohort, we observed larger volumes in the basal ganglia, thalamus, ventricles, and certain cortical regions, alongside reductions in total white matter, cerebellar, and limbic structures (amygdala and parahippocampal gyrus).

This shared alteration pattern of abnormality is consistent with a possible convergent dysregulation of glutamatergic networks (Zoghbi and Bear, 2012; Moretto et al., 2018), though causal inference is limited by the cross-sectional and exploratory nature of this study.

However, comparisons between groups also revealed distinct volumetric trajectories, potentially reflecting gene-specific synaptic vulnerabilities, developmental timing effects, or the existence of diverse compensatory mechanisms (Michetti et al., 2022). These between-group differences should nonetheless be interpreted with caution given the exploratory nature of these comparisons and the sample size constraints.

Clinically, our cohort reflected the known phenotypic spectra of each disorder. Consistent with the literature, phenotypic differences emerged at the level of severity distribution and specific symptom subtypes rather than overall prevalence, partly limited by the high baseline rates across all three groups and the absence of more granular standardized neuropsychological assessments. GRIN mutations were associated with severe ID and mild to profound language impairments, often accompanied by hypotonia (Platzer et al., 2017). SYNGAP1 patients exhibited marked cognitive and language deficits, along with frequent hypotonia, ataxia, epilepsy, and a high prevalence of psychiatric symptoms, particularly ASD (Vlaskamp et al., 2019). Individuals with STXBP1 mutations presented severe ID and profound language impairment, along with hyperkinetic movements and epilepsy (Xian et al., 2022).

1.Convergent Volumetric Patterns: Potential Multiscale Disruption in Glutamatergic Circuits

Convergent Volumetric Patterns: Potential Multiscale Disruption in Glutamatergic Circuits

We observed shared volume reductions across GRIN, SYNGAP1, and STXBP1 cohorts in regions sensitive to synaptic dysfunction, including the parahippocampal cortex, amygdala, cerebellum, and WM. Some of these regions, particularly limbic cortices, exhibit elevated aerobic glycolysis, a form of glucose metabolism that supports synaptic plasticity and local energy demand beyond oxidative phosphorylation (Vaishnavi et al., 2010). Such high metabolic activity may increase susceptibility to excitatory imbalance. In contrast, the cerebellum and WM do not exhibit high glycolytic rates but may be vulnerable due to their reliance on finely tuned excitation-inhibition balance (Sotelo, 2004) and glutamate-dependent glial support, respectively (Fern and Matute, 2019).

The widespread cerebellar volume deficit in our cohort suggests a global vulnerability to glutamatergic imbalance, which can induce excitotoxicity, impaired plasticity, and neuronal loss (Moretto et al., 2018; Iskusnykh et al., 2024; Nicosia et al., 2024), potentially contributing to motor impairments and cognitive deficits (Kansal et al., 2017). In STXBP1, this effect appears particularly severe; possibly due to the cerebellum's high density of inhibitory interneurons (Hirano et al., 2002) and their strong dependence on STXBP1-regulated GABA release (Chen et al., 2020). Hence, STXBP1 haploinsufficiency appears to contribute to local disinhibition and hyperexcitability (Platzer et al., 2017; Dos Santos et al., 2023), and network disorganization (Zoghbi and Bear, 2012), which may underlie the pronounced cerebellar atrophy observed in these patients. SYNGAP1 and GRIN mutations, in contrast, disrupt postsynaptic NMDA receptor and Ras/MAPK signaling (Cha et al., 2025; Gamache et al., 2020), critical pathways for Purkinje cell plasticity, dendritic arborization, and survival (Watanabe and Kano, 2011). Given that Purkinje cells are the sole output of the cerebellar cortex and play a trophic role during cerebellar circuit maturation, their developmental arrest or loss can lead to cortical disorganization and reduced cerebellar volume (Sotelo, 2004; Watanabe and Kano, 2011).

While most cerebellar lobes exhibited atrophy, enlargement of specific lobes—particularly in SYNGAP1—may reflect compensatory motor adaptation, though in SYNGAP1 it could instead indicate maladaptive reorganization linked to increased ataxia. Additionally, positive correlations between specific cerebellar region volumes and sleep disturbances, could suggest localized plastic responses within cerebellar–arousal circuits. While potentially compensatory, such changes may also be maladaptive, given the cerebellum's role in sleep regulation (Lee et al., 2023) and the frequent occurrence of sleep-related symptoms in these disorders.

This pattern of mixed structural changes extends to other subcortical structures. Notably, basal ganglia enlargement correlated with worse motor outcomes, suggesting that structural overgrowth does not necessarily indicate functional compensation. Given the basal ganglia's role in motor and cognitive control (Leisman et al., 2014), such hypertrophy may instead reflect dysfunctional plasticity, mirroring findings in ASD, where striatal enlargement is associated with repetitive behaviors (Langen et al., 2014).

The observed WM deficit likely reflects multifactorial injury, including glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity, impaired oligodendrocyte maturation, and astrocyte dysfunction, all of which can promote glial damage, demyelination, and tissue loss (Fern and Matute, 2019). Consistent with this finding, ventricular enlargement was also present across the cohort. This expansion may reflect passive dilation secondary to bilateral WM reduction. Notably, ventricular volume positively correlated with the severity of ID, language impairment, and gross motor dysfunction.

2.Divergent Adaptations: Gene-Specific Signatures

Divergent Adaptations: Gene-Specific Signatures

Despite the widespread WM loss, patients exhibited transient cortical gray matter overgrowth in the frontal, parietal, occipital, and temporal lobes, particularly during early development. While previous studies in non-syndromic neurodevelopmental disorders have linked larger brain volumes to symptom severity (Lee et al., 2021), we report an inverse correlation in syndromic glutamatergic disorders. In other syndromic conditions like Rett (Takeguchi et al., 2022) and Down syndrome (Gunbey et al., 2017), cortical volume reductions are typically associated with impaired connectivity. In contrast, the inverse correlation between TIV and symptom severity observed in our cohort may reflect an early compensatory response aimed at preserving neuronal function in the face of disrupted glutamatergic signaling.

The amygdala emerged as another structurally vulnerable region, with smaller volumes observed across the cohort, particularly in GRIN-LoF and SYNGAP1. Despite this, age-stratified analyses showed preserved growth trajectories, contrasting with idiopathic autism where early overgrowth is followed by volume decline (Lee et al., 2022; Amaral et al., 2008). This pattern suggests that early-onset amygdala hypoplasia in monogenic glutamatergic synaptopathies likely reflects primary glutamatergic dysfunction rather than secondary compensatory effects. These reductions may result from early glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity (Moretto et al., 2018) or from altered neurodevelopmental mechanisms (Nicosia et al., 2024). Nevertheless, we did not find a correlation between amygdala size and the presence of behavioral abnormalities, although only their presence or absence was assessed, not their severity. In contrast, the posterior insula showed a distinct age-related trajectory, with relatively larger volumes in early childhood that progressively converged towards normative values with increasing age. While the amygdala appears particularly vulnerable to early excitatory imbalance during critical developmental windows (Ulfig et al., 2003; Lopez de Armentia and Sah, 2003), the insula follows a more protracted maturational course (Alcauter et al., 2015), potentially allowing greater postnatal synaptic remodeling. The transient insular enlargement may therefore reflect delayed synaptic pruning during early development, a process critically dependent on glutamatergic signaling (Faust et al., 2021). Whether this maturational pattern differs across the three synaptopathies by gene and functional mechanism warrants further investigation.

In our cohort, we found that volume reductions in the cuneus, anterior cingulate, and subcallosal areas correlated with the presence of epilepsy. This suggests these regions could represent convergent hubs of alteration in individuals with epilepsy, though this interpretation is constrained by the absence of an analysis based on epilepsy type in our sample and the limited literature on the subcallosal areas (Galovic et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2025; Whelan et al., 2018).

Distinct structure–function relationships were observed across altered brain regions. Corpus callosum volume correlated positively with inattention, suggesting disrupted interhemispheric connectivity (Roland et al., 2017), while right frontal pole volume was associated with psychiatric symptoms (Nowrangi et al., 2021). Notably, left subcallosal area showed a negative correlation with language impairment and ID. Although this region is not part of classical language circuits, it is involved in a limbic-prefrontal hub associated with emotional regulation and cognitive integration, which could indirectly influence cognitive and communicative development in glutamatergic synaptopathies, given the role of glutamate in modulating higher cortical functions (Li et al., 2020).

Despite these shared patterns of brain volume abnormalities, each disorder exhibited preliminary differential volumetric findings at an exploratory level, probably reflecting gene-specific developmental windows (Meredith et al., 2012; Clement et al., 2013; Bennett et al., 2019) regional susceptibility (Hayasaka et al., 2011), and compensatory capacities (Plessen et al., 2009).

While all three genes are expressed during early development, their spatial and temporal expression patterns differ in ways that may relate to our findings. STXBP1 is broadly expressed across neuronal populations, but its haploinsufficiency appears to have a preferential impact on inhibitory interneurons with GABAergic dysfunction (Kim et al., 2024). This selective vulnerability, beyond its global effects on presynaptic vesicle cycling and excitatory transmission (McLeod et al., 2023), could explain the pronounced cerebellar volume reductions seen in STXBP1 patients in our study.

Corpus callosum (CC) alterations also potentially reflect gene-specific effects. Although no significant differences in total CC volume were observed between the patient and age-matched templates, regional patterns revealed important distinctions. Posterior body reduction was present across all groups and was most pronounced in STXBP1 and GRIN-LoF, whereas SYNGAP1 patients uniquely exhibited anterior body enlargement. This contrast may reflect divergent mechanisms of WM development: callosal thinning in STXBP1 and GRIN-LoF may result from impaired interhemispheric connectivity (Roland et al., 2017), while the anterior enlargement observed in SYNGAP1 may indicate failed pruning or early overconnectivity (Shashi et al., 2012; De León Reyes et al., 2020).

Additional nominally significant distinct features included enlargement of the supplementary motor cortex in STXBP1, possibly reflecting compensatory mechanisms for cerebellar dysfunction (Tzvi et al., 2017). GRIN-LoF and STXBP1 also showed increased volume in the posterior insula, involved in sensory, emotional, and autonomic processing (Kittleson et al., 2024). These exploratory findings are consistent with the high cortical expression of GRIN and STXBP1 genes during early brain development (Kang et al., 2011) and may involve insular circuits relevant to sensory-affective integration. Despite the frequent sensory and emotional dysregulation in SYNGAP1-related disorders (Kranak et al., 2024), no volumetric alterations were found in the posterior insula. This may suggest that sensory-affective dysfunction in SYNGAP1 may arise from broader cortical–subcortical network disruption rather than focal insular changes.

Conclusion

This study identifies a complex neuroanatomical pattern across GRIN, SYNGAP1, and STXBP1 synaptopathies, revealing shared brain structure alterations and possibly gene-specific effects. Among all brain regions, the cerebellum emerged as a candidate marker, showing consistent atrophy across the cohort. Amygdala volume deficit, especially in SYNGAP1 and GRIN-LoF, may serve as a key potential volumetric feature of gene-driven limbic disruption and behavioral vulnerability. Additional findings include ventricular enlargement associated with WM loss, transient cortical gray matter overgrowth likely reflecting early-stage adaptation to glutamatergic disruption, and subcortical hypertrophy linked to motor dysfunction. Conversely, marked cerebellar atrophy in STXBP1 may reflect selective GABAergic vulnerability, and anterior callosal enlargement in SYNGAP1 may indicate failed pruning or early overconnectivity. Altogether, these patterns suggest that brain volumetry, when combined with gene expression data and circuit-level context, could potentially suggest both overlapping and distinct pathways of glutamatergic dysfunction. In addition, this work underscores the potential of volumetric MRI as a valuable tool beyond conventional radiological assessment, contributing to a more detailed neuroanatomical characterization of these conditions, and laying the groundwork for future validation in larger cohorts, where quantitative volumetric measures may ultimately serve as informative features associated with disease severity and progression.

Limitations and future directions

This study provides new insights into glutamatergic disorders but highlights several limitations and the need for further research. Despite representing a relatively large sample for these rare conditions, the cohort remains small and unbalanced compared to other volumetric studies. The use of age-specific normative templates, motivated by the retrospective multicenter design and the rarity of the conditions, allows estimation of deviations from normative references but is not equivalent to a standard case-control design. The multi-site nature of the data precluded formal harmonization; however, a sensitivity analysis restricted to main-center images yielded consistent results. Furthermore, the partial confounding between acquisition site and diagnostic group — as STXBP1 cases were acquired exclusively at the main center — should be considered when interpreting the cross-diagnostic comparisons. The clinical measures available represent relatively coarse characterizations of complex neurodevelopmental phenotypes, limiting the precision of brain-behavior associations; future prospective studies should incorporate additional standardized neuropsychological assessments. Age imbalance was a further limitation, mitigated by volume normalization and covariate adjustment. Larger, longitudinal studies would enhance statistical power and clarify neurodevelopmental trajectories.

The cross-diagnostic group comparisons did not survive correction for multiple comparisons and should be interpreted as exploratory; although nominally significant findings showed a neuroanatomically coherent pattern with moderate-to-large effect sizes, they require replication in larger independent samples. The functional impact of genetic variants (gain vs. loss-of-function) may represent a cross-disease factor influencing brain structure, but subgroup imbalance and limited sample size preclude firm conclusions. The GRIN group encompasses three different genes and both loss- and gain-of-function variants, which may have distinct neuroanatomical profiles. To address these limitations, a second study with a larger and more balanced cohort is planned, aiming to explore candidate biomarkers of hyper- and hypoglutamatergic states across synaptopathies and to provide deeper insight into how altered glutamate transmission shapes brain development.
