Bone matrix-inspired whitlockite porous ceramic with effects of autophagy activation contributes to bone regeneration
Authors: Yuyi Wang, Cong Feng, Zemin Hou, Puxin Liu, Chuyao Xu, Xuening Chen, Zhengyong Li, Yumei Xiao, Xiangfeng Li, Xiangdong Zhu, Kai Zhang, Antonios G. Mikos, Xingdong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Whitlockite (WH), a natural magnesium-containing calcium phosphate second in abundance only to hydroxyapatite (HAp) in the inorganic phase of bone tissue, has attracted great attention in bone repair recently. Previous studies have shown that porous structures play a pivotal role in bone defect repair using biomaterials. However, the traditional fabrication process of porous ceramic through high-temperature sintering frequently results in the dehydration and breakdown of WH. To overcome this challenge, we develop an innovative fabrication of porous whitlockite ceramic via in-situ phase transformation from calcium phosphate ceramic. Furthermore, we perform molecular dynamics (MD) and determine intermediates to delve into a comprehensive exploration of the underpinning mechanism of the transformation. In-vitro cellular responses reveal the superior cell proliferation, adhesion, spreading, migration, and osteogenic and angiogenic differentiation of porous WH ceramic. Mechanistically, our results reveal that the Mg2+-mediated regulation of ATP levels activates the AMPK-FoxO signaling axis, which enhances osteogenesis through autophagy. Intramuscular implantation evaluation confirms the satisfactory osteoinduction, and further rat mandibular defect implantation reveals significant bone regeneration in comparison to calcium phosphate ceramic. Together, the porous WH ceramic encompasses enhanced potential for the regenerative amelioration of bone defects.

Introduction

Bones serve as the repository for 99% of calcium and 50% of magnesium in body (Fig. S1) [1], which is deposited on the highly ordered bundles of collagen fibers at the nanoscopic (Fig. 1a) [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Even though HAp (hydroxyapatite, Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2) represents the major phase found in hard tissues, an estimated 35 wt% of the WH (whitlockite, Ca18Mg2(PO4)12 (HPO4)2) phase exists in the mineral of human dentine and 25 wt% in the mineral of human bones [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Notably, WH is predominantly found within bones subjected to increased dynamic loading. Interestingly, a higher proportion of the WH phase has been observed in the early stages of mineralization and in juvenile bones [[10], [11], [12]], which suggests its active involvement in the bone remodeling process [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Furthermore, the substantial Mg content within WH warrants attention. XRD analysis (Fig. 1b, and S2) reveals that WH content varies across different skeletal sites in rats [22], with the mandible identified as a particularly WH-rich region. Despite these findings, the concentration of WH was overlooked for decades due to detection difficulties and complexities associated with achieving homogeneity in the WH phase due to the precipitation of HAp and β-TCP (β-Tricalcium Phosphate, Ca3(PO4)2). While recent analyses have questioned the discovery of WH in pathological tissues and its biological role in normal human bone [23,24], WH has consistently demonstrated biocompatibility and osteoconductivity in both in-vitro and in-vivo studies, and can be synthesized under physiologically relevant conditions [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Notably, a recent study reported the successful precipitation of WH nanoparticles in a simple ternary aqueous system [13,29]. Furthermore, in composite scaffolds WH has shown enhanced differentiation behavior and superior bone regeneration compared with HAp and β-TCP [12,[30], [31], [32]]. Taken together, these findings indicate that WH is a promising biomaterial for bone engineering applications [33,34].

The concept of "tissue-inducing biomaterials" emerged as part of the definition of biomaterials for the 21st century, representing the new progressive direction of biomaterials development. This modern biomaterial can drive tissue regeneration without the addition of cells or bioactive factors [35]. In the field of bone tissue engineering, it is required that the repair materials possess osteoinduction. Regarding small bone defects, the osteoconductivity of materials is sufficient. However, bone defects are often large and complex. Therefore, materials with superior osteoinduction would render strong clinical performance. At present, the most extensive research on osteoinductive materials centers around calcium phosphate (CaP) ceramics. However, reports on the biological performance and osteoinduction of WH ceramic are considerably scarce. Previous studies have shown that porous structure play a pivotal role in osteoinduction of CaP ceramics [[36], [37], [38], [39], [40], [41]]. These ceramics are typically produced by specific techniques to generate bodies with porosity, which are then subjected to high-temperature sintering. However, when exposed to temperatures exceeding 800 °C, WH undergoes dehydration and transforms into magnesium-doped tricalcium phosphate (MTCP). This transformation precludes the fabrication of porous WH ceramics through traditional methods; consequently, current research on WH-based bone repair materials focuses primarily on composite formulations.

To address this challenge, this study investigates an alternative strategy for fabricating porous whitlockite ceramics, which involves utilizing prefabricated porous biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) ceramics in a hydrothermal reaction (Fig. 1c). In this synthesis process, key factors, including the precursors, solvent composition and reaction parameters were meticulously optimized, allowing the generation of WH ceramics with a tunable grain size to be achieved. The innovation of this process lies in its departure from traditional ceramic fabrication paradigms, culminating in the successful synthesis of porous WH ceramics with enhanced osteoinduction. Throughout different intervals of reaction time, products were systematically collected from every stage, enabling a thorough kinetic precipitation pathway analysis of WH. It was determined that the formation of WH involved a continuous series of phase transformation processes, eventually resulting in a more stable phase. β-TCP and WH have closely related crystal structures, both crystallizing in the trigonal crystal system with the space group R3c (#161) [42]. In the z-axis direction, they consist of two types of columns (A and B) that are periodically arranged. The B column remains constant, while variations typically occur in the A column [43]. Further insights from MD simulations suggest a more competitive binding of Mg2+ with HPO42− over Ca2+, thereby effectively constructing the A column of WH [29]. The general proliferation and differentiation of the three ceramics (HAp, BCP, and WH) were evaluated using mouse bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Simultaneously, the osteogenic potential of porous WH ceramic were assessed via implantation into canine dorsal muscle and mandibular defects of rats (Fig. 1c). Findings from both in-vitro osteogenic differentiation and in-vivo osteoinduction studies suggest that porous WH ceramic have a significant advantage over both porous HAp ceramic and porous BCP ceramic in the domain of bone tissue repair. This underpins the potential efficacy of porous WH ceramic in bone tissue engineering applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

The following chemicals were purchased from Chron Chemicals (Chengdu, Sichuan, China) and used without further purification: MgCl2·6H2O, H3PO4 and ethanol. Phosphoric acid (H3PO4, AR, ≥85.0%) was purchased from Aladdin Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Magnesium chloride hexahydrate (MgCl2·6H2O, AR, ≥98.0%) and ethanol were obtained from Chron Chemicals (Chengdu, Sichuan, China).

HAp ceramic and BCP ceramic were provided by the National Engineering Research Center for Biomaterials of Sichuan University, China. HAp and BCP porous ceramics were fabricated using the H2O2 foaming method and sintered at 1100 °C for 2 h. The detailed process could be found in our previous work [44,45].

Preparation of porous WH ceramic

The methodology for this study involved an in-situ synthesis of porous WH ceramic from porous BCP ceramic via specific hydrothermal treatments. Initially, the pre-existing porous BCP ceramic (1500 mg) was transferred into Teflon autoclaves, each with a capacity of 200 mL, containing a mixture (135 mL) of MgCl2 (30 mmol L−1) and H3PO4 (22 mmol L−1). The autoclaves were subsequently heated to 180 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 and held at that temperature for 12 h, after which the system was allowed to cool to 25 °C. After the hydrothermal treatment, the resulting products were washed with deionized water and ethanol for three times. This was followed by a 12 h drying process conducted at 60 °C. The processed products were then subjected to a final sintering process at 700 °C for 2 h to enhance their mechanical properties without altering the composition of the phases (Figs. S3 and S4). The resultant ceramic was designated as WH. For meaningful comparative analysis, samples of BCP ceramic and HAp ceramic were also prepared and utilized as control groups, labeled as BCP and HAp respectively.

Material characterization

The morphology of BCP and WH was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSE-900LV, Japan) equipped with Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), and the acceleration voltage was adjusted to 20 kV. WH and BCP ceramics were ground into fine powder and analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, XRD-6100 diffractometer, Shimadzu, Japan) with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 30 mA. Qualitative and quantitative phase analyses were conducted on an MDI Jade 6.0 software. The functional groups of the samples were determined by Fourier transform infrared spectroscope (FTIR, INVENIO R, BRUKER, Germany), and the spectra (400-4000 cm−1) were obtained at a resolution of 4 cm−1, using an average signal of 64 scans. The surface compositional analysis and determination of the chemical states of the sample's elements were conducted using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, AXIS Ultra, KRATOS, UK). High Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM, Talos F200s, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operated at 200 kV, was used to examine the individual shape and lattice distance by Selected Area Electron Diffraction (SAED).

The specific surface area (SSA) of three porous ceramics was measured by the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method (ASAP 2460, Micromeritics, USA), and the degree of porosity along with the distribution of pore size in three porous ceramics were assessed by mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP, AutoPore IV 9500, Micromeritics). The characterization was performed in strict accordance with the Chinese standard of GB/T 41672 (Implants for surgery—Osteoinductive calcium phosphate bioceramics).

A universal testing machine (AGS-X, Shimadzu, Japan) evaluated the mechanical strength of the ceramics at a loading speed of 1 mm min−1. Throughout the evaluation, stress-strain diagrams were persistently captured. Each ceramic exhibited a rectangular solid with dimensions of 5 × 5 × 9 mm. Each group consisted of at least three parallel samples. The detailed process could be found in our previous work [46].

The ceramics were immersed in Tris-HCl buffer solution (0.1 mol L−1, pH = 7.4 ± 0.2) at the ratio of 100 mg/mL for 14 days to evaluate their abilities to release Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions. The method was executed at 37 °C within a vibratory water bath. At each of the time-points, 1 mL of the submersed mixture was removed to measure the Ca2+ concentration via a Calcium Colorimetric Assay Kit (S1063S, Beyotime, China), with an equivalent volume of fresh Tris-HCl buffer solution added for replenishment. Then, solution was measured Mg2+ concentration released from WH ceramic using an Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, OPTIMA8000, PerkinElmer, USA). The detailed process could be found in our previous work [47].

Zeta potential of all the ceramics was measured using electrokinetic analyzer (SurpassTM 3, Anton-Paar, Austria) which can be used to measure zeta potential of the ceramic materials. Measurements were performed using a cylindrical cell. For each ceramic, samples (dimensions of 1.5 × 1.5 × 2 mm) were transferred into the cylindrical compartment and mounted between support disks and filters on both sides of the granular sample plug, and a KCl solution (1 mmol L−1) was used as the electrolyte. The electrolyte pressure was varied between 200 and 600 mBar. The detailed process could refer to in this work [48]. Each ceramic was measured in triplicate using independent samples, and all calculations were performed by the instrument software.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations

MD simulations of ions were performed to explore the interaction between the Ca2+, Mg2+ and HPO42− by using GROMACS 2019.6 software [49]. The number of HPO42−, PO43−, Ca2+, Mg2+ and H2O in the system is 14, 1, 15, 6, 11146, respectively. The general Amber force field was utilized for the parameter and topology generation of substance. The simulation box's dimensions were set generously, ensuring a distance larger than 1.0 nm between all atoms and the box's boundaries. The box was populated with an explicit solvent simple point charge model (SPC216 water molecules). These water molecules were later replaced with Na+ and Cl− counterions, ensuring electrical neutrality throughout the simulation system. Optimization of the entire system was carried out via the steepest descent method, reducing any unreasonable atomic overlaps or contacts. The simulation system was pre-equilibrated sufficiently via both the NVT and NPT ensembles, with each running for 100 ps at 453.15 K temperature and 3 bar pressure, respectively. Following these steps, a 100 ns MD simulation was conducted under periodic boundary conditions. Temperature and pressure were controlled at 453.15 K and 3 bar using the V-rescale and Parrinello-Rahman methods, respectively. The Newton equation of motion was calculated using leapfrog integration with a time step of 2 fs. Long-range electrostatic interactions calculations used the Particle Mesh-Ewald (PME) method with 0.16 nm Fourier spacing while the LINCS method ensured bond length constraints. The detailed process can refer to the previous work [50]. Visualization, trajectory analysis, and animation of the molecular dynamics were achieved with VMD (version 1.9.3) and PyMOL (version 2.4.1) software [51].

Cell spreading and proliferation

Mouse bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs, Cyagen Biosciences, Guangzhou, China) were seeded on the ceramics in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. BMSCs were cultured with α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM, Servicebio, China) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% antibiotics (penicillin-streptomycin, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After culturing for 1 and 7 days, fluorescein diacetate (FDA, Sigma, USA) and propidium iodide (PI, Sigma, USA) were used to stain the cells on ceramics. Then, the staining was visualized by a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM880, Zeiss, Germany). The proliferation of BMSCs was quantified by using the Cell Counting Kit 8 assay (CCK8, Dojindo, Japan) at the time points of days 1, 4, and 7. After culturing for 4 and 7 days, the cells on the ceramics were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Chron Chemicals, China), and were also stained with TRITC conjugated-phalloidin (1: 200, Sigma, USA) for cytoskeletal protein (F-actin) and DAPI (1: 1000, Sigma, USA) for cell nuclei, and then examined under a CLSM. Cell spreading area was analyzed using Zen (Zeiss, Germany) software based on the staining results, and cells from three individual samples were analyzed for each group. Specifically, for each independent biological sample, the spreading area was measured for 4 or 5 cells, and the resulting mean was used as the representative value for that sample. After culturing for 4 days, the cells seeded on ceramics were fixed to processing with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Chron Chemicals, China), followed by an overnight preservation at 4 °C. Subsequently, gradient ethanol was utilized for dehydration of these samples. The specimens were then subjected to a drying process inside a critical point drier (HCP-2, Hitachi, Japan), and then observed via SEM. The number of branches of a single cell were counted based on SEM images (branch referred to projections from the cell body with a length greater than 3 μm), and a total of 8 cells from three individual samples were analyzed for each group. The detailed process could be found in our previous work [52].

Biological performances

BMSCs were seeded on three ceramics at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. The culture medium was renewed on the first day, followed by replacements every alternate day. BCIP/NBT Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining kit (Beyotime, China) was utilized for ALP staining after 7 days. Concurrently, the method for measuring ALP activity was executed. Summarizing the procedure briefly, cells from each group underwent a wash with PBS, were broken down by the RIPA Lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and centrifuged (at 8000 rpm for 15 min) at 4 °C to collect supernatants. The assessment of ALP activity involved the use of an Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit (AS-72218, AnaSpec, USA), and the total protein content was gauged via the PierceTM BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The ALP activity was represented as a nanogram of ALP per milligram of total protein.

For the purpose of labeling osteogenic specific proteins, such as bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) and Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), BMSCs were seeded on ceramics. After culturing for 7 and 14 days, immunofluorescent staining was carried out. The samples were then treated with primary antibodies of BMP2 (Abcam, UK) and Runx2 (Abcam, UK) in a 4 °C environment overnight. After three rounds of PBS washing, the samples underwent treatment with an Alexa Flour 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (Proteintech, USA). A DAPI stain (Sigma, USA) of 5-min duration was used to stain the cell nuclei. Digital images were finally acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM880, Zeiss, Germany), maintaining consistent light excitation intensity and exposure duration. For each material, fluorescence intensity was measured in a representative region of three independent samples using ZEN (Zeiss, Germany) software.

To evaluate the effects of three ceramics on modulating HUVECs function, the extraction solution was prepared for wound scratch assay and tube formation assay. HAp, BCP and WH were incubated in the culture medium under 37 °C for 24 h, respectively. After that, the culture medium was centrifuged for 20 min at 1000g and the supernatant was collected as the extraction solution. For wound scratch assay, the HUVECs were seeded on the 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and grown to confluence. Next, the scratch wound was made with a 200 μL pipette tip and 1 mL of the extraction solution (2% FBS) was added to each well. After 6h, 12h and 24 h of incubation, the HUVECs were observed under optical microscope. The area of the initial wound was also measured and the wound-healing rate was determined based on the initial wound area. For tube formation assay, before seeding the cells, 100 μL of Matrigel was put into each well of a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Each well was seeded with 2.5 × 104 HUVECs and cultured with 300 μL extraction solution. After 4 h, tube formation in each well was examined under optical microscope. The number of meshes was analyzed by ImageJ.

Gene expression was evaluated with a quantitative reverse transcriptase-PCR. The specific sequences of primers can be referenced in Table S2, Table S3 and Table S4. BMSCs or HUVECs were seeded on three ceramics at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured for a duration of 4 and 7 days. TRIZOL (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) reagent was implemented to get total RNA as manufacturer guidelines. Conversion of total RNA to complementary DNA (cDNA) was carried out using an iScript cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-RAD, USA). Subsequently, a SoFast EvaGreen Supermix kit (Bio-RAD, USA) on a CFX96 real-time PCR detection platform (Bio-RAD, USA) was utilized to perform the quantitative PCR.

mRNA sequencing (RNA-seq) experimental method and analysis process

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIZOL according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA purity and quantification were evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Then the libraries were constructed using VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Premixed Version) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated. About 48M raw reads for each sample were generated. Raw reads of fastq format were firstly processed using fastp and the low quality reads were removed to obtain the clean reads. Then about 47M clean reads for each sample were retained for subsequent analyses. The clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT. FPKM of each gene was calculated and the read counts of each gene were obtained by HTSeq-count. PCA analysis were performed using R (v 3.2.0) to evaluate the biological duplication of samples. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2. Q value < 0.05 and foldchange >2 or foldchange <0.5 was set as the threshold for significantly differential expression gene (DEGs). Hierarchical cluster analysis of DEGs was performed using R (v 3.2.0) to demonstrate the expression pattern of genes in different groups and samples. The radar map of top 30 genes was drawn to show the expression of up-regulated or down-regulated DEGs using R packet ggradar. Based on the hypergeometric distribution, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs were performed to screen the significant enriched term using R (v 3.2.0), respectively. R (v 3.2.0) was used to draw the column diagram, the chord diagram and bubble diagram of the significant enrichment term. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software. The analysis was used a predefined gene set, and the genes were ranked according to the degree of differential expression in the two types of samples. Then it is tested whether the predefined gene set was enriched at the top or bottom of the ranking list.

In-vivo canine intramuscular implantation

The animal experiment was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Sichuan University (No. K2024001), and four beagles were supplied from Sichuan Province Medical Experimental Animal Center of China (8 - 10 kg, male, 12 months). Three kinds of ceramics in the form of disks (Φ 12 mm × 2 mm, diameter = 12 mm, high = 2 mm) were implanted on each side of canine latissimus dorsi muscle under general sterile conditions. After implanting for 12 weeks, the beagles were sacrificed with an overdose of pentobarbital sodium and saturated potassium chloride, and the samples with their surrounding tissue were retrieved for further analysis.

In-vivo bone regeneration of mandibular defects in SD rat model

A total of ten male Sprague Dawley rats (300 – 350g, male, 8 weeks) were randomly divided into two groups (BCP and WH). The animal experiment was also approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Sichuan University (NO. KS2024001). The rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (1 mg kg−1). After slitting the skin and muscle, a trephine drill was used to create a critical-sized defect (Φ 5 mm) with constant saline solution cooling. After that, the grafts (the form of disk, Φ 5 mm × 1.5 mm, diameter = 5 mm, high = 1.5 mm) were implanted into the defects, and the muscle and skin were sutured and cleansed using iodophor. The rats were sacrificed by injecting overdosed pentobarbital sodium at 45 days postoperatively. The mandibular specimens were retrieved and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 14 days for the subsequent analysis.

Micro-computed tomography images and analyses (Micro-CT)

With the purpose of examining new bone formation in ceramics after implantation, a Micro-CT (SCANCO Medical AG, Switzerland) was utilized, operating at a voltage of 70 kV and a current of 114 μA. The setup boasted a spatial resolution of 22 μm. Initially, a threshold range was established for all samples and applied to isolate the ceramic and new bone from the implant, following the methodology described in our previous work [[53], [54], [55]]. Subsequently, radiographic images were reconstructed and interpreted in accordance with established methodologies, ensuring that the results are directly comparable. Using Micro-CT reconstruction, the growth of new bone in ceramics after implantation can be visualized. Here, the contrast between the newly formed bone (displayed in white) and the ceramic material (displayed in blue) is easily discernible according to their differing threshold values. Evaluations of the percentage in the bone volume fraction (BV/TV) were conducted.

Histological analysis and related markers detection

The samples were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for two weeks. Decalcification of soft tissue slices utilized a 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic (Chron Chemicals, China) acid solution. Following dehydration, specimens were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and subjected to H&E staining for histological observation. Imaging was performed with a digital scanner (BA600, Motic China Group Co., Ltd.). Software analysis was carried out using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (IPP, Media Cybernetic, USA). The protein expressions of osteocalcin (OCN) and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) were also performed by immunofluorescent staining. The staining process involved the use of OCN (Affinity, USA), α-SMA (Abcam, UK), Goat Anti-Mouse IgG H&L (Abcam, UK) and Goat Anti-Rabbit Secondary Antibody AF647 Conjugated (SAB, China). Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma, USA). The staining was examined with a Pannoramic DESK (3DHISTECH, Hungary), and analysis of mean optical density (MOD) was quantitatively assessed through IPP 6.0 software.

Statistical analysis

The methodology delineated in this study mandated the establishment of a minimum of three concurrent samples for every experimental group. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was employed in the data processing with all groups, and two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test was employed in the data processing with all groups at different time points. For these operations, we employed GraphPad Prism 8.0 software, based in La Jolla, CA, USA. Statistically significant differences were indicated by a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

In-situ preparation of porous WH ceramic

To mimic the natural components of bone minerals, porous WH ceramic was synthesized in-situ from porous BCP ceramic under low-pH (pH < 3) conditions with an abundant supply of Mg2+ and PO43−. The addition of Mg2+ ions in the low pH environment during the synthesis process plays a crucial role in the transformation of WH.

SEM images (Fig. 2a) elucidate a substantial morphological transformation observed in BCP grains subsequent to the reaction, resulting in the adoption of apparent hexagonal crystal form resembling WH, with a noteworthy increase in grain size corroborated by quantitative analysis employing the Image J software (Fig. S5). Furthermore, EDS mapping images (Fig. 2b) visually demonstrate the present of Mg within WH. From TEM micrograph (Fig. 2c), WH presents a rhombohedral morphology. Furthermore, HRTEM image and SAED pattern (Fig. 2d) exhibit diffraction stripes with a spacing of 0.334 nm and 0.488 nm, corresponding to the characteristic crystal plane (122) and (110) of WH. Additionally, a detailed comparative examination was conducted contrasting the prepared WH ceramic with the raw BCP ceramic. The results of the XRD pattern for the WH ceramic coincides with major peaks at 2θ of 25.98°, 28.02°, 31.28°, 32.71°, and 34.64° that corresponds to the planes (1010), (214), (0210), (128), and (220) respectively (Fig. 2e). The XRD analysis of the WH ceramic demonstrates that the synthesized WH is notably pure, with no discernible heterogeneous phases. Regarding the BCP ceramics, compositional deconstruction elucidates that it comprises 33.3 wt% HAp and 66.7 wt% β-TCP. The distinctive peaks discerned in their XRD patterns align precisely with those observed in previously referenced XRD patterns for WH (JCPDS 70-2064), HAp (JCPDS 74-0566), and β-TCP (JCPDS 09-0169). In the present work, to confirm the purity of the synthesized samples, and to assess their structural parameters more closely, Rietveld refinement of WH was carried out, and results indicate that the prepared sample crystallizes in the R3c space group. The lattice parameters were determined to be a = 10.366 Å and c = 37.163 Å, with a corresponding unit cell volume of 3458.6 Å3. These findings are in good agreement with previously reported values for whitlockite, confirming the structural integrity of the synthesized phase.

By shortening the hydrothermal reaction time, we obtained a product named WHHAp, which is composed of WH and HAp. FTIR spectra (Fig. 2f) was further performed to substantiate the presence of crucial functional groups in the synthesized WH sample. WH ceramic has an additional peak at 920 cm−1, ascribed to the stretching vibration of the P-(OH) bond manifest in the HPO42− structure, a structure not present in either β-TCP or HAp [9,15]. The synthesized substance as WH were further identified by FTIR. Characteristic FTIR peaks of the CaP are also evident. These encompass the stretching mode of P-O observed at 987 cm−1, a PO43− vibrational range discernible between 1150 cm −1 and 970 cm −1, as well as a conspicuous double peak at 601 cm−1 and 553 cm−1, attributed to the bending of the PO43−. These observations, corresponding to the known properties of CaP, lend further credence to the proposed chemical constituents of the synthesized WH sample.

The surface elemental characteristics of BCP, WH, and WHHAp were subjected to further investigation utilizing XPS. As portrayed in XPS spectra (Fig. 2g–i), the broad-scale XPS spectra depict Ca 2s, Ca 2p, P 2s, P 2p, O 1s, and C 1s peaks, respectively. Both WH and WHHAp additionally exhibit pronounced Mg KLL auger peaks that could be indicative of the presence of Mg within their crystal structures. The effect of spin-orbit splitting is noticeable in phosphorus and its compounds, particularly in phosphates, wherein an asymmetrical peak envelope from overlapping components becomes discernible (Fig. 2j–l) [58]. The presence of HPO42− in WH causes a shift in the position of the P 2p1/2 and P 2p3/2, both exhibiting escalated peak intensities compared to BCP (Fig. S6). Through a detailed analysis of the XPS results, the ratios between cations and anions of WH and BCP, represented by Ca/P, were computed to be 1.086 and 1.306 respectively. Corresponding theoretical values were found to be 1.286 and 1.521, establishing a close proximity to the experimental analyses and further validating the obtained results.

Taken together, the integration of the characterization techniques above confirms the successful in-situ transformation of porous WH ceramic using porous BCP ceramic as the initial scaffold.

Characterization of porous WH ceramic

In an effort to delineate the in-vivo ionic environment of the three ceramics, respective ion release attributes were quantified within a Tris-HCl buffer medium (Fig. 2m). The release curve revealed that the concentration of Ca2+ released from BCP was roughly 2 times that released from WH during the initial stages (0.5 – 3d). With the extension of time, the released Ca2+ from WH showed an upward trend while that of the BCP gradually declined, with results bringing to light a release of Ca2+ from WH being 1.5 times that of BCP at day 14. HAp maintained a consistently low profile in Ca2+ release throughout the duration of this investigation. The Mg2+ release characteristics of WH were further scrutinized, and the result indicated a sustained albeit reduced release of Mg2+. The observed reductions in Mg2+ from WH and Ca2+ from BCP may stem from an initial burst release of material constituents followed by reprecipitation of ions onto the material surfaces, which in turn limits subsequent ion release.

The in vitro degradation rates of the scaffolds were evaluated by measuring weight loss in citric acid buffer, with the results presented in Fig. S7. During the initial stage, HAp and WH exhibited similar weight loss. As degradation progressed, all three ceramics showed continuous degradation; by day 7, the weight loss followed the order BCP > WH > HAp, which is consistent with the above ion release attributes.

To further investigate the pore characteristics, the pore size distribution analysis utilizing MIP device was conducted on macropores and micropores (Fig. S8) in the three ceramic scaffolds. It is worth noting that all the three groups possessed a similar macropores distribution of 200-800 μm, interconnected pores distribution of 10-100 μm and micropores distribution of 0.1-10 μm. However, the most probable pore size for HAp and WH measures at 45 μm, respectively, surpassing BCP. Phase transformation from BCP to WH

To investigate the mechanism of reaction, the composition of the product phases was explored at different hydrothermal reaction durations (1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 10 h, and 12 h). The corresponding XRD patterns are presented in Fig. 3a while a ternary diagram based on the phase component composition is displayed in Fig. 3b. SEM images reveal that as reaction time increases, the number of small grains decreases markedly, while the quantity and size of WH-characteristic hexagonal crystals increase substantially (Fig. S11). During the early stage of reaction (0-4 h), the system was constituted by ternary system. As the reaction progressed, β-TCP gradually decreased, while WH correspondingly increased. Approximately around 4 h, β-TCP was entirely consumed, thus transforming the system into one comprising the remaining HAp and the newly formed WH. Following, the reaction proceeded further (4-12 h), during which the residual HAp continued to transform into WH. Ultimately, at the completion of the reaction, the system was entirely constituted by WH. Hydrothermal reactions were conducted on BCP in the absence of Mg (MgCl2) or P (H3PO4), as shown in Fig. S12 that presents the XRD patterns of the reaction products. In the absence of Mg (MgCl2), the resulting hydrothermal product phase is composed of HAp and dicalcium phosphate (DCP, CaHPO4), as the presence of H3PO4 contribute to the low pH of the entire reaction environment. Conversely, in the absence of P (H3PO4), the hydrothermal product phase consists of HAp, which represents the most thermodynamically stable phase under neutral conditions in CaP. Hence, the presence of an appropriate amount of Mg (MgCl2) and P (H3PO4) plays a crucial role in the hydrothermal transformation of BCP into WH.

In order to get a better understanding of the early stages of reaction, a simulation concerning the formation of Mg-HPO4 in the solution was executed at the molecular level. This was facilitated by the use of Gromacs-2020.3 software, consequently elucidating the mechanism underlying WH formation. The simulation disclosed the binding of divalent cations, Ca2+ and Mg2+, with the HPO42− anion. The Radial Distribution Function (RDF) was utilized to investigate the impact of Ca2+ and Mg2+ binding with HPO42 at 100 ns. The RDF was utilized to investigate the impact of Ca2+ and Mg2+ binding with HPO42− at 100 ns. The data indicated in Fig. 3d imply that the probability of HPO42− distribution around Mg2+ exceeds that around Ca2+. The energy profile of the Mg-HPO4 system was observed to decrease from −4.3 × 103 kJ mol−1 to −1.6 × 104 kJ mol−1 as time increased from 0 to 100 ns (Fig. 3e). This reduction in energy signifies that the binding of anions and cations in the system is exothermic, which further suggests the system's evolution. Fig. 3f presents the typical distributions of ions at various simulation times. Initially, the ions were scattered irregularly but eventually converged under the influence of the Coulomb force, ultimately forming a pre-nucleation cluster. MD simulations insinuate a higher propensity for Mg2+ to bind competitively with HPO42− over Ca2+, essentially contributing to the construction of the A column of WH.

Discussion

The osteoinduction of biomaterials can be enhanced significantly through strategic optimization of material characteristics. These include factors such as phase composition, ionic environment, macro- and micro-pore structure, mechanical properties, and protein adsorption capacity [60,61]. Each of these material attributes, individually or in concert, has the potential to modulate the osteoinductive process directly or indirectly in a substantive manner. Based on the characterization results, it can be affirmed that we have successfully synthesized WH ceramic, which display a composition echoing the natural components of bone minerals. A rigorous analysis will be performed, giving particular emphasis to three representative ceramics: HAp, BCP, and WH, facilitating a comprehensive understanding of the correlation between the material's properties and its osteoinductive potential.

Currently, extensive research has been conducted surrounding the synthesis of WH, with significant emphasis placed on coprecipitation and hydrothermal methods [13,14,29,[62], [63], [64], [65]]. HAp (Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2), β-TCP (Ca3(PO4)2), and WH (Ca18Mg2(HPO4)2(PO4)12) share similar chemical compositions, and each possess unique structures. With respect to the prepared WH ceramic, XRD analysis demonstrates considerable purity of the WH compound [66]. Insight into the molecular formulae illustrates that WH possesses a distinctive HPO42− structure, as evidenced by the FTIR results. This specialized structure leads to an peak at 920 cm −1. However, the HPO42− demonstrates instability at elevated temperatures. To ascertain the thermal stability of WH, Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and Derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) were performed (Fig. S22), and the weight loss experienced a marked increase, peaking at around 820 °C, which is the combined effects of water molecule evaporation and the concurrent transformation of HPO42− into P2O74−, alongside the breakage of Mg-HPO4 bonds [15,67]. Further temperature elevation, notably beyond 900 °C, induced a sustained weight discarding process, culminating in the complete dehydration and consequent transformation of the WH ceramic structure into the MTCP configuration. Because of the instability of WH at high temperatures, it is impractical to fabricate porous WH ceramic with mechanical strength through high-temperature sintering (900-1100 °C). Furthermore, as a magnesium-bearing mineral naturally present in bone tissue, WH is expected to exhibit more pronounced biological effects than magnesium-doped hydroxyapatite and MTCP.

The pore structure of CaP ceramics plays a fundamental role in determining their osteoinduction [68]. These ceramics exhibit a porous architecture characterized by a diverse range of pore sizes, spanning from micropores to macropores, while maintaining an optimal balance of pore volume, porosity, and interconnectivity. In the domain of bone tissue engineering, the presence of a well-suited pore structure assumes a critical function by facilitating tissue ingrowth and promoting the diffusion of vital nutrients. A meticulously designed pore structure enables efficient infiltration of cells and fosters angiogenesis within the scaffold, thereby facilitating the efficient transportation of oxygen and essential nutrients necessary for viable cell proliferation and successful tissue regeneration. Consequently, it is imperative for WH ceramic, renowned for their exceptional osteoinduction, to possess a porous structure. In support of this, SEM image illustrates that WH ceramic effectively retain the macro-pores and interconnected pore structure inherited from BCP. From a macroscopic perspective, merging the insights from MIP and BET analysis reveals that, compared to BCP, WH demonstrates 1.8 times increase in SSA, a 10% rise in porosity, and an enhancement in total intrusion volume by 0.235 mL g−1. Intrusion volume serves as a critical metric for evaluating scaffold porosity, encompassing both macropore and micropore volumes. Compared to BCP, WH exhibits a similar micropore distribution but possesses significantly more macropores, providing a structural framework conducive to vascular ingrowth and subsequent osteogenesis [69]. This finding is further supported by the enhanced bone formation observed within the pores of the WH group in rat mandibular defect models (Fig. 7i).

The superior mechanical performance of WH scaffolds, despite high porosity, stems from three synergistic factors. First, they retain macroporous architecture and interconnected pore. Second, the unique crystal structure, incorporating Mg and HPO42−, provides high inherent stiffness. Third, favorable microstructural evolution—specifically significant grain growth and compact morphologies—reduces defect density and blunts crack initiation. With Fig. 2n illustrating compressive strength and Fig. S9 portraying compressive modulus, these results indicated that porous WH ceramic exhibited a compressive strength exceeding 0.5 MPa for HAp and 1 MPa for BCP, while the compressive modulus increased significantly by 1.8 times. Compressive testing therefore demonstrates that WH meets the mechanical requirements for bone defect–filling materials [[70], [71], [72], [73], [74]].

Prior studies have extensively described WH formation in powder systems (e.g., by precipitation, dissolution–precipitation, and hydrothermal methods), providing fundamental insights into WH nucleation, growth, and synthesis [13,25,43]. In the following sections, we will delve into a comprehensive elucidation surrounding the mechanism of this reaction process. The research not only establishes an innovative synthesis pathway for superior WH ceramics but also contributes to the broadening of knowledge in the domain of bioactive ceramic synthesis. Based on the variation observed in the product phase composition throughout the reaction, the reaction was divided into four stages, and a reaction mechanism was proposed.

One primary distinction among the three phases HAp, WH, and β-TCP lies in the presence of HPO42− in their structures. Hence, FTIR analysis utilizing P-OH bonds enables differentiation between them. The presence of HPO42− serves as a crucial parameter for analyzing the phase transformation during the hydrothermal process.

In the early stage (stage I), β-TCP in BCP dissolves significantly due to its higher solubility, releasing a significant amount of PO43− into solution. This PO43− should undergo protonation with the preexisting H3PO4 in the solution. Consequently, the resultant solution is enriched with a substantial concentration of H2PO4− and HPO42− ions. Furthermore, the dissolution of β-TCP leads to a notable release of Ca2+. Notably, the thermodynamic dissociation constants of MgHPO4 and CaHPO4 at a temperature of 25 °C have been determined as 0.0011 mol L−1 and 0.0020 mol L−1 [75], respectively, clarifying that Mg2+ exhibits a higher affinity towards HPO42− in comparison to Ca2+. Previous study also observed that this preferential binding of Mg2+ to HPO42− plays a crucial role in facilitating the formation of WH's crystal lattice A-column structure while concurrently impeding the nucleation of CaP compounds:(1)Mg2++ HPO42− → MgHPO4

Our study provides further insights into the preferential binding of Mg2+ and HPO42− in the reaction environment through MD simulations. For further details and visualization, please refer to Video S1. The RDF reveals that, in comparison to Ca2+, Mg2+ resides in closer proximity to HPO42−, or in areas maintaining equivalent distances, the presence of Mg2+ is more pronounced. Energy of Ca-HPO4 system is notably lower than that of Mg-HPO4 system due to the higher Ca2+ concentration. However, as the time increased, energy of the Ca-HPO4 system exhibited erratic fluctuations, each of which corresponds to a decrease in energy of Mg-HPO4 system. These fluctuations of system energy coincide with the snapshots of stabilizing ions at different times in the solution system. At 0 ns, all ions in the environment were distributed arbitrarily. Under the influence of Coulomb force, by 1 ns the environment witnessed the agglomeration of most anions and cations to form two ion-cluster structures. However, there were still small quantities of unbound Mg2+ and Ca2+. At 10 ns, unbound Mg2+ binds to one of the ion clusters, leaving only unbound Ca2+. By 17 ns, these two ion clusters converge into one pre-nucleation cluster. As time progresses, Mg2+ invades deeper into the pre-nucleation cluster, thereby displacing Ca2+ and binding with HPO42−. MD simulations corroborate that the preferential binding of Mg2+ to HPO42− serves as a fundamental prerequisite for the formation of Column A of WH, distinguishing its structure from β-TCP and ensuring phase transformation by stabilizing acidic phases to hinder calcium phosphate (CaP) nucleation.

As temperature increases, reaction proceeded to stage II, where the chemical environment becomes more active, resulting in β-TCP continuously releasing Ca2+ and PO43−. When β-TCP is nearly consumed, reaction proceeded enters stage III. The solubility of HAp is much lower than that of β-TCP. Initially, HAp releases a small amount of Ca2+ and PO43−, but as temperature increases, the chemical environment becomes more active, further disrupting the stability of HAp. Consequently, a large amount of Ca2+ and PO43− is released as raw materials for WH growth. As HAp is depleted, the reaction proceeded to stage IV, where WH and various ions, such as Ca2+, PO43−, and Mg2+ in the solution, reach a state of dissolution and deposition equilibrium. Furthermore, during our experimental process, we attempted to use pure HAp as the starting material but were unable to obtain phase-pure WH under the same hydrothermal conditions. Based on the above analysis, it is speculated that because HAp is comparatively thermodynamically and chemically stable (low solubility), it was unable to drive this phase transformation reaction while maintaining the fundamental structure of the material. Consequently, BCP was selected as the precursor. Furthermore, an optimal balance of β-TCP within the BCP is critical: insufficient β-TCP limits the availability of the soluble precursor necessary for WH formation, whereas an excessive β-TCP fraction may disrupt reaction kinetics and lead to incomplete or heterogeneous conversion.

The substantial enhancement of cytocompatibility was significantly discernible in the presence of the WH. It was evident that WH offered marked advantages in cell proliferation, adhesion, and spreading over the other two ceramics. More importantly, relative to HAp and BCP, WH prominently promoted the osteogenic differentiation. More importantly, relative to HAp and BCP, WH prominently promoted the osteogenic differentiation. In the early phase, the concentration of Ca2+ released from WH was lower than that from BCP; however, after a 7-day period, WH exceeded BCP in Ca2+ release, possibly due to the principal supply of Ca2+ shifting to HAp. The long-term observation stretching up to 14 days revealed the notably lower Ca2+ release from HAp compared to BCP and WH. Hence, BCP and WH outperform HAp due to its provision of Ca2+ and PO43−, which play requisite roles in calcium nodule formation. Between WH and BCP, both exhibiting similar ion release, the salient question arises as to why the former showcases superior performance in enhancing osteogenesis?

WH demonstrates a significantly more negative surface potential than HAp and BCP, resulting in a higher density of surface hydroxyl groups attributable to its intrinsic composition and crystal structure [59]. This phenomenon of having a more negative surface potential facilitates enhanced protein adsorption to occur under similar conditions. BET analysis ranks the SSA as follows: HAp > WH > BCP. A larger SSA indicates that the material possesses a greater surface area for protein adsorption. In considering these two crucial factors together, WH demonstrates a superior ability for protein adsorption. Furthermore, cell adhesion is a fundamental prerequisite for anchorage-dependent cells to survive on the material. This precedes several cell activities such as proliferation, spreading, and differentiation. More negative surface potential enhances initial cell adhesion [76], and a larger SSA provides more adhesion sites for cells, which triggers a series of subsequent cell activities, ultimately promoting differentiation.

The layer of protein adsorbed may provide a provisional matrix to cell differentiation through the integrin-related signaling pathway. After culturing with BMSCs or implantation into the canine dorsal muscle, WH exhibits more efficient protein recruitment. Those proteins are more easily recognized by osteogenesis-related proteins and cells, culminating in higher expressions of BMP2, Runx2 and OCN in WH than those in HAp and BCP. It is commonly acknowledged that BMP2 can promote the differentiation of MSCs into osteoblasts [77], and OCN is one of important markers of osteoblastic differentiation. A preceding experiment established the osteoinductive capacity of BMP2 to follow a dose-dependent trajectory, and a higher amount of BMP2 led to more bone formation. Further research specifies the sequence of osteogenic potential to follow the order of WH > BCP > HAp.

Apart from Ca2+ and PO43−, WH also provides a sustainable release of Mg2+, a multifunctional therapeutic ion beneficial for promoting cell migration, proliferation and angiogenesis [20,78,79]. In particular, the early release of Mg2+ from the material can stimulate angiogenesis and neurite outgrowth, thereby reestablish the neurovascular network and create a pro-osteogenic niche that enhances neurovascular remodeling and bone repair [80,81]. Mg-containing WH, a crucial inorganic component present in natural bone tissue, endowed the WH ceramic with increased activity than BCP and HAp in biological events related to neurogenesis, angiogenesis, and osteogenesis [82]. Our results further confirm the angiogenic properties of WH, demonstrating that WH significantly upregulates the Ang1 gene expression compared to HAp and BCP, which is consistent with findings from other studies [83,84]. Ang1 promotes endothelial cell survival, vascular stabilization, and maturation, all of which are essential for the formation of functional blood vessels and the process of vascular remodeling [85]. Interestingly, Mg2+ release is a distinguishing feature of WH that locally augments new bone formation in the regeneration process, exhibiting superior bone regenerative outcome than other CaP in bone defect models.

RNA-Seq analysis revealed that signaling pathways associated with osteogenesis, including Wnt/β-catenin and TGF-β, as well as pathways related to autophagy, such as AMPK and FoxO, were significantly upregulated. Previous studies have demonstrated that autophagy plays a crucial role in promoting osteogenic differentiation [[86], [87], [88]]. Given the close association between autophagy and energy metabolism, regulating the osteogenic microenvironment through metabolic modulation represents an ideal approach [89]. Compared with BCP, WH provides a physiologically relevant concentration of Mg2+ within the microenvironment, which significantly enhances osteogenic induction by activating autophagy [90,91]. As an essential cofactor for ATP, Mg2+ plays a pivotal role in regulating glucose metabolism and reducing lipid accumulation [92,93]. Enzymes involved in glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, and the respiratory chain are highly dependent on magnesium as either an allosteric modulator or a cofactor [94], ultimately inducing a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis [91]. Compared to oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis generates ATP at a faster rate but with lower efficiency [95], resulting in a significant decrease in intracellular ATP levels.

The classical role of AMPK is as a cellular energy sensor that is activated in response to declining energy status, signaled by an increased AMP/ATP and ADP/ATP ratio. RNA-Seq and qRT-PCR analyses demonstrated that in BMSCs cultured on WH ceramic, the expression levels of key AMPK-associated genes, including Cab39l, Prkab2, and Prkag2, were significantly upregulated. Under conditions of ATP depletion, CAB39L interacts with the LKB1-STRAD complex, promoting LKB1 activation [96], which in turn triggers the activation of AMPK [97]. Notably, FoxO1 and FoxO3 were highly expressed upon AMPK activation [[98], [99], [100]], and it is well established that the AMPK/FoxO axis is directly involved in regulating autophagy [101]. The downstream target genes of FoxO, including Bnip3 and Atg12, well-characterized markers of autophagy induction, were significantly upregulated, providing direct signals for autophagic activation [[102], [103], [104]]. In addition, LAMP-2, a key regulator essential for the maturation of autophagosomes and phagosomes, also exhibited significantly elevated expression levels [105,106].

Autophagy is an intracellular self-degradative and recycling mechanism that plays a fundamental role in the differentiation and function of bone metabolism-related cells, including MSCs, osteoblasts, osteocytes, and osteoclasts [107]. Previous research indicates that enhanced autophagy in BMSCs facilitates cellular self-renewal and differentiation, thereby promoting osteogenic activity through the modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin and TGF-β signaling pathways [[108], [109], [110], [111]]. Autophagy is intrinsically linked to these pathways, activating the Wnt signaling cascade by degrading inhibitory proteins such as YAP and bolstering bone regeneration by stimulating the secretion of ligands like TGF-β1. GSEA analysis further confirmed these findings. Through the Mg2+-mediated regulation of ATP levels, the AMPK-FoxO axis is activated [112], leading to the induction of autophagy and thereby promoting BMSC osteogenic differentiation. Notably, magnesium-coated biomaterials have demonstrated similar biological effects, promoting osteogenic differentiation and enhancing osseointegration [113,114]. Nevertheless, further studies incorporating a Mg2+ chelator, an equivalent dose of soluble Mg2+, inhibitor or knockdown experiments are necessary to elucidate the mechanisms by which WH-derived Mg2+ activates the AMPK-FoxO signaling axis, leading to enhanced osteogenesis through autophagy.

Recent studies indicate that WH exhibits intrinsic anti-inflammatory activity: nanoparticulate WH was reported to markedly reduce production of pro-inflammatory mediators such as NO, IL-6 and TNF-α, even at low doses, and this effect may be partly attributable to sustained release of PO43− and Mg2+ under acidic microenvironments [115]. Moreover, the sustained release of Mg2+ from WH has been shown to promote cell migration, proliferation and angiogenesis and to enhance osteogenic responses compared with other calcium phosphates, suggesting a possible coupling between the material's ionic release profile and a pro-regenerative immune microenvironment. Together, these findings imply that WH ceramics may modulate host inflammatory responses toward tissue repair; accordingly, we recommend that future studies include targeted immunological assessments (e.g., macrophage polarization markers CD86/CD206 and cytokine profiling, as well as in vivo inflammatory cell characterization) to validate the immunomodulatory role of porous WH scaffolds.

Emerging research highlights the intrinsic anti-resorptive activity of WH; specifically, nanoparticulate WH has been shown to markedly inhibit osteoclast differentiation and fusion by downregulating key markers such as TRAP, NFATc1, and Cathepsin K, even during the early stages of bone healing. This inhibitory effect is largely driven by the sustained release of Mg2+ and PO43− ions, which stabilize the local microenvironment and interfere with RANKL-induced signaling pathways [12]. Furthermore, the combination of WH with agentslike alendronate shows a synergistic effect in suppressing osteoclast activity in osteoporotic models [116]. Compared with HAp, WH-based coatings or scaffolds have been shown to effectively reduce early-stage osteoclast recruitment, thereby preventing premature material degradation and maintaining structural integrity for subsequent bone ingrowth [117]. Together, these findings imply that WH ceramics can effectively modulate the bone remodeling balance toward a pro-regenerative state; accordingly, we recommend that future studies include targeted assessments of the osteoclastogenic niche (e.g., RANKL/RANK/OPG signaling pathways and dynamic resorption-remodeling coupling) to fully validate the therapeutic role of porous WH scaffolds in high-turnover bone defects.

Previous studies have demonstrated that osteoinductive CaP ceramics hold equivalent potential to autologous bone and bioactive factors in repairing critical-sized bone defects in various locations. However, it remains crucial to enhance the osteoinduction of bone repair materials for addressing larger and more complex defect to meet the requirements of regenerative medicine [118]. We proffered a systematic evaluation of the osteoinduction via their implantation and longitudinal monitoring over a period of 12 weeks. The assessments of BV/TV ratio and H&E staining revealed that the WH ceramic induced the formation of more bone tissue, primarily within the ceramic pores, the spaces created by ceramic degradation, and the environment surrounding the implanted material. Similar study has also observed that materials with superior osteoinduction promote the growth of new bone tissue that adheres closely to the pore walls and covers a more extensive area [119]. Concurrently, IFC indicated that the WH ceramic fostered an enhanced secretion of OCN, adding credence to the suggestion that these materials stimulate cells into releasing bone inducing signaling molecules. In summary, WH ceramic exhibits more superior osteoinduction compared with HAp and BCP. Both in terms of accelerating the timeframe for osteogenic.

Furthermore, we conducted a study on the repair of mandibular critical-sized defect models to assess the bone reparative performance of WH ceramic. From Fig. 1b, it is apparent that the mandible is a site rich in WH. Consequently, we suggest that WH porous ceramic may exert an enhanced repair on mandibular defects. 3D reconstructions and HE staining visibly demonstrated that the WH group had more new bone formation at the defect site compared to BCP. WH represents an inorganic phase of bone, unlike β-TCP, which is present in BCP. Biologically, materials that closely mimicking the phase of bone might confer advantages in binding with host bone, promoting osseointegration, and facilitating mineralization. This might lead to a better realization of bone regeneration in the defect area. Moreover, in comparison to BCP, WH demonstrated the potential to enhance the formation of blood vessels, as observed through the high expressions of angiogenic proteins, specifically α-SMA. WH, being a Mg-rich material, is acknowledged to facilitate vascular regeneration as Mg2+ ions are released [120]. The superior vascularization capability constitutes a decisive factor in this process, facilitating the supply of oxygen, nutrients, soluble factors to various cells, and the clearance of metabolic waste and carbon dioxide. Additionally, the more negative surface potential of WH is advantageous in recruiting more osteoinductive signaling molecules (OCN), thereby activating the expression of cell cascade osteogenic genes. In summary, the biomimetic WH, after implantation, releases Mg2+ which promotes vascular regeneration in the defect region providing a favorable osteogenic microenvironment [81,[121], [122], [123]]. Subsequently, the material aspect of WH promotes new bone regeneration in the defect area from various perspectives, effectively integrating with the host bone, to ultimately realize the repair of mandibular defects.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this work highlights a novel approach to preparing porous WH ceramic with enhanced osteoinduction. The in-situ preparation of porous WH ceramic using porous BCP ceramic under low pH conditions results in a material that retains the structural properties of BCP while acquiring the unique characteristics of WH. The characterization results confirm the successful transformation of BCP ceramic into WH ceramic and demonstrate the excellent pore structure, SSA, and mechanical strength of the resulting porous WH ceramic. Moreover, the biological performance evaluation reveals the superior cell proliferation, adhesion, spreading, and osteogenic potential of porous WH ceramic, and we reveal that Mg2+-mediated regulation of ATP levels activates the AMPK-FoxO signaling axis, leading to enhanced osteogenesis through autophagy. In addition, the intramuscular implantation evaluation and rat mandibular defect implantation reveal the superior biological properties and osteogenic potential of porous WH ceramic, indicating its potential as bone defect repair materials. WH possesses unique piezoelectric properties that HAp and BCP lack [15]. Future research could focus on enhancing its osteogenic potential by integrating these advanced piezoelectric characteristics with pro-angiogenic and neurogenic capabilities. Altogether, this study introduces a novel porous calcium phosphate ceramic material simulating inorganic phase of bone, and provides new evidence elucidating the relationship between osteogenesis and autophagy in bone repair materials, offering valuable insights for the design and development of advanced bone repair scaffolds.
