Lesion Volume as a Predictor for Return to Work After Endovascular Treatment: A 4‐Year Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Gisle Berg Helland, Mona Kristiansen Beyer, Brian Anthony B. Enriquez, Hege Ihle‐Hansen, Håkon Ihle‐Hansen, Stein Andersson, Esten Høyland Leonardsen, Helle Stangeland, Bettina Ùjhelyi, Guri Hagberg, Hanne Flinstad Harbo, Anne Hege Aamodt, Einar August Høgestøl
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere are no known objective biomarkers for predicting return to work (RTW) in ischemic stroke survivors. This study aims to explore the predictive utility and defines a cutoff value of lesion volume on RTW after endovascular treatment.MethodsWe included patients aged <65 years undergoing endovascular treatment at Oslo University Hospital between January 2017 and May 2019. Employment status was obtained at both baseline and a 4‐year follow‐up. Stroke lesion volumes were segmented using magnetic resonance imaging scans 24 hours after endovascular treatment. Logistic regression models were conducted to assess the impact of lesion volume on RTW status at follow‐up, adjusted for patients’ characteristics, stroke‐related factors, and treatment. We calculated the receiver operating characteristic curve to determine the optimal lesion volume cutoff. Machine learning regression models were used to assess the predictive abilities of baseline clinical and imaging variables for RTW.ResultsOf the 109 individuals treated, 81 (74%) were employed at baseline. Among these, 60 completed 4‐year follow‐up with magnetic resonance imaging available for stroke lesion segmentation and were included in the analyses. Mean age at stroke onset was 51.8 years (range, 23.5–64.9 years), and 50% were female. Median lesion volume was 18 mL (interquartile ranges, 45—47 mL). After 4 years, 34 (57%) had successfully RTW. The odds for not RTW increased by 5% for every 1 mL increase in lesion volume (adjusted odds ratio, 1.05 [95% CI, 1.02–1.11]; P = 0.02). A lesion volume cutoff value of 29 cm3 yielded a sensitivity of 0.91 and specificity of 0.65 for predicting RTW. Notably, the most influential feature in the machine learning model for predicting RTW was lesion volume.ConclusionLesion volume was the most robust predictor of RTW 4 years after endovascular treatment. Our findings suggest that a cutoff of 29 cm3 is suitable to distinguish between those with high and low chance of RTW.

Return to work (RTW) is associated with enhanced well‐being and improved quality of life for stroke survivors. 1 Stroke imposes a substantial burden on society, and work‐related issues account for a considerable share of those costs. 2 , 3 In heterogeneous stroke populations, the reported rates of RTW in working‐age adults vary, ranging from 7% to 81%. 4 , 5 Studies focusing on patients receiving endovascular treatment (EVT) report that ≈1 of 3 achieve RTW within the first year after discharge. 6 , 7

In this study, RTW was defined as return to any paid work or enrollment as a student. Several factors have been identified as determinants for successful RTW, with male sex and younger age being the most consistent protective factors. 8 , 9 Time since the acute incident is also important, as there is a growing number of patients achieving RTW in the years following a stroke. 8 Other potential prognostic factors for RTW in patients undergoing EVT include the addition of intravenous thrombolysis, successful recanalization (Thrombolysis in Cerebral Ischemia 2b–3), medical complications, and length of hospital stay. 7

The modified Rankin Scale and the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) are key measures of functional poststroke outcomes and stroke severity, and studies indicate that lower modified Rankin Scale and NIHSS scores during the index stay increases the likelihood of RTW. 6 , 8 , 10 None of the factors listed above, individually, are sufficient predictors of RTW. 11 , 12 In recent years, lesion volume has emerged as a potential biomarker for functional and cognitive outcome, 13 , 14 , 15 but its capacity to predict RTW has not been investigated.

We aimed to explore the predictive value of lesion volume for RTW in patients undergoing EVT and establish a lesion volume cutoff to guide prognostic expectations. Secondary, we aimed to assess the predictive capabilities and influence of clinical and imaging variables on employment status 4 years post‐EVT, using an interpretable machine learning (ML) model.

Methods

Study Sample and Design

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. To facilitate replicability, the pretrained machine learning model is made publicly available at https://github.com/gislebh/Return_to_Work_after_Stroke. This study uses data from the OSCAR (Oslo Acute Reperfusion Stroke Study; study identifier NCT06220981), a prospective cohort study of consecutive patients with acute ischemic stroke treated with EVT at Oslo University Hospital. The Oslo University Hospital is a comprehensive stroke center receiving EVT candidates from local stroke units. In this substudy, all patients aged ≤65 years who underwent EVT between January 1, 2017, and May 9, 2019, were included. Follow‐up was performed from September 2021 to June 2022. In Norway, the restrictions on working after the age of 70 years made 65 years an appropriate age limit to our evaluation. Baseline data were collected from the OSCAR and medical records. We adhered to the Strenghtening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines when reporting our findings. 16 The first author had access to all data and assumes responsibility for the integrity of the data and accuracy of the data analysis.

Imaging Acquisition and Imaging Variables

Magnetic resonance imaging scans were acquired 24 hours post‐EVT, at 4 different Siemens scanners at Oslo University Hospital (Table S1). Lesions were identified as areas with diffusion restriction on diffusion‐weighted imaging, indicative of irreversible brain infarction. 17 In 2 patients without 24‐hour imaging, initial admission diffusion‐weighted imaging data were used. Lesion segmentation was manually executed using the ``Insight Segmentation and Registration Toolkit‐Snap’’ (IKT‐Snap) software (v. 4.0.0) (Figure 1). 18 Segmentations were performed by trained researcher G.B.H. and overseen by experienced neuroradiologist M.K.B., who was blinded to the outcome and clinical data. To enhance the interpretability further, we performed a univariable logistic regression analysis, categorizing lesion volume. Lesion volumes were classified into small, medium, and large (<15, 15–70, and >70 cm3), based on previously published cutoffs from the Efficacy and Safety of Nerinitide for the Treatment of Acute Ischaemic Stroke (ESCAPE‐NA1) trial, which looked at stroke lesion volume and functional outcome (modified Rankin Scale). 14

Examples of diffusion‐weighted images together with corresponding lesion segmentation and categorization. A, Left middle cerebral artery infarct, small lesion (volume 6.1 cm3). B, Posterior cerebral artery infarct, medium lesion (volume 22.0 cm3). C, Right middle cerebral artery infarct, large lesion (volume 94.7 cm3).

Lesion location is grouped to left or right anterior or posterior circulation, as described on the diagnostic computed tomography angiography acquired at admission. Reperfusion grade was assessed using the revised modified Treatment in Cerebral Ischemia (mTICI) scoring system by the interventional neuroradiologist during the final Digital Subtraction Angiography procedure. The mTICI ranges from 0 to 3, with 0 to 2A indicating reperfusion of less than one‐third of the affected vascular territory. 2B indicates reperfusion of more than one‐third, 2C near‐complete, and 3 represents complete recanalization. A score of 2B or higher is usually considered a “successful” procedural outcome, and 2C or better is considered “excellent.” 19 , 20 Missing reperfusion scores were retrospectively completed by an interventional neuroradiologist blinded for clinical data, including outcomes. For analysis, mTICI scores were dichotomized, with 3 defined as an “excellent” procedural outcome. 21

Follow‐Up

All patients included at baseline were invited to a 4‐year follow‐up examination. Those unable to attend in person were interviewed by telephone to collect basic demographic and clinical information. The patients were provided 2 self‐report questionnaires related to their mental health and quality of life, including the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale and the EuroQol‐5Dimensions‐5Levels. 22 , 23 Fatigue was assessed using a 9‐point fatigue severity scale. 24 Information on adherence to work was obtained, and “RTW” was defined as part‐time or full‐time paid work, or being enlisted as a student. Participants, who stated that they retired early because of stroke impairments, were considered as not returned to work (NRTW). Statistical comparisons between those who RTW, did NRTW, and “retired” can be found in Table S2. Information concerning education level was collected retrospectively at follow‐up. For the analyses, ``primary school’’ and ``high school’’ were categorized into ``high school’’ and ``college’’ and ``university’’ into ``higher education.’’ NIHSS scores were categorized as minor (0–4), moderate (5–15), and severe (16–42), in line with international guidelines. 25 Categorization of admission NIHSS scores was not performed because of the minimal number of patients falling into the reference category.

Statistical Analysis

Traditional statistical analyses were performed using the R (v. 4.3.2) statistical software. 26 Baseline characteristics are presented as number (percentage), medians and interquartile ranges, means and SDs, as well as ranges, as appropriate. We conducted 2‐sided t‐tests to analyze continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, whereas the Mann‐Whitney U test was used for data with nonnormal distribution. For categorical variables, χ2 test and Fischer exact test were used based on observed frequencies in the contingency tables.

To explore the association between lesion volume and RTW, we performed a logistic regression analysis with lesion volume as a continuous variable. Results were reported for both univariable and multivariable models, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. Variables selected for the univariable logistic regression analysis were based on clinical expertise and insights from previous studies. Variables included sex, age, education, affected vascular territory, NIHSS score on admission and at discharge, mTICI score, and whether intravenous thrombolysis was administered or not. 8 In the multivariable logistic regression model, sex, age, and education were predefined variables. Other variables were incorporated if they had a P value <0.2 in univariable analyses and variation inflation factor among variables was <5. 27 An interaction analysis between NIHSS score and lesion volume was performed. To further validate our findings, we conducted the aforementioned logistic regressions, normalizing lesion volume to the estimated total intracranial volume, as determined by FreeSurfer, version 7.3.2. 28 T1 weighted images taken 24 hours post‐EVT were used for this analysis.

To establish a lesion volume cut‐off, we constructed a receiver operating characteristic curve, which illustrates the performance of a binary classifier by varying its discrimination treshold to highlight the trade‐off between true positives and false positives. We then determined the lesion volume that optimally balanced sensitivity and specificity using Youden's index.

ML Model

To explore the predictive properties of a larger set of baseline data using a data‐driven approach, an ML model was developed using python with building blocks from scikit‐learn. 29 A data set was assembled using domain expertise, comprising 28 baseline features (Table S3). While inspecting the data set, we identified some missing values (35/1740 = 2%), which were imputed with the mean of their respective features.

To establish the model, we performed a 10‐fold cross‐validation stratified for outcome and sex to increase the robustness of the results. Evaluation metrics included receiver operating characteristic and area under the curve, sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. We used a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regularization to perform automatic feature selection. LASSO penalizes coefficients, shrinking less important ones to zero, thus performing both regularization and variable selection. 30 Furthermore, we conducted a permutation test to assess the statistical significance of the predictive performance of our ML model compared with chance levels. In this test, we repeatedly shuffled the employment outcome variable while keeping the predictors intact. This process was repeated 100 times to establish a distribution of baseline performances of models fitted to noise, against which the performance of the true model was compared. 31 Statistical significance was assessed by evaluating whether the mean area under the curve achieved by the true model was in the 95th percentile of the areas under the curve in the baseline distribution, equivalent to a significance threshold of 0.05. The ML modeling method and reporting of results in this study align with published recommendations. 32

Ethical Approval

The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the regional ethics committee (Regional Committee for Medical Ethics Southeast Norway, REC ID: 152864). Informed consent was obtained from all eligible study participants, and for those unable to provide consent, their next‐of‐kin were approached to seek consent on their behalf.

Results

A total of 416 patients underwent EVT, of whom 133 were aged <65 years at stroke onset. Among those employed at baseline, 60 provided consent and were followed up after 4 years, as seen in the flowchart in Figure 2.

Flowchart of patient selection with an illustration of enrollment, inclusion, exclusion, and loss to follow up. DWI indicates diffusion‐weighted image; and OSCAR, Oslo Acute Reperfusion Stroke Study.

Mean age at baseline was 52 years (SD, 9 years; range, 23.5–64.9 years); 50% were female. Median NIHSS score on admission was 12 (interquartile range, 19‐7) and decreased to 3 (interquartile range, 7‐1) on discharge. The median lesion volume within the first 24 hours was 18 mL (interquartile range, 45—47 mL). The patient who RTW and had the largest lesion volume showed a volume of 79 cm3, whereas the patient who NRTW with the smallest lesion volume had a volume of 6.1 cm3.

Recanalization with an excellent procedural outcome was achieved in 21 (35%) of the participants. At the 4‐year follow‐up, 34 (57%) participants had RTW and 26 (43%) were NRTW. Higher education (P = 0.03) and posterior infarcts (P = 0.05) were found as protective factors against NRTW.

After adjusting the model for age, sex, stroke territory, education, intravenous thrombolysis, mTICI, and NIHSS score at discharge, lesion volume remained significant (OR, 1.05 [95% CI, 1.02–1.11]; P = 0.02). No significant interaction was observed between NIHSS score at discharge and lesion volume (P≈0.9). Furthermore, adjusting for intracranial volume did not alter the results for lesion volume. Establishing a Prognostic Cutoff Value for Lesion Volume

In the univariable model, lesion volume alone showed an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.86. The optimal cutoff for lesion volume was 29 cm3, yielding a sensitivity of 0.91 and a specificity of 0.65 for RTW. The positive predictive value for RTW was 78%, and the negative predictive value was 85%.

ML Model

The logistic regression ML model, as illustrated in Figure 4, yielded a mean area under the curve of 0.86 (SD, 0.13), demonstrating an accuracy of 75%. The model achieved a sensitivity of 0.77 and specificity of 0.72 for identifying subjects who RTW and NRTW, respectively. The permutation test saw none of its 100 iterations yield areas under the curve above 0.86, instead resulting in a mean area under the curve of 0.53 (SD, 0.10). This substantial difference in areas under the curve highly suggests that the model's predictive ability significantly outperforms random models.

This figure provides a representation of the 10‐fold cross‐validation process used to validate the predictive performance of the machine learning model on not returning to work. It delineates the division into 10 unique folds, each consisting of 6 subjects serving as the test set in one iteration of the validation process. The performance of the model is quantified through area under the curve, calculated as mean of folds.

Among the features considered in the model, lesion volume (mean OR = 4.98) measured as a continuous variable, emerged as the most influential factor in predicting the outcome. For a comprehensive overview, we have presented the top 8 most influential factors in Table S6.

Discussion

In this study of working‐age adults undergoing EVT for acute ischemic stroke, lesion volume emerged as the most robust biomarker for predicting RTW. Our findings suggest that a cutoff of 29 cm3 is suitable to distinguish between those with high and low chance of RTW. To our knowledge, the importance of lesion volume and the use of artificial intelligence to assess clinical variables on RTW have not been previously explored.

The relationship between lesion volume and RTW underscores the critical impact of stroke severity on vocational outcomes and highlights its role as a promising biomarker of RTW. Furthermore, our analysis of ML modeling demonstrated the potential to anticipate RTW outcomes based on lesion volume and other baseline variables, although this warrants further validation. The proportion of patients in this study who RTW is consistent with findings from other stroke studies. 6 , 7 , 8 , 10 , 33 , 34 Education emerged as a protective factor for RTW. However, its significance diminished when considered alongside other variables. Contrary to previous studies, we identified an association between lesion location and RTW, 35 , 36 although significance was not retained in the multivariable model. Nonetheless, a more precise exploration of localization could be valuable, as previously demonstrated by the concept of strategic locations. 37 Further individualization of predictions, combining lesion size, localization, and hemispheric dominance, warrants future studies, especially because recent evidence indicates a significant difference in RTW outcomes based on whether the dominant or nondominant hemisphere is affected. 38

Previous research has uncovered the imprecise predictions of clinicians and randomness in the selection of individuals for rehabilitation. 39 , 40 , 41 ML models offer a potential pathway toward a more objective, equitable, and personalized stroke follow‐up approach, hence reducing the undue influence of individual clinician biases. As a contribution to the growing field of stroke prediction models, we have made our pretrained model available for download online. An obstacle to using our study's result in clinical practice is the absence of routine measurement of lesion volume. Looking ahead, the integration of artificial intelligence–powered tools for image analysis holds promise for enhancing lesion volume assessments, also when it comes to regular clinical scans, such as those used in our study. 42

Strengths of our study were the well‐described cohort with extensive clinical and radiological measures both at baseline and 4‐year follow‐up. The main limitation of this study is the relatively modest sample size. Although the inclusion of diverse patients enhances generalizability, particularly to young stroke patients undergoing EVT, caution is necessary when extending our findings to broader ischemic stroke populations. Furthermore, the applicability of our results to countries with different social security systems and rehabilitation facilities than Norway should be approached with care. Additionally, several important variables were not addressed in our study, such as motivation, job type, income, rehabilitation, and the perception of social support, all of which have been established as influential factors in RTW outcomes. 8 , 43 Future research could potentially benefit from obtaining the perspective of employers, as their role in facilitating RTW is a reasonable hypothesis and deserves investigation. As with other stroke prognostic ML models to date, another limitation is the lack of a separate test set. This diminishes certainty about the model's generalizability, posing challenges in comparing different models and predicting their real‐world performance, and is one of the reasons for making our pretrained model available to others.

In conclusion, our study underscores the role of lesion volume as a predictor of RTW after stroke. To our knowledge, this is the first objective imaging biomarker found to be associated with RTW.
