3D TGF-β1-expressing mesenchymal stem cells enhance wound healing in a diabetic mouse model
Authors: Dong-Sik Chae, Sang Joon An, Seongho Han, Sung-Whan Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHuman mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are a promising stem cell source; however, their therapeutic efficacy in chronic wound healing remains limited. This study evaluates the therapeutic potential of transforming growth factor (TGF)-β1-modified, three-dimensionally cultured MSCs (A/T-3D) for enhancing wound healing. Methods: The TGF-β1 gene was inserted into a safe genomic locus in adipose-derived MSCs (ASCs) using transcription activator-like effector nucleases. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis showed that A/T-3D upregulated key factors related to wound healing, including epidermal growth factor (EGF), TGF-β1, fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-A, compared to unmodified ASCs. Results: In vitro scratch wound assays indicated that co-culture with A/T-3D-conditioned medium significantly accelerated wound closure in fibroblasts. In vivo, skin excision in nude mice treated with A/T-3D injections resulted in rapid wound closure, enhanced cellularity, and increased reepithelialization. High engraftment rates of A/T-3D and elevated expression of angiogenic factors were observed in the wound bed, highlighting their direct contribution to tissue repair. Conclusions: Collectively, these findings suggest that A/T-3D MSCs have significant therapeutic potential for wound healing through the secretion of epithelialization and angiogenic factors, along with enhanced engraftment capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04421-3.

Introduction

Diabetic wound healing poses significant clinical challenges, which is mainly due to impaired vascularization, chronic inflammation, and reduced cellular proliferation [1]. In diabetes patients, wounds often fail to progress through the normal stages of healing, leading to chronic ulcers that are often difficult to treat [2]. increasing the infection risk, limb amputation, and overall morbidity. Conventional treatments such as debridement, antibiotics, and skin grafts often fail to produce satisfactory outcomes, particularly in the context of diabetic ulcers [3] where the underlying pathology continues to disrupt the healing process.

Stem cell therapy has emerged as a promising approach to enhance wound healing because of its potential to regenerate damaged tissues, promote angiogenesis, and modulate inflammation [4]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), particularly those derived from human adipose tissue, have shown significant therapeutic potential in various tissue repair models, including wound healing [5, 6]. However, the current 2D monolayer culture methods used to expand MSCs have notable limitations. These methods often fail to preserve the full regenerative capacity of cells, and prolonged culture times can lead to cellular senescence and loss of function [7]. Furthermore, stem cell survival and integration into the wound environment are often limited, which reduces their long-term efficacy in clinical settings.

Transforming Growth Factor (TGF)-β is a multifunctional cytokine that plays a critical role in wound healing [8]. In normal physiological conditions, TGF-β orchestrates cellular activities involved in wound repair, such as inflammation regulation, fibroblast proliferation, and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition [8]. Through these functions, TGF-β aids in forming a stable tissue matrix and scar formation. However, TGF-β signaling dysregulation is observed in diabetic conditions, contributing to the poor healing outcomes commonly associated with diabetic wounds [9]. Specifically, high glucose levels and chronic inflammatory states impair TGF-β’s ability to perform its regenerative role effectively. This impaired signaling reduces fibroblast recruitment, collagen synthesis, and angiogenesis, further delaying wound closure.

Recent advances in 3D stem cell culture systems offer potential solutions to these limitations. Three-dimensional cultures better mimic the native stem cell niche, promoting enhanced cell-cell interactions and more physiologically relevant behaviors [10]. 3D-cultured MSCs have been shown to exhibit improved viability, increased paracrine signaling, and enhanced therapeutic potential compared with their 2D-cultured counterparts [11]. Recent studies suggest that 3D culture systems may improve the outcomes of stem cell-based therapies, particularly in challenging wound environments such as diabetic wounds. These systems enable cell expansion in a manner that preserves stemness and function over extended periods.

In this study, we generated TGF-β expressing human adipose-derived MSCs (ASCs), named A/T, and investigated the effects of 3D-cultured A/T (A/T-3D) on wound healing in a diabetic mouse model. Specifically, we hypothesized that A/T-3D would accelerate wound closure and improve tissue regeneration more effectively than traditional cultured cells. By exploring the therapeutic potential of A/T-3D, this study seeks to advance the development of more effective treatments for chronic diabetic wounds, ultimately offering a promising alternative to existing therapies.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), human adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs), and human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) were obtained from Thermo Scientific Inc. (Rockford, IL, USA). To form 3D spheroids, cells (5 × 104 cells) were suspended in 25 µL of culture medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), α-MEM, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin, as previously reported [12]. The cell suspension was transferred to the lid of culture plates and maintained in an inverted position for five days to allow spheroid formation. Spheroids were dissociated by incubating them with 0.05% trypsin/EDTA for 5 min, after which the enzymatic activity was halted with fresh culture medium. These dissociated cells were then used for subsequent experiments.

Donor vector construction

The gene for TGF-β1 was synthesized and inserted into the adeno-associated virus integration site 1 (AAVS1) safe harbor site of the targeting donor vector (System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA), using Nde I and Sal I restriction sites (Supplementary Fig. 1) [13].

Transfection and selection, fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)

ASCs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. For electroporation, human AMMs were harvested, counted, and resuspended at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells in 10 µL of electroporation buffer with 0.6 µg of each of the following: AAVS1 left TALE-Nuclease vector, AAVS1 right TALE-Nuclease vector, and AAVS1 HR Donor (System Biosciences) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Electroporation was conducted using the Neon Transfection System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The transfection efficiency was about 15 ~ 23%. Five days post-transfection, TGF-β1 knock-in cells were selected by incubating them with 5 µg/mL puromycin for seven days. Puromycin-resistant cells were subsequently resuspended in FACS buffer and sorted according to standard protocols (Supplementary Fig. 2) [14]. FACS-purified (99.1% GFP-positive cells) cells were additionally expanded culturing with 5 µg/mL puromycin and used in this study.

Genomic DNA extraction and junction PCR

Genomic DNA was extracted from cultured cells using the G-spin™ Total DNA Extraction Mini Kit (Intron Biotechnology), following the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 120 ng of genomic DNA was then subjected to touch-down PCR for 36 cycles, followed by a secondary PCR, as previously outlined [14]. The amplified genomic DNA band was visualized using ethidium bromide and photographed with a Chemi Doc XRS imaging system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Conditioned media (CM) collection

To collect conditioned media (CM), the 3D spheroid cells were cultured in glucose-containing DMEM with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin (Gibco), using a spinner flask (Thermo Fisher) and magnetic stirring for three days. The culture media were centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min to remove cell debris, and the supernatants were collected for further experiments. For cell dissociation, spheroids were treated with 0.05% trypsin/ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid for five minutes, after which trypsinization was halted by adding fresh α-MEM with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin.

Gene analysis

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed as previously described [15]. Total RNA was extracted from each cell sample using RNA-stat (Iso-Tex Diagnostics, Friendswood, TX, USA), and reverse transcription was carried out using Taqman Reverse Transcription Reagents (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The synthesized complementary DNA (cDNA) was amplified using qRT-PCR with human- or mouse-specific primers and probes on an ABI PRISM 7000 Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems). Relative mRNA expression levels were normalized to GAPDH as a reference gene. Primers used included: for human-EGF (Hs01099999_m1), TGF-β (Hs00998133_m1), VEGF-A (Hs99999070_m1), FGF-2 (Hs00266645_m1), HGF (Hs00300159_m1), GAPDH (Hs99999905_m1); and for mouse-FGF-2 (Mm00433287_m1), EGF (Mm01135184_m1), HGF (Mm01135184_m1) (TGF-β1 (Mm01178820_m1), VEGF-A (Mm01204733_m1), and GAPDH (Mm99999915_g1). All primers and probes were purchased from Applied Biosystems.

Matrigel tube formation assay

To evaluate tube formation capability, conditioned media (CM) were prepared as modified from previous methods. HUVECs at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well were cultured in low-glucose DMEM (Gibco) containing 1% FBS (control) or CM from stromal vascular fraction (SVF) and ASCs on Matrigel-coated glass slides (NUNC). After a 5-hour incubation, representative fields were randomly imaged under an inverted microscope, and tube length and branching points were quantified as previously described [15].

Scratch migration assay

The scratch migration assay was conducted following established methods [16]. Briefly, HDFs were seeded at 1 × 105 cells per well in 24-well plates and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 27 h to form confluent monolayers. A scratch was created in each monolayer using a sterile pipette tip, and cells were treated with CM from each cell type. Images of wound closure were captured at five random locations, and wound area analysis was performed using NIH Image software (http://rsb.info.nih.gov/nih-image/).

Generation of type 1 diabetes mouse model

This study was conducted following the approval of all experimental protocols by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Catholic Kwandong University. Male nude mice, aged 12 to 14 weeks, were obtained from Joongang Laboratory Animal Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea). To induce type 1 diabetes, the mice were intraperitoneally administered with streptozotocin (STZ; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) at a dose of 45 mg/kg. The STZ was prepared in 50 mM sterile citrate buffer (0.05 M sodium citrate, adjusted to a pH of 4.5). Injections of either STZ or citrate buffer (control group) were performed daily for five consecutive days during the initial week. After four weeks, blood glucose levels were assessed. Only mice with glucose levels exceeding 280 mg/dL were selected for further experimentation. In this study, nude mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 100 mg/kg. The euthanasia process was performed in strict accordance with the ethical guidelines established by IACUC of Catholic Kwandong University and adhered to the standards outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Full-thickness excisional wound model and cell transplantation

Animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Laboratory Animal at Catholic Kwandong University. Male nude mice (12–14 weeks old, 20–26 g) were divided into three groups: PBS only (n = 7), ASCs (n = 7), and A/T-3D (n = 7). An excisional wound model was created following a modified protocol [17, 18]. Mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) and two 6-mm full-thickness skin wounds were generated on each side of the midline using a biopsy punch. Cells (1 × 106 in 100 µL PBS) were injected intradermally around each wound. Donut-shaped silicone splints (Grace Bio-Labs, Bend, OR, USA) were applied to center each wound within a splint.

Wound analysis

Wound analysis was performed following established method [18]. Wound closure was defined as complete (100%) re-epithelialization without any visible drainage. The wound area was measured by tracing the wound margin using NIH Image software, with investigators blinded to treatment groups. The percentage of wound closure was calculated as (original wound area − new wound area) /original wound area×100 [18]. Splints were aligned with the wound edges, and the holes in the splints indicated the initial wound size.

Histological scoring criteria and evaluation

Histological scores were assessed using 3 parameters: dermal and epidermal regeneration, granulation tissue thickness, and angiogenesis (assessed only for the 2-week wound tissue). Each parameter was assigned a score on a scale of 1–9 to indicate extent of tissue recovery and structural organization. For dermal and epidermal regeneration, scores were assigned according to the level of tissue remodelling. Minimal dermis and epidermis organization were scored 1–3. Moderate tissue organization was scored 4–6, and total derma and epidermal remodelling was scored 7–9. For granulation tissue thickness, the scores were assigned according to thickness of the granulation tissue layer. Thin granulation tissue was scored 1–3, moderate thickness was scored 4–6, and thick granulation tissue was scored 7–9. Finally, angiogenesis was assessed for the 2-week wound tissue, and capillary density was utilized as the measure. Tissues with capillary density of < 300/mm² were assigned a score of 1–3, and a capillary density of 300–500/mm² were scored 4–6, and a capillary density > 500/mm² were assigned score of 7–9 indicating a high degree of vascularization.

Histological analysis

Histological analysis was conducted as described previously [18]. Mice were euthanized on day 10, and skin wound samples were collected. Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek USA, Torrance, CA, USA), and sectioned at 10 µm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for general morphology. For immunofluorescence, sections were treated with sodium borohydride (1 mg/mL) to reduce autofluorescence, then stained with mouse anti-cytokeratin primary antibody (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) followed by Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody (1:400, Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA). To assess capillary density, tissue sections were stained with a biotinylated isolectin B4 primary antibody (anti-ILB4, 1:250; Vector Laboratories Inc., Burlingame, CA, USA), followed by a streptavidin-conjugated Alexa Fluor 555 secondary antibody (1:400; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma). Quantification was performed by counting ILB4-positive cells in five randomly selected fields across three sections per sample. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons between two groups were made using the Student’s t-test, while multiple comparisons were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test, using SPSS version 11.0. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Angionenic property after targeted knock-in of TGF-β1 in ASCs and subsequent 3D culturing

To establish a cell line expressing TGF-β1 via gene editing, we employed transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs). The AAVS1 locus on chromosome 19 was selected as an integration site. The donor plasmid included a phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK) promoter-driven TGF-β1 cassette and an elongation factor-1 alpha (EF1α) promoter driving GFP-T2A-puromycin expression (Fig. 1A). ASCs were transfected with TALENs and donor plasmids. GFP-positive transfected cells (initially < 10%) were enriched by puromycin selection (42.5%) and further purified by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), yielding 99.1% GFP-positive cells (Fig. 1B). Genomic integration of the donor plasmid was confirmed using touchdown PCR [19, 20] to detect the correct 5′ junction fragment (960 bp; Fig. 1C). TGF-β expression in the edited ASC line (A/T) was confirmed by qRT-PCR, showing significantly higher TGF-β1 levels compared to untreated ASCs (Fig. 1D). 3D spheroid cultured A/T-3D are shown in Fig. 1E, with GFP expression visible in A/T-3D.

Next, to assess the angiogenic potential of A/T-3D, we analyzed the expression levels of key proangiogenic genes using qRT-PCR. The genes and proteins EGF, FGF-2, TGF-β1, and VEGF-A showed significantly higher expression in A/T-3D cells compared to human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and ASCs (Fig. 1F and Supplementary Fig. 4). Based on these findings, we hypothesized that A/T-3D cells could enhance angiogenic activity in chronic wounds.

Secreted factors derived from A/T-3D promote endothelial tube formation and cell migration

To examine the potential of proteins secreted by A/T-3D to enhance endothelial cell tube formation or cell migration, we conducted Matrigel tube formation and scratch wound closure assays in CM. First, A/T-3D and ASC were cultured in low-glucose DMEM containing 1% FBS in a suspension cell culture system for 5 d. The CM from each cell type was collected and used for experiments. The results demonstrated that A/T-3D CM significantly increased both tube length and branching points compared to untreated control ASCs (Fig. 2A, B). Moreover, the scratch wound closure assay demonstrated that CM from A/T-3D cells notably accelerated fibroblast migration relative to that from ASCs (Fig. 2C, D), suggesting that factors secreted by A/T-3D cells exhibit superior angiogenic and cell migration effects compared to those produced by ASCs.

A/T-3D transplantation enhances wound healing

To assess the in vivo wound healing efficacy of A/T-3D, diabetes was induced in nude mice before creating excisional wounds. Intradermal injections of ASC, A/T-3D, or PBS were administered at five points, positioned 0.3 cm from the wound edges. Wounds treated with A/T-3D exhibited accelerated healing, with significant improvements observed on days 3,5,7 and 10 compared to those treated with PBS (Fig. 3A, B). In addition, A/T-3D treated group showed accelerated wound healing on days 10 compared to A/T, ASC and ASC-3D treated groups.

A/T-3D enhances epithelial regeneration and capillary density in wounds

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of A/T-3 D on wound healing, we immunostained the basal epidermis of skin wounds using cytokeratin, an epithelial cell marker, 14 d post-transplantation. The results indicated a significant increase in cytokeratin-positive cells in the A/T-3D-treated group compared to that in the ASC or ASC-3D group, suggesting enhanced epithelial regeneration (Fig. 5A, B).

Additionally, we performed immunohistochemistry on the wound tissue sections to examine endothelial cell markers. Capillary density, measured using the endothelial marker ILB4, was notably higher in the A/T-3D-treated group than in the ASC or ASC-3D-treated group (Fig. 5C, D).

A/T-3D transplantation induces multiple angiogenic factors for wound healing

The engraftment potential of A/T-3D was evaluated using a diabetic wound model. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated significantly higher A/T-3D engraftment in the wound area than in ASC control (Fig. 6A, B).

To further investigate the mechanisms underlying wound healing, we assessed the expression levels of key angiogenic and inflammatory factors in the wound tissues five days post-cell injection. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed that A/T-3D treatment significantly upregulated multiple angiogenic factors, including EGF, FGF-2, HGF, and VEGF-A, compared to ASC or ASC-3D-treated wounds, suggesting that A/T-3D transplantation promotes various angiogenic factors favorable for wound repair and regeneration (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the characteristics and therapeutic potential of A/T-3D in diabetic wound healing. The key findings are as follows: [1] A/T-3D demonstrated angiogenic properties in vitro through secreted factors, and [2] A/T-3D injection in vivo significantly accelerated wound healing by promoting re-epithelialization and angiogenesis. Together, these results suggest that TGF-β1 gene-edited and 3D-cultured A/T-3D may offer a promising new approach for effective skin regeneration.

Chronic conditions such as obesity and diabetes frequently result in refractory wounds complicated by ongoing inflammation, hypoxia, infection, and insufficient vascularization [21]. Conventional therapies often fail to manage complex wounds effectively [2]. Stem cell-based approaches have recently emerged as potential solutions for non-healing wounds [22], although the optimization of their efficacy and consistency in tissue repair remains a challenge. Advances in genome editing and stem cell technology have opened new avenues for therapeutic innovation under difficult-to-treat conditions [23]. Building on these advances, we aimed to develop a safe and effective approach that integrate gene editing and stem cell technology to promote tissue regeneration.

TGF-β is essential in wound healing, guiding inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling phases [24]. It supports tissue repair by regulating cell behavior and extracellular matrix production. In healthy individuals, balanced TGF-β signaling enables effective wound closure. However, in diabetics, impaired TGF-β activity contributes to chronic wounds through prolonged inflammation, delayed cell growth, and inadequate vascularization [25]. Enhancement of TGF-β signaling in diabetic wounds has shown therapeutic promise [26]. We therefore hypothesize that gene-edited ASCs overexpressing TGF-β1 may improve wound healing outcomes. Combining these cells with 3D culture systems could further enhance the repair of chronic wounds.

Through TGF-β1 gene introduction into ASCs and 3D culturing, these cells show significantly elevated expression of essential wound healing factors, including EGF, FGF, VEGF-A, and TGF-β1. EGF promotes keratinocyte and fibroblast migration, supporting reepithelialization and tissue repair [27], while FGF stimulates endothelial proliferation and migration, aiding angiogenesis [28]. VEGF-A drives endothelial cell proliferation and tube formation and is critical for wound bed vascularization [29]. TGF-β1 regulates extracellular matrix production and fibroblast activation [30], creating an environment favorable for endothelial migration and angiogenesis. Our results demonstrate that A/T-3D secretory factors enhanced in vitro Matrigel tube formation and fibroblast migration, and the injection of A/T-3D cells accelerated diabetic wound healing in vivo. These findings suggest that these four angiogenic paracrine growth factors act synergistically to promote diabetic wound healing.

Angiogenesis and epithelialization are crucial for diabetic wound healing because they restore blood flow and skin integrity, respectively. Key angiogenic factors like VEGF, FGF, and PDGF support blood vessel formation by stimulating endothelial cell proliferation and migration. However, in diabetic wounds, low levels of these factors and impaired endothelial cell responses due to high blood sugar levels result in limited blood flow and persistent hypoxia, thereby hindering wound healing. Therapeutic approaches that boost angiogenic factor levels promote enhanced capillary formation in ischemic wounds [31]. Epithelialization is also essential for skin barrier restoration and is often impaired in diabetic wounds owing to reduced keratinocyte activity and prolonged inflammation. Growth factors like TGF-β and EGF promote keratinocyte migration and proliferation [32, 33]. Consistent with these findings, A/T-3D injections accelerate capillary density and re-epithelialization, suggesting that angiogenic paracrine factors may positively influence these outcomes. Thus, combining angiogenic-focused and epithelialization-focused therapies may provide a more comprehensive approach to diabetic wound healing. Stem cells engineered to enhance both angiogenesis and epithelialization can address multiple deficiencies in the diabetic wound environment by boosting blood vessel formation and accelerating keratinocyte migration. By supplying both angiogenic and epithelialization-promoting factors, these advanced therapies are better suited to overcome the unique challenges of diabetic wound repair, offering a promising strategy for more effective and lasting healing outcomes.

Various angiogenic factors and cytokines secreted by transplanted stem cells play essential paracrine roles in promoting tissue regeneration in the host [15]. Previous studies have shown that the primary therapeutic effect of stem cell therapy stems from the release of pro-angiogenic and anti-inflammatory factors [34]. In line with these findings, our study demonstrated that A/T-3D injection enhanced the levels of angiogenic factors in wound tissues. Additionally, the high engraftment rate of A/T-3D at the wound sites suggests their strong potential for tissue integration and sustained therapeutic effects, distinguishing them from unmodified ASCs.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the application of TGF-β1-modified, 3D-cultured ASCs in diabetic wound treatment holds substantial potential. By upregulating key factors associated with wound healing, these cells address multiple deficits in the diabetic wound environment, promoting angiogenesis, fibroblast activation, and re-epithelialization. This approach represents an effective therapeutic strategy for diabetic wound repair and addresses the critical need for enhanced cellular responses and tissue regeneration in patients with diabetes.
