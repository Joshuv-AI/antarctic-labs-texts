Elastoplastic Analysis of Metallic Parts Employing a Meshless Method
Authors: Ajay Chhillar, Rajender Singh, Prabhakar Sharma, Abdullah Naser
M Asiri, Saiful Islam, Abdul Razak
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The use of finite element method-based approaches has been popular in studying the elastoplastic behavior of metal parts. However, there has been a growing demand for meshless methods. In response, researchers have developed a meshless solution for 2D elastoplastic evaluation of metal components. This approach obtains the locally symmetric weak form of the governing elastoplastic integral equations at each node throughout the problem area and boundary. The elastoplastic constitutive relationships consider a small deformation rate independent associative flow theory applicable to isotropic hardening materials. The proposed solution algorithm can handle loading, unloading, and reverse loading. Numerical results were computed using Gaussian and spline weight functions, and the presented meshless solution proved to be robust and accurate for conducting the elastoplastic investigation of metallic parts. Furthermore, the Gaussian weight function was found to be more robust than the spline weight function. In conclusion, this paper presents a reliable meshless solution for elastoplastic analysis and highlights the advantages of using Gaussian weight functions.

Introduction

The elastoplastic characterization of metal components fascinates structural engineers,1 mechanical product designers, bulk-forming engineers,2 automotive engineers, and sheet-metal forming engineers.3 Researchers in the academic sector and technology firms throughout the globe are also working to streamline the process of assessing and modifying products.4 To anticipate the behavior of the material under the effect of load and hence prevent the high residual stresses, crack generation, and forming defects in the manufacturing of structural and mechanical components, stress analysis is commonly carried out for the whole body under consideration, and the elastic–plastic theory has to be employed where elastic strains are of the same order of plastic strain for an accurate longer product life.5 The elastoplastic analysis of the metal generally takes the form of partial differential equations. The exact solution of such equations is not possible, and if a solution exists, then it is for a limited number of problems with simple boundaries. But in this age of missiles and space, knowledge of more realistic behavior of the material is required. Today, there are numerous techniques available in the literature through which solutions to such problems can be had, and the finite element method (FEM) is one of the renowned and powerful techniques that has been applied for a long time to get solutions for such elastoplastic problems.6 In this technique, the complicated geometries are handled by simple geometries such as triangles, quadrilaterals, tetrahedrons, etc., and the numerical equations of the problem domain are approximated over such simple geometry and then are assembled to anticipate the cumulative behavior. Until 1990, this technique had wide publicity for solving various complex engineering problems. But this method had some inherent limitations in terms of domain decomposition (mesh), and it motivated the researchers to alleviate such problems, and then they started generating a system of the equation over the nodes despite simple geometries.7−10 In 1977, the first meshless method that was reported in the literature was smooth partial hydrodynamics (SPH)11 for solving fluid problems.11 In 1977, Lucy12 applied the same SPH technique to solve astrophysical problems. However, this technique did not fetch much appreciation. The diffuse element method (DEM), however, marked the beginning of the exponential growth of meshless approaches,13 and thereafter, many numerous meshless methods were reported in the literature such as the element free Galerkin (EFG) method,14 reproducing kernel particle method (RKPM),15 finite point method (FPM),16 hp-meshless cloud method,17 boundary node method (BNM),18 meshless local Petrov–Galerkin (MLPG) method,19 local boundary integral equation (LBIE) method, discrete least square meshless method (DLSM),20 generalized mesh-free approximation (GMF),21 and “truly meshless point weighted least squares” (PWLS) method,22 and this list does not terminate here. In all the above-listed techniques, approximated field functions are computed based on the scatter interpolation technique and not based on the isoperimetric formulation as in FEM. Although most of the above methods are called meshless, they require mesh for integration purposes and hence are not truly meshless. The MLPG method, LBIE method, and PWLS method are categorized as truly meshless because no domain decomposition in the form of mesh is needed for integration purposes. The so-called meshless techniques are less expensive and more adaptable because they do not need a mesh. If the techniques are genuinely meshless, the adaptability increases even higher. According to ref (23), the MLPG method is not just a method; rather, it is a concept, and we can obtain various MLPG methods through the different choices of test functions and trial functions. LBIE is also a variant of the MLPG method known as MLPG 4. When comparing the MLPG technique to the LBIE method, singularity integrals are another area in which the two diverge. No singular integrals emerge in the case of the provided MLPG approach, but they could in the case of LBIE.24 So in the last decade, remarkable success has been reported regarding the truly meshless methods MLPG and LBIE, with these methods applied to solve numerous engineering and science problems.25 Because of its fast convergence, excellent accuracy, and resilience, the MLPG approach is projected to eventually replace FEM or BEM. The previous two decades have seen meshless techniques mostly used for linear elastic analysis of materials, whereas research into their use for inelastic evaluation or elastoplastic evaluation has been sporadic but is receiving interest today.26−28 Kagarnovin et al. applied the EFG method for elastoplastic stress analysis around a crack tip through power hardening law.29 Gu implemented the total deformation theory of Hencky for solving elastoplastic analysis of solids using a local meshless approach based on moving least square approximation.30 For significant strain plasticity issues involving high impact and contact, Chen et al. devised a meshless method.31

In the present work, the MLPG method proposed by Atluri and Zhu24 is extended for elastoplastic analysis of metal parts. The governing equations of elastoplasticity are developed using the local symmetric weak form across an overlaying local subdomain, and field approximation between dispersed nodes is accomplished using the movable least square approximation method. The constitutive law combines rate-independent tiny deformation with the associative flow theory of plasticity to develop a relationship between kinetic and kinematic parameters. An isotropic hardening model is used to apply the von Mises yielding criterion. To solve a nonlinear system of equations at these distributed nodes, an incremental iterative technique is used. The objective of this research is to investigate the application of the MLPG approach for elastoplastic analysis of metal parts with elastic and plastic strains of the same order utilizing Gaussian and spline weight functions with both linear and quadratic basis functions. Furthermore, the research compares the findings to those obtained using FEM to check the correctness and efficacy of the applied formulation utilizing basic elastoplastic metal part components, and the convergence of the suggested elastoplastic resolution methods is also explored.

In Section 2 of this paper, moving least square approximation is described for the field variable at the nodes sprinkled over the domain and the boundary. Section 3 describes meshless discretization, elastoplastic formulation, and elasoplastic solution algorithm. Section 4 presents numerical problems undertaken to validate the accuracy and effectiveness of the implemented elastoplastic meshless formulation for metal parts. The conclusion and discussion for the implemented meshless formulation are given in Section 5.

The Moving Least Square Approximation Scheme (MLS)

A mesh-free approach is a numerical technique where the algebraic system equations are created without first specifying the mesh across the full problem area. This technique involves dispersing nodes or points over the full issue area, and the border in which the values of the field variable must be determined. Some method is required to approximatively value the field variable at these dispersed nodes to construct a connection between them throughout the domain and boundary of the stated issue. A variety of methods exist, including the one named for Donald Shepard in 1968 as the “Shepard function”,32 the generalization of the Shepard function as introduced in 1981 that is the moving least squares method by Lancaster and Salkauskas,33 and the compactly supported radial basis function by Liu et al. in 1995.34 A comparison of some of the interpolation techniques was presented by Lam and associates.35 The universality of the meshless approach relies on the approximation scheme’s excellent accuracy, cheap computing cost, simple computational implementation, and ease of extension to multidimensional problems using arbitrary nodes.19,36 One such approach that is employed in the present deployment is the MLS approximations.

Elastoplastic Constitutive Relation

In eq 25, the [Dep] matrix is needed to solve, and the test matrix denotes the elastoplastic constitutive function. This elastoplastic constitutive relation is more complex than the elastic constitutive relation where the material obeys Hooke’s law. However, in the elastoplastic range, Hooke’s law is not valid, and stresses and strains are no longer proportional to each other; hence, strains are not uniquely defined by stresses, and they depend upon the history of material loading. As elastoplastic strains are a function of the loading path, it will be more logical to compute differential elastoplastic strains along the loading path for calculating stresses. The term “incremental flow theory” is often used to refer to this concept.

Elastoplastic Solution Algorithm

In the above-presented formulation, eqs 16–18, 21, and 25 turn out to be nonlinear due to the [Dep] matrix. Here in eq 18, stiffness is a function of deformation due to the material nonlinearity. In eq 18, when stiffness matrix is nonlinear, then it is not good to apply the whole load at a time and find displacement or deformation after solving eq 18. To solve this nonlinear set of equations with good accuracy, an incremental iterative approach is applied, meaning that the specimen is loaded or unloaded by small increments of loads and a solution is iterated to get the displacement at each load increment until convergence of the solution is not achieved. The meaning of convergence is that the iteration process is carried out until the internal forces equilibrate the externally applied load/traction.43 So the solution is iterated until the calculated displacement is good enough to develop internal forces that can resist the external force or traction. To apply nonuniform load steps, a proportional load parameter is used; if the loading parameter is a positive number, then it means that the specimen is loaded, whereas if the loading parameter is chosen to be negative, then it means that the specimen is unloaded. The total load applied at a specific load increment will be an algebraic sum of all previous load steps. The solution steps for the elastoplastic nonlinear equations can be referred from Owen and Hinton [4] and Nayak and Zienkiewicz [5].

All these nonlinear equations are solved for all quadrature points of all the nodes for the current iteration. Now after this iteration, it is checked whether the internal force vector is approximately equal to (within the permissible convergence tolerance sought by the analyzer) the externally applied force vector in case the iteration process may be ended as needed during a nonlinear equation solution. Here, the modified Newton–Raphson scheme is employed and is preferred to make the analysis process economical as in the full Newton–Raphson scheme generating stiffness matrix, shape function derivatives need to be calculated at each iteration of a load step, which makes the analysis costly. Here, a force-based convergence criterion is used so that iteration termination can be carried out for the current load increment.40

Here in eq 40, represents the total external applied load up to load step, and and are the L2 norm of total external force and out-of-balance load vector, respectively. ηTol is the permissible convergence tolerance sought by the analyzer. This ηTol is usually of the order of 10–3 to 10–4. If the convergence takes place in the current iteration, then the out-of-balance load vector is updated to .

The following step is writing the various quantities, i.e., stresses, plastic strain, effective stresses, yield stresses, etc., onto the disc for further postprocessing purposes. After the data writing, the updated out-of-balance load vector is used, and the next increment of load is added to update of balance load vector. This updated out-of-balance load vector is used in place of . Now again, the balance load vector is solved to find the incremental displacement due to the load step or current load increment. In this way, the whole process is repeated until the total load is applied.

Numerical Problems

To validate the accuracy and effectiveness of the above-presented elastoplastic meshless formulation, numerical problems are presented here in this current section. The problems solved include the uniform normal displacement patch test, shear deformation patch test, plate with a hole under tensile loading, and a thicker walled cylinder under internal pressure. The results are validated through the well-established finite element method (FEM) based ABAQUS software. To improve the accuracy of the FEM model, the mesh is progressively refined until the L2 error norm deviation is of the order 10–4 in each load increment in the patch tests. Such close convergence tolerance can be treated as a good practical exact solution. In FEM and meshless methods, the quadrature coordinates are not the same, so to compare the results, nodal coordinates are the same in all cases.

Conclusions

The elastoplastic analysis of metal components under plane stress and plane strain is a crucial aspect of material engineering. Traditional finite element methods have been widely used in this area, but the demand for meshless techniques is increasing. In this paper, a genuine meshless technique is presented for the elastoplastic analysis of metal components under plane stress and plane strain. The following are the main outcomes:The governing equations for the scattered nodes over the problem area are determined, and integral equations are generated via weak form integration over a small subdomain.Meshless form functions are produced using the moving least squares approach, and the penalty technique is used to enforce boundary conditions.The constitutive relationship includes both isotropic hardening and completely plastic material experiencing little deformation, with the rate-independent flow concept and von Mises yielding conditions used to derive the material constitutive relationships.The modified Newton–Raphson technique is employed in a meshless manner to solve the elastoplastic nonlinear equations, capable of handling any loading route.Numerical studies employing Gaussian and spline weight functions on linear and quadratic basis functions demonstrate that the meshless formulation is accurate and resilient for performing elastoplastic analysis on metal components if a sufficient support domain size is selected.The findings demonstrate that the Gaussian weight function is more resilient than the spline weight function, highlighting the benefits of using meshless techniques for elastoplastic analysis.
