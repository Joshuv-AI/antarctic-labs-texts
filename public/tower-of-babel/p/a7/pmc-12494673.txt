Identifying opioid misuse in cancer pain: validation of the prescription opioid misuse index in a multicenter study
Authors: Virginie Guastella, Mélanie Bianchi, Eliane Hanniet, Bruno Pereira, Nicolas Authier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe prevalence of analgesic opioid (AO) misuse among patients with cancer-related chronic pain remains poorly understood, and no screening tool has been validated for this population. The Prescription Opioid Misuse Index (POMI), an eight-item self-administered questionnaire, was developed for chronic non-cancer pain. This study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the POMI in a population of patients with chronic cancer-related pain.MethodsThis prospective observational psychometric study was conducted in two oncology day hospitals at the Clermont-Ferrand University Hospital. Eligible patients had active cancer, chronic pain lasting ≥ 3 months and were receiving daily opioid therapy. The POMI questionnaire was completed at inclusion (TEST) and 2 weeks later (RETEST). Psychometric properties were assessed following standard guidelines, including internal consistency, test–retest reliability and external validity using DSM-5 criteria for opioid use disorder as the reference standard.ResultsA total of 138 patients were included (58% men), with a mean age of 64 ± 10 years. Internal consistency of the POMI was low (Kuder–Richardson coefficient: 0.22). Test–retest reliability, assessed in 68 patients, showed moderate agreement (Lin’s concordance: 0.43 [0.24–0.63]). Correlation between POMI scores and DSM-5 criteria was modest (Spearman’s ρ = 0.25). According to the POMI, 11.6% of patients demonstrated opioid misuse versus 17.4% with DSM-5-defined use disorder.ConclusionsThe POMI lacks sufficient psychometric validity for use in oncology populations. Future tools should be specifically designed to address the clinical complexity and unique context of chronic cancer-related pain and opioid use.

Introduction

Pain is one of the most common and distressing symptoms in oncology, affecting most of the ten million people diagnosed with cancer each year [1]. Its causes are multifactorial, linked to the tumor itself, anticancer treatments (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy) and diagnostic or supportive procedures [2]. The International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP) recognizes cancer-related pain as a major risk factor for chronic pain, prompting the French National Authority for Health (HAS) to recommend early intervention, within the first week of onset and close monitoring to prevent chronification [3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) similarly emphasizes the importance of effective pain management to preserve quality of life [4, 5], recommending opioid analgesics for moderate to severe cancer-related pain [2]. Yet pain remains undertreated: about one-third of cancer patients report moderate to severe pain, rising to 54.6% in advanced or terminal stages [6].

However, the use of opioids in oncology is not without risks. Opioid misuse remains a major concern in oncology, underscoring the need for appropriate tools to evaluate and monitor it. Although few instruments have been validated to assess opioid misuse in oncology, early identification remains essential, as it may limit the loss of chance for patients by preventing treatment disruption, preserving analgesic efficacy and supporting optimal survival outcomes. Patients with cancer are no less susceptible to opioid use disorders (OUD) than non-cancer patients, with manifestations including aberrant drug-related behaviours such as “doctor shopping”, misuse or addiction [7, 8]. A recent meta-analysis [9] underscored the difficulty in estimating the true prevalence of opioid misuse in cancer populations, partly due to the absence of assessment tools specifically validated in this context. Another more recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported highly variable prevalence estimates of opioid misuse in patients with cancer [10]. While several screening instruments have been developed for patients with chronic non-malignant pain [11, 12], none has been validated in oncology settings. Currently, three scales are used to identify opioid misuse: [1] the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) remains the reference standard for identifying OUD [13], but these criteria often conflate addiction and misuse, potentially leading to overestimations of prevalence [14, 15], [2] the Current Opioid Misuse Measure, which is not used much in clinics but mainly for research because of its length [16] and [3] the Prescription Opioid Misuse Index (POMI), which specifically assesses clinical misuse [17]. The POMI scale was validated in 2008 in 137 patients recruited from community substance abuse treatment programs, regional jails, pain clinics and private internal medicine practices in the USA.

A French version, the POMI-5F, is available for this population and validated in chronic non-cancer pain patients [18]. However, its psychometric properties have not yet been assessed in patients with chronic cancer-related pain.

The primary aim of the present study is to evaluate the psychometric properties of the POMI, with a focus on its reliability and validity among patients with chronic cancer-related pain.

Secondary objectives included exploring correlations between POMI scores and sociodemographic factors, lifestyle habits, pain-related anxiety and the extent of information provided to patients regarding opioid-related risks. These correlations were analyzed both to explore factors associated with POMI scores and, in some cases, to provide evidence supporting the construct validity of the scale.

Methods

Study design and ethical considerations

We conducted a multicenter, prospective, observational psychometric study from February 2022 to December 2024.

This survey involving human participants was performed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by an ethics committee and approved by the CPP (Committee for the Protection of Persons) Ouest III ethics committee (Reference No. N 22.03440.000136). The study adhered to the French MR-003 reference methodology, which is the French regulatory framework governing personal data protection in health research and which does not require specific authorization from the CNIL (Commission nationale de l’informatique et des libertés). It was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05761587). Participation was anonymous, and all patients received an information sheet prior to inclusion. A non-objection certificate was signed by investigators for each participant during data collection.

Description of the scales

The POMI is a self-evaluation scale. It is an eight-item inventory, including yes or no questions regarding dose, frequency of use, the need for early refills, a doctor expressing concern of misuse, feeling high from the medication, taking medication due to stress, obtaining prescriptions from multiple physicians and pain control. A total score ≥ two is considered indicative of opioid misuse [17].

The DSM-5 provides standardized diagnostic criteria, including those for substance use disorders, and is considered a reference tool in clinical and research settings. The DSM-5 criteria for substance use disorders are composed of 11 items, covering areas such as impaired control, social impairment, risky use and pharmacological criteria (tolerance and withdrawal). Each criterion is assessed as either present or absent based on the individual’s behavior over the past 12 months. The severity of the substance use disorder is then categorized according to the number of criteria met: 2–3 criteria indicate a mild disorder, 4–5 a moderate disorder and 6 or more a severe disorder.

Study setting, recruitment and participants

The study was coordinated by pain physicians at Clermont-Ferrand University Hospital (CHU), in collaboration with oncologists and advanced practice nurses. Participant recruitment and data collection were conducted by two physicians external to the department, at two oncology day hospitals, located on separate CHU: the Gabriel Montpied site specializing in thoracic oncology and the Estaing site specializing in digestive oncology.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) a confirmed cancer diagnosis, followed regularly in day hospital settings, (3) chronic cancer-related pain for ≥ 3 months and (4) daily opioid use for at least 1 month.

Exclusion criteria were (1) patients undergoing opioid tapering or discontinuation, (2) no active cancer treatment, (3) inability or refusal to participate and (4) pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Outcome measures

The main outcome variable was the POMI score. The original version demonstrated sensitivity of 82% and specificity of 92%. A refined version excluding items 4 and 5 (POMI princeps) yielded the highest internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.84). The French adaptation, POMI-5F (items 1, 2, 3, 5, 6), showed 95% sensitivity, 54% specificity and Cronbach’s alpha = 0.71.

Secondary outcomes included two binary self-report items developed by the study team: (1) opioid use in anticipation of anxiety-provoking pain episodes (“anxiety” item 9) and (2) perception of having received adequate information about opioid-related risks (“information” item 10).

Additional variables collected included sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, relationship status, professional situation), personal/family medical history, pain profile (type, intensity, location), opioid use (type, dosage) and history of substance use or abuse.

Three POMI item combinations were analyzed: POMI princeps: items 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8; POMI-5F: items 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 and a combination retrospectively tailored by our team based on the psychometric findings, called the POMI-Oncol: items 2, 6, 7 + the “information” item, 10.

The study followed a two-phase process. During the TEST phase, participants were provided with an information document and, after giving informed consent, completed the POMI questionnaire, custom items and selected Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) items. Data collection and management

Data were collected via paper and electronic questionnaires and entered into REDCap, a secure electronic data capture system hosted by CHU Clermont-Ferrand.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation was fixed according to Consensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN) recommendations [19, 20]. A cohort of 150 participants was required, including at least 50 for the test–retest analysis to assess reproducibility and criterion validity with adequate statistical power.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the population

One hundred thirty-nine patients were recruited, of whom 138 completed the initial questionnaire. Participants were enrolled from two different sites: Gabriel Montpied site (42.7%, n = 59) and Estaing site (57.2%, n = 79). The mean age was 64 ± 9.6 years. Most participants were male (57.2%), married or in a couple (61.6%) and either retired (52.2%) or employed (32.6%). A total of 68 patients completed the RETEST within 10 to 14 days.

Reliability

Internal consistency

For POMI (original-8 item version), the Kuder–Richardson (KR-20) was 0.22, indicating poor internal consistency and 37% (51/138) of patients had a total score ≥ 2.

For alternative versions: POMI princeps (items 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8) had a KR-20 of 0.25; 11.6% (16/138) scored ≥ 2; POMI-5F (items 1, 2, 3, 5, 6) had a KR-20 of 0.24; 9.4% (13/138) scored ≥ 2; POMI-Oncol (items 2, 6, 7 and “information”) had the highest KR-20 (0.31), with 21% (29/138) scoring ≥ 2.

These results suggest that none of the tested versions reached the standard threshold for internal consistency (KR-20 ≥ 0.70), although POMI-Oncol showed relatively better psychometric properties, with 0.31.

Reproducibility

The minimum number of patients required for the retest was reached, with a completion rate of 49%. Among these 68 patients completing both the TEST and RETEST, 32 (63%) had identical POMI scores. Test–retest reliability assessed by Lin’s concordance coefficient was 0.43 [95% CI: 0.24–0.63] for POMI princeps (low reliability), 0.50 [0.33–0.68] for POMI-5F (moderate) and 0.57 [0.42–0.72] for POMI-Oncol (approaching satisfactory).

Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated in addition to the agreement rate, using a cutoff of < 2 vs ≥ 2 between the two tests. Kappa values for individual items ranged from − 0.04 (item 3) to 0.64 (item 10, “information”), as shown in Fig. 5. Although raw agreement was high for several items (e.g. Q3, Q5, Q7, Q8), the corresponding kappa values were low, due to limited variation in patient responses.Fig. 5Kappa’s concordance coefficient for each item

Criterion validity

The correlation between the POMI scores and DSM-5 criteria for opioid use disorder was modest: r = 0.25 for POMI princeps, r = 0.22 for POMI-5F, r = 0.20 for POMI-Oncol.

Among 24 patients with a DSM-5 score ≥ 2: 9 had a POMI princeps score ≥ 2, 5 had a POMI-5F score ≥ 2 and 10 had a POMI-Oncol score ≥ 2.

Discussion

This is the first study to assess the validity of the POMI scale in patients with chronic cancer-related pain.

A total of 138 patients were included, mostly men (57%), with a mean age of 64 years. The internal consistency of the POMI was low (KR-20 = 0.22), and test–retest reliability was moderate (Lin’s concordance = 0.43). Criterion validity was limited, with a weak correlation between POMI and DSM-5 scores (rho = 0.25). These findings suggest that the POMI, in its current form, may not be suitable for reliably screening opioid misuse in this population.

It highlights that items 4 and 10 (related to patient information) received a high rate of “yes” and “no” responses, indicating that these questions resonated strongly with patients. This suggests that the topic of information and communication about opioid use is particularly meaningful in this population. This is especially relevant for item 10, which was newly developed by our team. We introduced this item because we identified a gap in daily clinical practice regarding how well patients are informed about the safe and appropriate use of opioids. These findings suggest that the issue of patient information may be a key domain to explore in the development of future opioid misuse screening tools tailored to cancer pain populations. We also developed item 9 related to patient anxiety. Item 9 should be interpreted as medication use driven by fear of pain recurrence, reflecting anxiety related to the return of pain.

Overall, 11.6% of participants had a POMI score ≥ 2, while 17.4% met DSM-5 criteria for opioid use disorder. The modest overlap between tools suggests they may capture different aspects of opioid-related behaviour. Furthermore, reproducibility using a cutoff of < 2 vs ≥ 2 between the two tests was questionable. Indeed, although raw agreement was high for several items, the corresponding kappa values were low. This mismatch can be explained by the fact that most patients gave the same answers either always “yes” or always “no”. When responses do not vary much, the kappa statistic may appear low even if there is good agreement. This suggests that the low kappa values may not be due to poor reliability, but rather because these items do not distinguish clearly enough between patients with and without misuse behaviours.

Correlation between POMI and pain intensity

There was no significant correlation between POMI scores and pain intensity as measured by the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI). Higher levels of reported pain were not associated with higher misuse scores. The average pain intensity was 3.9/10 (SD ± 2.3) on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) at both TEST and RETEST. The maximum pain reported in the previous 24 h was 4.5/10 (SD ± 2.7). Notably, 73% of patients reported more than 50% pain relief from their opioid treatment. These findings do not support the hypothesis that opioid misuse, as measured by the POMI, is directly related to the level of pain experienced. However, given the limited validity of the POMI in this population, it is difficult to draw firm conclusions. This further underscores the need for reliable tools that can assess opioid misuse independently of pain severity or analgesic efficacy, particularly in patients receiving opioids for cancer-related pain.

Comparison with the literature

Compared to the population described in the 2019 French situation report [9] and in the original POMI validation study in primary care [22], our cohort presents several distinctive features. Participants in our study were older (mean age 64 vs. 56 years), more often male (57.2%) and presented with a shorter pain duration (6–12 months vs. 1–5 years), which aligns with the epidemiology of the cancer population in France (mean age ~ 68 years, 57% male).

Pain relief was more frequently effective in our population (73% vs. 57%), and opioid prescriptions primarily involved strong opioids such as morphine and oxycodone, in contrast to the lower-potency opioids (tramadol, codeine) commonly used in primary care settings. Our sample underrepresented cancers typically associated with higher misuse rates, such as head and neck cancers [23], due to the structure of our recruitment sites.

Although we were unable to validate the POMI in this specific population, the observed misuse prevalence (ranging from 9.4 to 21%) aligns with previously reported estimates in oncology settings. These rates remain substantially lower than those reported in chronic non-cancer pain populations, where prevalence can reach up to 50%. However, given the absence of a fully validated tool specifically designed to assess opioid misuse in patients with chronic cancer-related pain, these preliminary figures, while uncertain, offer a useful starting point for future research and scale development.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it was designed prospectively and conducted across two oncology centers, with data collected by diverse examiners. The use of different examiners reflects real-world clinical conditions, where various professionals with diverse backgrounds assess patients. The use of different examiners may also reduce the risk of bias associated with a single individual’s subjective interpretation.

This multicentre approach enhances the generalizability of our findings. The sample size was large, and the included population was clearly defined and specifically targeted—patients receiving outpatient oncology care, a group often overlooked in research on opioid misuse. Importantly, this is the first study to evaluate the psychometric performance of the POMI scale in a cancer population. It led to the development of a shortened and adapted version, the POMI-Oncol, based on four items selected for both statistical performance and clinical relevance. This tailored version demonstrated improved internal consistency (KR-20 = 0.31) and reproducibility (Lin = 0.57), suggesting potential for better assessment in oncology settings.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The involvement of multiple examiners can indeed introduce inter-rater variability. The gold standard used for external validation, the DSM-5 criteria, may not be ideally suited for evaluating prescribed opioid misuse, as it is designed to diagnose substance use disorders. This mismatch may have contributed to inconsistencies in the validation process. Moreover, patients found the DSM-5 questions intrusive or difficult to interpret, potentially introducing response bias. Future studies could mitigate this by incorporating decoy items or neutral framing. Additionally, social desirability bias likely affected responses, especially given the stigma surrounding opioid use in oncology. Some patients expressed discomfort or confusion when responding to certain POMI items, particularly those who underused medication due to fears of side effects or addiction. Furthermore, the POMI scale, originally designed for a general population, includes items that may not be fully appropriate for the complexity of cancer-related pain management. From a clinical standpoint, a lack of training in pain management among some practitioners, especially non-specialists, may have contributed to suboptimal opioid education and follow-up, which were frequently cited by investigators as underlying causes of misuse.

Although the minimum number of participants for the retest was reached, the fact that only 49% of participants completed it introduces a risk of bias. Even if a sensitivity analysis comparing respondents and non-respondents to the retest did not reveal any major differences, which provides some reassurance regarding the representativeness of the sample, it is possible that patients who did not complete the retest have a higher rate of problematic use.

The population studied may also have been overly heterogeneous, and certain high-risk subgroups (e.g. patients with head and neck cancers [23]) were underrepresented due to the types of cancers treated at the inclusion sites. Finally, recruitment proved challenging and had to be extended beyond the planned period. Investigators were not the primary opioid prescribers and depended heavily on medical records and the collaboration of oncologists and advanced practice nurses.

Future studies

Several tools have been developed to assess opioid misuse risk in patients with chronic non-cancer pain, including the Opioid Risk Tool (ORT), the Current Opioid Misuse Measure (COMM) and the Screener and Opioid Assessment for Patients with Pain-Revised (SOAPP-R). These instruments underwent validation using methodologies that may offer guidance for future studies in cancer populations. For instance, the SOAPP-R was validated using both internal consistency metrics (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88) and predictive validity through long-term clinical outcomes (e.g. aberrant drug-related behaviours) over a 6-month period [24]. Similarly, the COMM demonstrated good test–retest reliability (r = 0.86) and criterion validity when compared to urine toxicology results and clinician ratings [25]. In contrast, our study relied on cross-sectional data and a DSM-5 symptom count, which may not fully capture the complex spectrum of opioid misuse in cancer patients. Unlike in chronic non-cancer pain, opioid misuse in oncology may often be influenced by disease trajectory, prognosis and psychosocial context, which necessitates a tailored approach to scale development and validation. Future studies should consider longitudinal designs, integration of objective data (e.g. prescription monitoring, urine testing) and inclusion of patient-reported outcomes over time to better assess predictive validity. Additionally, cognitive interviews and qualitative feedback, rarely included in earlier POMI validations, would be essential to ensure that items are culturally and contextually appropriate for cancer patients. Incorporating such approaches could improve content validity and patient acceptability.

Conclusion

This study offers an original and methodologically robust contribution to the assessment of prescription opioid misuse in patients with chronic cancer-related pain. While multiple POMI-based configurations, including a novel oncology-specific version, were tested and compared, the results suggest that the tool’s current psychometric performance remains insufficient for reliable use in this context. These findings underscore the need for further research, using advanced methodologies such as item weighting or Delphi consensus, to adapt and refine screening instruments to the specific challenges of cancer pain management. Ultimately, this work highlights the importance of developing a dedicated and validated tool to support safer and more informed opioid prescribing in supportive cancer care.
