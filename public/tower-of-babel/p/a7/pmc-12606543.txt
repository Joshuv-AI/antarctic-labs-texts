Carbon footprint and staff demands of surgery and chemoradiation for muscle‐invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Vera C Rutten, Simone A W Hesseling, Martine Franckena, Tahlita C M Zuiverloon, Debbie G J Robbrecht, Maarten J IJzerman, Wenxin Lin, Daan Mes, Nicole Hunfeld, Joost L Boormans
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo calculate the environmental and labour impact of two complete care pathways for patients with muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) with similar oncological outcomes: radical cystectomy (RC) and chemoradiation (CRT), by quantifying the total carbon footprint and staff demands.Patients and MethodsThe RC was robot‐assisted surgery with pelvic lymph node dissection and ileal conduit. CRT included 20 fractions of 2/2.75 Gy with mitomycin C (Day 1) and capecitabine every radiation day. Waste audits identified medical products (n = 284), for which a cradle‐to‐gate environmental impact analysis was conducted from treatment selection to first post‐treatment evaluation. Energy use was based on primary data, technical specifications or literature. Patient travel was based on the average distance, assuming petrol‐fuelled cars. Staff demands were assessed by timing clinical staff assignment. The carbon footprint was calculated in CO2‐equivalents using the Ecoinvent Database 3.10.ResultsThe carbon footprint was 208 kg CO2‐equivalents for RC vs 264 kg CO2‐equivalents for CRT. The larger footprint of CRT arose from travel movements and energy use: 5‐ and 1.5‐fold higher than RC, respectively. Main contributors to the total footprint were medical products for RC (49%), and patient travel for CRT (74%). A hotspot analysis identified 10 medical products with the highest impact, which accounted for 60% of the total impact of all medical products. RC averaged 93.5 staff hours per patient vs 29.0 staff hours per patient for CRT.ConclusionsThis is the first study to analyse the environmental impact of complete care pathways in uro‐oncology. Key strategies to reduce the carbon footprint of MIBC treatment include low‐impact patient travel, reviewing high‐impact surgical products, and using renewable energy by hospitals. Insight into staff demands per treatment assists efficient allocation of scarce personnel.Keywords: bladder cancer, carbon footprint, environmental hotspots, staff demands, greenhouse gas, sustainability

Introduction

Urothelial cancer is the 10th most common cancer worldwide, with almost 615 000 cases in 2022 [1]. The current standard of care for patients with non‐metastatic muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) is surgical removal of the bladder, i.e., radical cystectomy (RC) and pelvic lymph node dissection (PLND) [2]. For patients unfit or unwilling to undergo surgery, trimodal therapy, i.e., transurethral resection of bladder tumour (TURBT) followed by chemoradiation (CRT), was considered a valid alternative [2]. A recent study demonstrated similar oncological outcomes for patients with MIBC treated with either CRT or RC [3]. Therefore, clinical guidelines now state that CRT should be considered equally effective as RC [2].

The healthcare sector adds to climate change as it contributes to CO2 emissions. In the Netherlands, the healthcare sector accounts for 7% of the national greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions [4, 5]. Additionally, the healthcare sector faces severe staffing shortages, which is expected to reach 191 000 full‐time equivalents by 2033 in the Netherlands alone [6]. This threatens the quality and accessibility of healthcare as demand increases due to the ageing population. To ensure a sustainable and future‐proof healthcare system, it is crucial to reduce the sector's environmental impact while ensuring effective workforce allocation.

There is growing recognition to address sustainable practices in healthcare. Recent urological studies assessed the carbon footprint of individual medical products, such as cystoscopes, ureteroscopes, and surgical procedures like TURBT [7, 8, 9]. However, the ecological footprint and resource demands of complete urological care pathways remain poorly understood. Additionally, there is little information available about the staff needed to complete a full care pathway.

In this study, we aimed to calculate the environmental and labour impact of two complete care pathways with similar oncological outcomes: RC and CRT, by quantifying the total carbon footprint and staff demands. We identified targetable ‘hotspots’ to reduce GHG emissions and foster discussion on taking sustainability into account in clinical decision‐making.

Patients and Methods

Defining the System Boundaries

The carbon footprint of RC and CRT was assessed from treatment selection at the multidisciplinary tumour board meeting, until (and including) the first evaluation visit post‐treatment. RC was robot‐assisted surgery with PLND and ileal conduit, including 7 days of postoperative hospitalisation. CRT included 20 fractions of 2/2.75 Gy with intravenously mitomycin C on Day 1 and oral capecitabine on all radiation days (825 mg/m2 twice daily). Standardised treatment for an individual patient without complications was assumed. Figure 1 outlines all steps and procedures included per care pathway.

Overview of all steps and procedures per treatment phase included for analysis of the carbon footprint and staff demands of the care pathways of patients with MIBC undergoing RC or CRT.

All in‐hospital procedures directly facilitating treatment were included. Factors considered included medical products and their packaging, energy use, medication, patient travel movements to and from the hospital, and food consumption during hospitalisation. Energy use and food consumption outside the hospital were considered out of scope, as the focus was on factors directly controlled by the hospital. Staff travel and energy use for general hospital facilities, like heating and lighting, were excluded for feasibility reasons and because they were assumed to be similar across care pathways. Staff demands were analysed per function, and direct patient‐related tasks were timed through interviews and observations.

Medical Products and Packaging

Medical products were identified through observations and waste audits. Observations were conducted at the outpatient department, radiation oncology unit and at the day treatment facility of the oncology department. Waste audits were conducted at the operating theatre during two RC procedures and daily at the ward during the hospital admission of two patients. The average use of products was calculated per RC and per hospitalisation day and used for further analysis. All identified products were collected, registered, and weighed. The packaging material of the products was registered, photographed, and weighed. As primary material compositions for packaging materials were unavailable, impact data were assigned based on material determination, examination of photographs, and expert knowledge (S.A.W.H.) of commonly used packaging materials in the medical sector.

A total of 68% of the medical products were weighed, while for 30%, direct weighing was not possible, so their weight was estimated. These estimations were based on: (a) product specifications (11%), (b) data from similar products in previous studies [10] (7%), or (c) comparable products within the current data collection (11%). Products for which weight could neither be measured nor estimated were excluded from the analysis (2%).

A hotspot analysis was performed by identifying 10 medical products with the highest (absolute) carbon footprint.

Energy Consumption

The carbon footprint of energy consumption was calculated by matching the kilowatt‐hours to relevant emission factors. Primary data were used when available. If unavailable, technical specifications were used to estimate energy consumption. If neither were accessible, estimates were based on literature. Energy consumption was calculated for the linear accelerator, sterilisation equipment, anaesthesia equipment, air circulation systems in the operation room, and the DaVinci Xi® surgical system (produced by Intuitive Surgical Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Medication Use

The impact of outpatient, clinical, and perioperative medication and their packaging was included if data on the active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) and/or packaging weight was available. If neither was available, no data were included.

Staff Demands

Staff demands were defined as the total number of hours worked by medical staff to deliver each care pathway. Healthcare professionals with direct patient contact were included and their involvement in care delivery was assessed. Interviews and observations were conducted to estimate direct patient‐related time at the outpatient department (for RC and CRT), at the radiation oncology unit at the day treatment facility of the oncology department. For surgery, an independent observer attended four RC procedures and the average time spent by all members of the surgical team was calculated. As the composition of the surgical team differed, the final composition of the team was standardised based on expert opinion (J.L.B.). For analysis, we assumed the surgical team to consist of two anaesthesiologists (of which one in residency), two anaesthesiology assistants (one in residency), four surgical assistants (two in residency), three urologists (one in residency), and one medical student. During postoperative hospitalisation, medical staff on the ward tracked their own time expenditures and patient‐staff interactions in minutes for two patients using sign‐up sheets. Additionally, the medical staff was observed for 2 days by an independent observer. The average time expenditure per day per group of healthcare professionals (i.e., doctor, nurse etc.) was calculated.

Patient Travel Movements

The average travel distance per treatment modality was calculated by estimating the travel distance of 220 patients with MIBC treated between 2020 and 2022 (RC = 111 patients, CRT = 109). The shortest route by car from each patient's home to the hospital was determined with Google Maps® (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA) and used to calculate the average travel distance per treatment modality. This was multiplied by two to represent a round trip. For RC, three round trips were assumed per patient, compared to 23 for CRT (Fig. 1). Considering treatment intensity, it was assumed that all patients travelled by petrol‐fuelled car.

Food Consumption

For breakfast and lunch, primary data were unavailable as patients selected these meals from mobile food carts. Assumptions were made based on expert opinion (J.L.B.), assuming patients begin with lighter foods and gradually progress to more solid food after RC. On postoperative Day 1, breakfast and lunch were assumed to consist of a bowl of yoghurt. From postoperative Days 2 to 7, breakfast and lunch were assumed to include two sandwiches (one with ham and one with cheese).

For dinner, records of main meals ordered from the hospital kitchen between 30 May and 17 June 2024, were reviewed retrospectively. Out of 191 main meals, 96 desserts, and 84 side dishes, the top five most popular options in each category were identified. The quantities of the major nutrition groups (i.e., number of grammes of vegetables, chicken, fish, potatoes, pasta) of these favourite meals were calculated to create an average standard meal. This standard meal was used for further analysis, assuming one meal per day over a 7‐day postoperative hospital stay. The composition and weight of the meals were known and used to apply specific impact factors for animal products and a general factor for vegetables. Parenteral and enteral nutrition via nasogastric tube were considered out of scope, as we assumed uncomplicated treatment.

Carbon Footprint Calculation

An environmental impact assessment was performed to analyse the GHG impact associated with the materials of the product groups based on mass. A cradle‐to‐gate approach was used, i.e., from material extraction to material manufacturing. Each product or packaging unit was modelled using material composition assumptions, meaning the type of mass and materials used in the product, found through desk research and weighing of individual components (products and packaging). The Ecoinvent Life Cycle Assessment Inventory Database 3.10 (Ecoinvent, Zürich, Switzerland) is an impact database that defines GHG impacts per product, taking processes required to create a product into account. This database was used to define material compositions for each product or packaging group using the material and manufacturing process entries. These compositions were used to calculate a weighted average of impact intensities per product group. A carbon footprint, expressed in CO2‐equivalents, was calculated using the ReCiPe2016 impact assessment method [11]. A more detailed explanation on the carbon footprint calculation can be found in Data S1.

Results

Data Characteristics and Data Quality

In total, 284 medical products and their packaging were identified. An overview of the data characteristics and the achieved data quality is depicted in Tables S1 and S2. With regards to the coverage of medical products, in both RC and CRT, 95% of the carbon footprint was covered by products in the database. The remaining 5% was covered by extrapolation.

For medication, 38% (8 out of 21) of the identified medications were included in the analysis. The remaining 62% (13 medications) could not be included due to insufficient data on the API. A complete list of the identified and included medications is provided in Table S3.

Regarding patient's travel movements, the average travel distance to the hospital was 36 km for RC patients and 22 km for CRT patients. In both treatment groups, ~20% of patients lived within 10 km of the hospital (22% for RC, 23% for CRT). The total distance travelled by an individual patient to complete CRT was 1013 km and 215 km for RC.

Carbon Footprint per Treatment Modality

The carbon footprint per patient of CRT was 264 vs 208 kg CO2‐equivalent for RC (Fig. 2A). The difference between the two treatment modalities can be attributed to travel movements and energy consumption, which accounted for a 5‐fold and 1.5‐fold higher contribution to the footprint of CRT vs RC, respectively. In both care pathways, the treatment phase had the highest impact on carbon footprint: 133 vs 233 kg CO2‐equivalent for RC vs CRT (Fig. 3A,D).

For CRT, patients’ travel movements accounted for 74% of the total carbon footprint, followed by energy consumption by the linear accelerator (22%) (Fig. 3D).

For RC, medical products accounted for 49% of the total carbon footprint whereas patients’ travel movements and energy consumption contributed for 20% and 18%, respectively. Half of the energy consumption was due to the sterilisation of reusable instruments, and 50% was attributed to energy used during surgery, including air circulation, anaesthesia equipment, and the Da Vinci Xi surgical system (Fig. 3B). A hotspot analysis identified 10 medical products with the highest (absolute) carbon footprint (Fig. 3C). These products accounted for 60% of the total impact of all medical products.

Staff Demands

Delivering the care pathway for RC required 93.5 h of personnel, while CRT was completed in 29.0 h. Figure 2B illustrates the distribution of hours among various personnel categories. For RC, nurses accounted for 35% of the total hours spent, followed by surgical and anaesthesia assistants (32%) and doctors (30%). The remaining 3% was accounted for by supporting staff, i.e., personnel at the central blood withdrawal facility, nutrition assistants in the clinical ward, appointment desk officers, and paramedics (i.e., physiotherapists). For CRT, radiotherapy assistants contributed the most hours (76%), followed by doctors (14%), and nurses (10%).

Discussion

This study quantified the carbon footprint of RC at 208 kg CO2‐equivalent per patient and 264 kg CO2‐equivalent for CRT. Key environmental contributors were travel movements, medical products, and energy consumption. For medical products, 10 hotspots were identified, including materials for sterile exposure of the patient and robotic system, surgical staples, wash cloths, and surgical gloves.

To reduce the carbon footprint of RC or CRT, interventions that reduce energy consumption, patient's travel movements, and use of medical products are important. Additionally, RC was three times more labour‐intensive than CRT, which may impact treatment selection during busy periods or times of crises. Insight into the staff demands of both care pathways helps to inform efficient allocation of medical personnel. When treatments are equally effective, carbon footprint and staff demands may serve as additional considerations in treatment decision‐making by healthcare providers and policymakers. While these aspects may not be the primary concern for individual patients, they should be considered to maintain an accessible and sustainable healthcare system.

For CRT, the greatest potential to reduce the carbon footprint lies in optimising patient transportation and energy use. Strategies include promoting sustainable travel options for patients, purchasing green or renewable energy, and improving the efficiency of chemoradiation equipment by reducing standby time or even shutting down equipment, if possible. The use of renewable energy sources, which emit significantly fewer GHG than fossil fuels, contributes to a lower carbon footprint. Additionally, the implementation of hypofractionation in oncological treatment, i.e., delivering more doses of radiation per treatment, helps to reduce emissions.

For RC, attempts to reduce the carbon footprint should focus on high‐impact medical products. The 10 items identified in the hotspot analysis accounted for a relatively large proportion of the total carbon footprint of medical products of the RC care pathway (i.e., 60%) and are therefore an important leverage point for impact reduction. Interestingly, only one of these products, disposable urostomy bag, was urology specific. Other products are used in all surgical procedures. i.e., sterile covers, wash cloths, surgical gowns, and only one was specific to robot‐assisted procedures, i.e., instrument arm drape. Additionally, the hospital's management team and medical staff should evaluate the existing infrastructure to determine the most efficient way to sterilise reusable instruments vs using disposables. Simultaneous increase of the use of reusable products and optimisation of sterilisation procedures can reduce raw material consumption.

The current findings might be extrapolated to other tumour types for which radical surgery or CRT have similar oncological outcomes. Recently, it was demonstrated that radiotherapy for early‐stage non‐small cell lung cancer resulted in 172 kg CO2‐equivalent per treatment compared to 547 kg for surgery [12]. These results differ from ours, as the radiotherapy regimen used a hypofractionation approach of 3, 5 or 8 fractions vs 20 fractions in our study, and energy consumption from general hospital systems and staff travel were included [12]. These differences emphasise the impact of system boundaries of environmental impact analysis on the results.

Patient travel has previously been reported as an important contributor to carbon footprint. In patients with breast and prostate cancer who received external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), patient travel accounted for 70–80% of the carbon footprint [13]. Another EBRT study demonstrated that patient travel accounted for 43% of the total footprint, and mitigation strategies to reduce the footprint included treatment in the nearest facility (−12.2%), optimising transportation modes (−9.3%), and hypofractionation (−5.9%) [14]. In breast cancer treatment, carbon footprints decreased linearly when switching from conventional regimens to hypofractionation: 1358 kg CO2‐equivalent for 25 fractions vs 378 kg for 5 fractions [15].

This study has several strengths. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the carbon footprint of two complete care pathways in uro‐oncology. It offers valuable insights for discussions on enhancing sustainability in healthcare, particularly for tumour types where radical surgery or CRT are common treatment strategies. The data quality and coverage of medical products was good (Tables S1 and S2).

This study has some limitations. First, including exclusively healthcare professionals with direct patient contact may have led to an underestimation of the hours required to deliver each treatment. Additionally, as this study was conducted in an academic centre with educational responsibilities, the number of healthcare professionals involved may be overestimated compared to non‐teaching hospitals. Second, medication use was only partially considered, leading to an underestimation of the carbon footprint. For CRT, no impact data were available for either capecitabine or mitomycin C. For RC, impact data were only available for 38% of the medications, accounting for 4.3% of RC's total carbon footprint. The remaining 62% of medications could not be assessed due to insufficient API information. Third, we assumed a scenario of uncomplicated treatment. However, patients may experience complications during or after treatment, leading to additional imaging, hospital visits, and interventions that increase the carbon footprint. This applies to both treatment pathways, although any grade and severe 90‐day postoperative complications following RC are typically not uncommon, and therefore likely to contribute substantially to the overall carbon footprint [16, 17]. Due to the variability and complexity of these scenarios, it was deemed unfeasible to calculate the footprint of all scenarios of common post‐treatment complications. Fourth, follow‐up visits, including cystoscopies for CRT after the first post‐treatment evaluation were not included. Finally, we did not perform lifecycle assessments for the two care pathways. Due to the complexity of care pathways, this was deemed unfeasible.

Conclusion

In this study, we quantified for the first time the carbon footprint and staff demands of two complete care pathways in non‐metastatic MIBC: RC vs CRT. These parameters potentially impact future clinical decision‐making in MIBC. We identified actionable targets in both care pathways to promote sustainable healthcare: (i) optimise patient transportation in CRT, (ii) review of high‐impact medical products in RC, and (iii) purchase green energy consumption in both. These strategies will reduce the carbon footprint of both treatments. An important advantage of CRT is that it is much less labour‐intense than radical surgery.
