Risk Factors of Malnutrition in Children with Cystic Fibrosis: A 14-year Retrospective Study from Saudi Arabia
Authors: Farah A. Albrahim, Yazan S. Said, Kholoud A. Althobaiti, Razan M. Bader, Reem A. Aljunaid, Wesam A. Alyahya, Rabie Y. Khattab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Malnutrition is a common concern in children with cystic fibrosis, impacting their growth and lung function. Despite its significance, research on malnutrition risk factors in children with cystic fibrosis is limited in Saudi Arabia.Objectives:This study aimed to determine the nutritional status and its associated factors in children with cystic fibrosis.Methods:This retrospective study included all pediatric patients (aged <18 years) with cystic fibrosis who were followed in the pediatric pulmonology clinic at a tertiary care hospital in Dammam, Saudi Arabia, between January 2010 and July 2023.Results:A total of 48 children with cystic fibrosis were included (mean age: 9.7 ± 5.6 years; females: 70.8%). The mean body mass index-for-age/weight-for-length z-score value was −1.61 ± 1.87. Most children were malnourished (60.4%). Compared to those who were not malnourished, malnourished children had significantly delayed diagnosis (P = 0.004), higher hospital admission rates (P = 0.015), uncontrolled fat malabsorption (P = 0.034), and poor appetite (P < 0.001). Independent factors associated with the risk of malnutrition were age (P = 0.014) and fair/poor appetite and intake (P = 0.008 and 0.002, respectively).Conclusions:In this cohort from Saudi Arabia, the prevalence of malnutrition among children with cystic fibrosis was high. In addition, factors associated with malnutrition in this population were age, poor appetite and intake, delayed diagnosis, and uncontrolled fat malabsorption.

INTRODUCTION

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a progressive autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the gene encoding for the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) protein, leading to thickened secretions, chronic respiratory infections, impaired mucociliary clearance, and gastrointestinal complications, particularly malabsorption. Nutritional status, particularly growth parameters and body mass index (BMI), has been shown to predict pulmonary function in individuals with CF. Early-life nutritional indicators are especially important, as they correlate with subsequent respiratory outcomes and overall clinical status. Therefore, optimizing nutrition remains a central component of CF management.[1] The global prevalence of CF varies considerably. Estimates suggest it affects approximately 1 in 30,000 to 1 in 50,000 individuals in Southeast Asian and Middle Eastern populations.[2] Further, a systematic review by Hammoudeh et al.,[3] which analyzed 17 studies across nine Arab nations (Bahrain, Egypt, Jordan, Kuwait, Morocco, Oman, Saudi Arabia, Tunisia, and the United Arab Emirates) revealed a wide range of prevalence estimates, ranging from approximately 1 in 33,000 in Bahrain to 1 in 2664 in Egypt.

Children with CF are particularly vulnerable to malnutrition due to a combination of factors, including increased energy requirements, elevated basal metabolic rates, fat and nutrient losses from malabsorption, and insufficient caloric intake due to poor appetite, frequent infections, and respiratory distress.[4] Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT), critical for managing malabsorption, may also be compromised by suboptimal dosing or poor adherence.[5] Persistent malnutrition in CF patients has been associated with stunted growth, developmental delays, and poorer clinical outcomes.[67] Recurrent chest infections and inconsistent use of PERT further exacerbate nutritional challenges.[89]

Timely and continuous nutritional management, paired with patient and caregiver education, is essential for ensuring adequate growth, maintaining optimal lung function, and improving quality of life. Poor nutritional status in children with CF is linked to worsened pulmonary outcomes, reduced survival, and increased hospitalizations, whereas improved nutrition is associated with better lung function, fewer infections, and longer life expectancy.[4]

In the developed countries, research has identified the major determinants of malnutrition in CF, leading to comprehensive nutritional management guidelines and the development of evidence-based standards.[1011] These efforts have recently shifted focus toward the emerging challenge of overnutrition, as the introduction of highly effective modulator therapies, such as elexacaftor–tezacaftor–ivacaftor, has dramatically improved nutrient absorption and reduced energy expenditure in affected individuals.[121314] The CFTR mutation profile in Saudi Arabia differs significantly from that of Western populations, with possible implications for disease severity and nutritional risk.[1516] Additionally, cultural, socioeconomic, and healthcare system differences may further influence nutritional outcomes. Despite these unique factors, no published studies to date have examined malnutrition risk factors or nutritional status in Saudi children with CF. The absence of local data presents a barrier to developing context-specific clinical strategies. Investigating these factors is crucial to guide effective interventions and ensure accurate CF management in Saudi Arabia.

This study aims to fill this gap by exploring the prevalence and contributing factors associated with malnutrition in Saudi children with CF and evaluating clinical outcomes associated with nutritional status. Findings from this study would support the development of early, targeted nutritional interventions that could improve prognosis and quality of life for affected children.

METHODS

Study design, setting, and participants

This retrospective study included children (aged <18 years) with CF who received care at the Pediatric Pulmonology Clinic of King Fahad Specialist Hospital, Dammam, Saudi Arabia, between January 2010 and July 2023. Patients were divided into malnourished and control groups. Inclusion criteria were confirmed CF diagnosis via genetic and sweat chloride testing. To ensure comprehensive inclusion of the target population, children with CF-related liver and renal diseases and diabetes were included in the study. Patients with significant unrelated comorbidities, such as Crohn’s disease and chronic kidney disease, or incomplete data were excluded.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of King Fahad Specialist Hospital and Imam Abdulrahman bin Faisal University, Dammam, Saudi Arabia.

Data collection

Patient data were extracted from medical records, and included demographics (age, sex, and nationality), CF-related clinical details (age at diagnosis, family history, consanguinity, gene mutation, disease stage [such as newly diagnosed within 6 months] and status, recurrent chest infections [defined as more than twice per year], and advanced/end-stage lung disease [as diagnosed by the physician]), CF burden (hospital admissions, length of stay, pediatric intensive care unit [PICU] admissions), and indicators of fat malabsorption (fecal fat test, fecal elastase test, steatorrhea presence, and patient history). Additionally, gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea, constipation, abdominal pain) and inadequate intake were noted if reported at least twice yearly. Oxygen support (bilevel positive airway pressure [BiPAP], ventilator, and home oxygen), comorbidities (pancreatic insufficiency, bronchiectasis, liver disease, diabetes mellitus, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and meconium ileus), and medication lists were also documented.

The impact of CFTR modulators in this cohort was minimal, as they have only recently become available in selected centers across Saudi Arabia. At the time of data collection, only one patient in the cohort was receiving CFTR modulator therapy. Moreover, compliance with PERT was assessed based on parental reports during clinic visits. Appetite and dietary intake were evaluated using the standard documentation method used by dietitians at the study site. Based on caregiver reports and clinical interviews conducted during routine dietitian assessments, intake was categorized as follows: Good, ≥75% of meals consumed; Fair, ~50% of meals consumed; and Poor, ~25% of meals consumed. This classification is consistently applied and clearly documented in each clinical dietitian’s note. Additional nutritional information, including the use of nutrition support (nasogastric tube or gastrostomy feeding), high-calorie supplements, medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) oil, and fat-soluble vitamin supplementation, was extracted from medical records. Biochemical data for prealbumin, prothrombin time, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, vitamin A, and vitamin E levels were also obtained from the records.

Anthropometric measurements

Weight, height, and BMI were collected at the first and last clinic follow-up, growth was assessed using z-score values with the WHO growth chart from birth to 24 months and CDC growth chart from 2 years to 18 years. The nutritional goal for children with CF is to achieve and maintain optimal growth, defined as a weight-for-length (WFL) z-score greater than 0 for children under 2 years of age, and a BMI-for-age z-score of 0 or higher for those aged ≥2 years.[4] Children were classified as malnourished if their BMI-for-age or WFL z-score was below −1. The malnourished group included patients with BMI-for-age or WFL z-scores of −1 to −1.99 (mild malnutrition), −2 to −2.99 (moderate malnutrition), and ≤−3 (severe malnutrition). The control group included patients with a BMI-for-age or WFL z-score value >−1 (normal BMI-for-age or WFL z-score value between 1 and −1, overweight BMI-for-age or WFL >1 and obese >1.645). Stunting was identified as height/age z-score ≤−2.[17]

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers and percentages. Continuous variables are presented as means ± SD. Independent student t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test, where applicable, were used to compare group means for continuous variables between malnourished and control group. Chi-square or Fisher test were used to compare categorical variables. Logistic regression was used to explore associations between various clinical and demographic variables and malnutrition status. Variables with a P value <0.05 in the univariate analyses were entered into a multiple logistic regression model using backward stepwise elimination. The final model retained only variables that remained independently significant. An association with a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

RESULTS

Study population

A total of 57 children were initially included, but 9 were excluded due to significant unrelated comorbidities or incomplete data. Therefore, 48 patients were included in the final analysis [Figure 1]. Table 1 presents the demographics and clinical characteristics of the included patients. Most patients were female (70.8%), with a mean age of 9.7 ± 5.6 years. Nearly half of the patients were diagnosed during infancy (43.8%), while two patients (4.2%) were diagnosed after the age of 10 years. Most had early diagnoses via symptomatic presentation. The most commonly found mutation was the homozygous 3120+1G>A mutation (12.5%).

Clinical manifestations

The most common pulmonary manifestations were recurrent chest infections (29.2%) and advanced or end-stage lung disease (18.7%). Oxygen support was required in some patients, with nine (18.7%) using BiPAP and two (4.2%) on home oxygen. Positive sputum cultures revealed Pseudomonas aeruginosa in 20 patients (41.7%) and Staphylococcus aureus in 17 patients (35.4%). Abdominal pain and constipation were reported in 17 (35.4%) and 10 (20.8%) patients, respectively, while uncontrolled fat malabsorption was noted in 15 patients (31.3%). In terms of comorbidities, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency was identified in 38 patients (79.2%) and meconium ileus in three patients (6.3%).

Nutritional and medical management

Table 2 summarizes the nutritional and medical management, and medication usage among the studied participants. Data revealed that 28 (58.3%) of patients were on high-calorie supplementation, 20 (41.7%) were not on oral supplements, and 8 (16.7%) were non-compliant and refused to take high calorie supplementation. Eleven children (22.9%) were on MCT oil and 40 (83.3%) were on PERT. Additionally, 32 (66.7%) were using proton pump inhibitors (PPIs). The usual dose of PERT was 500–2500 lipase units/kg/meal, taken with meals with a maximum dose of 10,000 per day. PERT was co-administered with PPI in 31 patients (64.5%). Fat-soluble vitamin supplementation was common in 37 (77.1%) patients, and 5 (10.4%) received nutrition support through tube feeding, while 1 patient was planned for percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube insertion. However, only one patient (2.1%) was on CFTR modulators. Additionally, one (2.1%) patient had a lung transplant at the age of 16 years, and one patient was planned for liver and lung transplant. At the time of data collection, 40 patients were alive and 8 were deceased.

Table 4 highlights the comparison between malnourished and control groups. Age significantly differed between the two groups (mean age, control: 6.6 years; malnourished: 11.6 years; P = 0.002). In the malnourished group, only 27.6% were diagnosed before the age of 1 year compared with 68% in the control group (P = 0.004), signifying an association between delayed diagnosis and malnourishment. Malnourished patients had significantly higher hospital admission rates (4.07 ± 4.48) compared to the control group (1.37 ± 1.46) (P = 0.015) and were more frequently admitted to the PICU (P = 0.037). Though malnourished individuals tended to have longer hospital stays, the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.133). Additionally, malnourished patients showed a higher prevalence of uncontrolled fat malabsorption (51.7% vs. 21.1% in the control group; P = 0.034), and oxygen support, particularly BiPAP, was significantly associated with malnutrition (P = 0.005).

Appetite and nutritional biomarkers

Good appetite was reported in 47.4% of the control group compared with only 3.4% in the malnourished group (P < 0.001), with poor intake predominant in the malnourished group (55.2%). Vitamin D deficiency was prevalent in both groups, but higher in the malnourished group compared with the control group (78.6% vs. 47.1%; P = 0.03). Z-score values for BMI-age or WFL were significantly lower in malnourished children compared with the control group at the last clinic follow-up (P < 0.001). However, the z-score values between initial and last clinic visits for malnourished patients was not statistically significant (P = 0.571).

Gender, comorbidities, compliance to PERT and other nutritional markers like high calorie supplementation intake, tube feeding, and levels of prothrombin time, pre-albumin, vitamin E, and vitamin A did not show significant differences between the two groups.

Association between selected risk factors and malnutrition

Variables that demonstrated a significant association with malnutrition status in the univariate analysis (P < 0.05) were considered for inclusion in the multiple logistic regression model. These variables included age, delayed diagnosis, disease stage, uncontrolled fat malabsorption, oxygen support, hospital admissions, PICU admissions, appetite and intake, and vitamin D level. A backward stepwise elimination approach was applied, whereby all the above candidate variables were initially entered into the model, and then sequentially removed based on statistical significance and model fit. In the final model, only two independent predictors of malnutrition were retained: age (P = 0.014) and appetite/intake (P = 0.008) [Table 5].

DISCUSSION

This study describes the nutritional status and associated risk factors for malnutrition in a cohort of Saudi Arabian children with CF. The results revealed a high prevalence of malnutrition and its link to disease progression, offering valuable descriptive insights into the relationship between clinical deterioration and nutritional status. These findings underscore the importance of developing population-specific nutritional guidelines and implementing early intervention strategies to address these challenges effectively.

The high prevalence of malnutrition in the studied cohort (60.4%) is consistent with findings from other studies conducted in developing countries such as India (65%), Bahrain (72%), and Saudi Arabia (65%).[181920] However, studies in developed countries showed lower prevalence. In the US, one study found 12% malnutrition among 49 children with CF using BMI-for-age/weight-for-length z-scores.[21] Another study reported 26.8% malnutrition using the 10th BMI percentile (z-score: −1.28).[22] In Greece, a study on 68 children and adolescents with CF found 22.1% malnutrition.[23] The difference in prevalence of malnutrition across different studies and countries may be due to various factors. Developed countries have comprehensive newborn screening programs, enabling early CF detection.[24] Specialized CF clinics in these countries provide multidisciplinary care, including nutritional support. Additionally, accessibility and adherence to PERT are better in developed countries, unlike the issues faced in some developing nations.

Although Saudi Arabia has a national newborn screening program, it currently does not include CF.[25] In our cohort, two children were diagnosed after the age of 10, highlighting potential gaps in early detection, follow-up, or program coverage. Bridging this healthcare gap through improved infrastructure and awareness can cut global malnutrition rates. The persistent health challenges encountered by patients with CF underscore the importance of early diagnosis, nutritional support, and holistic care to effectively manage the condition. This includes the utilization of CFTR modulators, which have shown promising outcomes in enhancing lung function, promoting weight gain, and improving nutritional status and BMI.[45] CFTR modulators have only recently become available in selected centers in Saudi Arabia, their use remains extremely limited, with only a single patient in our cohort receiving CFTR modulator therapy at the time of data collection. The limited utilization of CFTR modulators at our center primarily stems from their high cost. Additionally, most of the mutations identified in this cohort are not listed in the mutations eligible for CFTR modulators. Given this minimal exposure, CFTR modulator use is not expected to confound the overall results or affect the analysis.

Ensuring the consistent availability of PERT to enhance compliance is of utmost importance. Non-compliance in some instances among our patients resulted from occasional shortages or interruptions in stock. Addressing vitamin D deficiencies becomes crucial in the comprehensive management of malnutrition among patients with CF, emphasizing the importance of tailored nutritional interventions and supplementation strategies.

The current study found that significantly fewer children in the malnourished group had received a CF diagnosis before the age of 1 year compared with the control group. Delayed diagnosis contributes significantly to malnutrition, thereby highlighting the need for early detection.[26] Timely monitored nutritional interventions are essential for preventing malnutrition in children with CF. The current study also found that the rates of recurrent infections and end-stage lung disease were higher among in the malnourished group, underscoring the need for tailored interventions addressing evolving nutritional needs at various disease stages. Our study findings also highlight how malnutrition impacts healthcare use, with malnourished patients having more hospital admissions and longer stays.

Significantly fewer patients in the malnourished group had a good appetite compared with the control group. Similarly, more than half of those in the malnourished group had poor intake. It is important to acknowledge that poor appetite may not only contribute to the development of malnutrition but can also result from it. Few patients in the current study received tube-based feeding, which highlights the need for multidisciplinary education, comprehensive nutritional assessment, and timely initiation of enteral nutrition support for children unable to meet their nutritional needs orally.[27282930]

In the multiple logistic regression analysis, both age and appetite/intake were found to be significantly associated with malnutrition, with older children and those with reduced appetite or intake being more likely to be malnourished. In our cohort, the mean age of malnourished children was notably higher than that of the control group (11.63 vs. 6.64 years). This observation is consistent with a study on CF in Greece, which also found a positive correlation between older age and malnutrition risk.[31] Another study found that among CF patients aged ≥16 years, younger age was identified as an independent risk factor for malnutrition.[32] These findings may help inform future prospective research aimed at better understanding causal pathways and refining intervention strategies, while also highlighting the importance of tailored nutritional interventions and regular assessments to meet the evolving needs of CF patients throughout their lifespan.

Limitations and future directions

This study has some limitations. As a retrospective observational study, it is subject to inherent biases, including selection and information bias. In addition, the study has limited generalizability, as only patients from a single center were included. Further, data on important potential confounders such as socioeconomic status, parental education level, and household food security were not available in the medical records, limiting the ability to control for these variables in the analysis. Furthermore, the associations observed, such as that between low appetite and malnutrition, may reflect reverse causality. The limited number of patients should be considered while interpreting the results of the current study. Future prospective research is needed to explore targeted nutritional interventions and their impact on long-term outcomes in this patient population.

CONCLUSIONS

The current study highlights the multifaceted nature of malnutrition in children with CF. Age, poor appetite and intake, delayed diagnosis, uncontrolled fat malabsorption, and disease progression were significantly associated with malnutrition. Moreover, malnutrition itself may further contribute to these factors, underscoring the importance of early nutritional intervention. These findings highlight the need for a targeted nutritional intervention for children with CF.
