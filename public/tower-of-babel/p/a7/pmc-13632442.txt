The impact of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio on staging and mortality risk stratification of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome
Authors: Ming Ying, Zhaohui Ding, Ying Luo, Junyu Chen, Shaodi Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a systemic disorder involving pathophysiological interactions among metabolic risk factors, renal system, and cardiovascular system, with consequent systemic inflammatory and oxidative stress responses. Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) has been reported to be a prognostic indicator of systemic inflammation and immunity, but its mortality predictive value in CKM syndrome has not been investigated.MethodsTotally 19,525 participants were included from NHANES database (1999–2018), and were divided into 4 groups by their NLR level (quartile 1 to 4). Study outcomes were all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Binary and ordinal logistic regression was used to investigate the relationship between NLR and CKM stages, and cox regression model and restricted cubic spline (RCS) were used to assessed the association between the NLR and outcomes. Subgroup analysis was conducted to investigate the risk in different CKM stage.ResultsDuring the median follow-up of 9.2 years, 10.93% patients (n = 2,926) died, of which 3.35% patients (n = 944) died from cardiovascular disease. Compared to patients with NLR in quartile 1 (as reference), elevated NLR quartiles were associated with a higher prevalence of advanced CKM stage. After adjustment, compared to NLR in quartile 1, the hazard radio of cardiovascular mortality was 1.46 ([95%CI 1.03–2.06], P = 0.035) for quartile 2, 1.45 ([95%CI 1.04–2.03], P = 0.030) for quartile 3, and 1.95 ([95%CI 1.41–2.70], P < 0.001) for quartile 4. RCS analysis also showed a linear association between NLR and cardiovascular mortality (P for nonlinearity = 0.722). For patients at advanced CKM stage, elevated NLR level was significantly associated with increased risk of mortality compared with those at early CKM stage.ConclusionThis study finds that higher NLR level is associated with increased risk of mortality, and NLR serves as an independent predictor of advanced CKM stages, indicating that NLR can be used as a risk stratification tool for different CKM stages.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05146-y.Keywords: Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, All-cause mortality, Cardiovascular mortality

Introduction

The growing understanding of the interconnected pathophysiology among the metabolic risk factors, including obesity, diabetes mellitus (DM), cardiovascular disease (CVD), and chronic kidney disease (CKD), has prompted the emergence of the concept of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome [1]. CKM syndrome is defined as a systemic disorder driven by pathophysiological interactions between cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic system, ultimately resulting in multiorgan dysfunction and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [2]. To reflect the progression of CKM syndrome, the American Heart Association (AHA) developed a staging system that categorizes the condition from stage 0 to stage 4 [1]. Recent studies have reported that the prevalence of CKM stage 1 or higher was as high as 90% in US adults between 2011 and 2020, and 15% of them were advanced CKM stages (stages 3 or 4), leading to a significant public health challenge [3, 4].

The high prevalence of CKM syndrome requires effective biomarkers for early detection and management. Notably, accumulating evidence has indicated that systemic inflammation plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis and progression of CKM syndrome, serving as a key link among the dysregulated metabolic, cardiovascular, and renal systems [5, 6]. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), calculated from a complete blood count, offers distinct advantages of accessibility, reproducibility, and cost-effectiveness, and is a practical and widely applicable tool in clinical practice for assessing systemic inflammatory status [7]. As a composite indicator, NLR integrates the opposing roles of neutrophils and lymphocytes in the inflammatory response, an elevated NLR not only indicates an imbalance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory pathways but also reflects the severity of systemic inflammation, which is closely linked to the pathophysiological processes of the CKM syndrome [8].

Accumulating clinical evidence has validated the value of NLR in evaluating inflammatory-related diseases closely associated with CKM syndrome. For instance, previous studies have shown that high NLR is strongly associated with the progression of coronary atherosclerosis as well as the worse cardiovascular risk profile, including increased incidence of myocardial infarction, heart failure, and cardiovascular mortality (9–13]. In addition, a higher NLR was confirmed to be an independent risk factor for mortality and worsening renal function in diabetic patients, further supporting its potential as a prognostic marker in populations with metabolic and renal comorbidities (14–16]. However, the association between the NLR and mortality risk has not been clearly demonstrated in CKM patients.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the cooperation between NLR and the CKM stage, and further evaluate the predictive value of NLR for the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality risk in a large, nationally representative sample of CKM patients.

Methods

Study design and population

Data were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database, which is a program managed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the National Centers for Health Statistics in US. A total of 101,316 participants were covered in the NHANES database during 1999–2018.

Participants with age ≥ 20 years old were involved in the study. Individuals meeting the following criteria were excluded: (1) within pregnancy; (2) insufficient or missing data to determine CKM stage; (3) insufficient or missing data about NLR (neutrophil and lymphocyte); (4) insufficient or missing data on follow-up information. Finally, totally 19,525 eligible patients were included in the study analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow illustration. Abbreviation: CKM: cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic; NLR: neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio

This study program follows the guidelines of Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) for reporting observational studies. NHANES protocols were approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the NCHS, and informed written consents were obtained from all the participants.

Outcomes and exposure definitions

The primary outcomes of study were all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. Mortality data were sourced from the National Death Index (NDI) database (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-linkage/mortality-public.htm). The NLR was calculated by dividing the neutrophil count by the lymphocyte count from complete blood counts. Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome stages were identified in accordance with the 2023 American Heart Association (AHA) Presidential Advisory on CKM Health [2]. The definition of CKM stages were showed in supplemental Table 1.

Covariates definitions

Information regarding age, gender, ethnicity, education, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and medical history, which includes cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, was collected through self-reported questionnaires. Diabetes is diagnosed based on fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%, healthcare diagnoses, or use of glucose-lowering medication. Hypertension is diagnosed based on systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg, healthcare diagnoses, or use of antihypertensive drugs. Metabolic syndrome is defined as ≥ 3 of the following: elevated waist circumference, low high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDLC) level (< 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women), fasting serum triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, elevated blood pressure (systolic blood pressure ≥ 130, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg, and/or use of blood pressure-lowering medications), or prediabetes. CVD included heart failure, coronary heart disease, and stroke according to patients’ self-report. CKD was defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73m2 or urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) ≥ 30 mg/g according to CKD-EPI 2021 creatinine equation [17].

Statistical analysis

Complex sample weighting analysis was performed using NHANES-recommended interview weights (implemented with the ‘survey’ package in R). Study participants were categorized into quartiles according to their NLR values. For continuous variables with normal distribution, means (± standard error [SE]) were reported, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Differences among groups were evaluated with ANOVA analysis for continuous data and chi-square tests for categorical data.

To evaluate the association between NLR and CKM syndrome, we conducted multivariable logistic regression analyses (both binary and ordinal), with results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). For the ordinal model, the proportional odds (parallel lines) assumption was formally tested and found to be violated, suggesting potential heterogeneity in the association across CKM stages. Multinomial logistic regression analysis was used to test the association between NLR quartiles and CKM stages (Supplemental Table 2). Cox proportional hazards models were conducted to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CI for mortality outcomes. In the multivariate regression model, the selection of covariates was based on prior literature and clinical relevance. Model 1 was unadjusted; model 2 adjusted for age, gender, ethnicity, education, marital status, family income to federal poverty ratio (PIR) level, smoking, drinking, and body mass index level; model 3 adjusted for model 2 plus CKM stages, eGFR, low density lipoprotein cholesterol, HbA1c, statins use, antihypertensive use and anti-diabetic use. In addition, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis was used to investigate the association between NLR and mortality in the whole population, early CKM stage (CKM 1–2), and advanced CKM stage (CKM 3–4). To further examine NLR’s association with mortality, we performed subgroup analyses categorized by CKM stage (1–2 compared with 3–4). The statistical analysis was performed by R software (version 4.4.1). Two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Population characteristics

Totally 19,525 participants (mean age [SE]: 47.36 ± 0.22 years old; 50.46% female) were included, and patients were divided into 4 groups based on their NLR level (Q1 [n = 5,390], NLR < 1.50; Q2 [n = 4,635], 1.50 ≤ NLR < 1.95; Q3 [n = 4,762], 1.95 ≤ NLR < 2.59; Q4 [n = 4,738], NLR ≥ 2.59). Overall, 69.16% individuals (n = 8,871) were non-hispanic white people, 41.32% individuals (n = 9,633) had high school diploma or less, 16.82% individuals (n = 3,199) were never married, 19.21% participants (n = 5,311) had with a PIR level < 1.30, 21.23% participants (n = 3,992) were smokers, and 32.27% participants (n = 5,381) were drinkers. In addition, 20.55% patients (n = 3,559) were at CKM stage 1, 52.66% patients (n = 9,986) were at CKM stage 2, 5.45% patients (n = 1,792) were at CKM stage 3, and 9.71% patients (n = 2,409) were at CKM stage 4 (Fig. 2).

With the increase of NLR level, the incidence of comorbidities also increased significantly, such as diabetes mellitus (9.49% vs. 10.69% vs. 13.13% vs. 16.93%, P < 0.001), hypertension (45.14% vs. 45.99% vs. 50.53% vs. 59.82%, P < 0.001), chronic kidney disease (9.10% vs. 10.30% vs. 12.78% vs. 19.02%, P < 0.001), cardiovascular disease (6.62% vs. 7.76% vs. 9.63% vs. 14.88%, P < 0.001), and metabolic syndrome (34.97% vs. 35.61% vs. 39.17% vs. 44.59%, P < 0.001). Association between NLR and CKM stages

Both ordinal and binary logistic regression models consistently demonstrated a positive association between higher NLR and advanced CKM stage. In the ordinal logistic regression model assessing the full spectrum of CKM stages (0 to 4), individuals with NLR in Q3 and Q4 had significantly greater odds of being in a more advanced CKM stage compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q3: OR = 1.20, [95CI% 1.06–1.35], P = 0.003; Q4: OR = 1.37 [95%CI 1.22–1.55], P < 0.001). No significant association was observed for Q2 (OR = 1.02 [95%CI 0.92–1.13], P = 0.748).

In the binary logistic regression, compared with Q1 of NLR, higher NLR quartiles were significantly associated with a higher risk of CKM staging (Q2: OR = 1.09 [95%CI 0.90–1.33], P = 0.368; Q3: OR = 1.26 [95%CI 1.02–1.56], P = 0.033; Q4: OR = 1.25 [95%CI 1.01–1.55], P = 0.041). In addition, higher NLR quartiles were significantly associated with an increased probability of advanced CKM stages (CKM 3–4) (Q2: OR = 1.21 [95%CI 0.99–1.49], P = 0.068; Q3: OR = 1.45 [95%CI 1.16–1.80], P < 0.001; Q4: OR = 1.73 [95%CI 1.42–2.12], P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Association between NLR and mortality outcomes

During the median follow-up of 9.2 [4.8–14.3] years, 10.93% patients (n = 2,926) experience all-cause mortality, of which 3.35% patients (n = 944) died of cardiovascular disease. With the increase of NLR level, the incidence of all-cause mortality (Q1 to Q4: 7.27% vs. 8.38% vs. 10.10% vs. 17.99%, P < 0.001) and cardiovascular mortality (Q1 to Q4: 1.84% vs. 2.59% vs. 3.04% vs. 5.94%, P < 0.001) increased.

After adjusting for confounders, high NLR level (treated as continuous variables) was significantly associated with cardiovascular mortality (HR = 1.13 [95%CI 1.08–1.18], P < 0.001) as well as all-cause mortality (HR = 1.12 [95%CI 1.09–1.16], P < 0.001). When treated as categorical variables, patients with higher NLR quartile still had increased risk of cardiovascular mortality (HR = Q2: 1.46 [95%CI 1.03–2.06], P = 0.035; Q3: HR = 1.45 [95%CI 1.04–2.03], P = 0.030; Q4: HR = 1.95 [95%CI 1.41–2.70], P < 0.001) and all-cause mortality (Q2: HR = 0.93 [95%CI 0.76–1.14], P = 0.508; Q3: HR = 0.96 [95%CI 0.76–1.16], P = 0.684; Q4: HR = 1.29 [95%CI 1.11–1.51], P < 0.001) than those with NLR in Q1 (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

In subgroup analysis, among patients at early CKM stage (CKM 1–2), only those with NLR in Q4 had significantly higher risk of cardiovascular mortality (Q2: HR = 0.99 [95%CI 0.57–1.70], P = 0.962; Q3: HR = 1.22 [95%CI 0.67–2.21], P = 0.517; Q4: HR = 1.92 [95%CI 1.12–3.29], P = 0.017) compare with Q1 of NLR. Among patients at advanced CKM stage (CKM 3–4), those with higher NLR quartile had increased risk of cardiovascular mortality (Q2: HR = 1.96 [95%CI 1.24–3.12], P = 0.004; Q3: HR = 1.66 [95%CI 1.09–2.55], P = 0.019; Q4: HR = 2.00 [95%CI 1.34–2.99], P = 0.017) (P for interaction = 0.127). For all-cause mortality, among patients at early CKM stage (CKM 1–2), no significant risk was observed in higher NLR quartile (Q2: HR = 0.81 [95%CI 0.60–1.08], P = 0.143; Q3: HR = 0.842 [95%CI 0.63–1.13], P = 0.253; Q4: HR = 1.18 [95%CI 0.93–1.50], P = 0.166) compare with Q1 of NLR. Among patients at advanced CKM stage (CKM 3–4), those with NLR in Q4 had significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality (Q2: HR = 1.16 [95%CI 0.90–1.49], P = 0.256; Q3: HR = 1.14 [95%CI 0.93–1.40], P = 0.220; Q4: HR = 1.42 [95%CI 1.17–1.73], P < 0.001) (P for interaction = 0.299). Discussion

In this study, we assessed the association between NLR level and all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality among patients with different CKM stages. In addition, we investigated the relationship between NLR level and CKM progressing. Our findings demonstrated that elevated NLR level was significantly associated with the higher prevalence advanced CKM stages (CKM 3–4). Results of multivariable regression analyses and RCS analysis showed that patients with elevated NLR level was associated with higher risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, and this risk was significantly observed in patients with advanced CKM stage.

Recently, several studies have proved that high NLR is associated with increased risk of poor prognosis or adverse cardiovascular events. Among individuals with diabetes, Dong et al. found that high NLR level could increase 1.76-fold risk of cardiovascular mortality [14]. Among patients with chronic kidney disease, increased NLR was discovered to be associated with short- and long- term mortality [18, 19]. In patients with coronary artery disease, elevated NLR was reported to be associated with worse clinical outcomes [20, 21]. In addition, among patients with obesity, higher NLR level is also an independent risk factor for insulin resistance and adverse cardiovascular events [22–24]. For CKM syndrome, a systemic disorder driven by pathophysiological interactions among cardiovascular, renal and metabolic systems, there is limited study to investigate the predictive value of the NLR for adverse outcomes currently. In our study, we observed that patients with NLR in quartile 4 had a 29% higher risk of all-cause mortality, and a 95% higher risk of cardiovascular mortality, compared with subjects in quartile 1. Especially, our findings revealed that in patients with advanced CKM stage, elevated NLR level significantly associated with increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality. It should be noted that the proportional odds assumption for the ordinal model was not satisfied, potentially due to the complexity of CKM stage classification and the uneven distribution of participants across stages. Nevertheless, the results remain informative for understanding the general direction and strength of the association.

The neutrophil count and lymphocyte count are routinely assessed indicators in blood tests. Elevated neutrophil counts signify sustained nonspecific inflammatory pathways, while reduced lymphocyte counts reflect a relatively compromised immune status [21, 25]. The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), derived from these two parameters, demonstrates superior predictive value for immuno-inflammatory responses compared to using either count independently. Excess and dysfunctional visceral adipose tissue is a core driver underlying CKM pathogenesis [1, 26]. Impaired adipocytes overproduce proinflammatory cytokines and oxidative mediators, which cause endothelial, myocardial and renal parenchymal injury. Such inflammatory insult accelerates subclinical atherosclerosis, early cardiac dysfunction and progressive renal impairment, elevating the long-term risks of CVD, renal failure and all-cause mortality [27–29]. These pathological alterations subsequently exacerbate subclinical coronary atherosclerosis, induce early myocardial structural and functional abnormalities, and accelerate renal function deterioration. This pathological progression significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, physical disability, and mortality [1].

Our large nationally representative study evaluated the associations of NLR with CKM staging and mortality risk., and confirmed that NLR serves an independent predictor of advanced staging and mortality risk in CKM. Our findings suggested that NLR, as a marker reflecting systemic inflammatory status, whose biochemical parameters can be easily obtained from routine blood tests, should be regularly and systematically assessed. In addition, our results showed that elevated NLR levels did not significantly increase the risk of mortality in patients during the early stages of CKM. (CKM 1–2), Therefore, it is clinically necessary to detect it in the initial stage of CKM syndrome as well as early therapeutic intervention.

Limitation

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, certain components used to define CKM syndrome—such as elevated waist circumference and body weight—did not account for ethnicity-specific thresholds, particularly for Asian populations. This was primarily due to the lack of systematically collected Asian racial/ethnic identifiers in NHANES prior to the 2011–2012 cycle. To maximize the sample size and statistical power, we included data from the 1999–2000 cycle onward, which necessitated the use of general cutoffs rather than ethnicity-adjusted criteria. Secondly, although extensive efforts were made to adjust for a broad range of potential confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Thirdly, the NLR value was measured only at a single baseline blood test without repeated dynamic detection during long-term follow-up. Transient elevation of NLR triggered by incidental acute infection or acute inflammatory disorders cannot be excluded. Fourthly, data related to some cardiovascular diseases, smoking, alcohol consumption and medication use were collected via self-reporting by subjects, which was susceptible to recall bias and reporting inaccuracy. Finally, this is an observational retrospective cohort study based on publicly available cross-sectional baseline data, which cannot establish definitive causal relationship between elevated NLR and progressive CKM or increased mortality risk.

Conclusion

Our study reveals that elevated NLR level is an independent predictor of advanced CKM stage, and is associated with higher risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality. Particularly, among patients at advanced CKM stage, elevated NLR is significantly associated with the mortality outcomes. These findings suggest that NLR, an inexpensive and routinely available inflammatory marker, may help refine mortality risk stratification among individuals with advanced CKM syndrome. Further prospective studies are needed to determine whether NLR-guided monitoring or targeted interventions can improve clinical outcomes.
