Concerns Regarding Control Group Design and Statistical Rigor in the Study on ER Stress–Induced Neuroinflammation Via LCN2
Authors: Na Liang, Chenxuan Liu, Yuhan Ke, Xu Li, Junjie Cao
Published: 2025

We read with great interest the recent article by Huang et al., which revealed the crucial role of Lipocalin 2 (LCN2) in connecting endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) and neuroinflammation after optic nerve injury, and found it to be of great value.1 Building on this valuable contribution, we respectfully note that several aspects of the interpretation and analysis may benefit from clarification or additional supporting analyses to further strengthen the robustness and clinical translatability of the conclusions.

First, we appreciate the author’s acknowledgment of the inherent limitations of using the contralateral eye as a control in the Discussion section. However, we believe that this is not just a minor limitation for future research to address, but a fundamental flaw in the experimental design that may shake the quantitative basis of key findings in this study. This study demonstrates the various pathological changes induced by optic nerve crush (ONC), such as upregulation of ERS, increased expression of LCN2, activation of microglia, and loss of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs). All quantitative comparison benchmarks for these changes were derived from the contralateral non-surgical eye of the same animal. Although the author validated in the supplementary materials that there was no difference in RGC survival rate and microglial density between the contralateral eye and the blank control eye during the acute phase (3 days), the 3-day validation alone is not sufficient to support the rationale for using the contralateral eye as a control throughout the entire study period (up to 4 weeks). An increasing amount of evidence suggests that unilateral eye or nerve damage can trigger widespread and systemic neuroinflammatory responses, and affect undamaged tissues on the opposite side through cross pathways.2,3 Especially in experimental glaucoma models, studies have clearly reported the activation of reactive microglia in contralateral control eyes.4 This “contralateral effect” means that the so-called “undamaged” contralateral eye is not in a true physiological resting state, and it may have been subtly immune or stress-regulated by experimental procedures. If there is also a slight ERS or inflammatory background in the contralateral eye, the actual differences between the ONC damaged eye and the contralateral eye (such as ERS markers, LCN2 levels, and upregulation of inflammatory factors) may be systematically underestimated. This will discount the actual biological effects of the “significant differences” observed by the author. In addition, the key finding of this study is the “bridging” effect of LCN2. If the contralateral eye is not an ideal baseline, the expression dynamics of LCN2 after ONC and its association with ERS/inflammation may need to be re-evaluated. Although the use of the contralateral eye as a control has a precedent in methodology and is convenient to operate, this approach is still inappropriate in long-term studies involving neuroinflammation and response. To confirm the accuracy of research findings, it is necessary to establish an independent, non-manipulated blank control group of animals to provide a true physiological benchmark.

Besides, the argumentation system of this study largely relies on a series of statistical comparisons from omics analysis to targeted validation. We noticed that the author used inconsistent statistical rigor standards in data analysis at different levels. Specifically, when conducting differential expression analysis on the raw RNA seq data, the authors correctly used the DESeq2 method and controlled for the false discovery rate (FDR < 0.01), which conforms to the standards of high-throughput data analysis. However, in subsequent low throughput validation experiments targeting specific targets, such as validation of multiple ER stress genes through qPCR (Figure 2A) or quantification of multiple cellular indicators through immunofluorescence (Figures 6B–D), although multiple comparisons involving multiple groups and indicators were also involved, the statistical method was only described as using analysis of variance (ANOVA) combined with Tukey’s post hoc test, and the entire text did not report whether any statistical corrections were made for such low throughput multiple comparisons (such as Bonferroni or FDR control). This methodological inconsistency constitutes a core concern. Relaxing statistical control in low throughput validation significantly increases the family wise error rate, leading to a higher risk of false positive results for some “significant” P values (such as * P < 0.01 in Figure 2A). This directly affects the reliability of key confirmatory conclusions, such as “4PBA can effectively inhibit specific ER stress genes” or “rLCN2 can significantly exacerbate specific inflammatory parameters.” We strongly recommend that the authors apply strict post hoc correction to all these low throughput multiple comparison analyses and re-evaluate their significance. Maintaining consistency in statistical rigor across different levels of analysis is the cornerstone of ensuring that the conclusions of the entire study, from discovery to validation, are solid and credible.

In summary, the authors demonstrated that gene therapy targeting LCN2 has higher specificity and efficacy than extensive ER stress suppression. Using LCN2 as a new therapeutic target has added therapeutic strategies for optic neuropathy. More comprehensive and prespecified analyses would yield more precise and reliable estimates, thereby refining the conclusions and better informing clinical decision making.
