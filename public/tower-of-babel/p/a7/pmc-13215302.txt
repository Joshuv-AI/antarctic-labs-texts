Immunogenicity and safety of an investigational quadrivalent measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella vaccine in children aged 4–6 years: A phase II, randomized, multi-country trial
Authors: Md Ahsan Habib, Teena Hughes, Li-Min Huang, Carmen Deseda, Dace Zavadska, Michael Povey, Rajaram Raja, Marie-Ange Demoitié, Venkata Hemanth Kondagunta, MMRVNS 20-001 study group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTwo-dose childhood vaccination against measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella is widely recommended. Quadrivalent (MMRV) vaccines can be used for one or both immunizations against these diseases. In some countries, e.g. the United States, only one MMRV vaccine is licensed. Availability of another MMRV vaccine could strengthen supply resilience, optimal vaccine coverage, and disease control. In this phase II, single-blind, multi-country study, healthy children aged 4–6 y, previously primed with a first dose of any combination of measles/mumps/rubella/varicella-containing vaccine(s) in their second year of life, were randomized 2:2:2:1:1 to receive one dose of a GSK investigational MMRV (MMRVNS; three formulations) or a licensed MMRV vaccine (two lots). Immunogenicity was assessed at Day 43 post-vaccination. Adverse events (AEs) were recorded up to Day 4 (solicited administration-site and systemic AEs), Day 43 (solicited systemic and unsolicited AEs), and Day 181 (serious AEs [SAEs]) post-vaccination. Of 801 participants enrolled, 796 were vaccinated and 765 completed the study. Antigen-specific antibody geometric mean concentrations and seroresponse rates were generally comparable between MMRVNS (regardless of formulation) and MMRV recipients. Administration-site pain (29.0%–39.0% of participants) and systemic drowsiness (9.0%–15.9%) were the most common solicited AEs. Unsolicited AEs (mostly upper respiratory tract infections) were reported in 18.5%–27.2% of participants. At least one SAE (none considered vaccine-related) occurred in each group. Except for one reported fatality, all SAEs resolved. Compared to the licensed standard-of-care MMRV vaccine, the investigational MMRVNS formulations generated comparable immune responses and had a similarly acceptable safety profile, when administered as second dose. These results support further clinical development of MMRVNS.KEYWORDS: Immunogenicity, quadrivalent vaccine, measles-mumps-rubella-varicella vaccines, pediatric, phase II, safety, second dose

Introduction

Measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella are common, highly contagious viral infections mostly occurring during childhood and adolescence. These infections can lead to serious complications, disabilities, and death in otherwise healthy individuals or unborn children.1–5 Globally, measles was estimated to account for an estimated 10 million cases (107,500 deaths)6 and rubella for approximately 35,700 cases in 2023,7 while up to 500,000 cases of mumps are reported to occur on average each year.8,9 The global annual burden of varicella was estimated at 84 million cases,10 with 4.2 million severe complications resulting in hospitalization, and 4,200 varicella-associated deaths.11 Secondary attack rates among susceptible household and institutional contacts are high, reaching 90% for measles,12 65% for mumps,13 and 61%–100% for varicella.14

Measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella are all vaccine-preventable diseases, and the progressive implementation of universal or context-specific vaccination initiated by the World Health Organization (WHO) with the launch of the Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) in 1974 has provided substantial gains in global childhood survival over the past 50 y. Across the 14 global diseases targeted by this program since its inception, measles vaccination was identified as the greatest contributor to the improvement in childhood survival, accounting for 60.8% (93.7 million) of 154.0 million total lives saved by vaccination between 1974–2024.15 Within 25 y of introducing the two-dose universal varicella vaccination in the United States (US), disease incidence has decreased by >97% across all age groups.16 Severe varicella outcomes have been nearly eliminated in persons younger than 20 y and herpes zoster incidence has declined in a stepwise manner (as vaccinated children age).16 The implementation of routine vaccination programs against measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella thus provides major public health benefits and subsequently also economic savings.15–22 However, the benefits of vaccination against these highly communicable, outbreak-prone diseases rely on high vaccination rates to achieve and maintain herd immunity.23

Recommendations for vaccination by either co-administration of a trivalent measles-mumps-rubella and a monovalent varicella vaccine (termed MMR+V) or administration of a quadrivalent (MMRV) vaccine in different doses vary between countries. For instance, even though MMRV vaccines are licensed for both the first- and second-dose pediatric vaccination in different European countries,24 they are not implemented for first-dose vaccination in all member states.25,26 In many countries, first-dose vaccination with either MMR+V or MMRV is approved (except for MMR recommended in Australia for children aged 1–4 y), with MMRV recommended as the second dose.25 Importantly, recommendations regarding second-dose vaccination are not uniform across healthcare settings. In the US, the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) recommends a two-dose schedule for childhood vaccination against measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella, with a first dose administered at 12–15 months of age and the second at 4–6 y of age.27,28 For the first dose, MMR+V separate vaccination is recommended over quadrivalent MMRV vaccination, unless the parents or caregivers express a preference for MMRV vaccination.27,29 For the second-dose administration, MMRV vaccination is preferred,28 as it offers the advantage of reducing the number of injections required to achieve complete vaccination.27,29 In line with these recommendations, MMRV vaccination is estimated to account for roughly 13% (range: 5%–24%) and 80% (52%–98%) of all first and second doses of measles-mumps-rubella-varicella childhood vaccination series in the US, respectively.30

In the US, one live-attenuated MMRV vaccine (ProQuad, Merck Sharpe & Dohme LLC) has been available since 2005 for vaccination in children from 12 months to 12 y of age.27,31 Another MMRV vaccine (Priorix-Tetra, GSK) is approved for use in several countries, including some European Union member states,32 Canada,33 and Australia,34 but is not licensed in the US. Despite their wide availability, routinely recommended vaccines against measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella may undergo occasional shortages.28,35,36 Considering issues beyond supply capacity, availability of a single MMRV vaccine may limit vaccine access and therefore impact the vaccination coverage.37 Furthermore, the possibility of health authorities to tailor and expand immunization strategies based on specific epidemiological needs could be negatively affected. Licensure of an alternative MMRV vaccine could therefore provide public health benefits.

This study evaluated how the immunogenicity and safety of an investigational quadrivalent (MMRVNS) vaccine compared to a licensed standard-of-care (MMRV) vaccine, when administered as the second dose to children aged 4–6 y who were primed with a first dose of any combination of measles-, mumps-, rubella-, and varicella-containing vaccine(s).

Methods

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles derived from the Declaration of Helsinki and the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences International Ethical Guidelines, International Council for Harmonization Good Clinical Practice Guidelines, and applicable laws and regulatory requirements. The study protocol and its amendment, written study participant information, informed consent and assent documents, investigator’s brochure, and any other relevant documents were reviewed and approved by an independent ethics committee or institutional review board at each participating site. Participants’ parent(s)/legally acceptable representative(s) (LAR[s]) could withdraw consent for study participation at any time. The primary reason for study withdrawal was documented in the electronic case report form. The study is registered on ClinicalTrials.org (NCT05630846). The study protocol can be accessed at gsk-studyregister.com (protocol number: 217715).

To monitor the study to protect the interests of study participants while preserving the integrity and credibility of the study, two Independent Data Monitoring Committee (IDMC) review meetings were organized after 200 and 400 vaccinated participants had completed the 43-d post-vaccination safety follow-up period. Further to IDMC evaluations, the Sponsor also conducted monthly Safety Review Team (SRT) meetings up to enrollment and vaccination of the first 200 participants through the 43-d safety follow-up period. Both IDMC (unblinded) and SRT (blinded) reviews of cumulative safety data yielded positive outcomes regarding study continuation as no safety concerns were detected.

Study design and population

This phase II, single-blind, randomized, active-controlled study was conducted across 49 sites in four countries (Colombia, Latvia, Taiwan, and the US, including Puerto Rico) between December 2022 and October 2024. All participating countries had national vaccination schedules against measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella aligned with ACIP recommendations in the US.

Eligible participants were healthy male or female children aged 4–6 y, who previously received a first dose of any combination of measles-, mumps-, rubella-, and varicella-containing vaccine(s) during their second year of life, had no history of measles, mumps, rubella, or varicella disease, and for whom parent(s)/LAR(s) could comply with protocol requirements and provided a written informed consent for study participation. A full list of inclusion and exclusion criteria is provided in Supplements (Supplementary Methods).

Study interventions

Three different MMRVNS vaccine formulations were compared to the commercially available MMRV vaccine (active control). These formulations varied for some or all individual virus potencies, including the high-potency varicella formulation (VH-MMRVNS; high viral potency for varicella and standard potencies for all other viruses), the high-potency mumps formulation (MH-MMRVNS; high viral potency for mumps and standard potencies for all other viruses), and the low-potency formulation (L-MMRVNS; low potencies for all viruses). Further details of MMRVNS and MMRV vaccine compositions are provided in Supplements (Supplementary Methods).

Study procedures

After assessment of eligibility, participants for whom parent(s)/LAR(s) had provided informed consent were randomized to one of the three MMRVNS vaccine formulations or two lots of MMRV vaccine (to ensure representativeness of data in the active control group) following a 2:2:2:1:1 ratio. Allocation of participants to intervention groups was performed before vaccination with a centralized interactive voice response system/interactive web response system accounting for country and center as minimization factors.

This study was single-blind, with investigators and/or their associated staff and study personnel administering the vaccines being aware of the intervention assignment. Participants and their parent(s)/LAR(s), as well as the laboratory personnel conducting sample testing, were blinded to the intervention. Unblinding was allowed in specific circumstances (e.g., medical emergency), when knowledge of the intervention was essential for the clinical management or welfare of the participant, and did not preclude further study continuation for the participant. The date and reason for unblinding a participant’s intervention assignment were documented.

The vaccines were administered as a single subcutaneous injection in the left upper arm on Visit 1 (Day 1). Participants were followed up for 181 d post-vaccination (study end). Throughout the study period, concomitant routine administration of diphtheria-tetanus-acellular pertussis-containing vaccine was allowed in accordance with the local immunization practices and standard of care.

Blood samples were collected at baseline (Day 1) and post-vaccination (Visit 3, Day 43) for immunological assessments (Figure 1). Levels of immunoglobulin G (IgG) to measles, mumps, and rubella were quantified with an MMR multiplex Luminex assay; anti-varicella zoster virus (VZV) glycoprotein E (gE) IgG were quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).38 Seroresponse was defined, regardless of baseline antibody concentrations, as achieving Day 43 IgG levels above the thresholds considered to offer clinical benefit: 116 milli-international units (mIU)/mL for anti-measles IgG, 296 arbitrary units (AU)/mL for anti-mumps IgG, 24 IU/mL for anti-rubella IgG, and 300 mIU/mL for anti-VZV gE IgG. The seroresponse thresholds were defined through bridging with established assays and, for the anti-VZV gE ELISA, validated with varicella efficacy data (part of internal Sponsor data not published).38–42

Safety of the study participants was monitored throughout the study, including a safety call on Day 2 or Day 3, visits on Day 15 and Day 43, and a safety follow-up contact on Day 181 (Figure 1). Participants’ parent(s)/LAR(s) were instructed on how to report both specific and non-specific symptoms to the study staff. The occurrences of solicited adverse events (AEs) within 4 d (administration-site AEs [pain, redness, swelling], systemic AEs [drowsiness, loss of appetite]) and 43 d post-vaccination (systemic AEs [fever, measles/rubella-like rash, varicella-like rash, other rash]) were collected from the observer report documented in the electronic diary. Unsolicited AEs (within 43 d post-vaccination) and serious AEs (SAEs; up to study end) were collected and classified according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) System Organ Class and Preferred Term (SOC and PT) terminology. Unsolicited AEs were reported to the study personnel, either proactively at the time of occurrence (if an AE was medically attended and/or caused concern) or during the scheduled visits and safety calls, upon inquiry by the study personnel. The maximum intensity of each recorded AE was graded on a scale ranging from 1 (mild) to 3 (severe). Grade 3 was defined as cries when the limb is moved or spontaneously painful (pain at the administration site), a greatest surface diameter >20 mm (redness, swelling), no food uptake at all (loss of appetite), a body temperature >39.5°C (fever), ≥51 lesions (measles/rubella-like rash and/or varicella-like rash, which may contain spots, bumps, blisters, or crusts), or an AE preventing normal everyday activities such as attendance at a childcare facility (drowsiness, other rash, unsolicited AEs, SAEs).

Objectives

The primary objective was to evaluate the magnitude of the immune response to the three MMRVNS vaccine formulations and the MMRV vaccine, based on anti-measles, anti-mumps, anti-rubella, and anti-VZV gE IgG geometric mean concentrations (GMCs) at Day 43.

The secondary objectives were to assess the proportion of participants achieving predefined anti-measles, anti-mumps, anti-rubella, and anti-VZV gE antibody seroresponse thresholds at Day 43, as well as safety and reactogenicity following administration of any MMRVNS formulation or the MMRV vaccine, based on percentages of participants reporting AEs within the 4-d or 43-d periods post-vaccination, and SAEs up to study end. Further details of study objectives are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical methods

Assuming a 20% drop-out rate, 800 participants were planned to be enrolled to obtain an evaluable population of 640 participants (160 in each MMRVNS formulation group and 80 in each MMRV vaccine lot group) for the assessment of the primary objective. The study was not powered to formally test non-inferiority or equivalence between the study groups; therefore, all immunogenicity and safety data were analyzed descriptively. The data for the two lots used in the MMRV group were pooled for the purpose of the analyses.

Analyses of the immunogenicity data were based on the per protocol set, which included participants who received the study intervention in accordance with the protocol, were not unblinded, had available immunogenicity results at pre- and post-vaccination for at least one antigen, complied with the blood draw interval between study vaccination and post-vaccination blood draw sample, had no intercurrent conditions that could interfere with immunogenicity, and did not receive any prohibited concomitant vaccination or medication. Antibody GMCs and seroresponse rates were summarized for each vaccine group and each antigen with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), derived assuming a normal distribution of the log-transformed antibody concentrations with unknown variance (for antibody GMCs) or based on the Clopper-Pearson method (for seroresponse rates). Antibody concentrations below the assay cut-off were assigned half of the cut-off value.

Safety analyses (percentages of participants reporting solicited/unsolicited AEs and SAEs) were carried out in the exposed set (ES), which included all participants receiving the study intervention.

Results

Study participants

Overall, 801 participants were enrolled (200 in the VH-MMRVNS group, 202 in the MH-MMRVNS group, 203 in the L-MMRVNS group, and 196 in the pooled MMRV group), of whom 796 (99.4%) received study vaccination and 765 (95.5%) completed the study (Figure 2). Immunogenicity analyses were conducted on the per-protocol set of 679 children (509 in the MMRVNS groups and 170 in the pooled MMRV group), while safety analyses included all 796 vaccinated participants. The first main reason for study discontinuation was loss to follow-up (6/200 [3.0%] participants in the VH-MMRVNS group, 5/202 [2.5%] in the MH-MMRVNS group, 2/203 [1.0%] in the L-MMRVNS group, and 5/196 [2.6%] in the MMRV group). Another notable reason was withdrawal by the participant’s parent(s)/LAR(s) (5/200 [2.5%] participants in the VH-MMRVNS group, 4/202 [2.0%] in the MH-MMRVNS group, 5/203 [2.5%] in the L-MMRVNS group, and 1/196 [0.5%] in the MMRV group). One participant (in the VH-MMRVNS group) discontinued the study due to an AE requiring expedited reporting. This event was a fatal SAE (homicide) and was assessed as not related to the study vaccine. No other participants discontinued due to a solicited or unsolicited AE, or an SAE.

Immunogenicity

At Day 43, anti-measles, anti-mumps, anti-rubella, and anti-VZV gE IgG GMCs were generally comparable among the MMRVNS groups and between MMRVNS and MMRV groups (based on overlapping CIs). The only exception was noted for anti-mumps IgG in MH-MMRVNS recipients, whose GMC (4770 AU/mL; 95% CI: 4259–5342) was higher compared to VH-MMRVNS (3369 AU/mL; 2976–3814) and L-MMRVNS (3114 AU/mL; 2771–3499), as well as MMRV recipients (3176 AU/mL; 2830–3563) (Table 2).

At Day 43, seroresponse rates for measles, mumps, rubella, and VZV gE antigens were ≥95.2% and comparable across vaccine groups. Seroresponse rates for the rubella and VZV gE antigens were 100% in all vaccine groups (Table 2).

Safety and reactogenicity

Within the 4-d post-vaccination period, at least one solicited administration-site AE was reported by 92/198 (46.5%) participants in the VH-MMRVNS group, 82/200 (41.0%) in the MH-MMRVNS group, 76/203 (37.4%) in the L-MMRVNS group, and 96/195 (49.2%) in the MMRV group. Across all vaccine groups, pain at the administration site was the most frequent event (29.0%–39.0% of participants) and redness was the most frequent grade 3 event (3.9%–8.2%) (Figure 3). In all vaccine groups, administration-site AEs had a median duration ≤2.0 d and resolved without medical attention.

At least one event of drowsiness or loss of appetite within 4 d post-vaccination was reported by 37/198 (18.7%) participants in the VH-MMRVNS group, 26/200 (13.0%) in the MH-MMRVNS group, 46/203 (22.7%) in the L-MMRVNS group, and 43/195 (22.1%) in the MMRV group. Across all vaccine groups, drowsiness was the most frequent solicited systemic AE (9.0%–15.9% of participants); grade 3 drowsiness and loss of appetite were both reported by 0.5%–1.0% of participants (Figure 3). In all vaccine groups, drowsiness and loss of appetite events had a median duration of 1.0 d and resolved without medical attention.

Within the 43-d period post-vaccination, at least one solicited systemic AE occurred in 42/198 (21.2%) participants in the VH-MMRVNS group, 44/200 (22.0%) in the MH-MMRVNS group, 46/203 (22.7%) in the L-MMRVNS group, and 40/195 (20.5%) in the MMRV group. Across all vaccine groups, fever was the most frequent AE (12.3%–13.8% of participants) and the most frequent grade 3 event (1.0%–2.1%) with a median duration of 1.0 d. Eight participants required medical attention for fever (4/198 [2.0%] in the VH-MMRVNS group, 1/200 [0.5%] in the MH-MMRVNS group, and 3/203 [1.5%] in the L-MMRVNS group). Measles/rubella-like rash occurred in 2.6%–6.6% of participants (grade 3: 0.0%–0.5%), varicella-like rash in 2.5%–3.1% (grade 3: 0.0%–0.5%), and other rash in 7.2%–9.4% (grade 3: 0.0%–0.5%) (Figure 3). In contrast to no participants in the VH-MMRVNS and MH-MMRVNS groups, one participant (1/203 [0.5%]) required medical attention for measles/rubella-like rash in the L-MMRVNS group and one participant (1/195 [0.5%]) for varicella-like rash in the MMRV group. Events of measles/rubella-like rash and varicella-like rash resolved within a median duration of 1.0–3.0 d depending on the vaccine group.

Unsolicited AEs within 43 d post-vaccination were reported by 44/198 (22.2%) participants in the VH-MMRVNS group, 37/200 (18.5%) in the MH-MMRVNS group, 48/203 (23.6%) in the L-MMRVNS group, and 53/195 (27.2%) in the MMRV group. When classified by MedDRA terminology (SOC and PT), the most prevalent unsolicited AEs across all vaccine groups were infections and infestations (11.5%–19.0% of participants; mostly upper respiratory tract infection), followed by respiratory, thoracic, and mediastinal disorders (3.0%–3.9%; mostly cough and rhinitis allergic), injury, poisoning, and procedural complication (1.5%–4.0%; mostly skin laceration), and gastrointestinal disorders (1.5%–3.6% of participants; mostly vomiting). One grade 3 unsolicited AE was reported (1/200 [0.5%] participant with pneumonia in the MH-MMRVNS group) and assessed as not causally related to study vaccination. Unsolicited AEs considered related to vaccination were reported by 0/198 (0.0%) participants in the VH-MMRVNS, 1/200 (0.5%) participants in the MH-MMRVNS (acute otitis media), 2/203 (1.0%) in the L-MMRVNS (one each for administration-site bruising and irritability), and 5/195 (2.6%) in the MMRV group (seven AEs in total: vomiting, administration-site discomfort, administration-site induration, administration-site warmth, headache, somnolence, and urticaria). A total of 29/198 (14.6%) participants in the VH-MMRVNS group, 25/200 (12.5%) in the MH-MMRVNS group, 34/203 (16.7%) in the L-MMRVNS group, and 33/195 (16.9%) in the MMRV group experienced an unsolicited AE leading to medical attention (mostly upper respiratory tract infection) (Table 3).

Discussion

This study showed that the immune responses in terms of antibody GMCs and seroresponse rates at Day 43 post-vaccination for all tested antigens were comparable across the three MMRVNS formulations and the licensed standard-of-care MMRV vaccine. While anti-mumps GMCs were higher in the MH-MMRVNS group, the clinical significance of this finding is currently unclear. All formulations of the MMRVNS vaccine were considered immunogenic at Day 43. This study also evidenced the acceptable safety and reactogenicity profile of the MMRVNS vaccine (regardless of the formulation), which was comparable to the licensed MMRV vaccine. The reported incidences and severity of solicited and unsolicited AEs were within the same ranges in the MMRVNS and MMRV vaccine groups. Moreover, none of the documented SAEs were assessed as causally related to study vaccination. Collectively, these data are consistent with the favorable immunogenicity and safety profile already described for MMRV vaccines in the pediatric population.43–45

The strengths of this study include its multi-country design and the active comparison with the commercially available MMRV vaccine, allowing for a relevant contextualization of the study findings with regard to current routine immunization practice (i.e., comparison with a standard of care and focus on a population that is eligible for second-dose vaccination, according to country-specific recommendations). Furthermore, participants’ parent(s)/LAR(s) remained blinded to the vaccine group assignment in this single-blind study, thus minimizing reporting bias and improving the quality of safety assessments compared with an open-label design. The main study limitation is its size; as a phase II study, it was not powered for formal statistical comparisons nor to demonstrate non-inferiority, so vaccine immunogenicity and safety were compared only descriptively between the MMRVNS and MMRV groups.

Despite being vaccine-preventable diseases, the global health burden of measles, mumps, rubella, and varicella remains substantial.5,8,10,46 Additionally, in 2024, the number of measles cases in Europe reached a record high of 127,350, the highest since 1997, due to gaps in immunization coverage.47 The progressive implementation of universal or context-specific vaccinations against these common diseases through the WHO EPI has proved to be instrumental in improving infant survival worldwide.15 By 2025, nationwide immunization programs have been introduced in 191 countries for measles, 124 countries for mumps, and 178 countries for rubella,48 while 45 countries worldwide have implemented varicella vaccination.26,49 A major advantage of MMRV vaccination is that it offers a convenient immunization against four diseases in the same injection, thus having potential to increase vaccine compliance and flexibility of immunization programs. Considering the US and other examples of countries with one licensed MMRV vaccine, availability of another quadrivalent vaccine would provide a valuable alternative for pediatric immunization to the health authorities and healthcare professionals.28,35,36 Redundancy and diversity in supply through the introduction of another, similarly immunogenic, quadrivalent vaccine could help maintain an adequate vaccine coverage and protection against the four diseases.

In conclusion, the results of this study demonstrate that the immunogenicity of the MMRVNS investigational vaccine was similar to the existing standard of care. Furthermore, MMRVNS had an acceptable safety profile following administration of a single (second) dose to healthy children aged 4–6 y previously vaccinated with a first dose of any combination of measles-, mumps-, rubella-, and varicella-containing vaccine(s). These results provide evidence supporting further clinical development of the MMRVNS vaccine, including progression to phase III trials in the target population.
