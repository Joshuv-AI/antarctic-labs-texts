Optimization of Nitrogen Injection Parameters for a Partial W‑Type Ventilation Goaf at Duanwang Mine
Authors: Xiaodong Zhang, Zongxiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To optimize nitrogen injection for preventing coal spontaneous combustion in the goaf, closed oxygen consumption tests and field data calibrated the oxygen criterion for the suffocation zone. A numerical model of the 150405 panel goaf with partial “W” ventilation was established to analyze gas drainage and nitrogen injection effects. Increasing the gas drainage volume narrowed the oxidation zone on the conveyor side but expanded it on the track side. Oxidation zone width first decreased and then slightly increased with injection distance and decayed exponentially with higher injection rates. The optimal injection location was identified 40 m from the face on the conveyor side, with a rate of 145.5 m3/h, providing practical guidance for fire prevention.

Introduction

Coal spontaneous combustion (CSC) is recognized as one of the primary thermodynamic disasters affecting coal mine safety production. Among China’s key coal mines, gassy mines account for more than 70% of the total. Furthermore, mines with high risk of coal spontaneous combustion reach 56%, while 49% of mines are simultaneously threatened by compound disasters involving both gas and spontaneous combustion. , Due to the gradual depletion of shallow coal resources and driven by continued energy demand, coal mining depths are continuously increasing. Under deep mining conditions, geological conditions become more complex, while coal seam gas content and ground temperature rise significantly. Consequently, mining difficulty is substantially increased, and the risk of coal spontaneous combustion is heightened. In China, annual coal losses caused by spontaneous combustion are estimated at 20 million tons. Furthermore, over 90% of coal mine fires are attributed to spontaneous combustion, resulting in enormous economic losses and severe ecological damage. Additionally, toxic gases such as CO are continuously generated during coal smoldering. These gases not only pose a risk of poisoning to personnel but may also accumulate, potentially forming explosive atmospheres that can trigger gas explosions.

Representative theories explaining the mechanism of CSC include the pyrite effect theory, the bacteria effect theory, the phenol group theory, the coal–oxygen complex theory, and the free radical theory. Some of these theories are considered to have limitations in explaining the mechanism, while the coal–oxygen complex theory is widely recognized by most scholars. Kam et al. proposed a dual-pathway reaction model, suggesting that the CSC process can be divided into direct oxidation and chemical adsorption. Qi et al. , argued that the low-temperature oxidation of coal not only follows the dual-pathway reaction model but also involves self-reactions among initial active groups in coal. Li et al. , discovered that oxygen-containing functional groups and active sites can transform into each other. Additionally, active sites were found to be oxidized, even at room temperature. Wang et al. constructed active structural units during CSC and established a reaction sequence consisting of 13 units. Zhou et al. simulated the coal oxidation process in the goaf and observed that CO generation is delayed when the oxygen concentration is ≤9%, while the probability of coal–oxygen reactions increases when the concentration exceeds 9%. Zhou et al. demonstrated through cross-point temperature (CPT) analysis that both the CPT and apparent activation energy of coal increase with higher gas content, while the reaction enthalpy with oxygen decreases. Li et al. , found via closed oxygen consumption experiments that the amounts of CO and CO2 released and the oxygen consumption rate during coal oxidation increase exponentially with rising temperature. The above studies demonstrate that oxidation reactions between coal and oxygen still occur in goaf under low-temperature conditions. The reaction process is primarily controlled by the oxygen concentration, temperature, and gas content.

Inerting technology is commonly employed to prevent CSC in the goaf. Among these techniques, nitrogen injection has become the most widely used preventive measure due to its high efficiency and safety. Owing to the complexity and inaccessibility of the goaf, Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) software is widely employed as an effective tool for studying internal processes. Liu et al. found that, compared to CO2 which tends to sink, nitrogen migrates and diffuses more readily upward in the goaf. This characteristic gives nitrogen an advantage in inerting the upper flammable regions. Yuan et al. demonstrated that nitrogen injection strategies are not universally applicable in the goaf. For active working faces, the effectiveness largely depends on the injection location and flow rate. Liu et al. found that in a three-connected goaf, nitrogen injection at the transition zone between the cooling zone and the oxidation zone can reduce the oxidation zone area by 25%. Chen et al. demonstrated that the location of the gas extraction negative pressure point is a key factor in determining the optimal nitrogen injection position and duration. Based on this, a prediction model was developed to identify the optimal injection point relative to the extraction location. Nitrogen injection is an effective technique for fire prevention and extinguishment. However, its effectiveness must be optimized through methods such as CFD simulation, with the key factors being the determination of the optimal injection location and flow rate.

The gas migration pattern in a coal mine goaf is recognized as a foundation for fire prevention and control. It is fundamentally controlled by the ventilation method of the working face. The U-type ventilation, as a traditional layout, is widely adopted in coal mining. However, its goaf flow field is characterized by a distinct unidirectional “one-source, one-sink” pattern. This characteristic leads to an extensive air leakage zone and facilitates the accumulation of gas in the upper corner. To address these issues, a W-type ventilation system has been widely adopted. The excessive gas concentration at the upper corner is effectively mitigated by the “two-source, one-sink” flow pattern, which reduces the pressure differential between the two ends of the working face. Cheng et al. investigated the two ventilation systems using numerical simulation. The W-type system was shown to reduce goaf air leakage by 31% and the working face end pressure differential by 35%, effectively addressing the gas overconcentration problem at the upper corner. Compared with U-type ventilation, the flow field structure, pressure distribution, and air leakage paths in the goaf under W-type ventilation are fundamentally altered and a more complex flow field is created. This difference is particularly pronounced in variants such as the biased-W-type ventilation, where the gas migration patterns in the goaf deviate further from the conventional model due to its asymmetric structure. Consequently, key technical parameters for goaf disaster prevention, such as nitrogen injection locations and flow rates, which were developed based on U-type ventilation conditions, may no longer be directly applicable when applied to W-type ventilation layouts. Failure to perform targeted optimizations will result in a low inertization efficiency and may potentially introduce new risks.

Therefore, closed oxygen consumption experiments were first conducted to determine the SSOS of coal samples from the 150405 working face of the Duanwang Mine. This concentration served as the criterion for defining the three zones of spontaneous combustion in the goaf and provided the basis for numerical simulation. Combined with field measurement data, a three-dimensional geometric model of the 150405 goaf was constructed. The influence of the gas drainage volume on the distribution of the oxidation zone was then analyzed. Finally, the optimal nitrogen injection location and flow rate for the goaf were determined, providing a theoretical basis for on-site fire prevention and extinguishment practices.

Methods and Materials

Experimental Setup

The experimental setup consisted of multiple interconnected components including a computer, a gas concentration collector, a gas pump, a flow meter, a constant-temperature chamber, temperature detectors, preheating pipes, and a drying tube. The connections of the tubing within the setup are shown in Figure . The coal sample canister was maintained at standard atmospheric pressure and had a volume of 3000 mL, with the coal sample particle size ranging from 20 to 60 mesh and the temperature set at 30 °C. The inlet pipe and temperature detector were inserted into the cleaned and dried canister, followed by loading of the coal sample. Prior to the experiment, the closed pipeline was opened, and the air pump was started to allow rapid air inflow into the experimental pipeline, purging the coal sample to eliminate interference from other gases. The coal sample was preheated by using a constant-temperature chamber. After the sample reached the target temperature, the gas concentration monitoring system was activated. When the oxygen concentration inside the device approached that of ambient air, the experimental pipeline was sealed to initiate the closed oxygen consumption experiment.

Numerical Simulation

Model Construction

The 150405 working face was ventilated using a partial “W”-type ventilation system with two intakes and one return. The conveyor and track airways served as intake airways, while the return airway was used for exhaust. The return airway was arranged parallel to the track and conveyor airways, located 30 m from the track airway. A simplified three-dimensional physical model was developed based on field measurement data from the Duanwang Mine 150405 working face. The origin of the coordinate system (0, 0, 0) was set at the intersection of the working face and the bottom boundary of the goaf, as shown in Figure . The dimensions of the goaf were set as 200 m in length, 160 m in width, and 40 m in height, while the conveyor airway, track airway, and return airway were all configured with identical dimensions of 20 m in length, 4 m in width, and 4 m in height.

Goaf model.

Parameter Setting

During modeling, the inlets of the conveyor airway and track airway as well as the gas extraction outlet were set as velocity-inlets. The outlet of the return airway was defined as an outflow boundary. The interfaces between the working face and the goaf and between the working face and the roadways were configured as interfaces. The boundary between the loose coal zone and the collapsed rock zone in the goaf was set as an interior surface. The goaf was assumed to be a homogeneous porous medium, and the heterogeneity of collapsed rock masses and the spatial variation of porosity were neglected in the simulation. The RNG k-ε model was employed during the simulation. The entire three-dimensional model was treated as a fluid zone by default in Gambit. The basic parameters used are listed in Table .

Results and Discussion

Results of the Closed Oxygen Consumption Experiment

The low-temperature oxidation of coal is essentially a free radical chain reaction initiated by complex physicochemical interactions between coal and oxygen. Therefore, the oxygen consumption characteristics of coal samples can reflect their oxidative reactivity to some extent. The variation in the oxygen volume fraction over time obtained from the closed oxygen consumption experiment is shown in Figure . During the experiment, oxygen was continuously consumed by the coal sample, resulting in a monotonic decrease in oxygen concentration within the closed apparatus. As the oxygen partial pressure decreased, the oxygen reaction rate of the coal sample decayed and asymptotically approached zero. It should be noted that the reaction did not cease completely but continued at an extremely low rate. Based on this behavior, the residual oxygen concentration at which the oxygen consumption rate approaches an extremely low value in the closed system is defined as the steady-state oxygen concentration (SSOC).

Oxygen volume fraction versus time curve.

Typically, the SSOC measured by closed oxygen consumption experiments is regarded as the oxygen concentration threshold for defining the suffocation zone when classifying the three zones of spontaneous combustion in the goaf based on the oxidation characteristics of coal samples. However, due to its low metamorphic grade and weak oxidative activity, No. 15 coal in the Duanwang Mine exhibited an exceptionally high SSOC of 18.23% in experiments conducted on samples from the 150405 working face. If this value (18.23%) were directly adopted as the criterion for the suffocation zone, then the oxidation zone would be omitted, which is inconsistent with field observations. Considering that complex factors such as air leakage, oxygen replenishment, and uneven sulfur distribution in the coal can significantly enhance local oxidation and heat release, the oxygen concentration threshold for the suffocation zone was conservatively set at 8% in the classification of the three spontaneous combustion zones in the goaf of the 150405 working face. This approach aims to balance engineering safety and the applicability of the classical empirical model (21% ∼ 18% ∼ 8%).

Gas Extraction Rate

Differences in the gas extraction rate change the absolute gas content in the goaf. They also significantly alter the spatial distribution of oxygen through dilution displacement and pressure field reconstruction. To determine the effect of extraction volume on the oxygen concentration field, three extraction rates were set at 60, 100, and 120 m3/min. The variation in the oxidation zone width within the goaf under different gas extraction rates is shown in Figure . As the extraction rate increased from 60 to 120 m3/min, the oxidation zone width on the conveyor airway side decreased linearly (R 2 = 0.762), while that on the track airway side increased correspondingly (R 2 = 0.315). This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the coupling effect of the extraction opening location, airflow velocity differences, and the reconstruction of the negative pressure field. The extraction opening is located on the conveyor airway side, creating a local high negative pressure zone. The airflow velocity in the conveyor airway is relatively high (2.0832 m/s). When extraction is enhanced, the resulting negative pressure significantly counteracts the air pressure from the rib, thereby inhibiting air leakage and extracting oxygen. This causes a stable linear contraction of the oxidation zone (R 2 = 0.762). In contrast, the track airway had a lower airflow velocity (1.0417 m/s). Under the influence of the negative pressure gradient in the goaf, the pressure difference between its rib and goaf increases. Air is induced to leak into the goaf, providing an oxygen source that promotes expansion of the oxidation zone. However, due to the greater distance from the extraction opening and interference from fractures, the air leakage is unstable, resulting in a weak correlation (R 2 = 0.315). The difference in the initial air pressure between the two roadways leads to opposite trends in the oxidation zone behavior.

Selection of Nitrogen Injection Location

The distance between the nitrogen injection inlet and the working face significantly influences the inertization effect in the goaf. If the distance is too short, then the local pressure may become excessively high, which can cause nitrogen reflux. This may contaminate the fresh airflow and reduce inertization efficiency. If the distance is too large, then the inertization front may fail to cover key areas effectively. To determine the optimal nitrogen injection location in the goaf, simulations were conducted with a fixed injection rate of 900 m3/h. Nitrogen injection inlets were placed along the conveyor airway side of the goaf at distances ranging from 10 to 60 m from the working face, in increments of 10 m. As the distance between the nitrogen injection inlet and the working face increased, the maximum width of the oxidation zone generally showed a significantly decreasing trend. The change in the oxidation zone width on the track airway side was relatively gradual, while a sharp decrease was observed on the conveyor airway side. When the distance increased from 50 to 60 m, a certain rebound in the maximum oxidation zone width occurred. This phenomenon may be attributed to insufficient air leakage pressure after the nitrogen inlet was moved farther away, which prevented the effective delivery of nitrogen to the entire oxidation zone, leading to its renewed expansion.

The fitting curve of the maximum oxidation zone width versus the position of the nitrogen injection inlet is shown in Figure . The fitting results indicate that the minimum oxidation zone width was achieved when the nitrogen injection inlet was located 40 m from the working face, suggesting that this position is optimal for nitrogen injection.

Determination of Nitrogen Injection Rate

A complex air leakage flow field exists within the goaf. The injected nitrogen interacts with the air leakage flow. If the nitrogen injection rate is too low, then the nitrogen quickly merges into the leakage channels and fails to cover the target inertization area. If the injection rate is too high, a sudden pressure rise may occur, which could cause an abnormal outflow of high-concentration gas toward the working face or nontarget areas. Therefore, sufficient momentum and volume are required to overcome the effects of air leakage, enabling nitrogen to break through the leakage barrier and diffuse steadily into the predetermined inertization zone. The nitrogen injection inlet was placed 40 m from the working face on the conveyor airway side. Nitrogen injection rates were set at 500, 600, 700, 800, 900, 1000, 1140, and 1300 m3/h. Numerical simulations of the oxygen concentration field in the goaf were carried out to determine the optimal injection rate. The simulation results are presented in Table .

During normal production, the advance rate of the 150405 working face in the Duanwang Mine was maintained at 2.86 m/d. When geological anomalies or maintenance activities occurred, the average advance rate was reduced to 2.04 m/d. The shortest spontaneous combustion period was determined to be 61 days. The maximum allowable width of the oxidation zone in the goaf can be expressed as follows: 4 Lm=v×τ where L m is the maximum allowable width of the natural oxidation zone, m, v is the daily advance rate of the working face, m/d, and τ is the shortest spontaneous combustion period, d.

The maximum allowable width of the oxidation zone was calculated to be 124.44 m. Accordingly, the optimal nitrogen injection rate was determined to be 145.5 m3/h.

Conclusions

(1)Owing to the high measured SSOC value (18.23%) of the No. 15 coal sample from the Duanwang Mine, which could lead to the omission of the oxidation zone if directly adopted, the oxygen concentration threshold for the suffocation zone was conservatively set at 8% in this study. This modification enhances the safety of risk assessment while maintaining consistency with classical empirical models.(2)Increasing the gas extraction rate was found to have an asymmetric effect on the oxidation zones on both sides of the goaf. The width of the oxidation zone on the conveyor airway side exhibited a linear negative correlation with the extraction rate, while that on the track airway side showed a linear positive correlation.(3)The maximum width of the oxidation zone initially decreased and then increased with greater distance between the nitrogen injection inlet and the working face, while it decreased continuously with higher nitrogen injection rates. Based on field production conditions, the optimal nitrogen injection location was determined to be 40 m from the working face, and the optimal injection rate was identified as 145.5 m3/h.
