Neuroglobin-promoted autophagy inhibits the proliferation, migration and invasion of human glioblastoma cells through the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway
Authors: Li Li, Hongfeng Zhang, Wenqing Li, Zhijun Yuan, Wushi Zhao, Zhenzhao Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuroglobin (Ngb) is a novel oxygen-binding or sensor protein that is present primarily in neurons and the brain. It plays a critical role in sensing oxygen and hypoxia signals and in transducing intracellular signaling pathways. However, the specific functions and mechanism of Ngb in the glioblastoma remain unclear.MethodsThe expression of Ngb in glioma brain tissues and cells was measured by Western blot, immunohistochemical staining and retrieved from TCGA databases. Loss or gain of function assays, CCK-8 assay, scratch and transwell assay, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, acridine orange staining, evaluation of fluorescent LC3 puncta, and flow cytometry analysis were conducted to determine the effects of Ngb overexpression or knockdown on glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) cell proliferation, invasion and autophagy, and Ngb/EGFR interactions were verified by glutathione S-Transferase (GST) pull-down assay and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), and the mechanism of Ngb on autophagy was investigated by Western blot. The animal model of glioma was further employed to assess the inhibitory effect of Ngb overexpression on GBM cell growth.ResultsThe study demonstrated that the expression of Ngb was significantly downregulated in glioma tissues and cell lines and that its downregulation was positively correlated with poor patient survival, further demonstrating that Ngb promoted belcin1 and LC3-dependent autophagy in the GBM cells. We found that inhibitory effect of Ngb on cellular invasion is associated with the suppression of EGFR/PI3K/AKT/m-TOR signaling. Furthermore, our study revealed that increasing Ngb expression in GBM cells notably decreased their ability to migrate and invade in vitro and suppressed tumor growth in vivo. The overexpression of Ngb resulted in cell cycle arrest at the S phase and increased apoptosis. Finally, our findings indicated that Ngb increased autophagy, which may inhibit the invasion of glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) by interaction with the amino acid 1–42 domains of Ngb with EGFR.ConclusionsIn this study, our research findings indicated that Ngb promoted autophagy and suppressed the migration and invasion of GBM cells through inactivation of the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/m-TOR signaling pathway, highlighting that autophagy activation may suppress the invasion of GBM cells with low Ngb expression.

Introduction

Glioma is a leading and deadly form of brain malignancy in humans [1, 2], with half of gliomas being glioblastoma multiforme (GBM). Even though multiple therapies, including surgical resection, adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy with temozolomide, have been developed for the standard therapeutic treatment of GBM, the prognosis remains poor because of its aggressive and invasive nature, with an average survival time not exceeding 15 months [3, 4]. Intrinsic or acquired resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy represents a substantial clinical challenge in the management of GBM patients [5, 6]. Thus, identifying novel prognostic and therapeutic targets and understanding the pathogenesis of GBM are urgently needed to extend the survival time of GBM patients.

Neuroglobin (Ngb) is chiefly present in the central and peripheral nervous systems, where it may serve to supply oxygen to brain tissue [7]. Accumulating evidence indicates that Ngb exerts antiapoptotic and neuroprotective effects through the regulation of terminal oxidase activity, detoxifying reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, facilitating O2 diffusion to mitochondria and defending against oxidative stress [8, 9]. Numerous previous studies have shown that Ngb promotes neuronal survival under pathological conditions such as hypoxia, ischemia, oxidative stress, stroke, and neurodegenerative diseases [10–12]. Recently, multiple studies have reported that Ngb plays a crucial role in human cancers [13]. Previous reports have revealed that hypoxic conditions result in the upregulation of both Ngb mRNA and protein expression in human glioblastoma cells, shedding new light on how tumor cells to adapt to and survive in hypoxic microenvironments [14, 15]. Ngb expression is upregulated in the non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cancer tissues compared with normal tissues; moreover, hypoxic conditions induce the overexpression of Ngb in lung cancer cells [16]. Furthermore, Ngb plays a protective role in oestrogen receptor (ER) α-positive cancer cells, such as HepG2, HeLa, MCF-7, and SK-N-BE cells to prevent oxidative stress damage [17]. In DLD-1 colorectal carcinoma cells, ERβ-dependent Ngb overexpression also weakens 17β-oestradiol-induced apoptosis upon oxidative stress injury [18]. The upregulation of Ngb expression diminishes the responsiveness of MCF-7 breast cancer cells to paclitaxel treatment, decreasing their sensitivity to this therapeutic intervention [19]. However, Ngb has anticancer effects on hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) through the inhibition of oxidative stress and the arrest of cell cycle progression through the activation of the carcinogenic proteins Raf/MEK/Erk [20]. A prior study revealed Ngb in both a human astrocytoma cell line (U251) and a rat astrocytoma cell line (C6), as well as in human astrocytoma tissues [21]. However, the molecular mechanisms of Ngb and its role in human glioma are still not well understood.

Autophagy functions as a principal cytoplasmic degradation pathway responsible for delivering cytoplasmic macromolecules and damaged organelles to lysosomes, which are digested by double-membrane vesicles [22]. Autophagy can be either constitutive or in response to different degrees of stress. Autophagy not only maintains cellular homeostasis, but is involved in various conditions, including apoptosis, aging, and tumor growth [23]. The control of autophagy is complex and differs across various cancer cell types. In certain cancers, autophagy prevents tumor formation and promotes cell death, whereas in others, it it is a crucial factor that supports tumor growth and survival. Previous studies have demonstrated that the activation of autophagy plays a role in inhibiting the advancement of GBM in its early stages, while potentially promoting the proliferation of GBM cells in later stages. During the autophagic flux, various dynamic membrane reorganizations occur, starting with the lengthening and closing of phagophores to generate autophagosomes, which ultimately combine efficiently with lysosomes to produce autolysosomes. Research has indicated that Ngb is vital for increasing energy metabolism and initiating autophagy in human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells [24], indicating that Ngb might also be involved in autophagy in cancer cells.

Although many studies have investigated the function of autophagy in GBM, the pathological effect of Ngb on the regulation of autophagy in human GBM remains poorly understood [25–27]. In the present study, we found that the expression of Ngb was selectively downregulated in glioma cells and brain tissues, compared with normal astrocytes and normal brain tissues, respectively. Knockdown of Ngb promoted the growth, movement, and invasion of GBM cells, whereas increased Ngb expression had the reverse effect. More importantly, overexpression of Ngb promoted autophagy in the GBM cells through the EGFR/PI3K/AKT /mTOR signaling pathway. We specially determined the fragments of Ngb that bound EGFR and elucidated how Ngb/EGFR interactions activated its downstream cascades. Our results prove for the first time that Ngb-promoted autophagy inhibits the migration and invasion of tumor cells in the GBM.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

The Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology (Shanghai, China) provided the human glioblastoma cell lines such as LN-229, U87, U251, and SHG-44. Normal human astrocytes (NHAs, Cat. #CC2565) were acquired from the Lonza Group. The cells were all grown in DMEM (Servicebio, Cat. #G4515, Wuhan, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Cat. #16140089, USA) at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2/95% air in a humidified incubator. For the experiments involving starvation, the cells underwent two PBS rinses followed by a rinse with an amino acid-free medium (DMEM low glucose without amino acids (D9800, USBiological) supplemented with 4.5 g/L glucose, 0.1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 10% dialyzed FBS) and maintained in a starvation medium for the indicated times. For each starvation test, FBS was dialyzed against PBS with dialysis tubing with a 3,500 MW cut-off (SpectrumLabs, 132111) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The antibodies used were as follows. Anti-neuroglobin (#13499-1-AP), anti-Beclin1 (#11306-1-AP), anti-LC3 (#14600-1-AP) and anti-GAPDH (#10494-1-AP) antibodies were purchased from Proteintech Technology (Wuhan, China). Anti-p-Akt (Ser473) (#4060) and anti-Akt (#4685) antibodies were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Anti-EGFR (phospho Tyr1068) (#ab40815), anti-EGFR (#ab32198), anti-PI3Kinase p85 beta (phospho Y464) (ab138364), anti-PI3Kinase p85 beta (ab180967), anti-mTOR (phospho S2448) (ab109268) and anti-mTOR (ab32028) antibodies were obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Rapamycin (553211), bafilomycin A1 (BAF, B1793), erlotinib (SML3621), chloroquine diphosphate (CQ, C6628) and 3-methyladenine (3-MA, M9281) were purchased from Sigma‒Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The 0.1% crystal violet dye was purchased from Beyotime Biological Co., Ltd. (Beyotime Institute of Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Transwell chambers (#3422) and Matrigel (#356234) were purchased from Corning Lifesciences (Corning, NY, USA) and BD Biosciences, respectively.

Clinical specimens

We acquired tissues from six patients with grade IV glioma and nontumour brain tissues from six patients from the Department of Neurosurgery at the Central Hospital of Wuhan. All cases were confirmed by a pathologist, and the six nontumour brain tissues, which were collected during decompressive surgery, served as normal controls. Comprehensive clinical and pathological data were available for all the glioma samples. Brain tissues were sampled and preserved in liquid nitrogen for Western blot analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Central Hospital of Wuhan (approval number: WHZXKYL2024-10), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

TCGA and GTEx databases

Data from the TCGA database, which contained standardized gene transcript counts for 163 glioma cases and 5 types of paracarcinoma tissue, were accessed online. Clinicopathological data, including age at first diagnosis, sex, primary therapy outcome, IDH status, 1p/19q codeletion and histological type, were downloaded for analysis. Owing to the limited number of normal brain samples in the TCGA database, we downloaded 202 normal brain samples from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) database for combination with the TCGA data into a single group to improve the accuracy of our preliminary analysis. Data analysis and processing were conducted with R 4.2.2.

Stably Ngb knockdown and overexpression transfection plasmids

The lentiviral vector LV2-pGLV with Ngb shRNA or scramble nontargeting shRNA and the LV5 vector expressing Ngb were constructed by GenePharma Co. (Shanghai, China), which were confirmed through sequencing. The target sequence for human Ngb shRNA was 5’-GGTGATGCTCGTGATTGATGC-3’, and its knockdown efficiency has been previously confirmed [28]. LN-229 and SHG-44 cells were transfected with these lentiviral particles and then selected with puromycin (2 µg/ml) over a two-week period to generate stable cell lines with either Ngb knockdown or overexpression, as well as their corresponding control cell lines. The pEGFP-C1-Ngb wild-type and truncation constructs (△1–18, 1–35, 1–42, 106–151, 128–151, and 138–151) were generated in our laboratory as previously reported [29].

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from glioma cells, and quantitative real-time PCR was performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols and as detailed in our previous research [30]. The primers were synthesized as previously described [20].

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell growth was estimated by a CCK-8 assay (C0037, Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol and as previously described in our study [30].

Flow cytometry analysis

U87 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were plated at a density of 4 × 105 cells in 6-well plates. Glioma cells transduced with different vectors were prepared for flow cytometry analysis according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Analysis of apoptosis and the cell cycle was carried out with a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). For cell cycle assessment, a PI/RNase Staining Buffer Solution Cell Cycle Analysis Kit (#550825; BD Bioscience, USA) was used. To measure apoptosis rates, an Annexin V/PI kit (#C1062M; Beyotime, China) was used.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular or tissue protein was extracted with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After centrifugation at 14,000×g and 4 °C for 20 min, the proteins were collected and mixed with 5× loading buffer (#P0015, Beyotime, China) before being boiled for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) per lane were subjected to Western blot analysis. The proteins were separated and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (#IPVH00010; Millipore, Billerica, USA), which were then thoroughly washed three times, immersed in 5% nonfat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) and incubated with the abovementioned primary antibodies in TBST overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes in TBST, the blots were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase in TBST for 1 h at room temperature (1:10000). Following three washes in TBST, detection of the signals from the blots was performed with an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Pierce Biotech, Rockford, IL) and quantified by with NIH ImageJ 1.4.3 (Bethesda, MD, USA) after normalization to the loading control.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analysis

Cell lysates were prepared with 500 µg of protein with 1× IP buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. The samples were preabsorbed with 10 µl of protein A/G agarose beads (Beyotime Biotechnology) for 1 h at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation at 2500 rpm at 4 °C to remove nonspecifically bound proteins. The supernatant was then incubated with 2 µg of anti-Ngb antibody and gently rotated at 4 °C overnight. Next, 20 µl of protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads (Beyotime Biotechnology) was added, and the immunocomplexes were incubated for 4 h at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was thoroughly washed three times with IP buffer, mixed with 2× loading buffer and subjected to heating at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by immunoblot analysis.

Wound-healing and transwell assays

A wound-healing assay was carried out in 6-well cell culture plates (BD Bioscience, USA). The centre of each well was vertically scratched with a 200 µL pipette tip on the monolayer cells. The edges of the wounds were imaged, and the cells were then cultured in DMEM without FBS for an additional 24 h. Images were taken again at the same positions with a microscope. For the Transwell assays, the Transwell chamber was first immersed in complete medium for 2 h of hydration. GBM cells were collected, and their numbers were determined under a microscope; then, the cells were placed into the upper wells at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. DMEM supplemented with 0.1% BSA served as the medium in the chamber, and the lower chamber contained DMEM supplemented with 15% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, the chambers were removed, and the cells that had crossed the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The cells were then photographed with a microscope (Olympus, USA), and five random microscopic fields were observed. For invasion assays, the chambers were precoated with Matrigel (#356234; BD Biosciences), and the subsequent procedures were performed in a manner similar to that used for the migration assays.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

Cells transfected with either the vector or Ngb plasmids were fixed in a solution containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 30 min, followed by secondary fixation in osmium tetroxide (OsO4). The samples underwent dehydration through a graded sequence of ethanol solutions. The samples were subsequently embedded in capsules filled with embedding resin and maintained at 70 °C for approximately 9 h. Ultrathin sections of the samples were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The stained sections were examined via a transmission electron microscope (Tecnai G2 20 TWIN, FEI, Hillsboro, USA).

Glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay

The GST pull-down assay was conducted following methods described in earlier publications [20]. U87 cells were cotransfected with p-GST-EGFR and p-Ngb-EGFP-N1 wild-type or its truncated variants at a 1:1 ratio for 48 h. GST-lysis buffer was used to prepare cell lysates, and 400 µg of total soluble protein from each sample was incubated with 30 µl of glutathione sepharose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA) overnight at 4 °C with gentle mixing. Following thorough washing with GST-lysis buffer, the immunoprecipitated complexes were analysed by immunoblot analysis. Antibodies against GST, EGFR or Ngb were used to detect the respective proteins.

Immunohistochemical analysis and Immunofluorescence staining

Brain tissues embedded in paraffin were sliced into five-micrometer sections, deparaffinized with xylene, and rehydrated in graded alcohol, after which endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked. The sections were boiled in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min for antigen retrieval. Thereafter, the sections were incubated with primary anti-Ngb antibody at a 1:100 dilution in PBS at 4 °C overnight. The sections were subsequently treated with the appropriate biotinylated secondary antibody (DAKO, Denmark), developed with ABC reagent kits, and counterstained with Mayer’s haematoxylin. To perform immunofluorescence staining, U87 cells that had been stimulated were carefully rinsed with PBS and then fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 30 min at room temperature, incubated with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 30 min to achieve permeabilization, and subsequently blocked with 10% donkey serum for an additional 60 min. Overnight incubation of the cells at 4 °C was performed with primary antibodies against LC3 (# 14600-1-AP). Following three thorough washes with PBS containing 0.25% Tween-20, the cells were incubated with secondary antibodies for an hour at room temperature. Hoechst 33,342 was used to counterstain the nuclei. The cells were then immediately examined with an Olympus microscope (IX81, Olympus, Japan).

Evaluation of fluorescent LC3 puncta

Stably transfected U87 cells cultured on coverslips were transduced with mRFP-GFP-LC3 adenoviruses (Hanbio, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the cells were washed with PBS, starved in media without amino acids for 48 hours, treated with or without 50 µM CQ for an additional 2 hours, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, mounted with a reagent containing 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (HY-D0814A; MedChemExpress), and viewed under a confocal microscope (NCF950, Ningbo, China). Fluorescent puncta were manually counted to determine the number of GFP and mRFP dots in five fields from three separate glioma cell samples with a 60× objective. The nuclear count was determined by quantifying DAPI-stained nuclei within the same field. The number of dots per cell was calculated by dividing the total number of dots by the number of nuclei present in each microscopic field.

Tumor xenografts in nude mice

Cells stably transfected with parental U87 cells were adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 107 cells/ml. A total of 2 × 106 cells were suspended in 200 µl of PBS, and the cells containing either the vector or the Ngb overexpression plasmid were subcutaneously injected into the bilateral subcutaneous spaces of the mice. Concurrently, mice injected with vectors or Ngb overexpression plasmids were also intraperitoneally administered 3-MA. The tumor volume was first measured on Day 3, after which the tumour size was monitored every 3 days. To calculate tumour volume, the formula used was (tumour width)2 × length × 0.5. On the 45th day, tumour tissues were harvested for weighing or Western blot analysis.

Generation of stable cell line and orthotopic glioma mouse model

To establish the stable U87 cell line with Ngb overexpression and carrying luciferase, pLVX-CMV-luciferase-Ngb-puro was transfected with pMD2.G/ psPAX2 into 293T cells to produce lentiviruses. Subsequently, the U87 cell line was infected with the lentivirus for 48 h and selected using puromycin.

Four-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. U87 cells labelled with luciferase were intracranially injected into the brain (coordinates: 1 mm anterior and 2 mm lateral to the bregma at a depth of 3 mm). All mice were injected with 10 mg of D-Luciferin potassium (P1041, Promega, USA) via the caudal vein and images were captured by an IVIS (In Vivo Imaging System, Bruker Xtreme BI, Bruker, USA) on the 18 th day after tumor injection.

Evaluation of autophagy by acridine orange staining

The percentage of cells that exhibited positive staining with acridine orange was measured by flow cytometry. After treatment, the cells were trypsinized, collected, and then centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min. After the supernatant was removed, the cell pellets were reconstituted in PBS to achieve a concentration of approximately 1 × 108 cells/ml. To achieve a final concentration of 100 ng/ml, acridine orange (Sigma‒Aldrich, A6014, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in 1X PBS and stored in the dark until it was applied. For flow cytometry, 10 µL of acridine orange solution was added to each sample and mixed in the dark for 15 min. Acridine orange emits green fluorescence when excited at 525 nm and red fluorescence at 620 nm.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8 software was used to conduct the statistical analysis. The data are shown as the mean and standard deviation. Differences between groups were assessed by one-way ANOVA, and multiple comparisons among groups were analysed by Dunnett’s post hoc test. A p value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Overall survival curves were generated with the GlioVis portal (https://gliovis.bioinfo.cnio.es) based on data from the CGGA glioma cancer dataset.

Results

Ngb is downregulated in malignant gliobastoma cells

To assess the role of Ngb in glioma, we studied Ngb expression in glioblastoma cells. First, Western blot analysis revealed that the levels of Ngb protein in four human glioma cell lines (SHG-44, LN-229, U251, and U87) were significantly lower than those in normal human astroglial cells (NHAs) (Fig. 1A and B). Among these cell lines, SHG-44 had the highest level of Ngb expression, while U87 had the lowest expression. Therefore, in subsequent experiments, we knocked down Ngb expression in SHG-44 cells and overexpressed Ngb in U87 cells. Similarly, Western blot analysis revealed a notable reduction in Ngb expression in glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) tissues compared with normal brain tissues (Fig. 1C). Additionally, Ngb expression levels were assessed in glioma patients at different tumor stages and in normal brain tissue by immunohistochemical staining. The immunohistochemical analysis revealed that a progressive decrease in the expression level of Ngb correlated with the increasing grade of glioma (Fig. 1D). Further examination of Ngb expression was performed with the TCGA dataset combined with GTEx (163 glioma tissues and 207 normal tissues) and revealed that Ngb levels were significantly reduced in GBM tissues relative to those in normal brain tissues, with a correlation to WHO grade (Fig. 1E and F). Additionally, we evaluated the relationship between Ngb expression and the prognosis of GBM patients. The results demonstrated that higher Ngb expression was positively associated with better prognosis in glioma patients (p = 0, Fig. 1G). Analysis of the clinical data of TCGA (n = 669) revealed a significant association of histological type (p = 0.015) and 1p/19q codeletion (p = 0.002) with Ngb levels in glioma patients, but with no association with age, gender, primary therapy outcome, or IDH status (Table 1). Taken together, these findings suggest that Ngb exerts a tumor-suppressive function in GBM.

Effects of Ngb on autophagy in GBM cells

To determine whether Ngb regulates autophagy in GBM cells, we stably transfected U87 cells with an Ngb overexpression plasmid and cultured them under normal conditions for different durations (24, 48, and 72 h). Western blot analysis revealed that prolonged culture times (48 and 72 h) significantly increased the expression levels of Beclin1 and LC3I/II compared to those of the vector control group (Fig. 2A-D). Conversely, we stably transfected SHG-44 cells with an Ngb knockdown plasmid and subjected them to the same conditions. Western blot results revealed that Beclin1 expression was consistently downregulated at all time points, whereas LC3I/II expression was downregulated at 48 and 72 h (Fig. 2E-H). Autophagy flux was evaluated to determine whether the initiation of autophagy or the blockage of late-stage autophagy led to the accumulation of LC3B-II [31]. Initially, the use of chloroquine (CQ) to inhibit LC3B degradation in autolysosomes resulted in a significant decrease in autophagic flux in U87 vector cells compared with that in Ngb-overexpressing cells during extended amino acid starvation for 48 h (Fig. 2I and J). Additionally, U87 cells expressing tandem mCherry-eGFP-LC3B were used to observe autophagosome maturation, with autophagosomes appearing yellow and autolysosomes appearing red because of the quenching of eGFP fluorescence under acidic conditions. Compared with Ngb overexpressing cells, vector-containing cells presented a decreased proportion of autolysosomes (Fig. 2K, L and M), which implies that Ngb promotes in autophagosome maturation, confirming its participation in glioma cell autophagy. In summary, these findings reveal that Ngb promotes autophagy in GBM cell lines under normal culture and nutrient-deprived conditions, underscoring the crucial contribution of Ngb to the autophagy-mediated development and occurrence of glioma.

Ngb-promoted autophagy inhibits glioma cell proliferation and invasion

First, the U87-MG cell line was stably transfected with Ngb. Flow cytometry analysis of the cell cycle and apoptosis revealed that overexpression of Ngb significantly decreased the percentage of U87/Ngb cells in the S phase (29.48% vs. 47.35% of the vector control, 12 h) and promoted apoptosis in GBM cells (Fig. 3A-C). In addition, the results of the cell scratch assay revealed that the migration and invasion abilities of GBM cells were significantly inhibited by increased Ngb expression (Fig. 3D). However, when combined with 3-methyladenine (3-MA, 5 mM), an autophagy inhibitor, the inhibitory effects of Ngb on the proliferation, migration, and invasion of U87-MG cells were greatly reduced (Fig. 3A-D). These results indicate that Ngb inhibits GBM cell migration and invasion by promoting autophagy, thereby functioning as a tumour suppressor in GBM. Stable knockdown of Ngb in the SHG44 cell line was also performed. Flow cytometry analysis of the cell cycle and apoptosis revealed that knockdown of Ngb expression significantly increased the percentage of U87/sh-Ngb cells in the S phase (26.72% vs. 15.03% of the N-con control, 12 h) and inhibited GBM cell apoptosis (Fig. 3E-G). Moreover, the results of the cell scratch assay revealed that knocking down Ngb expression significantly increased the migration and invasion abilities of GBM cells (Fig. 3H). After the cells were incubated with rapamycin (100 nM), an autophagy-inducing agent, the effects of Ngb knockdown on SHG44 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion were impaired (Fig. 3E-H). These findings suggest that, rather than having a cytoprotective role, autophagy mediates the antitumour mechanism of Ngb.

Ngb promotes cell apoptosis and inhibits cell invasion upon starvation through increased autophagy

Owing to the pathological characteristics of ischaemic necrosis and the sensitivity of autophagy to ischemia in GBM cells, a cell model mimicking ischemia in vivo was constructed to investigate the effect of Ngb on autophagy under ischemic conditions. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that after 24 h of serum deprivation, the LC3 fluorescence density increased in the Ngb-overexpressing group. (Fig. 4A). Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the increase in the number of intracellular autophagosomes in Ngb-overexpressing GBM cells (Fig. 4B). Acridine orange staining, which was assessed by flow cytometry, was used to determine the number of acidic vesicles and revealed a significant increase in the number of acidic vesicles in the Ngb group compared with that in the control group after 24 h of serum deprivation treatment (Fig. 4C). These results indicate that under simulated ischaemic conditions in vitro, Ngb promotes autophagy in GBM cells, thereby exerting antitumour effects. Additionally, after 24 h of serum deprivation treatment, the cell cycle, apoptosis, migration, and invasion of GBM cells were assessed. Compared with the control group, the Ngb overexpression group had fewer S-phase GBM cells and exhibited the promotion of GBM cell apoptosis (Fig. 5A-D). Furthermore, 3-methyladenine (3-MA), an autophagy inhibitor, reduced the number of cells in the Ngb overexpression group that entered the G1 phase. Under the same treatment conditions, the apoptosis rate of GBM cells was inhibited by treatment with 3-methyladenine or bafilomycin A1 (100 nM, 24 h). After 24 h of serum deprivation treatment, the migration and invasion abilities of cells in the Ngb overexpression group significantly decreased. Nevertheless, combined treatment with 3-methyladenine or bafilomycin A1 reversed the reduction in GBM cell migration and invasion caused by Ngb (Fig. 5E-H). Collectively, these data indicate that Ngb inhibits the growth, migration, and invasion of GBM cells by increasing autophagy.

Ngb regulates the proliferation, migration and invasion of GBM cells through the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway

Because the PI3K/Akt signalling pathway plays a crucial role in promoting cell survival and inhibiting autophagy [32, 33], we further investigated whether Ngb regulates Akt-autophagy signalling in GBM cells. In U87-MG cells stably expressing Ngb, serum deprivation treatment was used to induce autophagy for 24 h. Western blot analysis revealed that overexpression of Ngb significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of EGFR Tyr1068, PI3K p85, Akt Ser473, and mTOR Ser2448 in serum-deprived GBM cells (Fig. 6). In summary, these data indicate that Ngb modulates the proliferation, migration, and invasion of GBM cells through the EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR signalling pathway.

Ngb interacts with EGFR to inhibit GBM growth

To further elucidate the potential mechanism through which Ngb regulates the EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR cascade, the interaction between Ngb and EGFR was investigated. In GBM wild-type cells with low Ngb expression, co-immunoprecipitation results revealed that stimulation with erlotinib, an EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitor, led to decreases in the expression levels of both endogenous EGFR and Ngb. Additionally, in GBM cell lines overexpressing Ngb, the expression levels of both EGFR and Ngb were assessed under the same treatment conditions, with both showing a more significant reduction, indicating that Ngb binds to EGFR to inhibit the growth and invasion of GBM cells (Fig. 7A and B). To further elucidate the interaction between Ngb and EGFR in GBM, full-length EGFR expression plasmids were constructed and cotransfected with different Ngb truncation fragments. The binding of the Ngb fragments to EGFR was detected with a glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay. Different truncation fragments of Ngb (Ngb Δ1–18, 1–35, 1–42, 106–151, 128–151, and 138–151) were constructed to investigate their binding to EGFR. The results revealed that wild-type Ngb could bind to EGFR, and the Ngb truncation fragments Δ1–42, Δ106–151, and Δ128–151 strongly bound to EGFR (Fig. 7C), suggesting that the functional domain of Ngb binding to EGFR may be located within these three segments. Subsequent experiments were conducted to investigate the binding status between Ngb truncations and endogenous EGFR. Coimmunoprecipitation analysis demonstrated that the Ngb truncation protein Δ1–42 had the strongest binding affinity for EGFR (Fig. 7D).

Ngb-promoted autophagy inhibits GBM growth in vivo

To further verify the anticancer effects of Ngb on GBM cell proliferation in vivo, nude mice were subcutaneously injected with equal quantities of control cells or GBM cells that stably overexpress Ngb. The tumor size was evaluated every three days and continuously monitored for 45 days. Compared with those in the control group, the tumors in the group stably overexpressing Ngb were significantly reduced in volume and weight, further corroborating the in vitro findings that Ngb inhibits GBM cell growth (Fig. 9A). Additionally, we also evaluated whether overexpressing Ngb impaired tumor growth in an orthotopic glioma mouse model, and our data revealed that Ngb clearly inhibited tumor growth in the mouse brain (Fig. 9D and E). Furthermore, 3-MA was administered via intraperitoneal injection to nude mice inoculated with GBM cells, and tumor size was continuously monitored. Compared with mice not receive 3-MA, those that received 3-MA had larger tumour volumes and weights, indicating that Ngb inhibits tumor cell growth through autophagy (Fig. 9B and C). Western blot analysis was employed to measure the protein expression levels of genes related to the EGFR/PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway and autophagy markers in the experimental group of GBM tumors. The findings demonstrated that Ngb significantly reduced the expression levels of p-EGFR Tyr1068, p-PI3K p85, p-Akt Ser473, and p-mTOR Ser2448, which was consistent with the in vitro experimental results. Moreover, treatment with 3-MA attenuated the effect of Ngb. Importantly, Ngb upregulated the expression levels of the autophagy-related proteins LC3 I/II and Beclin1. Notably, after treatment with 3-MA, the Ngb-mediated promotion of GBM cell autophagy was significantly inhibited (Fig. 9F and G).

Discussion

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is the most common malignant tumor of the central nervous system. Neuroglobin (Ngb) is a hexacoordinated haem protein predominantly expressed in the central and peripheral nervous systems. Upon its upregulation and subsequent localization to mitochondria, Ngb plays a protective role in promoting neuronal survival under oxidative stress. Accumulating evidence indicates that Ngb is involved in multiple types of cancer [34, 35]. Increased Ngb levels are regularly observed in multiple types of cancer, including NSCLC [16], bladder/thyroid tumors [15] and E-α receptor expressing breast cancer cells [36]. In addition, evidence has shown that Ngb overexpression in HCC cells results in tumor-suppressive effects, with Ngb playing an essential role in cell proliferation by linking O2/ROS signals to intracellular signals [20]. In pancreatic cancer, a previous study revealed that Ngb acts as a potential tumor suppressor by inhibiting the GNAI1/EGFR/AKT/ERK pathway, which is vital for cell growth and survival. Ngb expression is typically downregulated in patients with pancreatic cancer and is associated with poor outcomes and an older age. However, overexpressing Ngb can trigger cell cycle arrest and apoptosis and increase sensitivity to the EGFR inhibitor gefitinib [37]. Xiang and colleagues demonstrated that Ngb functions as a tumor suppressor in colorectal cancer (CRC), highlighting its diagnostic and therapeutic potential. Ngb expression is frequently downregulated in CRC because of hypermethylation, unlike in normal tissues. Overexpression of Ngb induces G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis, thereby decreasing CRC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro, and suppressing tumor growth and angiogenesis in vivo [38]. Therefore, it is evident that the biological function of Ngb varies across different types of cancer cells and tissues. To the best of our knowledge, few studies have investigated the clinical significance and the relationship between Ngb and autophagy in glioma. Therefore, a thorough examination of Ngb in gliomas may offer important insights into this lethal disease.

This study clarified the expression pattern of Ngb throughout the development of GBM and its ability to predict the prognosis of glioma patients. Additionally, the role and underlying mechanisms of Ngb in glioma cells were explored. Given that Beclin1 serves as a vital regulator of autophagy [39], we determined whether Ngb regulates autophagy by measuring the expression of Beclin1. Our findings indicate that Ngb increased autophagy in glioma cells, and that Ngb overexpression induced autophagy through the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/mTOR-dependent pathways. Although Ngb may play a crucial role in autophagy, the function of Ngb-mediated autophagy in glioblastoma has not been thoroughly examined. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to illustrate that Ngb modulates autophagy in glioblastoma.

Approaches that induce S phase arrest and apoptosis in glioma cells may be advantageous for the treatment of GBM [40]. Apoptosis represents a widespread form of programmed cell death observed in cancer cells [41]. According to our study, Ngb initiates apoptosis and S phase cell cycle arrest in glioma cells, as determined by flow cytometry analysis. Apoptosis occurs primarily through two pathways: the intrinsic pathway and the extrinsic pathway [41]. The intrinsic pathway, which is driven by mitochondria, involves the activation of caspase-9 and other caspases, such as caspase-3, -6, and − 7. Ngb binds to both oxygen and mitochondrial cytochrome C [42]. The interaction between Ngb and cytochrome C is modulated by hypoxic conditions; however, the precise mechanism under ischaemic conditions remains to be elucidated. Our results suggest that Ngb might play a role as a signalling molecule by converting oxygen signals into intracellular cascades that promote apoptosis. In summary, Ngb exerts its effects by activating the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, thus facilitating the release of cytochrome C from mitochondria. Additionally, the extrinsic apoptotic pathway involves the initial activation of caspase-8, which subsequently initiates a cascade of downstream signalling events. These two pathways ultimately lead to the cleavage of caspase-3, resulting in apoptosis. In the current study, overexpression of Ngb in U87 cells induced apoptosis in glioma cells, primarily through the activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway.

Previous studies have shown that autophagy exhibits both oncogenic and tumour-inhibitory properties during the progression of malignant transformation [43]. On the one hand, autophagy can suppress GBM by degrading carcinogenic metabolic substrates, toxic misfolded proteins, and damaged organelles and by inducing autophagic cell death [44]. On the other hand, it can also maintain mitochondrial function and support metabolism by recycling intracellular substrates, thereby promoting tumour growth [45]. It is widely believed that tumour cells are resistant to extreme stress conditions, particularly in response to the limited availability of nutrients and oxygen within the tumour microenvironment. These conditions are especially severe in the central region of solid tumours, where autophagy levels are markedly increased. These findings suggest that in some contexts, autophagy might be an adaptive tool for tumours, enabling them to survive and proliferate despite unfavourable conditions. Some studies suggest that the involvement of autophagy in tumorigenesis is shaped by genetic and tissue-specific factors, emphasizing the need to understand its disputed role in GBM. Autophagy is suppressed by the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway. The role of Ngb in inhibiting glioma development via the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/mTOR autophagy pathway remains to be elucidated. Therefore, this study sought to determine whether the regulation of autophagy by Ngb is mediated through the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling cascade.

EGFR and the signalling pathways it mediates play crucial physiological roles. Upon the binding of specific ligands to the extracellular domain of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), signal transduction is initiated. The phosphorylated tyrosine residues of EGFR create docking sites for various downstream effectors, which are involved in the activation and regulation of signal transduction cascades, including those involving PI3K/AKT, JAK/STAT, and RAS/MAPK [46]. The EGFR–mTOR pathway, which regulates autophagy, plays a pivotal role in cancer progression. According to Wei et al., EGFR can inhibit autophagy through a mechanism that does not depend on mTOR and involves interaction with Beclin1 [47]. De Iuliis V et al. proposed that EGFR suppresses autophagy by interacting with Beclin1 during the early stages of starvation. Following this, the activation of EGFR results in its phosphorylation, leading to the release of Beclin1 and the initiation of autophagy [48]. The physical interaction of Ngb with EGFR to inactivate the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pathway in an mTOR-dependent manner, as well as whether Ngb increases the dissociation of Beclin1 from EGFR and upregulates Beclin1 in GBM to promote autophagy, require further investigation.

We elucidated the detailed mechanism of Ngb–EGFR interactions during ischaemia-induced autophagy. Ngb and EGFR demonstrated direct binding, which was intensified under conditions of nutrient deprivation. The sequential truncation of the Ngb protein indicated that the binding of Ngb to EGFR predominantly depended on the integrity of the Ngb 1–42 segment. Additional deletion of the Ngb 1–42 amino acid sequence significantly reduced the interaction between Ngb and EGFR. Overexpression of the Ngb 1–42 amino acid sequence not only markedly increased the interactions between Ngb and EGFR but also potentiated Ngb-induced inactivation of the PI3K/AKT pathway in glioma cells. Therefore, the interaction between Ngb and EGFR is essential for PI3K/AKT inactivation under ischaemic conditions. Further investigations are needed to determine whether a single mutation in the oxygen-binding site (H64L) and Gαi-binding sites (E53Q and E118Q) of Ngb affects Ngb-EGFR binding under nutrient-deprived conditions. NgbH64L may stabilize Ngb in its oxygenated state, thereby increasing its binding to EGFR. In line with the increased EGFR binding, NgbH64L may also increase PI3K/AKT inactivation and autophagy activation. This evidence collectively supports the notion that Ngb promotes autophagy through the coupling of PI3K/AKT inactivation in ischaemic glioma cells.

Our data proved that Ngb promoted autophagy by specifically inhibiting the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Autophagy plays a crucial role in various pathological conditions; however, its relationship with apoptosis remains a subject of debate [44]. Studies have confirmed that autophagy occurs before apoptosis and is a prerequisite for apoptosis. Autophagy induces cell death through the downregulation of oncogene expression and the activation of pro-apoptotic factors, thereby inhibiting tumour development [49]. However, autophagy does not induce cell death; instead, it functions to attenuate apoptosis by establishing cellular conditions that favour survival. Autophagy maintains endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondrial function to limit apoptosis [50]. Radiotherapeutic and chemotherapeutic resistances are two of the most prevalent issues in cancer treatment, and autophagy plays a significant role in this process by helping tumour cells evade radio/chemotherapy-induced apoptosis. Additionally, autophagy supplies nutrients to cells in hypoxic or nutrient-depleted environments, protecting glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) cells from death. Autophagy and apoptosis may have either a conflicting or supportive relationship on the basis of the stimuli and conditions within the cell. These processes can occur simultaneously and collaborate to cause cell death. Furthermore, apoptosis can expedite the progression of cells towards autophagic cell death [45].

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that neuroglobin (Ngb) modulates the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/mTOR signalling pathway and influences cell death in glioblastoma multiforme (GBM). Ngb directly interacts with the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), thereby inhibiting the phosphorylation of proteins in the EGFR/PI3K/AKT/mTOR cascade. This inhibition effectively suppresses the PI3K/AKT signalling pathway, thereby reducing the proliferation, migration, and invasion of human glioblastoma cells through increased autophagy. Our study highlights the role of Ngb as a critical regulator of glioma progression and provides novel insights for the development of therapeutic interventions.
