Feasibility and Acceptability of Brief Behavioral Treatment for Insomnia After Eating Disorder Treatment: The TIRED Study
Authors: Kara A. Christensen Pacella, Angeline R. Bottera, Kelsie T. Forbush, Jaelin Isquith, Kyle De Young
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe purpose of this pilot trial was to evaluate the novel use of Brief Behavioral Treatment for Insomnia (BBTI) for people with residual insomnia disorder following eating disorder (ED) treatment.MethodsParticipants (N = 6) completed a multiple‐baseline design study of BBTI. The primary outcome was insomnia severity, measured by the Insomnia Severity Index. Secondary outcomes included sleep efficiency and daytime fatigue. Exploratory outcomes were ED‐related impairment and symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and depression symptoms.ResultsBBTI reduced insomnia symptom severity from baseline to end of treatment. BBTI also resulted in improvements in sleep efficiency and reductions in daytime fatigue. ED‐related and other psychopathology outcomes largely did not change.DiscussionBBTI showed preliminary evidence of efficacy, acceptability, and feasibility among people with residual insomnia following ED treatment. Future studies should use randomized controlled designs to better elicit the effects of BBTI on ED‐related outcomes.

Summary Although insomnia commonly persists following treatment for eating disorders, few studies have examined the application of evidence‐based insomnia treatments among this population.This multiple‐baseline design study found that Brief Behavioral Treatment for Insomnia reduced insomnia symptoms and was highly acceptable and feasible to administer.Findings support the need for large‐scale trials of insomnia treatment for people with eating disorders.

Introduction

Insomnia disorder is characterized by difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking early in the morning, despite adequate opportunities to sleep (American Psychiatric Association 2022). Insomnia may demonstrate a bidirectional relationship with eating disorder (ED) symptoms (Christensen and Short 2021). In fact, insomnia is more common among people with EDs (Allison et al. 2016; Padez‐Vieira and Afonso 2016), with one study finding that 66% of college students with probable EDs reported clinically significant insomnia symptoms (Christensen Pacella et al. 2024).

Both sleep–wake and eating behaviors are strongly influenced by circadian rhythms; individuals with insomnia and EDs both demonstrate significant disruptions to their circadian rhythms that impact when they feel sleepy versus alert or hungry versus sated (Lack et al. 2017; Menculini et al. 2019). The biobehavioral circadian model of restrictive and binge eating posits that circadian rhythm disruptions are linked to appetitive disruptions through mechanisms such as disrupting regular eating cycles and metabolic functioning (De Young and Bottera 2022). At the same time, appetitive disruptions, such as eating later in the day or meal skipping, may increase circadian disruptions (Challet 2019). Among those with ED vulnerabilities, appetitive disruptions may increase risk for binge eating and restrictive eating, which then further disrupt circadian rhythms. Consequently, treating insomnia symptoms has the potential to reduce both sleep and ED difficulties.

Treating Residual Insomnia Symptoms Following ED Treatment

There are limited data about insomnia's natural course over ED treatment; however, in a recent study, insomnia symptoms decreased following ED treatment, but the magnitude of this change was small (d = −0.38) and not as substantive as that of ED outcomes (d = −2.01) (Christensen Pacella et al. 2025). Further, of the approximately 50% of individuals in the study who reported clinically significant insomnia before treatment, less than half experienced insomnia symptom remission at end‐of‐treatment. These findings suggest that residual insomnia after ED treatment is common, highlighting the necessity of identifying best practices to address these symptoms. Additionally, although ED treatment may reduce some ED‐specific factors that may contribute to insomnia, such as diurnally shifted meal timing, malnutrition, or late‐night exercise, ED treatment may not target insomnia‐specific maintaining mechanisms (Hertenstein et al. 2022), necessitating specialized insomnia treatments to reduce residual insomnia. Failure to treat residual insomnia may increase the risk of ED relapse, as observed among common ED comorbidities, such as depression and anxiety (e.g., Chen et al. 2017; Manber et al. 2008). Therefore, it is necessary to evaluate treatments for residual insomnia symptoms among people recovering from EDs.

The TIRED Study: Treating Insomnia in People Recovering From EDs

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends cognitive‐behavioral approaches as first‐line treatments for insomnia disorder (Schutte‐Rodin et al. 2008) as they are highly efficacious, have minimal side effect profiles (van Straten et al. 2018), and are durable, with improvements frequently maintained even one year post‐treatment (Van Der Zweerde et al. 2019). Insomnia treatment may also simultaneously reduce symptoms of depression (Ho et al. 2020) and anxiety disorders (Taylor and Pruiksma 2014), which are highly comorbid with both insomnia (Khurshid 2018) and EDs (Hambleton et al. 2022). Thus, insomnia treatments may offer a parsimonious transdiagnostic approach to reduce maintaining relations among sleep, eating, and internalizing pathologies. Although the efficacy of psychological treatments for insomnia is well‐established among people with common ED comorbidities such as depression, anxiety, and post‐traumatic stress disorder (Hertenstein et al. 2022), there is limited knowledge of the efficacy of insomnia treatment among people with EDs.

For this study, participants completed Brief Behavioral Treatment for Insomnia (BBTI) (Buysse et al. 2011; Troxel et al. 2012), which is an empirically supported treatment for insomnia disorder. BBTI, although similar to CBT‐I, differs in a few components that make it suitable as a follow‐up to ED treatment. Although both treatments involve psychoeducation and implementing stimulus control and sleep restriction, BBTI maintains a focus on behavioral processes (see session outlines in Figure S1). By contrast, CBT‐I includes cognitive mechanisms, such as thought restructuring, which are covered in ED treatments, such as CBT‐E (Fairburn 2008). Consequently, BBTI has a shorter treatment duration (four sessions over four weeks) and shorter session lengths (20–75 min), which was deemed potentially more feasible. BBTI was developed for providers who are not sleep‐medicine experts and uses a standardized treatment manual, which promotes its feasibility for use among ED treatment providers.

We used a single‐case multiple‐baseline design (MBD) to test the initial feasibility, acceptability, and efficacy of BBTI for future large‐scale clinical trials. MBDs are optimal when an intervention reversal (e.g., A‐B‐A design) would be difficult to use (Kazdin 2020). MBDs allow each subject to act as their own “control” by providing data during a baseline and intervention phase. Additionally, the treatment effect can be replicated across individuals, which strengthens the reliability of causal inferences of treatment effects (Kazdin 2020). Compared to group‐based research designs, fewer participants are required, which is an advantage when evaluating new treatments (Kazdin 2020). Although MBDs allow researchers to maximize external and internal validity for small samples, these designs are not used frequently in ED research, despite calls for their implementation (De Young and Bottera 2018; Melles et al. 2025). Consequently, this study provides an innovative and methodologically rigorous step forward in single‐case research in EDs.

We examined levels of ED‐related impairment, ED behaviors, and symptoms of depression and anxiety at pretreatment, posttreatment, and 1‐ to 3‐month follow‐ups.

Methods

Participants

Six individuals with residual insomnia disorder completed the study after discharging from a guided self‐help cognitive‐behavior therapy for EDs protocol called BEST‐U (Bohrer et al. 2023; Forbush et al. 2023). Two additional participants were ruled ineligible after intake due to symptoms consistent with undiagnosed sleep apnea (n = 1) and delayed sleep‐phase disorder (n = 1). Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

BEST‐U inclusion criteria required participants to be university students over the age of 18 with a non‐low weight ED and without (1) significant psychiatric comorbidity that interfered with treatment; (2) medical instability; (3) moderate‐to‐severe suicidal ideation; (4) a body mass index < 19.0 kg/m2; (5) medical conditions that affected eating behavior or body weight, such as Type I diabetes mellitus; or (6) atypical anorexia nervosa.

For this trial, participants were required to meet DSM‐5 criteria for insomnia disorder (American Psychiatric Association 2013) after completing BEST‐U. We excluded participants with conditions for which BBTI would be contraindicated (e.g., bipolar disorder, seizure disorders, untreated obstructive sleep apnea; Troxel et al. 2012) and those with symptoms consistent with other untreated sleep disorders.

Procedure

Eligibility Assessment

Potential participants completed the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) at the conclusion of BEST‐U. Those scoring ten or higher‐indicating potential insomnia disorder per the recommended psychometric cutoff from Morin et al. (2011)‐ were invited to complete an eligibility assessment via Zoom that consisted of the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐5 Sleep Disorders‐Revised (SCISD‐R) (Pruiksma et al. 2020), an assessment of ED pathology, and baseline questionnaires. The eligibility assessment and BBTI were conducted by the first author (K.A.C.P., Participants 1–3) or second author (A.R.B., Participant 4–6). Both authors had completed supervised training in insomnia treatment and provided care under the supervision of the third author (K.T.F.).

Intervention Protocol

After the eligibility assessment, enrolled participants completed a minimum baseline period of three weeks, during which they completed the ISI and an adapted version of the Eating Pathology Symptom Inventory (EPSI) weekly. ISI scores were visually inspected after Week 3 and, if necessary, at Weeks 4 and 5 of the baseline. Participants who demonstrated insomnia severity stability, as indicated by similar scores on the ISI across the baseline period or an increasing severity trend (i.e., a slope greater than or equal to 0), were moved to the intervention phase.

Participants then began four consecutive weeks of BBTI (see protocol components in Figure S1). Although typically providers deliver BBTI via two in‐person sessions and two phone sessions, this trial was initiated during the COVID‐19 pandemic, so all procedures were conducted remotely using a secure telehealth platform. Research suggests that digital insomnia treatment is equally effective to in‐person treatment (Hasan et al. 2022).

Participants completed daily sleep diaries and the weekly ISI and EPSI through REDCap. To enhance the accuracy of data, particularly the sleep diaries, participants had 24 h to complete each survey. Data from the sleep diaries were examined at each session to refine sleep restriction and titration recommendations.

Following completion of BBTI, participants completed an end‐of‐treatment survey to evaluate progress on sleep, ED, and other psychopathology indices, as well as acceptability of treatment. They were emailed follow‐up surveys at 1 and 3 months post‐BBTI completion to evaluate stability of treatment effects. Participants were paid for completion of the weekly surveys and follow‐ups, but not sleep diaries or attending BBTI sessions.

Instruments

Given the small sample size, Cronbach's α was not calculated for measures (Rouquette and Falissard 2011). See Table S1 for assessment schedule.

Sleep Measures

Flinders Fatigue Scale (FFS) (Gradisar et al. 2007)

The FFS is a seven‐item measure that assesses daytime fatigue resulting from insomnia. The FFS has shown sensitivity to changes resulting from insomnia treatment, discriminant validity from measures capturing daytime sleepiness, and excellent internal reliability (Gradisar et al. 2007).

Insomnity Severity Index (ISI) (Bastien et al. 2001)

The ISI is a seven‐item measure that assesses past‐month insomnia severity and impairment. We used a modified version to assess past‐week insomnia symptoms. Psychometric studies found adequate internal reliability and concurrent validity for this measure (Bastien et al. 2001). Receiver operator curve analysis identified a score of 10 as an optimal cutoff for detecting insomnia in a community sample (Morin et al. 2011).

Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐5 Sleep Disorders‐Revised (SCISD‐R) (Pruiksma et al. 2020)

The SCISD‐R is a semi‐structured interview for assessing DSM‐5 sleep disorders. The SCISD‐R has shown excellent interrater reliability for diagnosing insomnia disorder (Pruiksma et al. 2020) and was used to confirm the insomnia disorder diagnosis.

Sleep Diaries

Participants completed daily sleep diaries, indicating the time they got in bed for the evening, sleep onset latency, total time awake after initial sleep onset (wake after sleep onset; WASO), the time they woke up in the morning, the time they got out of bed in the morning, sleep quality, caffeine use, and napping behaviors (frequency, duration) for the previous day. Time‐in‐bed (TIB) was derived from the participant‐reported time of getting in and out of bed. We calculated total sleep time (TST) by using the total amount of sleep from the time of sleep initiation to reported morning wakeup time minus WASO. Finally, we calculated our secondary outcome of sleep efficiency by dividing TST by TIB and multiplying by 100.

Psychopathology Measures

Clinical Impairment Assessment (CIA) (Bohn and Fairburn 2008)

The CIA is a 16‐item measure that assesses the impact of ED symptoms on psychosocial outcomes, with higher scores indicating greater impairment (Reas et al. 2016). A score of 16 or above is typically used to indicate clinically significant ED pathology (Bohn and Fairburn 2008).

Eating Pathology Symptoms Inventory (EPSI) (Forbush et al. 2013)

The EPSI is a 45‐item measure that assesses ED symptoms over the past month. For the purposes of this study, we used a weekly adaptation. The monthly version of the EPSI has demonstrated good convergent validity with other ED assessment tools and divergent validity from anxiety and depressive symptoms (Forbush et al. 2013). For this study, scores on the Binge Eating, Excessive Exercise, Restricting, and Purging scales were used as exploratory outcomes.

Inventory of Depression and Anxiety Symptoms‐II (IDAS‐II): General Depression Subscale (Watson and O'Hara 2017)

The General Depression subscale of the IDAS‐II consists of 20 questions, with higher scores indicating greater depression symptoms. The IDAS‐II has demonstrated good internal consistency, temporal stability, and construct validity (Watson et al. 2012).

Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7) (Spitzer et al. 2006)

The GAD‐7 is a seven‐item measure that assesses symptoms of generalized anxiety disorder, with higher scores indicating greater symptoms. The GAD‐7 has demonstrated good convergent validity (Spitzer et al. 2006).

Other Measures

Demographics Questionnaire

We used the demographics questionnaire administered in the parent trial to assess age, gender, race, and ethnicity.

Treatment Evaluation Questionnaire

This measure was developed by our team to assess acceptability. Participants used a 7‐point Likert scale to rate treatment acceptability, satisfaction with improvements in sleep, and plans to implement strategies in the future.

Registered Report

The University of Kansas Institutional Review Board (#00145995) approved this study. The study protocol was published as a registered report in the International Journal of Eating Disorders (Christensen et al. 2021) and preregistration materials can be found at https://osf.io/v54wh. We report the following deviations from the original registration: (1) instead of using a composite measure of ED behaviors as a secondary outcome, we reported each behavior separately to allow the reader to understand the granular effects of BBTI on different ED symptoms; (2) due to missing data resulting from difficulties using the wearable sleep trackers, we derived the sleep efficiency parameter from the participants' sleep diaries. Reliance on sleep diaries is standard practice in BBTI.

Data Analysis

For the primary outcome of insomnia symptom severity, we used the scdhlm web application (Pustejovsky et al. 2023) to calculate treatment effects with between‐case standardized mean differences (BC‐SMD). BC‐SMD effect sizes have similar interpretability to Cohen's d. The baseline phase was assumed to have a level time trend with case‐level random effects, whereas the treatment phase was modeled with changes in linear trend with fixed effects of level and linear trend and options for case‐level random effects for level and linear trend (Table S2). We prioritized more parsimonious models while considering model fit using AIC and BIC and model convergence.

We used the Simulation Model Assessment (SMA) Program with level, trend, and autocorrelation factors to simulate 5000 models based on each case to evaluate the significance of changes in insomnia severity at the participant level (Borckardt 2006; Borckardt et al. 2008). Phase vector selection was determined through visual inspection and a priori hypotheses (i.e., baseline as stable or increasing, treatment phase as decreasing).

For the secondary outcomes, we computed BC‐SMD to evaluate the effects of BBTI on sleep efficiency. BC‐SMD was not calculated for daytime fatigue because participants did not complete this measure on a weekly basis. Instead, we used paired t tests to examine differences in baseline and end‐of‐treatment, 1‐month follow‐up, and 3‐month follow‐up for daytime fatigue.

For the exploratory analyses, we examined stability in each ED behavior during the baseline period to guide interpretability and computed BC‐SMD. We used paired t tests to examine differences between baseline and end‐of‐treatment, 1‐month follow‐up, and 3‐month follow‐up for ED‐related impairment, anxiety, and depression symptoms.

Results

Treatment Engagement

All participants completed all four BBTI sessions. Participants completed all baseline, end‐of‐treatment, 1‐month follow‐up, and 3‐month follow‐up surveys, and 95.8% of weekly surveys. Completion of sleep diaries was also excellent (M = 94.66%, range = 76.79–100%).

Primary Outcome: Insomnia Symptom Severity

Baseline to End‐of‐Treatment Effects

The BC‐SMD for insomnia at end‐of‐treatment was −1.89 (SE = 0.78, 95% CI = −3.78, −0.01), which indicated a large significant decrease in insomnia symptom severity at the group level. Five out of six participants reported ISI scores below 10 at end‐of‐treatment, suggesting remission of clinically significant insomnia. Subject‐level data are graphed in Figure 1. Baseline to Follow‐Up Period Effects

The BC‐SMD for follow‐ups was −1.57 (SE = 0.78, 95% CI = −3.50, 0.35), which is a large effect; however, because the confidence interval contained zero, this effect was not considered significant. Five out of six participants reported ISI scores below 10 at both follow‐up assessments, suggesting that remission of clinically significant insomnia was maintained over time. Because the BC‐SMD was not significant, we did not report simulation analyses to limit Type I errors.

Secondary Outcomes: Sleep Efficiency and Daytime Fatigue

The BC‐SMD for sleep efficiency at end‐of‐treatment was 1.63 (SE = 0.45, 95% CI = 0.69, 2.56), which indicated a large significant increase in sleep efficiency. Mean sleep efficiency before treatment was 85.45% (SD = 6.14) and during treatment was 93.61% (SD = 1.17). All clients reported sleep efficiencies of greater than 90% at end‐of‐treatment, suggesting that clients had succeeded in sleep consolidation objectives. Sleep efficiency was not calculated at follow‐ups as participants were no longer completing sleep diaries after treatment ended. Daytime fatigue significantly decreased from baseline to end‐of‐treatment (t(5) = 3.01, p = 0.03, d = −1.23) and remained below initial levels at 1‐month (t(5) = 7.46, p < 0.001, d = 3.05) and 3‐month follow‐up (t(5) = 2.89, p = 0.03, d = 1.18). Exploratory Outcomes

For three ED behaviors, there was either no stability during the baseline phase (i.e., excessive exercise, restriction) or a reduction (i.e., binge eating), which limited the interpretability of the findings. Overall, all confidence intervals for reported BC‐SMDs included zero, suggesting no change in ED symptoms during or after BBTI. Only one participant reported purging during the study; therefore, BC‐SMD was not reported (see Table 3 note). ED‐related clinical impairment significantly decreased from baseline to end‐of‐treatment and remained below initial levels at 1‐month follow‐up. However, on average, impairment returned to baseline levels at 3‐month follow‐up. At baseline, three participants were above the CIA cutoff score of 16, which indicated probable clinically significant ED pathology. At 3‐month follow‐up, two of these participants remained above the cutoff. Generalized anxiety symptoms did not significantly change throughout the treatment. Depression symptoms significantly decreased at end‐of‐treatment and remained lower at 1‐month follow‐up; however, they returned to baseline levels at 3‐month follow‐up. Acceptability and Feasibility

Participants rated the BBTI program highly (Table 5). Participants found logistical aspects of the program, including time spent in session, time answering surveys, ease of answering sleep diaries through REDCap, ease with scheduling with their coach, amount of coaching, enjoyment of participation, and length of the program, acceptable. Participants also endorsed multiple indices of change/improvement, including changes in sleep behaviors and habits, coaching's impact on sleep, improved sleep quality, and learning new information about sleep. Finally, participants also reported confidence and trust in the program, as indicated by willingness to return to the program if they needed future sleep treatment, willingness to recommend the program, pride in changes made during the treatment, and plans to continue using information from the program.

Qualitative data provided information about strengths and potential modifications. Two participants identified the sleep coaching (e.g., the sessions with the provider) as the best part of the program. Two participants noted favorable aspects related to the ability to personalize the program, including building a custom “sleep prescription” and the ability to pace themselves in adjusting their sleep. Two participants noted the efficiency of the program.

Participants had relatively few suggestions for intervention improvements. Two participants left the response blank and two replied that they had no suggested changes. One participant suggested the treatment could be longer but noted that they felt the length of time was “just enough” for them to feel resolution with their sleep problems. One participant suggested including a 3‐month check‐in visit.

Discussion

This was the first study to test the preliminary efficacy of BBTI for insomnia symptoms among people recovering from an ED. Overall, results suggested that BBTI was efficacious, feasible, and acceptable for treating insomnia symptoms in this population. BBTI also had significant impacts on the secondary outcomes of fatigue and sleep efficiency at the end of treatment. Findings for exploratory analyses were mixed; although ED‐related impairment decreased from baseline to the end‐of‐treatment, these effects were not maintained at follow‐up, and other exploratory outcomes largely did not show significant changes. Critically, participants had already completed a course of ED treatment prior to BBTI, which may have limited the opportunity to observe further improvements in some ED outcomes. However, four out of six participants reported CIA scores below the clinical cutoff at the 3‐month follow‐up, suggesting maintenance of recovery from EDs.

BBTI Showed Preliminary Efficacy, Acceptability, and Feasibility

Our findings are consistent with a large body of evidence suggesting that behavioral interventions for insomnia are efficacious among people with comorbidities (Hertenstein et al. 2022). In this study, BBTI significantly decreased insomnia symptom severity between the treatment and baseline phases, with large effects at the end‐of‐treatment and at the follow‐ups; furthermore, five out of six participants reported final ISI scores below 10, which is commonly used as a clinical cutoff for probable insomnia disorder. However, despite a large effect size at follow‐up time points, the large confidence interval contained zero. Such large confidence intervals are relatively common in small samples, which allows readers to see the variability in types of outcomes achieved and highlights the potential to determine for whom treatment may be most effective. Thus, these findings should not be interpreted as suggesting that effects of BBTI are temporary, particularly given meta‐analytic evidence suggesting that behavioral treatment for insomnia has moderate effects on insomnia at 3‐month follow‐up (Van Der Zweerde et al. 2019). Furthermore, we used an intent‐to‐treat analytic approach; however, it is important to note that Participant 4, who remained above the ISI cutoff after BBTI, reported work scheduling restrictions that may have kept them from achieving the full effects of sleep restriction/titration. Future research in larger samples is required to determine the longevity of BBTI effects in this population.

In terms of acceptability and feasibility, participants provided highly favorable evaluations and had relatively few suggestions for improvements to the treatment. Although findings related to engagement must be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size and use of a payment incentive structure for completing weekly and follow‐up surveys, there were no study dropouts and all participants completed the four sessions of BBTI. Similarly, even though the study did not incentivize completing sleep diaries, this sample had a mean completion rate of about 95%. In part, our high compliance rates may be attributed to participation in ED treatment prior to BBTI, which normalized daily logging of eating behaviors and weekly survey completion and may have primed higher compliance during BBTI. It is also possible that individuals who are willing to complete insomnia treatment after ED treatment are particularly motivated. Taken together, these findings support the idea that BBTI is an acceptable and feasible intervention option for this population.

Effects of BBTI on ED‐Related Outcomes and Other Psychopathology

Interpretation of BBTI effects on ED behaviors should be cautious because it was not necessary nor feasible to establish stability in each type of ED behavior during baseline. However, we did not see effects of BBTI on ED behaviors. This is not entirely unsurprising, as participants began BBTI after completing ED treatment and 50% had already achieved ED recovery according to the CIA cutoff score. It is possible that there were floor effects, limiting continued improvement in symptom change. However, there also were not significant increases in ED behaviors, suggesting that ED treatment benefits were maintained without relapse. Indeed, four out of six participants had CIA scores below the clinical cutoff at the 3‐month follow‐up, compared to three out of six at baseline. Although ED‐related impairment decreased from baseline to end‐of‐treatment and remained lower at the 1‐month follow‐up, it was not statistically significantly different from baseline levels at the 3‐month follow‐up. Taken together, findings point to the possibility that BBTI may have protective effects against ED relapse. Findings should also be considered with potential participant outliers. Although Participant 1 fully engaged with BBTI, they exhibited persistently elevated ED and internalizing pathology that was much higher than the other participants, which may have limited improvement. As mentioned before, Participant 4 reported difficulty engaging with the active ingredients of BBTI due to work schedules. Future studies should use larger samples in controlled designs to evaluate the longitudinal effects of BBTI on ED outcomes by comparing individuals with residual insomnia who do or do not receive BBTI after ED treatment.

BBTI did not show significant effects on generalized anxiety disorder or depressive symptoms, with the exception that depression was significantly lower at 1‐month follow‐up than at baseline. These findings were not consistent with meta‐analytic work on the effects of insomnia treatments on comorbid internalizing symptoms (Gee et al. 2019; Ye et al. 2015) or a study that found a sleep intervention for residual insomnia following adequate pharmacotherapy for depression decreased depression (Wing et al. 2018). Ultimately, it is unclear if the findings from this study reflect small or negligible effects of BBTI on internalizing symptoms or better represent a protective mechanism by which individuals were less likely to experience relapse.

Adaptation of BBTI for Populations With EDs

We did not make any ED‐specific adaptations to BBTI; however, directly addressing problematic sleep and eating behaviors as mutual maintaining processes may enhance outcomes. BBTI and ED treatment approaches are highly compatible and feasible for integration. BBTI encourages individuals to wake at the same time every day and establish a regular sleep schedule, which is tracked through daily sleep diaries. This is consistent with ED interventions such as logging meals and maintaining “regular eating.” Taken together, an integrated approach may emphasize maintaining predictable daily rhythms for eating and sleep. Additionally, it would be highly feasible to incorporate elements of ED relapse prevention into BBTI. For example, clinicians could also use regular sleep patterns to inquire about and support regular eating, such as planning mealtimes alongside the proposed sleep schedule. Finally, clinicians could identify and target problematic sleep behaviors that reinforce ED pathology (e.g., using sleep to avoid eating or hunger) and evaluate if ED‐related thoughts interfere with engagement with BBTI components.

Limitations and Strengths

Limitations include the risk of Type I errors, which were increased due to the number of inferential tests. However, all tests were preregistered, and we report subject‐level data and analytic parameters for reproducibility. Additionally, despite gender and racial representation in the sample, the generalizability across other subject characteristics is unknown and can only be formally evaluated using tests of moderation in larger group‐based research. Further, we did not collect sleep diary information at follow‐up, impeding our ability to assess whether improvements in sleep efficiency were maintained in the months following treatment. Finally, the inability to use objective measurements of sleep (e.g., actigraphy) due to substantial missing data exposes some outcome variables to the influence of unreliability and bias that can permeate self‐report. For example, when self‐reporting their sleep, individuals with insomnia tend to underestimate their time spent sleeping and their sleep efficiency (Harvey and Tang 2012) and overestimate the time it takes them to fall asleep initially and when awake during the night (Mercer et al. 2002). This phenomenon is referred to as sleep–wake state discrepancy. However, a recent clinical trial for insomnia demonstrated that sleep–wake state discrepancy did not impact the efficacy of treatment (Moulder et al. 2024), and subjective measures of sleep can provide important information on the impact of sleep disturbances missed by objective measurement (Christensen Pacella et al. 2024).

There were also several notable strengths to the study. Single‐case experimental designs are severely underused in ED research; instead, case series are often conducted as uncontrolled trials, which makes understanding potential causal relationships more challenging (De Young and Bottera 2018; Melles et al. 2025). By establishing stability with insomnia symptoms prior to starting BBTI, we were able to increase our confidence in interpreting observed effects as being driven by the intervention and not merely regression toward the mean or other time effects. Second, we evaluated a comprehensive set of outcomes, allowing for a deeper exploration into potential multi‐faceted effects of this intervention. Third, ED treatment research has frequently been limited by low enrollment of men and members of racial and ethnic minority groups (Burnette et al. 2022). In this study, we had equal representation of men and women, and two‐thirds of the sample belonged to a minoritized racial group.

Conclusions

This is the first study to examine the efficacy of BBTI in treating residual insomnia following ED treatment. Due to its acceptability, feasibility, and preliminary efficacy, BBTI for residual insomnia warrants further large‐scale study, particularly, on ED relapse risk. Future iterations of BBTI for populations with EDs may benefit from tailored adaptations to concurrently regulate sleeping and eating behaviors.
