Exploring Novel Data-Driven Clustering Methods for Uncovering Patterns in Longitudinal Neonatal Postoperative Temperature Measurements
Authors: Stephanie M Helman, Nathan T Riek, Susan M Sereika, Ahmad P Tafti, Robert Olsen, J William Gaynor, Amy Jo Lisanti, Salah S Al-Zaiti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify distinct postoperative temperature trajectories in neonates with congenital heart defects after cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), using advanced unsupervised machine learning clustering methods, corroborate findings, and evaluate their prognostic value on outcomes.Patients and MethodsA secondary cohort analysis of prospective data collected from a single pediatric referral center’s CardioAccess data registry, consistent of neonates who underwent CPB between January 1, 2015, and January 1, 2019, was performed. Postoperative temperatures were extracted from medical records (48 hours). Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) performance was compared with self-organizing maps (SOM) and k-means clustering. Cluster membership and model fit were optimized for 3 temperature clusters per method. The primary outcome was a composite of postoperative complications. Clustering techniques were compared and associated with outcomes using adjusted multivariable binary logistic regression.ResultsNeonates of ≥34 weeks’ gestation underwent CPB (N=450). GBTM, SOM, and k-means identified membership for 3 groups: (1) persistent hypothermia (n=38 [9%]; n=49 [11%]; and n=40 [9%], respectively); (2) resolving hypothermia (n=233 [51%]; n=227 [50%]; and n=147 [33%], respectively); and (3) normothermia (n=179 [40%]; n=174 [39%]; and n=263 [58%], respectively). Concordance between techniques found strong agreement between GBTM and SOM (κ=0.92) and weak agreement between GBTM and k-means (κ=0.41). After adjustment, persistently hypothermic neonates compared with normothermic neonates were associated with higher odds of the complication composite outcome in the GBTM (odds ratio [OR], 2.8; 95% CI, 1.0-7.3; P=.04) and SOM (OR, 2.3; 95% CI, 1.0-5.4; P=.04) models, but not in the k-means model (OR, 1.4; 95% CI, 0.7-3.1; P=.38).ConclusionExploring concordance between different machine learning techniques shows that temperature in neonates after CPB follows unique trajectories. Those exhibiting persistent hypothermia trends are at higher risk of adverse outcomes.

Clinicians across diverse care environments have been inundated with advancements in bedside technologies over the last several decades. Intelligent decision support systems are an example of technology development that is evolving at a rapid pace.1,2 Digitization of the electronic health record (EHR), thereby capturing and storing massive quantities of patient data, is one of the primary drivers for such progression. These advantages make it possible for interprofessional research teams to leverage these data for visualization, pattern recognition, and predictive modeling.3 Tools can support clinicians in recognizing important signals about their patients’ health status.

Researchers must explore and account for the complex temporal interactions in multidimensional data by using unsupervised machine learning (ML) methods such as group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM), self-organizing map (SOM), and k-means clustering to reveal hidden patterns in unexplored data. Evidence suggests that physiologically meaningful and distinguishable clusters can be derived from repeated vital sign measurements in critically ill populations.4 It is important to compare multiple data-driven techniques for corroboration and validation to ensure clinically meaningful interpretation. We previously used GBTM to discover underlying patterns in postoperative neonatal longitudinal temperatures. We found that neonates who remained persistently hypothermic for 48 hours after cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) were more likely to experience serious postoperative complications compared with normothermic neonates.5

Disruption of autonomic thermoregulation can occur in hospitalized individuals when heat loss (because of environmental exposure) is far greater than heat production. When this imbalance occurs, hypothermia results. Hypothermia is generally defined as a body temperature <36.5 °C.6, 7, 8 The purpose of this study was to use 2 new data-driven approaches, SOM and k-means clustering, to reveal hidden patterns in longitudinal temperature data collected in postoperative neonates with congenital heart defects (CHDs) after their first CPB; to explore the concordance between these sophisticated ML techniques and GBTM; and to examine whether temperature group assignment has prognostic value for assessing odds of complications.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

This was a secondary cohort analysis of prospectively collected data from 1 free-standing children’s hospital. A local CardioAccess repository leveraged locally stored data collected for the Society of Thoracic Surgeons and Pediatric Cardiac Critical Care Consortium (PC4)9 registries. We also accessed EHR flowsheet rows comprising temperature documentation, temperature probe site, and any artificial heat source use (mode, set temperature [goal], and skin temperature). This study was approved by the overseeing data governance committee and data coordinating center institutional review board.

Sample and Setting

Study Sample

We included consecutive neonates admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit (ICU) between January 1, 2015, and January 1, 2019, with CHD ≥34 weeks’ gestation who had completed their first CPB. Exclusion criteria were neonates <34 weeks’ gestation (extreme prematurity) and those requiring extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support after CPB. Neonates with sparse temperature recordings (n=5)10 or nonphysiologic temperature measurements of <32 °C and >44 °C (n=0) were also excluded.4,11 Our final sample size comprised 450 neonates.

Setting

This study took place at a single, free-standing pediatric referral center where approximately 100-180 neonatal CPB surgeries are performed annually. The cardiac ICU has approximately 36 beds and cares for critically ill surgical and medical patients with CHD across the lifespan. This study was conducted between Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (data provision) and the University of Pittsburgh (dataset development and analysis).

Sample Size Justification

There are no reported standards about the sample size necessary for SOM or k-means clustering. For k-means, some researchers suggest an arbitrary sample size of 500 to ensure adequate clustering. However, others emphasize that the dimensionality of the data is most important for finding cluster structures. Thus, it is highly recommended that the minimal sample size is no less than 2k cases, where k is the number of variables.12

Variables

Independent Variable

The primary independent variable is longitudinal neonatal temperature after CPB. We collected all rectal and skin temperature data from the EHR from postoperative hours 0 to 48. The temperatures obtained from EHR documentation were used to derive distinct temperature trajectories/clusters. Given that temperatures were collected and documented on time-varying intervals, we used hourly bins to compute mean temperature per window, which yielded 48 sequential temperature readings per neonate. There were, on average, 1.78 windows missing per neonate. Missing windows mostly occurred systematically between hours 41 and 48 when care in the cardiac ICU is de-escalated. We used linear interpolation to impute hourly windows with no temperature readings when surrounding temperatures were available, and missing hourly windows at the beginning or end of the 48 hours were extrapolated using mean patient temperature.

Dependent Variables

The primary study outcomes have been previously defined in detail.5 All outcomes were dichotomized. If outcomes were predefined in existing literature, we used the established binarized definition. If continuous outcomes were not defined, we identified the upper quartile of the continuous distribution and used that cutoff to dichotomize the variable.5,13,14 Postoperative outcomes occurring during the first 48 hours after surgery included the following: (1) excessive bleeding, (2) cardiac arrest, (3) cardiac arrhythmias, and (4) illness severity (vasoactive inotrope score ≥ 20).5,13,14 Outcomes occurring after the 48-hour postoperative period included the following: (1) delayed first successful extubation,5,15,16 (2) prolonged cardiac ICU length of stay (LOS), (3) very poor weight gain (change in age and sex Z-score normalized weight over the hospitalization of ≤ −2),5,17, 18, 19, 20 (4) 30-day mortality, and (5) a composite complication that incorporated having at least 1 of the outcomes described above.

Statistical Analysis

SOM and K-Means Clusters Were Used as Complimentary Clustering Methods for Longitudinal Temperature Data

Self-organizing map is an unsupervised ML approach and is primarily used for data visualization, feature extraction, clustering, and classification. There are no predetermined labels, and the model segments the data into groups by inferring patterns. The underlying structure of the SOM is an artificial neural network. For this study, a 3 × 1 grid was used to create three clusters. The initial learning rate was set to 0.001, and the SOM was trained for 500,000 iterations. The learning rate followed a decay function such that at iteration t, lr(t)=lr/(1+t/T), where T is the number of iterations divided by 2. Sigma was initially set to 0.5 and represents the spread of the neighborhood function. At iteration t, sigma(t) = sigma/(1+t/T), where T is the number of iterations divided by 2.

K-means clustering is another unsupervised ML clustering method frequently applied to health care data to classify disease phenotypes, especially in sepsis research.21, 22, 23, 24 Interestingly, these sepsis studies used temperature measurements as a feature to influence the derivation of unique clusters. K-means clustering iteratively partitions data in k-clusters by assigning data point means that are closest to the distance metric of a given cluster. For this study, we first started with a simple dendrogram tree to describe the arrangement of how clusters are produced. We used a partitioning algorithm based on k-means clustering for longitudinal data to identify the distribution of optimal clustering across all data time points. We chose the number of clusters a priori. Driven by prior research,5,25,26 we enforced 3 clusters that we hypothesized would correspond to persistent hypothermia, resolving hypothermia, and normothermia groups. Because longitudinal data were used, 1 of the distance metrics was set to dynamic time warping, an algorithm used to quantify the similarity between 2 temporal sequences.27 In this case, the 2 temporal sequences (2 patients’ 48-hour temperature windows) are the input data and the cluster center. The maximum number of iterations was set at 50. K-means can stop early if the inertia is less than a set threshold for 2 consecutive iterations. Inertia is the sum of the distances from each of the data points to their nearest cluster center. Inertia was set to 1×10−6. To ensure the adequacy of our approach, we repeated all calculations with varying numbers of clusters and evaluated the results regarding relevant criteria (stability). The final performance of these clusters in predicting study outcomes was used as a criterion for the solution quality. A grid search was also used to find the best hyperparameters for both SOM and k-means. Python version 3.11.5 was used for SOM and k-means cluster model building (Supplemental Appendix 1, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Evaluate Independent Clinical Characteristics of SOM and K-Means Cluster/Group Assignment Using Multivariate Multinomial Logistic Regression

Demographic and clinical characteristics significant at the univariate level were entered into a multivariate multinominal logistic regression (LR) model to identify independent clinical characteristics of cluster/group assignment. Clincial characteristics were thoroughly evaluated for multicollinearity. For clincial characteristicss significant in multivariate analysis, we reported odds ratios (ORs) and CIs. This analysis was performed using SAS software, version 9.4. (SAS Institute Inc).

Evaluate Complication Associations With SOM and K-Means Cluster/Group Assignment Using Adjusted Multivariable Binary LR

In each multivariable binary LR model, we used SOM and k-means cluster/group assignment as the primary independent variable(s), adjusting for significant clinical correlates of group assignment and other variables that were found to significantly correlate with a given outcome in the univariate analysis (P<.10). Collinearity diagnostics included tolerance (1−R2), variance inflation factor (inverse of tolerance >10), condition index (>30), and assessment of variance proportions between 2 variables (>0.5). In the case of collinearity, the decision for variable inclusion was based on which variable was more clinically meaningful. For predictors significant in the multivariable model, we reported ORs and CIs. The adequacy of each multivariable LR model was assessed using the Hosmer and Lemeshow test. Regression models were developed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM Corp).

Post Hoc Analysis

In post hoc sensitivity analyses, we evaluated the associations of predictors on complications. We assessed the difference between regression models blocked with persistent hypothermia (G1) assignment alone, and then again with the resolving hypothermia (G2) combined. This exercise was conducted to confirm that the addition of SOM and k-means group 2 did not change SOM and k-means group 1 significant associations with complications. The χ2 test results are reported for both SOM and k-means.

Compare Performance and Concordance Between GBTM, SOM, and K-Means Clustering Models Using McNemar and κ Statistical Tests

The intent of these clusters/groups is classification; their final performance in predicting study outcomes was used as a criterion for the solution quality. The McNemar test to compare GBTM against SOM and k-means group assignment was conducted as well. This test indicated whether overall GBTM group assignment was statistically different than the SOM and k-means classification. We also conducted a κ test to assess the level of agreement between 2 categorical variables (GBTM and SOM; and GBTM and k-means); κ is a test statistic that measures interrater agreement or the agreement between >2 raters or methods.

Both race and biological sex were analyzed as clinical characteristics that could influence outcome prediction. Neither race nor biological sex was significantly associated with any of the outcomes outlined in this study, and therefore they were not used in the adjusted multivariable binary LR models. Race was collapsed as a White and non-White categorical variable due to sparseness within the subcategories of race provided by the data registry. Original race categories defined by the PC4 data registry were White, Black, Asian, Native American, Native Pacific Islander, and other. Biological sex was handled as a dichotomous variable and was defined as male and female.

SOM Temperature Group Assignment

In the final sample of 450 neonates, SOM identified group membership and model fit was optimized for 3 temperature groups. Three temperature groups showed very distinct trajectory patterns (Figure 1). Group 1 identified 49 neonates (11%) who arrived to the cardiac ICU with low temperatures and persistently stayed below 36.5 °C for the 48-hour evaluation period (persistent hypothermia; G1). Group 2 identified 227 neonates (50%) who arrived to the cardiac ICU with low temperatures, but their hypothermia resolved during the early hours of postoperative recovery (resolving hypothermia; G2). Group 3 identified 174 neonates (39%) who arrived to the cardiac ICU with normal temperatures and maintained their normothermia for the duration of the study (normothermia; G3).

Postoperative self-organizing map neonatal temperature trajectories. The Euclidean distance for a 3-group cluster was 1173.09, and for a 4-group cluster, it was 1141.48. The Euclidean distance metric was used for model adequacy and tends to decrease as you add more clusters, eventually reaching zero when the number of clusters equals the number of patients.

SOM Group Assignment and Association With Clinical Characteristics and Complications

Independent clinical characteristics associated with SOM persistent hypothermia group assignment are reported in Supplemental Table 1 (available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/). In an adjusted multivariable binary LR (Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 2, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/), G1 group assignment was an independent and significant predictor of postoperative cardiac arrest (OR, 11.3; 95% CI, 2.7-48.0; P<.001), delayed first successful extubation (OR, 6.1; 95% CI, 2.0-19.0; P=.002), longer stay in the cardiac ICU (OR, 3.5; 95% CI, 1.7-7.4; P<.001), very poor weight gain (OR, 3.3; 95% CI, 1.3-8.7; P=.01), and 30-day mortality (OR, 5.8; 95% CI, 1.6-21.0; P=.008). Neonates in G1 were also at higher odds of experiencing the composite complication (OR, 2.3; 95% CI, 1.0-5.4; P=.04), after adjusting for potential confounders. Neonates in G1 did not experience higher odds of severe bleeding or postoperative arrhythmias. Neonates in G2 did not experience higher odds of any of the complications or study outcomes. The adequacy of each adjusted multivariable binary LR model was assessed using the Hosmer and Lemeshow test (Supplemental Table 3, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

Post Hoc Analysis

Associations of predictors for each complication found that the addition of the k-means resolving hypothermia group did change the k-means persistent hypothermia group significant associations with cardiac arrest, 30-day mortality, delayed first successful extubation, and very poor weight gain complications (Supplemental Table 8, available online at https://www.mcpdigitalhealth.org/).

McNemar and κ Test Results

Using the McNemar test to compare GBTM and SOM group assignment, the test indicated that overall group assignment was statistically different between the 2 classification techniques (χ2=13.8(2); P<.001). Using the McNemar test to compare GBTM and k-means group assignment, the test indicated that overall group assignment was statistically different between the 2 classification techniques (χ2=75.3(3); P<.001). However, when evaluating GBTM and SOM resolving hypothermia groups individually, the McNemar test found that the groups were not statistically different (P=.26). Similarly, when evaluating the GBTM and k-means persistent hypothermia groups individually, the McNemar test found that the groups were not statistically different (P=.88). Furthermore, the κ test result for GBTM and SOM group assignment was 0.92, indicating strong agreement beyond chance between classification methods. The κ test result for GBTM and k-means clustering assignment was 0.41, indicating weak to moderate agreement between classification methods.

Discussion

This study found that GBTM and SOM group assignment agreement was significantly better than GBTM and k-means cluster/group assignment agreement. Neonates in the SOM G1 experienced higher odds of cardiac arrest, 30-day mortality, delayed first successful extubation, longer cardiac ICU LOS, very poor weight gain, and having at least 1 postoperative complication (composite outcome) comapred to neonates in SOM G3. Neonates assigned to SOM G1 did not have higher odds of severe bleeding or postoperative arrhythmia than those in SOM G3. These results are similar to those found when using GBTM group assignment as the independent variable in our previous multivariable binary LR models.5 The only difference was that GBTM G1 assignment was significantly associated with having higher odds of postoperative arrhythmia and SOM G1 assignment was not.

Next, k-means clustering G1 and G2 were both independent predictors of postoperative cardiac arrest, postoperative arrhythmia, and longer cardiac ICU LOS. Neonates assigned to k-means G2 were at higher odds of very poor weight gain than those assigned to k-means G3. The k-means G1 and G2 groups were not independent predictors of severe bleeding, 30-day mortality, delayed first successful extubation, or having at least 1 postoperative complication (composite outcome). These results were different from those found using GBTM group assignment as the independent variable in our multivariable binary LR models.5 Mainly, GBTM G2 was not predictive of any postoperative complications.

When compared with 2 different unsupervised ML clustering methods, GBTM performance was shown to be a robust method for finding distinct groups of neonates when measuring 1 phenomenon (temperature) over time. The intent of the SOM groups and k-means clusters was classification, and the final performance of the clusters/groups was to predict study outcomes and compare results to GBTM outcome prediction. The SOM and GBTM group assignment showed strong agreement, and outcome predictions were remarkably similar, which is significant because these methods are extremely different methodologically. GBTM is a parametric method, whereas SOM is derived from an artificial neural network.

GBTM discovers distinct developmental temporal patterns evolving over time to describe a phenomenon in complex longitudinal datasets.28,29 This method is unsupervised, meaning we do not know the outcome. GBTM uses the same variable and the same subject over time to capture the connectedness of behavior and how variables relate. Comparing results of more than 1 data-driven method with our GBTM results supports the idea that the GBTM classification findings were not found by chance alone but because of unique underlying hidden patterns in our longitudinal data. The fact that SOM found similar patterns as GBTM corroborates our findings, which identified novel temperature trajectories in neonates after CPB, and may have important clinical implications.

Interestingly, the GBTM and k-means classification methods shared a weak to moderate agreement. K-means clustering split the neonates with complications between the resolving hypothermia and persistent hypothermia groups. K-means did not separate patients with persistent hypothermia into 1 group like GBTM and SOM. This could be because GBTM and SOM are known to handle complex and highly dimensional data. Importantly, SOM can visualize high-dimensional data at lower dimensions without disrupting the initial structure of the data.20,30 K-means may have also not been the strongest modeling technique because it performs better when the clusters are of similar size.23 Our groups were very different in size across all 3 methods. Further, k-means is a clustering approach that aims to minimize variance within clusters. SOM is an unsupervised ML method that learns from all time points, simultaneously allowing the model to learn shape and time series patterns among the temperature trajectories. Although k-means is a more straightforward method, SOM is a more holistic approach. Our analysis serves as an important example supporting the use of a team science approach in ML research, with clinicians and data scientists ensuring that the most fitting ML algorithm is chosen based on the clinical data set identified for analysis. If an inappropriate ML method is chosen, then there is a risk for incorrect interpretation of data with erroneous clinical implications.

Our study has limitations. One major limitation is using race alone without socioeconomic and structural drivers. Future study designs should include more meaningful variables such as insurance type, Yost index,31 or validated neighborhood deprivation indices. Future research should also include additional clinical sites to expand sample diversity and representativeness. This study adds to the current body of literature focused on intelligent decision support systems and health care predictive analytics. Specifically, valuable ML applications are used to reveal clinically important and hidden patterns that will otherwise go unseen by clinicians because of the massive quantity of patient data that are generated by health care systems. If the aim of clinical research is to change or improve practice, we need to make sure our predictive models generalize and can stand on their own in data that the models have never seen before.

Conclusion

After testing 3 different data-driven and complimentary clustering/grouping methods, 2 methods corroborated that mild to moderate doses of hypothermia (depth) persisting over longer periods (duration) share significant associations with postoperative complications in neonates with CHD. Evaluating multiple ML clustering approaches is warranted to identify clinically meaningful clusters that are substantiated by various techniques. Our study’s findings have potentially identified a new area of focus—persistent hypothermia prevention to reduce the rate of postoperative complications. Definitions of hypothermia have the potential to become data-driven instead of consensus based and take both nadir value and persistence into account. This study highlights the conceivable impact of unintentional postoperative hypothermia depth and duration on prognostic significance in the CHD neonatal population.
