Characteristics and causes of ocular trauma – A retrospective, multi-center study
Authors: Nasser A. Alsabaani, Ibrahim A. Al Zehefa, Ahmed S. Al Zomia, Mohammed A. Al-Amri, Abdullah J. Korkoman, Ziyad S. Aldawh, Osama H. Alalmaie, Ahmed A. Alamoud, Saeed S. Alqahtani, Ashwaq Y. Asiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:Ocular trauma remains a significant public health concern worldwide, with regional variations in its incidence and characteristics. This study aimed to examine the trends and features of ocular trauma in the Asser region of Saudi Arabia, where limited data currently exist.METHODS:A retrospective study was conducted using medical records from patients treated in the Ophthalmology Departments of three hospitals in the Asser region between January 1, 2010, and December 30, 2022. The collected data included demographics, injury details, symptoms, treatment timing, hospitalization, interventions, and visual outcomes. Descriptive statistics were used for analysis.RESULTS:A total of 148 patients were included in the study. Open globe injuries were the most common (66.3%), with penetrating injuries being the most frequent subtype (55.4%). Closed globe injuries (contusions) accounted for 33.8% of cases. Most injuries occurred at home (40.5%) or during transportation (34.5%), with driving being the most frequently reported circumstance (36.5%). Common symptoms were pain (64.9%), reduced visual acuity (54.1%), and redness (18.2%). Notably, 98.0% of patients did not wear protective eyewear. Regarding treatment timing, 43.2% received care within 12 h, while 31.8% waited over 24 h. Hospitalization was required in 75.7% of cases, with 7.4% involving orbital fractures. The most frequent interventions included primary corneal closure and globe repair. Visual outcomes showed temporary impairment in 46.6% of patients, no impairment in 45.3%, and permanent impairment in 8.1%.CONCLUSION:This study highlights the distinct patterns of ocular trauma in the Asser region, providing essential data for improving preventive strategies and healthcare responses. Further research is necessary to reduce ocular trauma incidence in similar settings.

INTRODUCTION

Ocular trauma is one of the leading causes of morbidity and blindness worldwide.[1] It affects nearly 500,000 individuals and often requires surgical intervention, placing a significant burden on healthcare systems and society.[2] The impact of ocular trauma is frequently underestimated, partly because such injuries often affect only one eye and are underreported in epidemiological data.[2] Studies from low-and middle-income countries have identified ocular injuries as a major contributor to the socioeconomic burden in these populations.[3]

The World Health Organization estimates that ocular trauma affects approximately 55 million people annually, accounting for 5% to 16% of ophthalmology consultations.[4] A study based on the Global Burden of Disease indicated a decline in both incidence and burden of ocular trauma between 1990 and 2019, with males experiencing higher rates of incidence and years lived with disability (YLDs). Young and middle-aged adults were found to bear the greatest burden. Regionally, Australasia recorded the highest age-standardized YLD rates per 100,000 population, with New Zealand reporting the highest national burden.[5]

Despite advancements in socioeconomic conditions, lifestyle changes, and industrial growth, the risk of eye injuries has increased. Without timely and appropriate treatment, ocular trauma can lead to severe consequences, including substantial economic costs. Therefore, a thorough understanding of the epidemiological patterns of ocular trauma is essential for developing effective prevention strategies.[67] There is a lack of epidemiological data on ocular trauma in Saudi Arabia, which prompted us to investigate the pattern of ocular trauma in the Aseer region.

These facilities were selected due to their high patient volume and comprehensive ophthalmology services, ensuring a representative sample of ocular trauma cases in the region.

Study population

The study included all patients, regardless of their age, who were hospitalized due to ocular or orbital trauma within the predefined study duration. Patients with incomplete or insufficient medical records and those not admitted primarily for ocular or orbital trauma were excluded from the analysis.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was sought and obtained from the Saudi Ministry of Health to ensure that the study adhered to ethical principles and patient privacy regulations. Anonymization techniques were used to protect patient identities and maintain confidentiality.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted to provide a comprehensive summary of both demographic and clinical characteristics within the study cohort. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations. We used the R software for conducting statistical analysis.

The majority of injuries were penetrating injuries (55.4%), followed by contusion or closed-globe injuries (33.8%). Rupture injuries (6.8%) and perforating injuries (4.1%) were relatively uncommon. The most common locations where ocular injuries occurred were home (40.5%) and transportation-related settings (34.5%), together accounting for about three-quarters of all cases.

Injuries at work (18.9%) and sports or recreational activities (2.7%) were less frequent. The most frequent circumstances were driving (36.5%), followed by moving or walking (13.5%), working (11.5%), and playing (8.8%). Violence-related incidents accounted for 6.8%, while other circumstances (23.0%) made up nearly a quarter of the cases [Table 2].

Among the patients, an overwhelming majority (98.0%) were not using protective eyewear at the time of the trauma. The interval between injury and primary surgical intervention varied: 43.2% received treatment within 12 h, while 31.8% experienced a delay of more than 24 h. The distribution of injury laterality showed that 50.7% of cases involved the right eye, 42.6% the left eye, and 6.8% had bilateral involvement. Hospitalization was required in 75.7% of cases, and 7.4% were found to have associated orbital fractures. General anesthesia was administered in 70.9% of patients. The most common surgical interventions were primary corneal closure and globe repair, each performed in 10.1% of cases. Regarding visual outcomes, 46.6% of patients experienced temporary visual impairment, 45.3% had no visual impairment, and 8.1% suffered from permanent vision loss [Table 4].

DISCUSSION

This multi-center retrospective study provides a critical analysis of the characteristics and causes of ocular trauma in the Aseer region, revealing a significant public health challenge marked by a high frequency of severe injuries and clear, preventable risk factors. The demographic profile of our cohort, which is overwhelmingly young and male, aligns with the well-documented global epidemiology of ocular trauma, a pattern consistently reported in studies from diverse settings, including Libya, Malaysia, and Nigeria.[1212] This demographic is frequently involved in a spectrum of high-risk activities, from professional duties to recreational pursuits, which inherently elevates their exposure to potential eye hazards and solidifies their status as the primary target audience for any preventive strategy.[891011]

A central and concerning finding is the predominance of vision-threatening open globe injuries. The severity of these injury types underscores the high-energy or sharp-force mechanisms involved and immediately suggests a grave prognosis if not managed urgently and effectively. This pattern is particularly alarming when considered alongside the data on the circumstances of injury. The fact that a substantial proportion of these severe traumas occur not in controlled industrial settings but in domestic environments and during routine activities like driving indicates a widespread and potentially underestimated risk perception among the general population. This is critically compounded by the finding that an overwhelming 98% of patients were not using any form of protective eyewear at the time of their injury. This stark statistic points to a profound deficit in public awareness and a cultural norm that does not yet prioritize routine eye safety, representing the most significant opportunity for prevention.[131415]

The management timeline of these injuries reveals another layer of complexity. The finding that nearly a third of patients experienced a delay of more than 24 h before receiving primary surgical repair is clinically significant and echoes challenges noted in other developing contexts, such as Tanzania.[3] Such delays can exacerbate the initial damage, increase the risk of devastating complications like endophthalmitis, and ultimately compromise visual recovery, turning a potentially salvageable situation into one resulting in permanent disability. This highlights a critical area for healthcare system strengthening, suggesting a need to optimize emergency referral pathways and improve logistical coordination to ensure timely access to specialist care.

The clinical and personal consequences of these incidents are profound. While a portion of patients recover fully or experience temporary impairment, the subset who sustain permanent vision loss faces a lifelong disability with associated personal, social, and economic ramifications. The trajectory from a momentary accident to a permanent visual disability underscores the long-term societal burden imposed by ocular trauma, a burden that extends beyond the individual to the healthcare system and society at large.[45]

Implications for public health and clinical practice

In light of these findings, the imperative for a coordinated and multifaceted response is clear. Public health initiatives must be launched to cultivate a culture of safety, specifically promoting the habitual use of protective eyewear during a wide range of activities, from household chores and DIY projects to sports and driving. Simultaneously, occupational safety standards require rigorous enforcement and expansion, particularly in professions identified as high-risk, such as military service and manual labor, where the use of certified safety glasses should be non-negotiable. Within the healthcare sector, efforts must be directed towards streamlining patient pathways from initial emergency presentation to definitive ophthalmic surgical intervention, ensuring that delays do not contribute to negative outcomes. Ultimately, redirecting efforts towards robust prevention is not only a medical necessity but also a socioeconomic imperative to alleviate the preventable burden of blindness and visual impairment on individuals and the community as a whole.[67]

Strengths and limitations

This study provides valuable insights into the pattern of ocular trauma in the Aseer region; however, its findings must be interpreted considering its strengths and limitations. A key strength is its multi-center design, incorporating data from three major hospitals, including a military facility, which enhances the representativeness of the sample and captures a broader spectrum of injury circumstances than a single-center study would allow. Furthermore, the application of the standardized BETT system for classifying injuries improves the clinical accuracy and international comparability of our data.

Notwithstanding these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of the study introduces a potential for bias, as it relies on the accuracy and completeness of pre-existing medical records. Second, the analysis was restricted to hospitalized patients, which may lead to an overestimation of injury severity and limit the generalizability of our findings to less severe cases managed in outpatient settings. Finally, while this study describes the initial management and short-term visual outcomes, it does not assess long-term functional results or the impact on patients’ quality of life, which are crucial areas for future prospective research.

CONCLUSION

This study establishes that ocular trauma in the Aseer region predominantly affects young males and is characterized by a high frequency of severe, vision-threatening open globe injuries. The prevalence of these injuries during routine activities such as driving and domestic tasks, compounded by an almost universal lack of protective eyewear use, underscores a critical public health gap. Significant treatment delays further exacerbate visual outcomes. These findings highlight an urgent need for targeted public health initiatives focused on preventive education, the promotion of protective eyewear, and the strengthening of emergency ocular care pathways to mitigate the substantial and often preventable burden of vision loss.
