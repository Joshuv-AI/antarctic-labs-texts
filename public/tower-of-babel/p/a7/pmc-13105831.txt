Combined Robotic Morgagni‐Larrey Hernia Repair and Lobectomy for Lung Cancer
Authors: Alfonso Fiorelli, Salvatore Tolone, Francesca Capasso, Vincenzo Di Filippo, Alessia Guarino, Francesco Coppolino, Ludovico Docimo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHerein, we reported a combined robotic surgery approach used to manage pre‐existing ipsilateral Morgagni‐Larrey hernia and early‐stage lung cancer concurrently. The reduction of the colon into the abdominal cavity facilitated robotic lobectomy and prevented life‐threatening postoperative complications from the presence of a hernia after lobectomy.

Introduction

Herein, we reported a combined robotic surgery approach used to manage pre‐existing ipsilateral Morgagni‐Larrey hernia and early‐stage lung cancer concurrently to facilitate lobectomy and to prevent life‐threatening postoperative complications from the presence of hernia after lobectomy.

Case Description

A 75‐year‐old morbidly obese woman with a history of cardiac disease and COPD and with an unknown preexisting right Morgagni‐Larrey was referred to our hospital for management of early‐stage lung adenocarcinoma of RLL (Clinical Stage: T1bN0M0). No other lesions were found on the whole‐body PET (Figure 1). The patient did not complain of symptoms related to a hernia. After the MDT assessment, she was scheduled for concomitant hernia repair and lobectomy by robotic surgery.

Multiplanar reconstruction of preoperative PET‐CT scan showed the pre‐existing Morgagni‐Larrey hernia * and ipsilateral lung cancer**.

The patient was intubated with a double‐lumen tube, and the Morgagni‐Larrey hernia was repaired by a standard robotic approach. The transverse colon was reduced (Figure 2A) and the hernia defect was closed with a running 2–0 lock suture (Figure 2B). Video S1 summarized the operation. Following, the patient was placed in a right lateral decubitus position, and a standard full robotic approach with CO2 use was performed. The lung was freed from the hernia sac (Figure 2C and Figure 2D). The anterior part of the oblique fissure was dissected to expose the pulmonary artery. The Pulmonary ligament and posterior mediastinal pleura were dissected, and hilar LNs and level 8 and 9 mediastinal LNs were removed. Vascular and bronchus structures of RLL were isolated and divided with dedicated robotic staplers in the following sequence: (i) lower pulmonary vein; (ii) lower pulmonary artery; and (iii) lower bronchus. The specimen was retrieved, and the removal of level 4 and 2 mediastinal LNs completed the operation. One chest tube drain was placed in the pleural cavity. Video S2 summarized the procedure.

Abdominal view showed the hernia defect before (A) and after repair (B). Thoracic view showed hernia sac before (C) and after dissection (D) from lung parenchyma.

Total operative duration was 315 min, with 90 min for hernia repair, 125 min for lobectomy, and 170 mL total estimated blood loss. No intraoperative and postoperative complications were found. Surgical pathology confirmed a pT1bN0M0 adenocarcinoma, with negative surgical margins. Chest drainage was removed in POD 3, and the patient was discharged in POD 5. At the 1‐month follow‐up, the CT scan showed the expansion of remaining lobes without hernia recurrence (Figure 1B). The patient is followed for lung cancer based on standardized protocol. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for this publication.

Discussion

Minimally invasive lobectomy is the main treatment for early‐stage lung cancer in elderly and frail patients, as the present, due to less surgical trauma compared to thoracotomy [1]. However, in this case, the anterior mediastinum was occupied by colon herniation due to pre‐existing Morgagni‐Larrey hernia that limited surgical maneuvers for performing lobectomy. Furthermore, the increased negative intrathoracic pressure and positive intra‐abdominal pressures following lobectomy could increase the risk of life‐threatening complications (i.e., obstruction, strangulation, and perforation) correlated to the preexisting diaphragmatic hernia, as previously reported [2]. Thus, we planned the concomitant surgical repair of hernia and lobectomy. Minimally invasive surgery for combined hiatal hernia repair with contralateral [3] and ipsilateral [4] lobectomy has been reported as safe, feasible, and effective, but doing both procedures on the same site with robotic surgery has not been reported before the present.

Robotic surgery was preferred over the conventional laparoscopic and/or thoracoscopic approach, as the 3D imaging and the increased degree of instrument movements facilitated the repair of the hernia defect and the lysis of adhesions between the lung parenchyma and the hernia sac. Combined procedure compared to staged procedures presented several technical advantages. (i) The repositioning of the colon into the abdominal cavity facilitated lobectomy, as the lung was easily dissected by the empty hernia sac without risk of colic perforation, while the presence of ipsilateral lung cancer did not affect the hernia repair. (ii) Potential ipsilateral thoracic injuries related to the hernia repair were managed during lobectomy concurrently. (iii) The hernia repair prevented any type of postoperative complications related to the presence of a hernia after lobectomy. Furthermore, doing the two procedures concurrently prevented any delays for additional lung cancer therapy and reduced the healthcare cost of a second admission.

From a technical point of view, the direct suture rather than the use of prosthetic mesh seemed to be the more appropriate approach for hernia repair. (i) The defect was smaller than 30 cm2, which is usually the cutoff size to advise use of mesh. (ii) There was no tissue loss of the diaphragm that made the primary repair unsuitable. (iii) The closure of the defect was tension‐free, preventing the risk of recurrence. Confirming that, other authors [5, 6] reported the laparoscopic repair of a giant Morgagni hernia by direct suturing, and no recurrence was found. Furthermore, the hernia sac was close to the phrenic nerve and thus, in line with other authors [7, 8], it was not excised to avoid any possible injury to the phrenic nerve and/or the pericardium.

During the procedure, any iatrogenic communication between the abdomen and pleural cavity should be avoided to prevent potential contamination from ipsilateral lung cancer. For that, this strategy was not indicated in the presence of ipsilateral PE.
