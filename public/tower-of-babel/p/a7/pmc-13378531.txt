A machine learning approach to quantifying fall conversion risk in fall-naïve Parkinson’s patients
Authors: AElizabeth Jansen, Paul Cantlay, Christina Felix, Anson B Rosenfeldt, Cielita Lopez-Lennon, Hubert Fernandez, Eric Zimmerman, Peter B Imrey, Leland E Dibble, Jay L Alberts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Recurrent falls associated with Parkinson’s disease (PD) contribute to diminished quality of life and treatment expense. Current fall prediction models do not adequately predict the transition from non-faller to recurrent faller in people with Parkinson’s disease (PwPD). The aim of this project was to develop a machine learning model to identify fall risk to characterize the transition to a recurrent faller.Methods:Baseline clinical motor, biomechanical, cognitive, and quality of life data from 246 PwPD in a clinical trial were combined with subsequent falls reported in diaries over 12 months. Fall-naïve participants (n = 174) were regressed on baseline data to develop a fall conversion prediction model using an XGBoost algorithm. Mean Area Under the Curve (AUC) under repeated cross-validation was the primary performance metric, with a feature importance analysis highlighting contributing variables.Results:The derived model achieved an across-fold mean cross-validated AUC of 0.63, with mean sensitivity and specificity of 66% and 51%, respectively, and higher sensitivity of 77% for multiple falls, all at a threshold of 0.2. Features contributing to model performance included three biomechanical balance assessments, NeuroQoL lower extremity and MDS-UPDRS part II questionnaires, the Trails Making Test A and the Symbol Digit Matching Test.Conclusions:The utilization of multifaceted data, biomechanical balance and self-report measures has utility in identifying PwPD at risk of experiencing a first fall and conversion to recurrent faller. Greater precision in identifying the first and subsequent falls has potential to guide interventions and behavior modification to mitigate transition to recurrent faller.Trial registry name:Pragmatic Cyclical Lower Extremity Exercise Trial for Parkinson’s Disease (CYCLE-II)Trial URL:NCT04000360.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, Falls prevention, Recurrent faller, Machine learning

Introduction

Falls are a serious concern for people with Parkinson’s disease (PwPD) across the continuum of the disease [1,2] and contribute to an overall diminished quality of life (QoL) [1,3]. Recurrent fallers have higher rates of injury, hospitalization, loss of independence, and reduced life expectancy, compared to PwPD who are non-fallers [1,3–6]. The reported annual fall rates for PwPD average 60% or approximately two to three times that of their healthy peers [6,7]. Notably, nearly 50% of falls for PwPD are injurious [5,8], and two-thirds of patients who fall are recurrent fallers, defined as falling two or more times annually [3,7,9]. Falls and their resultant injuries for PwPD are major contributors to increased health service costs and utilization in this population [6,8].

Despite the frequency of falls for PwPD, models predicting the transition from not falling to recurrent falling often have notable limitations which hinder their effectiveness in preventing a first fall [10]. The most impactful limitation is reliance on a previous and recent fall, which creates a paradox in predicting the first fall [5,8,10,11]. Given the potential high costs of an injurious fall, first or subsequent [12,13], resultant issues related to PwPD who develop a fear of falling [4,9,11], and the additional complexities of falls for this population [3], prevention of a first fall is imperative. Of particular concern is a patient converting to a recurrent faller [9]. Effective quantification of risk of a first fall, and understanding that specific risk factors for recurrent falling may be different from single fallers, is pertinent for PwPD [9]. Inability to identify patients with higher risks of a first fall limits opportunities for preventive interventions such as environmental modifications, balance training and targeted physical therapy, thus forcing providers and patients to largely function in a reactive manner to falls rather than proactive [10,14].

Clinically, the evaluation of postural instability for PwPD is assessed within the MDS-UPDRS III via a retropulsion pull task. Patients are asked to stand with their feet shoulder width apart [15]. The clinician stands behind the patient and gives a strong and sudden pull backwards on their shoulders, and counts the number of corrective action steps the patient takes to prevent falling [15]. While this test is the clinical gold standard for measuring postural instability, it is known to be a poor predictor of future falls [16,17], as it lacks objectivity and specificity, is known to lack standardization in scoring [18], and fails to represent the environmental complexity in which falls occur (i.e. moving forward, tripping, and spontaneous falls) [16,17]. Falls are most often a result of a complex interplay of factors including gait, balance, cognitive declines and the changing environment; therefore, the retropulsion task, which only captures a small fraction of this complex interplay, is not an accurate indicator of individual fall risk [16,17]. A clinically objective and precise measure of balance and instability capturing different balance stances has potential to provide greater insight into postural control and its declines associated with PD and its progression.

The simultaneous performance of a motor and cognitive task, or dual-tasking, increases postural instability and exacerbates gait impairments in PwPD [19–21]. Cognitive impairments associated with PD, along with other non-motor symptoms such as autonomic dysfunction, vision changes, and sleep problems, may contribute to fall risk and should be considered in assessing a patient’s risk of future falls [22]. Importantly, cognitive impairments and other non-motor issues may present early and frequently in the disease course, and may go unrecognized in the context of falls prevention [8,9,11].

Prediction modeling has been used to assess the relative strengths of motor and non-motor risk factors for falls in PwPD, including previous falls, gait and balance disorders, disease severity and duration, executive function, fear of falling, and freezing of gait [11]. Previous models indicate longer disease duration and poor cognitive performance, specifically spatial memory and information processing speed, were strong predictors of future falls [11]. These data highlight the relevance of including cognitive measures in the modeling of falls, and the need for an integrated assessment tool which incorporates multiple domains of executive functioning and explores the relationships between gait, cognitive function, and falls.

The present study utilizes baseline data from a cohort of fall-naïve subjects entering a randomized clinical trial from two study groups, an exercise and a control group, and follows them for 12 months to monitor falls. A separate informative and complimentary study [11] was conducted using exclusively control group baseline data from the same cohort of patients (n = 123), and identified predictors for which patients were most likely to fall at least once in the next 12 months. Importantly, the previous study did not remove pre-study fallers from their sample and did not differentiate between single fallers and recurrent fallers over the subsequent time period. The previous study highlighted that safe and effective locomotion requires an interplay between cognitive and motor processes [4,11,23]; however, a limitation exists in this work, as it was unable to aid in the risk assessment of a first fall and the conversion to a recurrent faller.

The primary aim of this project was to develop a model to stratify the risk levels of a first fall and characterize the transition from non-faller to recurrent faller in PwPD. The model utilized baseline clinical motor, biomechanical, cognitive, and QoL questionnaire data from PwPD enrolled in the CYClical Lower Extremity Exercise for Parkinson’s disease II (CYCLE-II) trial [24]. Participants in the aerobic exercise and usual and customary care group were monitored for falls over 12 months. A secondary aim was to identify specific outcomes with the greatest impact on participants’ probability of transitioning to a recurrent faller.

Methods

Study population

Participants were enrolled in the CYCLE-II study [24]. Participants were recruited from the Cleveland Clinic (Cleveland, Ohio) and the University of Utah (Salt Lake City, Utah), and randomized (1:1) between an in-home aerobic exercise intervention (stationary cycling) or a usual and customary care group (no prescribed exercise through the study). The study protocol involved comprehensive testing including assessments of motor function, cognition, and patient self-report QoL at baseline.

Full data sets were collected from 246 PD participants. Prior to enrollment, patients reported the number of falls occurring in the preceding six months and reported subsequent falls over the 12-month study period in a fall diary. This study defined a fall as any loss of balance resulting in coming to rest on a lower surface than intended, such as a chair or the ground. Extenuating circumstances such as ice, external influence such as being pushed or knocked over, or tripping on a foreign object did not alter the inclusion of a fall. During the study period, participants were contacted biweekly by research personnel and asked if they had any falls in the past two weeks. All fall details were recorded by research staff into a study database. Baseline cognitive and motor assessments were conducted in On and Off antiparkinsonian medication states on two separate testing days, separated by at least 24 h. Assessments of QoL reflect the past seven calendar days in a normal medication state, and were therefore collected in the On antiparkinsonian medication state only (Table 1).

Machine learning techniques

Seventy-two patients indicated one or more falls in the six months prior to study enrollment and were therefore excluded from this analysis to focus on predictors of falls for prior non-fallers. Of the remaining 174 patients, 94 reported no falls during the duration of the 12-month study, 47 reported one fall, and 33 reported two or more falls.

Modelling was conducted to quantify risk of fall-conversion, specifically, the probability of an individual experiencing their first fall during the 12-month study, exclusively from baseline data. A binary classification XGBoost algorithm was used because of its ability to handle complex and non-linear data with high performance and high efficiency.

The model was trained using data from 94 participants who never fell during the study and 33 who did not fall prior to enrollment but fell at least twice during the 12-month study period (recurrent fallers). Because idiosyncratic single falls are common in healthy older adults [23], the 47 patients who fell exactly once during the study can contribute noise to model training without additional predictive signal. These participants were therefore excluded from training, allowing the model to focus on only the strongest signal and characteristics of a non-faller versus a recurrent faller [25]. Once training was completed, the 47 one-time fallers were added back into the testing set as fall-converters for performance evaluation on the full cohort. Class weights were applied to the training data where the positive class of 2-time fallers were weighed at 1.75 times the importance of the non-faller group to reduce class imbalance. All baseline clinical motor, non-motor, and QoL for both On- and Off-Medication states, as well as study exercise group (exercise vs. usual and customary care), were used for training and evaluation on the 0-time and 2+-time fallers using (3x) repeated stratified 5-fold cross validation, for a total of 15 folds. In addition to cross validation, a low learning rate, and reduced rounds of gradient boosting were all used to prevent overfitting. Within each fold, a simple preliminary model was fit with all candidate predictors and used solely to extract the SHAP [26,27] feature importance values for the fold. Feature importance rankings indicated a decline in feature contribution between the 5th and 12th features across folds. Models were therefore evaluated iteratively using feature set sizes within this range, with seven features ultimately providing the best overall model performance. The top seven features by SHAP [26,27] value were used as input metrics for a second model in the same cross validation fold where performance metrics were calculated on the corresponding testing set with data from 0-, 1-, and 2+-time fallers. Feature selection was performed independently between folds of the cross validated model, ensuring only the training set for each fold informed the feature importances for that fold, mitigating the risk of data leakage. Finally, a single model was trained on the data from all 0-time and 2+-time fallers simultaneously to derive a unified set of input features and report final feature importances across the training cohort. Performance metrics were reported from the cross validated model and were not calculated for this unified feature importance only model.

Model discrimination was measured by the receiver operating characteristic AUC, a metric that is not impacted by the selection of an intervention threshold. The model output used in performance evaluation is the probability of a fall within 12 months, ranging from 0 to 1. Probabilities closer to 1 indicated higher confidence of an individual falling in the subsequent 12 months and probabilities closer to 0 indicated higher confidence of continuing without falls. The threshold for intervention action was set as a predicted probability of 0.2, or 20%, to achieve high sensitivity at a recognized cost of decreasing specificity. This threshold was chosen to minimize false negatives and reduce missed opportunities for early intervention prior to fall conversion.

Results

Participant falls metrics comparisons

Cross-validated performance of the model trained on 0-time and 2- or-more-time fallers, evaluated on the full data set including the 47 one-time fallers, yielded an average AUC of 0.63, per fold and average AUC curves (Fig. 1A). An overall average AUC of 0.63 indicates mild signal but limited discriminative ability. The model achieved a mean Brier score of 0.29 and mean Brier Skill Score of −0.16, indicating that the mean squared error is higher than a reference baseline model which assigns all individuals the same predicted probability equal to the overall prevalence (i.e., the proportion of positive cases in the cohort). Using the threshold of 0.2 for classification, the model achieved an average sensitivity of 66% to all fallers (including 58% to one-time fallers and 77% to 2+ time fallers), an average specificity of 51% to all fallers, and a corresponding 49% false-positive rate. The negative Brier skill score suggests a challenge in model calibration, the primary AUC result highlights the presence of underlying signal. The low Brier skill score indicates overconfident predictions likely resulting from including only 0- and 2+-time fallers in the training set as seen in Fig. 1B with prediction probability distributions focused near 0 and 1. The model establishes a foundational understanding of clinical symptoms and their impact on fall-conversion that could be refined and enhanced with additional modelling in a future state.

A low threshold of 20% limits mis-classified fall-converters, i.e., false negative errors. Using a lower predicted probability threshold reduces false negatives and, conversely, increases false positives. Shapley [26,27] feature importance values were extracted from the model to quantify the marginal contribution of each metric to the model’s prediction for each individual participant. They provide an in-depth view of how the model reacts to raw feature values across the dataset on a point-by-point basis, and their summation across all individuals results in a measure of each input’s global importance to the model.

Discussion

In this project, biomechanical measures of balance, cognitive, and patient self-reported QoL outcomes demonstrated potential in identifying PwPD who may transition from a non-faller to a recurrent faller over 12 months. The multifactorial combination of data outcomes that aid in identifying this transition is not surprising, as effective ambulation in the community and home environments requires an interplay between cognitive and motor functioning [1,11,28,29]. Measuring and monitoring cognitive and motor performance in PwPD has inherent value in monitoring disease progression and guiding intervention decisions. The present data provide further justification to systematically monitor and quantify cognitive and motor performance as changes in performance could raise a potential early warning flag for transition to a recurrent faller. Prevention of a first fall and conversion to recurrent faller may provide an opportunity to be proactive in preventing or mitigating the decreased QoL associated with falls and potentially reduce the economic, individual and systemic, burden of falls [1–3].

Lifestyle and environmental modifications (i.e. removing loose rugs, wearing secured footwear, holding handrails on stairs) are low in cost, particularly when compared to the cost of an injurious fall, making false negatives substantially more consequential than false positives [1,30,31]. Therefore, a low threshold was chosen to maximize sensitivity subject to a minimum specificity of 50% to minimize false negatives and reduce missed opportunities for early intervention prior to fall conversion [1,32]. Fall conversion in this project was evaluated with a 20% threshold for intervention, which achieves a high level of overall sensitivity, however, it is recognized the selected threshold consequently has low specificity. Depending on the intended intervention, selection of a different threshold for the same model is possible, and the reported AUC, measuring model discrimination, is independent of this selection. The environmental and lifestyle modification recommendations could aid in the timely implementation of fall prevention strategies in a proactive, rather than reactive, manner [1].

A central challenge in our analysis was accurately predicting one-time fallers over 12 months. Those who transitioned to one-time fallers had physical, cognitive, and self-reported QoL values similar to those who never fell (Fig. 2A). While correct prediction of individuals who fall exactly once proved difficult, the model correctly identified over 75% of the 2+ fall group. Difficulty in predicting one-time fallers is likely a result of differing risk factors between one-time fallers and recurrent fallers [9]. Similarly to their healthy older peers [32], extrinsic factors contribute to falls in PwPD [1,31], adding more noise and complicating the risk stratification of fall conversion, specifically in those who experience only one fall. The precise definition of falls used in this study, combined with the sometimes random nature of falls, reduces the model’s predictive capability of single fallers whose falls are driven by environmental factors such as a slippery surface or an external force. Recurrent fallers were detected at a much higher rate in part because the prevalence of those who fell repeatedly over the course of the 12-month study due to external influence was much lower than those with an isolated fall. Future, large sample, longitudinal studies are planned to develop a model specifically focused on predicting the first fall of PwPD. Considering the importance of dual-tasking in community and home ambulation, the planned project will utilize more complex cognitive-motor assessments to better tax the motor control processes underlying ambulation.

Balance volume data collected through the CCBA demonstrated important contributions to the model in this project, representing three of the seven most influential factors (Fig. 2B). Postural instability in PwPD contributes significantly to an individual’s risk of falling, and PwPD have significantly greater levels of postural instability compared to their healthy peers [33]. The CCBA is highly sensitive and objective compared to the clinical retropulsion pull test of balance which lacks standardization in execution and scoring [18], and is known to capture only a small portion of the complicated interplay of factors contributing to falls [16,17]. The precision in monitoring balance with the CCBA facilitates the longitudinal tracking of subtle changes in postural control that is not possible using the standard scoring (0–4) from the retropulsion test [15]. Considering the proliferation of mobile devices, such as tablets and phones, into the healthcare setting, the use of the CCBA to objectively quantify postural stability makes it a feasible approach to augment clinical ratings to better understand and precisely track changes in function that may be too nuanced for the human eye to identify or track over time.

Consistent with previous studies, cognitive performance is relevant in predicting future falls [7,8,11]. Specifically, the Trail Making Test A, assessing attention and working memory, and the Symbol Digit Matching Test (SDMT), evaluating information processing and implicit learning, both contributed to accurate risk-stratification transitioning to a recurrent faller. Poor performance on these tests, indicated by a slow overall time for completion of Trails Test A and a low number of correct answers in the SDMT, is evidence of worsening cognitive function. Fall-converters took over 2 seconds (7%) longer to complete the Trails Test A, and had two (8%) less correct answers in the SDMT on average when compared to non-fallers. Issues with attention may cause challenges with ambulation in PwPD while completing a simultaneous task, i.e. having a conversation or following directions while walking [1,19,28]. Slowed information processing likely impacts the capability to rapidly identify and adapt to environmental hazards such as an uneven sidewalk or slippery surface which both contribute to risk of falling [11,28]. Decreasing cognitive performance is linked to postural instability and overall slowing of gait, which impacts future falls in PwPD [11]; therefore, objective and quantitative data of cognition is critical to monitor as its potential to relate to increased fall risk is high [28].

While objective measures of postural stability and cognition are important aspects of function that should be measured by providers, PwPD perspectives and impressions of their capabilities are important in understanding future fall risk. The Neuro QoL lower extremity and MDS-UPDRS Part II score (Activities of Daily Living), evaluate the patient’s confidence in their mobility and ease of completion of activities of daily living [15]. The contribution to the model of patient self-report questionnaires underscores patient awareness and perceptions of changes and detriments in their daily functioning, which highlights the importance of combining objective outcomes of cognition and balance with patient reported outcomes to provide a comprehensive overview of mobility and its changes over time. Future studies are planned to evaluate if changes in patient reported outcomes precede changes in objective outcomes for fall risk stratification and transition to recurrent falling.

Technological advances have influenced many areas of healthcare; however, gaps remain which present challenges and opportunities for an improved continuum of care [34]. A task force through the International Parkinson Movement Disorders Society (2016) proposed a list of technical objectives to be utilized for patient data tracking [34]. A successful example of a current clinical objective and digital platform which meets these challenges is the “Waiting Room of the Future” (WROTF) utilized at the Cleveland Clinic Center for Neurological Restoration, which has been previously detailed [35]. The CCBA balance modules are currently in the process of being integrated into the WROTF workflow. The integration of the CCBA will result in a single platform providing motor, cognitive, and QoL data with the capability of populating the patient electronic health record (EHR) with important data which can be tracked longitudinally to aid in the early identification of fall risk. While preventive measures and interventions are effective for PwPD in fall prevention [1,2], an integrative and comprehensive tool that identifies which patients may be most suited for physical or occupational therapy has potential to reduce the probability of transitioning to a recurrent faller. Further, comprehensive data gathered during these assessments over multiple years of visits has potential to provide high quality, standardized data that could be used to develop new fall prediction models and refine our current model [14].

Access to specialty care for PwPD is a current public health issue, with healthcare disparities across race, sex, and geographic regions [36]. Amongst PwPD utilizing Medicare in the United States, 40 percent did not see a neurologist within a calendar year, and fewer than 10 percent saw a Movement Disorders Specialist [36]. The rates for seeking and obtaining specialty care in PD is significantly lower for non-whites, women, and PwPD in rural areas. As PD is particularly diverse in symptom presentation and progression between patients and lacks a specific biomarker or objective clinical diagnostic test, it can be especially difficult for physicians without expertise in PD to diagnose and effectively manage [36]. Additionally, because of the heterogeneity of disease symptom onset and progression, objective measures of function that are routinely gathered longitudinally and expressed relative to large datasets from centers employing a WROTF approach can be used to augment clinical decision-making for providers serving under-served and rural PwPD [36].

Several of the factors which proved to be important to our model, namely the Trails Making Test A and patient QoL questionnaires, are currently available for administration in clinical care; however, in a busy clinical setting, these metrics are often not obtained and tracked across time [35]. The WROTF replaces traditional “waiting room time” prior to seeing their provider with active time focused on completing objective domains of motor, cognitive and patient self-report QoL questionnaires [35,37]. Data from these assessments are immediately populated into the patient EHR with interpretable clinical aids for the provider to share and discuss findings with the patient during that visit. A unique aspect of the WROTF and its impact on patient care is that data are used to ensure the patient and provider are on the same page as it relates to current level of function relative to past performance. The objective and quantitative data collected acts to augment clinical expertise, especially in areas of the disease management which may be difficult to treat and manage effectively, such as challenges mobilty and cognition [38–40].

A large and critical clinical gap exists regarding which factors are most influential for a PwPD converting from a non-faller to recurrent faller. The main strength of the current study lies in its ability to utilize a comprehensive clinical dataset including motor, cognitive and QoL data for a large group of PwPD who were previous non-fallers, following them for 12 months with prospective falls data collection, the preferred method of tracking falls accurately in PD [6,9], and identifying factors which contributed most strongly to risk factors most associated with PwPD converting from a non-faller to a recurrent faller. The findings from this project reflect an important early step in understanding a clinically important and previously poorly understood clinical concern associated with PD, and may be used in conjunction with larger datasets in the future to continue to explore the subtle factors which may influence the transition from non-faller to recurrent faller in PD.
