Chorioamnionitis treatment for women with isolated intrapartum fever: A cost‐effectiveness analysis
Authors: Prakrunya Subhasree Badrinarayan, Megha Arora, Aaron B. Caughey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveIntrapartum fever (≥38°C) occurs in many laboring patients annually in the United States, but whether an isolated fever should be treated as chorioamnionitis remains controversial. Antibiotic treatment may help reduce the impact of infection as compared to expectant management but may lead to other complications and increase costs. This study evaluated the cost‐effectiveness of treating isolated intrapartum fever as chorioamnionitis.MethodsWe constructed a decision‐analytic model in TreeAge Pro to compare outcomes between expectant management and antibiotic treatment with ampicillin/gentamicin for isolated intrapartum fever. Our theoretical cohort was 222,000 patients, representing laboring patients with an intrapartum temperature ≥38°C in the United States annually. Outcomes were postpartum endometritis, neonatal early‐onset sepsis (nEOS), neonatal death, neonatal neurodevelopmental delay (NDD), acute kidney injury (AKI) from gentamicin toxicity, costs, and quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs). We calculated the incremental cost per QALY ratio with a willingness‐to‐pay threshold of $100,000/QALY. We discounted QALYs at a rate of 3%. Model inputs were derived from literature and assessed using univariate sensitivity analyses.ResultsIn our theoretical cohort of 222,000 individuals with a fever in labor, antibiotic treatment reduced 7481 cases of postpartum endometritis (8649 vs. 16,131), 6047 cases of nEOS (961 vs. 7009), 71 fewer neonatal deaths (1254 vs. 1325), and 415 fewer cases of NDD (15,498 vs. 15,913) compared to expectant management. However, gentamicin use for chorioamnionitis resulted in 6036 more cases of AKI (8802 vs. 2766). Treating intrapartum fever as chorioamnionitis decreased costs and increased QALYs at baseline; thus, it was a dominant strategy. In the Monte Carlo multivariable probabilistic simulation, treating intrapartum fever with antibiotics proved cost‐effective in 99.94% of trials, indicating that the cost‐effectiveness of this strategy was robust to variation in the underlying variables.ConclusionTreating intrapartum fever with antibiotics is a cost‐effective strategy to reduce the adverse outcomes of untreated intrapartum fever. Adopting a protocol to treat all intrapartum fevers ≥38°C as chorioamnionitis with appropriate antibiotics may lead to improved outcomes and reduced costs.

BACKGROUND

Isolated intrapartum fever is defined as a maternal temperature of ≥38°C during labor without additional clinical signs of infection [1]. Intrapartum fever has a widely varied prevalence of 1%–10% and may arise from noninfectious causes, such as epidural‐associated fever, or represent an early stage of intraamniotic infection [2, 3, 4, 5]. Diagnosis is based solely on the presence of fever, and management often involves expectant observation [6]. About a third of laboring patients with isolated fever may progress to clinical chorioamnionitis, a more severe condition characterized by fever of ≥38°C accompanied by other signs of intraamniotic infection, including maternal or fetal tachycardia, uterine tenderness, foul‐smelling or purulent amniotic fluid, or maternal leukocytosis [1, 7]. The prevalence of clinical chorioamnionitis is 1% to 6% in term gestations [8].

Both intrapartum fever and chorioamnionitis carry significant risks for both maternal and neonatal health, including postpartum endometritis and neonatal early‐onset sepsis (nEOS). The inflammatory response associated with chorioamnionitis is implicated in adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, including cerebral palsy [9]. Management of chorioamnionitis requires immediate administration of broad‐spectrum antibiotics, such as ampicillin and gentamicin, to mitigate infection‐related complications [1]. However, distinguishing between infectious and noninfectious causes of intrapartum fever, such as epidural‐related maternal fever, remains a clinical challenge, underscoring the need for careful assessment to guide appropriate management [6].

A recent retrospective cohort study compared outcomes among 458 women at ≥34 weeks’ gestation with intrapartum fever, of whom 227 with isolated fever received ampicillin alone and 231 with clinical chorioamnionitis received ampicillin plus gentamicin. Women with isolated fever had significantly higher rates of postpartum endometritis (8.8% vs. 3.9%) and early‑onset neonatal sepsis (4.4% vs. 0.4%) compared with those with clinical chorioamnionitis, and multivariate analysis demonstrated increased odds of both outcomes in the isolated fever group despite treatment differences [1]. Building on these findings, our study examined the cost‑effectiveness of treating isolated intrapartum fever as clinical chorioamnionitis.

Although many labor and delivery units in the United States administer empiric antibiotics for isolated intrapartum fever, this practice remains variable. Current guidance distinguishes isolated fever from suspected intraamniotic infection, and expectant management continues to be utilized in some settings, particularly when additional clinical criteria for chorioamnionitis are absent [6, 8]. This variability reflects ongoing uncertainty regarding whether universal antibiotic treatment meaningfully improves outcomes relative to observation alone, particularly given potential risks of overtreatment and antimicrobial exposure. Our study was designed to inform this area of clinical ambiguity by evaluating the economic and health outcome implications of treating all isolated intrapartum fevers as clinical chorioamnionitis, thereby providing evidence to guide protocol development in settings where management remains inconsistent. We hypothesized that treating all intrapartum fever with antibiotic would be cost‐effective, given that prior clinical studies demonstrated reductions in maternal and neonatal infectious morbidity with antibiotic administration for intrapartum fever.

METHODS

A decision‐analytic model was developed using TreeAge Pro software (2024; TreeAge Software, Inc.) to compare expectant management versus antibiotic therapy with ampicillin–gentamicin in laboring patients with a singleton gestation and isolated fever in the United States. The two initial nodes of the study were isolated intrapartum fever and clinical chorioamnionitis (Figure 1). Outcomes evaluated for each node included postpartum endometritis, nEOS, neurodevelopmental delay (NDD), neonatal death, and gentamicin‐induced acute kidney injury (AKI; if in a treatment‐receiving branch). The baseline probability, cost, utility, and duration values were obtained from the literature (Table 1). As there were no human subjects in this theoretical model, the study was deemed exempt from institutional review board approval by the Institutional Review Board at Oregon Health & Science University.

Model inputs.

Our theoretical cohort was composed of 222,000 individuals, representing the estimated population of patients with singleton gestations who labor and develop an intrapartum fever (≥ 38°C) in the United States annually [46, 47, 48, 49, 50, 51, 52]. All women in the cohort were assumed to have delivered at term (37 weeks or greater). Intrapartum fever was defined as an oral temperature of ≥38°C, maintained for a minimum of 30 min, with measurement taken via oral thermometer with no other additional infectious signs [1]. Clinical chorioamnionitis was characterized by an intrapartum fever of ≥38°C along with at least two of the following symptoms: uterine tenderness, leukocytosis exceeding 15,000/mm3, fetal tachycardia, or the presence of malodorous amniotic fluid or purulent vaginal discharge [1]. The recommended treatment for isolated intrapartum fever was expectant management and was compared to antibiotic treatment with ampicillin/gentamicin. Ampicillin combined with gentamicin was selected as the treatment regimen because it represents the most commonly recommended and utilized first‐line therapy for suspected intraamniotic infection in contemporary US obstetric practice [6, 8]. This combination is endorsed in national guidance and reflects standard empiric coverage for common perinatal pathogens. In addition, gentamicin carries a known risk of nephrotoxicity, which allowed our model to incorporate a measurable and clinically recognized adverse effect [22]. By evaluating cost‐effectiveness using a regimen with established potential toxicity, our findings provide a conservative estimate of benefit; if a regimen associated with known adverse effects remains cost‐effective, alternative antibiotic strategies with lower toxicity profiles would be expected to demonstrate equal or greater value.

The probabilities of postpartum endometritis and nEOS were derived from a study that examined clinical outcomes between patients with treated and untreated intrapartum fever [1]. We estimated that 31% of women initially diagnosed with isolated intrapartum fever would progress to clinical chorioamnionitis [7]. The probability of neonatal death from early‐onset sepsis was adjusted for term gestational age and causative agent [13]. The rate of gentamicin‐induced AKI was calculated based on a study of post‐surgical AKI rates in gynecologic patients receiving gentamicin for surgical antibiotic prophylaxis [22].

Clinical outcomes, including postpartum endometritis, nEOS, NDD, neonatal death, and gentamicin‐induced AKI, informed the cost measures, which were derived from the literature and adjusted to 2024 US dollars using the Consumer Price Index. Costs were evaluated from a societal perspective, encompassing all incurred expenses rather than only patient or healthcare system costs. The cost of ampicillin and gentamicin treatment was based on a regimen of ampicillin 2 g administered four times daily and gentamicin 240 mg once daily [1, 33, 34]. Only one primary source was used for cost estimates because average cost data are infrequently reported in peer‐reviewed literature, and available estimates often vary widely depending on treatment setting and institutional practices.

Effectiveness was evaluated from both maternal and neonatal perspectives. Quality‐adjusted life years (QALYs) were calculated by applying utilities to the length of time associated with a particular health state or scenario. Utilities ranged from 0 to 1, with 0 representing death and 1 representing ideal health. The utility value for maternal AKI was derived from a study on aortic valve replacement, where AKI was reported as a surgical complication [32, 53]. The durations of postpartum endometritis, nEOS, and AKI were set at 30 days based on clinical judgement. QALYs were discounted at a rate of 3%. For each utility parameter, clinical judgment was applied to ensure that the selected estimate appropriately reflected the health state and was consistent with the overall model structure.

We calculated the incidence of postpartum endometritis, nEOS, NDD, neonatal death, and gentamicin‐induced AKI for each treatment strategy, as well as the total costs and QALYs associated with each. Using these data, we generated the incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio (ICER) for treating intrapartum fever as clinical chorioamnionitis. The ICER was calculated by dividing the additional costs of antibiotic treatment by the QALYs gained. Our willingness‐to‐pay threshold for cost‐effectiveness was set at $100,000 per QALY.

We conducted one‐way sensitivity analyses of all probabilities, costs, and utilities to identify the variables that had the greatest impact on our model's robustness. Each variable was tested within a range spanning three standard deviations above and below the mean (the model parameter input), with inputs sourced from the literature. When published data were limited or variability was not well characterized, we selected expanded ranges to encompass all clinically plausible estimates to avoid overstating certainty in the base‐case assumptions. We further examined the most influential variables using additional one‐way sensitivity analyses to determine threshold values at which conclusions would change. To assess the simultaneous impact of uncertainty across all model inputs, we performed a Monte Carlo multivariable probabilistic sensitivity simulation with 10,000 samples. Probabilities and utilities were modeled using beta distributions constrained between 0 and 1, costs were modeled using gamma distributions to reflect right‐skewness beginning at $0, and life expectancies and durations were modeled using triangular distributions. This approach allows readers to evaluate the stability of our findings across a broad range of clinically reasonable scenarios rather than relying solely on base‐case estimates.

RESULTS

In our theoretical cohort of 222,000 individuals, antibiotic treatment resulted in 7481 fewer cases of postpartum endometritis (8649 vs. 16,131), 6047 fewer cases of nEOS (961 vs. 7009), 71 fewer neonatal deaths (1254 vs. 1325), and 415 fewer cases of NDD (15,498 vs. 15,913) compared to expectant management. However, gentamicin use resulted in 6036 more cases of AKI (8802 vs. 2766). Treating intrapartum fever as chorioamnionitis with antibiotics was a dominant strategy with cost savings of $438 million and a QALY increase of 11,031 compared to the treating with expectant management (Table 2.)

For the one‐way sensitivity analysis, we varied the impact of antibiotics, allowing the model to evaluate outcomes under assumptions ranging from no antibiotic benefit to the full effect observed in the source data. Treating with antibiotics remained the dominant strategy even when only 13.6% of the assumed antibiotic benefit was realized, and the strategy remained cost‐effective when at least 4.6% of the assumed benefit from antibiotics was present (Figure 2). In the Monte Carlo multivariable probabilistic simulation, treating intrapartum fever with antibiotics proved cost‐effective in 99.94% of trials, indicating that the cost‐effectiveness of this strategy is robust to variation in the underlying probabilities, costs, and utilities (Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

Our results showed that treating isolated intrapartum fever with ampicillin and gentamicin was cost‐effective, with noted cost savings, supporting our initial hypothesis. Several studies have evaluated the efficacy of antibiotic use in preventing postpartum maternal and neonatal complications associated with infection; however, none have directly compared overall quality‐of‐life measures or specific costs between treatment strategies [1, 6, 7, 48, 54]. Our study in a theoretical population of laboring patients with an intrapartum temperature ≥ 38°C found that the addition of ampicillin and gentamicin to treat intrapartum fever should reduce the rates of postpartum endometritis, nEOS, neonatal death, and neonatal NDD, while increasing the risk of gentamicin‐induced AKI. Given the downstream costs related to infectious morbidity, antibiotic treatment was both less expensive and led to improved outcomes in the context of infection prophylaxis for isolated intrapartum fever.

Notably, our theoretical cohort was limited to women with singleton, term pregnancies in labor, excluding deliveries via scheduled cesarean, preterm deliveries, and multiple gestations. Additionally, some of the literature used in our model included ampicillin in the expectant management arm, while the antibiotic treatment arm used a combination of ampicillin and gentamicin [1], suggesting that the true magnitude of the effectiveness of antibiotic treatment may be even greater than that which was observed in our model. Because our model inputs are sourced from existing literature, the model is vulnerable to the inherent variability of each input. However, model inputs were robustly explored through univariable and multivariable sensitivity analyses, and the model was largely robust to variation. Although gentamicin‐associated AKI likely overestimates risk in pregnant patients, it was not a significant driver in sensitivity analyses, and unmeasured vestibular or ototoxic effects highlight the need for future studies to further evaluate these outcomes. Further, our model was unable to capture variability in clinical practice, as many institutions already treat isolated intrapartum fever with antibiotics or use different antibiotic regimens for chorioamnionitis, nor did it account for antibiotic resistance or potential long‐term effects of neonatal antibiotic exposure. These factors represent important areas for future investigation.

Additionally, while our model demonstrates cost savings and improved outcomes with routine antibiotic treatment of isolated intrapartum fever, the potential impact of increasing antimicrobial resistance warrants consideration. Widespread use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics, including ampicillin and gentamicin, has been associated with rising resistance among common perinatal pathogens such as Escherichia coli and Group B Streptococcus, which may reduce treatment effectiveness over time and increase downstream healthcare costs [13, 55, 56, 57]. Also, antibiotic exposure may influence neonatal microbiome development, with potential long‐term implications that are not fully captured in short‐term economic models. As our model did not incorporate the potential impact of evolving antimicrobial resistance, future research should examine how broader intrapartum antibiotic use may affect resistance patterns, long‐term effectiveness, and healthcare costs.

Our findings suggest that a strategy of treating isolated fever with antibiotics reduces costs while improving maternal and neonatal outcomes. With clinical studies demonstrating both clinical benefits and potential cost savings, antibiotic treatment appears to be a justified investment to improve patient outcomes.
