Performances of bayesian structured additive regression and logistic models in assessing factors associated with modern contraceptive use among ever-in-union women in Nigeria
Authors: Oluwafunto Adepeju Aladekomo, Adeniyi Francis Fagbamigbe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundModern contraceptive use plays a key role in reducing unintended pregnancies, improving maternal health, and curbing rapid population growth. In Nigeria, despite growing awareness and government-led interventions, usage remains low and unevenly distributed across regions. While most studies focus on individual-level determinants, limited attention has been paid to how geographic and spatial differences influence contraceptive behavior. This study compared the performance of Bayesian structured additive regression with frequentist logistic regression in identifying factors associated with modern contraceptive use among ever-in-union women in Nigeria.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used data from the 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) of ever-in-union women aged 15–49 years. Bayesian structured additive regression and frequentist logistic regression models were applied. The deviance information criterion (DIC) was used for model evaluation.ResultsModern contraceptive prevalence was 11.5% among 31,152 ever-in-union women. Strong north-south disparities were identified, with higher uptake in urban and southern regions. A nonlinear relationship was observed between age at first marriage and contraceptive use, with 20.9% of women marrying before age 15years. Women with more than three children alive had 48% higher odds of contraceptive use (aOR = 1.48; 95% CrI: 1.24–1.66). Working women (aOR = 1.91; 95% CrI: 1.89–2.23) and those knowledgeable about modern methods (aOR = 1.78; 95% CrI: 1.66–2.25) also had higher odds.

Background

Modern contraceptive use refers to scientifically approved methods such as oral pills, condoms, intrauterine devices (IUDs), injectables, implants, and sterilization for preventing unintended pregnancies and managing family size. These strategies benefit maternal health, minimize adolescent births, lower infant mortality, and slow population increase [1].

Globally, modern contraceptive prevalence has increased minimally, but Sub-Saharan Africa has continued to face challenges. In this case, modern contraceptive prevalence in Africa rose from 23.6% in 2008 to 28.5% in 2015 [2], while the region accounted for 21% of the global burden of low use [3]. In Nigeria, the modern contraceptive prevalence rate (mCPR) among currently married women was 11.1% in 2013 and increased only slightly to 17% by 2018 [1], despite numerous interventions. Despite years of initiatives, including the Nigerian government’s alignment with the global Family Planning 2020 (FP2020) initiative, modern contraceptive use remains low, contributing to Nigeria’s continued annual population growth rate of 3.2% [4–6]. The FP2020 initiative, which aimed to reach 120 million additional women and girls worldwide with modern contraception by 2020, made significant progress but fell short of its primary goal. Recognizing this, Nigeria has renewed its commitment under FP2030 to expand equitable access to family planning, particularly for adolescents, youth, and underserved populations.

Recent spatial analyses have revealed ongoing regional differences, with greater uptake in urban and southern regions as compared to rural and northern areas [7, 8]. These findings show the importance of investigating how spatial factors interact with sociodemographic and socioeconomic characteristics to impact modern contraceptive usage.

Most studies on the use of modern contraceptives in Nigeria did not consider the spatial effect with regard to the use of modern contraceptives [9–13]. Most of these studies used frequentist methods to identify age, region, education, age at first marriage, and number of children ever born as factors associated with modern contraceptive use [14–17]. Although frequentist methods have helped us understand factors at the individual level, they frequently overlook the bigger picture, particularly how a person’s location can influence their access to and usage of contraceptives. Bayesian techniques not only provide more accurate estimates, but they also assist in identifying geographic patterns and clusters with particularly low rates of contraceptive use. This deeper insight is crucial for shaping policy strategies and designing evidence-based interventions tailored to meet the unique needs of different regions across Nigeria [18, 19].

This study aimed to compare the performances of Bayesian structural additive regression with frequentist logistic regression in assessing the factors associated with modern contraceptive use among ever-in-union women in Nigeria. Specifically, it addresses the following questions: [1] Which statistical modeling approach between the Bayesian structured additive and the Frequentist logistic regression offers better model performance? [2] What individual, demographic, and socioeconomic factors are associated with modern contraceptive use in Nigeria? [3] What are the spatial variations in modern contraceptive use across Nigeria’s six geopolitical zones?

This research contributes to the existing body of literature by highlighting the added value of spatial modeling using Bayesian methods and offering insights into region-specific disparities that can inform policy and programming for increased contraceptive uptake in Nigeria.

Methods

Study setting and design

The study population is ever-in-union women aged 15 to 49 years in Nigeria. Nigeria has 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory (Fig. 1).

Map of Nigeria showing 6 geopolitical zones, 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory

This study is a secondary analytical cross-sectional study that used nationally representative secondary data from the 2018 Nigeria Demographic Health Survey (NDHS). The questionnaire for data collection consisted of modules on primary information about households, the sexual and reproductive health of the respondents, and the birth history of the respondents.

Sampling

The DHS data in Nigeria is collected via a two-stage, stratified sampling design in all 36 states in Nigeria and the FCT with the clusters as the primary sampling units. While clusters are selected at the first stage, households are selected at the second stage. All eligible women of reproductive age (15–49 years) living in the selected households were interviewed.

Data management

We extracted data from 31,152 women of reproductive age 15–49 years who were ever-in-union. For data collection, the NDHS used computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) in 2018, with more details published elsewhere [1]. IBM SPSS (version 20) was used for descriptive analysis. All Bayesian structured additive regression (STAR) analysis were implemented in R (version 4.0.3) using the BayesX R interface (R2BayesX package, version 0.3–1.3). While variations in categorical data were presented with frequency tables and percentages. We included sampling weights in the analysis to account for the unequal probability of selection at the cluster levels and to account for nonresponse since the samples are not self-weighted.

Variables

The outcome of interest in this study is modern contraceptive use, represented as a binary response variable classified as either Yes or No. Any respondent who uses at least one of the different types of modern contraceptives within 12 months preceding the survey date was classified as a user (Yes) or nonuser (No). The explanatory variables are the respondent’s current age, respondent’s age at first cohabitation/marriage, respondent’s age at first birth, partners’ age, religion, ethnicity, number of children alive, number of children had, family size intention, residential type, wealth index, level of education, partners’ level of education, geopolitical zone, working status, knowledge of family planning, and access to media.

Model selection and diagnostics

Model performance was evaluated using the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC). DIC is a Bayesian model selection criterion that assesses model fit while penalizing complexity. It was chosen because it is specifically suited for Bayesian models estimated via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), unlike likelihood-based criteria such as AIC or BIC which rely on maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). Further details of prior information and model specification are shown in Appendix 1.

Results

Description of study participants

Out of 31,152 eligible ever-in-union women, referring to those who were married, living with a partner, widowed, divorced, or separated, 3,582 (11.5%) were users of modern contraceptive methods, while 27,570 (88.5%) were nonusers (Fig. 1). The overall arithmetic mean age of the respondents was 32.28 years (standard deviation = 8.76).

Among this population of ever-in-union women, a large majority were Hausa (37.1%) and practiced Islam (55.0%). Most reported a family size intention of approximately 5–9 children (50.1%) (Table 1). In terms of residential and educational status, most of these women lived in rural areas (63.1%) and had no formal education (42.8%). Although 70.9% were currently employed, most still fell within the poorer (21.6%) and poorest (21.5%) wealth index categories. Furthermore, a high proportion (66.6%) lacked knowledge of family planning (Tables 1 and 2).

Comparison of the logistic regression and STAR model

Based on the principles of model selection, deviance reflects how much unexplained information remains after a model has been fitted [21]; therefore, a lower value indicates a better fit. As shown in Table 2, model performance improved progressively as additional covariates were included. For the frequentist logistic regression, log-likelihood values ranged from 22230.788 to 17007.148, while in the Bayesian structured additive regression, DIC values ranged from 187.181 to 110.152. These results suggest that the Bayesian approach provided a more flexible and better-fitting model for this dataset, likely due to its ability to account for nonlinear relationships and spatial effects.

Model 6, which achieved the lowest log-likelihood and DIC values in their respective approaches, was selected as the best-fitting model. DIC was particularly suited for assessing the Bayesian models because it balances fit and complexity and is derived from posterior distributions estimated via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC).

Logistic regression of modern contraceptive use among the studied women ever-in-union

Based on Model 6, respondents aged 30–39 years have higher odds (OR = 1.49, p-value = 0.036) of using modern contraceptives than those in the age category of 15–19 years. Additionally, those whose partners are between the ages of 25–29 have lower odds (OR = 0.090, p-value = 0.001) of using it compared to the reference category (Table 3). Respondents who are traditionalists are less likely (OR = 0.186, p-value = 0.021) to use modern contraceptives compared to Christians. Based on ethnicity, Yorubas had higher odds (OR = 1.433, p-value = 0.001) of using modern methods compared to Hausas, and respondents with more than three children alive had higher odds (OR = 1.720, p-value = 0.001) of using modern methods of contraception compared to those who had approximately three children or fewer. Those whose family size intention is equal to or above ten have lower odds (OR = 0.502, p-value = 0.001) of using modern methods compared to those whose intention is for a family size of at most four. Respondents in the Southeast have lower odds (OR = 0.461, p-value = 0.001) of using modern methods of contraception compared to those in the North Central region of Nigeria. Urban residents seem to be more likely to use modern contraception, as they have higher odds of (OR = 1.188, p-value = 0.001). Respondents’ level of education is seen to have an impact on the use of these modern methods, as those with the highest level of education have higher odds (OR = 2.237, p-value = 0.001) than those without education, and the same is true for their partner with higher odds (OR = 1.632, p-value = 0.001) at the highest level of education. Ever-in-union women who are in the richest level of wealth index have higher odds (OR = 1.988, p-value = 0.001) of using these methods compared to those who fall in the poorest category. It is important to note also that those who know about family planning through a method or more have higher odds (OR = 1.280, p-value = 0.001) of using these modern methods compared to those who do not know at all.

The Bayesian framework is advantageous as it allows direct evaluation of the full posterior distribution without relying on large-sample Gaussian approximations. Spatial effects

The spatial effect maps illustrate how geographic location independently influences modern contraceptive use after adjusting for socio-demographic and contextual factors. In several Northern states, particularly those in the North Central and parts of the Northeast, positive spatial effects were observed. This indicates that, despite socio-economic and infrastructural disadvantages, contraceptive uptake in these areas is higher than the model predicted based on individual and household characteristics alone. Conversely, many Southern states, including Lagos and parts of the Southwest, exhibited negative spatial effects, suggesting that contraceptive use in these regions is lower than expected given their generally favorable socio-demographic profiles.

These insights further highlight the strength of the Bayesian structured additive regression model in detecting unobserved geographic patterns that the frequentist logistic regression could not account for [23]. While the frequentist model identified important individual-level predictors, it assumes independence across observations and overlooks spatial dependencies. By incorporating spatially structured random effects, the Bayesian approach not only provided a better model fit but also revealed region-specific influences critical for understanding and addressing disparities in contraceptive use. This demonstrates the added value of Bayesian methods for designing targeted, location-specific interventions (Fig. 2).

These nonlinear relationships highlight the strength of the Bayesian structured additive regression model, which captures complex patterns in the data without assuming a strictly linear association between predictors and the outcome [31]. Unlike frequentist logistic regression, which would require explicit transformation or categorization of continuous variables to account for such effects, the Bayesian approach naturally models these relationships using smooth functions. This provides deeper insights into how key predictors are associated with contraceptive use across their entire range, supporting more precise policy and programmatic interventions.

Discussion

Recognizing the factors that contribute to the use of modern methods of contraception serves a significant role in designing successful strategies that can lead to increased use and subsequently to a reduction in the rate of population growth [7, 8]. Additionally, it will assist policymakers in making important decisions on resource distribution. This study found a substantial difference in the usage of modern contraceptive techniques, showing that most ever-in-union women are nonusers of these modern techniques compared to those in Nigeria who use them. It was discovered that those who are currently within 30–39 years old use modern contraceptives more, and this can be based on women trying to stop birthing at this age after they might have given birth to their desired number of children [8, 25, 26].

Further analysis was conducted to determine what the major concerns were, and it was found that those who got married between the ages of 20–24 years used modern methods most compared to other age categories [8, 25]. Those above this age range used it less, and it could be as a result of wanting children during those years of reproduction. This study showed that those who got married between the ages of 15–19 used modern contraceptives less than those within 20–24 years, which could be due to factors such as lack of maturity, low level of education, or none [1].

The use of these modern methods was found to be more common among women with more children either currently alive or ever had and among those whose family size intention was less than or equal to four. This suggests that the more children a married woman has, the more likely she is to seek ways to prevent further pregnancies. Conversely, women who intend to have larger families tend to use modern contraceptive methods less frequently. Similar patterns have been observed in previous studies [7, 8, 25, 29].

Ethnicity and religion were also major factors contributing to the use of modern contraceptive methods. It was observed that Hausa’s were the least users of these modern methods, which could be linked to their belief systems about polygamy, especially among Muslims and traditionalists [7, 8]. The respondent’s level of education and that of their partner were seen to have a major effect on the use of modern contraceptives; the more educated these women were, the more they knew the importance and benefits of these methods, which led to their increased usage. This study also showed that those in urban areas used it more, and those who were down the ladder of the wealth index used it less compared to those up the ladder and currently working. These findings have also been reported in earlier studies [27, 31].

These regional differences align with the spatial distribution patterns previously discussed and further emphasize the need to understand individual- and household-level predictors that vary across regions [1, 7, 8, 25]. Further analysis revealed that knowledge of modern contraceptives and exposure to media significantly influenced their usage. However, women in the northern regions had the lowest levels of exposure to both, which may partly explain the lower rates of contraceptive use observed in these areas. The results showed that it was not only a factor of these two things but also a number of others, such as their level of education, wealth index, age of the women at their first birth and first marriage, their partner’s age and level of education, religion, ethnicity, family size intention, number of children ever born and presently alive, work status and residential type. All these factors contributed to the reason for their lack of use or low use of modern contraceptives, which has remained so from past studies [1, 7, 8, 28].

Modern contraceptives have been studied over the years on different grounds of implementation [22, 34], but it is interesting to note that many of these studies used the frequentist approach in solving the challenges faced. Unfortunately, after comparing the frequentist approach with the Bayesian approach in this study, the Bayesian approach was found to provide a better fitting model for factors associated with modern contraceptive use than the frequentist approach. This method allowed us to specifically discover the major states where MC use was higher or lower than expected, after adjusting for socio-demographic and contextual factors.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study showed that, among other social-economic, and sociodemographic, knowledge indicators, the location of a woman has a great impact on the use of modern contraceptives. With our results, we can see that even with several interventions that had gone forward in the past years regarding this subject, there has still not been a decline in the rate of growth in the country. This can be subjected to the wrong channeling of these interventions. Additionally, the STAR approach yielded a better, more reliable result compared to the binary logistic approach. As deviance is a measure of overall fit of a model, the model with the least deviance shows the best fit, we found that the Bayesian approach provided a better model fit in this study, and this can be attributed to effective number of parameters (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:pD$$\end{document}) that was added to the posterior mean deviance, giving a measure of fit that is penalized for complexity [7, 30].

With this result, the following recommendations are given, researchers may consider working more with the Bayesian approach, particularly when spatial or nonlinear effects are of interest, as it can offer additional insights alongside conventional methods. Also, the government or nongovernmental organizations are advised to channel every form of modern contraceptive initiative to the locations that lack it the most, most especially the northern region of Nigeria. There should not only be a free supply of these modern contraceptives but there should be a proper education system put in place for the women in those regions because research shows that the more educated these women get, the more they are open-minded to use these modern methods for their health preservation, that of their children and to help the population at large [8, 28]. Policymakers and program implementers may use spatial data to guide the distribution of family planning resources and interventions. Specific actions include increasing funding and outreach in low contraceptive use areas, developing regionally adapted education campaigns, expanding mobile and community-based services, and institutionalizing spatial data use in national and state family planning strategies. By focusing these efforts on regions with the lowest contraceptive use, particularly in the northern states, Nigeria can make more efficient progress toward its FP2030 goals and ensure that all women and families have equitable access to family planning services [1].

Strength and limitation

This study demonstrates the strength of Bayesian structured additive regression in modeling nonlinear and spatial effects, providing a better fit than frequentist methods for understanding factors influencing modern contraceptive use. By uncovering geographic patterns and contextual influences, it offers policy-relevant insights that can guide targeted interventions, particularly in regions such as northern Nigeria where uptake remains low.

However, as the analysis is based on a single cross-sectional dataset, it limits generalizability beyond Nigeria. Additionally, the focus on spatial and socio-demographic factors means the study does not evaluate the effectiveness of previous family planning interventions, limiting conclusions about whether low adoption is due to implementation gaps or persistent socio-cultural barriers.

Furthermore, residual confounding cannot be ruled out, as unmeasured supply-side factors (such as contraceptive availability and health system capacity) and cultural norms may still influence uptake. Finally, while penalized splines help to control complexity, the inclusion of multiple smooth terms carries some risk of overfitting, which could affect the precision of individual estimates.
