Perovskite immunoadjuvant: Remodeling the immunosuppressive microenvironment via photothermal catalysis to enhance T-cell recruitment and amplify the immune response
Authors: Rui Zhang, Xiang Li, Bingbing Duan, Xinyue Hu, Wenxin Li, Xiuni He, Ting Zhang, Xin Mi, Shunye Zhang, Zhiguang Fu, Yu Luo, Xiaofeng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immunotherapeutic efficacy is markedly compromised in “cold” tumors, largely as a consequence of the immunosuppressive milieu within the TME, characterized by hypoxia, a fortified antioxidant defense (e.g., elevated glutathione), and insufficient immunogenicity. This necessitates next-generation strategiy that can remodel the TME and trigger strong anti-tumor immunity. Herein, we engineered an intelligent photothermo-catalytic immunoadjuvant based on polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP)-modified lanthanum manganite oxide perovskites (La0.93MnO3@PVP, LMOP), which synergizes with photothermal therapy (PTT) to amplify systemic anti-tumor immunity. LMOP demonstrated inherent catalytic activities mimicking both catalase (CAT) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), which are markedly enhanced under PTT-induced hyperthermia, establishing a thermo-catalytic cascade. This synergy effectively remodeled the TME by generating O2 to alleviate hypoxia and depleting glutathione to disrupt the antioxidant shield. The resulting oxidative stress, coupled with PTT, not only inflicted direct cytotoxicity but also induced mitochondrial damage, thereby triggering a potent immunogenic cell death (ICD) cascade. Remarkably, LMOP-mediated PTT effectively suppressed tumor growth, promoted dendritic cell (DC) maturation and extensive accumulation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. This immunoadjuvant design strategy presents a promising paradigm for converting immunosuppressive “cold” tumors into “hot,” therapy-responsive phenotypes.

Introduction

Immunotherapeutic approaches have dramatically improved outcomes for patients with diverse metastatic neoplasms [1]. However, its clinical efficacy is severely hampered when applied to immunologically “cold” tumors [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. The crucial factor stems notoriously immunosuppressive microenvironment (TME),which characterized by severe hypoxia and redox imbalance (e.g., elevated glutathione levels(GSH)) [6,7]. These barriers synergistically suppress tumor immunogenicity [8,9] and induce T-cell exhaustion [10,11]. Therefore, developing innovative strategies that can reshape immunosuppressive TME and trigger strong anti-tumor immune responses, represent an urgent clinical need to transform “cold” tumors into immunologically “hot” phenotypes [12] (see Scheme 1).Scheme 1Schematic illustration of LMOP nanovaccine for synergistic photothermal-immunotherapy. Nanozymes represent a category of nanostructures endowed with inherent catalytic characteristics reminiscent of natural enzymes, have emerged as promising bioactive agents for remodeling the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Specifically, those with CAT-like activity counteract tumor hypoxia by decomposing endogenous H2O2 to generate O2 [18,19]. Additionally, those with GPx-like activity deplete intracellular glutathione (GSH), which undermines the antioxidant defense system of tumor cells, thereby intensifying oxidative stress within the TME [20,21]. These catalytic activities contribute to remodeling TME, thereby creating more favorable conditions for potent anti-tumor immune responses.

Photothermal therapy (PTT) has been recognized as a potent strategy to enhance anti-tumor immunity [22]. By converting near-infrared (NIR) light into localized hyperthermia, PTT enables minimally invasive tumor ablation with spatiotemporal precision [23]. Beyond direct cell killing, a compelling advantage of PTT is its ability to elicit immunogenic cell death (ICD), wherein therapy-induced cellular stress triggers the systemic release of tumor-associated antigens and danger signals [24]. Unlike conventional cell death, ICD reprograms dying tumor cells into an autologous vaccine by triggering the emission of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). These involve the surface exposure of calreticulin (CRT), the active secretion of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) into the extracellular space, and the liberation of high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) from the nucleus [25,26]. These DAMPs serve as “eat-me” signals and endogenous adjuvants that stimulate dendritic cell (DC) maturation within the TME, thereby enhancing antigen presentation, recruiting CD4+/CD8+ T cells and promoting their intratumoral infiltration, and ultimately activating tumor-specific adaptive immune responses [27].

Herein, we engineered a polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP)-modified lanthanum manganite oxide perovskite (La0.93MnO3@PVP, LMOP) as an intelligent photothermo-catalytic immunoadjuvant for synergistic photo-immunotherapy. The unique perovskite structure of La0.93MnO3, with its mixed Mn valence states (Mn3+/Mn4+), endows it with intrinsic dual-enzyme-like activities (CAT- and GPx-like). Moreover, the photothermal effect of LMOP markedly potentiated tumor cell killing efficacy, (η ≈ 34 %). Our in vitro studies showed that this photothermal-synergized catalytic effect effectively remodels the intracellular redox environment, damages mitochondria, and triggers a powerful ICD cascade, culminating in efficient DC maturation. Remarkably, in a Panc02 pancreatic cancer model, we demonstrated that LMOP-mediated PTT not only achieved profound inhibition on tumor growth but also activated potent antitumor immunity. This was reflected by pronounced DC maturation and extensive intratumoral infiltration of both CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes. This work presents a potent strategy that integrates enzyme-like activities, photothermal ablation, and TME modulation to amplify anti-tumor immune response, thus providing a promising approach for converting immunologically “cold” tumors into responsive “hot” ones.

Results and discussion

Preparation of LMOP nanoplatform

The preparation of La0.93MnO3 (LMO) was accomplished via a single-step hydrothermal route, involving the co-hydrolysis Mn(NO3)2·4H2O, La(NO3)3·6H2O, and PEG-6000 in an aqueous NH3·H2O solution. Subsequent surface coating with PVP yielded the final LMOP product (Fig. 1a). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observation showed that the resulting LMOP possessed an irregular, solid morphology (Fig. 1b). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) analysis further revealed a well-defined lattice spacing of 0.276 nm, which is assignable to the (110) crystallographic plane of LMOP (Fig. 1c). Subsequently, the crystalline phase was additionally verified by X-ray diffraction (XRD), where the characteristic reflections matched well with the reference pattern of La0.93MnO3 (PDF#01-082-1152) (Fig. 1d). These results are consistent with a rhombic symmetric crystal structure [28], and an R-3c space group configuration (Fig. 1e). Importantly, comparative XRD measurements performed before and after ultrasonication indicated that the crystalline structure remained intact throughout the dispersion process (Fig. S1). A homogeneous spatial distribution of La, Mn, and O throughout the LMOP matrix was corroborated by EDX mapping. (Fig. 1f). The modification with PVP could improve the dispersibility and biocompatibility [29]. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed that the mean hydrodynamic size dropped from 558.2 nm to 244.6 nm following PVPylation, accompanied by a narrow polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.214, suggestive of markedly enhanced monodispersity (Fig. 1g). Concurrently, upon incorporation of phosphate, the surface charge underwent a substantial reversal, decreasing from +3.16 mV for LMO to −4.97 mV for LMOP, further confirming the successful PVP modification (Fig. S2). The FTIR spectra display characteristic peaks of PVP at approximately 1650 cm−1 (ν C=O) and 1285 cm−1 (ν C-N) in LMOP [30], further confirming successful surface modification (Fig. S3). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) quantified the PVP content to be approximately 9 % by weight (Fig. 1h). To further investigate the impact of PVP modification on LMOP's colloidal properties, a more in-depth stability evaluation was conducted in various environments, such as phosphate buffered saline (PBS), water, or Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). Notably, LMOP maintained negligible fluctuations in hydrodynamic size, PDI and ζ potential over a 5-day storage period, demonstrating excellent colloidal stability. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was subsequently employed to examine the surface elemental composition of LMOP, and the survey spectrum exhibited prominent peaks for La 3d, O 1s, and Mn 2p (Fig. 1k). By deconvoluting the high-resolution XPS core-level spectra, the valence states pertaining to La and Mn were established. For La 3d spectrum, the peaks at 855.16/851.38 eV and 838.28/834.68 eV were assigned to the La 3d3/2 and La 3d5/2 orbitals respectively. The energy separation between these doublets was measured to be 17.0 eV, a value characteristic of the La3+ oxidation state (Fig. 1l) [31]. For Mn 2p spectrum, the signals centered at 641.85 eV and 653.44 eV were ascribed to Mn3+ species, whereas those at 644.56 eV and 656.08 eV originated from Mn4+ within the Mn2p3/2 orbitals (Fig. 1m) [32]. This coexistence of both Mn3+ and Mn4+ valence states is essential for the subsequent catalytic activities of LMOP. Besides, O 1s spectrum was deconvoluted into two constituent peaks assigning to lattice oxygen (529.35 eV) within the perovskite and surface-adsorbed oxygenate species (531.27 eV) (Fig. 1n).

Photothermal and catalysis performances of LMOP

We first investigated the intrinsic catalytic properties of LMOP. LMOP exhibits prominent CAT-like activity, rapidly decomposing H2O2 to generate O2, and thus alleviating hypoxia to sensitize cancer cells to subsequent therapies (Fig. 2a-b, Fig. S4). Concurrently, LMOP exhibited potent GPx-like activity, effectively depleting the primary cellular antioxidant GSH in both a time- and concentration-dependent fashion, as verified by 5,5′-dithiobis(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) probe (Fig. 2c-d, Fig. S5–6). Notably, NIR-II laser irradiation significantly enhanced these enzyme-mimetic activities of LMOP. Mechanistically, the acidic TME facilitates the availability of catalytic Mn sites. As shown in Fig. S7, the release of Mn ions at a tumor-mimicking pH 6.0 was significantly greater than that at neutral pH 7.4, and this acid-triggered dissociation was further accelerated upon NIR-II laser exposure. The augmented Mn release combined with intensified catalytic functions drives efficient O2 generation and GSH consumption, enabling LMOP to effectively remodel immunosuppressive TME (Fig. S8). In addition to its catalytic features, the photothermal performance of LMOP upon 1064 nm laser excitation was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 2e-f, the aqueous dispersion of LMOP exhibited a dose-dependent, power-dependent, and time-dependent temperature increase (Fig. S9). For example, LMOP + NIR-II solution (200 μg/mL, 2.0 W cm−2) reached the therapeutically effective threshold of 45 °C within 10 min. This targeted hyperthermia can effectively kill tumor cells without subjecting surrounding healthy tissues to prolonged thermal stress. Furthermore, the photothermal conversion efficiency (η) was calculated to be approximately 34 % by monitoring the temperature profile over the heating and cooling cycles following an established protocol [33] (Fig. 2h). Briefly, the calculation of η value incorporated the temperature-time curves of the LMOP dispersion (200 μg mL−1) and the pure solvent upon1064 nm irradiation (2 W cm−2), the material's absorbance at this wavelength (A1064 = 0.255), and the solvent's mass (0.3 g) and specific heat capacity (4.2 J g−1 K−1). This analysis yielded an η value of 33.7 %, indicating an excellent photothermal conversion capability for LMOP.

Meanwhile, the infrared thermal images (Fig. 2i and S9) further revealed that the LMOP NPs solutions temperature increased rapidly with a concentration-dependent manner and different laser power densities within minutes [33]. Collectively, these results confirm that LMOP is a highly efficient and stable NIR-II photothermal agent endowed with intrinsic TME-reprogramming catalytic activities, making it an ideal candidate for synergistic photothermal-immunotherapy.

In vitro photothermal catalytic efficacy of LMOP

Capitalizing on the exceptional enzyme-like activities and photothermal performance of LMOP, its therapeutic potential was subsequently investigated in vitro. Initially, using Cyanine2 (Cy2)-labeled LMOP, we evaluated its time-dependent cellular internalization. Fluorescence images and quantitative analysis revealed a time-dependent enhancement in intracellular green fluorescence intensity, as LMOP co-incubation prolonged, indicating cellular internalization of LMOP (Fig. 3a and b, Fig. S10). Next, the in vitro biocompatibility was evaluated. Cell-Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays on both L929 fibroblast and Panc02 cancer cells revealed negligible cytotoxicity, showing excellent biocompatibility of LMOP (Fig. 3c and d, Fig. S11). Upon laser irradiation, LMOP exhibited significant tumoricidal activity. By screening a range of laser powers, we identified the conditions that achieved the half-maximal lethal effect on tumor cells (Fig. S12). In contrast to their inherent safety, LMOP exhibited potent photothermal cytotoxicity on Panc02 cells upon NIR-II irradiation (Fig. 3e). This potent killing effect was visually corroborated by calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) co-staining assay, which displayed widespread cell death in the LMOP + NIR-II group (Fig. 3f and i). As we have identified that LMOP possessed intrinsic enzyme-like activities, we next evaluate its capacity in vitro. The oxygen-sensitive probe [Ru(dpp)3]Cl2 (RDPP) staining was employed to evaluate intracellular O2 production. In contrast to the control group's intense red fluorescence, cells treated with LMOP showed a diminished signal, reflecting elevated O2 generation. More importantly, this red fluorescence was further reduced after NIR-II irradiation, indicating photothermal-enhanced CAT-like activity of LMOP (Fig. 3h and k and Fig. S13). Concurrently,we measured intracellular GSH levels to evaluate the GPx-like activity of LMOP. As shown in Fig. 3l, LMOP + NIR-II can consume GSH effectively, indicating that photothermal enhanced LMOP-mediated GSH consumption. Collectively, the photothermal catalysis of LMOP synergistically induced tumor cell death, alleviated hypoxia, and reduced anti-oxidant defenses.

LMOP Phototherapy Induced Mitochondrial Damage in Panc02 cells

To investigate the underlying cell-killing mechanism by LMOP-mediated photothermal catalytic therapy, we first focused on the mitochondria, the cellular energy hub and a key regulator of apoptosis (Fig. 4a). The dissipation of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) is a critical early hallmark of mitochondrial dysfunction. Using the fluorescent probe JC-1, we observed that unlike the control groups which displayed strong red fluorescence (JC-1 aggregates, indicating high MMP), cells treated with LMOP + NIR-II exhibited a predominant green fluorescence (JC-1 monomers, indicating low MMP) (Fig. 4b and c). This visually confirmed that our combination therapy inflicted severe mitochondrial damage. Consistent with this, intracellular ATP levels were significantly depleted in the LMOP + NIR-II group, indicating that the energy production function of mitochondria was compromised (Fig. 4d). Mitochondrial damage is a key upstream event that activates the intrinsic apoptosis pathway. We therefore investigated key molecules in the apoptosis cascade. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed an extensive release of the pivotal pro-apoptotic protein, Cytochrome c, from the mitochondria into the cytoplasm following the LMOP + NIR-II treatment (Fig. 4e and g). We therefore evaluated several key DAMP markers, such as HMGB1, CRT,and ATP [35]. Notably, LMOP + NIR-II treatment promoted a marked cell-surface CRT externalization, a crucial “eat-me” signal (Fig. 5a-b, Fig. S14). To validate whether these ICD signals could translate into systemic immune activation, after subjecting Panc02 cells to the corresponding treatments, we collected their culture supernatants and incubated bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) with these conditioned media for 48 h. Mature DCs are indispensable for the generation of effective anti-tumor immunity [36], the percentage of mature DCs was determined by flow cytometric analysis. LMOP + NIR-Ⅱ treatment significantly increased this proportion, which more than doubled compared to the control group (Fig. 5e and f). Additionally, the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) is also important for alleviating immunosuppression. We also investigated TAM phenotype polarization. After co-culture with tumor cells from LMOP + NIR-II group, the proportion of pro-inflammatory M1-phenotype macrophages (CD86+) significantly increased, while the proportion of immunosuppressive M2-phenotype macrophages (CD206+) correspondingly decreased, indicating effective TAM repolarization toward an anti-tumor M1 phenotype (Fig. 5g-i). Quantitative ELISA assays corroborated that exposure to LMOP combined with NIR-II irradiation markedly boosted the release of the classical cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. (Fig. 5j-l), which may further reinforce M1 polarization and amplify the immune response. Collectively, these results demonstrate that LMOP-mediated photothermal catalytic therapy not promote tumor cell death but triggers an ICD cascade to remodel the immune microenvironment.

In vivo biosafety and anti-tumor efficacy of LMOP

The promising in vitro therapeutic and immunostimulatory performance prompted us to further investigate its in vivo efficacy. Prior to in vivo application, the biosafety of LMOP was evaluated. An in vitro hemolysis assay revealed negligible erythrocyte damage (<5 %) even at a high content of 100 μg/mL, demonstrating excellent compatibility with circulating blood of LMOP suitable for intravenous administration (Fig. S17). Subsequently, Panc02 tumor-bearing mice were generated to assess the in vivo antitumor efficacy of LMOP (Fig. 6a). At the time when subcutaneous Panc02 tumors reached nearly 70 mm3, the mouse were categorized into four treatment subsets: (n = 5): (i) Control, (ii) NIR-II, (iii) LMOP, (iv) LMOP + NIR-II group. An equal volume of PBS or LMOP (15 mg kg−1) was injected into the mice via tail vein injection on days 0 and 4. Then, the mouse with NIR-II treatment were exposed to laser irradiation (1064 nm, 1 W cm−2) for 10 min at 12h post-injection on both Day 0 and Day 4, following each corresponding LMOP administration. This ensures consistency of photothermal catalytic activation after each dosing cycle. During the 14-day treatment period, Body weight and tumor volume averages of all mice were tracked at two-day intervals for each group to evaluate therapeutic effectiveness. Mice treated with LMOP + NIR-II exhibited a rapid temperature increase at the tumor to over 50 °C, whereas control groups showed no significant heating (Fig. 6b and Fig. S18). This potent photothermal effect translated into remarkable therapeutic efficacy. The dynamic changes in tumor growth revealed that (Fig. 6c and Fig. S19), the tumors in the Control and NIR-II groups grew aggressively, while LMOP group alone showed limited inhibitory effect. We next conducted the immunohistochemistry and immunofluo-rescence analysis for tumor sections. The IHC micrographs in Fig. 6d–f exhibited that LMOP + NIR-II treatment resulted in markedly decreased cell proliferation and increased apoptotic cell death relative to the other groups. The biosafety of LMOP under irradiation was further evidenced by the absence of significant body weight variations among all groups throughout the study (Fig. S20). Photothermal catalytic therapy of LMOP boosted antitumor immune responses

As we have identified that LMOP + NIR-II group could effectively activate antitumor immune responses in vitro, we next investigated if such immune activation can be found in vivo. The initiation of adaptive immunity relies on the triggering of antigen-presenting cells, such as DCs, within the lymphatic nodes responsible for tumor drainage, where they prime naive T cells. To determine DC maturation in lymph nodes, flow cytometric analysis was undertaken as the initial step, following the treatment regimen depicted in Fig. 7a. Compared to the control group, monotherapies induced a moderate increase in DC maturation, the LMOP + NIR-II combination treatment significantly enhanced the population of mature DCs (CD11+CD80+CD86+) to 23.0 % (Fig. 7b and e and Fig. S35). Mature DCs subsequently prime and activate T lymphocytes. Consistent with this, we observed a broad activation of T cell populations. Specifically, the population of CD4+ helper T cells, which are crucial for orchestrating the overall immune response, was markedly expanded to 10.0 % in the LMOP + NIR-II group (Fig. 7c and f and Fig. S36). More importantlly, the percentage of CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, which serve as the major drivers of antitumor cytotoxicity, was also dramatically elevated to 27.5 % in the LMOP + NIR-II group (Fig. 7d and g and Fig. S37). The gating strategy of flow cytometry for mature DCs, T cells in the spleen is shown in Fig. S35–37. Conclusion

In this study, we successfully engineered a multifunctional photothermo-catalytic immunoadjuvant, LMOP, for the synergistic photo-immunotherapy of pancreatic cancer. LMOP possessed excellent photothermal properties not only benifited thermal ablation, but also acted as an amplifier for its intrinsic enzyme-like activities. Such design allowed for alleviation of tumor hypoxia through O2 generation and disruption in anti-oxidant defenses by depleting intracellular GSH. Together with PTT inducing profound mitochondrial damage and ICD, the elicited immunity exerts potent tumoricidal effects, restraining neoplastic growth across cell-based assays and whole-animal studies. Future studies could explore the combination of this photothermo-catalytic therapy with blocking antibodies against immune checkpoints, exemplified by anti-PD-L1, to achieve an even more durable, long-term anti-tumor memory.
