Septal myectomy for obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: Comparison of outcomes of the transapical beating-heart and transaortic on-pump approaches
Authors: Younus Qamar, Rui Li, Jeffrey B Geske, Jing Fang, Brian D Lahr, Alexander T Lee, Steve R Ommen, Song Wan, Hartzell V Schaff, Xiang Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTransaortic on-pump septal myectomy (SM) is the established standard for surgical septal reduction, but transapical beating-heart SM recently has emerged as a less invasive alternative. This study compares the early outcomes of these 2 approaches to performing SM in patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (oHCM).MethodsWe analyzed the outcomes of 200 consecutive patients undergoing transapical beating-heart myectomy and 200 patients receiving transaortic on-pump myectomy. The primary outcome was procedural success, defined as a peak left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) gradient <30 mm Hg on predismissal echocardiography. Secondary outcomes included transfusion and postoperative atrial fibrillation rates and intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay.ResultsBoth techniques significantly reduced LVOT gradient (median reduction: transapical, 102 mm Hg to 15 mm Hg; transaortic, 88 mm Hg to 12 mm Hg; P < .001 for both). Transapical beating-heart myectomy was associated with a shorter ICU stay (median, 24.0 hours vs 26.0 hours; P < .001), a lower atrial fibrillation rate (9.5% vs 30.0%; P < .001), and fewer transfusions (8.0% vs 22.0%; P < .001) but resulted in a higher incidence of new-onset left bundle branch block (63.8% vs 37.0%; P < .001) and longer postoperative hospital stay (median, 11 days vs 5 days; P < .001), owing mainly to institutional protocols. Overall 30-day mortality was 0.5% (n = 1) in the transapical beating-heart myectomy group and 1.0% (n = 2) in the transaortic on-pump myectomy group (P = .562).ConclusionsTransapical beating-heart SM provides comparable hemodynamic outcomes to transaortic on-pump myectomy with advantages in ICU recovery time, transfusion rates, and postoperative atrial fibrillation. It may be a viable, less invasive alternative for selected oHCM patients. Further studies, including randomized trials, are needed to assess long-term outcomes.Key Words: transapical beating-heart septal myectomy, transaortic on-pump septal myectomy, septal myectomy, septal reduction therapy, obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, systolic anterior motion, mitral regurgitation

Comparable LVOT gradient reductions with transapical beating-heart septal myectomy.

Central Message

Transapical beating-heart septal myectomy achieves comparable hemodynamic outcomes to transaortic on-pump myectomy while reducing intensive care unit stay, transfusion rates, and postoperative atrial fibrillation.

Perspective

Septal myectomy remains the gold standard for symptomatic obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. This study presents the first direct comparison of transapical beating-heart and transaortic on-pump myectomy, showing comparable efficacy in reducing left ventricular outflow tract gradient. The transapical approach may allow quicker recovery and reduced postoperative morbidity, offering a viable option for selected patients.

Practice guidelines from the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology and the European Society of Cardiology recommend septal reduction therapy (SRT) to relieve left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction for symptomatic patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (oHCM) who are in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class III/IV, despite optimal medical therapy.1, 2, 3 This recommendation is based on multiple retrospective studies confirming benefits associated with SRT, including abolishing or significantly reducing LVOT gradient in >90% of cases, reducing systolic anterior motion (SAM)-mediated mitral regurgitation (MR), and improving exercise capacity and symptoms.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10

The standard operation for septal myectomy (SM) involves a median sternotomy, the use of cardiopulmonary bypass, and access to the hypertrophied septum through an aortotomy. Despite its proven safety and efficacy, the use of SM in many regions is limited, even with the high prevalence of oHCM, in part due to the technical challenges associated with the procedure and the invasiveness of transsternal access. To address the growing demand for less-invasive approaches, some surgeons have used thoracoscopic and robotic assistance for SM.11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Recently, a team in Wuhan, China developed an innovative beating-heart myectomy device, allowing surgeons to perform SM through a transapical approach without the support of cardiopulmonary bypass.16, 17, 18 The procedure is performed through a limited anterior thoracotomy under transesophageal echocardiographic (TEE) guidance, which provides real-time assessment of LVOT gradient and SAM. The purported benefits of this less-invasive, transapical beating-heart approach appeal to both patients and clinicians. However, no study has previously compared the outcomes of this technique with the conventional transaortic on-pump SM.

In the present study, we aimed to describe the characteristics of patients with oHCM undergoing transapical beating-heart SM and compare then with patients undergoing transaortic on-pump SM. We also compared the 2 methods in terms of procedure-related morbidity and mortality and relief of LVOT obstruction.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Mayo Clinic (23-011415, approved February 5, 2024) and Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (2022-S013, 2022-S013-1, 2022-S013-2, 2022-S013-3, and 2022-S013-4, approved April 15, 2022). The need for individual patient consent was waived, as relevant identifiers were not included in the database, and patients who declined participation in clinical research were excluded. A learning curve has been reported for transapical beating-heart SM.19 To account for this, we retrospectively reviewed cases from July 2023 and selected the most recent 200 consecutive patients who underwent transapical-beating heart SM performed by a single surgeon (X.W.) at Tongji Hospital, Wuhan, China. For comparison, we also identified 200 consecutive patients who underwent transaortic on-pump SM for oHCM during the same period, performed by a single surgeon (H.V.S.) at the Mayo Clinic, Rochester, Minnesota. Patients were included if they had oHCM with septal hypertrophy limited to the subaortic and/or midventricular septal regions (zones 1 and 2).20 We excluded patients age <18 years and those with septal hypertrophy involving the apical septal region (zone 3). Also excluded were patients undergoing SM with major concomitant cardiovascular procedures, including coronary artery bypass grafting for coronary artery disease, mitral valve repair for associated intrinsic degenerative mitral valve abnormalities, or repair/replacement for other native valvular diseases. Patients who initially underwent transapical beating-heart SM through a mini-thoracotomy but were converted to a median sternotomy for a transaortic on-pump SM also were included in an intention-to-treat analysis.

Data Collection

Relevant demographic data, baseline comorbidities, echocardiographic measurements, operative characteristics, and postoperative outcomes were obtained by actively reviewing patients’ health records. To ensure consistency of data collection, we adhered strictly to Society for Thoracic Surgeons (version 4.20.1) criteria when collecting patient information from Tongji Hospital and the Mayo Clinic.

Outcomes

Procedural success was defined as a peak LVOT gradient <30 mm Hg and moderate or less residual MR on postoperative echocardiography performed before hospital dismissal. Other outcomes of interest included hospital recovery metrics, such as the need for red blood cell (RBC) transfusion, postoperative atrial fibrillation rate, and the duration of intensive care unit (ICU) stay. Major adverse events included 30-day mortality, complete heart block requiring permanent pacemaker implantation, sternotomy conversion for patients undergoing transapical-beating heart myectomy through a thoracotomy, iatrogenic ventricular septal defect, iatrogenic valvular injury, and imaging-validated cerebral infarction (stroke).

Operative Technique

As previously reported, a transapical beating-heart SM is performed through a mini-thoracotomy, typically positioned in the fifth, or less commonly, the sixth intercostal space at the left midclavicular line.17, 18, 19 Following pericardiotomy, double circumferential purse-string sutures with Teflon felt pledgets are placed in the left ventricular (LV) apex and secured with snares to provide hemostasis and allow insertion of the beating-heart myectomy device (BMD; Wei-Xin-Tan).16 After systemic heparinization, LV apical and peripheral arterial pressures are measured; an isoproterenol provocation test is performed if the resting LVOT gradient is < 50 mm Hg.

The BMD device is introduced into the LVOT through the apical puncture under TEE guidance, and the location of the resection window is identified by 3-dimensional imaging. The first resection is performed in the basal anterior septum, located 5 to 10 mm beneath the aortic valve in the long-axis view and extended to the midpoint of the septum in the short-axis view. The second resection is performed slightly anterior to the first resection, with the BMD rotated 60 to 120° clockwise from the initial position. Following each resection, the morphology of the septal bulge, the LVOT gradient, the grade of MR, and the remaining septal thickness are evaluated. Further resections are performed based on preprocedural planning and real-time echocardiographic evaluations. After completing the SM, the provocation test is repeated. If the results are unsatisfactory, additional resections are performed. The apical puncture is then closed with purse-string sutures.

The technique for transaortic on-pump SM has been described previously.8,21 In brief, following median sternotomy, initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass, and cardioplegic arrest, an oblique aortotomy is performed and extended toward the base of the noncoronary sinus. Then the septum is incised using a number 10 knife blade, beginning just to the right of the nadir of the right aortic cusp and extending the incision upward and leftward toward the hinge point of the anterior mitral leaflet. The septectomy is further lengthened toward the LV apex. The pressure gradient between the left ventricle and the aorta is measured at rest and after premature ventricular contraction, both before and after SM, using direct needle manometry and intraoperative TEE to ensure an adequate reduction in LVOT gradient. After initial myectomy, if there is a residual gradient of >15 to 20 mm Hg associated with SAM, cardiopulmonary bypass is resumed for further septal excision.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR); categorical variables, as frequency and percentage. Differences between the 2 study groups with respect to baseline, operative, and early postoperative measures were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, trend tests for ordinal variables, and the Pearson χ2 tests (or Fisher's exact tests when an expected cell frequency was ≤1) for categorical variables. For echocardiographic variables (peak LVOT gradient, basal septal thickness, and posterior wall thickness), the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare pre- and postoperative measurements within each group. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. Compared to patients who underwent transaortic on-pump SM, those who underwent transapical beating-heart SM were younger (median age, 57.0 years vs 60.2 years; P < .001), had lower body mass index (median, 25.3 kg/m2 vs 31.5 kg/m2; P < .001), and were less symptomatic (NYHA functional class III/IV limitation, 48.0% vs 87.5%; P < .001). These patients also were less likely to have comorbid illnesses and intensive medical treatment. Postprandial symptom exacerbation was reported more frequently in patients who underwent transapical beating-heart SM, and renal disease was more prevalent in this group (5.0% vs 0.5%; P = .006). Importantly, a similar proportion of patients in both groups had a history of prior septal reduction procedures, including SM and alcohol septal ablation.

Patients who underwent transapical beating-heart SM also had less severe LV hypertrophy than those who underwent transaortic on-pump SM, as reflected by both the lower posterior wall thickness (9 mm [IQR, 9-10 mm] vs 12 mm [IQR, 10-14 mm]; P < .001) and the lower LV mass index (76.4 g/m2 [IQR, 65.2-97.0 g/m2] vs 134.0 g/m2 [IQR, 108.0-161.0 g/m2]; P < .001). However, there was no difference in preoperative basal septal thickness or E/e′ ratio between the 2 groups, and the difference in LV diastolic dimension was not clinically significant (45 mm [IQR, 43-48 mm] vs 46 mm [IQR, 43-50 mm]). Compared to patients who underwent transapical beating-heart SM, those who underwent transaortic on-pump SM had significantly higher maximal left atrial volumes (92.0 mL [IQR, 73.0-116.0 mL] vs 81.5 mL [IQR, 68.1-101.2 mL]; P = .003).

Among the patients who underwent transaortic on-pump SM, the median cardiopulmonary bypass time was 35 minutes (IQR, 27-47 minutes), and the median aortic cross-clamp time was 22 minutes (IQR, 17-30 minutes). Five patients (2.5%) in the transapical beating-heart SM group required conversion to median sternotomy. The median weight of resected myocardium was significantly greater in the transapical beating-heart SM group compared to the transaortic on-pump SM group (3.7 g [IQR, 2.4-5.1 g] vs 2.2 g [IQR, 1.8-2.9 g]; P < .001).

Among the 186 patients (93%) in the transapical beating-heart SM group with paired measurements, there was a significant reduction in the median LVOT gradient, from 102 mm Hg preoperatively to 15 mm Hg postoperatively on prehospital dismissal transthoracic echocardiography (P < .001) (Figure 1). Similarly, in the transaortic on-pump SM group, 184 patients (92%) with paired measurements showed a decrease in median LVOT gradient, from 88 mm Hg preoperatively to 12 mm Hg postoperatively (P < .001). Among patients with postoperative assessments, procedural success based on relief of LVOT obstruction was similar in the 2 groups (P = .445). Procedure-related morbidity and mortality are summarized in Table 4. The transapical beating-heart SM group had a shorter ICU stay (median, 24.0 hours [IQR, 20.4-30.6 hours] vs 26.0 hours [IQR, 22.2-48.1 hours]; P < .001). Notably, fewer patients in the transapical beating-heart SM group required RBC transfusions (8.0% vs 22.0%; P < .001), and these patients had a higher median serum hemoglobin level at the time of hospital dismissal (12.9 g/dL [IQR, 11.4-14.4 g/dL] vs 10.0 g/dL [IQR, 9.1-11.4 g/dL]; P < .001). Overall, the transapical beating-heart SM group had a longer median postoperative hospital stay (11 days vs 5 days; P < .001); however, this was associated with a lower frequency of rehospitalization within 30 days of hospital dismissal (1% vs 9%; P < .001). Overall 30-day mortality was similar in the 2 groups, with 1 death (0.5%) in the transapical beating-heart SM group and 2 deaths (1.0%) in the transaortic on-pump SM group (P = .562).

Discussion

Although previous studies have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of transapical beating-heart SM,17, 18, 19 data directly comparing short-term outcomes between transapical beating-heart SM and transaortic on-pump SM remain lacking. In this intention-to-treat analysis, we found that transapical beating-heart myectomy was associated with slightly shorter ICU stays, a lower incidence of postoperative atrial fibrillation, and fewer RBC transfusions; however, 30-day mortality was similar in the 2 groups, as were rates of new-onset renal failure requiring dialysis and complete heart block that necessitated permanent pacemaker implantation. Importantly, hemodynamic outcomes, including relief of LVOT obstruction and associated SAM-mediated MR, were comparable in the 2 groups.

There were notable differences in the clinical characteristics and care protocols of the 2 groups of patients with oHCM undergoing SPT. Patients from Tongji Hospital were younger, were slightly less symptomatic, and had higher preoperative LVOT gradients, perhaps related to less intensive medical therapy with beta-blocking medications. Hospital confinement was longer in the transapical beating-heart myectomy cohort owing to institutional requirements, which likely influenced the readmission rate and predismissal serum hemoglobin levels. Despite these differences, a comparison of the SRT methods seems valid, as operations were performed by experienced teams in each institution. Until randomized trials are performed, the present study provides the best available comparative data on the 2 methods for surgical SRT.

Data from nonrandomized, retrospective studies of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), both with and without the use of cardiopulmonary bypass, have suggested that avoiding the deleterious systemic inflammatory effects of the extracorporeal circuit and aortic manipulation in off-pump CABG may significantly reduce short-term mortality and morbidity compared to conventional CABG.22, 23, 24 Similarly, in our study, the transapical beating-heart myectomy procedure was associated with lower rates of postoperative atrial fibrillation and RBC transfusion and shorter ICU stays, although no differences were found in the incidence of postoperative stroke or 30-day mortality. The lower-than-expected incidence of postoperative atrial fibrillation may be attributed to the avoidance of atrial trauma and less intrapericardial blood accumulation and effusion.25

Given the choice, virtually all patients would prefer a minimally invasive approach over an approach requiring a more extensive, sternal-splitting incision. This assumes that the outcomes of both methods are generally comparable and that there is an advantage regarding minimizing pain and recovery time from the operation. The least invasive method for SRT in oHCM is alcohol septal ablation, but the hemodynamic results and symptom relief are not equivalent to those achieved by SM.26 Importantly, our study shows that early hemodynamic outcomes of transapical beating-heart SM are comparable to those of transaortic on-pump SM. Owing to differences in postoperative care protocols, we could not compare pain scores or analgesic use among patients undergoing transapical beating-heart SM and those undergoing the standard transaortic approach. Indeed, quantifying patient experience will be a crucial aspect of future studies comparing this novel myectomy technique with the standard transsternal approach. It is notable that in one study of mitral valve surgery, minimally invasive methods were associated with reduced opioid use but not with decreased postoperative pain scores compared to transsternal repair.27 Many patients undergoing minimally invasive procedures have unrealistic expectations about recovery time and pain levels.

The broader application of SM for managing symptomatic patients with oHCM has been limited by the relatively small number of cardiac surgeons with sufficient experience in performing the operation. Several studies have demonstrated a clear association between institutional volume of myectomies and patient outcomes, but in North America, 95% of centers perform fewer than 5 myectomies per year.28 Many surgeons with limited experience with SM are unsure of the thickness or extent of septal myocardium that must be resected to effectively relieve LVOT obstruction. A clear advantage of performing myectomy on a beating heart is the ability to use intraoperative TEE to provide real-time hemodynamic assessment of the LVOT gradient, ensuring that the septectomy is neither excessive nor insufficient. In addition, in some patients with midventricular or long-segment subaortic hypertrophy, a combination of transaortic and transapical incisions may be required to provide adequate relief of LVOT obstruction, increasing the complexity and risk of the surgery.29,30 Again, the transapical approach may be beneficial to selectively target the midventricular and apical septal regions (zones 2 and 3).20 The transapical approach also may offer a more efficient method for septal reduction in patients with small aortic annuli and patients who have undergone aortic valve replacement but have residual dynamic LVOT obstruction.31,32 Given these considerations, the novel transapical beating-heart SM approach may represent an important advance in expanding surgical options for SRT in symptomatic patients with oHCM.

A key takeaway from the present study is that subaortic LVOT obstruction almost always can be relieved by adequate septectomy performed through an aortotomy or transapically. Debate among surgeons is ongoing regarding the need for concomitant intervention on the mitral valve or papillary muscle at the time of myectomy.6 This study adds to the literature by demonstrating that an extended SM adequately relieves LVOT obstruction in most patients.

As with transaortic SM, success with transapical beating-heart SM will require a deep understanding of oHCM physiology and the support of an experienced echocardiographer for intraoperative guidance. It remains to be seen whether this novel technique for performing SM can be widely adopted globally with the excellent results documented in this report. Transapical beating-heart SM with echocardiography-guided, tailored resections has the potential to expedite the learning curve for surgical SRT, enabling novice and less-experienced cardiac surgeons in low-volume centers to perform an adequate SM successfully and ultimately promoting the broader dissemination of surgical care for oHCM.

Study Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. First, the retrospective design inherently introduces selection bias. Although consecutive patients were included, the choice of surgical approach (transapical beating-heart SM vs transaortic on-pump SM) was not randomized, and differences in baseline characteristics between the 2 groups might have influenced the outcomes. For instance, patients undergoing transapical beating-heart SM were younger, had a lower body mass index, and had less severe symptoms compared to those undergoing transaortic on-pump SM, which could have introduced confounding variables when comparing procedural success and morbidity. In addition, such factors as the experience of the surgical teams, institutional practices, and varying standards of perioperative care might have impacted outcomes. Furthermore, the learning curve associated with the transapical beating-heart SM procedure, although partially accounted for by selecting the most recent 200 consecutive patients from the cohort, still might have influenced the early results of this procedure.

Another limitation is the lack of long-term follow-up data, as the study focused primarily on early postoperative outcomes. Longer-term data on survival, recurrence of LVOT obstruction, and quality of life are needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the benefits and risks associated with transapical beating-heart SM. Finally, although the sample size was sufficient for statistical analysis, the patient cohort was limited to those treated at 2 large academic institutions, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other institutions or populations.

In summary, although this study provides valuable insights into the comparative outcomes of transapical beating-heart SM and transaortic on-pump SM, future prospective, multi-institutional trials with longer follow-up are needed to validate these findings further and assess the long-term outcomes of this novel procedure.

Conclusions

In this retrospective intention-to-treat analysis, transapical beating-heart SM was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative atrial fibrillation, fewer RBC transfusions, and shorter ICU stays compared to transaortic on-pump SM. Importantly, 30-day mortality was acceptably low at ≤1% with both techniques, which provided comparable hemodynamic relief, as evidenced by similar reductions in LVOT gradient. These findings suggest that transapical beating-heart SM may benefit short-term recovery without compromising hemodynamic outcomes; however, further prospective studies with longer follow-up are needed to validate these results and assess the long-term impact of this novel surgical SPT method.
