Factors Influencing Sleep Quality in Postoperative Urological Patients and its Correlation with Ward Noise Exposure Levels
Authors: Jun Shen, Xun Xie, KuiYuan Zhang, YouYou Mu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to identify factors influencing sleep quality and examine the correlation between sleep quality and ward noise exposure levels in postoperative urological patients.Methods:A retrospective cohort study was conducted by analysing clinical data from 182 patients who underwent urological surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine between January 2021 and December 2024. The participants were stratified into two groups according to their average postoperative Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS) scores: poor sleep quality group (PSQ, AIS > 6) and good sleep quality group (GSQ, AIS ≤ 6). Comparative analysis of clinical variables was performed, with multivariate logistic regression employed to identify the risk factors for poor sleep quality. Correlation between sleep quality and ward noise exposure levels was assessed using Pearson’s correlation analysis.Results:Among the 182 postoperative urological patients, 82 and 100 were categorised into the PSQ and GSQ groups, respectively. The overall mean ward noise exposure level was 61.55 ± 7.75 dB, and the mean AIS score was 6.74 ± 1.65. The PSQ group showed significantly higher AIS scores, noise exposure levels, age, rates of not placed intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (IV-PCA) catheters, postoperative length of stay (PLOS) and anxiety/depression scores than the GSQ group (all P < 0.05). No other baseline characteristics differed significantly. Multivariate logistic regression confirmed age, IV-PCA retention, PLOS, anxiety/depression score and noise exposure as significant predictors of sleep quality, with noise exposure being the strongest independent predictor (OR > 1, P < 0.05). A significant positive correlation existed between AIS score and noise levels (r > 0, P < 0.001).Conclusion:Postoperative sleep quality in patients who underwent urological surgery requires improvement and is significantly correlated with ward noise exposure.

INTRODUCTION

Urological surgical procedures, encompassing interventions for renal, ureteral, bladder, urethral and male reproductive system disorders, effectively enhance patient health status and quality of life.[1] Sleep constitutes a fundamental physiological requirement, and high-quality postoperative sleep is critical for recovery in patients who underwent traumatic surgery.[2] However, patients who had urological surgery are subjected to multiple influencing factors—including surgical trauma, pain, catheter-related irritation and environmental changes—which contribute to varying degrees of sleep quality deterioration postoperatively. Clinical manifestations, occurring for at least 1 postoperative day, include sleep fragmentation, frequent nocturnal awakenings with difficulty falling back asleep, insufficient sleep duration, early morning awakening and circadian rhythm disturbances; collectively, they adversely affect patient prognosis.[3] Postoperative sleep disturbances compromise immune and endocrine functions, hindering effective tissue and organ repair whilst prolonging recovery. They also adversely affect psychological well-being and physiological homeostasis, leading to fatigue and significantly impeding postoperative rehabilitation and quality of life. Consequently, clinical attention to sleep quality in this population is imperative, and identifying modifiable influencing factors is crucial for implementing targeted interventions to improve prognosis.

Environmental factors within hospital wards, particularly noise pollution, represent significant extrinsic determinants of inpatient sleep quality. Hospital wards frequently exhibit elevated noise levels, primarily originating from medical equipment alarms, healthcare staff conversations and therapeutic or nursing interventions.[4] Domestic research demonstrated that noise management interventions improve sleep quality and mitigate negative emotional states in patients undergoing open thoracic surgery for lung cancer, thereby facilitating enhanced recovery.[5] Nevertheless, quantitative evidence regarding the relationship between ward noise exposure and sleep quality remains limited for the specific cohort of postoperative urological patients. Therefore, the present study was designed to analyse the risk factors affecting sleep quality following urological surgery and quantitatively examine the relationship between postoperative sleep quality and ward noise exposure levels in surgical patients. The results will inform evidence-based noise control strategies to improve sleep outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample Selection

From January 2021 to December 2024, a preliminary screening identified 200 eligible patient medical records within the specified timeframe. Following the application of predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 182 patients were ultimately included in the analysis. Eighteen patients were excluded for the following reasons: preoperative use of hypnotic medications (n = 8), postoperative transfer to non-urological departments prior to discharge (n = 6), incomplete or missing medical records (n = 2) and severe comorbidities/complications contraindicating continued study participation (n = 2).

General Information

A retrospective cohort study was conducted by analysing the clinical data of 182 patients who underwent urological surgery at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine between January 2021 and December 2024. This study complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to medical ethics principles regarding informed consent and confidentiality.[6] The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2025-01-20). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

(1) The inclusion criteria were as follows: complete clinical and radiological documentation; minimum hospital stay duration of 3 days and preoperative good sleep status (Athens Insomnia Scale [AIS] score < 6) and ability to complete questionnaire assessments independently.

(2) The exclusion criteria were as follows: preoperative diagnosis of obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) or other severe sleep disorders; severe dysfunction of major organs (cardiac, hepatic or pulmonary) confirmed by clinical staging, laboratory parameters or comorbid psychiatric disorders/cognitive impairments; prolonged use (>3 consecutive days) of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, opioid analgesics or antidepressant/anxiolytic medications within 1 month prior to surgery; major postoperative complications within 72 hours (e.g., sepsis, unplanned reoperation, haemorrhage requiring transfusion); concurrent participation in other interventional trials or withdrawal/loss to follow-up resulting in incomplete data and significant hearing impairment (pure-tone audiometry average threshold > 40 dB).

METHODS

The data were derived from medical records; treatment and assessment databases and included sleep disorder conditions, noise exposure conditions and basic information.

Assessment of Sleep Disturbances

Postoperative sleep quality was evaluated using the AIS,[7] which demonstrates a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.900 and a retest reliability of 0.923. This instrument comprises eight items as follows: sleep induction difficulties, nocturnal awakenings, early morning awakening, total sleep duration, overall sleep quality, daytime fatigue, daytime emotional instability and impaired daytime attention/memory. Each item is scored from 0 (no symptoms) to 3 (severe symptoms), yielding a cumulative score ranging from 0 to 24. Assessments were conducted daily at 8:00 AM from the first postoperative day until discharge, with the mean values calculated for analysis. The AIS scores were interpreted as follows: 0–3 (no clinically significant sleep disturbance), 4–6 (subthreshold insomnia) and 7–24 (clinically significant insomnia). Patients with mean AIS scores >6 were categorised into the poor sleep quality (PSQ) group, and those scoring ≤6 were assigned to the good sleep quality (GSQ) group.

Basic Data Collection

Data, including baseline demographic characteristics, hospitalisation course documentation, medical orders, nursing notes, anaesthesia records, preoperative and postoperative assessment forms and sleep quality evaluation scales, were collected from the electronic medical record system. Two experienced physicians independently extracted the data and established the database, with subsequent cross-verification performed to ensure accuracy. The basic data were as follows: gender (male, female); age (≥ 60 years old, < 60 years old); American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification (I, II); place of residence (rural, urban); presence of comorbidities (diabetes, coronary heart disease, hypertension) (present, absent); educational level (high school or above, below high school); disease type (kidney disease, ureteral disease, bladder disease, urethral disease and male reproductive system disease); malnutrition (yes, no); body mass index; operation time; placement of intravenous analgesic pump (placed, not placed) and postoperative hospital stay. Postoperative anxiety and depression scores were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale[8] to evaluate the patient’s anxiety and depression levels during hospitalisation (assessed once daily at 8 AM from the day after surgery until discharge, with the average score taken). The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the total scale is 0.903, and the retest reliability is 0.890. The scale includes the following two subscales: depression and anxiety, each subscale consists of seven questions, each scored using a 4-point Likert scale (0–3) with a score range of 0–21. The higher the score, the more severe the anxiety and depression. For the measurement of noise exposure level in the ward, the HS6288E multifunctional noise analyser (Shanghai Gaozhi Precision Instrument Co., Ltd.) was placed at a position more than 1 m away from the wall and other reflective surfaces and 1.2 m above the ground. The measurement time was set to 24 hours, the measurement data were automatically stored, and the equivalent noise level in the ward was automatically determined (i.e., average value). All measurement instruments were calibrated daily without error.

Quality Control

Standardisation of operation. A double-check mechanism was adopted for data recording, and audio recording was simultaneously activated during data entry to facilitate traceability in the later stage. For people with low educational levels, trained explainers were provided to explain the meaning of each question item by item. Instructions must be uniform but not imply any answer tendency. Personnel training. Each tester received systematic training, including theoretical training, practical operation training and case study. Scenario simulation tests were set up, and only when the tester’s scale testing qualification rate and data rework rate meet the standards can they start the scale testing work.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were subjected to normality assessment via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent samples t-tests or paired t-tests were applied for between-group or within-group comparisons, respectively, when the homogeneity of variance was confirmed. Categorical variables were presented as n (%) and compared via χ2 tests. Variables yielding P < 0.1 in univariate analyses were entered into multivariate logistic regression models to identify the independent predictors of poor postoperative sleep quality. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to test multicollinearity among the independent variables. All VIF values were less than 5, indicating that the collinearity problem was not serious. Pearson correlation analysis quantified the association between ward noise exposure levels and sleep quality. P < 0.05 defined statistical significance.

RESULTS

Sleep Disturbances and Ward Noise Exposure

Among 182 urological surgery patients, 82 (45.1%) were classified as having poor sleep quality, and 100 (54.9%) demonstrated good sleep quality. The overall mean ward noise exposure level was 61.55 ± 7.75 dB, and the mean AIS score was 6.74 ± 1.65. The PSQ group exhibited significantly higher AIS scores and ward noise exposure levels compared with the GSQ group (P < 0.05; Table 1).

Multivariate Logistic Regression Analysis

The following variables demonstrating associations with sleep quality in the univariate analyses (P < 0.1) were included as covariates in the multivariate analysis: age (categorised as 0 = < 60 years, 1 = ≥ 60 years); IV-PCA catheter retention status (1 = not retained, 0 = retained); (PLOS; postoperative anxiety score; postoperative depression score and ward noise exposure level. Categorical variables were dummy-coded, and continuous variables were analysed in their raw form. Sleep quality served as the dependent variable (1 = poor sleep quality, 0 = good sleep quality). Prior to modelling, multicollinearity among all independent variables was assessed, with VIF values all < 5, indicating the absence of substantial collinearity that could compromise model stability.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that age (OR = 3.056, 95% CI: 1.660–5.624, P < 0.001); IV-PCA catheter non-retention (OR = 2.733, 95% CI: 1.756–4.253, P < 0.001); PLOS (OR = 1.925, 95% CI: 1.040–2.338, P = 0.001); postoperative anxiety score (OR = 1.780, 95% CI: 1.403–2.258, P = 0.005); postoperative depression score (OR = 1.312, 95% CI: 1.021–1.695, P = 0.026) and ward noise exposure level (OR = 3.719, 95% CI: 1.890-5.914, P < 0.001) were significant independent predictors of postoperative sleep quality (all OR >1, P < 0.05). Notably, ward noise exposure demonstrated the strongest effect size among these variables. Detailed results are presented in Table 3.

DISCUSSION

Suboptimal sleep quality in patients following traumatic surgical procedures adversely affects mental acuity, physical stamina and nutritional intake. It also contributes to immunosuppression, endocrine dysregulation, delayed wound healing, protracted convalescence, elevated risks of postoperative complications and diminished quality of life.[910] This investigation revealed a substantial prevalence of sleep disturbances (45.05%) among patients who underwent urological surgery, underscoring the pervasiveness of this clinical challenge.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified age, IV-PCA catheter retention, PLOS, anxiety/depression scores and ward noise exposure as significant predictors of impaired postoperative sleep quality. The underlying mechanisms warrant elaboration. (1) Age: Santos-Orlandi et al.[11] documented age-dependent deterioration in sleep architecture, aligning with our findings. This phenomenon may be attributed to age-related decline in neurological regulatory functions and diminished melatonin secretion − a critical neurohormone facilitating sleep initiation. Furthermore, the progressive degeneration of organ systems and attenuated metabolic activity in elderly patients may impair neural conduction and reduce pain tolerance.[12] Given the inherently traumatic nature of urological procedures, such physiological vulnerabilities exacerbate postoperative discomfort and thereby compromise sleep continuity. (2) Placement of intravenous analgesic pump: Intravenous analgesic pump provides sustained analgesia through regulated drug delivery, maintaining therapeutic plasma concentrations. Patients without intravenous analgesic pump experience uncontrolled postoperative pain, which fragments sleep architecture through frequent awakenings and reduced sleep efficiency.[13] Additionally, pain-induced inflammatory cascades release pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β and TNF-α). These mediators activate cerebral microglia, promoting neuroinflammation that disrupts blood–brain barrier integrity. Subsequent central influx of peripheral cytokines further destabilises sleep–wake regulation.[14] (3) Postoperative hospital stay: Prolonged hospitalisation subjects patients to environmental disruptions (e.g., altered circadian cues, intrusive clinical care activities) and maladaptive behavioural patterns (e.g. irregular sleep–wake cycles, physical inactivity). These iatrogenic factors collectively degrade sleep homeostasis. (4) Postoperative anxiety and depression: Patients may have negative emotions such as anxiety and depression due to pain and weakness after surgery.[15] Heightened anxiety/depression induces hyperarousal states, clinically manifesting as sleep initiation difficulties, sleep maintenance insomnia, early morning awakenings and reduced total sleep time.[16] Furthermore, these mood disorders potentiate disturbing dream phenomena (e.g., nightmares), which further fragment restorative sleep.[17]

This study revealed that the mean ward noise exposure level among 182 urological surgery patients was 61.55 ± 7.75 dB, substantially exceeding recommended thresholds and underscoring the severity of acoustic pollution in unmitigated urological surgical wards. The PSQ group was exposed to significantly higher noise levels compared with the GSQ group. Notably, ward noise exposure demonstrated the strongest independent effect on postoperative sleep quality [odds ratio (OR): 3.719; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.890–5.914), which may be attributed to contextual factors such as ward location and medical equipment density/layout. The significant positive correlation between AIS scores and noise levels aligns with the findings of Tonna et al.,[18] confirming the critical association between acoustic environmental quality and sleep outcomes in this surgical population. The underlying mechanisms may involve the following aspects: ward noise originates from diverse and complex sources, including medical equipment and instrument alarms, staff conversations, therapeutic and nursing procedures, mechanical ventilation systems, external environmental transmissions and patient-generated noises. Prolonged exposure to high noise levels can compromise cardiovascular stability and induce endocrine dysregulation, resulting in a hyperarousal state that ultimately degrades sleep quality and elevates AIS scores.[19] Noise exposure concurrently heightens neuronal pain sensitivity, amplifying postoperative pain perception that significantly disrupts sleep architecture.[2021] Furthermore, chronic exposure to elevated noise levels in ward environments adversely affects emotional states, increases psychological distress and exacerbates anxiety and depression − all of which collectively contribute to impaired sleep quality.[22]

In response to the identified factors, the following interventions are recommended to improve patient outcomes. Firstly, healthcare providers should offer targeted support for elderly patients undergoing urological surgery, such as maintaining regular sleep routines, optimising sleeping environments, avoiding overstimulation, regulating pre-sleep diet and encouraging mild physical activity to promote relaxation. Secondly, appropriate use and extended retention of intravenous analgesic pumps should be considered under medical guidance to alleviate postoperative pain and thereby enhance sleep quality. Thirdly, perioperative care should include evidence-based practices to accelerate recovery, supplemented by psychological support to reduce anxiety and depression. Fourthly, comprehensive noise management should be implemented in wards, including installing sound-absorbing and insulating materials in ceilings and walls, replacing outdated noisy equipment, utilising soundproof doors/windows and adding damping devices to movable items such as carts and beds. Medical device alarms (e.g., monitors, ventilators) should be configured to activate only in critical situations. Staff should communicate quietly − using wireless communication systems instead of calling aloud − and refine nursing protocols to minimise disruptions (e.g., gentle handling, reducing nighttime procedures and clustering non-urgent tasks). Noisy equipment should be placed away from patient areas, and dedicated quiet rooms may be considered where feasible. Patients must be provided with high-quality earplugs or noise-cancelling headphones and educated to cooperate with care activities. Finally, a dedicated acoustic management team should be established to regularly monitor noise levels, evaluate effectiveness and dynamically adjust strategies based on audit results.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, as a retrospective analysis constrained by available data volume and completeness, this work did not perform an a priori sample size calculation, potentially resulting in an underpowered study. Secondly, the retrospective design may introduce selection and information biases. The relatively small sample size from a single centre may limit the generalisability of our findings. Therefore, large-scale, multicentre randomised controlled trials are warranted to validate these conclusions. Thirdly, analyses were not stratified by surgical approach (e.g., minimally invasive vs. open procedures). Future studies should investigate how the magnitude of surgical trauma influences sleep quality. Fourthly, the use of an integrated noise dosimeter measuring average acoustic energy over 24-hour periods may have underestimated the disruptive effect of nocturnal noise − particularly transient peak noise levels − by averaging typically high daytime readings with ideally low nighttime levels. Future research should differentiate diurnal/nocturnal periods and analyse time-specific noise profiles in relation to sleep architecture.

CONCLUSION

Postoperative sleep quality requires significant enhancement in patients undergoing urological surgery and demonstrates a strong positive correlation with ward noise exposure levels. Clinicians should prioritise vigilance for elderly patients, those without intravenous analgesic pump, individuals experiencing prolonged postoperative hospitalisation and patients exhibiting elevated anxiety/depression scores.
