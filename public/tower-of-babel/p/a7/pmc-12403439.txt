Partner involvement and emotional and informational support gaps as predictors of postpartum birth trauma symptoms: a multi-center cross-sectional study of 230 women at 42 days postpartum
Authors: Lingling Li, Lanlan Xiao, Xu Yuan, Jie Wu, Jinxiao Li, Xiuping Chen, Xiaohui Zhai, Bei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostpartum birth trauma symptoms, encompassing psychological and physical sequelae related to childbirth experiences, pose a significant threat to maternal well-being. These symptoms, as measured in this study, are not equivalent to a clinical diagnosis of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) but reflect self-reported experiences based on the City Birth Trauma Scale.MethodsThis multi-center cross-sectional survey enrolled 230 women at 42 days postpartum from four hospitals in Shanghai between February and July 2024. Birth trauma symptoms were assessed using the Chinese City Birth Trauma Scale. Emotional and informational support expectation gaps were calculated as the difference between the importance score and the actual support score in each domain of the Postpartum Social Support Scale (gap = importance − actual), with larger positive values indicating greater unmet needs. Partner involvement was classified as high, medium, or low. Multiple linear regression identified independent predictors of trauma severity.ResultsBirth trauma symptoms were assessed using the Chinese City Birth Trauma Scale. The full sample was analyzed for symptom severity, while the proportion of participants scoring above the ≥ 28 cut-off (Brazilian validation study) was used to estimate the prevalence of clinically significant symptoms. Of all participants, 54.3% scored ≥ 28, indicating clinically significant childbirth-related PTSD symptom levels on the City Birth Trauma Scale, with a mean trauma score of 45.12 ± 16.25. Larger emotional (β = 0.102, p = 0.018) and informational (β = 0.158, p = 0.024) support gaps were independently associated with greater trauma severity, as were low education (β = 0.281, p < 0.001), low household income (β = 0.172, p = 0.014), formula feeding (β = 0.180, p = 0.010), and low partner involvement (β = 0.165, p = 0.018). High partner involvement demonstrated a protective effect comparable to socioeconomic factors. The final model explained 29% of the variance in trauma scores.ConclusionsInadequate partner involvement and larger emotional and informational support gaps are significantly associated with greater postpartum birth trauma severity. Interventions should prioritize partner-inclusive care and targeted support to address unmet informational and emotional needs. Routine psychosocial screening at postpartum follow-up may facilitate early identification and intervention for at-risk women.Keywords: Postpartum women, Birth trauma, Partner involvement, Emotional support, Informational support

Introduction

Postpartum birth trauma, encompassing both psychological and physical injuries sustained during or after childbirth, is increasingly recognized as a major maternal health concern worldwide [1]. Defined within the framework of DSM-5–based post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) criteria, birth trauma affects not only the immediate postpartum experience but also long-term mental health, mother–infant bonding, and family well-being. Global prevalence estimates range widely from 9 to 44%, reflecting differences in diagnostic tools, cultural perceptions, and healthcare systems [2, 3]. Recent large-scale meta-analyses and multi-country cohort studies published between 2020 and 2023 have confirmed that CB-PTSD remains a substantial public health concern, with pooled prevalence estimates of 6–10% in unselected postpartum populations and over 15% in women with high-risk obstetric factors, and have highlighted persistent cross-cultural differences in symptom presentation and risk profiles [4–7]. In China, the rapid transformation of obstetric care practices and shifting sociocultural norms have heightened awareness of maternal mental health, yet reliable national prevalence data remain limited [8, 9]. The 42-day postpartum period represents a particularly critical phase: physiologically, most reproductive organs (excluding lactation-related breast changes) have returned to a pre-pregnancy state, and psychologically, mothers are transitioning from acute postpartum recovery to longer-term adaptation [10]. This timing coincides with the standard postpartum check recommended by the World Health Organization and many national guidelines, providing an optimal window for comprehensive assessment of maternal physical and mental health [11, 12]. Moreover, previous longitudinal studies have shown that while some childbirth-related post-traumatic stress disorder (CB-PTSD) symptoms may emerge soon after delivery, their persistence or exacerbation often becomes more apparent several weeks later, with clinically significant symptoms frequently detectable by six weeks postpartum [13–15]. Recent longitudinal evidence from the United Kingdom, Australia, and Nordic countries (2020–2024) has shown that symptom trajectories established in the first six weeks postpartum strongly predict CB-PTSD persistence up to one year, underscoring the importance of early identification and intervention [16]. This period thus offers a balance between physical recovery and the continued presence of emotional responses, enabling the detection of unresolved trauma that may otherwise be missed in the immediate postpartum period. Evidence suggests that unresolved trauma during this window can predispose women to chronic PTSD, postpartum depression, and diminished parenting confidence, thereby influencing infant development trajectories and family functioning [17, 18]. In this study, “postpartum birth trauma symptoms” refers to the severity of self-reported psychological and physical sequelae assessed by the City Birth Trauma Scale, which is a validated screening tool based on DSM-5 PTSD criteria, and does not constitute a formal clinical diagnosis [19]. Moreover, we use the term “clinically significant symptoms” to describe scores above the ≥ 28 threshold on the City BiTS, following the Brazilian validation, while recognizing that this is not equivalent to a confirmed clinical diagnosis [20].

Social support has long been identified as a buffering factor against psychological distress following childbirth [21]. It is multidimensional, encompassing material, emotional, informational, and comparison-based forms of assistance, each of which can influence maternal coping differently. Emotional support, defined as expressions of empathy, reassurance, and understanding, can directly mitigate anxiety and fear responses [22]. Informational support, involving the provision of accurate, timely, and relevant health guidance, empowers mothers to make informed decisions about recovery and newborn care [23]. However, despite the conceptual clarity of these domains, many studies aggregate all types of social support into a single composite score, obscuring the specific contributions of each. Moreover, the concept of a “support expectation gap” — the difference between the support a woman deems important and the support she actually receives — remains underexplored, especially in the postpartum context. While earlier work has shown that larger overall gaps correlate with worse psychological outcomes, few studies have disentangled the relative roles of emotional and informational gaps, which may be the most actionable targets for clinical intervention. In the Chinese context, additional barriers arise from changing family structures, the decline of traditional extended family support systems, and rural–urban disparities in healthcare access. These trends have amplified the mismatch between women’s expectations and actual postpartum support, yet quantitative analyses that isolate specific support dimensions are scarce. A recent study from South Korea has further shown that unmet informational and emotional support needs independently predict CB-PTSD severity, even when controlling for obstetric complications and depressive symptoms [24].

Beyond professional and family-provided support, partner involvement during the perinatal period is emerging as a critical determinant of maternal mental health [25]. High partner engagement — demonstrated through participation in antenatal visits, presence during labor, and active postpartum caregiving — has been associated with reduced maternal stress, enhanced breastfeeding initiation, and lower risk of postpartum mood disorders [26]. However, empirical evidence directly linking partner involvement to postpartum birth trauma symptoms is limited, particularly within Asian populations. Existing studies often treat partner presence as a binary variable (present vs. absent) during delivery, neglecting the quality, consistency, and breadth of involvement across the perinatal continuum [27, 28]. This represents a significant research gap, as partner engagement may interact with other support domains to buffer trauma. In societies undergoing rapid sociocultural transition, such as China, the role of the partner may be particularly pivotal in compensating for weakened extended family networks. By integrating partner involvement into a multidimensional model of social support, researchers can better identify high-risk women and design targeted, family-centered interventions.

Against this backdrop, the present multi-center cross-sectional study was designed to address three major gaps in the literature: (1) the need for dimension-specific analysis of postpartum social support, focusing on emotional and informational domains; (2) the lack of quantitative assessment of partner involvement as an independent predictor of postpartum trauma; and (3) the absence of updated, multi-site data from China reflecting contemporary patterns of support and trauma incidence. By surveying 230 women at 42 days postpartum across four hospitals in Shanghai between February and July 2024, we sought to quantify the prevalence and severity of birth trauma symptoms, characterize the magnitude of emotional and informational support gaps, and evaluate the independent and combined effects of these gaps and partner involvement on trauma severity. We hypothesized that greater emotional and informational support gaps, along with lower partner involvement, would be significantly associated with higher trauma scores, even after adjusting for socioeconomic and obstetric factors. Our findings aim to inform the development of postpartum care models that prioritize high-yield support domains and actively engage partners, thereby contributing to a more holistic, family-centered approach to maternal mental health in China and beyond.

Materials and methods

This study adopted a cross-sectional design and was conducted across four hospitals in Shanghai, including two general hospitals and two specialized maternity hospitals, between February and July 2024. A convenience sampling approach was employed to recruit postpartum women who attended routine 42-day follow-up visits at these institutions. Eligibility criteria included: (1) age 18 years or older; (2) ability to communicate in Mandarin and comprehend the study instruments; (3) completion of a live birth; and (4) completion of the Chinese City Birth Trauma Scale (City BiTS) [29]. All eligible women, including those scoring zero, were invited to participate. In subsequent subgroup analyses, we also examined the prevalence and correlates of clinically significant symptom levels based on a total score ≥ 28, as proposed in a Brazilian validation study, while retaining the full sample for analyses of symptom severity [20]. Women were excluded if they had severe postpartum complications that prevented participation (e.g., postpartum hemorrhage requiring intensive care), significant psychiatric illness diagnosed prior to pregnancy, or a score of zero on the City BiTS.

Sample size estimation was guided by the principle that at least 5–10 participants are required per independent variable in regression analysis. With the Chinese Postpartum Social Support Scale containing 34 items, a minimum of 170 participants was required. Sample size estimation was further supported by an a priori power analysis. Based on previous studies examining the association between postpartum social support and trauma severity, we anticipated a small-to-moderate effect size (f2 = 0.08) in the final multivariable regression model. Using G*Power 3.1 for linear multiple regression with a fixed model and R [2] deviation from zero, and specifying α = 0.05, power (1 − β) = 0.80, and 10 predictors, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 184. Allowing for a potential 20% non-response or invalid questionnaire rate, the adjusted target sample size was 230, which was achieved in the present study. All participants provided written informed consent before data collection, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University (Approval No. 2024SLYS6). The research adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision).

Instruments

Symptoms indicative of childbirth-related psychological and physical distress (“postpartum birth trauma symptoms”) were measured using the City Birth Trauma Scale (City BiTS), originally developed by Ayers et al. [19] based on DSM-5 PTSD criteria and subsequently translated and validated for Chinese populations by Nie et al. [29]. The scale comprises 29 items across two symptom dimensions (birth-related and general), along with criteria for symptom duration, distress, and functional impairment. Responses are scored on a frequency scale from 0 (none) to 3 (≥ 5 times), with higher scores indicating more severe trauma symptoms. In the Chinese validation study, the total scale α was 0.934. In our sample, the total scale α was 0.93, with the birth-related symptom dimension α = 0.92 and the general symptom dimension α = 0.91. These values indicate excellent internal consistency for both instruments in this study.

Postpartum social support was assessed using the Postpartum Social Support Questionnaire (PSSQ) originally developed by Logsdon et al. [30] to measure perceived social support during the postpartum period. The Chinese version of the scale was translated, culturally adapted, and psychometrically validated by Lu et al. [31]. This version evaluates both the importance of and the actual support received in four domains: material, emotional, informational, and comparison support. Each item is rated on a 1–7 Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater perceived importance or greater actual support received. For each domain, an “expectation gap” score was calculated as: expectation gap = importance score – actual support score. A positive gap value indicates that the perceived importance of the support exceeded the support actually received (i.e., unmet expectations), a value of zero indicates that the perceived importance and received support were equal, and a negative value indicates that the received support exceeded the perceived importance. This operationalization is consistent with the original Chinese validation of the PSSQ by Lu et al. [31]in which the importance score is interpreted as a proxy for the individual’s subjective expectation for that type of support, regardless of whether it is expected from partners, family members, or healthcare professionals. Therefore, larger positive gap values reflect a greater degree of perceived unmet need. In the validation study by Zhong et al. [31]Cronbach’s α values for the four subscales ranged from 0.893 to 0.944, with a total scale α of 0.94, indicating high internal consistency. In our sample, Cronbach’s α values for the PSQ subscales were 0.90 (material support), 0.91 (emotional support), 0.92 (informational support), and 0.89 (comparison support), with a total scale α of 0.94, confirming excellent reliability in the current study population.

Partner involvement was assessed via a structured self-report item asking participants to classify their partner’s involvement during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period as high (active participation in prenatal visits, presence at delivery, daily caregiving), medium (inconsistent or partial participation), or low (minimal presence or involvement). This classification method was adapted from existing perinatal partner-support frameworks, with operational definitions provided to participants to enhance reliability.

Data collection

Data collection occurred during scheduled 42-day postpartum clinic visits. Trained research nurses explained the study purpose and procedures to eligible participants in a private consultation room to ensure confidentiality. Demographic and obstetric data—including age, parity, education level, household registration, occupation, monthly household income, primary infant caregiver, and feeding method—were obtained via a structured questionnaire. Participants then completed the City BiTS and PSQ scales electronically using a secure QR-code-linked platform on a tablet provided by the study team. The survey platform incorporated logic checks, required responses for all items, and prevented submission of incomplete questionnaires. To minimize response bias, research staff were available to clarify questions but did not influence responses. For the purpose of statistical analysis, participant age was categorized as < 30 years or ≥ 30 years. This threshold was selected because, in Chinese obstetric practice, age ≥ 30 years for primiparous women is often associated with increased obstetric risk and corresponds to the conventional definition of “advanced maternal age” in many clinical guidelines [32]. Additionally, 30 years approximated the median age in our sample, allowing for balanced subgroup sizes. Monthly household income was dichotomized at 8,000 RMB, which is close to the 2023 Shanghai average per capita monthly disposable income reported by the National Bureau of Statistics, thus providing a contextually relevant socio-economic classification [33].

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed; group comparisons were performed using independent-samples t-tests or one-way ANOVA as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess associations between trauma severity and (1) importance scores, (2) actual support scores, and (3) expectation gap scores in each support domain. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed in three steps: Model 1 examined unadjusted associations between each social support gap score and trauma severity; Model 2 adjusted for demographic covariates (age, education, household income, parity, occupation, household registration, and feeding method); Model 3 further adjusted for partner involvement. Additional models were constructed replacing support gap scores with the corresponding actual support scores to examine their independent effects. All regression results are presented with unstandardized coefficients (B), standardized coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals (CI), t-values, and p-values, regardless of statistical significance. Categorical variables were dummy-coded, and multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF < 5). Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

Results

General demographic characteristics

A total of 230 postpartum women met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed. The sample comprised 126 women (54.8%) younger than 30 years and 104 women (45.2%) aged 30 years or older. Most participants were primiparous (79.1%, n = 182), had a college education or higher (85.2%, n = 196), and reported a household monthly income exceeding 8000 RMB (78.3%, n = 180). Regarding partner involvement, 47.8% (n = 110) reported high involvement, 34.8% (n = 80) medium involvement, and 17.4% (n = 40) low involvement. Feeding practices at 42 days postpartum included breastfeeding in 40.0% (n = 92), mixed feeding in 51.3% (n = 118), and formula feeding in 8.7% (n = 20). These baseline characteristics provided a diverse sample for examining the influence of socioeconomic and family participation factors on postpartum trauma symptoms. The distribution of partner involvement categories allowed for meaningful comparisons between high- and low-support groups, which served as a key control to assess our hypothesis regarding the protective role of partner engagement (Table 1). Table 2 presents demographic comparisons between participants with high vs. low trauma symptom levels. Participants were stratified into high trauma (City BiTS ≥ 28, n = 125) and low trauma (City BiTS < 28, n = 105) groups. As shown in Table 6, women in the high trauma group were more likely to have lower educational attainment (p = 0.002), lower household income (p = 0.001), and formula feeding (p = 0.009). Partner involvement also differed significantly between groups, with low involvement reported by 26.4% of women in the high trauma group compared with only 6.7% in the low trauma group (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed for age or parity.

Birth trauma symptoms measurement

Using the Chinese City Birth Trauma Scale (City BiTS), scores in the total sample (n = 230) ranged from 0 to 112, with a mean of 38.21 ± 18.72. Based on the ≥ 28 cut-off proposed in a Brazilian validation study, 125 women (54.3%) met the threshold for clinically significant levels of childbirth-related PTSD symptoms. These symptoms reflect self-reported severity rather than a formal clinical diagnosis. The total trauma score ranged from 28 to 112, with a mean of 45.12 ± 16.25. When stratified by partner involvement, women reporting low involvement had significantly higher trauma scores than those with medium or high involvement (p < 0.05). This observation directly supports our hypothesis that partner engagement serves as a protective factor against birth trauma symptoms. Additionally, women with lower educational attainment and household income exhibited higher trauma scores, reinforcing the role of socioeconomic status as a determinant of postpartum psychological outcomes.

Social support measurement and expectation gaps

Analysis of the Postpartum Social Support Scale revealed that the mean importance score for total social support was 186.92 ± 39.85, while the mean actual support score was 181.35 ± 39.76, yielding an average expectation gap of 12.04 ± 2.10 (Table 3). Among the four domains, informational support had the largest gap (4.15 ± 0.55), followed closely by emotional support (3.88 ± 0.72). Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated that actual support in emotional (r = −0.328, p < 0.001, Fig. 1) and informational (r = −0.421, p < 0.001, Fig. 2) domains was inversely related to trauma severity, while the corresponding expectation gaps were positively correlated with trauma scores (p < 0.05, Table 4). These results are consistent with our hypothesis that larger emotional and informational support gaps predict greater trauma severity, even after accounting for overall support levels. A complete Pearson correlation matrix of all main variables is provided in Table 5. Trauma severity was inversely correlated with actual emotional support (r = − 0.328, p < 0.01), actual informational support (r = − 0.421, p < 0.01), and partner involvement level (r = − 0.289, p < 0.01). Conversely, trauma severity was positively correlated with emotional support gap (r = 0.167, p < 0.05) and informational support gap (r = 0.159, p < 0.05). Strong correlations were observed between actual support scores and their corresponding gap scores (|r| >0.580, p < 0.01), and between emotional and informational support scores (r = 0.712, p < 0.01).

Partner involvement measurement

Partner involvement emerged as an independent predictor of trauma severity in the multivariate regression model. Compared with high involvement, low involvement was associated with an average increase of 2.21 points in trauma scores (β = 0.165, p = 0.018), after controlling for educational level, household income, feeding method, and social support gaps. This effect was comparable in magnitude to that of low household income and greater than the effect of formula feeding. These findings support the hypothesis that partner engagement independently mitigates postpartum trauma symptoms and interacts synergistically with emotional and informational support to enhance maternal psychological outcomes.

Statistical analysis outcomes and model validation

Stepwise multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations between social support measures and postpartum birth trauma severity. For support expectation gaps (Table 6), in unadjusted models (Model 1), both emotional support gap (B = 0.125, β = 0.118, p = 0.018) and informational support gap (B = 0.154, β = 0.165, p = 0.023) were significantly associated with higher trauma scores. These associations remained significant after adjusting for demographic covariates (Model 2) and persisted in the fully adjusted model including partner involvement (Model 3: emotional support gap, B = 0.108, β = 0.102, p = 0.019; informational support gap, B = 0.118, β = 0.158, p = 0.024). For actual support scores (Table 7), both emotional and informational support showed significant inverse associations with trauma scores in Model 1 (emotional: B = − 0.185, β = −0.165, p = 0.001; informational: B = − 0.201, β = −0.182, p < 0.001). After adjustment for demographics (Model 2) and full adjustment including partner involvement (Model 3), the negative association for informational support remained robust (B = − 0.176, β = −0.162, p = 0.003), whereas the association for emotional support was attenuated but still significant (B = − 0.128, β = −0.118, p = 0.033). Across all models, lower educational level, lower household income, formula feeding, and lower partner involvement were consistently associated with higher trauma severity scores. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values for all predictors were below 2.0, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity. The fully adjusted models explained approximately 28.0–30.0% of the variance in trauma scores (adjusted R [2] range: 0.280–0.300), confirming the robustness of these associations.

Discussion

This multi-center cross-sectional study involving 230 postpartum women at 42 days after delivery identified several independent predictors of postpartum birth trauma severity. Consistent with our hypothesis, greater emotional and informational support expectation gaps, as well as lower partner involvement, were significantly associated with higher trauma scores, independent of socioeconomic status, feeding method, and obstetric history. Notably, partner involvement emerged as a protective factor of comparable magnitude to income and education, underscoring the critical role of interpersonal dynamics within the postpartum recovery context. The results also reaffirmed previously reported associations between low socioeconomic indicators, formula feeding, and adverse maternal psychological outcomes, but extended this knowledge by quantifying the additive influence of partner engagement on trauma severity [34]. These findings strengthen the argument for integrating partner-focused interventions into standard postpartum care protocols. In sensitivity analyses using actual support scores instead of expectation gaps, informational support remained a significant negative predictor of trauma severity after full adjustment, whereas emotional support was attenuated and did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that while unmet expectations in both domains are important, actual receipt of informational support may be particularly critical for mitigating trauma symptoms. Across all models, the associations of partner involvement and socioeconomic variables with trauma severity were consistent, indicating robust relationships independent of social support measures [35].

The relationship between social support and postpartum psychological outcomes has been well documented, with most prior studies emphasizing composite support scores rather than domain-specific analyses [36, 37]. By separately evaluating emotional and informational support, this study provides more granular insight into which aspects of support most strongly influence trauma severity. Emotional support mitigates postpartum stress by providing reassurance, empathy, and a sense of belonging, while informational support equips women with the knowledge needed to manage physical recovery and newborn care effectively [38]. The finding that informational support gaps exerted a slightly stronger association with trauma severity than emotional support gaps aligns with previous reports in Chinese populations, where health literacy disparities and limited access to high-quality postpartum education remain persistent challenges [35]. Our results also extend earlier international research by explicitly quantifying the magnitude of these gaps and their independent contributions after adjusting for partner involvement and other confounders.

Although the importance of partner presence during childbirth has been recognized in some Western studies, partner involvement is often operationalized narrowly, focusing on delivery-room attendance without accounting for prenatal or postpartum contributions [39]. Our study advances the field by adopting a broader definition of partner involvement, incorporating active participation in prenatal visits, delivery support, and postpartum caregiving. This approach allowed us to demonstrate that consistent, multi-phase involvement is associated with lower trauma scores even after controlling for traditional predictors such as education and income. The protective mechanism may be multifactorial: partners can provide continuous emotional reassurance, share caregiving burdens, facilitate timely healthcare access, and serve as advocates within the healthcare system [40]. In the context of modern Chinese society, where extended family support may be diminished due to urban migration and smaller household sizes, partner engagement may play an increasingly pivotal role in maternal recovery [41].

By integrating partner involvement into a multidimensional framework of postpartum social support, this study addresses a notable gap in the literature and offers a more comprehensive model for predicting birth trauma risk. The inclusion of expectation gap measures for both emotional and informational support enables targeted identification of women at risk, which has practical implications for designing individualized intervention programs. Clinically, our findings support a shift from uniform postpartum care packages toward modular, needs-based interventions, wherein women with large informational gaps could be prioritized for structured educational sessions, while those with emotional support deficits might benefit from peer-support programs or mental health counseling. In the context of routine postpartum care programs, these findings underscore the importance of integrating structured screening for emotional and informational support needs into the standard 42-day postpartum check-up. Practical measures could include incorporating brief validated screening tools into maternal health records, ensuring that both the mother and her partner attend postnatal education sessions, and providing targeted referrals to mental health or peer-support services where gaps are identified. Embedding partner-inclusive education modules into routine follow-up may enhance both emotional reassurance and the quality of informational support received. Scientifically, the combined examination of partner involvement and domain-specific support gaps opens new avenues for research into interactive effects between formal and informal support systems, particularly in diverse cultural contexts.

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, given the cross-sectional nature of this study, we cannot exclude the possibility of reverse causality, whereby higher trauma symptom severity may negatively influence women’s perceptions of the adequacy of social support. In such cases, the observed association between support gaps and trauma severity could partly reflect altered perception or recall rather than a purely causal effect of insufficient support. Second, although previous studies have reported associations between mode of delivery and postpartum birth trauma, we did not include delivery mode as a primary risk factor in the final analysis. This was because the study population was recruited exclusively from high-level obstetric hospitals, where delivery practices are relatively standardized and the distribution of delivery modes was highly unbalanced, with a predominance of cesarean sections. Preliminary analyses did not reveal significant differences in trauma scores between vaginal and cesarean deliveries at 42 days postpartum after adjusting for other variables. Given this homogeneity and our study’s primary focus on social support gaps and partner involvement, delivery mode was not retained in the multivariate model. Nevertheless, in populations with more diverse delivery practices, mode of delivery may interact with psychosocial factors to influence birth trauma risk, and future multi-center studies are warranted to explore this relationship in greater depth. Third, partner involvement was assessed through self-report, which may be subject to social desirability bias, particularly in cultures where idealized parental roles are strongly endorsed. Future research should consider triangulating self-reports with partner questionnaires or observational data. Fourth, while the sample was drawn from multiple hospitals, all were located in Shanghai, potentially limiting generalizability to rural regions or other sociocultural contexts. Fifth, the measurement of emotional and informational support gaps relied on subjective ratings, which may be influenced by individual response styles or transient mood states. Finally, maternal mental health beyond childbirth-related trauma, such as postpartum depression, was not assessed in this study. Instruments such as the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) could help clarify whether depression mediates the observed association between social support and trauma symptoms [42]. Future studies should incorporate such measures to elucidate potential mediation pathways. Additionally, cultural factors may have influenced both the perception and reporting of social support and trauma symptoms. In Chinese cultural settings, societal expectations surrounding maternal resilience and family harmony may lead some women to underreport emotional distress or perceived support deficits. Rapid shifts in family structure, with declining availability of traditional “zuo yuezi” (postpartum confinement) support, may also exacerbate informational and emotional gaps in ways that differ from other cultural contexts. These dynamics, combined with varying levels of health literacy, should be considered when generalizing our findings internationally.

To strengthen the evidence base for these findings, future studies could employ mixed-methods designs, combining quantitative measures with qualitative interviews to capture the nuanced experiences of postpartum women. Experimental designs, such as randomized controlled trials (RCTs), could be used to test the efficacy of targeted partner-involvement interventions or informational support programs in reducing trauma symptoms. Additionally, ecological momentary assessment (EMA) via mobile applications could provide real-time data on support receipt and emotional states, reducing recall bias inherent in retrospective surveys. Comparative studies across cultural contexts could elucidate whether the protective role of partner involvement is universally applicable or modulated by cultural norms and healthcare system structures.

The integration of partner involvement and domain-specific social support assessment into postpartum care holds considerable promise for reducing birth trauma incidence and severity. In practical terms, this could involve partner-inclusive prenatal education sessions, structured postpartum home visits involving both mother and partner, and digital platforms delivering tailored informational content to address identified gaps. Future research should explore the longitudinal impact of such interventions on maternal mental health, parenting confidence, and infant developmental outcomes. Moreover, health policy initiatives that formally recognize and incentivize partner engagement—such as extended paternity leave and partner-inclusive hospital policies—could amplify the reach and sustainability of these benefits. Ultimately, by reframing postpartum care as a shared responsibility between healthcare systems, families, and communities, we can move toward a more holistic and equitable approach to maternal mental health.

Conclusion

This study revealed a considerable high prevalence of postpartum birth trauma symptoms, with low education, low income, formula feeding, inadequate partner support, and poor perceived social support as key risk factors. Strengthening partner involvement, enhancing social support, and promoting breastfeeding may help reduce the psychological burden of childbirth. Integrating routine psychosocial screening into perinatal care is recommended. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm these associations over time, explore causal pathways, and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions in reducing postpartum birth trauma.
