Healing after war/conflict: transformative health workforce strategies for a resilient future: a systematic scoping review
Authors: Aregawi Gebreyesus, Tesfay Brhane Gebremariam, Mengistu Hagazi Tequare, Samuel Kidane, Mache Tsadik, Measho Gebreslassie Gebregziabher, Mengistu Welday Gebremichael, Leake Gebremeskel, Kalayu Brhane Mruts, Mulu Woldegiorgis, Tilahun Haregu, Alula M Teklu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman resources for health (HRH) are critical for effective healthcare delivery, particularly in post-conflict settings where health systems face severe challenges. Post-conflict environments often exacerbate pre-existing HRH challenges, such as shortages, poor working conditions, and limited training opportunities. This scoping review aims to identify and synthesize evidence on interventions addressing HRH in post-conflict settings, highlighting strategies, outcomes, and barriers to their implementation.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across multiple databases (e.g., PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science) and grey literature sources, including WHO and ReBUILD Consortium reports. Inclusion criteria encompassed quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-method studies, reviews, and guidelines focused on HRH interventions in post-conflict settings published since 1990. Data were charted and analyzed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) framework to identify key themes, interventions, and outcomes.ResultsThe review included 14 studies, comprising primary research and systematic, scoping, and narrative reviews. Key interventions identified include recruitment and deployment strategies, capacity-building programs, incentive schemes, and retention mechanisms. Case studies from Sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East highlighted innovative policies, such as community-based recruitment and accelerated training programs. Barriers to implementation included political instability, lack of funding, and uncoordinated efforts among stakeholders. Successful initiatives involved long-term planning, local community engagement, and integration with broader health system recovery strategies.ConclusionEffective HRH interventions in post-conflict settings require comprehensive planning, coordination among stakeholders, and sustained investment. Policies should prioritize retention, capacity building, and equitable deployment to rebuild resilient health systems. These findings provide a roadmap for policymakers and practitioners to enhance HRH recovery efforts in similar contexts.Keywords: Post-war/conflict, Human workforce, Human resources for health, Post-war/conflict intervention

Introduction

The health workforce is a cornerstone of any robust health system [1]. Comprising a diverse array of skilled professionals, including doctors, nurses, and allied health personnel, this dedicated cadre plays a pivotal role in delivering essential healthcare services. The effectiveness of health interventions and the overall resilience of a healthcare system hinge upon the strength, competency, and distribution of this vital workforce [1–3]. Recognizing the significance of a well-supported and adequately trained health workforce is imperative for achieving sustainable healthcare outcomes [4].

Conflicts severely impact health systems, particularly Human Resources for Health (HRH). Challenges include inadequate education, attacks on health facilities, increased workload, and staff shortages [5]. While HRH issues in conflict-affected settings are similar to those in other low-income countries, the conflict exacerbates these problems [5]. Post-conflict periods may present opportunities for health system reform, but success depends on leadership, financing, and capacity [6]. Deployment policies often remain unchanged during conflicts, with managers adapting practices pragmatically to ensure staff safety [7]. Recovery processes are hindered by political, technical, and administrative obstacles [8]. The long-term sustainability of the health workforce is undermined by short-term, narrowly focused policymaking [5].

Healthcare workers providing services in different settings of conflict and disasters have faced different challenges, including direct attacks in the form of threats, detainment, and death [9], displacement as well as psychological issues [10], with an overall increased risk of violence [11]. Workers in helping professions experience compassion fatigue and develop a state of mental and physical exhaustion in addition to the primary trauma as a result of providing services to traumatized clients, which then impacts the quality of the services they provide [12–14]. Thus, the aim of this scoping review was to review post-war/conflict interventions on human resources for health.

Methods

Evidence synthesis using a systematic scoping review

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included all types of research (quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-method studies) and all research designs (primary, systematic review, and narrative review) that discussed interventions on human resources for health during the post-conflict period. Additionally, guidelines, frameworks, performance reports, and books related to post-conflict health system recovery, restoration, and reconstruction were included. Papers published from January 1990 to December 2022 were included to make the findings relatively recent. The review and synthesis of common interventions and their effectiveness, facilitators, and barriers across the health system functions used to draft the strategic recommendations. On the other hand, non-research articles (such as seminar and symposium reports, editorials, protocols, agreements, and simple commentaries), and papers written about conflict and humanitarian settings without any post-conflict concept, were excluded. Evidence regarding service exclusively for military personnel, the military health care system, pre-service training and education, and health research were also excluded.

Information sources and search items

Literature was systematically searched from databases (such as PubMed, Ovid Medline, Embase, CINHAL, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar), and websites like ReBuild Consortium, Relief, and the WHO. Search terms were created by combining the terms restoration, rehabilitation, recovery, and reconstruction with the human resources for health in post-war/conflict and fragile contexts.

Screening of source of evidence

The selection for eligibility applied two-step screening. First, articles addressing human resources for health issues during the post-conflict period or in conflict-affected and fragile settings were considered eligible by title and abstract. Second, a full article review focused on any substantive themes about post-conflict or post-war human resources for health.

Data charting and data items

We used the customized data extraction tool from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for a mixed-study type. Data charting focusing on the selected best interventions was also conducted using an Excel sheet containing the country name, name of intervention, area of human resources for health, year of implementation, main achievements, barriers and enablers of implementation, and reference.

Evidence synthesis

Using the charted items, the main approaches or interventions, outcomes, and challenges of post-conflict human resources for health interventions were identified and discussed. The selected evidence was then synthesized across the components of human resources for health interventions. Successful and failed post-war recovery efforts and their determinant factors were analyzed. The findings were then discussed and interpreted in accordance with preexisting national and global health system policies, strategies, and guidelines.

Findings

Characteristic of studies

Among 14 articles, 10 were qualitative, and 4 were mixed-methods studies. Eight were reviews, and 5 were primary research. Six studies used a case-study design, while others utilized systematic review (2), scoping review (2), narrative review (2), and descriptive observational (1) designs. The studies covered multiple countries with post-conflict experiences from 2005 to 2022, with 12 published in peer-reviewed journals. Ten of the fourteen studies were conducted in Sub-Saharan African countries.

Mechanisms for the fulfillment of the adequacy of human resources for health

Case studies from Uganda, Zimbabwe, Sierra Leone, and Cambodia highlighted challenges in HRH recruitment during post-conflict periods, primarily due to destroyed training institutions and significant loss of staff [15]. A qualitative review in Zimbabwe and Uganda found that healthcare worker (HCW) drainage was caused by factors such as low pay, insecurity, psychological problems, political factors, organizational culture, local amenities, and personal preferences. In response, Ministries of Health (MOH) planned HRH management at a ministerial level based on national health assessments, addressing recruitment, training, professional relationships, management, working conditions, remuneration, and policies to attract staff to rural areas [16]. A 2022 systematic review identified negative pushing factors for HCWs during post-conflict as fear, insecurity, lack of continuing education, deteriorating work environments, and health labor market surpluses [17].

Liberia’s case study emphasized that strong leadership and uniform objectives are crucial, alongside acknowledging weaknesses and seeking help [18]. In Palestine, a comprehensive computerized personnel information system supported recruitment and planning. However, NGOs and aid agencies often attract public health workers with lucrative packages, shifting labor market dynamics and hindering the rebuilding of routine public health services [19]. A review of African countries revealed that the HRH arena is influenced by various actors with corporate agendas. Key lessons included: donor agencies’ less assertive role in Human Resource Development (HRD), the impact of non-health factors on HRD, inadequate attention to civil service provisions and salaries, the long-term and costly nature of HRD, the benefit of sector-wide strategies, the need for significant investment in a crippled workforce, and the importance of early rehabilitation of human resources and training capacity [20].

A case study in Uganda, Sierra Leone, Cambodia, and Zimbabwe, based on stakeholder mapping, key informant interviews, and document reviews, indicated the complexity of health labor markets in fragile and post-conflict settings. Policies must avoid distorting these markets and draining staff from underserved areas. International support should reinforce resilience, provide decision-making flexibility, and consider long-term HRH implications. Incentive packages for staff in challenging areas should go beyond short-term financial measures to include recognition, security, decent housing, working conditions, training, pay, and re-establishment of trust and teamwork [15].

In Kosovo, after the civil war, the Kosovo Rehabilitation Centre for Torture Victims (KRCT) was established to address HRH needs and rehabilitate HCWs. This program involved new curricula, training of trainers, stress awareness and management training for PHC staff, integration of curricula into HRH policies, and support for staff and stress management within the PHC system [21]. A 2017 quantitative scoping review described case studies on HCW recruitment and production in post-war eras. In Afghanistan, Mali, Somalia, Sudan, and Yemen, community nomination was used for midwifery candidates. Afghanistan and Yemen selected candidates from communities lacking qualified midwives, expecting them to work there upon graduation. New curricula were also developed to produce midwifery professionals at various levels to reduce maternal and child mortality [22]. Cambodia, with a longer post-conflict period, initially focused on rapidly increasing staff production, but quality varied due to uncoordinated curricula and a lack of expert trainers [15].

Mechanisms for retention and reintegration

A 2016 case study detailed countries’ post-war experiences, noting that pre-existing retention challenges were exacerbated. Low and irregular payments, lack of career progression, unsuitable accommodation, and poor working conditions contributed to retention issues in Uganda’s public sector. Financial limitations and wage bill ceilings caused low and delayed payments across four settings. Working for NGOs was more attractive due to better financial incentives and training. The departure of NGOs led to an inability to sustain staff, causing many in northern Uganda to move to South Sudan, which was experiencing an aid boom. Another pattern was the movement from the private non-for-profit (PNFP) to the public sector in Uganda due to better pay and retirement incentives [15].

A case study review found that comprehensive HRH policies, including education, training, and incentive systems, effectively improved HCW retention. Active involvement, manageable workloads, work-life flexibility, and promotion opportunities reduced attrition. Common reasons to remain in post-conflict settings included a personal calling, connections, and community support. Effective working conditions, leadership, communication, regular pay and benefits, promotions, and skill development opportunities also motivated retention [17].

A 2014 qualitative scoping review described mechanisms from various countries. Beyond direct financial incentives, indirect financial (scholarships, housing, allowances) and non-financial (career development, good working conditions, involvement in decision-making) incentives were used to attract workers to rural areas and redeploy them to underserved facilities [19]. A systematic literature review on improving retention found that paid incentives and recruitment from within communities increased retention in five of six fragile and conflict-affected states [17].

In Afghanistan, the Minister of Public Health appealed to expatriate Afghan physicians to return. In South Sudan, the Sudanese Physician Reintegration Program (SPRP) facilitated voluntary return. In Lebanon, the repatriation of externally displaced HCWs with additional training abroad had an unintended impact on the local healthcare workforce, creating competition for younger HCWs [23]. A review focusing on incentive environments and deployment systems stated that conflict/post-conflict dynamics distort attraction and retention across health sub-sectors. In northern Uganda, the PNFP sector remained functional during conflict, while the public sector was boosted post-conflict with development investments and allowances. Retention in the PNFP sector relied on personal factors, while many expressed intentions to leave. Rural health workers in Zimbabwe faced similar challenges [24].

One systematic review suggested policies focusing on improving financial incentives, professional development, work flexibility, and identifying staff with strong community ties [17]. A qualitative study in Sierra Leone, Liberia, and the Democratic Republic of Congo highlighted the importance of supervision, community support, regular supplies, performance rewards, and consistent remuneration for CHW retention and performance, despite persistent challenges like inadequate supervision and supply shortages [25]. The review on incentives emphasized the need for fair and balanced HRH incentive packages across sectors to avoid distorting labor markets. Investments in the immediate post-conflict period must consider long-term implications. Balanced incentive packages should include recognition, security, decent housing, working conditions, training, pay, and re-establishment of trust and teamwork [24].

Mechanisms for capacity building

A qualitative study in three African countries described their post-conflict experiences in rebuilding HCW capacity. Policies in all three outlined CHW roles in connecting communities to the health system, providing basic care, promoting health, conducting surveillance, and mobilizing communities, with defined training requirements [25]. In Kosovo, the KRCT’s capacity-building structure, combining training of trainers and health staff with curriculum development in stress awareness and management, proved effective. Training enhanced coping mechanisms, teamwork, and client interaction. The interactive methodology was well-received, leading to accreditation of the stress management module, improved team-building by mixing doctors and nurses, positive impact from Steering Committee involvement, and the training approach serving as a mode [21].

Mechanisms for motivation and rehabilitation

A case study review in four post-conflict/crisis settings underscored the need for HCW motivation in rehabilitating health systems. Low salaries, lack of career progression, delayed salaries, and insufficient accommodation demotivated HCWs across countries. While housing received investment, career progression and difficult working conditions persisted. Low pay was not a primary challenge immediately post-conflict but gained importance as systems were re-established [15]. A 2022 qualitative systematic review revealed that inadequate resources were demotivating, leading to attrition, while adequate resources and conditions of service were critical for retaining clinical workers [17]. Limited health system reconstruction can lead to repeated brain drain due to poor financial management, lack of information access, and un-incentivizing environments. In Zimbabwe, motivating factors for HCW return included good working conditions, adequate drugs and equipment, and a well-developed human resources policy [23].

A systematic review on post-conflict management through incentives highlighted the use of direct (pay, bonuses) and indirect (scholarships, housing, schooling, allowances) financial incentives, as well as non-financial incentives (career development, good working conditions, involvement in decision-making). In Afghanistan, a 75% salary increase plus a specialization scholarship significantly increased rural job acceptance [19]. In Sierra Leone, peer supervisors motivated CHWs. A scoping review added that reinforcing supervision and improving working conditions are key, and post-crisis moments offer opportunities for learning and innovation [24]. A case-study review of Sierra Leone’s post-conflict experience (2002–2012) showed that HRH policies included drafting the National Health Policy and an incentive mechanism with increased salaries, though its effectiveness was not demonstrated [26].

Extraction of key strategies/principles/ for post-conflict HRM

In Timor-Leste, Zimbabwe, and Armenia, governments and international agencies sought to develop policies for the return and integration of conflict-displaced HCWs and to rehabilitate post-conflict health systems. Key principles included guaranteeing a secure working environment, conducting comprehensive assessments to identify gaps, and implementing a national health policy framework that explicitly addresses HCW reintegration, ideally as part of a reconciliation process [23]. A qualitative study in Liberia described rebuilding HRH policies post-conflict. After 2006, the MOH initiated three reform actions: building an experienced and visionary leadership team, strengthening partnerships for resource mobilization, and developing an evidence-based National Health Policy & Plan (NHP&P) [18].

Major challenges, limitations, and failure experience in post-conflict HRM

Reviews and case studies highlighted several challenges and limitations: Despite new policies, implementation faced obstacles. In Afghanistan, the Priority Reform and Restructuring (PRR) process was hindered by political interference [19]. Post-conflict, NGOs often draft new job descriptions without central coordination, leading to fragmentation. NGOs’ lucrative packages attract public health workers, shifting labor market dynamics and hindering the rebuilding of routine services [19]. In Sierra Leone, a lack of coordination among actors involved in health system reconstruction created challenges for the MOH in controlling the workforce [15]. A lack of national standards and quality assurance in education and training institutions, exemplified by South Sudan’s lack of structured postgraduate training and a unified accreditation system, impacted the quality of HCWs [19]. Many standard international strategies fail to account for specific local challenges [18].

Post-conflict, there is a risk of distorting health worker supply and salaries. Foreign staff can fill gaps, but complaints about their skills and resentment towards their higher salaries are common. Training can be mishandled, leading to overproduction of poorly trained staff or in-service training that doesn’t improve performance. New HRH policies can also create long-term problems. Confidence in the government and willingness to engage in public sector employment depend on the political settlement [24]. In Sierra Leone, DRC, and Liberia, there was no written guidance or indicators for managing or measuring CHW performance [25].

Discussion

This systematic scoping review synthesizes evidence on human resource (HR) interventions in post-conflict health systems, focusing on core functions identified by WHO guidelines [24]: policy and planning, retention and reintegration, recruitment and deployment, capacity building, motivation and rehabilitation, performance management, leadership, staff development, health workforce information, and task shifting. Our analysis of 14 articles highlights critical challenges and common strategies employed in post-conflict settings.

Prolonged conflict profoundly impacts the health workforce, leading to severe diminishment in numbers, mal-distribution, and a decline in capacity, as consistently reported across various conflict-affected nations [26–31]. Common challenges during and immediately post-conflict include poor working conditions, safety concerns, financial strain, limited access to training, increased workload due to shortages, and breakdown of standard HR practices like rotation and incentives for rural deployment [16, 17, 30, 32–34].

Strategic HR planning and information systems

Based on this review, a recurring theme is the critical need for comprehensive HR assessments and robust information systems in the immediate aftermath of conflict. Countries like Uganda, Kosovo, Liberia, and Sierra Leone undertook national surveys of human resources for health to establish baselines [25, 26, 29, 31]. The implementation of computerized personnel information systems, as seen in Palestine, proved vital for supporting effective recruitment and planning processes [17]. This aligns with WHO recommendations emphasizing overall health system and HR assessment as foundational for rebuilding [24]. Such assessments are crucial for identifying gaps and priorities before implementing national health policies, offering clarity and reassurance to health workers considering return [35].

Policy development for retention and reintegration

The rapid development of new HRH policies, programs, and structures is a consistent intervention in post-conflict nations. Kosovo’s Rehabilitation Center Program [21] and Liberia’s HRH reform actions [25] exemplify this, focusing on areas like structure, recruitment, retention, integration, production, and capacity building. Sierra Leone, Liberia, and the Democratic Republic of Congo also established new HRH policies, often including performance rewards and regular remuneration to incentivize community health workers [21, 25, 26]. These findings underscore that proactive policy formulation is essential to prevent chronic shortages and mal-distribution of health workforces in the aftermath of crises [33]. However, the effectiveness of these new policies is often undermined by significant implementation challenges, including political interference, nepotism, and a lack of centralized coordination among NGOs and aid agencies, leading to fragmented performance management systems [19, 28, 31]. This highlights a crucial gap in the literature regarding how to overcome these political and coordination barriers effectively.

Diverse approaches to retention and reintegration

Post-conflict retention and reintegration strategies are multifaceted. Motivation for return migration, as observed in Sudan and Afghanistan, often stems from a desire for family reunification, national contribution, and the opportunity to practice medicine [17, 34, 36]. Programs like the Sudanese Physician Reintegration Program in South Sudan demonstrate structured efforts to facilitate the return of expatriate health workers [30]. Conversely, the repatriation of externally displaced health workers, particularly those with advanced training, can inadvertently create competition and unemployment for local healthcare workers, as seen in Lebanon [35]. Factors promoting retention for those who remained or returned include a personal calling, social ties, community support, and crucially, improved working conditions. These include good leadership, effective communication, regular and adequate pay, employment benefits, formal promotions, and opportunities for continuous learning and skill development [16, 25, 33, 37].

Financial incentives, such as increased salaries and scholarships for specialization in rural areas, proved highly effective in Afghanistan [19] while hard-to-reach allowances and regularized salary payments improved retention in Zimbabwe and Uganda [34, 36]. These diverse approaches highlight the importance of context-specific and multi-pronged retention strategies that address both intrinsic and extrinsic motivators.

Challenges and opportunities in capacity building

Conflict often leaves health workers with inadequate training and a critical shortage of skilled professionals [38, 39]. Investing in training capacity is consistently prioritized, aiming to enhance the technical quality of training facilities [40]. Countries like Kosovo [29, 40], DRC [25], Sierra Leone, and Liberia [16, 25, 41] implemented various capacity-building programs, with modules ranging from short courses to year-long training. These initiatives are vital for skill development and the overall rehabilitation of both health professionals and the health system [42].

However, a significant challenge emerges from the potential for “unplanned overproduction of poorly trained professionals” through accelerated or emergency training programs. While such programs can rapidly increase the number of health workers, as seen with physicians and midwives in Afghanistan [34], they risk creating a workforce with limited capacity for career development and lower academic standards [8, 32, 43, 44]. Furthermore, a lack of national standards and quality assurance in education and training institutions, coupled with in-service training that may not translate to improved performance, presents substantial barriers to sustainable capacity building [19, 24, 25]. This suggests a need for a more strategic, quality-assured approach to capacity building, balancing immediate needs with long-term professional development.

Motivation and incentive structures

Post-conflict settings widely utilize incentive packages to motivate and retain health workers, particularly in hard-to-serve areas [45]. These incentives extend beyond direct financial remuneration to include recognition of achievements, improved security, decent housing, retirement benefits, career development opportunities, good working conditions, manageable workloads, work-life balance flexibility, and involvement in decision-making [15, 17, 19, 22, 23]. Indirect financial incentives, such as scholarships and subsidized housing, are also employed to encourage deployment to rural or understaffed facilities [19].

The literature consistently demonstrates that effective incentive programs aim to both motivate immediate performance and foster sustained commitment [45]. Acknowledgment and rewards are crucial for maintaining morale, enthusiasm, and a sense of value among health workers [46]. However, the specific design and long-term impact of these diverse incentive packages in different post-conflict contexts require further rigorous evaluation.

Engagement with existing literature and contribution of this review

This review confirms and expands upon the established understanding of health workforce challenges in fragile and conflict-affected settings, as outlined in broader WHO guidelines and general HRH [8, 24, 27]. While existing reviews often highlight the depletion of health workforces and the importance of rebuilding, this scoping review uniquely synthesizes specific interventions and their common challenges across multiple post-conflict countries. By detailing strategies for policy development, retention, reintegration, and capacity building, it offers a more granular understanding of what has been attempted on the ground. Our review particularly illuminates the gap between policy formulation and effective implementation, identifying recurring barriers such as political interference and uncoordinated aid efforts that impede sustainable HRH recovery.

Gaps in the evidence and limitations of the study

Despite identifying various interventions, significant gaps remain in the literature: Most interventions described in this review lack rigorous evaluation of their effectiveness and long-term impact. This makes it challenging to identify and recommend evidence-based best practices. The lack of critical appraisal of included articles may limit the identification of biases or weaknesses in the reported findings. In addition to that, the exclusion of gray literature, time constraints, and language restrictions (limited to English publications) likely resulted in an incomplete global coverage of relevant studies, potentially overlooking valuable lessons from non-English sources or unpublished reports.

Policy relevance and recommendations

Based on these findings, we offer the following actionable recommendations for governments and donors operating in post-conflict settings: Governments and donors should immediately support comprehensive national HRH assessments and the establishment of robust, computerized personnel information systems to inform evidence-based planning. New HRH policies must be developed proactively, explicitly addressing post-conflict realities. These policies should be centrally coordinated among all stakeholders (government, NGOs, aid agencies) to prevent fragmentation and political interference. A combination of financial incentives (e.g., competitive salaries, rural allowances, scholarships), non-financial motivators (e.g., recognition, career development, improved working conditions), and support for social ties should be integrated into retention strategies.

Investments in capacity building must prioritize quality assurance and national standards to avoid the overproduction of poorly trained staff. Training programs should balance immediate skill gaps with long-term professional development pathways. In addition to that, all post-conflict HRH policies, strategies, and interventions should include systematic and scientific monitoring and evaluation frameworks from their inception to identify effective practices and foster continuous learning and adaptation.

Conclusion

Rebuilding a resilient health workforce in post-conflict nations requires a strategic, coordinated, and adaptive approach. While common challenges and interventions across countries have been identified, critical gaps remain, particularly regarding rigorous evaluation and a comprehensive focus on all core HR functions. Moving forward, a stronger emphasis on evidence-based policy, integrated program design, and continuous learning will be vital for ensuring sustainable health workforce recovery and ultimately, improved health outcomes for vulnerable populations.
