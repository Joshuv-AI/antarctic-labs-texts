Timing of response in patients with relapsed/refractory FLT3mut+ acute myeloid leukemia treated with gilteritinib
Authors: Alexander E Perl, Mark J Levis, Andrew H Wei, Hee-Je Kim, June-Won Cheong, Jian Li, Sergey Bondarenko, Hisayuki Yokoyama, Naoko Hosono, Nahla Hasabou, Dina Elsouda, Juan Ariza, Jamie Jung-Hee An, Jianxiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Most (89%) remitting patients achieved CRc within 6 gilteritinib cycles; more later vs early responders achieved a best response of CR.•Given the potential for delayed response, at least 6 gilteritinib cycles should be administered before determining treatment failure.

Introduction

Gilteritinib is an FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) inhibitor of both FLT3 internal tandem duplication and FLT3 tyrosine kinase domain mutations1 and was shown in 2 randomized phase 3 studies (ADMIRAL and COMMODORE) to improve the survival of patients with relapsed/refractory (R/R) acute myeloid leukemia (AML) bearing these FLT3 mutations (FLT3mut+) compared with standard salvage chemotherapy.2,3 This led to its global regulatory approval in this patient population.4, 5, 6 According to the European LeukemiaNet 2022 AML guidelines, if a response is not seen by the second cycle of salvage chemotherapy, patients are considered refractory and unlikely to benefit from further treatment.7 However, this definition was developed in the context of intensive cytotoxic agents with significant toxicities that are expected to rapidly lead to temporary marrow aplasia and regeneration with normal hematopoiesis.7,8 For molecularly targeted agents, such as second-generation FLT3 inhibitors that are given daily until response is achieved,9 blast clearance can occur rapidly in the blood but may take longer in the marrow, and full hematopoietic reconstitution may be required for several months because FLT3 inhibition suppresses both leukemic and normal myeloid cell production.10

In the ADMIRAL and COMMODORE studies, although composite complete remission (CRc) rates were higher in the gilteritinib arm (50.0%-54.3%) compared with the salvage chemotherapy arm (20.3%-21.8%), the median time to CRc with gilteritinib was longer (2.2-2.3 months) compared with salvage chemotherapy (1.2-1.3 months).2,3 Despite the gilteritinib label recommending treatment for a minimum of 6 months,4,5 patients with delayed responses may be more likely to discontinue treatment prematurely and, consequently, may not receive sufficient clinical benefit. Additional evidence is needed to understand the cumulative response to each treatment cycle to better determine the optimal duration of gilteritinib therapy. This post hoc pooled analysis of the ADMIRAL and COMMODORE trials aimed to investigate the timing of response, as measured by CRc, for patients with R/R FLT3mut+ AML treated with gilteritinib monotherapy.

Methods

Patient population and study design

This was a post hoc pooled analysis of the gilteritinib arm of the phase 3 ADMIRAL (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02421939) and COMMODORE (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03182244) trials. The study designs of both trials have been previously published.2,3 Patients received daily 120 mg gilteritinib dosing in continuous 28-day cycles. Gilteritinib doses may have been interrupted, reduced to 80 mg/d, with a further reduction to 40 mg/d because of a dose interruption or reduction adverse event or if the patient already experienced clinical benefit. Patients who did not achieve CRc after cycle 1 may have had their doses escalated to 200 mg/d. All randomized patients who received at least 1 dose of gilteritinib and achieved CRc before on-study hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) from both trials were included. CRc was defined as the achievement of CR, CR with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi), or CR with incomplete platelet recovery (CRp) using a modified International Working Group11 definition of CRi that allowed both neutrophils of less than 1 × 109/L and platelets of <100 × 109/L, with or without red blood cell or platelet transfusion independence, as was typical in clinical trials of single-agent FLT3 inhibitors at the time of enrollment.

In this post hoc analysis, we defined a patient’s CRc response as early or later based on the best response achieved during their respective treatment period before on-study HSCT. This CRc definition was consistent to that used in the primary analysis of the ADMIRAL and COMMODORE trials. Patients were defined as early responders (ERs) if they achieved CRc by the end of their second gilteritinib cycle or as later responders (LRs) if they achieved CRc after the initiation of their third gilteritinib cycle. The definition of ERs was based on the practice of defining chemotherapy responders within 2 cycles of intensive chemotherapy.7 Patients who achieved a best response of partial remission were classified as non-CRc responders. Patients who only achieved CRc after HSCT were not included as ERs or LRs. Definitions of remission responses are provided in supplemental Table 1.

In both trials, patients could have resumed gilteritinib treatment after HSCT if they were alive between 30 and 90 days after HSCT; had successful engraftment, as demonstrated by an absolute neutrophil count ≥0.5 × 109/L and platelet count ≥20 × 109/L; did not have grade ≥2 acute graft-versus-host disease; and were in CRc.

The ADMIRAL and COMMODORE trial protocols were approved by institutional review boards or independent ethics committees at participating sites and conducted in accordance with the applicable regulations/guidelines governing clinical study conduct and ethical principles. All patients provided written informed consent at the time of enrollment.

Study outcomes

Variables were assessed by responder status (ER or LR) and included baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, type of remission (CRi, CRp, or CR), overall survival (OS), transplantation rate, gilteritinib exposure, transfusion rates, and exposure-adjusted treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs). Patients were considered transfusion independent at baseline if they did not receive red blood cells or platelet transfusions from 28 days before the date of their first gilteritinib dose and the earlier of either 28 days after the first gilteritinib dose or their end-of-treatment date. After baseline, patients were considered transfusion independent if they did not receive red blood cell or platelet transfusions for 56 consecutive days from 29 days after first dose until the last dose date.

Statistical analyses

Clinical outcome and safety data from the gilteritinib arm of both studies were pooled together. Continuous variables were reported descriptively. OS analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method indexed at the time of CRc, to avoid potential survivor bias, until the date of death from any cause or indexed at the time of CRc and censored at the date of HSCT. A multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted with responder status (ER vs LR) as the dependent variable and age, race, sex, cytogenetic risk status per European LeukemiaNet 2010 risk stratification,12 preselected chemotherapy, FLT3mut+ type, prior FLT3 inhibitor use, baseline bone marrow blast percentage, and AML comutations as covariates. All statistical comparisons were made using a 2-sided 95% confidence interval (CI) test, with statistical significance assessed at an α level of 0.05. All comparisons were done for exploratory purposes, and no multiplicity adjustments were made. All data processing, summarization, and analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 or higher.

Results

Patient and treatment disposition

A total of 380 patients were treated with gilteritinib in the pooled analysis. Of those, 205 (53.9%) achieved a CRc response, of whom 166 (81.0%) achieved CRc before on-study HSCT (supplemental Figure 1). From all gilteritinib-treated patients, there were 175 non-CRc responders (46.1%), 53 patients (13.9%) achieved partial remission, 103 (27.1%) were nonresponders, and 19 (5.0%) had response data that were not available or not evaluable. The median time to initial CRc for gilteritinib-treated patients who achieved CRc before on-study HSCT was 1.8 months (range, 0.9-8.4).

Of 166 patients who achieved best overall CRc response before on-study HSCT, 60 (36.1%) were ERs and 106 (63.9%) were LRs. The median time to CRc was 1.0 month (range, 0-2) for ERs and 2.8 months (range, 3-11) for LRs. The primary end-of-treatment reason was disease relapse, lack of efficacy, or progressive disease in 43 of 60 ERs (71.7%) and 62 of 106 LRs (58.5%) and death in 7 (11.7%) and 11 (10.4%) ERs and LRs, respectively.

Patient characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were broadly similar between ERs and LRs (Table 1). Over half of ERs (n = 39 [65.0%]) and LRs (n = 54 [50.9%]) were female. The median age was 58.5 years (range, 27-75) and 58.5 years (range, 18-82) for ERs and LRs, respectively. Most patients had not previously been treated with FLT3 inhibitors (ERs, n = 53 [88.3%]; LRs, n = 90 [84.9%]). Nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1) comutations were present in 24 ERs (40.0%) and 32 LRs (30.2%).

CRc response rate and duration

Of the 166 CRc responders, 60 (36.1%) achieved CRc after cycle 2, 119 (71.7%) after cycle 4, and 148 (89.2%) after cycle 6 (Figure 2). Within the first 6 cycles of treatment, the most common response among CRc responders was CRi. In the ER and LR groups, CR was achieved in 13 of 60 patients (21.7%) and 41 of 106 patients (38.7%), respectively (Table 2).

A total of 23 of 106 LRs (21.7%) achieved CRi or CRp at an earlier treatment cycle but then subsequently achieved CR with additional treatment. In contrast, 87 of 106 LRs (82.1%) had no response or partial response at an earlier treatment cycle but then achieved CRc later with continuation of treatment. Of the 87 LRs who had no response or a partial response at an earlier treatment cycle, 54 of 87 patients (62.2%) received a continuous gilteritinib dose of 120 mg, 19 of 87 (21.8%) had a gilteritinib dose increase to 200 mg, and 13 of 87 (14.9%) had a gilteritinib dose decrease to 80 mg during the course of treatment. One patient had their dose increased from 120 to 200 mg but subsequently decreased their dose from 200 to 120 mg and then 80 mg over the course of their treatment.

The median duration of CRc was 2.7 months (range, 0-66) for ERs and 3.5 months (range, 0-61) for LRs. When censored at the last known CRc date or subsequent HSCT, the median duration of CRc was 3.6 months (range, <0.1 to 65.6+) for ERs (n = 37) and 4.6 months (range, <0.1 to 60.7+) for LRs (n = 71).

OS

Of the 166 CRc responders included in this analysis, 130 (78.3%) died by the end of the ADMIRAL and COMMODORE study periods. Overall, the median OS indexed by the date of achieving CRc was 9 months (95% CI, 8-11). The median OS indexed by the date of achieving CRc was 9 months (95% CI, 7-11) for ERs and 10 months (95% CI, 8-12) for LRs (hazard ratio, 0.935; 95% CI, 0.653-1.338; Figure 3). The OS rates at 12 and 24 months were 27.9% (95% CI, 17.1-39.8) and 20.6% (95% CI, 11.2-31.9) in ERs and 39.1% (95% CI, 29.5-48.5) and 21.5% (95% CI, 14.0-30.1) in LRs, respectively (Figure 3). The median OS censored at the date of HSCT and indexed by the date of achieving CRc was 9 months (95% CI, 7-10) for ERs and 9 months (95% CI, 8-11) for LRs (supplemental Figure 2).

OS by responder status indexed on the date of achieving CRc.

HSCT

After achieving CRc, 14 of 60 ERs (23.3%) and 20 of 106 LRs (18.9%) underwent on-study HSCT. In patients who underwent on-study HSCT, 3 of 14 ERs (21.4%) and 12 of 20 LRs (60.0%) achieved CR as their best response before on-study HSCT. After HSCT, 6 of 14 ERs (42.9%) and 12 of 20 LRs (60.0%) met the prespecified criteria and resumed gilteritinib treatment after transplant.

Transfusion status

After treatment, 17 of 43 transfusion-dependent ERs (39.5%) became transfusion independent, and 10 of 17 ERs (58.8%) remained transfusion independent. In LRs, 54 of 86 (62.8%) became transfusion independent, and 18 of 20 (90.0%) remained transfusion independent after treatment. In ERs and LRs, 24 of 43 (55.8%) and 32 of 86 (37.2%), respectively, remained transfusion dependent after treatment.

Gilteritinib duration and exposure

The median treatment duration censored at the date of subsequent HSCT was 4.7 months (95% CI, 3.6-5.1) for ERs and 8.0 months (95% CI, 7.0-9.4) for LRs. The mean daily dose was 111.4 mg/d (standard deviation, 20.6) in ERs and 123.5 mg/d (standard deviation, 31.0) in LRs. The median dose intensity was 114.1 mg/d (range, 64.6-180.5) in ERs and 120.0 mg/d (range, 46.4-191.9) in LRs (supplemental Table 2). Dose increases and decreases were reported in 6 of 60 (10.0%) and 29 of 60 (48.3%) ERs, respectively, and 36 of 106 (34.0%) and 45 of 106 (42.5%) LRs, respectively. In ERs and LRs, 42 of 60 (70.0%) and 75 of 102 (73.5%) patients had dose interruptions, respectively.

Safety

Grade ≥3 TEAEs occurred at a rates of 21.3 events per patient-year (E/PY) in ERs and 16.3 E/PY in LRs (supplemental Table 3). The rate of drug-related grade ≥3 TEAEs was 11.4 E/PY in ERs and 7.8 E/PY in LRs. Within the first 30 days of gilteritinib treatment, the rates of drug-related TEAEs were 10.6 E/PY in ERs and 6.6 E/PY in LRs. The rates of drug-related TEAEs leading to gilteritinib reductions or interruptions were 0.43 E/PY and 0.97 E/PY in ERs, respectively, and 0.22 E/PY and 0.91 E/PY in LRs, respectively.

Discussion

This post hoc analysis demonstrated that the vast majority of patients who achieved a response to gilteritinib monotherapy do so within 6 cycles of treatment. LRs were more likely to achieve a best response of CR with full count recovery compared with ERs. Approximately 80% of LRs who had no or partial response in earlier treatment cycles achieved CRc after continuation of treatment. These results highlight the importance of maintaining patients on gilteritinib treatment even if they have not achieved a response after cycle 2 of treatment.

By the end of cycle 2, the CRc rate among all 380 pooled gilteritinib-treated patients was 15.8%. This is comparable to the overall CRc rate of the salvage chemotherapy–treated patients from the ADMIRAL (21.8%) and COMMODORE (22.3%) studies.2,13 This suggests that the primary benefit of gilteritinib is driven by LRs and sustained treatment exposure. Given the higher overall CRc rate with gilteritinib in both studies (ADMIRAL, 54.3%; COMMODORE, 53.3%),2,13 it is unlikely that standard salvage chemotherapy would outperform gilteritinib among patients who discontinued study treatment; however, the rate of later responses in the salvage chemotherapy arms after subsequent or alternative therapies in the ADMIRAL and COMMODORE studies remains unknown.

Compared with traditional chemotherapy, which directly eliminates leukemic blasts, gilteritinib has 2 main mechanisms of cell death in FLT3mut+ cells: apoptosis and terminal myeloid differentiation.14,15 The disruption of downstream FLT3 signaling pathways inhibits the proliferation of leukemic blasts and induces terminal myeloid differentiation in the bone marrow.16,17 Terminal myeloid differentiation may lead to the appearance of persistent leukemia14 along with the persistence of detectable FLT3mut+, with marrow responses taking weeks to months to occur.10 In this analysis, a greater number of ERs achieved CRi compared with LRs, whereas more LRs achieved CR compared with ERs. Delayed response kinetics similar to that observed with gilteritinib has also been described with other targeted agents in AML, particularly those with differentiation-based mechanisms. Isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 inhibitors demonstrate median time to response of ∼2 months; however, responses may continue to emerge over several months.18,19 Consistent with this, the prescribing information for isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 inhibitors recommends the continuation of therapy for a minimum of 6 months in the absence of disease progression or unacceptable toxicity to allow adequate time for clinical response.18,19 Similarly, emerging data from patients treated with menin inhibitors suggest comparable response kinetics to gilteritinib, with median times to CR plus CR with partial hematologic recovery of ∼2 to 3 months, but with ongoing accrual of responses over time.20,21 Collectively, these findings highlight that delayed but clinically meaningful responses are a shared feature across multiple targeted therapies in AML and support continued treatment in the absence of progression.

In this post hoc analysis, a multivariate logistic regression revealed that female patients and patients with NPM1 comutations were more likely to be ERs vs LRs. Moreover, in the ADMIRAL trial, female patients had a greater survival benefit than the overall patient population,2 although in the COMMODORE trial, a greater survival benefit was observed in male patients compared with female patients.3 In the ADMIRAL trial, patients in the gilteritinib arm with DNMT3A/NPM1/FLT3 comutations had longer median survival than those in the gilteritinib arm of the overall patient population.2 DNMT3A mutations are more common in female patients than in male patients in clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential.22 However, genetic mutation data beyond NPM1 were not available for the COMMODORE trial population.

In addition to the observed association of female sex and NPM1 mutation with ERs, there was also a numerical trend suggesting that patients with lower baseline disease burden were more likely to achieve ER, as reflected by higher odds ratios for those with <30% and 30% to 50% bone marrow blasts compared with those with >50%. This is consistent with expectations because patients with lower disease burden may be expected to respond more rapidly to therapy. A similar trend was observed for patients without prior HSCT, who appeared more likely to achieve ER. However, neither of these associations reached statistical significance because the 95% CIs crossed unity, but they may be clinically meaningful when interpreting response dynamics. Additional studies are required to better evaluate the characteristics associated with gilteritinib responses.

In this analysis, survival outcomes were similar between ERs and LRs, irrespective of censoring at HSCT, and were similar to what was reported in the ADMIRAL2 and COMMODORE3 studies, indicating that the survival benefit observed with gilteritinib treatment was not dependent on achieving an early or later response. Similarly, in both ERs and LRs, >20% of patients underwent on-study HSCT, which suggests that gilteritinib may be a viable bridging strategy for HSCT regardless of their response timing. Of note, a greater proportion of LRs achieved CR as their best response before HSCT. A post hoc analysis of the ADMIRAL trial reported that patients who achieved CRc before HSCT and received post-HSCT maintenance gilteritinib therapy demonstrated longer median OS compared with patients who received a transplant without CRc or did not receive post-HSCT maintenance therapy.23 In combination, these results suggest that continuation of gilteritinib treatment before HSCT to achieve CRc may be beneficial to patients eligible for HSCT.

Transfusion independence has previously been described as only occurring in a subset of patients with variable timing. In line with this, in both ERs and LRs, only a subset of patients achieved transfusion independence. Furthermore, a greater number of LRs achieved transfusion independence compared with ERs, which reflects that a greater number of LRs achieved CR compared with ERs.

A greater number of LRs underwent dose increases compared with ERs, whereas dose decreases and interruptions were similar between both groups. The greater number of dose increases in the LR group may reflect the fact that these patients did not observe a response after 1 to 2 cycles. The increase of the gilteritinib dose from 120 to 200 mg was allowed during the ADMIRAL and COMMODORE studies if patients did not achieve CRc after 4 weeks (1 cycle) of treatment. Furthermore, data from a real-world study have shown that the most common dose modification within the first 4 cycles of gilteritinib treatment was a dose increase because of a lack of response.24 Although data on the reasons for dose modifications are not available, more ERs had a gilteritinib-related TEAE leading to a dose reduction, whereas both ERs and LRs had a similar rate of gilteritinib-related TEAEs leading to a dose interruption. The rates of TEAEs adjusted by patient-year were broadly similar between groups; however, ERs had a numerically higher rate of gilteritinib-related grade ≥3 TEAEs and gilteritinib-related TEAEs within the first 30 days of gilteritinib treatment, which may have contributed to the shorter treatment duration observed in ERs compared with LRs. Differences in the pharmacokinetics and exposure of gilteritinib were not measured in the ADMIRAL or COMMODORE trials and may have contributed to the timing of response and TEAE rates.

To our knowledge, this was the first comprehensive evaluation of the timing of response of gilteritinib monotherapy in a pooled analysis of 2 phase 3 randomized control trials covering different geographical regions; however, the use of this patient population may not accurately reflect the broader patient population receiving gilteritinib treatment in routine clinical practice, especially because 11.7% of pooled patients from the ADMIRAL and COMMODORE trials had received prior FLT3 inhibitor treatment, respectively. In addition, because of the post hoc nature of this analysis, there were limitations on what clinical characteristics could be analyzed. Apart from NPM1, comutation data were not collected in the COMMODORE trial, and the number of ERs and LRs from the ADMIRAL trial with non-NPM1 comutations had very small sample sizes. Furthermore, in both the ADMIRAL and COMMODORE trials, measurable residual disease data were not collected. Future studies should investigate the presence of measurable residual disease in ERs and LRs as a predictive biomarker to inform treatment decisions. Finally, formal statistical testing was not performed, and any observed differences should be considered nominal. Some subgroups, such as ERs or patients who achieved CR, had small sample sizes, and thus, these results should be interpreted with caution.

In conclusion, patients with R/R FLT3mut+ AML, especially those who tolerated the drug, should continue receiving gilteritinib monotherapy for a minimum of 6 months, given the possibility of achieving a later response. Because some patients may experience a later response and full count recovery, there is a need to adjust the assessment and treatment decision-making for gilteritinib compared with conventional chemotherapy.
