Associations between TMEM106B C‐terminal fragment aggregation, age, and TDP‐43 or tau pathology
Authors: Albert Acewicz, Sylwia Tarka, Michał Grzegorczyk, Radosław Rzepliński, Teresa Wierzba‐Bobrowicz, Tomasz Stępień
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransmembrane protein 106B (TMEM106B) is a lysosomal glycoprotein whose genetic polymorphisms contribute to the severity of neurodegenerative disorders associated with TDP‐43 pathology. Recent studies have revealed that TMEM106B can form amyloid filaments composed of C‐terminal fragments (CTFs) in the human brain. In the present study, we explored the relationships between TMEM106B, age, TDP‐43, and tau aggregates, and their roles in neurodegeneration. We used immunohistochemistry with an antibody against CTFs of TMEM106B on postmortem human brain fragments (amygdala, hippocampus, temporal cortex, frontal cortex, and basal ganglia) from patients with and without TDP‐43/tau pathology at different ages (6–94 years) and with different neurological conditions (subacute sclerosing panencephalitis, Alzheimer's disease, frontotemporal lobar degeneration, and neurologically healthy subjects). Our results revealed that TMEM106B CTF fibrillization is a common, nonspecific, diffuse, and age‐dependent phenomenon (appearing after >52 years of age) that affects neurons and neuroglia (most numerous in astrocytes and oligodendrocytes) and broad neuroanatomical regions (most severe in the temporal cortex). We did not find TMEM106B CTF aggregates in young subjects with TDP‐43/tau pathology (with subacute sclerosing panencephalitis), but we revealed differences in TMEM106B CTF fibrillization between Alzheimer's disease without TDP‐43 pathology, frontotemporal lobar degeneration with TDP‐43 pathology, and older healthy subjects without TDP‐43/tau pathology. Our results suggest that TMEM106B CTF aggregation is an age‐dependent phenomenon and may have a weak association with TDP‐43 or tau pathology, shedding new light on the complex relationships among TMEM106B, TDP‐43, and tau and the unclear role of TMEM106B fibril formation in the neurodegeneration process.

INTRODUCTION

Transmembrane protein 106B (TMEM106B) is a type II transmembrane glycoprotein localized in late endosome/lysosome compartments and is highly expressed in neurons and neuroglia [1, 2]. It can be divided into three domains: the N‐terminal cytosolic domain, transmembrane domain, and C‐terminal luminal domain, and regulates several aspects of lysosomal activity, including morphology, pH, maturation, trafficking, and exocytosis [1, 2]. Genetic studies have established TMEM106B variants as disease modifiers in several neurodegenerative disorders, particularly those characterized by TDP‐43 pathologies. TMEM106B genotypes increase the risk and cognitive impairment in frontotemporal lobar degeneration with TDP‐43 inclusions (FTLD‐TDP) [3, 4, 5, 6], TDP‐43 pathology/hippocampal sclerosis in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and limbic‐predominant age‐related TDP‐43 encephalopathy (LATE) [7, 8], and cognitive impairment and motor deterioration in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [9, 10].

Recent cryo‐electron microscopy studies have revealed that TMEM106B can form amyloid filaments composed of C‐terminal fragments (CTFs) in the brains of individuals with diverse neurodegenerative diseases, including FTLD‐TDP, AD, and ALS, and during normal aging [11, 12, 13, 14]. Compared to age‐matched controls, TMEM106B fibrillization is much greater in individuals with neurodegeneration, suggesting that TMEM106B fibrils are common pathological proteins that contribute to age‐dependent neurodegeneration [11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Further immunohistochemical studies revealed that TMEM106B CTF aggregates are present in neurons and neuroglia in a broad range of brain neuroanatomical regions and are expressed as granular or filamentous material in neuroglia and as cytoplasmic dots or aggregates in neurons [15, 16, 17]. Therefore, the interactions between TMEM106B fibrils and other pathological proteins, as well as their possible roles in neurodegeneration, remain unclear. Loss of TMEM106B increases TDP‐43 and tau aggregation in a cellular model of TDP‐43‐proteinopathy and in TMEM106B‐deficient mice [18, 19], and TDP‐43 accumulation may induce the formation of TMEM106B fibrils via disruption of lysosomal function [20, 21]. In contrast, TMEM106B fibrils do not colocalize with pathological proteins in cellular inclusions, and their distribution is independent of the pattern of the primary neurodegenerative process [16, 17, 22].

In the present study, we used an antibody against CTFs of TMEM106B and performed immunohistochemistry on postmortem human brain fragments from subjects of different ages and with various neurological conditions with TDP‐43/tau pathology to further explore the relationships between TMEM106B and TDP‐43 or tau and their roles in neurodegeneration. To distinguish the role of age in pathological protein aggregation, we included a group of patients with subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE), in whom we previously showed that young patients with SSPE (<25 years of age) could present pathological TDP‐43 and tau inclusions [23].

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study sample

The database (digitalbrain.ipin.edu.pl) of the Polish Brain Bank was queried for broad‐age patients with tau or TDP‐43 pathology, in which paraffin blocks, formalin‐fixed whole brains, or brain fragments were available for further research [23]. We enrolled five SSPE patients with TDP‐43 and tau pathology (SSPE+), five SSPE patients without TDP‐43 and tau pathology (SSPE−), ten AD patients with TDP‐43 pathology (AD+), nine AD patients without TDP‐43 pathology (AD−), seven FTLD patients with TDP‐43 pathology (FTLD‐TDP), and seven FTLD patients with tau pathology (FTLD‐tau). For comparison, we included seven neurologically normal subjects without TDP‐43 and tau pathology and less than 60 years of age (yCON−), 7 neurologically normal subjects with TDP‐43 and tau pathology and greater than 60 years of age (oCON+), and 5 neurologically normal subjects without TDP‐43/tau pathology and greater than 60 years of age (oCON−) (Table 1). Demographic, clinical, and neuropathological data were obtained and analyzed for all autopsies. Patients with SSPE were diagnosed with definite SSPE based on the diagnostic criteria proposed by Dyken [24]. All patients with AD had clinical dementia and a pathological diagnosis of at least an intermediate probability of AD according to the revised National Institute on Aging‐Alzheimer's Association criteria [25]. FTLD patients were diagnosed according to the established clinical diagnostic criteria for the behavioral variant of frontotemporal dementia and primary progressive aphasia [26, 27]. All clinical diagnoses were confirmed postmortem by experienced neuropathologists. Neurologically healthy control cases were selected based on age and the absence of cognitive impairment and neurological disorders. All the subjects died because of non‐neurological conditions. All brain autopsies were performed within 24 h after death. The data were analyzed according to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Studies of autopsy samples are considered exempt from human subject research by the Institute of Psychiatry and Neurology Review Board.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

The autopsied samples were fixed in 10% buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin. The following brain regions were selected and examined: amygdala (AM), hippocampus (subiculum, CA1, dentate gyrus) (HC), temporal cortex (superior temporal gyrus, middle temporal gyrus, inferior temporal gyrus) (TC), frontal cortex (superior frontal gyrus, middle frontal gyrus) (FC), and basal ganglia (putamen, caudate nucleus, globus pallidus) (BG). For standard neuropathological evaluation, paraffin‐embedded sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Bielschowsky's silver method. Diagnostic immunohistochemistry was performed manually or using a Leica Bond‐Max automated immunostainer with the following antibodies: phospho‐TDP‐43 (1:2000, PA5‐114661, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), phospho‐tau (1:250, MAB34941, R&D Systems, McKinley Place NE, MN), amyloid β (1:100, M0872, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA), and TMEM106B CTFs (1:100, SAB2106773, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). TMEM106B CTF immunostaining was performed with manual formic acid pretreatment (immersion in 98% FA for 6 min) and standard machine heat pretreatment (98°C for 30 min), as previously described by Perneel et al. [17]. Each case was screened for the presence of TMEM106B CTF aggregates, with raters blinded to the demographic, clinical, and pathological information. The overall TMEM106B score was graded semiquantitatively based on the number of positive cells in standard 40x microscopic fields, as described by Perneel et al. [17]: 0, none; 1, mild (small numbers of cells in some fields); 2, moderate (small numbers of cells in most fields); 3, abundant (at least moderate numbers of cells in almost every field); and 4, severe (numerous cells in every field) (Figure 1).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using the statistical package Statistica 13.3 (StatSoft, Inc., Tulsa, OK). The data distribution was tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (if non‐normally distributed) and as means ± standard deviations (if normally distributed) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test/Kruskal–Wallis H test and Student's t test/ANOVA, respectively. Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction and Newman–Keuls test were used for post hoc comparisons. Correlations between the ratings of TMEM106B CTFs in each anatomical region and group and age were analyzed using Spearman's rank‐order test. A p‐value ≤0.05 was considered significant.

Routine histological evaluation revealed the classical histopathological hallmarks of SSPE (neuronal loss and gliosis in cortical areas, widespread white matter demyelination, perivascular lymphocytic infiltration, and eosinophilic intranuclear inclusions) in the SSPE+ and SSPE− groups, AD (extracellular amyloid plaques, intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, neuronal loss, and gliosis in cortical areas) in the AD groups, and FTLD (intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, neuronal loss, and gliosis in cortical areas) in the FTLD groups (Figure 2). Immunohistochemical staining confirmed tau pathology in the SSPE+, AD+, AD−, FTLD‐tau, and oCON+ groups, TDP‐43 pathology in the SSPE+, AD+, FTLD‐TDP, and oCON+ groups, and amyloid β pathology in the AD+ and AD− groups (Figure 2). The most frequent TDP‐43 aggregates are tangles or globular neuronal cytoplasm inclusions (NCIs), followed by glial cytoplasm inclusions (GCIs) and dystrophic neurites (DNs). The dominant tau lesions were tangle‐, flame‐, globous‐, or granular‐shaped neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) and DNs, and the most common amyloid β plaques were neuritic/dense‐core and diffuse plaques (Figure 2). If present, pathological lesions were observed in all examined regions, with the most severe lesions in the TC and FC, followed by the HC and AM. Tau/TDP‐43 pathology was most severe in the AD+, AD−, FTDL‐TDP, and FTLD‐tau groups, followed by the oCON+ and SSPE+ groups, without significant differences among the groups in the semiquantitative assessment.

DISCUSSION

TDP‐43 and tau pathologies can be primary phenomena or can occur as a consequence of other distinctive pathologies [28]. SSPE is a fatal brain disorder induced by a mutated measles virus that affects children and younger adults a few years or decades after measles exposure [29]. Both subacute inflammatory processes and neurodegenerative mechanisms seem to play significant roles in SSPE pathogenesis, which may lead to TDP‐43 and tau pathologies in the later stages of the disease [23]. FTLD is characterized by a progressive decline in behavior or language, primarily affecting younger adults (<65 years of age), and is predominantly associated with TDP‐43 or tau pathology in the frontal and temporal lobes [26, 27]. AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disease and the most common cause of dementia in elderly individuals, with extracellular deposits of amyloid β (neuritic plaques) and intraneuronal deposits of tau (NFTs) as pathological hallmarks [30]. A growing body of evidence indicates that more than 75% of patients with AD have additional TDP‐43 inclusions in their brains, which contribute to their clinical presentation and disease progression [28, 31]. Furthermore, several postmortem studies have shown that TDP‐43 and tau pathologies are present in 40% and 90% of cognitively unimpaired aged subjects, respectively [32, 33].

In the present study, we used immunohistochemistry techniques on postmortem brain fragments from cases with distinct pathological conditions (SSPE, AD, FTLD, young/old neurologically healthy subjects) to further explore the relationships between TMEM106B CTFs, age, TDP‐43, and tau pathology. Our results are in agreement with previous results; TMEM106B CTF fibrils are a common, diffuse, age‐dependent phenomenon (appearing after >52 years of age), affecting neurons and neuroglia (most numerous in astrocytes and oligodendrocytes), and broad neuroanatomical regions (most severe in the temporal and frontal cortex) [11, 12, 13, 14, 16, 17]. It is tempting to hypothesize that TMEM106B CTF aggregation follows a stereotypical pattern of progression across various brain cells and structures, that is, initially occurs in astrocytes/oligodendrocytes, followed by neurons, and initially affects the brain cortex and subsequently the white matter and subcortical nuclei. However, the most striking result of our study is that we did not find TMEM106B CTF fibrils in SSPE patients with TDP‐43/tau pathology (mean age 17.6 years), whereas we did find these fibrils in AD patients with or without TDP‐43 pathology (mean age 78.8 and 74.5 years, respectively), FTLD‐tau and FTLD‐TDP patients (mean age 70.0 and 56.1 years, respectively), neurologically healthy subjects with or without TDP‐43 and tau pathology (mean age 77.6 and 71.4 years, respectively). Interestingly, we noticed significant differences in the general severity of TMEM106B CTF fibrillization in patients with AD without TDP‐43 pathology, patients with FTLD‐TDP, and cognitively unimpaired elderly subjects without TDP‐43 or tau pathology, which may suggest that tau plays an important role in the TMEM106B pathological process. However, we should bear in mind that there was a significant age difference between the AD− and FTLD‐TDP groups (74.5 vs. 56.1 years), and the healthy control group was relatively small (five cases). Thus, these findings may indicate that TMEM106B aggregation may have a weak association with TDP‐43/tau pathology and is selectively dependent on age. Our results are in partial agreement with data obtained by Marks et al. [22], Perneel et al. [17], Zhao et al. [15], and Neumann et al. [16], who revealed that TMEM106B fibrils do not colocalize with TDP‐43 and tau in cellular inclusions and that their distribution does not depend on the pattern of the primary neurodegenerative process. Interestingly, Du et al. [34] did not identify a difference in the prevalence of TMEM106B pathology between patients with C9orf72‐related ALS/FTD and healthy controls, whereas both C9orf72‐related ALS/FTD mice models and patients presented a disease‐dependent correlation between pathological TMEM106B and TDP‐43 nuclear clearance.

It is unclear whether the formation of TMEM106B amyloid fibrils represents a primary pathogenic process or a nonspecific secondary phenomenon. Under physiological conditions, the proteolytic processing of TMEM106B produces CTFs in the lysosomal lumen, which undergo lysosome‐dependent degradation [1, 35]. When lysosomal function is insufficient or when the levels of TMEM106B are increased, the CTFs of TMEM106B can initiate the process of fibrilization [1, 35]. Neuronal endolysosomes are highly sensitive systems that are vulnerable to stressors, genetic predispositions, environmental factors, aging, and pathological protein aggregation. For example, TDP‐43 aggregation can cause lysosomal damage associated with chaperone‐mediated autophagy dysregulation, and TMEM106B overexpression leads to lysosome enlargement, impaired lysosomal function, and increased cytotoxicity [36, 37, 38]. On the other hand, primary (and/or secondary) fibrillization of TMEM106B may increase the aggregation of other filamentous proteins and accelerate disease pathology via disruption of lysosomal function, directly affecting pathological protein aggregation, or indirectly affecting astroglial cells [35]. It has been suggested that increased TMEM106B fibrillization is associated with increased TDP‐43 dysfunction, whereas increased TMEM106B core deposition was observed in FTLD‐TDP type A (the most TDP‐43‐inclusion type diversive FTLD‐TDP), and deposition of the 29‐kDa sarkosyl‐insoluble TMEM106B core fragment was associated with increased urea‐soluble TDP‐43 burden [22]. Our study supports the hypothesis that TMEM106B aggregation is an age‐dependent phenomenon with a weak association with TDP‐43/tau pathology. Thus, whether the presence of TMEM106B fibrils can cause pathogenic effects and contribute to neurodegeneration remains a matter of debate. While we observed comparable amounts of TMEM106B CTFs in cognitively normal individuals and patients with AD and FTLD at similar ages, it is possible that even greater amounts of TMEM106B fibrils are necessary to initiate neurodegenerative processes and the TDP‐43/tau aggregation cascade. More studies evaluating TMEM106B CTF pathogenicity, disease contribution, and copathology are necessary to provide further insights into these issues.

Our study had some limitations. First, semiquantitative methods were used to assess the density of pathological changes; therefore, it is difficult to precisely compare the occurrence and severity of pathological proteins in brain regions. Second, one may wish to verify the colocalization of TMEM106B CTF fibrils and tau/TDP‐43 in the same inclusions or evaluate TDP‐43/tau severity and protein severity in different brain locations or other disease groups, that is, healthy subjects with selective tau pathology. Third, we did not include other factors that could interact with or aggravate TMEM106B fibrillization, such as the TMEM106B haplotype, β‐amyloid severity in AD patients, inflammation in SSPE patients, and genetic mutations in FTLD patients. The role of the TMEM106B haplotype seems to be particularly important, and it may be associated independently with both the formation of TMEM106B filaments and tau/TDP‐43 pathology without direct associations between the pathological proteins. Finally, phospho‐TDP‐43 immunoreactivity should be interpreted with caution, as TDP‐43 phosphorylation and mislocalization can coexist with residual or compensatory nuclear TDP‐43 RNA‐binding activity. These issues will be addressed in future studies.

CONCLUSIONS

The present study expands our knowledge of the complex relationships between TMEM106B, age, TDP‐43, and tau pathologies. We used immunohistochemistry on brain fragments from patients with TDP‐43 and/or tau pathology at different ages and confirmed that TMEM106B CTF fibrillization is a common, nonspecific, age‐related phenomenon. The most striking result is that TMEM106B fibril formation may have a weak association with TDP‐43/tau pathology, while we did not find TMEM106B CTF aggregates in young subjects with TDP‐43/tau pathology. Future detailed studies are necessary to better understand the precise mechanisms underlying TMEM106B fibrillization, copathologies, and disease contributions.
