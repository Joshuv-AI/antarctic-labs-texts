Symptoms of anxiety and depression in informal caregivers of persons with dementia: a latent profile analysis and computer-simulated network analysis
Authors: Xuan Liu, Yan Jia, Wenjing Kuang, Wenli Shi, Bing Cheng, Jiangyun Peng, Yiyang Wen, Chenyang Li, Chongqing Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost informal caregivers of persons with dementia suffer from symptoms of anxiety and depression, but there is still a lack of clear optimal intervention targets for this group. This study aims to explore the interrelationships between symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of persons with dementia, identifying key symptoms to provide a scientific basis for developing precise mental health prevention and intervention strategies.MethodsThis study utilized a cross-sectional design. The Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 and the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 were used to assess symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers. Latent profile analysis was used to identify subgroups of informal caregivers of persons with dementia with anxiety and depression profiles. We constructed anxiety-depression network structures and identified the core symptoms and bridging symptoms for each subgroup. Computer-simulated analysis was conducted to modulate each symptom to assess its impact on overall network connectivity.ResultsA total of 246 pairs of persons with dementia and informal caregivers were recruited from November 2024 to June 2025. Informal caregivers of persons with dementia were divided into two subgroups including the low anxiety-low depression group (66.26%) and the high anxiety-high depression group (33.74%). “Low mood” represented the core and bridging symptom of the low anxiety-low depression group and “uncontrollable worrying” of the high anxiety-high depression group. Computer simulation intervention suggested that in the low anxiety-low depression group, “easily irritable” and “feeling tense” were respectively identified as the targeted intervention symptoms that may alleviate and aggravate the symptoms of anxiety and depression. While in the high anxiety-high depression group, “uncontrollable worrying” and “loss of pleasure” were respectively identified as the targeted intervention symptoms that may alleviate and aggravate the symptoms of anxiety and depression.ConclusionOur findings highlight the crucial role of low mood and uncontrollable worrying in the network of symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of persons with dementia. Based on simulated intervention results, it has certain reference significance for future research to develop intervention strategies for easily irritable and uncontrollable worrying and prevent symptoms of feeling tense and loss of pleasure to improve informal caregivers’ mental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07404-y.Keywords: Dementia, Informal caregivers, Anxiety, Depression, Network analysis, Latent profile analysis, Computer-simulated analysis

Introduction

With changes in population structure and the increase in life expectancy, the number of persons with dementia (PwD) is growing rapidly, becoming an increasingly serious global public health issue [1]. There are currently 16.99 million dementia patients in China [2], the highest number of patients worldwide, accounting for approximately one-quarter of the global total [3]. Owing to an imbalance between the growing number of patients and the limited supply of healthcare services, over 90% of PwD in China live at home and are cared for by informal caregivers, such as spouses or other relatives [4]. Care of PwD at home has become a challenging health and social issue [5]. Caregivers are prone to high levels of symptoms of anxiety and depression in a chronic stress environment [6]. Research indicates that over half of informal caregivers of PwD exhibit symptoms of anxiety and depression [7]. Notably, symptoms of anxiety and depression frequently manifest as a comorbid state; they serve as bidirectional risk factors for one another, with co-occurrence rates ranging from 44% to 60% [8]. Several factors contribute to the development and exacerbation of these symptoms. Specifically, the long-term and continuous caregiving burden borne by informal caregivers of PwD, coupled with the need to manage the diverse behavioral and psychological symptoms of the patients, renders them particularly prone to caregiver burnout [9] and compassion fatigue [10]. These adverse psychological states, in turn, exacerbate their symptoms of anxiety and depression. The consequences of this co-occurring psychological distress are substantial. Informal caregivers who experience both symptoms of anxiety and depression tend to report heavier care responsibilities and burdens [11], which can compromise the quality of care they are able to provide [6]. Furthermore, this dual burden is associated with diminished life satisfaction [12], creating a cycle of significant psychological and physical strain for the caregivers. Current research mostly treats symptoms of anxiety and depression as a single integrated concept in clinical interventions [13], however, there are still limitations in our understanding of the comorbidity mechanism of symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of PwD. The Common Bridging Factor Theory (CB Factor Theory) proposed by Chen et al. [14] provided a new perspective for understanding the comorbidity mechanism of symptoms of anxiety and depression. This theory posits that the comorbidity of symptoms of anxiety and depression is a dynamic result of the interaction between symptoms.

Furthermore, it is emphasized that the networks of symptoms of anxiety and depression are not isolated; instead, one or more common psychopathological factors exist between them. These factors mediate signal transduction and inter-network association between the two symptom networks through specific neural functional connectivity and gene expression patterns, with the symptoms that fulfill this transduction role across networks defined as bridging symptoms. Identifying and intervening in such symptoms may more effectively prevent and treat symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of PwD.

Previous studies mainly relied on total scores or critical values to assess the symptom-level of anxiety and depression of informal caregivers of PwD, overlooking the potential qualitative differences among individuals who share similar scores. Actually, individual patterns of symptoms of anxiety and depression may vary even if the overall levels are similar [15, 16]. Most studies [17] suggested that characteristic categories of informal caregivers of PwD based on their symptoms of anxiety and depression scores were complex and highly heterogeneous, meaning these methods may fail to capture individual differences, In contrast, latent profile analysis(LPA) [18] method is an individual-centered approach that judges the latent features of individuals based on their explicit responses, classifies individuals with similar features into the same category, and calculates the proportion of each category in the overall analysis, thus LPA may provide a better understanding of the differences between different subgroups of symptoms of anxiety and depression in informal caregivers of PwD.

Increasing researches have shifted from group level exploration of symptoms of anxiety and depression intervention measures to more refined individual level analysis, aiming to better align with individual differences in symptom manifestations. However, a few studies applying the LPA method primarily focus on identifying influencing factors of external conditions or individual characteristics, and exploring controllable targets through the associations between influencing factors and distinct profiles [19, 20]. But this kind of approach neglects the investigation of interrelationships among internal symptoms and fails to fully analyze the correlations between different profiles and symptoms. More recently, network analysis(NA) has emerged as a promising method to employ graph theory to establish a relational network among observed variables, presenting the characteristics and information of a system in a network format [21]. NA allows researchers to identify core symptoms and their interactions between symptoms [22], as demonstrated in studies of diverse populations including adolescents [23] and disabled older individuals [24]. Despite these advancements in NA, it is unclear whether interventions for these symptoms are effective [25]. Computer-simulated intervention analysis based on the NodeIdentififyR algorithm (NIRA) [25] models the effects of both reducing and amplifying individual symptoms on overall network structure, systematically tests the effects of intervening on different symptoms within the network structure, identifying potential cascade effects. Theoretically, when combined with LPA, this integrated approach may identify distinct symptom profiles while simultaneously mapping the complex interconnections between symptoms within each profile. Importantly, this methodological innovation could be the most efficient symptom management and personalized intervention strategies for symptoms of anxiety and depression profiles among informal caregivers.

Accordingly, the present study aims to identify core symptom intervention targets for informal caregivers of PwD with different anxiety and depression profiles, combining LPA and NA. Then, we seek to determine which symptoms of anxiety and depression, when targeted, would have the most substantial impact on reducing overall symptom severity among informal caregivers of PwD theoretically through computer-simulated intervention analysis. The objectives of this study are (1) to explore the heterogeneity of symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of PwD through LPA, (2) to identify core symptoms and bridging symptoms of informal caregivers of PwD in different profiles through NA, and (3) to obtain intervention and prevention targets through computer-simulated intervention analysis.

Methods

Participants and sample

We used a cross-sectional design. A convenience sampling method was adopted from two tertiary-level general hospitals in Wuhan, Hubei Province, China, from November 2024 to June 2025. Inclusion criteria: Patients: (1) ≥ 60 years; (2) PwD met the diagnostic criteria for dementia as defined by the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD-10) [26]; (3) PwD lived at home with their family before admission (≥ 3 months) rather than nursing homes; Informal caregivers: (1) ≥ 18 years; (2) Length of care ≥ 3 months; ≥4 h of caregiving tasks per day over the past three months [27, 28]. Exclusion criteria: Informal caregivers: (1) Caregivers employed by the patient’s household; (2) Individuals with a mental disability like schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and those unable to communicate. This study’s symptoms of anxiety and depression scale comprises 16 symptoms, the network model requires estimating 16 threshold parameters and k (k-1)/2 = 120 pairwise association parameters (where k represents the number of nodes). Therefore, the total number of parameters is the sum of the pairwise association parameters and the threshold parameters, which equals 136 [29]. To ensure the reliability of the model, the sample size must not be less than the total number of parameters. To ensure sufficient reliability in model stability and classification accuracy for potential profile analysis, it is generally recommended that the sample size be at least 200 or more [30]. Considering a 20% dropout rate, the required sample size is at least 240 cases. Ultimately, a total of 260 questionnaires were distributed in this study, 14 samples were excluded from the final analysis of this study due to more than 20% of the items being unanswered. The final sample of 246 participants had complete data for all key variables on the GAD-7 and PHQ-9, resulting in an effective response rate of 94.62%.

Measures

Demographics

Researchers collected questionnaires through the distribution of questionnaires, including general information about PwD (gender, age, marital status, educational level, numbers of children, duration of illness, number of other chronic diseases) and general information about caregivers (gender, age, educational level, marital status, educational level, relationship with the PwD, whether to live with the PwD, average daily care duration, patient care duration).

Seven-item generalized anxiety disorder scale (GAD-7)

The Seven-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7), designed by Spitzer et al. [31], was used to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms. The GAD-7 consists of 7 items that evaluate the severity of anxiety symptoms experienced by participants in the previous month. The GAD-7 has been proven to be useful and reliable for the Chinese population [32]. Each item is rated on a four-point scale: 0 = “not at all”; 1 = “on some days”; 2 = “more than half of the days”; 3 = “almost every day”. The total score is obtained by summing the scores of the seven items, with a total score range of 0–21 points. According to the definition, a higher total score indicates more severe anxiety symptoms, with a score exceeding 5 indicating an anxious state. This scale demonstrated strong reliability in the study, with a Cronbach’s ɑ of 0.829.

Nine-item patient health questionnaire (PHQ-9)

The Nine-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [33] was used to assess the severity of depression symptoms. The Chinese version was released by Bian in 2009 [34]. The PHQ-9 consists of 9 items scored on a four-point Likert scale to evaluate the severity of depression symptoms experienced by participants in the previous month: 0=“None”, 1=“A few days”, 2=“Most of the time”, 3=“Almost every day”. The scores are summed to obtain the final score. A higher score indicates more severe depressive symptoms. A score exceeding 5 indicates a depressive state. The Cronbach’s ɑ in this study was 0.825.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was executed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan University of Science and Technology (Ethical Committee code:2024178). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before the start of the study. No personally identifiable information of the participants was disclosed in this manuscript.

Statistical analysis

First, data analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 software. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations. Then, LPA was conducted using Mplus 8.3 software, each item of scores of anxiety and depression was used in LPA. The model was fitted to establish 1 to 4 latent categories. The model fit was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), sample-size-adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC), entropy, Bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR). Among these, smaller AIC, BIC, and aBIC values indicate better model fit. Entropy values are used to evaluate the accuracy of model classification, generally requiring > 0.8, with values closer to 1 indicating greater category precision. When LMR and BLRT p-values are < 0.05, it indicates that the k-category model is superior to the k-1-category model, serving as the criterion for determining the optimal model.

Subsequently, based on the LPA scoring criteria, R 4.5.0 software was used to analyze different subgroups separately. A Gaussian graphical model (GGM) was employed to construct an anxiety-depression network model diagram, with the nine anxiety items and seven depression items serving as nodes. Although the items of GAD-7 and PHQ-9 are ordinal in nature, they were treated as quasi-continuous variables for GGM estimation in the network analysis. The connections between nodes are edges, and the thicker the edge, the stronger the correlation between the two symptoms. The network was regularized using the Gaussian least absolute sum of squares (GLASSO) algorithm and the extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) with γ = 0.5 to preserve true connections. Centrality metrics of the model were calculated [29]. In addition, strength is the most stable indicator in network analysis, referring to the sum of the absolute weights of all connections between a node and its directly connected nodes, which reflects the direct correlation between the node and other nodes in the network [35]. Nodes with higher centrality are considered central symptoms in the network structure. The accuracy and stability of the network model were assessed by calculating the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of edge weights and the correlation stability coefficient (CS-C).

Finally, we employed a computer simulation intervention method to identify the most effective intervention targets using the NIRA [25]. NIRA predicts the impact of network changes on overall symptom activity by systematically adjusting the network’s threshold parameters. Alleviating and aggravating interventions were simulated by lowering and raising symptom thresholds by two standard deviations, respectively. The effect of interventions is quantified using the predicted total score of the network’s dynamic state, with higher predicted total scores indicating more severe symptoms. Symptoms with the largest difference in predicted total scores before and after intervention are considered the most effective intervention targets.

Computer-simulated intervention

The effects of computer simulation interventions are shown in Fig. 4.

Computer-simulation intervention diagram: targeted symptom for the low anxiety-low depression (A, B); targeted symptom for the high anxiety-high depression (C, D)

Analysis indicates that the impact of symptoms on network behavior varies across different categories of network models in both alleviating and aggravating interventions. In the low anxiety-low depression group, as shown in Fig. (4A, B), alleviating A6 (Easily irritable) could significantly reduce the predicted total score from 4.16 to 4.00, which was the max decreases of overall symptom levels of the network. In contrast, interventions that aggravate A1 (Feeling tense) may at most increase overall symptom levels, increasing the predicted total score from 4.16 to 5.14. This indicates that these two symptoms may serve as potential targets for treatment and prevention, respectively. In the high anxiety-high depression group, as shown in (Fig. 4C, D), alleviating A2 (Uncontrollable worrying) could reduce the predicted total score from 12.95 to 12.51, which was the max decrease of overall symptom levels of the network, while D1 (Loss of pleasure) may at most increase overall symptom levels, increasing the predicted total score from 12.93 to 13.34. These nodes thus represent potential targets for symptom treatment and alleviation interventions.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is arguably the first study to explore the heterogeneous patterns of symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of PwD, identify core symptom intervention targets for informal caregivers through an innovative combination of LPA and NA, along with computer-simulated interventions. Our findings revealed two heterogeneous patterns labeled as low anxiety-low depression (66.26%) and high anxiety-high depression (33.74%). Additionally, the study found that intervention targets may prioritize “easily irritable” and “uncontrollable worrying” and prevention strategies may prioritize “feeling tense” and “loss of pleasure”, which may be particularly effective for informal caregivers of PwD. These findings provide valuable insights for developing personalized symptom management strategies and alleviating symptoms of anxiety and depression among caregivers.

Currently, there are no other similar LPA studies investigating the categories of symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of PwD. Our LPA identified distinct informal caregiver profiles in China, the majority (66.26%) presented with relatively low levels of symptoms of anxiety and depression, while a smaller proportion (33.74%) experienced significantly higher levels of symptoms. These findings highlight the heterogeneity in symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of PwD, anxiety and depression are bidirectional risk factors for each other and their interaction will strengthen the levels of various symptoms [8]. The symptom of anxiety of informal caregivers of PwD caused by the inability to solve caregiving problems may trigger and exacerbate self-denial depression; The low value perception and psychological resource depletion caused by depression would amplify risk concerns and exacerbate anxiety in reverse, ultimately forming a bidirectional reinforcing cycle of symptoms [6]. Notably, in this study, the proportion of people with low anxiety-low depression was relatively large, which is consistent with Garrido. et al [36], this may be attributed to the fact that most of our sample had a shorter duration of caregiving [37]. Studies have confirmed that a shorter duration of caregiving has been consistently associated with a lower caregiving burden [38].

NA revealed that “low mood” and “uncontrollable worrying” symptoms were as influential nodes in the overall symptom network. These findings suggest that these symptoms may play a central role in the maintenance and transmission of broader symptoms of anxiety and depression. Importantly, they also provide a core entry point for understanding the connections between symptoms. “Low mood” simultaneously serves as both a core and a bridging symptom, acting as a pivotal node that maintains the network's structural stability and the correlation between symptoms in the low anxiety-low depression group. Informal caregivers need to face the irreversible loss of cognitive function in patients which is difficult to change the progression of the disease through intervention. This feeling of powerlessness will gradually accumulate into psychological pressure, leading to low mood [39] or even more severe symptoms of anxiety and depression [40]. From the perspective of the intervention of comorbidity in psychopathology, interventions for “low mood” may alleviate its negative effects and disrupt the stability of the low anxiety-low depression network on caregivers. This holds positive implications for alleviating the psychological distress caused by symptoms of anxiety and depression in this group. On the contrary, from the perspective of the development of comorbidity in psychopathology, “low mood” is identified as the node with the strongest bridging connection. This result further suggests that this node would increase the risk of comorbidity transmission of symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of PwD in the low anxiety-low depression group. Thus, “low mood”, as an important preventive target for preventing their symptoms of anxiety and depression, deserves special attention.

Conversely, the central hub for symptom activation and interconnections in the high anxiety-high depression group is “uncontrollable worrying”. Unlike previous studies on medical students [41] and breast cancer patients [42], where “difficulty relaxing” was the most central symptom in anxiety and depression networks, the differing result highlights the relative specificity of caregivers for PwD. According to the contrast avoidance model of worry [43], worry is an active emotional regulation strategy that keeps individuals in a low-level negative emotional state to avoid the psychological impact caused by a sudden shift in emotions from positive or neutral to negative. During care of PwD, informal caregivers not only have to address uncontrollable issues such as sudden wandering caused by the progression of the patient's unstable condition [44], undertake long-term and continuous care responsibilities and face the risk of exhausting their own energy and resources [45], but also experience persistent worries due to the uncertainty about the patients' future quality of life. These concerns are difficult to control or stop due to the lack of clear solutions. This intractability in turn sustains and exacerbates the core status of such worries, thereby potentially influencing the development of other symptoms of anxiety and depression [46]. “Uncontrollable worry”, as a core position in the high anxiety-high depression population of informal caregivers of PwD, suggests that maintaining the symptoms of anxiety and depression network is indispensable. In addition, its bridging role underscores the interdependence among symptoms of anxiety and depression, emphasizing the importance of addressing the interactions among different symptoms.

Notably, our computer-simulated analysis results revealed differential intervention targets between low anxiety-low depression and high anxiety-high depression groups, and provided compelling evidence for potential symptom-specific intervention targets in informal caregivers of PwD. “Easily irritable” was identified as the target symptom for treatment in the low anxiety-low depression group. Although it is not a core symptom or a bridging symptom, research [47] has shown that the irritability of informal caregivers of PwD may exacerbate their symptoms of anxiety and depression. For caregivers, the “easily irritable” mood leads to a notable decline in emotional control. Even they are more likely to exhibit irritability when facing minor daily stimuli. This feeling of being unable to manage their emotions then reversely intensifies their symptom of anxiety. Meanwhile, emotional instability caused by irritability may lead to caregiver self-doubt and guilt, which in turn reinforces depressive tendencies [48]. In addition, “feeling tense” was identified as the target symptom for prevention, as exacerbating it may maximize the overall severity of the network. The result aligns with the goal of prevention which is usually aimed at situations that have not yet occurred but could lead to serious consequences. Early and effective intervention for informal caregivers of PwD to prevent “easily irritable” and “feeling tense” may be an important means of preventing the potential worsening of their symptoms of anxiety and depression.

In the high anxiety-high depression group, “uncontrollable worrying” was identified as the target symptom for treatment through computer-simulation intervention, as alleviating it would decrease the overall strength of the network the most. It is also a core symptom and a bridging symptom in this group. This further highlights the importance of addressing “uncontrollable worrying” in the high anxiety-depression group of informal caregivers of PwD. When exploring prevention goals and using computational simulations to enhance interventions, “loss of pleasure” was identified as a target symptom for prevention, as exacerbating it maximizes the overall severity of the network, which indicated that the intensification of loss of pleasure may exacerbate the interactions with other symptoms in the network, thereby potentially further negatively affecting the development of symptoms of anxiety and depression, suggesting the need to pay attention to this symptom to prevent its potential progression. Previous research has noted that approaches such as professional care training [49], respite services [50], and peer support [51], caregivers are provided with basic emergency response skills, reduce concerns about sudden risks from a practical perspective, increase happiness, and may help alleviate symptoms of anxiety and depression.

Taken together, this study conducted a deep analysis of symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of PwD. Prioritizing interventions targeting the core symptoms of “low mood” and “uncontrollable worrying” may help improve the treatment effectiveness of caregivers for co-occurring symptoms of anxiety and depression [52]. According to the results of the computer simulation intervention, “feeling tense” and “loss of pleasure” are the two key nodes with potentially significant impacts, indicating that preventing the deterioration of these symptoms could be associated with a significant reduction in overall symptom levels under simulated conditions. The simulated alleviation intervention also identified “easily irritable” and “uncontrollable worrying” as the target symptoms for alleviating overall symptom levels under simulated conditions. By studying and defining specific intervention goals, personalized diagnostic and treatment strategies that better accommodate individual differences are adopted to fully account for the complex relationship between symptom presentation and individual variation.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, this study constructed the network of symptoms of anxiety and depression in informal caregivers of PwD through a cross-sectional study design, which cannot dynamically display the trajectory of symptom evolution and its potential causal relationships. Future studies may implement a longitudinal tracking strategy to deepen understanding. Second, the participants in our study were recruited in China, the caregiving norms and symptom expression in China may limit the generalizability and applicability to other settings in China and in different cultural contexts of other countries. Future research could further explore diverse geographical regions to validate and expand these findings. Third, the convenience sampling recruitment method may introduce selection bias, leading to an underestimation of the true prevalence and severity of symptoms, making the identified high-risk group potentially conservative. Future studies could improve the validity of research findings by incorporating objective measurements. Fourth, this study did not stratify the specific impacts of various influencing factors on symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of PwD. Future research could include related factors for further investigation. Fifth, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce potential biases or variations in reporting. Future studies could enhance objectivity by incorporating additional assessment methods like observations or data obtained from qualitative interviews. Finally, the computational nature of our intervention simulations is mainly based on empirical data. In the future, validation through actual clinical intervention studies targeting the identified core symptoms should be verified.

Conclusion

This study adopted an individualized perspective to reveal two patterns of symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers. Through network analysis, we identified the central symptoms “uncontrollable worrying” and “low mood” as priority targets for intervention. NIRA simulation highlights the different symptomatic targets for preventive and therapeutic interventions. By prioritizing the alleviation of symptoms such as “feeling tense” and “loss of pleasure” and intervening in symptoms like “easily irritable” and “uncontrollable worrying”, it may be possible to potentially disrupt the bidirectional reinforcing cycle between symptoms of anxiety and depression. Our findings provide a theoretical basis and potential targets for precise intervention in symptoms of anxiety and depression among informal caregivers of PwD. Healthcare professionals could develop more effective disease management strategies based on the symptom characteristics of different subgroups to reduce the risk of worsening symptoms of anxiety and depression in informal caregivers of PwD in China.
