Cutoff Values of Pulse Wave Velocity Score to Estimate 10‐Year Cardiovascular Disease Risk by Framingham and SCORE2 Systems
Authors: Yusuf Bilal Çelenk, Burkay Yakar, Mehmet Onur Kaya, Erhan Önalan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTArterial stiffness measurement can predict risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) independently of traditional risk factors and can be used to improve cardiovascular risk assessments in various clinical scenarios. The current study aimed to determine PWV cutoff values for CVD risk classification of individuals based on different risk scoring systems. The participants' CVD risk was calculated using the Framingham and SCORE2 systems. Arterial stiffness was estimated by measuring PWV values. According to Framingham score system, 53.1% (n = 111) of the participants had low risk, 28.2% (n = 59) had intermediate risk, and 18.7% (n = 39) had high risk. According to SCORE2 system, 41.1% (n = 86) were low risk, 36.4% (n = 76) were intermediate risk, and 22.5% (n = 47) were high risk. The mean values of PWV in Framingham and SCORE2 risk groups were 7.09 ± 1.03 and 6.95 ± 0.89 m/s in the low‐risk group, 8.14 ± 1.08 and 7.85 ± 1.09 m/s in the intermediate‐risk group, and 9.24 ± 0.99 and 9.13 ± 1.33 m/s in the high‐risk group, respectively. In the determination of CVD risk groups, PWV cutoff values were 7.6 and 7.1 m/s for the low‐moderate risk group and 8.1 and 8.5 m/s for the intermediate and high‐risk groups according to Framingham and SCORE2 systems, respectively. There were significant differences in risk categorization between Framingham and SCORE2 systems. PWV value showed high positive correlation with both Framingham and SCORE2 system risk scores. PWV values may be a practical, rapid and useful method for cardiovascular risk categorization independent of external factors.

Introduction

Cardiovascular Diseases (CVD) are disorders of the heart and circulatory system. The disease is caused by a series of pathologies, most commonly atherosclerosis. CVD is a chronic condition that is often asymptomatic for a long time, and the underlying pathology develops over a lifetime. It is currently the leading cause of early death worldwide [1]. According to World Health Organization data, 17.9 million people die each year worldwide because of CVD, and it is estimated that this number will reach 23.6 million people each year by 2030 [2].

Several risk factors are responsible for the development of CVD. Some of these risk factors are non‐modifiable risk factors (age, gender, genetics), while others are modifiable risk factors (smoking, sedentary life, poor diet, high blood pressure, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and obesity). Reducing CVD mortality and morbidity depends on reducing modifiable risk factors [3]. Risk assessment is recommended to personalize CVD preventive treatment, to identify intervention targets for high‐risk individuals and preventive approaches for low‐risk individuals. Therefore, the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) recommends cardiovascular risk assessment for all individuals over 40 years of age [4].

Currently, CVD risk calculators are used for individuals without known CVD. The European Society of Cardiology's Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation (SCORE) tool and the Framingham overall cardiovascular risk profile are popular risk calculation tools that have been widely adopted in clinical practice. All risk calculators calculate the total risk of an individual, considering the effects of classical risk factors. It cannot be said that these systems provide perfect results for every individual and every population. For example, the Framingham risk calculator was developed for the American population, and when applied to the European population, a higher risk score is obtained [1]. In order to obtain the best risk assessment results, risk assessment tools should be reorganized according to societies and risk factors. Today, the snowballing increase in obesity is the most important factor in the increase of CVD. Visceral obesity is closely associated with insulin resistance, increased risk of diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and atherosclerosis [5]. The ATTICA study showed that visceral adiposity index (VAI) was independently associated with an increased 10‐year CVD risk. Therefore, VAI is another risk calculation method that can be used in cardiovascular risk assessment [6].

Arterial stiffness measurement is another risk assessment method. Aortic stiffness independently predicts cardiovascular risk and determines the therapeutic target to reduce the global burden of CVD. Arterial stiffness measurement can predict cardiovascular risk independently of traditional risk factors and can be used to improve cardiovascular risk assessments in various clinical scenarios. Therefore, it can be used as a cardiovascular risk calculator that can independently predict the risk of cardiovascular events in both clinical and community‐based cohorts [7]. Previous studies have associated increased arterial stiffness with increased risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD), CVD (coronary heart disease, heart failure, stroke) and mortality [8, 9, 10]. Lee et al. reported that the use of information on arterial stiffness with conventional risk calculators may improve predictive power. In addition, they reported that PWV values, a simple and non‐invasive method and the most widely used measure of arterial stiffness, may be useful in predicting cardiovascular risk [11]. Previous studies on arterial stiffness have focused on high‐risk individuals and disease outcome. Studies examining the importance of arterial stiffness as a risk assessment tool in long‐term follow‐up in individuals without CVD are limited [10].

We hypothesized that higher arterial stiffness is associated with an increased risk of morbidity and mortality in the community. We hypothesized that individuals' arterial stiffness may be more consistent in predicting risk when community, age and gender factors are taken into account. In this context, we investigated risk prediction based on arterial stiffness measurement using both the Framingham risk score system and the SCORE2 risk system. We considered that cutoff values for PWV values associated with CVD risk were insufficient in the literature. We aimed to determine the PWV cutoff values predictive of CVD risk. In this way, we aimed to determine the CVD risk prediction of individuals with PWV values, which is a non‐invasive method.

Methods

This cross‐sectional study was conducted at the department of family medicine clinic from December 2021 to January 2023. The study population included individuals aged 40–69 who did not have CVD. The sample size calculation was performed using the open access statistical program available at http://biostatapps.inonu.edu.tr/WSSPAS/ip. Sample size was calculated by comparing the means and std deviations of the groups with the Independent Samples One‐Way ANOVA test for three independent groups. When the Type I error (alpha) is set to 0.05, the test power (1‐beta) is 0.8, the effect size is 0.227, and the alternative hypothesis (H1) is two‐sided, a minimum sample size of 156 people is required to detect a significant difference using this correlation analysis. Post‐hoc power analysis was performed with the data obtained from the study. In the post‐hoc power analysis performed according to the means and std deviations of the PWV values (7.09 ± 1.03; 8.14 ± 1.08; 9.24 ± 0.99) of three independent groups; Power (1‐beta): The calculated power (1‐beta) based on this test is 1, considering type I error (alfa) of 0.05, sample size of 50, and effect size of 0.85 was determined.

Patients with known coronary artery disease, known malignant disease, vasculitis or rheumatological disease, endocrine diseases (thyroid, parathyroid, adrenal gland, pituitary), history of previous bariatric surgery, steroid use, organ failure, and pregnancy were excluded from the study. Considering the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study population, 209 people were randomly included in the study. Participants included in the study were divided into three groups according to their CVD risk scores as; Low Risk, Moderate Risk, High Risk. The groups were matched for age and gender, so the groups were comparable in terms of age and gender.

The study was conducted following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the Fırat University Ethics Committee (date: 18.11.2021, approval number: 2021/12‐ 07).

Data Collection

Sociodemographic characteristics and clinical history of the participants were obtained through a questionnaire. Modifiable and non‐modifiable cardiovascular risk factors were questioned and recorded. Measurements were performed after calibrating the devices used for height, weight and waist circumference measurements. Height was measured with a wall‐mounted stadiometer accurate to 0.1 cm, weight was measured with a digital scale accurate to 0.01 kg, and waist circumference (WC) was measured with a tape measure accurate to 0.1 cm from the midpoint of the lowest rib with the lateral iliac processes. Body mass index (BMI = kg/m2) was calculated with the formula. Blood pressures were measured in both arms in a sitting position in a quiet room after at least 5 min of rest. The mean of both measurements was recorded as systolic and diastolic blood pressure. Total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and triglyceride values of all participants were recorded from the hospital database.

Cardiovascular Risk Assessment

Framingham CVD risk model and Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation 2 (SCORE2) tools were used to assess participants cardiovascular risk. The Framingham risk calculation employed the ‘Online Calculator: Cardiovascular risk assessment (10‐year, general CVD, Framingham, 2008 paper)’ calculator [12]. Framingham Risk Scores (FRS) below 10% were considered as low risk, FRS ≥ 20% as high risk and other scores as intermediate risk [12].

Patients' ESC SCORE2 risk scores were calculated with the ESC CVD Risk Calculation mobile application, which was arranged according to the 2021 guideline. In the SCORE2 evaluation, ≥7.5% was defined as high risk and <2.5 was defined as low risk for those under the age of 50, and ≥10% was defined as high risk and <5 was defined as low risk for those between the ages of 50–69. All other risk values were considered moderate CVD risk [13].

VAI was calculated for women and men according to the formulas: VAI = [WC/39.68 + (1.88 × BMI)] × (TG/1.03) × (1.31/HDL) for men; and VAI = [WC/36.58 + (1.89 × BMI)] × (TG/0.81) × (1.52 / HDL) for women [14].

Evaluation of Arterial Stiffness

Arterial stiffness was measured non‐invasively in the upper arm using the Tel‐O‐Graph BT system (Aachen, Germany) by a single researcher. The measurement was conducted in a quiet and temperature‐controlled room (22–24°C). To determine the correct cuff size, the participants arm circumferences were measured, and then arterial stiffness was measured in the sitting position with the appropriate cuff (two sizes available: 24–34 cm and 32–42 cm). Tel‐O‐Graph BT is a validated device that can monitor 24‐h blood pressure and measure pulse wave velocity (PWV), central blood pressure (cBP), augmentation index (Aix), and central pulse pressure (cPP) to assess arterial stiffness. The PWV obtained is an indirect estimate of large artery stiffness based on mathematical models that include several parameters derived from pulse wave analysis and wave separation analysis, as previously published [15].

Statistical Analysis

IBM SPSS (Statistical package for Social Sciences) 22 statistical package program was used for statistical analysis. Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine whether continuous data were normally distributed. Descriptive statistics of the data were presented as mean ± standard deviation for variables showing normal distribution in continuous data, as [median (min–max)] for variables not showing normal distribution and as frequency, percentage [n (%)] for categorical variables. Pearson Chi‐Square and Fisher Exact tests were used to analyze the relationship between categorical data. McNemar–Bowker test was used to compare the risk groups of Framingham and score systems. Considering the distribution of continuous data, independent samples t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare two independent groups. Considering the distribution of continuous data, Independent Samples One‐Way ANOVA or Kruskal Wallis test was used to compare three independent groups. Bonferronni test was used for post‐hoc analysis. For the correlation analysis between continuous variables, Pearson or Spearman analyses were used considering the distribution of the data. ROC analysis was used to determine the exact value of the predictor variable according to the gold standard. Area under the curve, sensitivity, specificity and their confidence intervals were calculated. The cutoff value was found with Youden index. Significance level was determined as p < 0.05.

Discussion

Although many risk calculation tools for cardiovascular risk of individuals have been developed and are in use, they have limitations and unmet needs for improved risk stratification. Arterial stiffness is associated with a number of cardiovascular risk factors and is an independent predictor of cardiovascular mortality. PWV is the most widely used method for measuring arterial stiffness because of its non‐invasive and easy measurement. It has been firmly established that information on arterial stiffness is associated with the development of future cardiovascular events independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors in the general population as well as in various groups of patients with specific diseases. Moreover, this information on arterial stiffness, when associated with other risk stratification tools, may enable easier prediction of individuals' cardiovascular risk [11, 16]. The current study aimed to determine PWV cutoff values for cardiovascular risk stratification of individuals quickly and practically, especially by family physicians working in primary care.

In the current study, total cholesterol, LDL, triglyceride, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, waist circumference and BMI were found to be higher in individuals with high cardiovascular risk according to both the Framingham and SCORE2 systems. HDL level was lower in the risk group according to both risk calculators. Previous literature data have proven that dyslipidemia, high blood pressure and obesity are risk factors for CVDs [17]. Current CVD risk calculators calculate an individual's risk of developing CVD using the parameters mentioned above. The current study data are compatible with the literature, as expected.

According to both Framingham and SCORE2 risk calculators, VAI scores in the medium and high‐risk groups were statistically higher than those in the low‐risk group. However, VAI values were not significant in distinguishing between medium and high‐risk groups. Previous studies have shown a positive relationship between VAI and CVD risk [18, 19]. Another study reported that although a relationship was found between VAI and coronary heart disease, this relationship was not linear [20]. Therefore, it was reported that VAI scores could not be helpful in predicting 10‐year CVD risk [21]. Although the current study found a relationship between VAI and CVD risk, no difference was found in distinguishing between medium‐ and high‐risk individuals. According to previous literature data and current findings, we believe that there is a relationship between VAI and CVD risk. We believe that more comprehensive studies are needed for VAI to be used in CVD risk prediction.

In the current study, the SCORE2 system detected 86 low‐risk, 76 moderate‐risk, and 47 high‐risk individuals, while the Framingham system detected 111 low‐risk, 59 moderate‐risk, and 39 high‐risk individuals, respectively. The difference was statistically significant. Since scoring systems are developed in different populations, it is expected that they will give different results. One of the most significant issues today is that cardiovascular risk calculation systems may lead to inaccuracies when classifying patients' risk in different societies and due to individual characteristics [22]. Motamed et al. reported that in their study comparing CVD risk assessment tools, the mean SCORE2 score was higher than the mean Framingham risk score in both men and women [23]. In another study conducted in the Iranian population, the Framigham scoring system identified more risky individuals than the SCORE system in both men and women [24]. In the current study, the SCORE2 system identified more individuals at high risk. Turkey is the country where diabetes is most common among European societies, and two‐thirds of adults are either obese or overweight. This may be the reason why the SCORE2 classification system detects more risky individuals in the study population [25]. Current findings support the hypothesis that new predictive tools are needed for individual CVD risk prediction.

The current study found a positive and significant correlation between PWV value and both Framingham and SCORE2 systems. Previous studies have reported that PWV value, a measure of arterial stiffness, is an independent risk factor for CVD [26]. A study conducted in the Chinese population reported a positive association between PWV value and 10‐year ASCVD risk [27]. Another study showed that PWV value had a moderate positive correlation with four different risk scores [28]. In a multivariate analysis of 2232 participants followed for 7.8 years in the Framingham Heart Study cohort, it was found that high aortic PWV value was associated with a 48% increased risk of CVD [10]. A 9.4‐year follow‐up study conducted in Europe found that there was a 16%–20% increase in the development of coronary artery disease (CAD) for every 1 SD increase (3.4 m/s) in cfPWV [29]. Current findings have once again demonstrated the association between increased PWV values and increased risk of CVD.

Although PWV is considered the gold standard for diagnosing arterial stiffness, there is currently no consensus on the ideal cutoff point for CVD risk and early diagnosis. Guidelines recommend a PWV cutoff of 10 m/s as an indication of arterial stiffness [30]. The Chinese guide recommends a higher cutoff value (12 m/s) [31]. Different ideas have been put forward to reduce these values. A recent meta‐analysis study suggested a PWV threshold value of 9 m/s for arterial stiffness [32]. Santos‐Veloso et al. suggested a cutoff point of 8.2 m/s for the arterial stiffness indicator [33].

There are two problems with the use of PWV in CVD risk assessment. The first is that there is no consensus on the cutoff point, as mentioned above, and the second is that existing cutoff points indicate vascular stiffness. Current recommended cutoff points are associated with high‐risk patients and are inadequate for early diagnosis. In the current study, the PWV cutoff point for distinguishing low‐risk and moderate‐risk individuals was 7.6 m/s for the Framingham classification and 7.1 m/s for the SCORE2 classification. The PWV cutoff point for distinguishing moderate‐risk and high‐risk individuals was 8.1 and 8.5 m/s for the Framingham and SCORE2 classifications, respectively. The current study found a value lower than the 10 m/s cutoff point previously reported in the European guideline [30]. A mean PWV values of 8.14 m/s has previously been reported in hemodialysis patients at high risk for CVD [34]. Santos‐Veloso et al. reported the PWV cutoff point as 8.2 m/s for the estimation of arterial stiffness and recommended lowering the PWV cutoff points [33]. The study conducted in an atherosclerosis cohort in China reported that the mean PWV values of patients was 8.55 m/s [35]. Current information supports the PWV values of 8.1 and 8.5 m/s, which are the cutoff points for moderate and high‐risk individuals obtained in our study. The result of the meta‐analysis by De Wei An et al. similarly associated the PWV 9 m/s cutoff point with high risk [32]. In the light of the findings and literature data, we recommend that new studies be conducted to reduce PWV cutoff values.

Strengths and Limitations

Arterial stiffness was assessed using PWV values, which is considered the gold standard according to consensus guidelines. The risk of CVD was assessed using the most widely used and generally accepted Framigham and SCORE2 systems. This study has some limitations. Firstly, this was a cross‐sectional study and may be insufficient to reflect different populations (general population and racial/ethnic backgrounds). Due to the cross‐sectional design of the study, causal relationships cannot be established, and the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than predictive relationships. Another limitation is the lack of long‐term clinical follow‐up of the population. This may limit our ability to conclusively point out the relationship between PWV values and CVD risk scores. Another limitation is that the effects of participants' chronic diseases (HT, T2DM) and current treatments on our results are ignored. New studies are needed to explain the effects of individuals' chronic disease status and current treatments on PWV scores. The effect of physical activity, nutritional characteristics and medical treatments (medications used) on PWV values were ignored. Additionally, the lack of data on medication use, lifestyle factors, and disease control status may have introduced residual confounding. “Furthermore, the proposed PWV cutoff values are exploratory and require validation in independent and prospective cohorts before clinical application.”

Conclusion

Approximately 20% of the current study population was at high risk of CVD. There were significant differences in risk categorization between Framingham and SCORE2 systems. PWV value showed high positive correlation with both Framingham and SCORE2 system risk scores. PWV value may be a practical, rapid and useful method for cardiovascular risk categorization independent of external factors. Nevertheless, our findings need to be supported by future studies. We recommend investigating PWV cutoff values for cardiovascular risk stratification in large and diverse populations.
