Advancing large-molecule discovery with a unified digital platform for data analysis and workflow management
Authors: Eriberto Natali, Jana Hersch, Christoph Freiberg, Stephan Steigele
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe repertoire of large-molecule treatments continues to expand, resulting in diverse discovery and development workflows. This diversity yields a proliferation of software solutions and procedures for molecule registration, material tracking, experiment planning, data analytics, quality control, data sharing, and decision-making. Contrasting with this manual, labor intensive, and error-prone approach, we introduce the concept of a transformative solution: an integrated platform that translates this complexity into a harmonized, open architecture encompassing all workflows and hardware systems, covering the discovery process up to developability assessment. The benefits and complexities of such a platform are evident in examples spanning different use cases and maturity levels, such as developing multi-specific antibodies and antibody-drug conjugates using shared workflows or incorporating artificial intelligence for predictive and generative tasks. This review outlines state-of-the-art concepts behind a digital platform for automating and streamlining the discovery of new large-molecule treatments.

Introduction

The processes by which new therapeutic antibodies are discovered have dramatically changed over the past five decades. The first antibody discovery workflows were described in the 1970s and consisted of simple procedures in which animals were immunized, and the serum was purified to isolate the antibody fraction.1 A breakthrough occurred with the development of hybridoma technology in 1975,2 permitting the production of a virtually unlimited number of antibodies in vitro. This advancement enabled the production of proteins for multiple assays to characterize antibody drug candidates and enabled multistep discovery workflows. In the 1980s, new molecular biology techniques and display technologies heavily upscaled the experimental throughput, introducing the possibility to screen many antibodies in a high throughput fashion.3 In recent years, antibody discovery workflows have become increasingly complex, both in experimental procedures and data analysis. This complexity supported the development of diverse antibody formats and emphasized automation through the implementation of robotic liquid handling systems. Advanced multistep discovery workflows are in place across the biopharma industry, including at Sanofi,4–6 AstraZeneca,7 Novartis,8 Genmab,9 GSK,10 and AbbVie.11,12

While complex workflows enable more comprehensive and advanced hit characterization, they result in a vast amount of heterogeneous data that need to be processed, analyzed, and managed. Each experimental step of the discovery workflow generates files in various formats, depending on the instruments used. Adding an extra layer of complexity is the need for data integration and advanced computation, which cannot be fully accomplished in instrument software and often ends up being performed manually, e.g., in Excel sheets. Some experimental approaches, such as the calculation of IgG monomer percentage in size-exclusion chromatography when testing aggregation propensity of hits in developability assessment, require both instrument-software and manual calculations.13

The unruly mix of diverse software and manual approaches to data makes the discovery process more time-consuming, costly, and difficult to scale. It also triggers a host of problems, such as: (1) Tracing back different steps is difficult, especially when a certain point of the cascade needs to be reconstructed; (2) Data is not consolidated, harmonized, and structured, so implementing data science and artificial intelligence (AI) is challenging; (3) Automation is challenging due to fragmented digitalization of data analysis, and (4) metadata is often lost while moving through the workflows.

Numerous digital solutions to enhance efficiency and success in large-molecule research have been created. Laboratory information management systems (LIMS) evolved in the 1970s,14 primarily to support laboratory automation in development and manufacturing. These regulated environments demanded stringent data management and traceability, and LIMS provided the structured framework to manage and organize laboratory results. Traditional LIMS enforce a stringent data model and unifying vocabulary, but they typically focus on sample management,15 supporting only a limited number of specific workflows, and are not broadly applicable across an enterprise.

The first electronic laboratory notebooks (ELNs) were developed in the 1980s16 as rudimentary systems for storing and commenting on experimental data, replacing paper notebooks. There are more than 170 ELNs available on the market today, including open-source options.17 Key identifying features of ELNs are the ability to capture unstructured data such as free text and the absence of a unifying data model, allowing each user to organize their data and apply a unique vocabulary. This enables great flexibility but also results in a key downside – limited data searchability across groups. In addition, quality data models fitting modern science are time-consuming to build from scratch, and even when successful, they are not easily searchable unless a common vocabulary is enforced across all users.

In recent years, the industry’s focus on data science, AI, and machine learning (ML) has highlighted the growing need for structured data and robust data models. The once distinct lines between ELNs and LIMS have been blurring. The latest versions of ELNs now incorporate some LIMS components, to simplify the creation of the most common data schemas.18 These changes have eased the challenge of building partial data models from scratch and introduced some data structuring, but they do not address the main challenge of providing a single unified data model across the enterprise. New terminology has been introduced for such blended systems, including “laboratory execution,” “analytical ELN,” or “modern LIMS.”

Current data management solutions integrate some of the required data analysis and quality control (QC) tools within the digital platform itself.19–23 In addition, dedicated analysis systems have been developed for specific discovery technologies. Despite these advancements, no single solution addresses all data capture and analysis needs during large-molecule discovery. However, successful implementations of individual steps within the discovery pipeline have been demonstrated, including cell-line development,24 in-silico biologics design,25 clone redundancy analysis,26,27 glycopeptide characterization for vaccines,27 and peptide mapping.28,29 Consequently, many biopharmaceutical companies use a combination of multiple solutions, often overlapping within their digital ecosystem, and supplement them with in-house tools or general-purpose software like Excel, PowerPoint, and even e-mail. Here, we explore concepts for a unified biopharma digital platform designed to automate and streamline the discovery of new large molecules.

Building blocks of antibody discovery and the unified biopharma digital platform

During hit identification, an initial pool of molecules (e.g., from immunization of animals, libraries, in-silico designs) is screened to identify antibodies that interact with the target.30 This is followed by validation and characterization to understand the binding kinetics,31 epitope specificity,32 and preliminary functional activity,33 reducing the initial number of hits by making selections along the different rounds. During lead optimization, a smaller group of leads is refined to improve therapeutic potential and developability through techniques such as affinity maturation,34 humanization,34 and protein engineering.34 The engineered candidates then undergo extensive characterization of their biological, pharmacological, safety, toxicology, and developability profiles through numerous assessments, both in the wet lab and via in-silico predictions (Figure 1). This thorough process ultimately identifies a few selected therapeutic candidates for further development. While the number of tested molecules decreases throughout these stages, usually described as the “screening funnel,”35,36 the information content per molecule accumulates in the form of data and metadata (Figure 1). During the process, the number of clones can also temporarily increase, e.g., during engineering, where variant antibody clones are iteratively generated, tested, and selected, from one starting clone with desired properties.37 Digitalizing the entire discovery process is challenging because each project requires registration and access to all molecules, including their genealogy, engineering, production, and characterization, as well as physical storage.

To effectively address the information needs of such a complex process, we propose that the unified platform should enable: (1) Combined molecule, physical material and assay data registration along the laboratory workflows and tracing at every point of the cascade; (2) Normalization of results using a consistent data schema for accurate comparison; (3) Automation of workflows for unbiased and efficient analysis and result generation, including consistent QC and result validation; and (4) Holistic processing and availability of all associated metadata for informed decision making. Data integrity ‘by design’38 is a fundamental requirement, essential for ensuring the immutability of registered molecules, the reliability of experimental and in-silico results, and the consistency of associated metadata.

The Design-Make-Test-Analyze (DMTA) cycle,39 originally developed for small-molecule discovery, requires substantial refinements to be effectively applied to the more complex process of large-molecule discovery. For example, the design of improved large-molecule variants, such as in the affinity maturation of antibodies, requires the generation of new molecule libraries through genetic engineering approaches.40,41 Such characterization of antibody libraries presents a valuable source for future ML approaches.42 In addition, the assessment of highly complex multi-specific antibodies, made of several protein domains, requires the design, production, and characterization of numerous variants.43 Contrasting to the optimizations of small-molecule candidates, the design and production of large molecules are more time-consuming, and demand parallel screening approaches, often preceded by display techniques with a higher number of laboratories and people. Given these differences, repeated large-molecule screening and optimization workflows need to be embedded in continuous lineage tracking of molecules, physical materials, and assay data to ensure data integrity. This serves as a guiding concept for large-molecule discovery, and for a unified digital platform, consisting of molecule engineering, production, characterization, and analysis steps.

Such a unified platform must support the highly parallel cloning, production, and characterization (i.e., screening) of molecule variants while connecting all project-relevant information, such as molecular constituents, sequences, genealogy, analyses, and formed products. By integrating novel in-silico design methods with the registration of molecule libraries and individually engineered molecule variants, with antibody production and characterization, the unified platform ensures that wet-lab results and decisions continuously inform and update in-silico models, guiding molecule engineering strategies. The searchability and traceability of interconnected information form the foundation for in-silico design, experimental production, and characterization of new molecule variants. Comprehensive access to structured data enables decision-making throughout the lead optimization process and requests for new design proposals, whether realized through in-house or externalized (i.e., contract research organization (CRO)) processes (Figure 2). Antibody engineering is a rapidly advancing field influenced by recent breakthroughs in AI.44 While classical binder identification approaches are still heavily used for initial design, AI is increasingly incorporated in the modeling of structural components, using molecular dynamics simulations or homology-based modeling. Examples include ABodyBuilder for complementarity-determining regions (CDRs),45 ABAngle for VH – VL orientation,46 and tools for antibody humanization.47–49 Furthermore, developability predictions form the future basis for antibody production,50,51 with additional applications like epitope prediction,52 and multi-modal models guiding the development of many features, considering sequence, target structure, and other desired properties in parallel.53

The unified platform must support diverse binder identification approaches and seamlessly interface with AI tools, embedding their functionality within the project’s main framework. This integration prevents scientists from juggling different software and performing numerous manual operations (e.g., copying and pasting sequences or names). An embedded registration engine for individual antibody molecules and their molecular components is a critical digital infrastructure element that ensures data integrity. Furthermore, the platform must have capabilities to identify groups of redundant molecules in libraries and to determine the molecules’ uniqueness. Several components are needed to define the exact “uniqueness” of biologics. The sequence of an antibody complex with properly annotated subregions (including frameworks and CDRs) is not sufficient, as it represents only one aspect of the molecule. It must be complemented by appropriate supporting data on its structure, derived from structural models, and by biophysical properties. Information on chain multiplicity and positioning is key to understanding and defining the molecule’s structure for modeling its fit to the targeted biological effect.54 In addition, post-translational modifications are considered for any format that may be altered to achieve functional activity (e.g., different payloads and linkers).55

The physical process of generating antibody molecules as pools typically involves applying display technologies for the enrichment of binders to distinct antigens/targets,56 followed by next-generation sequencing.57 To support this, a digital system must track the source of the molecule libraries (e.g., naïve library from human or animal donors, immune library from immunized animals, or recombinant library generated by molecular biology techniques), the library type (e.g., random mutagenesis libraries, positional or combinatorial mutation libraries for affinity maturation),40,41 and the genealogy of pools (e.g., exposure to different antigens/targets and off-targets).

Producing distinct antibodies involves several intricate steps, including cloning, expression, and purification, all integrated with various lab automation systems.43,58,59 The workflow encompasses plasmid batch production,60 cell transfection61 (or co-transfection),62 cell culture,63 expression,64 and purification steps,65 including fractionation, pooling, and splitting.66 To ensure efficiency and quality, it is essential to track requests for molecule production, manage their QC, handle aliquots, and meticulously register and track all produced batches end-to-end. Furthermore, the platform must capture all post-translational modifications, such as glycosylation67 and conjugation,68 ensuring that their metadata is accurately recorded.

Characterizing molecules involves assessment of their developability, physicochemical and biological properties, function, and potency by various assays. The process interfaces with screening lab automation but must also support manual and custom processes.69 Key components include the automation of data capture, result computation, and QC70 – with a need for global and consistent normalized QC metrics and result parameters across all biophysical measurement principles.22 Each assay, whether automated or custom, is planned and executed as part of the project and cascade management, with real-time monitoring in the screening lab.71 These workflows are inherently linked to sample inventory management, which ensures traceability, availability, and contextual metadata for each sample throughout the discovery pipeline.

Results are analyzed to consolidate data and facilitate decision-making. At each project step, all relevant data are available and presented using multi-parametric decision tools. Decisions result in a hit list that triggers the next cycle round or adds more assays for further characterization of current molecules. Although the screening assay cascade is usually planned with custom decision-making criteria to select clones at each step, it must remain flexible to accommodate agile rescheduling of progressing cycles and project scope. For instance, Sanofi demonstrated an automated platform that generates and characterizes thousands of multi-specific antibody variants using robotic systems,43 by connecting the planning, execution, and monitoring of each step.

New antibody formats

A plethora of novel antibody formats have been described, with over 200 formats reported for bispecifics alone.72 This number continues to grow. Engineering new antibody formats present multiple challenges for registration. One major challenge is the presence of many possible constituents requiring registration (Figure 3A). A prime example is antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs),73 which combine an antibody, a linker, and a payload to achieve a therapeutic effect. The unified platform must distinguish between variants of such complex molecules and establish criteria for their uniqueness, which requires registration and tracking of many additional data points. For ADCs, this may include the modification technology, conjugation site, or the drug–antibody ratio. It is therefore crucial to ensure that the unified data model for molecule registration remains open to accommodate new, previously unknown components without disrupting the system. During the development of novel antibody formats, individual components are first tested independently and then reevaluated in their assembled final format. This underscores the importance of registering each component separately and maintaining uniqueness to ensure data integrity throughout the development process. For example, in a bispecific antibody, the two parental IgGs are tested for antigen binding separately and then in the recombined bispecific format. Some novel formats require complex, nonstandard assays for development, such as bridging enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and surface plasmon resonance (SPR)-based dual-binding assays,74 which necessitate appropriate result computation, QC, and registration. The basic elements can be likened to LEGO® bricks, which can be assembled in various permutations. The data model must be flexible enough to reuse and track molecular components across different formats. For example, one heavy chain variable region can be linked to other components and tracked when it forms an IgG, an scFv,75 a trispecific,76 or an ADC77 (Figure 3B).

Rise of AI and machine-learning

AI is having a profound effect on the biopharma industry by enabling, optimizing, and speeding up several data analysis and generative tasks, changing the way the right molecule is identified and developed into a drug. It simulates human intelligence in machines, enabling them to perform tasks that typically require human cognition, such as understanding language, recognizing patterns, and making decisions. ML, a subset of AI, enables computers to learn from data and make predictions. Deep learning, a further subset of ML, utilizes neural networks with many (deep) layers to train on large datasets, mimicking the way the human brain process information. These technologies are transforming industries by enabling new, more efficient workflows, reducing delivery times and costs, and compensating resource shortages.

In the early 2000s, AI began to make notable inroads into the biopharmaceutical industry, enhancing various aspects of drug discovery, such as molecular modeling, predictive analytics, and target identification.78,79 These early applications laid the groundwork for more advanced technologies in biopharma today, improving efficiency and outcomes in drug development. Building on this early work, deep learning, particularly transformer architectures, has revolutionized the field of AI. Introduced in the 2017 paper “Attention is All You Need,” transformers and their core mechanism, self-attention,80 have powered significant advancements in AI applications. This has led to the development of powerful models such as BERT and GPT for natural languages, as well as derivatives using amino acid or nucleotide sequences as language, treating motifs as words.81,82

In 2024, the Nobel Prize in Chemistry83 was awarded to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper from Google DeepMind, along with David Baker from the University of Washington, for their groundbreaking work both on AlphaFold protein structure prediction84 and on long-lasting research providing modeling and prediction of binding in-silico,85 respectively. AlphaFold is currently revolutionizing protein structure prediction by accurately determining the three-dimensional (3D) shapes of proteins using sequence information and leveraging transformer-like architectures. This ability opens new avenues in biological research and is being heavily exploited. For example, Genentech recently demonstrated the prediction of immunogenicity risk of MHC class II presentations based on Alphafold2.86

AI promises to open a new avenue for antibody discovery by improving many historically time-consuming and cumbersome processes. In 2021, Akbar and colleagues showed that the complex interactions between antibody epitopes and paratopes can be simplified to a vocabulary of motifs, making them amenable for ML and prediction.87 AI is now also widely used in the field of developability. The goal is to predict the success of developing an antibody molecule through in-silico methods and introduce these predictions as early as possible to prevent failures in later stages.88

Many examples of AI implementations in monoclonal antibody (mAb) discovery have been reported. One approach involves using molecular parameters, or “descriptors,”89,90 calculated from the antibody sequence and/or structure.91 Eli Lilly used a series of descriptors for mAb solubility (based on 111 mAbs) and showed excellent performance against an in-house high-throughput solubility measurement platform.92 Similarly, Sanofi predicted the solution behavior of mAbs,90 and Novo Nordisk designed antibodies with varying solubilities.93 Merck developed an antibody design platform called BioPhi, which uses deep learning generative models to humanize murine sequences.94 Models in this platform are trained on human variable region antibody sequences from the Observed Antibody Space database.95 They recognize non-human residues using deep learning attention mechanisms, predict the corresponding human residues, and output a humanized sequence. AI can in principle aid scientists by ‘predicting before testing’ which clones are likely to be good hits. This pre-selection can reduce the number of clones required to be tested, making the process faster and less expensive.96 More detailed insights can be found in reports by Vecchietti et al.97 and Kim et al.,98 which offer comprehensive discussions on AI/ML models and techniques focusing on antibody design and optimization, and on antibody development, respectively.

AI’s biggest impact is likely in the de-novo design of antibodies, where generative foundation models play a pivotal role.99 Foundation AI systems are (pre-)trained on extensive datasets and, unlike traditional models that are designed for specific applications, are versatile, and can adapt to numerous tasks with minimal-to-no finetuning. Examples include large language models like GPT and image generation models like DALL-E. State-of-the-art foundation models even enable zero-shot learning, allowing a model to recognize and classify data from categories it has never encountered during training. These models serve as foundational building blocks for developing specialized applications. In the future, we can anticipate a further rise in foundation models, particularly multi-modal models, to achieve an AI first end-to-end discovery and developability workflow.100,101 These models are universal because they learn in parallel on ‘variables,’ such as amino acid and nucleotide sequences, gene ontology, EC numbers, taxonomy, 3D structure, functional domains, production conditions, post-translational modifications, binders, and various assay results, including biophysical data. This capability allows users to direct various questions to a single model, such as “predict the binding affinity for my sequence motif to an antigen,” or more constrained queries like “predict the amino acid sequence for binders for all enzymes listed under EC number X,” or “sub-select all binders that target the following set of species.” Such models are ideally embedded in feedback loops, utilizing active or reinforcement learning strategies,102 to enable continuous learning in the iterative engineering process (Figure 2). The proposed unified platform must enable such continuous learning by orchestrating the process and serving as a ‘gatekeeper’ to deliver FAIR data – a prerequisite for ‘AI-first’ strategies.103

Current platforms for large-molecule discovery

While various commercial software platforms advertise use cases in antibody discovery, we focus here on example solutions with the broadest available coverage of the antibody discovery process across multiple systems in a single portfolio and with reported implementations in the scientific literature: Dotmatics,104 Genedata,105 and Schrödinger.106,107 We do not discuss ELNs (e.g., Revvity, Benchling, or IDBS), as these are not platforms for large-molecule research in the strict sense. By comparing key features and challenges of our example solutions, we aim to provide an outlook on the progress toward achieving a unified platform for large-molecule discovery (Figure 2).

Dotmatics has been widely recognized for its extensive capabilities in small-molecule drug discovery, offering tools to manage experiments, samples, and assay data.108 In recent years, its portfolio has been extended to support diverse use cases across antibody discovery with systems such as Geneious and Protein Metrics.109 In 2022, it launched the Antibody Solution, which combined its systems Geneious, Graphpad Prism, and Snapgene for antibody discovery.110 This solution did not focus on the engineering stage, and its registration capabilities were limited to one molecule format – the canonical monoclonal IgG.111 However, it supported production, characterization, and analysis, though it was not integrated with Protein Metrics for large-molecule mass spectrometry data analysis. Recently, Dotmatics introduced the Luma Antibody & Protein Engineering Solution, integrating its core systems into a comprehensive platform that now supports the engineering of multiple antibody modalities (including multispecifics), as well as screening and downstream workflows.112 Dotmatics is actively evolving Luma to accommodate also additional therapeutic modalities such as RNAs, cell therapies, ADCs, and vaccines in the future.113,114

Genedata has historically focused on developing systems for integrating and automating workflows specifically in large-molecule discovery.115 Genedata Biologics emerged as an alternative to ELNs and LIMS in 2007. This system was built on the premise that the common characteristics of antibody discovery workflows made a unified data model both possible and desirable. Its data model was designed to cover all stages of antibody discovery, including protein engineering.105 Scientists could register diverse molecules, including bi- and multi-specifics, as well as novel formats. The system enables bulk reformatting of domains into different antibody formats and allows tracking molecule–domain relationships, keeping track of corresponding antibody expression constructs, following the lineage of physical materials during antibody production, and integrating with laboratory automation systems. While assay results can be associated with the materials, dedicated tools for analytical technologies in discovery are provided by other systems within the Genedata portfolio. These systems integrate with Genedata Biologics in the Genedata Biopharma Platform,43,116 including Genedata Screener for high-throughput screening technologies such as ELISA, SPR, biolayer interferometry, and fluorescence-activated cell sorting,19–23 as well as Genedata Expressionist for large-molecule mass spectrometry data analysis.117,118 The Genedata Biopharma Platform supports discovery workflows of additional therapeutic modalities, including RNA, cell and gene therapies, ADCs, and vaccines.119–121

Schrödinger is known for its comprehensive suite of software solutions in computational chemistry and structural modeling. These include products for material science, small molecules, and more recently also biologics discovery.122 Widely used systems such as Glide for ligand-docking and Maestro for molecular modeling exemplify Schrödinger’s capabilities. Additionally, Schrödinger has introduced LiveDesign, a centralized platform for large-molecule discovery that emphasizes collaboration in computer-aided design, structural modeling, and virtual screening. These systems support simulation and design of large molecules but lack built-in registration spanning the discovery process end-to-end. Given the rigorous registration requirement to track molecules throughout the iterative processes, Schrödinger systems are sometimes integrated with CDD Vault, a third-party registration system.123 We are not aware of any focus on automating the production of large molecules or the assay analysis and technologies in the wet lab.

While most AI-based computations for antibody discovery are currently performed by scientists using custom in-house pipelines and tools, some software platforms have integrated AI computations directly into their features. One example is the Genedata platform, which includes a deep learning application called Imagence for automated image analysis of phenotypic screens.19 The Schrödinger platform integrates numerous predictive features and parameters across the antibody optimization, enabling scientists to prioritize leads and guide the engineering phase. It can compute potential aggregation hotspots, predict CDR loop conformations, and assess the impact of mutations on binding affinity, selectivity, and thermostability. Examples of its use include the computation of 3D structural homology models for mAbs.107 AI-based analytical and predictive software functionality, as described above, exhibits high variability in operational cost requirements depending on usage, general throughput, and agile scaling needs. Deployment strategies may include on-premises, cloud, or SaaS offerings,124 with additional considerations for protecting intellectual property and ensuring that no data leaks from the organization.125 All the mentioned platforms support some automated data processing workflows, where raw data from instruments is computed in multiple steps to obtain results. This capability significantly reduces data analysis times in antibody discovery processes. Examples of automatic computation of results with integration with lab robotics are reported for Genedata Biologics, Genedata Expressionist, and Genedata Screener.71,116 Ideally, this would lead to a scenario where scientists only need to select the experiments, and all data is automatically generated and computed throughout the campaign.

Future challenges

Integration of digital platforms into discovery workflows began long ago and has progressively intensified over the past decades, consolidating wet lab workflows into more streamlined digital processes. These developments are driven by continuous improvements in time, cost, and efficiency, ultimately accelerating the discovery and introduction of new drugs and modalities to the market.

Several future projections and challenges are already clear. For instance, AI is just beginning to redefine working principles in the biopharmaceutical industry. While many promises of AI remain to be proven, its rapid development continues to push boundaries. Effective future use of AI must solve challenges such as democratizing its use and availability.126 Current limitations are defined by the effort and cost required to train better (multi-modal) foundation models,127,128 where new or improved algorithms and model architectures play a pivotal role, as recently demonstrated by advancements in large-language-based reasoning models.129 These developments will also impact on new biopharma-relevant models.

A more severe problem is the increasingly limiting factor of the availability of training data, as has become evident with the apparent limits of training data for generative AI models.130,131 Therefore, mitigation strategies must be implemented. First, breaking up knowledge silos to increase the sample size for AI training within companies is crucial. Meta information for experimental data will best be provided by the scientists in the laboratories performing the experiments. Thus, a strong workflow-dependent data registration process is required. Breaking up knowledge silos across companies will result in an increased use of federated learning approaches, but with a strong privacy component per company. In the MELLODY132 project, it was shown that jointly trained models have greater predictive power than models trained with data from just one company.

ML has been implemented by adopting data from discovery workflows to train algorithms. In biopharma companies where an efficient data registration system that provides ML-friendly data structuring is unavailable, ML scientists must still invest considerable effort to retrieve historical unstructured data from repositories (e.g., data lakes) and convert it into ML-compatible datasets. To overcome such bottlenecks, one possible approach is to switch to workflows generating data in an ML-friendly format. The concept of wet labs built for ML to generate FAIR data ready for AI use has only recently started to be adopted,133 and digital platforms must facilitate and mediate this concept. More laboratories will be designed to efficiently generate data to refine these models online – such as the concept of “lab in the loop,”134 where the data flow harmoniously from the experiments into ML algorithms, and refined algorithms are used to inform new experiments in return. As of today, the impact of emerging reasoning models, such as the one introduced by Google in “Towards an AI co-scientist,”135 remains unpredictable. However, such technology could indeed enhance the identification of promising drug targets and fuel the discovery pipeline of the biopharma industry.

The increasing importance of CROs, and the externalization of research, must be supported by a robust registration system that traces molecules (physical location and project state136), and ensures consistent result computation and validation. This entails the concept of a “fully digital” biopharmaceutical company137 outsourcing all wet lab activities, potentially enabling the faster foundation of new enterprises, as no lab equipment is needed, and the required workforce is already in place. The work is defined by AI decisions on the assays of the projects, where human scientists rarely make decisions beyond evaluating and reviewing results and monitoring project progress.138,139 Such laboratories with highly automated procedures for large molecules are already implemented in certain limited workflows.59 As more automated workflows emerge, it becomes increasingly critical to define the right interfacing with the unified platform to support both automated work and bespoke human bench work, and to translate and interface between both (see Figure 4). Deepening the connection between modalities remains a future challenge, particularly in achieving a consistent integration of small and large-molecule discovery workflows.140 Currently, there is no global registration system that seamlessly bridges these domains. Furthermore, advancements in screening technologies continue to influence the design and operation of future laboratories. Substantial efforts are being made to prevent failures during clinical testing due to poor developability and biophysical properties, as occurred with, for example, bococizumab, from Pfizer,141 and MEDI1912, from Medimmune.88 Consequently, the industry consensus is to integrate developability testing as early as possible into antibody screening campaigns to mitigate the risk of expensive late-stage failures.142 Importantly, certain developability assays, such as differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)143 and capillary electrophoresis-sodium dodecyl sulfate (CE-SDS)144 for thermal stability and purity analysis, respectively, can now be performed at a high-throughput level. This allows routine characterization of hundreds to thousands of clones in parallel.

Any end-to-end drug discovery system must be designed with clinical development and regulatory readiness at its core. As regulatory landscapes continue to evolve, platforms must evolve with them. Today, this means supporting scientific workflows while ensuring traceability, data integrity, and standardized documentation from the outset. Built-in compliance features – aligned with ALCOA++ principles and regulations like FDA 21 CFR Part 11 and EudraLex Annex 11 – are essential for operating in GxP environments. Platforms such as Genedata even enable capabilities like automated patent filings, streamlining the path from discovery to development. As regulatory expectations grow more complex, the systems supporting discovery must advance in parallel.

Finally, novel model systems, such as organoids, organs-on-a-chip, and other 3D cell culture models,145 are increasingly adopted for routine use due to their high translational power. With the increase in multi-parametric and multi-technological readouts (e.g., mass spectrometry, next-generation sequencing, cellular and biochemical assays)146 with these systems, the challenge of consistent data analysis and QC will necessitate a stringent platform concept. The future platform will need to extend and include both upstream (e.g., target identification, and its supporting technologies like single-cell RNA-seq, multiplexed light-sheet microscopy, or spatial transcriptomics) and downstream processes, including process development, clinical trials, and manufacturing, seamlessly translating across regulated and non-regulated environments.

Conclusion

This review highlights the ongoing and inevitable increase in digitalization across all processes underlying biopharma research and development of new drug modalities. Driven by the need for fast turnaround, high parallelization of drug programs, and the goal of failing as early as possible, there is a critical need to deeply integrate each physical wet lab activity with digital systems, as envisioned by the idealized unified platform. The structure and elements of the cycle shown in Figure 2 serve as the building blocks of the platform, with a key focus on the registration of each component, and the integration of automation in data processing, data analysis, result validation, and QC. Additionally, the ability to track the complete lineage of molecules, samples, and assay data across the cycles and even across screening-optimization campaigns and projects opens new avenues to further break down data silos and provide metadata for more thorough data analysis.

AI and ML further elevate expectations for increased efficiency and the ability to target otherwise hard-to-reach molecular targets and disease areas. This places additional emphasis on the quality of produced data and its workflows. Consequently, classical labs must transform into “AI-enabled” labs addressing gaps in the availability of training data, achieving the highest quality standards, and enhancing AI model performance.

Several key players in the market already offer products that align with the platform concept. A common theme among these software offerings is their broad scope of applications, catering to the needs of global organizations. These applications range from molecule design and registration to result analysis and insight generation, all unified by a global concept for result computation and validation to avoid silos of different understandings both within and across organizations. Additionally, new assay formats and human model systems demand proven software to meet the future needs for higher quality data at greater volumes. Ultimately, the ability to support future workflows effectively will remain the final benchmark for all available digital platforms.
