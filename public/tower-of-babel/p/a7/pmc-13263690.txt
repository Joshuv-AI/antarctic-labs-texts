The Missing Millions: Building Cancer Pathology Artificial Intelligence for Safety Net Populations
Authors: Zeynab Jouzi, Mohamed Omar
Published: 2026

Pathology foundation models trained on millions of whole-slide images now enable tumor detection, grading, outcome prediction, and biomarker inference.1 Building these models requires substantial digitization capacity: high-throughput slide scanners costing $50,000 to $300,000 per unit, petabyte-scale storage, standardized quality control pipelines, and institutional data governance frameworks for secure data use. These requirements concentrate data generation within institutions that already have advanced pathology and research infrastructure, especially academic medical centers, National Cancer Institute (NCI)–designated cancer centers, and large integrated health systems. The Cancer Genome Atlas, a widely used data source for pathology artificial intelligence (AI) models, collected specimens exclusively through academic Tissue Source Sites,2 and major pathology foundation models have been trained primarily on data from similarly well-resourced institutions. As a result, the composition of training datasets reflects the institutional capacity for digitization rather than the distribution of cancer burden across the US health care system.

To our knowledge, none of the largest published models report care-setting provenance of their datasets or preserve ordering-site metadata that allows independent assessment of care-setting representativeness.

The Federally Qualified Health Centers Data Gap

The US health care safety net encompasses providers that deliver care to patients regardless of ability to pay, including Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs), public hospital systems, community health centers without FQHC designation, free and charitable clinics, and Indian Health Service facilities. Although the representation gap described here applies across this broader ecosystem, we focus on FQHCs because they serve the largest identifiable safety-net patient population, with more than 32 million individuals annually.3 Additionally, they report standardized demographic and clinical data to Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) through the Uniform Data System, and their federal designation provides a policy mechanism through which provenance requirements could be implemented. However, it should be noted that the structural barriers described here are likely more pronounced in undesignated safety-net settings without comparable federal funding and oversight.

Federally Qualified Health Centers-served populations face elevated cancer risk due to structural and socioeconomic factors,4 with screening rates substantially below the general population for breast, cervical, and colorectal cancer5 (Figure 1A). These lower screening rates are associated with later-stage presentation5 and higher rates of emergent surgical intervention,6 altering the case mix of cancers reaching tissue diagnosis.

However, pathology specimens originating from FQHC ordering sites are rarely documented in training and validation datasets for cancer pathology AI. This absence reflects how the pathology ecosystem is structured, rather than legal or data quality barriers (Figure 1C). The FQHCs provide primary and preventive care, but their specimens are typically processed by contracted hospitals or commercial laboratories, which may not maintain digitization infrastructure. Even when slides are scanned, ordering-site identifiers are rarely retained in research datasets, making safety-net specimens indistinguishable from other cases and invisible to representativeness tests in AI development.

Why FQHC Data Is Scientifically Essential

Do FQHC specimens differ from those from academic medical centers in ways that affect model performance? Multiple lines of evidence indicate that they do, through both technical and population-level mechanisms that cannot be addressed by simply adding more data from the same academic sources.

First, whole-slide images encode institution-specific technical signatures, and differences in fixation, staining, sectioning, and scanning introduce visual variation that models can learn. For instance, Dunn et al16 documented substantial stain variability across 247 laboratories processing sections from identical tissue blocks, and Dehkharghanian et al17 showed that deep learning models can identify the acquisition site of The Cancer Genome Atlas slides, even without explicit training. Howard et al18 showed that site-specific signatures can drive biased inferences and are not eliminated by standard color normalization. Because many FQHC specimens are processed outside academic workflows, their preanalytical distributions may fall outside current training data, and while stain normalization can partially mitigate domain shift,19 it does not eliminate structural workflow differences.

Second, FQHC-served populations differ systematically in demographics, comorbidity burden, and disease presentation. Structural inequities in environmental exposure, occupational risk, and preventive care access can shape tumor biology at the population level. For example, Zhu et al20 identified differences in multicellular niche architecture between Black American and White American patients with triple-negative breast cancer, which reflect structural and environmental variation rather than innate biology. Additionally, Gichoya et al21 showed that deep learning systems can predict patient self-reported race from medical images, and Zou et al22 argued that this capacity poses risks when race-associated image features function as learned shortcuts rather than clinically meaningful signals. If FQHC-linked data are missing from development and validation, these shortcut signals and calibration failures are more likely to surface at deployment.

Toward Representative Cancer Pathology AI

Several federal initiatives address components of the cancer disparity and data representation problems, but none close the specific gap in pathology AI training data. For example, HRSA’s Accelerating Cancer Screening program invested more than $20 million in partnerships between FQHCs and NCI-designated cancer centers to expand screening uptake but did not focus on building digitization or data sharing infrastructure. The NIH All of Us Program has enrolled over 245,000 participants with whole-genome sequencing data, mainly from historically underrepresented groups23; however, pathology images are not a component of its data resource. The STANDING Together consortium recommends reporting the demographic composition and potential biases of health datasets used in AI development, but these recommendations lack enforcement mechanisms and do not address care-setting provenance.24

Similarly, the NCI Persistent Poverty Initiative supports cancer prevention and control in economically disadvantaged regions,25 and the NCI Center to Reduce Cancer Health Disparities funds partnerships between institutions serving underserved populations and NCI-designated cancer centers to expand access and collaboration but do not invest in digitization or metadata capture. The NCI–FDA–HRSA memorandum of understanding on cancer diagnostics in underserved communities established the Cancer Diagnostic Devices Task Force to advance near-patient testing in underserved settings26; however, its mandate centers on point-of-care devices rather than whole-slide imaging or AI development. Digitization Infrastructure

Although academic medical centers and large integrated health systems have increasingly incorporated digital pathology infrastructure into routine workflows,27 individual FQHCs and contracted laboratories do not have the resources to independently build full digital pathology pipelines. The NCI and HRSA should fund regional hubs for slide scanning, secure storage, and ordering-site metadata capture for safety-net specimens. This funding should require ordering-site provenance, contributor access to digitized slides, and dedicated scanning capacity for safety-net cases.

We acknowledge that establishing such infrastructure would not be instantaneous, but with coordinated federal investment and oversight, representative safety-net pathology datasets could be established within three to five years, enabling models that generalize to safety-net patients.

Data Provenance Standards

Model developers should report training and validation sources at a level permitting independent evaluation, including the number of unique patients, slides, and contributing institutions; whether ordering-site metadata were captured; the proportion of cases from safety-net versus nonsafety-net settings; and technical variables such as scanner model and staining protocol. Where specific care setting labels are unavailable, facility identifiers can be mapped to HRSA health center designations to enable representational assessment while preserving de-identification. For instance, a provenance disclosure might report that “a model was trained on 45,000 slides from 8 academic medical centers and 2 integrated health systems, with 0% from safety-net settings and no ordering-site metadata captured”; such a profile would immediately flag a representational gap for independent evaluation.

Regulatory and funding agencies should formalize these expectations. For example, FDA should require care setting disclosure in premarket submissions, analogous to subgroup reporting in clinical trials. Similarly, HRSA should incorporate provenance elements into Uniform Data System reporting for health centers participating in federally supported digitization.

Multientity Governance

Technical approaches such as federated learning28 can reduce centralized data transfer by exchanging model weights instead of images. However, this alone does not resolve the underlying governance challenges of data stewardship, consent, and benefit sharing. Existing distributed research networks such as the Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics initiative and the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Network show that multisite research can proceed under standardized models whereas institutions retain local data control. Applying these models to pathology AI requires structured metadata standards for care-setting provenance, cross-entity agreements that recognize the role of ordering sites, and enforceable benefit-sharing mechanisms for safety net partners.

Community Oversight

Historically, research involving underserved populations has at times proceeded without sustained community engagement or reciprocal benefit, contributing to mistrust and uneven distribution of gains. Therefore, efforts to incorporate safety-net pathology data into AI development must be accompanied by accountable governance structures. Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute (PCORI) engagement awards could fund community advisory structures in FQHC networks to govern data use, transparency, and dissemination, and can also facilitate development of accessible consent frameworks for digital pathology. In parallel, PCORI research awards or similar mechanisms could evaluate whether safety-net inclusion improves calibration, performance, and clinical outcomes in participating communities.

Conclusion

The absence of FQHC pathology data from cancer AI is a structural gap with scientific and clinical consequences. Current models are trained and validated in well-resourced settings and are not optimized for safety net populations. Addressing this imbalance requires dedicated investment in digitization, enforceable care-setting provenance standards, and governance that gives FQHC-served communities meaningful authority over how their data are used.

Because pathology AI is consolidating around large common datasets, representational gaps established now will persist through subsequent model generations. Therefore, proactive inclusion of safety-net-linked specimens at this stage is far more feasible than retrospective correction after deployment at scale. Although we do not call for more bureaucracy that would impede innovation, we believe that embedding provenance standards within regulatory frameworks would strengthen validity, improve generalizability, and reinforce public trust in AI-assisted cancer diagnosis.
