A multicentre, prospective, observational study on detecting medication discrepancies using medication reconciliation as a tool at the emergency department among geriatric patients in northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Tilaye Arega Moges, Woretaw Sisay Zewdu, Taklo Simeneh Yazie, Faisel Dula Sema, Fisseha Nigussie Dagnew, Getachew Yitayew Tarekegn, Samuel Agegnew Wondm, Mekdes Kiflu, Tirsit Ketsela Zeleke, Fasil Bayafers Tamene, Yared Andargie Ferede, Samuel Berihun Dagnew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGeriatric patients often face issues related to polypharmacy and adverse drug events. Re-evaluating prescribed medications and considering deprescribing is critical. Medication discrepancies (MDs) during care transitions can compromise patient safety, as over 60% of medication errors occur at these points. This study aimed to assess the magnitude of MDs and their contributing factors through the medication reconciliation (MedRec) process among geriatric patients in emergency departments of comprehensive specialised hospitals in northwest Ethiopia, as well as to determine the acceptance of pharmaceutical interventions.DesignIn this multicentre prospective observational study, the best possible medication history (BPMH) was obtained within 24 hours of emergency department admission from at least two sources. A comparison of the BPMH list with medication orders from treating physicians revealed discrepancies. Data were analysed using STATA V.17.0, using multivariable logistic regression to assess variable associations.SettingAdult emergency departments of comprehensive specialised hospitals in northwest Ethiopia.ParticipantsOverall, 384 geriatric patients with chronic conditions and current medication use who visited the adult emergency department of the hospital from 10 January 2025 to 30 March 2025 were involved in this study.Outcome measuresMagnitude and types of MDs, acceptance of pharmacist interventions, and factors associated with MDs.ResultsIn total, 384 patients with chronic diseases visiting the hospital emergency department were recruited in the present study. Out of 384 patients involved in the study, 218 (56.77%) had encountered at least one MD. Omission error 190 (45.24%) was the most common type of MD, followed by wrong dose 82 (19.50%). Among 420 interventions, 80.48% of the total cases were accepted. Number of previous/home medications (≥5 medicines; adjusted OR (AOR)=3.12; 95% CI 1.190 to 8.151), older age (≥75 years; AOR=1.62; 95% CI 1.054 to 2.495), and number of comorbidities (≥3; AOR=1.65; 95% CI 1.066 to 2.546) were associated factors with MDs.ConclusionThis study revealed a high prevalence of MDs in the emergency department. Polypharmacy, comorbidities and older age were factors associated with MDs. The study findings show the need for a clinical pharmacist-led MedRec implementation to enhance patient safety.

Introduction

Medication errors (MEs) are the major cause of morbidity, with negative clinical and economic consequences for patients and the general public.1 More than 60% of MEs arise during healthcare transitions of patients (admission, transfer or discharge).2 Geriatric patients are frequently exposed to polypharmacy, multiple comorbidities and adverse drug events.3 It is imperative to re-evaluate medications prescribed to older adults, and deprescribing, which can be defined as the process of identifying and discontinuing drugs that can potentially harm rather than benefit a patient, should be considered in all older adults.4 5

Medication reconciliation (MedRec) is a widely accepted patient safety strategy. MedRec is a process that compares the actual medication regimen of a patient, which is referred to as the best possible medication history (BPMH), with the orders of the treating physician at care transitions.68 BPMH is a medication history list often obtained by clinical pharmacists that includes a history of all medications (prescribed and non-prescribed), using at least two different sources of medication information. Sources of information include, but are not limited to, patient and family or caregiver interviews, medication vials and bottles, patient medication records and previous medical records.9

Studies conducted so far have revealed the acceptance level of pharmaceutical interventions by the treating physicians; for instance, a retrospective cohort study of a Belgian University Hospital showed that among 386 interventions performed by the clinical pharmacists, 279 interventions (72.3%) were accepted.10 In the study conducted at Cooper University Hospital in Camden, about 22% of pharmacist recommendations were accepted.11 According to another study in Lebanon, 64.6% of interventions were accepted,12 and a study in Brazil revealed that a total of 138 (60%) pharmaceutical interventions were not accepted.13

Understanding the magnitude and predictors of medication discrepancies (MDs) in the emergency department in Ethiopia is crucial for developing targeted interventions to improve medication safety and patient care. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the magnitude of MDs and its contributing factors using the MedRec process among geriatric patients at the emergency department of the comprehensive specialised hospitals in northwest Ethiopia. It was also aimed at determining the pharmaceutical intervention acceptance level and categorising MDs according to the National Coordinating Council for Medication Error Reporting and Prevention (NCC MERP) index.14

Materials and methods

Study design

A multicentre prospective observational study was conducted among 384 geriatric patients with chronic disease at the adult emergency department of the comprehensive specialised hospitals.

Study period

Data were collected from 10 January 2025 to 30 March 2025.

Study settings

In northwest Ethiopia, there are five public comprehensive specialised hospitals, which include Debre Tabor, Debre Markos, Tibebe Ghion, Felege Hiwot and the University of Gondar comprehensive specialised hospital. Among these, the three comprehensive specialised hospitals, Debre Tabor, Debre Markos and Felege Hiwot, were selected using a lottery method to ensure random and unbiased selection for the study.

Felege Hiwot comprehensive specialised hospital is located in Bahir Dar City, the capital of the region, which is 565 km from Addis Ababa, and the hospital serves about 5 million people. The Debre Markos comprehensive specialised hospital is located 300 km from Addis Ababa and provides services for an estimated population of 5 million. The Debre Tabor comprehensive specialised hospital is located in Debre Tabor metropolitan city, which is located 667 km from Addis Ababa and 103 km from the Bahir Dar city administration. The hospital serves around 2.3 million people and provides clinical services for all age groups. It has seven internal medicine wards, a paediatric ward, a surgical ward, an orthopaedics ward, an obstetrics and gynaecology ward, a psychiatric ward, an intensive care unit, an adult emergency department and a paediatric emergency department, the emergency triage assessment and treatment.

Eligibility criteria

Geriatric patients (≥65 years) with one or more medications for chronic medical conditions visiting the emergency department were included in the present study. Patients were excluded if they were not willing to participate, did not give informed consent, were unable to communicate or had no caregivers.

Variables of the study

Dependent variable

MD (yes/no) which identifies discrepancies during MedRec at the emergency department of hospitals.

Independent variables

Encompass sociodemographic (age, sex, residence, educational status and living arrangement), clinical and health-related factors (comorbidities, number of pre-admission medications, recent hospitalisation, cognitive impairment and vital signs), medication-related variables (sources for the BPMH, high alert medications and therapeutic classes) and healthcare-related variables (hospital department, caregiver availability and time of admission). Potential confounders include age, polypharmacy, comorbidity burden and cognitive impairment. Pharmaceutical intervention variables: clarification, correction, stopping unnecessary medications, communication with prescribers, patient counselling, number of interventions, acceptance of interventions, time of intervention and rationale for interventions.

Data collection tool and data management

To collect the data from the study participants, a structured questionnaire following a review of the previous studies was used.121523 The questionnaire was prepared in English and then reviewed by experts for validation. An expert was also appointed for translation into Amharic, and it was translated back into English to check its consistency. Then, a pre-test was conducted on 5% of the patients (20 patients) in Tibebe Ghion Specialized Hospital. Cronbach’s alpha reliability test was computed, which yielded 0.72, a value between 0.70 and 0.95 is deemed acceptable,24 25 and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test was also performed (χ2=2.564, Sig.=0.959).26

MDs can lead to negative patient outcomes such as adverse drug events, increased healthcare costs, treatment delay, readmissions and medication-related complications.30 31 In the emergency department, the risk of MDs to patients may be even higher due to the complexity and time-sensitive nature of care.23 27 32 Patients in the emergency department may be unable to provide accurate information about their current medications due to the nature of their illness or injury. Additionally, the fast-paced environment of the emergency department can increase the risk of miscommunication or overlooked medication details between healthcare practitioners.6 18 21

Best possible medication history and detection of discrepancies

Two trained clinical pharmacists collected the data within 24 hours of arrival of the patient at the emergency department. Pre-admission or home medication history included medication names, dose, frequency of administration, route of medication administration and duration of therapy.

Clinical pharmacists followed the study participants prospectively from admission to the emergency department until either transferred to other transitions of care or discharged from the hospital. Related to this, they provided the pharmaceutical recommendations to the treating physicians and followed the status of the interventions and evaluated the treatment outcome of patients. A prospective observational study design was used to follow and observe the patients from admission to discharge or transfer to detect and rectify the types and patterns of MDs through MedRec during their stay at the emergency department. Most importantly, we conducted pharmaceutical interventions and followed the status of the acceptance rate of interventions by the treating physicians and ultimately made modifications to the therapeutic plan of patients based on the outcome of interventions.

The clinical pharmacists established BPMH. For completing the BPMH, at least two sources of medication information were consulted for all respondents. In this study, the BPMH involves: (1) patient interview (when needed); (2) verification of medication information from family or caregiver; (3) inspection of medication on hand like bottles, tablets and vials; and (4) previous medical records. Data related to MDs were detected through a comparison between the medication lists of physicians (each patient’s medication history list in the profile) and the BPMH collected by the clinical pharmacists. After identification through MedRec, MDs were categorised as follows: (1) commission of error (incorrect addition of a medication); (2) omission (a medication was missed); (3) wrong dose; (4) wrong frequency; and (5) duplication of order.9 19 31

Finally, two clinical pharmacists with an internist and senior nurse (a total of 4 experts) measured the detected MDs and categorised them using the NCC MERP index which shows the occurrence and the level of harm MEs caused14: A (no error, circumstances that can cause ME); B (error happened, but did not reach the patient); C (error happened and reached the patient, but with no harm); D (error happened and reached the patient and there is a need for monitoring, but no harm); E (error happened and reached the patient, need treatment and intervention, with temporary harm); F (error happened and reached the patient and initial or prolonged hospitalisation, causing temporary patient harm); G (error happened, with permanent harm); H (error happened, with near patient death); and I (error happened, resulted in patient death). Medications involved in MDs were categorised by WHO Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification codes.33 Interventions and their acceptance rate by treating physicians were classified according to the Pharmaceutical Care Network Europe 2019.34

Statistical analysis

The collected data was entered into STATA V.17.0 and was analysed after it was checked for its completeness and consistency. Quantile–Quantile (QQ) plot and histogram tests were used to test the normality of continuous variables. Descriptive statistics were used, and data were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, or the median for continuous variables. Multicollinearity was checked by using the variance inflation factor. Associations between variables were assessed by the χ2 test. Associated factors with the number of MDs were determined by using binary logistic regression analysis, and all independent variables with a p value <0.25 at bivariate analysis were chosen to be part of the final multivariable logistic regression analysis model. Missing data were evaluated for all variables, using complete-case analysis for those with less than 5% missing values. For variables with 5% or more missing data, multiple imputation using chained equations was used to minimise bias and enhance statistical efficiency. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the consistency of results between imputed and complete-case datasets. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the study design of our research.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study participants

In the present study, 384 patients with chronic diseases visiting each comprehensive specialised hospital emergency department from 10 January 2025 to 30 March 2025 were recruited. The mean age (±SD) of the patients was 74.24 (±9.34), and 195 (50.78%) were males. More than half of the patients were from urban areas (217, 56.51%), and the majority of patients (267, 69.53%) were married. Concerning the educational level of patients, more than one-third, 163 (42.45%), completed primary education, followed by tertiary education, 103 (26.82%). A total of 853 medications (both prescribed and non-prescribed) were identified for the completion of the BPMH as being used before admission to the emergency department. The average number of previous/home medications collected through the BPMH was 2.22 (±1.260), which plays a crucial role in the pharmacotherapy management of each patient. On hospital admission to the emergency department, the average number of medications increased to 3.74 (±1.172), with a total of 1435 medications prescribed or ordered by physicians in the emergency department. The average number of comorbidities in the study was 1.80 (±1.25), and about 148 (38.50%) patients had more than three comorbidities (table 1).

Out of the 384 patients, 218 (56.77%) had one or more MDs. Relative to the number of patients, 31.25% had two discrepancies, followed by 15.89% with one discrepancy, 7.8% with three discrepancies and 1.80% with more than four discrepancies. Association between medication discrepancies and explanatory variables

In bivariate logistic regression analysis, explanatory variables such as older age, residence, marital status, job status, number of medicines at admission to the emergency department, the number of previous/home medications and the number of comorbidities were assessed to determine their correlation with the occurrence of MDs.

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, variables with a p value <0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included. Patients using five or more home medications were found to be 3.12 times more likely to experience MDs compared with those using fewer medications (adjusted OR(AOR)=3.12; 95% CI 1.190 to 8.151; p=0.020). Similarly, patients older than 75 years were 1.62 times more prone to MDs than those aged between 65 and 74 years (AOR=1.62; 95% CI 1.054 to 2.495; p=0.028). Additionally, patients with three or more comorbidities were 1.65 times more likely to experience MDs compared with those with fewer comorbidities (AOR=1.65; 95% CI: 1.066 to 2.546; p=0.024). In summary, an increased number of home medications, older age (≥75 years) and a higher number of comorbidities were significantly associated with the occurrence of MDs (table 5).

Discussion

This multicentre prospective observational study aimed to assess the magnitude and types of MDs among older adults with chronic conditions in the emergency department of comprehensive specialised hospitals in northwest Ethiopia. The study highlights the heterogeneity of clinical features in older adults, noting that multiple comorbidities and polypharmacy are related to adverse drug events, interactions and medication non-adherence.3 35 MedRec is a crucial responsibility of clinical pharmacists, focusing on identifying and correcting MDs9 and has been demonstrated to significantly improve medication safety.22 Thus, MedRec has the potential to bridge the communication gap between the healthcare team and the patient.36 The study revealed that 56.77% (95% CI 48.5 to 68.9) of patients experienced at least one MD, aligning with previous research indicating high rates of such discrepancies during transitions of care, especially in emergency departments.1523 29 3740

Clinical pharmacists used at least two sources for medication information to document BPMH, with discrepancies potentially arising from the busy and critical nature of the emergency department environment.27 39 Physicians prioritise quick assessments and patient stabilisation in emergencies, making it difficult to gather detailed drug histories from patients or families. Clinical pharmacists, with their additional time, expertise in medication evaluation and fewer responsibilities in the emergency department, are better suited to perform thorough interviews on patient admission.27

Clinical pharmacists obtain BPMH in many hospitals within 24 hours using at least two sources of medication information.192141 In this study, MedRec was conducted within 24 hours of emergency department arrival to facilitate decisions on continuing or withholding medications during hospitalisation. The study highlighted that MDs were more likely to be associated with the number of sources consulted for the BPMH, as observed in the research conducted in Saudi Arabia.42 It contributes to the identification of potential drug-related problems and provides information necessary to remove, adjust or add therapies and ensure better patient safety.43

The most common type of MD observed in this study was omission error, followed by wrong dose. This aligns with the findings of other studies that have consistently identified omission errors as the most prevalent type of MD, as similarly reported in the literature, even if they can vary in percentages.32 43 44 Omission errors can occur when the home medication of a patient is not continued or reordered on admission or when a new medication is not prescribed at discharge. These errors can have significant clinical implications, as they may lead to suboptimal therapeutic management and adverse health outcomes.32

The clinical pharmacists, in collaboration with an internist and a senior nurse, identified and categorised the MDs according to the NCC MERP index, which shows the occurrence and the level of harm of MEs.14 Category B was the most prevalent, with 180 MDs (42.86%), followed by category D with 80 MDs (19.05%). In the multidisciplinary team, physicians, nurses and clinical pharmacists work together to provide medical care, nursing care and pharmaceutical care, respectively, to patients.45 Effective communication of the role of pharmacists in patient care encourages collaboration with physicians and nurses. However, on-the-job training is essential to enhance their pharmacotherapy competencies, which are crucial for delivering clinical pharmacy services.46 Reviewing and understanding the topic of MDs (MEs), especially by nurses, can help in advancing patient care. In clinical settings, nurses are on the front lines, encouraged to be one integrated team as nursing care to prevent the occurrence of MEs.47

The study found a high acceptance rate of interventions at 80.48%, suggesting treating physicians were receptive to suggestions by clinical pharmacists. Inconsistent with this study, a Brazilian study reported a 71% acceptance rate by prescribing physicians,13 and a study in Lebanon12 showed that 64.6% of interventions were accepted by prescribing physicians. In the previous study, about 72.3% of cases were accepted.10 This highlights the importance of involving pharmacists in the MedRec process at all transitions of care, including the emergency department, as they can play a crucial role in identifying and resolving MDs, thereby improving patient safety and quality of care.37 48

The present study reported factors associated with the occurrence of MDs, including a higher number of previous/home medications (≥5 medicines). This was consistent with previous studies that have identified the association of MDs with the number of drugs used before hospitalisation.12 19 22 23 40 49 In the current study, greater numbers of comorbidities (≥3 comorbidities) were significantly associated with MDs. Similar results and significant associations of the number of comorbidities with MDs were presented in a previous study.13 Older age (≥75 years) was also significantly associated with MDs in the present study, and other previous studies showed that older patients had a higher association with unintended MDs.43 50 This may be because older patients are often on polypharmacy, have polymorbidity, are enrolled in several disease management programmes and have a high rate of chronic conditions.

Patients with a higher number of medications and comorbidities are more likely to experience complex medication regimens, which can increase the likelihood of MEs during the MedRec process.27 28 38 Clinical pharmacists should be members of the emergency healthcare team in the hospital, where the MedRec process is among the top fundamental clinical services. The establishment of a BPMH list and the discussion of treating physicians about the pharmacotherapy plan can detect and reduce MDs, and this may reduce medication-related harm and enhance patient safety.

The study highlights significant implications for both clinicians and policymakers regarding medication management in older adults. Clinicians should engage in multidisciplinary collaboration, especially in emergency departments, and involve clinical pharmacists to improve medication history accuracy and patient safety. Policymakers are urged to establish pharmacist-led MedRec programmes and allocate resources for training and staffing. The implementation of electronic medical records can enhance medication safety, and patient engagement initiatives are essential for empowering older adults in their care. These strategies aim to minimise medication-related harm and improve healthcare outcomes.

This study demonstrates strengths such as a prospective observational design and the use of a structured questionnaire, enabling systematic data collection for MedRec. Trained clinical pharmacists contributed to the accuracy of the BPMH by gathering information from reliable sources. However, the present study has some methodological limitations, including its focus on comprehensive specialised hospitals in northwest Ethiopia, which may restrict the generalisability of the findings to other regions. Additionally, it did not assess the potential clinical impact of identified MDs or evaluate the long-term outcomes of pharmaceutical interventions.

Based on the findings of this study, several actions are recommended to enhance medication safety for older adults. Primarily, establishing a pharmacist-led MedRec service in emergency departments to swiftly identify MDs is recommended. Then, training healthcare professionals, particularly pharmacists, in effective MedRec practices to improve the accuracy of medication histories, promoting the use of electronic medical records and computerised physician order entry systems for reliable medication information. Educating and empowering patients to participate in their medication management, facilitating early discrepancy detection and better outcomes. Finally, conducting future research to assess the clinical consequences of MDs and the effectiveness of pharmacist-led interventions to inform policy and practice enhancements.

Conclusion

The present study revealed a higher magnitude of MDs in the emergency department of the comprehensive specialised hospitals. Factors associated with the occurrence of MDs included polypharmacy, the presence of comorbidities and older age. The present study findings highlight the need to address medication safety in the emergency department setting, and involving clinical pharmacists in the MedRec process can be a valuable strategy to enhance the detection and resolution of MDs. Clinical pharmacists play a crucial role in the patient care journey, focusing on enhancing the safety and effectiveness of medications, thereby reducing MDs which may cause adverse drug events.
