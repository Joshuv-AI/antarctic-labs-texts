The association between the Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology (PURE) healthy diet score and cognitive decline in Chinese older adults
Authors: Xinyue Su, Yuanxiu Wei, Gangling Wang, Qiguo Meng, Cheng Zu, Ziliang Ye, Yuanyuan Zhang, Xianhui Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology (PURE) healthy diet score emphasizes six protective food groups, yet its association with cognitive decline in aging populations remains unclear. This longitudinal analysis of 3106 participants used data from the 1997, 2000, 2004, and 2006 waves of the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS). Dietary quality was measured using the PURE healthy diet score (range 0–6) based on fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes, fish, and dairy. Cognitive function was assessed repeatedly using modified Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status-modified (TICS-m), yielding global (0–27) and standardized composite cognitive scores. Cognitive decline was defined as the annual rate of change in these scores over time. Linear mixed‑effects models evaluated the association between PURE score and cognitive decline. Over a median of 5.3 years, a higher score was associated with a slower rate of cognitive decline. Compared to a score of 0, those scoring 5–6 had an adjusted 0.42-points/year slower decline in global cognitive score (95%CI: 0.22–0.62; P < 0.001) and a 0.06-SDU/year slower decline in composite cognitive score (95%CI:0.03–0.09; P < 0.001). Higher adherence to the PURE healthy diet score was associated with a modest but statistically significant attenuation of cognitive decline in Chinese older adults, supporting its potential utility for cognitive health promotion.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Cognitive impairment represents a significant and growing public health burden in aging China, with an estimated 38 million adults aged 65 or older affected by mild cognitive impairment and approximately 15 million living with moderate to severe dementia1. Accumulating evidence underscores the influential role of diet as a modifiable factor in brain health, particularly in shaping cognitive trajectories over time2.

Poor dietary habits are linked not only to increased cardiovascular risk3,4 but also to accelerated cognitive decline5–7. These adverse associations are mediated through several interconnected biological pathways, including chronic systemic inflammation and neuroinflammation8, oxidative and nitrosative stress that damages neuronal integrity9, and impaired cerebrovascular function that compromises cerebral perfusion10. Together, these mechanisms promote neuropathological changes such as elevated amyloid-β burden, reduced neurogenesis, and diminished synaptic plasticity8–10.

Dietary patterns are commonly evaluated using composite indices such as the Alternative Healthy Eating Index‑2010 (AHEI‑2010)11, Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH)12, the Alternative Mediterranean Diet (aMed)13, and the Mediterranean‑DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) diet14, which incorporate both beneficial and unfavorable dietary components. In contrast, the Prospective Urban‑Rural Epidemiology (PURE) healthy diet score employs a simpler, additive framework that focuses exclusively on six protective food groups: vegetables, legumes, fruits, nuts, fish, and dairy13. Higher PURE scores, reflecting greater intake of these foods, have been associated with reduced risks of cardiovascular disease and mortality13. This positively oriented design, centered on increasing intake of universally recognized healthy foods, enhances cross‑cultural applicability and offers a more actionable public health message, especially for older adults.

Building on this mechanistic and methodological rationale, we hypothesize that a higher PURE healthy diet score is inversely associated with cognitive decline. However, the relationship between the PURE score and cognitive trajectories has not been examined in older adult populations. To address this knowledge gap, we investigated the longitudinal association between the PURE healthy diet score and cognitive decline using data from the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS).

Results

Study population characteristics

The final analytic sample consisted of 3106 participants (see Supplementary Fig. 1). Within this longitudinal cohort, 1547 participants (49.8%) contributed cognitive data from two survey waves, 948 (30.5%) from three waves, and 611 (19.7%) from all four waves. At baseline, participants had a mean age of 63.1 years (SD = 7.0) and a mean PURE healthy diet score of 2.2 points (SD = 1.3). No statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the included participants and those excluded from analysis (n = 1619), as detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

As shown in Table 1, participants with higher PURE healthy diet scores tended to be younger, male, urban residents, and with higher alcohol intake. Educational level increased significantly with higher diet scores (P for trend <0.001). Both global and composite cognitive scores at baseline and final follow-up showed strong positive trends across PURE score categories (all P for trend <0.001), indicating better baseline cognition and slower decline over time with higher diet quality. Nutritionally, higher PURE scores were associated with greater total energy intake and higher absolute consumption of fat, protein, carbohydrates, sodium, and potassium (all P for trend <0.001). No significant linear trend was observed for physical activity (P for trend = 0.305). Systolic and diastolic blood pressure varied across score groups (P for heterogeneity = 0.015 and 0.006, respectively), but without a consistent monotonic trend (P for trend =0.035 for SBP and 0.008 for DBP), reflecting fluctuation rather than a clear directional association with diet quality.

Relationship between PURE healthy diet score and cognitive decline

In this longitudinal analysis with a median follow-up of 5.3 years (interquartile range: 2.4–8.2), multivariable-adjusted models showed that a higher PURE healthy diet score was significantly associated with a decelerated rate of cognitive decline. Specifically, each 1-point increase in the PURE score corresponded to a 0.06-point per year reduction in the rate of global cognitive decline (95%CI: 0.03, 0.08; P < 0.001) and a 0.01-SDU per year reduction in the rate of decline for the standardized composite cognitive score (95%CI: 0.006, 0.012; P < 0.001). Compared to participants with a baseline PURE score of 0, those with scores of 5–6 exhibited a 0.42‑point per year slower decline in global cognition (95%CI: 0.22, 0.62; P < 0.001) and a 0.06‑SDU per year slower decline in the composite score (95%CI: 0.03, 0.09; P < 0.001) (Table 2). The SD of the annual rate of global cognitive decline within the cohort was 0.19 points/year. Thus, the 0.42-point/year slower decline associated with a PURE score of 5–6 corresponds to an effect size of approximately 2.2 SD units of annual change. Using cluster-robust standard errors yielded nearly identical estimates and confidence intervals (see Supplementary Table 3), supporting the robustness of these findings to potential model misspecification.

Although this study represents a secondary analysis within an established cohort, we performed a post hoc power estimation to assess the statistical adequacy of our sample. Given the observed effect size in our primary analysis, a 0.01 SDU per year reduction in the rate of cognitive decline per 1‑point increase in the PURE healthy diet score, and a sample size of 3106 participants with repeated cognitive assessments over a median follow‑up of 5.3 years, our mixed‑effects longitudinal design provided high statistical power (>90%) under conventional assumptions (two‑sided α = 0.05, residual SD ≈ 1 SDU). This level of power enabled detection of an effect as small as β ≥ 0.006 SDU per year, which corresponds closely to the lower bound of the observed confidence interval and aligns with clinically meaningful differences reported in prior cognitive aging research. Thus, the available sample size and longitudinal measurement structure were sufficient to robustly identify the reported associations.

Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our primary findings across multiple analytical scenarios. First, expanding the PURE healthy diet score to include red meat or whole grains did not materially alter the association with cognitive decline (Supplementary Tables 4, 5). Second, the protective association remained consistent when examining domain‑specific cognitive decline in verbal memory, attention, and calculation scores (Supplementary Table 6). Third, to address potential reverse causality, we repeated the analysis after excluding participants with baseline global cognitive scores below 6 points; the results were unchanged (Supplementary Table 7). Fourth, restricting the analysis to the first two cognitive assessments to minimize practice effects yielded similar estimates (Supplementary Table 8). Finally, in a competing‑risk framework using the Fine‑Gray model to account for death and incident cardiovascular disease as competing events, the PURE score remained significantly associated with clinically meaningful cognitive decline (defined as a ≥ 3‑point or ≥15% decrease over 5 years), reinforcing the primary findings (Supplementary Table 9). Together, these analyses support the stability and generalizability of the observed diet‑cognition relationship across different model specifications, outcome definitions, and potential sources of bias.

Stratified analyses for PURE Healthy Diet Score by potential effect modifiers

Stratified analyses revealed potential effect modification of the association between the PURE healthy diet score and cognitive decline by PA levels. The protective association appeared stronger among participants with higher PA levels (≥72.9 MET-hours/week, the median) compared to those with lower levels (<72.9 MET-hours/week), with a nominal interaction P value of 0.016. However, after applying the Benjamini‑Hochberg FDR correction for multiple testing, this interaction was no longer statistically significant (FDR‑adjusted P = 0.128). No other examined factors, including sex, age, BMI, alcohol consumption, smoking status, geographic regions, or dietary intakes of protein, fat, and carbohydrates, significantly modified the association between the PURE score and the rate of cognitive decline (Fig. 1).

Association between individual food components in the PURE healthy diet score and cognitive decline

Supplementary Table 10 presents the association between each food component of the PURE healthy diet score and the annual rate of change in cognitive scores, analyzed using multivariable-adjusted models. For each food group, participants were classified as having a score of 1 if their intake exceeded the population median, and a score of 0 if their intake was at or below the median.

Results indicated that higher intake of most food components was associated with a slower cognitive decline, reflected by positive β values. Specifically, higher consumption of dairy products (β = 0.12, 95%CI: 0.03, 0.21, P = 0.009), fruits (β = 0.11, 95%CI: 0.04, 0.17, P = 0.002), vegetables (β = 0.09, 95%CI: 0.04, 0.14, P < 0.001), fish (β = 0.11, 95%CI: 0.05, 0.17, P < 0.001), and nuts (β = 0.15, 95%CI: 0.05, 0.26, P = 0.004) was significantly associated with a reduced rate of decline in the global cognitive score. Similar protective trends were observed for the composite cognitive score across these food groups. In contrast, legume intake showed no significant association with cognitive change, with β values close to zero and non-significant P-values for both cognitive measures (global: β = −0.02, P = 0.471; composite: β = −0.00, P = 0.382).

To investigate whether the PURE healthy diet score reflects additive or synergistic associations, we performed hypothesis-driven statistical tests. First, a likelihood ratio test comparing a model containing only the six individual food components with one also including the total PURE score did not show a significant improvement in fit (P = 0.304). Second, targeted interaction analyses for biologically plausible food pairs (fruits-vegetables and fish-vegetables) revealed no statistically significant multiplicative interactions (P > 0.05 for both). These findings suggest that the association between the PURE score and slower cognitive decline is consistent with an additive model, with no evidence of significant synergy among its components in this cohort.

Mediation analysis of cardiometabolic pathways

We conducted formal multivariable-adjusted mediation analysis to examine whether the protective association between a higher PURE healthy diet score and slower cognitive decline was mediated through cardiometabolic health markers, including BMI, SBP, and DBP. None of these markers showed statistically significant mediation effects. The estimated proportions of the total effect mediated were 0.92% (95%CI: −4.85% to 9.62%; P = 0.424) for BMI, −0.20% (95% CI: −5.27% to 3.38%; P = 0.738) for SBP, and 0.63% (95%CI: −4.37% to 9.99%; P = 0.550) for DBP. All confidence intervals included zero, indicating no evidence of meaningful mediation through these pathways in our cohort (Supplementary Table 11).

Discussion

This study provides the first longitudinal evidence linking the PURE healthy diet score, a simple, food-based metric, to a decelerated rate of cognitive decline in a large cohort of Chinese older adults. Over a median follow-up of 5.3 years, multivariable-adjusted mixed-effects models revealed that a higher baseline PURE score was significantly associated with a slower annual rate of decline in both global and standardized composite cognitive scores. Participants with a score of 5-6 exhibited a substantially slower decline, equivalent to an effect size of approximately 2.2 standard deviations of the annual rate of change, compared to those with a score of 0. The robustness of this association was confirmed by an extensive series of sensitivity analyses, including competing-risk models. Importantly, formal mediation analysis found no statistically significant role for BMI, SBP, or DBP as mediators, suggesting that the underlying mechanisms may operate independently of these traditional cardiometabolic pathways.

Our findings reinforce the established link between high-quality dietary patterns and healthier cognitive aging. Our results extend the evidence base by demonstrating that the PURE healthy diet score, a simple metric focusing on six protective food groups, is longitudinally associated with a slower rate of cognitive decline in older Chinese adults. This association aligns with the protective effects observed for more complex global indices, such as the Mediterranean15, DASH16, AHEI17, and the MIND diets18. A key distinction lies in operational logic: unlike these indices which often combine recommendations to increase beneficial foods and restrict detrimental ones, the PURE score adopts a minimalistic and positively-framed approach focused solely on increased consumption. This structure may enhance its feasibility as a public health message, particularly for older populations.

An important consideration is whether the cognitive effects of specific dietary components vary across populations. While the general principle appears robust, effect heterogeneity is biologically plausible. Factors such as population-specific baseline nutritional status, genetic backgrounds (e.g., APOE genotype), and gut microbiome composition may modulate the relationship between foods like fish (rich in omega-3) or whole grains and cognitive outcomes. This underscores the value of validating dietary patterns within specific cultural and genetic contexts. In the Chinese context, our results are congruent with studies showing benefits of adapted dietary patterns, such as the Chinese-adapted MIND (cMIND) diet19,20, and with broader lifestyle research from cohorts like the Shanghai Aging Study21. Therefore, the primary contribution of this study is not to contest established indices but to validate a simpler, complementary tool. By demonstrating its association with cognitive preservation, we extend the utility of the PURE score beyond cardiometabolic health22. Its straightforward, affirmative framework (“what to add”) offers a potentially lower-barrier option for dietary guidance aimed at maintaining cognitive health in late life, even as the precise biological pathways warrant further investigation across diverse populations.

The lack of significant mediation through BMI or blood pressure directs inquiry toward alternative biological pathways through which the PURE diet may confer cognitive benefits. The combination of foods emphasized by the score is rich in nutrients plausibly operating through several synergistic or parallel mechanisms:

First, Anti-inflammatory and Antioxidant Pathways: The PURE healthy diet score, which emphasizes fruits, vegetables, nuts, and fish, may protect against cognitive decline in part by modulating key Alzheimer’s disease pathologies, including amyloid‑beta deposition and tau hyperphosphorylation. This protection likely operates through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant pathways. Vegetables, fruits, and nuts provide abundant vitamins C and E and polyphenols, which mitigate oxidative stress23, a recognized contributor to neuronal damage and the pathological aggregation of amyloid‑beta and tau proteins. Additionally, the omega‑3 polyunsaturated fatty acids found in fish exert potent anti‑inflammatory effects, such as modulating microglial activity and reducing pro‑inflammatory cytokines in the brain24. Together, these mechanisms may help slow the progression of proteinopathy‑driven cognitive decline.

Second, Gut-Brain Axis Modulation: This is an emerging frontier. The PURE diet, rich in vegetables, legumes, and nuts, provides ample dietary fiber. Fiber is fermented by the gut microbiota to produce short-chain fatty acids, which help maintain intestinal barrier integrity, reduce systemic inflammation, and may directly or indirectly (via immune and neuroendocrine pathways) influence brain function, synaptic plasticity, and microglial activity25. Thus, the PURE diet may exert neuroprotective effects by fostering a beneficial gut microbiome and modulating the gut-brain axis26.

Third, Metabolic and Cerebrovascular Health: While mediation through classical cardiometabolic markers was not significant, the PURE diet may support brain health by improving insulin sensitivity, lipid profiles, and endothelial function, thereby optimizing cerebral microvasculature and energy metabolism. Evidence from large multinational cohorts with MRI data suggests that high carbohydrate intake (particularly refined carbs) is associated with a higher risk of subclinical cerebrovascular injury, whereas fats from nuts, fish, and dairy show protective associations27. This implies that by promoting intake of healthy fats and optimizing macronutrient balance, the PURE diet may help preserve white matter integrity and cerebrovascular health.

Our component-specific analysis indicated that higher intake of fruits, vegetables, nuts, fish, and dairy was each independently associated with slower global cognitive decline, while legumes showed no significant association. This pattern may reflect the differential impact of specific nutrients on distinct neurobiological pathways. Linking our findings to putative mechanisms, we posit that the overall benefit of the PURE diet likely arises from additive contributions across multiple cognitive domains. For instance, the association of fish and nuts with cognitive health aligns with the established role of DHA in hippocampal synaptic plasticity28 and vitamin E in mitigating oxidative stress in neuronal membranes, mechanisms crucial for memory29. Concurrently, the association of fruits and vegetables may relate to the benefits of diverse phytochemicals on cerebral blood flow and neuroinflammation, supporting executive function and processing speed mediated by the prefrontal cortex30.

Thus, the lack of significant synergy among components supports an additive model of protection. The PURE diet may confer cognitive benefits by simultaneously supporting multiple pathways: enhancing neuronal structural integrity, antioxidant capacity, and vascular and metabolic health of the brain. Future research with larger samples is needed to dissect the specific effects of individual food groups on particular cognitive domains (e.g., episodic memory, executive function) to reveal more precise “food-to-brain-function” mappings.

Our findings carry direct implications for public health practice. The simplicity (six food groups) and positive framing (“eat more” rather than “restrict”) of the PURE score make it highly suitable as a core message for community-based health promotion programs targeting older adults. Actionable recommendations can be distilled into simple messages like “Aim for two servings of vegetables, one fruit, and one serving of dairy daily, plus fish twice a week and a handful of nuts regularly”. Such serving-based guidance is more accessible than complex recipes or caloric calculations for an older demographic. At the clinical level, healthcare providers and dietitians can utilize the PURE score as a rapid screening tool for dietary quality and a focus for brief intervention during routine consultations with middle-aged and older adults. Given that its benefits may be independent of changes in weight or blood pressure, as suggested by our mediation analysis, it offers a valuable, complementary strategy for older individuals who struggle with intensive lifestyle modifications or have difficulty controlling traditional vascular risk factors.

While the PURE healthy diet score demonstrated a clear association with slower cognitive decline in our cohort, it is important to note that this index is intentionally narrow in scope. It focuses on increasing six protective food groups and does not incorporate dietary components typically considered detrimental to health, such as processed meats, refined carbohydrates, or added sugars. This design enhances its simplicity and translatability across diverse settings but means that a high score does not necessarily reflect an overall balanced or high-quality diet. Individuals could theoretically achieve a high PURE score while still consuming substantial amounts of unhealthy foods. Furthermore, the score does not account for preparation methods, which may modulate the health effects of included foods. Future research is warranted to externally validate the PURE healthy diet score in diverse aging populations and to investigate its utility in multi-faceted dietary assessment frameworks.

Key strengths of this study include the use of longitudinal data from a nationally representative cohort, validated methods for dietary and cognitive assessment, long-term follow-up, sophisticated mixed-effects modeling adjusted for numerous potential confounders, and a rigorous set of sensitivity analyses including competing-risk models.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, despite adjustment for a wide range of covariates, the observational nature of our study precludes causal inference due to potential residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly measured lifestyle and clinical factors. These may include, but are not limited to, sleep quality, mental health status, specific micronutrient levels, use of cognition-related medications, as well as genetic predisposition (e.g., APOE ε4 genotype), detailed socioeconomic circumstances, and food preparation methods (e.g., frying or use of animal fats). This remains an inherent limitation of our study design. Second, dietary intake was assessed using multiple 24-h recalls administered on non-consecutive days across different survey years. While this method is standard in large-scale epidemiology31 and has been validated32 in our cohort, it is subject to measurement error. Within-person day-to-day variation and potential recall inaccuracy can introduce random error, which typically attenuates true associations toward the null. Systematic underreporting, particularly of certain foods, may introduce bias. Furthermore, although collecting recalls across multiple years helps capture habitual intake, seasonal variations in food consumption or long-term recall errors could still influence the calculated cumulative average. These limitations are inherent to self-reported dietary assessment and suggest that our observed associations may, if anything, be conservative estimates. Third, the PURE healthy diet score was constructed using cohort-specific median cutoffs. While this approach effectively ranks individuals according to relative adherence within the study population, it may limit direct quantitative comparability of score values with studies conducted in populations having substantially different dietary baselines. Nevertheless, this relative-ranking method remains valuable for identifying diet-outcome associations within similar sociocultural settings and offers a pragmatic, food-based metric for public health and clinical guidance, particularly in resource-limited contexts. Fourth, cognitive function was assessed using a face-to-face adapted version of the TICS‑m. Although the original validation was for telephone administration, the consistent application of our adapted protocol across all participants and survey waves ensures the internal validity of longitudinal comparisons within our study. Additionally, while practice effects were mitigated by long inter-assessment intervals and sensitivity analyses, their residual influence cannot be entirely ruled out. Therefore, while absolute scores may not be directly comparable across studies using different modes or protocols, this is unlikely to affect the validity of the primary exposure-outcome association reported herein. Fifth, neither our dataset nor the CHNS included direct biomarkers of Alzheimer’s pathology, such as cerebrospinal fluid measures or neuroimaging. This limits mechanistic insights into whether diet influences amyloid‑beta or tau pathways, underscoring the need for future studies to integrate blood‑based biomarkers. Finally, our cohort consisted of Chinese older adults. Caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to other ethnicities, cultural contexts, or younger populations. The applicability of the PURE score’s specific food components may require careful consideration of local dietary patterns and food availability in different settings.

This longitudinal study of Chinese older adults found that higher adherence to the PURE healthy diet score was associated with a significantly slower rate of cognitive decline. The association remained robust after adjustment for a wide range of sociodemographic, lifestyle, and clinical risk factors and was not mediated by body mass index or blood pressure. The PURE score, a simple, positively oriented, food-based dietary metric, demonstrates practical value for use in clinical and public health initiatives aimed at supporting brain health in aging populations. Taken together, these findings provide exploratory insights that contribute to hypothesis generation regarding diet and cognitive aging, rather than establishing causal inferences. Future research should seek to elucidate underlying biological mechanisms, potentially involving gut–brain and neurovascular pathways, by incorporating biomarkers such as neuroimaging and gut microbiome profiling. Ultimately, randomized controlled trials are warranted to determine whether interventions based on the PURE dietary pattern can effectively delay cognitive decline.

Methods

Data source and ethical approval

This study constitutes a longitudinal analysis using prospectively collected data from the CHNS, a publicly accessible, long-term, open-cohort study. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for the CHNS was obtained from the Institutional Review Boards at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill and the National Institute of Nutrition and Food Safety, China Center for Disease Control and Prevention. Written informed consent was secured from all participants.

Study population and design

The CHNS was initiated in 1989, with follow-ups conducted every 2–4 years. Detailed descriptions of the study design, sampling methods, and key findings have been published previously33–38. The study sample was drawn from nine provinces (Heilongjiang, Liaoning, Hubei, Hunan, Guizhou, Guangxi, Shandong, Henan, Jiangsu) and three major municipalities (Beijing, Shanghai, Chongqing), with data collected across multiple survey waves (1989, 1991, 1993, 1997, 2000, 2004, 2006, 2009, 2011, and 2015). A multi-stage random cluster sampling method was employed.

For the present analysis, we utilized data from the 1997, 2000, 2004, and 2006 survey waves during which cognitive function was assessed. We included participants aged 55–94 years at their first cognitive assessment (baseline). To analyze cognitive change over time, participants were required to have at least two valid cognitive assessments. From an initial pool of 4738 eligible participants, we excluded those with only one cognitive assessment (n = 1619) and individuals with extreme daily caloric intakes (n = 13; males: >4200 or <600 kcal/day; females: >3600 or <500 kcal/day)39. The final analytical sample thus comprised 3106 participants (see Supplementary Fig. 1 for the participant flow diagram).

Dietary assessment

Dietary data in the CHNS were collected using a validated multi-level assessment protocol33–38. At each survey wave, trained field staff conducted three consecutive 24-h dietary recalls at the individual level, alongside a three-day household weighing inventory of all foods and condiments. Recall days were randomly assigned between Monday and Sunday to ensure balanced representation across weekdays and weekends. Household-level consumption of cooking oil and condiments was converted to per‑capita estimates, and individual intake was apportioned accordingly. Nutrient intakes were computed based on the China Food Composition Tables. The validity of this integrated approach for assessing energy and nutrient intake has been established previously32. For the present analysis, nutrient and food intakes were averaged over the three recall days within each survey round.

PURE healthy diet score

The PURE healthy diet score was constructed based on the intake of six food groups: fruits, vegetables, legumes, nuts, fish, and dairy40. For each food group, the median intake was calculated across the entire study population. A score of 1 was assigned to participants whose intake exceeded the population median, and a score of 0 was assigned to those whose intake was equal to or below the median. The overall PURE score was derived by summing scores across all six components, yielding a total ranging from 0 to 6, with higher scores representing a healthier dietary pattern.

In our primary analysis, we used the standard 6‑component PURE score. Because evidence regarding red meat and whole grains in relation to cognitive health is more heterogeneous, and can be influenced by processing methods, cooking practices (particularly in Chinese diets)41–43, and overall dietary context, we did not include these items in the main score. Instead, we pre-specified sensitivity analyses in which we separately evaluated modified versions of the PURE score that incorporated red meat or whole grains as additional components.

Covariate assessment

Covariates were selected based on established or plausible associations with cognitive decline and/or the PURE healthy diet score. Demographic factors included sex, age (continuous), residence (urban/rural), and geographic region (north/south/central). Socioeconomic and lifestyle factors comprised educational attainment (illiteracy, primary school, middle school, or high school or above), occupational status (farmer, worker, unemployed, or other), smoking status (non‑smoker/smoker), alcohol consumption (non‑drinker/drinker), and physical activity. Clinical and anthropometric measures covered body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) blood pressure, and self-reported diabetes.

Physical activity (PA) was quantified as total metabolic equivalent of task‑hours per week (MET‑h/week), calculated by multiplying the duration of occupational, household, transportation, and leisure activities by their respective MET values. Blood pressure was measured by trained staff using a mercury sphygmomanometer after a 5‑min seated rest; the average of three consecutive readings was used for SBP and DBP44,45.

Evaluation of cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed using a modified, face‑to‑face version of the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS‑m)46, which has been used and validated in previous studies within the CHNS cohort47,48. All assessments were conducted face-to-face by trained field staff according to a standardized protocol49. The assessment comprised three tasks: (1) Verbal memory: Immediate and delayed recall of a 10‑word list (each scored 0–10 points); (2) Attention: Counting backward from 20 (scored 0–2 points); (3) Computational ability: Serial subtraction of 7 from 100, repeated five times (scored 0–5 points).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the global cognitive score, calculated by summing the scores from the three domains, yielding a total range of 0–27, with higher scores representing better cognitive performance. To account for potential differential weighting of the three components in the global score, a standardized composite score was also derived. This was computed by averaging the Z‑scores of the verbal memory domain and a combined score from the attention and computational ability domains5–7,50. The composite score is expressed in standard deviation units (SDU), providing a domain-balanced measure of overall cognitive function. A positive Z-score indicates better cognitive function than the mean population score, and a negative score indicates a poor cognitive score.

Cognitive decline was operationalized as the rate of longitudinal change (slope) in these scores over time. This rate was estimated using linear mixed-effects models to evaluate its association with the PURE healthy diet score.

Statistical analysis

Data completeness was high, with less than 5% missing values for most covariates, except for BMI (7.1%), SBP (5.9%), and DBP (5.9%). Missing data were handled using multiple imputation. We generated 5 imputed datasets using fully conditional specification (chained equations), with the imputation model including all analysis variables listed in the primary analytical model (e.g., exposure, outcomes, and all adjusted covariates). Results from the five imputed datasets were analyzed separately and then combined using Rubin’s rules to obtain pooled estimates, standard errors, and confidence intervals. Baseline characteristics are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Differences in characteristics across PURE healthy diet score categories (0–6 points) were evaluated using chi-square tests for categorical variables and analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables. Trends across ordered PURE score categories were assessed for continuous variables using linear regression models with the PURE score entered as an ordinal predictor.

We employed linear mixed-effects models to assess the longitudinal association between the PURE healthy diet score and cognitive function. Two continuous cognitive outcomes were analyzed: (1) the global cognitive score (range 0–27), and (2) the standardized composite cognitive score (expressed in SDU). For both measures, a higher score indicates better cognitive performance. Cognitive decline was modeled as the annual rate of change using linear mixed-effects models, with follow-up time (in years) included as a continuous predictor. The appropriateness of a linear time trend was evaluated by adding a quadratic term for time, and the quadratic term was not statistically significant (P > 0.10) for either cognitive outcome, supporting the use of a linear term in the primary model. Repeated cognitive scores were used as the dependent variable. The fixed effects comprised: the baseline PURE score (per 1‑point increment), follow‑up time, the interaction term follow‑up time × PURE score, and the following baseline covariates: sex, educational attainment, occupations, residence, geographic regions and dietary intakes of protein, fat, carbohydrates, sodium, and potassium as wall as repeat measured age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, PA, BMI, SBP, DBP, use of antihypertensive medications, diabetes status during the follow-up. A random intercept at the subject level was included to account for within‑subject correlation. The coefficient (β) for the follow‑up time × PURE score interaction term is the primary parameter of interest. It quantifies the difference in the annual rate of cognitive change associated with a 1‑point increase in the PURE score, holding other variables constant. Given that higher cognitive scores indicate better function, a positive β reflects a slower rate of cognitive decline (i.e., a protective effect associated with a higher PURE score), whereas a negative β indicates a more rapid decline. We evaluated potential multicollinearity among covariates in the fully adjusted multivariable model by calculating variance inflation factors (VIF). One covariate showed a VIF of 3.58, which lies slightly above the commonly used threshold of 3.0 but remains well below the more conservative cut-off of 5.0. All other covariates had VIFs below 3.0 (Supplementary Table 1). Given that this degree of collinearity is unlikely to substantially bias the regression estimates or compromise model stability, we retained the variable to ensure the model’s comprehensiveness and clinical interpretability.

The assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity of residuals were examined using standard diagnostic plots, including Q-Q plots and scale-location plots. While the residuals approximately followed a normal distribution, some degree of heteroscedasticity was observed (Supplementary Fig. 2). To ensure that our inferences were robust to potential violations of model assumptions, particularly homoscedasticity and the structure of within-subject correlation, we recomputed the standard errors of all primary models using cluster-robust variance estimation with the small-sample adjusted CR2 estimator (implemented via the clubSandwich package in R). The resulting point estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were virtually identical to those obtained from the original models, confirming the stability of our reported associations.

We conducted exploratory stratified analyses to assess the robustness of our findings and to explore potential effect modification by key demographic, clinical, and lifestyle factors. Stratification variables included sex (male vs. female), age (<65, 65-75, and ≥75 years), body mass index (BMI < 24 vs. ≥ 24 kg/m²), smoking status (non-smoker vs. smoker), alcohol consumption (non-drinker vs. drinker), education level (primary school or below vs. middle high school or above), PA (<72.9 vs. ≥72.9 MET-hours/week, based on the median), and geographic region (central, north, and south). In addition, dietary intakes of protein (<66.1 vs. ≥66.1 g/d), fat (<66.3 vs. ≥66.3 g/d), and carbohydrates (<288.0 vs. ≥288.0 g/d) were stratified based on their respective sample medians. Interaction terms between the PURE healthy diet score and each stratification variable were tested using likelihood ratio tests. To address the potential inflation of type I error due to multiple testing across interaction terms, we applied False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure for those interaction terms with a nominal P value < 0.05. In addition, to investigate potential mediating mechanisms underlying the observed association, we performed causal mediation analyses to evaluate the roles of BMI, SBP, and DBP as potential mediators. These analyses were conducted using nonparametric bootstrap resampling with 1000 simulations via the “mediation” package in R, with results expressed as bootstrap-estimated indirect effects and their corresponding 95% CIs.

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses, including: (1) Modification of the PURE healthy diet score by incorporating whole grains (classified as healthy if above the median intake) and red meat (classified as unhealthy if above the median intake), thereby expanding the theoretical score range to 0–7 points to test the stability of the diet metric; (2) Evaluation of domain-specific cognitive decline in verbal memory, attention, and calculation scores to assess whether associations were global or domain-dependent; (3) Exclusion of participants with baseline global cognitive scores below 6 points to mitigate potential reverse causality arising from pre-existing cognitive impairment; (4) Restriction of the analytical sample to participants with at least the first two cognitive assessments to minimize potential practice effects; (5) Application of a Fine-Gray competing risk model to examine the association between the PURE score and clinically meaningful cognitive decline (defined as a ≥3-point or ≥15% decrease over 5 years), treating death and incident cardiovascular disease as competing events.

A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, unless otherwise specified (e.g., when FDR correction was applied to interaction terms). All analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Specific packages included: “mice” (version 3.16.0) for multiple imputation, “lme4” (version 1.1.35.5) for linear mixed-effects models, “mediation” (version 4.5.1) for causal mediation analysis, and “clubSandwich” (version 0.6.1) for cluster-robust variance estimation.
