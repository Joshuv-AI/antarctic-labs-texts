The relationship among inflammatory biomarkers, hyperuricemia and chronic kidney disease: analysis of the NHANES 2015–2020
Authors: Huimin Li, Jingda Huang, Mindan Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammation and hyperuricemia are closely associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD). The systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) are emerging as novel biomarkers. While, the synergistic effects of these biomarkers with hyperuricemia on CKD remain unclear.MethodWe analyzed 10,226 participants from 2015–2020 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). The relationships among inflammatory biomarkers (SIRI, SII, MLR, NLR, and PLR), hyperuricemia and CKD were assessed by multivariate logistic regression models. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) and segmented regression models were used to evaluate the nonlinear relationships. The diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and incremental predictive value was further calculated by Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI). The interaction analysis was performed to explore the combined effects.ResultsSIRI, SII, MLR, and NLR were significantly linked with CKD. MLR exhibited a threshold effect at 0.22 (p-non-linear < 0.05), with significantly stronger association with CKD above this cutoff. SIRI demonstrated the best diagnostic accuracy among these biomarkers. Significant interactions were observed between hyperuricemia and inflammatory biomarkers (SIRI, SII, MLR, NLR), indicating that the association between inflammatory biomarkers and CKD is more pronounced in the presence of hyperuricemia.ConclusionThere were significant associations between inflammatory biomarkers (SII, SIRI, NLR, MLR) and CKD, with particularly stronger correlations observed in patients with hyperuricemia.Keywords: Inflammatory biomarkers, chronic kidney disease, hyperuricemia, interaction, NHANES

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has emerged as an important global health issue [1,2], with its prevalence rising markedly (reported median: 9.5%, interquartile range: 5.9–11.7%) [3]. CKD can coexist with other non-communicable diseases, increasing the risk of negative health outcomes for those affected [4]. CKD is also a distinct risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), which elevated all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality [5–7]. Furthermore, CKD increased public healthcare costs, and exacerbated the economic burden [8]. Therefore, identifying factors (such as hyperuricemia and inflammation [9,10] that linked with the onset and progression of CKD is of significant importance.

Chronic systemic inflammation is a significant risk factor for onset, and advancement to end-stage renal disease in CKD [11]. Unlike other organs with large blood flow volume, kidneys are lacked of advanced anti-inflammatory defenses and exposed to a heightened risk of inflammation-related injury [12]. Existing research has identified that in patients with CKD, high levels of C-reactive protein, plasma fibrinogen, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) are associated with the decreased renal function [13,14]. Uric acid is predominantly eliminated through renal excretion, with a minor contribution from intestinal clearance [15]. Its level increases along with the decrease of glomerular filtration [15]. Further studies have demonstrated that high baseline uric acid have an independent correlation with progressive renal dysfunction among subjects with preserved kidney function [16]. Additionally, a ‘J-shaped’ relationship was found between uric acid and all-cause mortality in CKD [16]. The mechanisms through which hyperuricemia induces kidney damage are complex. Firstly, uric acid can enhance the progression of CKD by inducing inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, and activating the renin-angiotensin system [17,18]. Secondly, uric acid is an effective antioxidant in the extracellular environment, whereas it functions as a pro-oxidant in the intracellular environment [19]. Moreover, it can stimulate NADPH oxidase and induce mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby contributing to the progression of disease [19].

Inflammation is a multifaceted immune response to injury or infection triggered by various stimuli [20]. Its hallmark features include acute microvascular reactions, edema, and the recruitment of neutrophils and monocytes [21]. The interact among platelets, granulocytes, the vascular wall, and pathogens, modulating the inflammatory response through both anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory mechanisms [22]. The monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) have been proven to reflect systemic inflammation and immune response and are widely used in the study of chronic inflammatory diseases [23]. For instance, MLR serves not only as an independent predictor of incident CKD [24], but also shows a significant link with proliferative diabetic retinopathy progression in Type 2 diabetes populations [25]. NLR can reflect the clinical severity in bronchiectasis, including functional impairment and progression [26]. It is also an independent predictor of coronary artery disease among centrally obese individuals [27]. High PLR is linked with the severity of ankylosing spondylitis [28]. In addition to those simple ratio indicators, the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), which is calculated from lymphocyte, neutrophil, and platelet counts [29], was first proposed in 2014 as an innovative inflammatory indicator for hepatocellular carcinoma prognosis [29]. First proposed in 2016, the Systemic Inflammation Response Index (SIRI), which is calculated from neutrophil, monocyte, and lymphocyte counts [30], was validated as an independent predictor of overall survival in chemotherapy-treated pancreatic cancer patients [30]. The associations between SII (or SIRI) and other diseases had been widely researched, such as psoriasis in adults, pneumonia incidence and severity in individuals with intracerebral hemorrhage, and stroke prevalence in asthma populations [31–33]. Current evidence lacks comprehensive studies investigating the synergistic impact of inflammatory biomarkers and hyperuricemia on CKD.

This study utilized the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2015–2020 data to explore the associations between inflammatory biomarkers (SIRI, SII, MLR, NLR, and PLR) and CKD prevalence, as well as the combined effects of hyperuricemia and inflammation-related biomarkers on CKD status. It aims to develop new tools for CKD evaluation and assist the preventive strategies for the population.

Materials and methods

Participants and data origin

This study was based on a cross-sectional design and included 25,531 participants from NHANES 2015–2020. NHANES is a program including U.S. adults and children, aimed to assess the health and nutritional status. Operated by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), NHANES data are utilized to assess nutritional status and its connection to health promotion and disease prevention (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). It combines information from interviews and physical examinations. All tests were conducted by trained medical personnel. The NCHS Institutional Review Board approved the study, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study enrolled participants over 20 years old, and excluded those with missing laboratory data (albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), serum creatinine, white blood cell count, platelet count, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and serum uric acid), incomplete covariate data, hematological diseases (e.g., leukemia, lymphoma, Hodgkin’s disease), or urate-lowering medications. Ultimately, 10,226 eligible participants were included (detailed in Figure 1).

Flow chart of the screening process for the selection of eligible participants.

Inflammatory biomarkers

SII = (neutrophil count) × (platelet count)/(lymphocyte count) [30]. SIRI = (neutrophil count) × (monocyte count)/(lymphocyte count) [29]. MLR = (monocyte count)/(lymphocyte count), NLR = (neutrophil count)/(lymphocyte count), and PLR = (platelet count/lymphocyte ratio count). The complete analytical methodology for blood cell measurement is available in https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Data/Nhanes/Public/2015/DataFiles/CBC_I.htm#Laboratory_Method_Files.

Chronic kidney disease

In this study, CKD was diagnosed when participants exhibited either estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or urine ACR ≥ 30 mg/g [34]. The eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation, which incorporated serum creatinine concentration, gender, race, and age [35].

The complete analytical methodology for serum creatinine measurement is available in https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Data/Nhanes/Public/2015/DataFiles/BIOPRO_I.htm#Laboratory_Method_Files. The complete analytical methodology for urinary protein and urinary creatinine measurement is available in https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Data/Nhanes/Public/2015/DataFiles/ALB_CR_I.htm#Laboratory_Method_Files.

Hyperuricemia

Hyperuricemia was defined as a serum uric acid ≥ 420 μmol/L in males and ≥ 360 μmol/L in females [36]. The complete analytical methodology is available in https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Data/Nhanes/Public/2015/DataFiles/BIOPRO_I.htm#Laboratory_Method_Files.

Covariates

Covariates spanned demographic, lifestyle-related behaviors, and health domains. Demographic variables comprised age, gender, race (Non-Hispanic White, Mexican American, Non-Hispanic Black, Other Hispanic, Other Race), education level (Less Than 9th Grade, 9-11th Grade, High School Grad/GED, Some College or AA Degree, College Graduate or Above), and poverty income ratio (PIR). Lifestyle-related behaviors comprised smoking status (never smoker, former smoker, current smoker) and alcohol consumption status (non-drinker, drinker). Health information included body mass index (BMI), diabetes (hemoglobin A1c ≥ 6.5%, fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, or use of antidiabetic medications), hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive medications), and dyslipidemia (elevated lipid levels or use of lipid-lowering medications), CVD, and cancer. Dyslipidemia was diagnosed when one or more of the following conditions were satisfied: (1) Total Cholesterol (TC) ≥ 6.21 mmol/L; (2) Triglycerides (TG) ≥ 2.26 mmol/L; (3) Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥ 4.14 mmol/L; (4) Low High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (HDL-C): HDL-C < 1.03 mmol/L in males and < 1.29 mmol/L in females; (5) Use of lipid-regulating medications [37]. CVD was defined based on self-reported diagnoses, including congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction (heart attack), and stroke. A positive response to any of these conditions was used to classify the participant as having CVD. Biochemical variables comprised serum uric acid.

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into CKD and non-CKD groups to present their characteristics. Sample weights were incorporated to ensure the results reflected the characteristics of the U.S. population. Continuous variables were presented as medians (interquartile ranges) for skewed distributions and means (standard deviations) for normally distributed data, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies (percentages). Intergroup comparisons employed t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for nonparametric data, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression models were used to assess the association between hs-CRP (SIRI, SII, MLR, NLR and PLR) and the prevalence of CKD. Additionally, these variables were categorized into quartiles, ranging from the lowest (Q1) to the highest (Q4). For each indicator, three logistic regression models were established: Model 1 was unadjusted, Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, race, education level, BMI, and PIR, and Model 3 further incorporated BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, serum uric acid, diabetes, dyslipidemia, CVD and cancer. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to investigate the nonlinear associations between inflammation-related biomarkers and CKD. For those inflammation biomarkers exhibiting nonlinear relationships with CKD, piecewise regression models were constructed using generalized linear models (GLMs) to fit segmented regression (two-piece GLMs) within each interval. Stratified analysis was conducted using the disease status of diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia as stratification variables to assess the consistency of associations between inflammatory biomarkers and CKD in subgroups. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the association between hyperuricemia and CKD prevalence. Additionally, a supplementary analysis was conducted by stratifying participants into three groups based on uric acid levels to examine the association with CKD: normal uric acid levels without gout, simple hyperuricemia without gout, and gout (self-reported or clinically diagnosed).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of inflammatory biomarkers, hyperuricemia, and their combinations for CKD. The analysis compared: (1) individual inflammatory biomarkers (SII, SIRI, NLR, MLR), (2) hyperuricemia alone, and (3) each inflammatory biomarker in combination with hyperuricemia. To assess incremental predictive value, Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) and Integrated Discrimination Improvement (IDI) indices were computed, comparing the combined models against either component alone.

To assess the interaction between inflammatory biomarkers and hyperuricemia in relation to with CKD, the stratified regression analysis was performed. In the regression model, inflammatory biomarkers and hyperuricemia were included as the primary independent variables, with their interaction term (hyperuricemia × inflammatory biomarkers) incorporated to evaluate the combined effect. The model was also adjusted for potential confounders to mitigate their impact on the results. Additionally, the mediation analysis was performed to explore potential mediating effects of inflammatory biomarkers in the association between hyperuricemia and CKD. The Bootstrap method, with 1,000 resamples, was used to estimate the indirect, direct, and total effects, and to test the significance of these effects. The proportion of the mediation effect was assessed by the ratio of the indirect effect to the total effect. Mediation was considered significant when the total effect was statistically significant, the 95% CI of the indirect effect did not include zero, and the mediation proportion was positive.

R version 4.4.0 was used to conduct statistical analysis and a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was defined as statistically significant. All analyses accounted for the complex sampling design.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participants

Among 25,531 participants, 10,226 individuals were finally enrolled in this study. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1 (weighted) and Supplementary Table 1 (unweighted). The mean age of the participants was 48 years, with females constituting 51.93% of the sample. A total of 1,788 participants (17.48%) were diagnosed with CKD. CKD patients were generally older, more likely to be female, obese, and have a history of smoking than those without CKD. They had lower alcohol consumption, education levels, and PIR, but a higher prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, hyperuricemia, dyslipidemia, CVD and cancer. CKD patients also had elevated levels of hs-CRP, SIRI, SII, MLR, NLR and PLR, with increased serum creatinine, ACR, uric acid and lower eGFR.

Association between inflammatory biomarkers (SIRI, SII, MLR, NLR, PLR) and CKD

Multivariable logistic regression models were developed to examine the association between inflammatory biomarkers (hs-CRP, SIRI, SII, MLR, NLR, PLR) and CKD (Table 2). The initial unadjusted analysis revealed significant positive associations between hs-CRP, SIRI, NLR, MLR and CKD (all p-values < 0.05). These associations persisted after adjustment for demographic and socioeconomic factors in Model 2. However, in the fully adjusted model (Model 3, incorporating metabolic factors and comorbidities), only SIRI (OR = 1.19, 95%CI: 1.09–1.29), NLR (OR = 1.17, 95%CI: 1.08–1.26) and MLR (OR = 2.56, 95%CI: 1.52–4.30) maintained independent associations (all p-values < 0.05). The association between hs-CRP and CKD became non-significant (OR = 1.01, 95%CI: 1.00–1.02, p-value = 0.186). Besides, across all models, SII as a continuous variable showed no significant association with CKD, with an OR of 1.00. However, different results were observed when SII was analyzed as a categorical variable, we observed different results. For sensitivity analysis, all inflammatory biomarkers were categorized into quartiles, using the lowest quartile (Q1) as the reference category. In all models, higher quartiles of SII, SIRI, and NLR were associated with CKD compared to Q1 (all p-values < 0.05). In Model 3, each unit increase in SII and SIRI in Q4 compared to Q1was associated with a 49% and 60% increase in CKD prevalence, respectively (p-value < 0.05). Similarly, in Model 3, each unit increase in NLR for participants in Q3 and Q4 compared to Q1 was associated with a 35% and 71% increase in CKD prevalence, respectively (p-value < 0.05). Additionally, while in Model 1 and Model 2, each unit increase in MLR for Q4 compared to Q1 was associated with an 101% and 27% increase in CKD prevalence, respectively (p-value < 0.05), this association was not observed in Model 3. PLR was significantly associated with CKD only in Model 1 (Q4 vs. Q1: OR = 1.24, 95%CI: 1.04–1.48, p-value = 0.020). The OR for SII as a continuous variable being 1.00, while the OR becomes greater than 1 when SII is categorized, could be due to several factors. First, there may be a non-linear relationship between SII and CKD. Second, the distribution of the continuous variable might be highly skewed or have extreme values, which could be smoothed out in the overall analysis. Finally, there might be a threshold effect, where the impact of SII on the outcome becomes significant only beyond a certain value or range.

Subgroup analysis showed significantly association between inflammatory biomarkers and CKD across different populations with metabolic abnormalities. Diagnostic performance comparison of inflammatory biomarkers and hyperuricemia in CKD

ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of four inflammatory biomarkers (SIRI, SII, NLR, MLR) and hyperuricemia for CKD. Results demonstrated that all inflammatory biomarkers exhibited superior diagnostic performance for CKD compared to hyperuricemia alone (AUC = 0.589), with SIRI showing the highest individual predictive value (AUC = 0.693). Notably, the combination of each inflammatory marker with hyperuricemia resulted in improved diagnostic accuracy compared to their respective single-marker models. Among these combined models, SIRI + hyperuricemia achieved the highest discriminative ability (AUC = 0.763). To further validate the predictive value of the combined models, NRI and IDI analyses were conducted. The combined models incorporating any inflammatory biomarker with hyperuricemia consistently demonstrated statistically significant enhancements in both NRI and IDI metrics (all p-values < 0.001, Supplementary Table 3) compared to models utilizing inflammatory biomarkers or hyperuricemia independently.

Comprehensive effects of hyperuricemia and inflammatory biomarkers on CKD

To investigate the potential comprehensive effects of hyperuricemia and inflammatory biomarkers on CKD, interaction analysis was further conducted. The interaction analysis showed that the relationship between SII, SIRI, NLR, and MLR with CKD varied depending on the presence of hyperuricemia (Figures 4 and 5). In participants with hyperuricemia, higher quartiles of SII were associated with CKD. Specifically, compared to Q1, each unit increase in SII in participants from Q3 and Q4 was associated with an 11% and 14% increased prevalence of CKD, respectively (p-value < 0.05). This association was not observed in individuals without hyperuricemia. The positive correlation between higher quartiles of SIRI and NLR with CKD was stronger in individuals with hyperuricemia compared to those without. Specifically, in hyperuricemia group, each unit increase in SIRI for participants in Q3 and Q4 was associated with an 9% and 15% increased prevalence of CKD, respectively (p-value < 0.05). In contrast, in the non-hyperuricemia group, each unit increase in SIRI for participants in Q4 compared to Q1 was associated with only a 4% increased prevalence of CKD (p-value < 0.05). Similarly, in hyperuricemia group, each unit increase in NLR in participants from Q3 and Q4 was associated with a 14% and 21% increased prevalence of CKD, respectively (p-value < 0.001). In the non-hyperuricemia group, however, each unit increase in NLR in participants from Q4 compared to Q1 was associated with only a 3% increased prevalence of CKD (p-value = 0.042). The interaction between MLR and hyperuricemia was examined in individuals with MLR values greater than 0.22. In hyperuricemia group, MLR was significantly associated with CKD (OR:1.59, 95% CI: 1.16–2.17, p-value = 0.006). In contrast, the association was weaker in non-hyperuricemia group (OR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.04–1.23, p-value = 0.006). In summary, significant interactions were observed between hyperuricemia and inflammatory biomarkers (SII, SIRI, NLR, MLR), indicating that the association between inflammatory biomarkers and CKD is more pronounced in the presence of hyperuricemia (all interaction p-values < 0.05).

The mediating effects of SII, SIRI, NLR, MLR and hs-CRP on the relationship between hyperuricemia and CKD were assessed, revealing significant heterogeneity in the mediating effects of different inflammation markers (Supplementary Figure 8). Statistically significant mediation effects were observed for SIRI, MLR and hs-CRP, suggesting their potential partial roles in the hyperuricemia-CKD association. Specifically, the indirect effect of SIRI was 0.0063 (95% CI: 0.0034–0.0095, p-value < 0.001), with a mediation proportion of 3.6%. The indirect effect of MLR was 0.0058 (95% CI: 0.0029–0.0088, p-value < 0.001), with a mediation proportion of 3.3%. In addition, the indirect effect of hs-CRP was 0.0066 (95% CI: 0.0043–0.0090, p-value < 0.001), with a mediation proportion of 3.8%.

Discussion

This study demonstrated a positive correlation between inflammatory biomarkers (SIRI, SII, MLR and NLR) and CKD. These results imply that these biomarkers could act as potential markers for CKD prevalence. This association remained statistically significant even after controlling for potential confounders, with these indicators showing a more stable correlation with CKD compared to hs-CRP. Analysis using RCS and piecewise regression models revealed non-linear relationships between MLR and PLR with CKD, with inflection points at 0.22 and 98.21, respectively. Notably, the positive association between MLR and CKD was observed only when MLR exceeded its inflection point. Moreover, this study demonstrated a significant association between hyperuricemia and CKD. This study also revealed that the combination of inflammatory biomarkers and hyperuricemia significantly improves the diagnostic accuracy for CKD. Further analysis demonstrated statistically significant interaction effects between hyperuricemia and inflammatory indices (SII, SIRI, NLR, MLR). The associations between inflammatory biomarkers and CKD were markedly stronger in the presence of hyperuricemia. Mediation analysis indicated that SIRI, MLR and hs-CRP may exhibit potential mediating effects in the hyperuricemia-CKD relationship, suggesting that these inflammatory indicators may be important factors linking hyperuricemia and CKD. Consequently, inflammatory biomarkers (SII, SIRI, NLR, and MLR > 0.22) showed potential as screening tools for CKD, particularly demonstrating greater clinical value in patients with comorbid hyperuricemia.

In renal biopsies from mice with AKI, the quantity of neutrophils and T cells in the renal interstitium was inversely associated with eGFR recovery [38]. The study suggests that impaired tubular repair results in sustained macrophage infiltration, followed by a secondary wave of T cell and neutrophil-driven pro-inflammatory immune activation [38]. This leads to further tubular damage and contributes to kidney atrophy as AKI progresses to CKD [38]. Monocytes can differentiate into various tissue-specific monocyte-derived cells [39].They can replace resident tissue macrophages, and play roles in either promoting inflammatory responses or aiding in inflammation resolution [39]. Compared to individuals with normal kidney function, CKD patients have an increased number of intermediate monocytes (Ims) [40]. This monocyte subset has the highest ability to rapidly produce pro-inflammatory cytokines and a strong potential to induce endothelial activation [40]. Regarding lymphocytes, previous studies have observed lymphocyte depletion and changes in lymphocyte subsets during the progression of CKD [41]. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) are classic immune regulatory cells crucial for maintaining immune homeostasis and promoting repair/regeneration [42]. A substantial body of research indicates that Tregs exhibit either quantitative or functional deficiencies in kidney disease [43,44]. A mouse model study has shown that Tregs play a protective role in kidney injury, regeneration, and fibrosis [44]. Tregs can protect the kidneys from further injury by suppressing renal inflammation and excessive immune responses [45]. Consequently, we consider that the composite biomarkers derived from leukocytes, platelets and lymphocytes could provide clinically meaningful information for evaluating CKD disease status.

Moreover, platelets, beyond hemostasis and thrombosis, also participate in immune and inflammatory responses [46,47]. Studies have found that platelet activity is increased in CKD patients [48,49]. However, our results showed no correlation between PLR and CKD, which may be due to the complex bidirectional relationship between platelets and CKD [50]. CKD may lead to platelet activation and aggregation by impairing endothelial integrity, altering hemodynamics such as hypercoagulability, anemia, and uremia [50,51]. Conversely, platelets can contribute to CKD progression by causing glomerular microcirculation disturbances through microthrombi formation, secreting pro-inflammatory factors, and promoting cell migration and inflammation via platelet-leukocyte interactions [52,53]. Additionally, platelet-leukocyte interactions can promote kidney disease progression by enhancing the generation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [54]. Currently, how platelet dysfunction alters the inflammatory state in CKD patients is not fully understood and requires further investigation.

Consistent with previous studies, this study supports the positive correlation between hyperuricemia and CKD. Moreover, this study showed interactions between hyperuricemia and inflammatory biomarkers (SIRI, SII, MLR, and NLR) in their effect on CKD. Multiple studies have confirmed that hyperuricemia is closely related to systemic inflammation. Experimental models have shown that rats with urate oxidase deficiency (UOX-KO), under sustained hyperuricemia, exhibit marked infiltration of macrophages in the kidneys, upregulation of inflammatory cytokines, and tubular apoptosis [55]. Clinical evidence also corroborates this correlation, individuals with elevated uric acid tend to exhibit elevated concentrations of CRP, TNF-α, and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [56,57]. Combining previous research with our findings, we propose that inflammation may be a key factor in the relationship between hyperuricemia and CKD. In a murine model of adenine diet-induced hyperuricemia, significant neutrophil infiltration was observed in renal tissue, with peak levels attained at day 14 [58]. Neutrophils, as key effector cells in acute inflammatory responses, serve as one of the most direct and early indicators of elevated inflammation in the body [59]. Hyperuricemia induces inflammation through various mechanisms, accelerating the onset and progression of CKD. Specifically, elevated uric acid may impair renal parenchymal cell structure and function by inducing the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway and triggering the inflammatory cytokines release [60]. This mechanism not only promotes renal inflammation but also accelerates fibrosis, further driving CKD progression [60]. Uric acid can enhance the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κВ) pathway, leading to the activation of downstream inflammatory genes and intensifying the inflammatory cascade [61]. Furthermore, uric acid crystals can directly induce renal tubular damage through inflammation mediated by physical mechanisms [17]. Monosodium urate crystals, upon binding to receptors on the surface of immune cells, are engulfed by these cells, activating inflammatory signaling pathways and further promoting immune cell activation and local inflammation [62]. Moreover, uric acid not only increases the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators in endothelial cells but also enhances the adhesion of monocytes, exacerbating vascular inflammation [63]. Studies demonstrate that hyperuricemia upregulates angiotensin II type 1 receptor expression in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), consequently triggering pro-inflammatory responses [64]. These series of changes lead to endothelial dysfunction, further impairing the microvascular function of the kidneys and providing conditions conducive to the development of CKD [64].

Yet, the specific mechanisms by which hyperuricemia leads to CKD and the exact work of inflammation remain incompletely understood and require further investigation. Our study offers clinicians insights into selecting treatment timing and targets for patients with concurrent hyperuricemia and CKD, from an inflammatory perspective. Considering our findings, the importance of inflammation in the relationship between CKD and hyperuricemia should be noted. Additionally, our findings provide a clear example for healthcare professionals to recognize the dangers of coexisting hyperuricemia and elevated inflammation levels on renal damage.

Our study has several strengths. First, it integrates various inflammatory biomarkers and their relationship with CKD, highlighting the impact of concurrent hyperuricemia and inflammation on CKD, which is crucial for future interventions and reducing disease burden in susceptible populations. Second, the study involved a nationally representative sample of considerable size, ensuring reliable results. Despite this, our study has several limitations. Firstly, as a cross-sectional study, these findings can only demonstrate associations rather than establish causal relationships between inflammatory biomarkers, hyperuricemia, and CKD. And the results of the mediation effects in this study represent preliminary theoretical explorations, providing hypotheses for future research, rather than confirmation of causal pathways. Future longitudinal studies should be conducted to investigate these causal relationships and how these factors influence the progression of CKD over time. Secondly, while we accounted for potential confounders that could affect model results, we acknowledge that other unmeasured confounders, such as autoimmune diseases, may still be present. Additionally, while these inflammatory biomarkers (SII, SIRI, NLR, MLR) are widely studied in other diseases and may be linked to CKD, their specificity and clinical application in CKD need further validation. Future research should focus on assessing their specificity and potential as clinical tools for CKD.

Conclusion

This study found the significant correlations between inflammatory biomarkers (SIRI, SII, MLR and NLR) and CKD. Among these biomarkers, SIRI demonstrated the most optimal clinical diagnostic value. Notably, MLR demonstrated a nonlinear relationship with CKD, showing significantly stronger positive correlations when MLR values exceeded 0.22. Furthermore, these inflammatory markers exhibited interaction effects with hyperuricemia in their associations with CKD. These findings suggested that inflammatory biomarkers may hold important clinical reference value for renal function assessment, particularly in patients with hyperuricemia.
