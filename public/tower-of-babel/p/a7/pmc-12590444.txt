Lipoic acid zwitterionic polymer engineered nanozymes ferried by neutrophil reprogramming ischemic microenvironment for stroke treatment
Authors: Mengcheng Guo, Haoyu Li, Qingran Guan, Lixue Zhang, Man Liu, Guanyu Qiao, Qingbiao Yang, Haochen Yao, Linlin Liu, Meili Shen, Yapeng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe accumulation of pathological reactive oxygen species (ROS) after reperfusion of ischemic stroke (IS) triggers the cascade of “oxidative stress-mitochondrial apoptosis-NLRP3 pyroptosis”, which aggravates neuronal death and neuroinflammation. In view of the limitations of traditional single-target therapies that are difficult to simultaneously block the vicious cycle of multiple pathways, a neutrophil-ridden zwitterionic engineered nanozyme (PLSP@CeO2) based on neutrophils was constructed. The system achieves efficient blood-brain barrier crossing through sialic acid/L-selectin-mediated targeted delivery, and executes a synergistic therapeutic mechanism: mimicking superoxide dismutase/catalase dual enzyme activity to scavenge ROS, simultaneously inhibiting mitochondrial apoptosis and NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, and driving microglia to polarize to the M2 anti-inflammatory phenotype to remodel the microenvironment. In vitro and in vivo experiments have confirmed that the system significantly reduces the volume of cerebral infarction, reduces neuronal death, and promotes angiogenesis and neurological repair. This study not only addresses key limitations of traditional nanozyme single-target therapy, but also establishes a multi-mechanism synergistic treatment paradigm based on endogenous immune cells, providing a new strategy for neurodegenerative diseases.Keywords: Nanoenzymes, Ischemic stroke, Neutrophil, Angiogenesis, Pyroptosis

Introduction

Ischemic stroke (IS) is a neurovascular disease with the highest disability and mortality rates globally. Its primary pathological mechanism is intricately linked to the cascade of injuries that occur following reperfusion [1]. When cerebral blood flow falls below the ischemic threshold (18 mL·100 g−1 min−1), neurons promptly experience energy metabolic failure, leading to irreversible necrosis [2]. Thrombolysis using clinical drugs like rt-PA or surgical thrombectomy can restore blood flow recanalization. However, during the initial phase of reperfusion, the abnormal opening of the permeability transition pore (mPTP) in neurons results in electron transport chain uncoupling. This, combined with the activity of NADPH oxidase (NOX2) in activated microglia, triggers spatiotemporal biphasic bursts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), ultimately causing irreversible oxidative stress injury [3]. Oxidative stress induces mitochondrial dysfunction, resulting in the collapse of the membrane potential and the subsequent release of pro-apoptotic factors like cytochrome C, which triggers the caspase-9/3 dependent apoptotic pathway. Additionally, ROS stimulate caspase-1-dependent cell rupture by activating the pyroptosis pathway through the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the release of a significant quantity of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL-18[[4], [5]]. These inflammatory factors create a positive feedback loop: they enhance ROS production by activating the NOX2/4 complex and exacerbate mitochondrial injury by causing mitochondrial DNA release. This establishes a detrimental cycle of oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell demise (pyroptosis/apoptosis) [1,6]. This secondary impact not only enlarges the scope of infarcted areas but also triggers the infiltration of peripheral immune cells and initiates neuroinflammatory cascades by inducing the pro-inflammatory state of microglia (M1 subtype) and compromising the integrity of the blood-brain barrier. Consequently, it exacerbates neuronal damage and impairment, influencing the effectiveness of rehabilitation and the prognosis of patients [7,8]. Therapeutic interventions, like antioxidants (e.g., edaravone) and caspase inhibitors, are commonly used in clinical settings to target pathways such as inflammation, pyroptosis, and apoptosis. However, the impact on neural repair outcomes is constrained [9]. Importantly, angiogenesis is recognized as a crucial factor in neural repair mechanisms [10,11]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a crucial regulatory factor with the ability to enhance vascular regeneration for improved perfusion in ischemic regions. Additionally, VEGF exhibits diverse neuroprotective effects. It directly influences neurons and glial cells, fostering neuronal proliferation, survival, and synaptic remodeling. Furthermore, VEGF stimulates Schwann cell extension and proliferation, guides astrocyte differentiation, and activates microglia, thereby promoting neural tissue regeneration and repair [[11], [12], [13]].

The pathological mechanism of ischemic stroke is characterized by multiple targets, including oxidative stress injury, inflammatory cascade reaction, and angiogenesis disorder. Functional nanoparticles have emerged as a significant strategy for overcoming the blood-brain barrier and providing neuroprotection due to their targeted delivery and ability to clear reactive oxygen species [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Cerium-based nanomaterials (CeNPs) exhibit distinctive REDOX cycle properties through the dynamic transformation between Ce3+ and Ce4+, providing them with superoxide dismutase-like (SOD) and catalase-like (CAT) activities. SOD facilitates the conversion of superoxide anions to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Additionally, the CAT-like activity decomposes H2O2 into oxygen (O2), triggering an antioxidant cascade reaction that effectively mitigates oxidative damage in the ischemic brain region [18]. The pathological mechanism of ischemic stroke is intricate, making it challenging to address comprehensively with a single antioxidant strategy. Protocatechualdehyde (PCA), a metabolite of salvianolic acid B, enhances the brain microenvironment through various targets: it eliminates ROS, interrupts the inflammatory and oxidative stress cycle, prevents neuronal apoptosis, and activates the VEGF pathway to stimulate angiogenesis. The concurrent use of CeNPs and PCA may produce a synergistic effect: CeNPs swiftly reduce oxidative stress levels by clearing ROS, while PCA repairs the compromised microenvironment via anti-inflammatory and angiogenic mechanisms. This combination is anticipated to surpass the constraints of singular therapies and offer a multi-faceted neuroprotective approach for ischemic stroke [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]].

Effectively delivering nanomedicines to damaged brain tissue in the treatment of ischemic stroke poses a significant challenge due to the presence of the blood-brain barrier (BBB). The BBB serves the dual purpose of safeguarding the brain from external agents while also impeding drug penetration [26,27]. Ischemia-reperfusion injury has been found to induce the secretion of inflammatory factors that attract immune cells from peripheral blood. Neutrophils, being the most abundant white blood cells in peripheral blood, are the predominant cells that migrate to the ischemic region of the brain following an ischemic stroke. L-selectin, a receptor highly expressed on neutrophils’ surface, plays a crucial role in this process. Sialic acid (SA) serves as the specific ligand for L-selectin, facilitating effective binding. Nanoparticles modified with SA can selectively adhere to neutrophils post intravenous administration and effectively traverse the BBB by transporting neutrophils into the injured brain parenchyma [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]].

In response to the aforementioned pathological mechanism, this study developed a delivery system utilizing cerium oxide nanomaterials (PLSP@CeO2) to hitch a ride on neutrophils, effectively suppressing inflammation, enhancing angiogenesis, and providing neuroprotective effects (Scheme 1). The system design presents several advantages. Firstly, the incorporation of a zwitterionic polymer coating with lipoic acid significantly improves the blood stability and cellular uptake efficiency of nanoparticles, reduces immune clearance, and optimizes bioavailability. Secondly, the modification with sialic acid enables targeted binding to neutrophils, overcoming the blood-brain barrier and facilitating precise delivery to ischaemic lesions. Additionally, the enzyme-like activity of PLSP@CeO2 (mimicking SOD and CAT) and the synergistic effect of the nanocarrier inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation by scavenging excess ROS, suppressing caspase-1-mediated cell pyroptosis, and reducing the release of pro-inflammatory factors. This ROS clearance also speeds up the restoration of the mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby reversing the progression of apoptosis, limiting neuronal damage, and disrupting the cycle of pyroptosis and apoptosis. Expanding on this groundwork, PLSP@CeO2 facilitates the transition of microglia into the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, enhancing the ischemic microenvironment, promoting neoangiogenesis, speeding up neuronal recovery, and achieving synergistic therapeutic effects. This four-link synergistic mechanism integrates antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, and repair-promoting effects. It effectively mitigates the vicious cycle triggered by ROS and provides a novel therapeutic approach for ischemic stroke.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of PLSP@CeO2

The zwitterionic polymer lipoic acid-based zwitterionic polymer (PLA) in this study was synthesized via ring-opening polymerization (ROP) of sulfo-betaine-functionalized lipoic acid derivative (LA-SB) and hydroxyl-functionalized lipoic acid derivative (LA-OH). The polymerization was conducted in a methanol/water mixed solvent at room temperature, employing thiol compounds as initiators. Chain propagation was mediated by disulfide exchange-induced ring-opening, followed by termination with iodoacetamide [[33], [34], [35], [36]]. UV absorption spectroscopy revealed a characteristic absorption peak at 251 nm corresponding to polydisulfide bonds (Fig. S1a–b). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirmed the presence of all expected functional groups: a broad absorption band at 3600 cm−1 was attributed to O–H stretching vibrations; peaks at 2935 cm−1 and 2846 cm−1 corresponded to asymmetric and symmetric C–H vibrations, respectively; C=O stretching of amide groups and N–H bending were observed at 1664 cm−1 and 1548 cm−1, respectively; sulfonate S=O stretching of organic sulfates appeared at 1210 cm−1; C–N absorption of quaternary ammonium and C–O stretching of primary alcohols were detected at 1037 cm−1 and 1017 cm−1, respectively; while C–S and S–S stretching vibrations of the polydisulfide backbone emerged at 725 cm−1 and 525 cm−1. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis indicated a weight-average molecular weight (Mw) of 8461 Da with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 1.84, reflecting moderate molecular weight distribution consistent with theoretical expectations. Additionally, the polymer structure was further validated by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S17). In vitro zeta potential measurements revealed a notable potential reversal phenomenon of PLA when dispersed in various buffer systems (pH 6.0, −7.7 mV; pH 7.4, 4.9 mV) (Fig. 2a). This characteristic establishes a physicochemical foundation for enabling profound tissue penetration [42,43]. To create a multifunctional nanozyme system, PLA, PCA, SA and Ce(NO3)3 were self-assembled at 60 °C and induced to form PLSP@CeO2 nanozyme complexes by precisely controlling the rate of NH3·H2O droplet acceleration. After dialysis purification and freeze-drying, brown-black PLSP@CeO2 nanozyme powder was obtained. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that the original CeO2 nanoparticles (NPs) were monodisperse with an average size of 9.7 nm (Fig. 1b, S2a). The particle size of PLSP@CeO2 significantly increased to 138.6 ± 28.6 nm after combining PLA, SA, and PCA (Fig. 1c–S2b), confirming efficient loading of each component. The characteristic absorption peaks in the UV–Vis spectrum between 220 and 800 nm further confirmed the successful combination of each component (Fig. 1d). FTIR provides clear vibrational evidence for the effective loading of each component in the composite material. In the FTIR spectrum of the PLSP@CeO2 complex (Fig. S3a), the broad absorption peak near 600 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of the Ce–O bond, confirming the presence of CeO2. The absorption peak at 1037 cm−1 corresponds to the C–N stretching vibration of the quaternary ammonium salt group in PLA, while the peak at 725 cm−1 originates from the C–S stretching vibration of polydisulfide bonds in PLA's main chain. This confirms the successful incorporation of PLA. Multiple absorption peaks in the range of 1600-1450 cm−1 are assigned to skeletal vibrations of benzene rings in PCA, indicating effective loading of PCA. Finally, a distinct and sharp absorption peak at 1400 cm−1 can be attributed to symmetrical stretching vibrations of carboxylate groups formed by complexation between SA's carboxyl group and Ce3+/Ce4+ ions. This clearly demonstrates that SA has been successfully anchored within the composite structure through carboxyl coordination. X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectra indicate that PLSP@CeO2 demonstrates a characteristic amorphous structure (Fig. 1e). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis disclosed a Ce3+ to Ce4+ valence-state ratio of 1:1.5 in the material (Fig. 1f). This unique valence distribution confers exceptional redox activity upon nanozymes, establishing a structural foundation for their outstanding antioxidant properties. Notably, PLSP@CeO2 exhibited remarkable dispersion stability in various solutions, including normal saline (0.9 % NaCl), phosphate buffered saline (PBS), serum (FBS), and DMEM medium, with no apparent aggregation observed within 72 h (Fig. 1g). To further assess its stability in pathologically relevant environments, a 7-day stability test was carried out under acidic conditions with elevated protein concentration (PBS, pH 6.5, 10 % FBS), mimicking the microenvironment of the ischemic penumbra. As shown in Fig. S3b, the hydrodynamic diameter of the nanoparticles increased only slightly from 138.6 nm to 150.8 nm, and the PDI remained consistently below 0.25, indicating superior colloidal stability. This remarkable stability can be attributed to the robust hydration layer formed by the PLA coating, which effectively suppresses protein adsorption and particle aggregation, thereby highlighting its strong potential for clinical applications.

PLSP@CeO2in vitro reactive oxygen species reactive oxygen species removal

An imbalance between the generation and scavenging of reactive ROS, including hydroxyl radicals (•OH), superoxide anion (O2•-), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), exacerbates ischemic injury [[40], [41]]. To systematically evaluate the antioxidative properties of PLSP@CeO2, this study focused on these three representative ROS. Experimental data demonstrated concentration-dependent scavenging efficiency of PLSP@CeO2. At 95 μg/mL, PLSP@CeO2 decomposed approximately 85.4 % of H2O2, with dissolved oxygen analyzer tests confirming its efficient catalytic decomposition of H2O2 into O2, indicative of prominent CAT-like enzymatic activity (Fig. 2a and b). Similarly, 200 μg/mL PLSP@CeO2 eliminated approximately 83.3 % of O2•- (Fig. 2c), demonstrating SOD-like activity. PLSP@CeO2 at 160 μg/mL achieved over 70.4 % •OH scavenging efficiency (Fig. 2d), corroborated by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy showing significantly diminished DMPO-OH adduct signals compared to CeO2 at equivalent concentrations (Fig. 2e), thus confirming the enhanced •OH scavenging capacity through PCA- CeO2 synergy. Furthermore, PLSP@CeO2 at merely 4 μg/mL scavenged over 70.3 % of DPPH• radicals (Fig. 2f), with comprehensive antioxidant activity significantly surpassing that of SA-PCA, CeO2, and PLA at matched concentrations (Fig. 2g–j). The antioxidative superiority of PLSP@CeO2 originates from its integrated design enabling synergistic effects: the CeO2 core (Ce3+/Ce4+) provides cascade SOD-like and CAT-like enzymatic activities, and incorporated PCA substantially enhances •OH radical scavenging, collectively establishing a “pan-ROS scavenging network.” In contrast, standalone CeO2 exhibits limited •OH elimination capacity, and SA-PCA lacks sustainable enzymatic catalytic functionality. These results systematically elucidate the multi-enzyme-mimetic catalytic properties of PLSP@CeO2 underlining its potential as a highly efficient broad-spectrum antioxidant.

PLSP@CeO2 protects cells from oxidative stress

The PC12 cell line, a well-established neuroendocrine model derived from rat adrenal medulla pheochromocytoma, holds considerable utility in investigating the mechanisms of neurodegenerative diseases and in the development of neuroprotective agents, given its capacity for neurotransmitter synthesis and secretion [[37], [38], [39]]. In this study, we utilized an H2O2-induced oxidative stress model to simulate stroke-induced cellular damage. First, the biocompatibility of PLSP@CeO2 was assessed via MTT assay (Fig. 3a). The results indicated that after 24 h of co-incubation with PC12 cells, PLSP@CeO2 exhibited no significant cytotoxicity even at a concentration of 200 μM, confirming its suitability for subsequent experiments. Confocal microscopy further demonstrated that Nile Red-labeled PLSP@CeO2 nanoparticles were efficiently internalized by PC12 cells in a time- and concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3b–S4), establishing a basis for investigating intracellular antioxidant effects. Upon establishing a 600 μM H2O2-induced oxidative stress model, this concentration markedly inhibited PC12 cell proliferation, reducing the cell viability to approximately 35 % (Fig. 3c). Notably, CeO2, SA-PCA, and PLSP@CeO2 all demonstrated potent antioxidative capacity against this damage. Specifically, PLSP@CeO2, through the synergistic antioxidant interaction between its components, restored cell viability to the normal range, surpassing the effects of CeO2 and SA-PCA (Fig. 3d). Intracellular ROS levels were quantitatively assessed using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. Collectively, quantitative assessments through cellular fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry (Fig. 3e–g) revealed that PLSP@CeO2 treatment reduced the proportion of ROS-positive cells by 83.9 % compared to the H2O2-treated group; its efficacy was significantly superior to that of CeO2 and SA-PCA treatments. These findings were conclusively validated by biochemical analyses, confirming the enhanced ROS-scavenging efficiency of PLSP@CeO2. Crucially, this demonstrates the superior antioxidant synergy achieved through the PCA-CeO2 coordination mechanism.

PLSP@CeO2 regulates mitochondrial function and reverses the process of apoptosis

Mitochondria, serving as the cellular energy factories and primary sources of ROS, exhibit functional disruption as a central mechanism in oxidative stress-induced apoptosis [[43], [44], [45]]. To evaluate the mitochondrial protective effects of PLSP@CeO2, we utilized the JC-1 fluorescent probe to monitor alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). Experimental results (Fig. 4a and b) revealed that H2O2 stimulation caused a marked reduction in ΔΨm, indicated by a 92.2 % decline in the red-to-green fluorescence ratio. Following PLSP@CeO2 treatment, the fluorescence ratio recovered to 85.2 % of the control level, significantly surpassing the values observed in PLA (17.1 %), CeO2 (22.4 %), and SA-PCA (60.2 %) control groups. These data demonstrates that the nanocomposite effectively preserves mitochondrial functional integrity. Further mechanistic investigations established that mitochondrial dysfunction drives programmed cell death via the apoptotic pathway: ΔΨm depolarization triggers mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), facilitating cytochrome c release and activation of the caspase-3 apoptotic cascade. Quantitative flow cytometric analysis (Fig. 4c and d) showed that H2O2 stimulation elevated the apoptosis rate in PC12 cells to 22.9 %, whereas PLSP@CeO2 pretreatment reduced this rate to 6.8 %. This protective trend was visually corroborated by Calcein-AM/PI double-staining assays, with the PLSP@CeO2-treated group exhibiting a substantially higher proportion of viable cells (Fig. 4e). To delineate the molecular mechanisms, Western blot (WB) analysis of key apoptotic pathway proteins was conducted. As illustrated in Fig. S5, PLSP@CeO2 significantly suppressed H2O2-induced cytochrome c release from mitochondria and caspase-3 activation, outperforming the CeO2 group. Collectively, these findings substantiate that PLSP@CeO2 confers robust mitochondrial protection through PCA-CeO2 synergy, thereby effectively inhibiting apoptotic pathway activation.

PLSP@CeO2 reduces pyroptosis and inhibits inflammation

Sustained ΔΨm disintegration activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the release of its N-terminal active fragment (GSDMD-N) via caspase-1 mediated cleavage of the GSDMD protein. Upon oligomerization, this fragment can create pores in the cell membrane, resulting in the release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, along with the discharge of cytoplasmic enzymes like lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), which is a significant biochemical characteristic of cell pyroptosis. To elucidate the specific mechanism through which PLSP@CeO2 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, this study first investigated the key upstream signaling events associated with NLRP3 activation. Potassium ion efflux, recognized as a core initiating signal for NLRP3 inflammasome activation, was markedly elevated in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated PC12 cells. Following treatment with PLSP@CeO2, the extracellular K+ concentration was significantly normalized, thereby directly suppressing this upstream activation signal (Fig. S6a). Additionally, the downstream inflammatory mediator ATP was found to be substantially released upon LPS stimulation; however, PLSP@CeO2 effectively reduced extracellular ATP levels, thereby inhibiting the amplification of inflammatory signals. To further determine the impact of the aforementioned upstream regulatory effects on NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, Western blot analysis was performed (Fig. S6b). Following LPS stimulation, significant upregulation of NLRP3 and ASC protein expression along with increased levels of GSDMD-N, were observed. In contrast, treatment with PLSP@CeO2 led to a marked reduction in all these molecular markers, compared to those in SA-PCA monotherapy group. These findings provide molecular evidence supporting the regulatory role of PLSP@CeO2 in modulating NLRP3 inflammasome activation and key mediators of pyroptosis. Collectively, the results from both upstream signaling and molecular analyses demonstrate that PLSP@CeO2 specifically suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the subsequent pyroptotic process by modulating “potassium efflux–ATP release–NLRP3/ASC activation” signaling axis, rather than acting through non-specific mechanisms. These findings further elucidate the molecular basis of its anti-inflammatory activity. To evaluate the anti-pyroptosis capability of PLSP@CeO2, we utilized immunofluorescence staining to measure caspase-1 levels, as depicted in Fig. 5a and b. The treatment with PLSP@CeO2 notably suppressed caspase-1 fluorescence compared to the LPS-stimulated group. Furthermore, we assessed LDH activity and the secretion of inflammatory factors in the cell culture supernatant, as illustrated in Fig. 5c. The PLSP@CeO2 treatment group exhibited a 65.3 % reduction in LDH release compared to the LPS group, while the levels of IL-1β (Fig. 5d) and IL-18 (Fig. 5e) decreased by 65.3 % and 71.1 %, respectively, aligning closely with the observed caspase-1 inhibition outcomes. These findings indicate that PLSP@CeO2 effectively impedes oxidative stress-induced pyroptosis by interrupting the caspase-1-GSDMD pathway. This significant advancement validates that PLSP@CeO2 efficiently scavenges ROS cascade by emulating the catalytic actions of SOD and CAT, thereby obstructing the oxidative stress-mediated mitochondrial damage-apoptosis/pyroptosis signaling pathway. This multi-enzyme synergistic catalytic system elucidates the molecular mechanism of PLSP@CeO2 neuroprotection and establishes a crucial theoretical foundation for the advancement of stroke treatment drugs utilizing bionic nanoenzymes.

PLSP@CeO2 take neutrophil-specific targeting of ischemic lesion sites

Neutrophils are among the initial immune cells that migrate to ischemic regions following an ischemic stroke, making them crucial for nano-delivery system research [46,47]. In order to evaluate the neutrophil-mediated transport capability of the bionic nanoenzyme PLSP@CeO2, neutrophils were co-cultured with Nile red-labeled nanoparticles and examined using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The PLSP@CeO2 group exhibited a more intense red fluorescence compared to the CeO2 group, with a 1.4-fold increase in intensity (Fig. 6a and b), suggesting that the surface modification with SA enhanced the specific endocytosis by neutrophils.

To assess the specific targeting efficacy of bionic nanoenzymes in ischemic brain regions, a middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) model was induced in Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats by embolizing their middle cerebral artery. Following 1.5 h of ischemia, blood flow was restored, and intravenous injections of indocyanine green (ICG) and either labeled CeO2 or PLSP@CeO2 were administered. Brain tissue samples were collected and examined after 22.5 h of treatment. In vivo imaging (IVIS) results (Fig. 6c and d) demonstrated that PLSP@CeO2 exhibited 4.3-fold and 2.1-fold greater accumulation in the ischemic area compared to ICG and CeO2, respectively. The biodistribution of PLSP@CeO2 in major organs was further investigated. As shown in Fig. S7a, the accumulation of PLSP@CeO2 in the liver was comparable to that of the unmodified CeO2 group, with no significant difference observed. Importantly, no notable uptake was detected in other non-target organs, including the heart, lungs, and spleen [48]. These results indicate that the neutrophil-mediated delivery strategy enhances brain-targeting efficiency without causing unintended accumulation in off-target organs, demonstrating excellent tissue selectivity and a favorable in vivo safety profile. Additionally, the co-localization of PLSP@CeO2 and neutrophils was examined using immunofluorescence staining. MCAO rats received intravenous injections of FITC and PLSP@CeO2 for 22.5 h post-reperfusion following 1.5 h of ischemia. Frozen brain tissue sections were stained with CD11b to identify neutrophils. Notably, Fig. 6f illustrates a significant overlap of green fluorescence from the nanoenzyme with red fluorescence from CD11b in the PLSP@CeO2 group, a feature absent in the FITC and CeO2 groups. These findings strongly support the notion that PLSP@CeO2 primarily targets ischemic regions through neutrophils, thereby enhancing nanoenzyme accumulation in these areas.

Validation of PLSP@CeO2's in vivo therapeutic efficacy and biosafety

To further validate the neuroprotective potential of PLSP@CeO2 in an in vivo ischemic stroke model, an MCAO model was induced to mimic clinical right cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury (Fig. 7a). Following 1.5 h of ischemia, the MCAO model received varying drug doses via tail vein injection and was reperfused for 24 h. Subsequently, brain tissues were harvested for analysis. 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) was utilized to differentiate between healthy (red) and infarcted (white) regions in the rat brain. As depicted in Fig. 7b and c, brain sections in the control group exhibited red coloring, while white infarct areas were observed in the MCAO model group. Conversely, the treatment group displayed reduced white infarct areas compared to the MCAO model group, with the PLSP@CeO2 group demonstrating the smallest infarct volume percentage, indicating superior therapeutic efficacy. When treated with different doses of PLSP@CeO2, at the optimal concentration of 20 mg/kg, the infarct volume was notably decreased (Fig. S7b–c). Then, we compared this dose group with other treatment groups. It turned out that the infarct areas of all treatment groups were smaller than those of the MCAO model group. Among them, the PLSP@CeO2 group had the smallest infarct volume, which shows that PLSP@CeO2 has the best therapeutic effect. Additionally, H&E staining (Fig. 7d) revealed notable brain cell atrophy in the ischemic hemisphere of untreated MCAO rats, characterized by decreased cell volume and nuclear pyknosis. In contrast, the cell morphology in each treatment group showed improvement, with the PLSP@CeO2 group closely resembling the normal control group, underscoring its exceptional neuroprotective properties. The alterations in neuron morphology and the number of Nissl vesicles post ischemic stroke serve as crucial indicators for assessing the extent of brain damage. Nissl bodies within neurons of the cortex and hippocampal CA region were identified through Nissl staining to evaluate neuronal functionality following ischemia-reperfusion injury induced by middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) in rats. In the control group (Fig. 7e), neurons within the cortex and hippocampal CA region exhibited well-defined morphology, with distinct dark blue Nissl bodies prominently present in the cytoplasm. Post MCAO, neurons in the cortex and hippocampal CA region displayed wrinkling and deformation, with damaged neurons exhibiting a lack of selective staining indicative of cell membrane rupture. Treatment with CeO2 and SA-PCA led to partial recovery of neurons in the cortex and hippocampal CA region, while the PLSP@CeO2 group demonstrated preservation of a majority of neurons in these areas. Furthermore, histological examination using H&E staining of major organs confirmed that the PLSP@CeO2 treatment group did not exhibit liver or kidney dysfunction or histopathological alterations (Fig. S8). Pharmacokinetic analysis demonstrated that PLSP@CeO2 exhibited markedly enhanced pharmacokinetic profiles compared to unmodified CeO2 nanoparticles (Fig. S9a, Table 2). Specifically, the maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) of PLSP@CeO2 reached 70.2 ± 5.8 mg/L, which was 9-fold higher than that of unmodified CeO2. The elimination half-life (t1/2) was extended to 15.2 ± 1.8 h (a 3.4-fold increase), and the area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC0-∞) reached 1125.8 ± 96.5 mg h/L, representing a 15.5-fold improvement. These favorable pharmacokinetic parameters suggest that surface modification effectively prolonged the circulation time of the nanoparticles, thereby providing an adequate temporal window for their targeted delivery to ischemic lesions via neutrophil-mediated “hitchhiking”. Biodistribution analysis indicated that PLSP@CeO2 was predominantly cleared via the liver and kidneys (Fig. S9b), supporting its normal metabolic pathway and good biocompatibility, with no significant accumulation in non-target peripheral organs. Importantly, a 14-day extended toxicity study revealed no evidence of systemic toxicity at therapeutic doses of PLSP@CeO2. All serum biochemical parameters remained within normal reference ranges and showed no statistically significant differences compared to the control group. Histopathological examination of H&E-stained tissue sections from major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) revealed no pathological alterations associated with the treatment (Fig. S10). Collectively, these new data provide a thorough assessment of the in vivo fate and safety of PLSP@CeO2, confirming not only its robust therapeutic efficacy but also its favorable pharmacokinetics and long-term biocompatibility, thereby supporting its potential for future clinical translation.

PLSP@CeO2 reversal of glial cell phenotype to remodel the ischemic inflammatory microenvironment

There is increasing evidence to suggest that inflammation and immune responses following a stroke can exacerbate neuronal damage and hinder subsequent repair to various extents during brain tissue injury [49]. ROS-induced oxidative stress is the primary causative factor in the pathological cascade of injury [50]. Hence, we employed the DHE fluorescent probe to assess ROS levels following cerebral ischemia in rats, where higher ROS levels were indicated by stronger red fluorescence (Fig. 8a). ROS levels were notably elevated in the brains of rats post-MCAO/R surgery, whereas treatment with nanoenzyme-based CeO2 and PLSP@CeO2 significantly decreased ROS levels in the brain. Microglia and astrocytes, being the primary immune cells in the brain, play a role in representing the inflammatory response in the brain. By altering the microglia phenotype (from the pro-inflammatory M1 type to the anti-inflammatory M2 type) and inducing astrocyte activation, it is possible to effectively suppress brain inflammation, which has emerged as a crucial therapeutic target for enhancing the inflammatory microenvironment in ischemic stroke.

In this study, we assessed the expression levels of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, an activated astrocyte marker), ionized calcium-binding linker molecule 1 (Iba-1, a microglia marker), CD86 (an M1 microglia marker), and CD206 (an M2 microglia marker) in brain sections from rats with MCAO using immunohistochemical staining (Fig. 8b, c, S11a). We observed elevated levels of Iba-1 and GFAP expression in brain sections of MCAO rats treated with saline alone compared to controls, indicating immune activation of resting microglia and astrocytes in the MCAO model. Following treatment with various drugs for 24 h, the number of Iba-1 positive cells decreased, with PLSP@CeO2 demonstrating the most potent inhibition of microglia activation. Additionally, PLSP@CeO2 treatment reduced the expression of the M1 microglia marker CD86 to 8.2 % (Fig. 8d and e) and increased the expression of the M2 microglia marker CD206 by 8.1-fold compared to control (Fig. S11a–c). We further evaluated the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6 associated with M1 markers, as well as anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β associated with M2 markers, in the infarct sites of MCAO rats using ELISA. Consistent with the immunofluorescence findings, MCAO significantly upregulated the secretion of proinflammatory factors in brain tissue (Fig. S11d–e). After 24 h of treatment, the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in all groups generally decreased, while the levels of anti-inflammatory factors exhibited an increasing trend (Fig. S11f–g). In conclusion, PLSP@CeO2 enhances therapeutic efficacy by scavenging ROS in the rat brain, suppressing astrocyte activation, promoting microglial polarization toward the M2 phenotype, and reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. These mechanisms collectively remodel the ischemic microenvironment, thereby maximizing the neuroprotective effects of PLSP@CeO2.

PLSP@CeO2 reverses pyroptosis/apoptosis in ischemic stroke

During ischemic stroke, a multimodal cell death mechanism involves NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis and the mitochondria-dependent apoptosis pathway, which together create a cascade-amplified pathway [[51], [52], [53]]. Ischemia and hypoxia in brain tissue not only activate NLRP3 inflammasomes due to energy metabolism disorders, triggering the ASC/caspase-1 signaling cascade and promoting the release of proinflammatory factors like IL-1β and TNF-α, but also induce mitochondrial apoptosis through the cytochrome C/caspase-9 pathway [[54], [55], [56]]. This interaction between pyroptosis and apoptosis establishes a detrimental cycle, resulting in the continual release of inflammatory factors, neuronal death, and the expansion of the infarction area. To address the multi-target intervention characteristics of PLSP@CeO2, the expression of NLRP3 and pyroptosis-related proteins, including ASC and caspase-1, was assessed through immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 9a–d). In the MCAO model group, NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1 levels were significantly elevated compared to the control group, indicating activation of the NLRP3-dependent neuroinflammatory pathway due to ischemic injury. PLSP@CeO2, as a combination of PLA, CeO2, and SA-PCA, effectively suppressed the expression of key proteins in this pathway, demonstrating the successful inhibition of cell pyroptosis by PLSP@CeO2.

NeuN/TUNEL staining was utilized to evaluate the inhibitory effect of PLSP@CeO2 on neuronal apoptosis in the hippocampal CA region, which plays a crucial role in sensory, motor, and cognitive memory functions in the cerebral cortex. As shown in Fig. 10a–d, the control group exhibited healthy neurons in the cerebral cortex, while the MCAO model group showed a significant increase in TUNEL-positive neurons, indicating apoptosis in 51.5 % of neurons. Following PLA treatment, the percentage of apoptotic neurons decreased to 33.1 %, slightly lower than the MCAO group. Importantly, neuronal apoptosis was notably reduced in the SA-PCA and PLSP@CeO2 treatment groups, with the latter demonstrating substantial neuronal protection and decreased damage in the cerebral cortex. The findings in the hippocampal CA region were consistent with those in the cerebral cortex, where neuronal apoptosis was prominent in the MCAO group, with TUNEL-positive neurons accounting for 57.1 %. Following treatment with PLA and CeO2, apoptosis rates were reduced to 31.1 % and 29.8 %, respectively, thus preserving some hippocampal neurons. Moreover, a significant inhibition of apoptosis was observed in 80.2 % and 90.1 % of neurons in the hippocampal CA region in the SA-PCA and PLSP@CeO2 treatment groups, respectively (Fig. 10e and f). These results indicate that PLSP@CeO2 effectively suppresses neuron apoptosis in the MCAO rat model. The treatment with PLSP@CeO2 notably decreased apoptosis in the cortex and hippocampal CA region, surpassing the individual effects of PLA, CeO2, and SA-PCA treatments, which aligns with the outcomes of experiments on H2O2-induced PC12 cell apoptosis. This study demonstrates that PLSP@CeO2 provides neuroprotection through dual mechanisms: by preventing NLRP3 inflammasome activation through ROS scavenging and by inhibiting caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis and inflammatory responses, while also modulating apoptotic pathways to reduce TUNEL-positive neurons. This combined inhibition of focal and apoptotic pathways disrupts the cascade amplification of ischemic brain injury, laying the groundwork for the development of multi-target neuroprotection strategies.

PLSP@CeO2 accelerates angiogenesis to break through the bottleneck of single-mechanism therapy

While traditional anti-inflammatory and antioxidant methods can slow the progression of acute injuries, achieving effective nerve remodeling remains a challenge. The reconstruction of neurovascular units is crucial for functional recovery following ischemic stroke, requiring the coordinated promotion of angiogenesis and neuroprotection [[57], [58], [59], [60]]. To investigate this further, we evaluated the influence of biomimetic nanozymes on brain endothelial cell migration and vascularization in vitro. Fig. 11a–c illustrate that PLSP@CeO2 nanozymes, following PCA functionalization, notably enhance endothelial cell migration and tube formation. For in vivo evaluation, we employed a rat MCAO/R model and administered PLA, CeO2, SA-PCA, and PLSP@CeO2 intravenously for seven consecutive days. Subsequently, brain tissues were stained with CD31 for immunofluorescence to identify neovascular endothelial cells (Fig. 11d and e). In comparison to the MCAO group, the PLA and CeO2 groups did not exhibit a significant increase in cerebral microvessel density. In contrast, noteworthy increases were observed in the SA-PCA and PLSP@CeO2 groups, with PLSP@CeO2 displaying superior effects. This suggests that PCA interacts synergistically with cerium oxide, enabling PLSP@CeO2 to enhance cerebral microcirculatory perfusion by stimulating intravascular regeneration, thereby creating a conducive environment for nerve repair. This finding not only highlights the distinct advantages of biomimetic nanozymes in the reconstruction of neurovascular units but also offers innovative insights for developing comprehensive stroke treatment strategies.

Although traditional anti-inflammatory/antioxidant strategies can delay the progression of acute-phase injury, they are difficult to achieve functional neural remodeling. The reconstruction of the neurovascular unit is the cornerstone of functional recovery after ischemic stroke, and the key lies in the coordinated promotion of angiogenesis and neuroprotection [[53], [54], [55], [56]]. For this reason, we explored the effects of biomimetic nanozymes on the migration and angiogenesis abilities of brain endothelial cells through in vitro cell experiments. As shown in Fig. 11a–c, it can be clearly observed that after functionalization modification with PCA, PLSP@CeO2 nanozymes accelerate the migration and tube formation of endothelial cells. To verify the in vivo efficacy, we established a rat MCAO/R model and intravenously administered PLA, CeO2, SA-PCA, or PLSP@CeO2 once daily for 7 days. After the treatment period, Western blot analysis demonstrated that PLSP@CeO2 significantly upregulated VEGF expression in ischemic brain tissue, with a more pronounced effect than that of SA-PCA (Fig. S12). In addition, immunofluorescence staining of brain sections using an antibody against CD31—a marker of newly formed brain microvascular endothelial cells—was performed to assess microvessel density (Fig. 11d and e). Compared with the MCAO group, no significant increase in microvessel density was observed in the PLA and CeO2 groups. In contrast, both SA-PCA and PLSP@CeO2 treatments led to a significant increase in microvessel density, with the PLSP@CeO2 group showing the most substantial improvement. These results indicate that the pro-angiogenic benefits of PLSP@CeO2 originate from the synergistic action of CeO2 and PCA: the CeO2 component effectively scavenges ROS and improves the angiogenic microenvironment, while the PCA component directly activates the VEGF pathway and promotes angiogenesis. Together, these mechanisms significantly enhance cerebral microcirculation perfusion and create a favorable environment for neural repair.

Therapeutic effect of PLSP@CeO2on MCAO in vivo

Neurological examinations were subsequently conducted to assess neurological prognosis in each rat group. The model group exhibited the most severe neurological deficits, whereas rats treated with PLSP@CeO2 showed significantly improved neurological scores. These findings suggest that PLSP@CeO2 may alleviate neurological impairments in rats subjected to MCAO. As behavioral tests can reflect neurological functions including sensorimotor and cognitive performance, several representative behavioral assessments were performed within 28 days post-stroke, including the rotarod test, cylinder test, adhesive removal test, and Morris water maze test. In the rotarod test, the average latency to fall of stroke rats treated with PLSP@CeO2 was significantly longer than those in the PLA, SA-PCA, and CeO2 treatment groups, approaching control group levels (Fig. 12a and b). In the cylinder test, MCAO model rats displayed the highest asymmetry rate, while the PLSP@CeO2 treatment group showed a significantly reduced asymmetry rate comparable to controls (Fig. 12c and d). Additionally, the adhesive removal test demonstrated similar time durations for tape contact and removal between the PLSP@CeO2 treatment group and controls (Fig. 12e–g). These behavioral findings collectively indicate that PLSP@CeO2 treatment post-stroke may promote long-term recovery of sensorimotor function. Finally, the Morris water maze test was conducted to evaluate therapeutic effects on learning and spatial memory. Fig. 12i displays representative swimming trajectories across groups, showing that PLSP@CeO2-treated rats exhibited target quadrant localization ability equivalent to controls. Quantitative analyses of escape latency and swimming path length during the hidden platform phase revealed significantly impaired performance in the model group compared to the PLSP@CeO2 group, which demonstrated control-comparable results (Fig. 12j–m). These data suggest that PLSP@CeO2 treatment may restore spatial memory deficits in MCAO rats. In conclusion, PLSP@CeO2 administration effectively mitigates both motor dysfunction and cognitive impairments in the MCAO rat stroke model.

Conclusions

This study developed a PLSP@CeO2 nano-delivery system that utilizes neutrophils as carriers to enhance nanocarrier biocompatibility. This was achieved by modifying the lipoic acid polymer surface and incorporating sialic acid to target neutrophils via L-selectin, exploiting their lesional chemotaxis properties for active drug delivery across the blood-brain barrier. This approach mitigates key limitations of conventional delivery methods. The system establishes a comprehensive therapeutic network encompassing antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, and pro-repair effects. Experimental findings demonstrate that PLSP@CeO2 effectively scavenges reactive oxygen species, inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and caspase-1-mediated pyroptosis, and promotes an anti-inflammatory microenvironment by inducing microglial M2 polarization. These results in a significant 42.1 % reduction in cerebral infarction volume and sustained 12-h treatment efficacy in animal models. The study introduces a novel approach of multi-pathway collaborative intervention for ischemic stroke treatment, offering promising prospects for immune microenvironment modulation and precise treatment of neurodegenerative diseases.

Experimental section/methods

Materials: Cerium(III) nitrate hexahydrate, lipoic acid, N,N′-disuccinimidyl carbonate, 2-aminoethanol, 3-Dimethylaminopropylamine, 1,3-propanesultone, 2-aminoethanethiol, iodoacetamide, sialic acid 4′,6-diamino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI), 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC), Protocatechualdehyde (PCA), 2,2′-azinobis(3-ethylbenzothiazole-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), DCFH-DA fluorescent probe, and JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential detection probe were all purchased from Adamas (China). Rat nylon thread (A5-283850) was purchased from Xinong Technology Co., Ltd. Annexin V/FITC apoptosis detection kit was purchased from Beyotime (China). Rat IL-6 ELISA kit, rat TNF-α ELISA kit, rat IL-10 ELISA kit, and TGF-β ELISA kit were purchased from Seville (China). CD86 antibody, CD206 antibody, Iba-1 antibody, and caspase-1 antibody were purchased from Servicebio (Wuhan, China), while others were ordered from Abcam (USA).

Characterization: Comprehensive characterization was performed through various microscopic and spectral analyses. Specifically, a Malvern particle size analyzer was used to determine particle size distribution and zeta potential, while morphology and elemental composition were assessed using TEM (JEOL JEM-F200, 100 kV, Japan), high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) (JEOL 2010), and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). Structural information and surface valence distribution of CeNPs were obtained through XRD (Bruker D2 Phaser, Germany) and XPS (Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, USA).

Preparation of composite cerium oxide (PLSP@CeO2) nanoparticles in one pot: First, add sialic acid (10 mL, 10 mg/mL) to a round-bottom flask and stir, then add catechol (10 mL, 10 mg/mL) and polymer (10 mL, 5 mg/mL). Stir at 60 °C for 30 min, then slowly add cerium nitrate (1.5 mL, 43.7 mg/mL). Adjust the pH to 12 using ammonia (0.1M, 0.5 mL/min), and react in the dark under nitrogen for at least 8 h. Afterwards, dialyze with a 1000 Da dialysis bag for 48 h, then freeze-dry to obtain the nanoparticles.

Acid responds to charge changes and complex dissociation: To evaluate the responsive release characteristics of bionic cerium oxide nanozymes, 1 mg of cerium oxide was re-suspended in phosphate buffered solutions (PB) under four different conditions: PB (pH 7.4), PB (pH 6.5), PB (pH 6.0), and PB (pH 5.5). These suspensions were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the reacted solutions were subjected to UV scanning and particle size potential testing.

Antioxidant test

SOD enzyme activity: The efficiency of removing O2•- is achieved by measuring the inhibition ratio of NBT photoreduction. A solution containing riboflavin (20 μM), methionine (12.5 mM), NBT (75 μM), and varying concentrations of NPs (50 μg/mL) is placed in PBS. The mixture is irradiated under a constant intensity ultraviolet light at 25 °C for 5 min. The negative control group refers to a group containing riboflavin, methionine, and NBT, but kept in the dark. The positive control group refers to a sample group containing riboflavin, methionine, and NBT that is irradiated with ultraviolet light for 5 min. The treated samples refer to the group containing riboflavin, methionine, NBT, and NPs, which are irradiated with ultraviolet light. All experiments are conducted in a dark environment.

CAT Enzyme Activity: CAT can catalyze the decomposition of H2O2 into H2O and O2. The change in O2 concentration is measured using a dissolved oxygen meter to determine the CAT mimetic activity, with reactions conducted at 25 °C in 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.4). Furthermore, we utilized a CAT detection kit to measure CAT mimetic activity, where the principle is based on colorimetric detection. In the presence of a large amount of H2O2, CAT catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2, and the remaining H2O2 can oxidize the chromogenic substrate, producing a red product with a maximum absorption wavelength of 520 nm, thereby reflecting the CAT activity in the sample.

Cells: Endothelial cells (HUVEC), neuronal cells (PC12), human leukemia cells (HL-60), and brain endothelial cells (HBMEC) were cultured in DMEM complete medium, which contains 89 % basic medium, 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin double antibiotics. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator.

Apoptosis: To analyze the situation of apoptosis, PC12 cells were inoculated into six-well culture plates at a concentration of 106 cells per well. After the cells attached, they were treated under conditions of 37 °C, adding 0.4 mM H2O2 and the sample to be tested, and incubated for a total of 24 h. After the treatment ended, the cell culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed with diluted 10 × PBS. The blank control group was treated with fresh culture medium, while the model group was stimulated solely with H2O2. The cells were collected, stained with the ANNEXI-FITC apoptosis detection kit, and analyzed by flow cytometry. Similarly, the live/dead cell staining method (Calcein AM/PI) was used to visually assess the anti-apoptotic activity of the NPs.

Cell Scratch Assay: Add approximately 5 × 105 endothelial cells to a 6-well plate, incubate for 24 h, and then use a pipette tip or sterile toothpick to vertically scratch the cell layer along the line marked on the back of the plate. After completing the scratch, wash the cells three times with sterile PBS to remove non-adherent cells. Add complete culture medium containing NPs and incubate together, then observe the results under a microscope. Angiogenesis: Add matrigel (10 mg/mL) to a 24-well plate, using 1 mL; apply it evenly with a pipette tip. Place it in a cell culture incubator to solidify for 30 min, then digest the endothelial cells that were starved for 12 h with trypsin, resuspend them, and seed onto the matrigel. Subsequently, observe the cell condition using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Animals: In this study, male SD rats weighing 220–250 g were used. The rats were kept in a constant environment with a 12 h light/dark cycle, temperature ranging from 20 to 26 °C, and humidity of 40–70 %. They had unrestricted access to food and water. A transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO/R) model was established using an improved suture occlusion method. First, the rats were fasted for 12 h prior to surgery without restricting water intake, anesthetized intraperitoneally with 20 % urethane (5 mL/kg), and fixed in a supine position on a small animal surgical table. After preparing the surgical area, an incision was made a bit to the right of the midline of the neck, and the right common carotid artery (CCA) and vagus nerve were isolated by separating the muscles and fascia; the external carotid artery (ECA) and internal carotid artery (ICA) were exposed. The distal ECA was ligated, and the branches were coagulated to prevent bleeding. A clip was applied at the bifurcation of the common carotid arteries, occluding the internal and common carotid arteries. An incision was made on the external carotid artery, and a suture was inserted from the ECA to the bifurcation of the carotid artery about 18–20 mm into the ICA, blocking the blood flow to the middle cerebral artery. After 1.5 h, the suture was removed for reperfusion, establishing the MCAO/R model. The sham group did not receive the suture insertion. After reperfusion, NPs (20 mg/kg) were injected via the tail vein, while the Sham and MCAO/R models received the same dose of saline. Throughout the surgical procedure, the body temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C.
