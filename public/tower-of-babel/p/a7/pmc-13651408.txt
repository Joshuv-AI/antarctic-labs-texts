Clinical, neuropsychological and radiological features in influenza virus-associated encephalitis: a case series
Authors: Rémy Chapelle, Charlotte Ducrohet, Irina Taifas, Lucie Patarin, Agathe Chammas, Olivier Casez, Virginie Poupinel-Descambres, Damien Ricard, Stéphane Kremer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfluenza virus-associated encephalitis is a rare but potentially threatening complication of influenza. Although it has been reported in various populations, its pathophysiological, clinical, radiological and neuropsychological features remain largely unknown. Further data are therefore needed to develop appropriate management strategies.Case presentationWe describe four French patients (two adults and two children) presenting with neurologic impairment after confirmed influenza, leading to the diagnosis of influenza virus-associated encephalitis. All of them were assessed using an extensive framework including EEGs, brain MRIs and cognitive evaluations. Relevant clinical and biological outcomes are reported for each patient.ConclusionsThis case series illustrates the diversity in the initial presentation, trajectories and outcomes of patients diagnosed with influenza virus-associated encephalitis. The reported clinical and paraclinical features point to potentially distinct forms of the disease in terms of pathophysiological mechanisms, with possible prognostic implications. For mild cases, these data suggest that complete recovery may be obtained within a few weeks after the infection, particularly at the neuropsychological level. They also emphasize the importance of early recognition and management of influenza virus-associated encephalitis.

Background

Influenza virus-associated encephalitis (IAE) is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of influenza [1, 2]. The epidemiological, clinical and paraclinical features of this complication remain largely unknown, especially in adults [3]. Consequently, there are currently no established guidelines for its diagnostic and therapeutic management [3, 4]. In this case series, we report four IAE cases (two children and two adults) diagnosed between 2022 and 2024. These cases were assessed longitudinally with an extensive framework including EEG, brain MRI and cognitive evaluations, thereby providing a comprehensive and up-to-date picture of IAE.

Case presentation

We describe four cases, presented below in chronological order. All these cases were managed in French hospital facilities between 2022 and 2024 and were identified through our care network. Patient 1

In December 2022, a 23-year-old man without significant medical history presented to his primary care physician for cough with fever and muscle pain that had been lasting for several days. Influenza was diagnosed and the patient returned home. Ten days later, he developed a confusional state with drowsiness, psychomotor slowing, dysarthria, anterograde amnesia, and severe ataxia. He was eventually admitted to the emergency department (ED) of a French hospital three days later. At that time, he also exhibited behavioral abnormalities, namely episodes of urination in the hospital room.

Thirteen days after the onset of flu-like symptoms, a lumbar puncture was performed and revealed a biological meningitis with 250 leukocytes per mm3 (reference range 0–4 /mm3), of which 85% were lymphocytes, and normal protein levels. Search for infectious agents in the blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), which did not include PCR for influenza viruses in the CSF, came back negative. However, nasopharyngeal PCR for influenza viruses revealed the presence of influenza virus A material.

A brain MRI was performed 12 days after the onset of flu-like symptoms, which was normal except for a slight posterior pontine FLAIR hyperintensity. Six days later, another brain MRI revealed signal abnormalities in the white matter of the insular, mesencephalic and pontine areas, namely FLAIR and T2 hyperintensity and T1 hypointensity, without contrast enhancement (see Fig. 1). Compared to the previous MRI, the posterior pontine hyperintensity had significantly increased. One month later, a follow-up brain MRI found a complete regression of these abnormalities. At the medullary level, one MRI was performed about 20 days after the onset of symptoms, showing a T2 hyperintensity of the terminal cone centered on the gray matter, without other abnormality (see Fig. 1).

Fifteen days after the onset of flu-like symptoms, a standard EEG showed a poorly modulated but reactive trace, without paroxysmal features. Successive EEGs performed in the course of the patient’s management showed a progressive trace improvement, with an almost normal trace 24 days after the inaugural symptoms. About three months later, the EEG trace was completely normal.

The patient was administered a cognitive screening five days after his admission to the ED, revealing an impairment of the overall efficiency with a score of 23/30 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment [5], 14/18 on the Batterie Rapide d’Efficience Frontale [6] and − 5.1 SD on the Computerised Speed Cognitive Test [7]. A new cognitive evaluation was performed one week later, revealing a clear improvement in cognitive functioning with a Montreal Cognitive Assessment score of 28/30 and an improved processing speed on the Computerised Speed Cognitive Test. An extensive neuropsychological evaluation more than three months after the onset of symptoms came back normal.

This whole picture was deemed compatible with IAE. No specific drug treatment was initiated, and the therapeutic management consisted in daily cognitive rehabilitation. The clinical evolution was characterized by a period of overall stability during the first 10 days following the admission to the ED. The symptoms then gradually improved. About one month after the onset of flu-like symptoms, a mild postural instability was the only clinical abnormality remaining. The patient was seen again about three months after the onset of symptoms. He had then no complaint and his clinical examination was normal. His recovery was deemed complete, and the follow-up was therefore discontinued.

Patient 2

In December 2022, a 72-year-old man underwent a total prostatectomy for a severe prostatic adenoma, which had caused multiple urinary tract infections. Besides this, his medical history included non-specific interstitial pneumonia, ventricular arrhythmia, and multiple cerebral ischemic strokes that occurred several years earlier (right temporal, left occipital, bilateral frontal), without any identified etiology (namely vasculitis) despite an extensive etiological assessment.

The day after his prostatectomy, the patient developed a fever and generalized seizure, leading to a cardiorespiratory arrest despite administration of Diazepam and Clonazepam. He was successfully resuscitated and intubated. A lumbar puncture was performed, revealing moderate hyperproteinorachia (0.9 g/L, reference range 0.28–0.52 g/L) and lactatorachia (4.3 mmol/L, reference range 0.5–1.5 mmol/L), without other biochemical abnormalities. A nasopharyngeal PCR returned positive for the influenza A virus. Other biological analyses were without unexpected abnormalities. Namely, search for infectious agents in the blood and CSF came back negative (but did not include PCR for influenza viruses in the CSF).

A brain MRI was performed 11 days later after the inaugural seizure (see Fig. 3), showing abnormalities compatible with Acute Necrotizing Encephalopathy (ANE). All MRIs performed until then had shown no abnormalities except those linked to the patient’s neurovascular history. A repeated MRI twenty days after the admission was unchanged compared to the previous one.

The evolution was characterized by a lack of awakening upon cessation of sedation, despite changes made to the medication regimen, namely by switching from Levetiracetam, which had been introduced initially, to Lacosamide. In this context, an EEG was performed five days after the admission to the intensive care unit, showing a slow microvolted trace without epileptic activity. The patient was also placed on Oseltamivir as soon as the results of the nasopharyngeal PCR were obtained. Despite this regimen, his pauci-relational neurologic state remained unchanged, and an attempt at extubation ultimately resulted in his death. The diagnosis of influenza virus-associated ANE was made retrospectively given this overall picture.

Patient 3

In February 2023, an 8-year-old girl with no significant medical history developed conjunctivitis and tonsillitis, followed the next day by fever and respiratory symptoms. One day later, she became asthenic, apathetic, and had one episode of vomiting. This led to admitting her to the ED, where a metabolic acidosis with a plasma pH at 7.26 (reference range 7.35–7.45) was detected. A nasopharyngeal PCR came back positive for influenza virus B. A CSF analysis performed the same day returned normal, but the next day a new lumbar puncture was carried out and revealed hyperproteinorachia (2.21 g/L, reference range 0.28–0.52 g/L) with intrathecal synthesis of immunoglobulins. No infectious agent was identified in the CSF, nor in the blood. Notably, the PCR for influenza viruses performed in the CSF came back negative. Metabolic, tumoral, and dysimmune markers also came back negative.

The day of her admission to the ED, a cerebral CT angiography showed no abnormalities. However, on a brain MRI performed the same day, several areas of diffusion hyperintensity with ADC decrease were visible (see Fig. 4). These abnormalities included features typical of MERS (Mild Encephalitis/Encephalopathy with a Reversible Splenial lesion), namely the involvement of the splenium of the corpus callosum in which a FLAIR hyperintensity could also be seen. A similar aspect was visible in the semioval centers and the cerebellar hemispheres. Five days after the onset of symptoms, a follow-up MRI showed a complete regression of the supratentorial diffusion abnormalities (see Fig. 5). In the posterior fossa, however, diffusion and FLAIR hyperintensities of both dentate nuclei could be seen on this follow-up MRI. At this stage of the disease, successive EEGs still showed an encephalitic-like slowing, primarily bi-occipital.

This picture soon led to consider the diagnosis of IAE. The treatment by corticosteroid bolus that had been initiated probabilistically was therefore supplemented with Oseltamivir and two courses of intravenous immunoglobulins. Under this treatment, a gradual clinical recovery was observed, congruent with the radiological improvement. Two weeks after her admission to the ED, the patient had almost returned to a normal neurologic state. A slight ataxia and a moderate decrease in verbal fluency were then the main signs remaining.

Patient 4

In January 2024, a 2-year-old girl with no medical or developmental history developed a cough with fever, followed the next day by a generalized convulsive seizure lasting five minutes. This prompted her admission to the ED. Upon admission, she experienced another generalized seizure, followed by behavioral disturbances, namely a marked irritability. A standard blood and CSF analysis was therefore performed, showing no abnormality. Five days later, a new lumbar puncture still found no abnormality (especially no biological evidence of meningitis, nor oligoclonal bands). All infectious analyses in the blood and CSF came back negative (although they did not include PCR for influenza viruses), as did the tests for neurologic autoantibodies. However, a nasopharyngeal PCR screening returned positive for the influenza A virus.

A brain MRI was conducted two days after the onset of symptoms, showing no abnormality. Three days later, a new brain MRI was rather suggestive of viral encephalitis, with several areas of diffusion hyperintensity and ADC decrease (see Fig. 6). A follow-up brain MRI one week later was entirely normalized.

The clinical course was initially marked by a significant neurologic deterioration. Four days after the onset of flu-like symptoms, she indeed became hypotonic and began exhibiting clonic movements of the left upper limb. A picture of focal status epilepticus eventually set in despite escalating therapy by Midazolam, Clonazepam, Levetiracetam, Fosphenytoin and Phenobarbital. It was therefore decided to proceed with sedation (namely using Midazolam and Sufentanil) and orotracheal intubation. The strengthening of the antiepileptic treatment with Levetiracetam eventually resolved the status epilepticus the next day. Repeated standard EEGs then showed a slow, poorly organized trace evocative of encephalitis rather than seizure.

Given this overall picture, the diagnosis of IAE was considered, and the patient was treated with Oseltamivir continued for five days. Under this treatment, her clinical state gradually improved. The intubation was removed after 48 h. Five days later, she had returned to her previous neurologic state, except for a slight motor deficit in all four limbs. The EEG trace had also improved, with only the persistence of a moderate slowing.

Discussion and conclusions

Influenza is a ubiquitous seasonal disease caused by three types of viruses known as Influenza Virus A, B and C. Infection by these viruses mainly results in respiratory symptoms and unspecific systemic signs such as fever, but influenza has also been occasionally linked with neurologic complications [13]. IAE is among the most common of these complications [1], and is associated with high mortality rates [2]. This makes its prevention, detection and adequate management especially important. However, IAE remains rare overall, and few data are available to build appropriate recommendations [1]. Because of this scarcity of data, even causal links between influenza virus infections and subsequent development of encephalitis are notoriously hard to establish [14]. In this context, no definition nor diagnostic criteria for IAE have been widely adopted [3]. At an operational level, some authors suggest that IAE be defined as altered levels of consciousness and/or other signs of encephalitis during a proven influenza virus infection, once other causes have been excluded [3, 15].

Because influenza virus are rarely isolated from the cerebrospinal fluid of patients suffering from IAE, some authors argue that IAE is no direct effect from the virus and that this disease should rather be called “influenza virus-associated encephalopathy” [16]. Others propose that the rare detection of the virus in the CSF of affected patients may rather be due to rapid clearance from the CSF and/or to initially low (but non-zero) viral concentration [17]. These debates illustrate the sparsity of knowledge on the pathophysiological mechanisms involved, although there are some evidence that dysregulation of the cytokine response to infection may be involved [18]. Neuro-invasive mechanisms cannot be excluded either, with a possible circulation of the virus through the olfactive nerve to reach the central nervous system [19]. Heterogeneity in the geographic distribution of cases is suggestive of genetic vulnerability factors [20]. Overall, it is conceivable that different pathophysiological pathways may be involved depending on the case, potentially accounting for the diversity in the clinical and paraclinical presentations of the disease.

Influenza virus A, B and C have been reported to cause IAE [18]. In children, influenza virus is thought to cause approximately 5% of acute encephalitis [17], with reported incidences of 0.21 per million person-years in Sweden [21] and 2.8 per million children and year in Australia [22]. IAE is more frequent in children under 5 years old [17]. It can also occur in adults, although more rarely [23]. In adults, the incidence seems equivalent in all age groups. Overall, the incidence of IAE seems to increase since the 2009 H1N1 pandemics [3]. Women may be more frequently affected than men by influenza virus B-associated encephalitis [24]. Reported risk factors for IAE mainly include the lack of vaccination against influenza [25] and preexistent chronic neurologic diseases [1].

Various clinical manifestations of IAE have been described. After a few days of free interval [18, 26], they can namely present as altered level of consciousness, seizures, confusion, ataxia and movement disorders [3, 23, 25]. Dysuria, negative visual symptoms, hemiplegia, cerebellar signs, opisthotonos, bulbar signs and intracranial hypertension signs have also been reported, although less frequently [2, 27]. Fever on admission is the norm in children [28]. Overall, there is no known pathognomonic sign of IAE [3]. The potential for clinical differences between children and adults is poorly understood because of the lack of reported adult cases. In a series of 385 pediatric and adult cases, neck stiffness was significantly more frequent in adults [23].

Blood test findings in IAE are nonspecific, although elevated transaminases levels can be seen [18]. When done, CSF analysis can show pleocytosis and/or hyperproteinorachia, or normal results [27]. The influenza virus itself is rarely isolated in the CSF [16]. This can make differential diagnoses with other viral encephalitis especially challenging [29].

IAE can take various aspects on brain MRI, including (but not limited to) cerebral multifocal or gyriform edema involving basal ganglia, the brain stem and/or cerebral and cerebellar white matter [25]. These abnormalities remain nonspecific overall. On CT-scans, limited hypodense lesions of the vertex and thalami as well as diffuse edema can be seen [27]. Intraparenchymal hemorrhages have been reported in three pediatric cases, without certain imputability to the IAE itself [30]. Some radiological patterns are more closely linked with IAE, such as ANE of childhood which is characterized by symmetric inflammatory brain lesions (typically including bilateral thalamic lesions with hemorrhagic necrosis) leading to rapidly deteriorating neurologic states and frequent fatal outcomes [18].

Nonspecific radiological aspects following viral encephalitis can also be seen in IAE, such as acute disseminated encephalomyelitis [25] and MERS [30, 31]. Although the latter was historically described as reversible FLAIR and diffusion abnormalities with decreased ADC limited to the splenium of the corpus callosum, more extensive forms involving the anterior part of the corpus callosum, the thalami and/or the semioval centers are also possible [32, 33]. Cases involving the cerebellum have also been marginally reported [34, 35], as well as cases followed by true cerebellitis [36]. Whatever the radiological findings, the clinical presentation and evolution are essential to the diagnosis. In a typical MERS, neurologic symptoms are expected to disappear completely within a month [32].

There are currently no established guidelines for the therapeutic management of IAE [4]. In addition to support and symptomatic treatments, immunomodulatory and antiviral drugs are widely use in practice [20]. Proposed immunomodulatory treatments include intravenous immunoglobulins, corticosteroids bolus and plasmatic exchanges [2, 20, 25]. Historical evidence suggested that corticosteroid bolus could only be beneficial when administered early in the disease course, but a retrospective study including 692 patients did not support the existence of this phenomenon [20]. Antiviral drugs used include Baloxavir [4] and Oseltamivir [24], with anecdotal evidence suggesting that the latter could be more effective when used at a high dose, especially in ANE [37]. Symptomatic treatments mainly aim at obtaining apyrexia [25] and normal intracranial pressure [2].

Prognostic data on IAE cases are scarce. In 44 retrospective adult cases, complete resolution was obtained in 60% of cases (although subclinical impairments may theoretically have persisted) [3]. Reported clinical sequalae in adult and pediatric patients mainly include motor and cognitive deficits [25, 27]. In a retrospective series of 385 cases, fatal outcomes in 14% of adults and 8% of children [23]. Other possible prognostic factors include the abnormality of brain MRI and CSF analysis [3] as well as age over 40 years [23]. Among 28 adult cases retrospectively identified in 2017, no follow-up information was available beyond three months [27].

In this context, the report of these four new cases of IAE is valuable for several reasons. First of all, they exemplify major epidemiological features of this condition, namely the fact that all influenza viruses can be involved and that both adults and children can be affected. They also illustrate the diversity of the clinical, biological and radiological features of these patients, not only at the time of diagnosis, but also in their follow-up. For example, MRI findings mainly consisted of symmetrical FLAIR and diffusion hyperintensity in patient 3, whereas a radiological picture suggestive of ANE was observed in patient 2. This diversity was reflected in the therapeutic strategies adopted in these cases, ranging from conservative management in patient 1 to immunomodulatory and antiviral treatment with intravenous immunoglobulins and Oseltamivir in patient 3. The resulting outcomes were highly heterogeneous, varying from complete resolution to death, which supports the idea that IAE is a complex diagnosis with potentially critical implications.

Although data remain too scarce to draw conclusions, it is tempting to interpret this clinical and paraclinical heterogeneity in terms of different pathophysiological mechanisms. For example, patient 1 exhibited a 10-day free interval between infectious and neurologic symptoms, and a florid clinical picture at onset. The analysis of his CSF found an increased WBC count, which is a hallmark of meningitis, but there was no other biological evidence supporting infectious meningitis. His brain MRI was characterized by bilateral symmetric, non-contrast-enhancing lesions involving (albeit not limited to) the insular and mesio-temporal regions, a common radiological pattern in autoimmune encephalitis [38]. This whole picture therefore suggests an inflammatory or autoimmune mechanism secondary to the viral infection, for which the name “post-influenza encephalitis” could be suitable. On the other hand, patient 3 developed neurologic symptoms only one day after the onset of her respiratory symptoms. Her CSF WBC count was normal. She presented with severe clinical abnormalities, but those were quickly reversible. The radiological abnormalities included characteristics typical of MERS, which were also regressive. This overall picture is therefore suggestive of a distinct pathophysiological pathway to patient 1, with no delayed inflammatory or autoimmune mechanism involved, which could therefore be described as a para-infectious encephalopathy. Note that the neuroradiological analysis of this case is hindered by the presence of cerebellar abnormalities in addition to the more typical MERS pattern. However, the distinct evolution of the signal abnormalities in the supra- and infratentorial regions suggests that they should probably not be linked to a single process. For example, the latter could correspond to a true cerebellitis, barely contributing to the initial symptoms, but explaining the persistent ataxia two weeks after the acute episode. The interpretation of this IAE episode in terms of para-infectious encephalopathy would therefore not apply to all findings in this patient. Such subtleties illustrate that more work is needed to properly define these diagnostic categories. The expected benefits of such efforts include refining therapeutic strategies to account for distinct pathophysiological pathways, therefore reducing the morbidity and mortality associated with IAE.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that the clinical, cognitive, neurophysiological and radiological follow-up of patient 1 was unusual, if not unique, because of its comprehensiveness and duration. It lasted 103 days after the first symptoms, while reported follow-ups of more than three months were hardly available so far [27]. These data obtained under a conservative management shed a light on the natural history of mild IAE. In this case, a typical 10-day free interval between the onset of respiratory symptoms and encephalitis symptoms was observed. A brain MRI performed early in the disease course was nearly normal, while typical radiological signs of IAE were present seven days later. These abnormalities disappeared around four weeks after the clinical encephalitis episode. This radiological evolution was well correlated with the results of repeated cognitive assessments and with the functional and clinical course of the disease. We believe that we are among the first to report results of repeated cognitive assessments performed during an episode of IAE, although these assessments were not extensive and were performed only for patient 1. The data obtained in the follow-up of this patient suggest a good clinical, radiological and neuropsychological correlation in IAE. As demonstrated in this case, the absence of clinical improvement during the first week of the disease is not predictive of the absence of subsequent improvement. Additional studies remain necessary to clarify radiological and, if appropriate, neuropsychological prognostic factors in IAE.
