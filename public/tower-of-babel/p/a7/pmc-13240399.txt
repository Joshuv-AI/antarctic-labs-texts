Worth their weight in gray matter? A narrative review of cost‐effectiveness analyses of monoclonal antibodies for Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Tuyet Thao Nguyen, Bozhidar T Chakalov, Megan Van Noord, Charles DeCarli, Jeffrey S Hoch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONMonoclonal antibodies (mAbs) for Alzheimer's disease (AD) represent major therapeutic advances, but their economic value is uncertain. This review narratively examines cost‐effectiveness analyses (CEAs) of aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab for early‐onset AD.METHODSWe searched Medline (via PubMed) and EMBASE in April 2026 and incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio (ICER) reports for CEAs of these mAbs. Inclusion required sufficient data to calculate ICERs. Two reviewers independently screened studies, and risk of bias was assessed using Risk of Bias in Systematic Reviews. Data were synthesized narratively due to methodological heterogeneity.RESULTSOf 755 records screened, 16 CEAs met inclusion criteria (six aducanumab, eight lecanemab, one aducanumab/donanemab, one lecanemab/donanemab). Almost all aducanumab studies reported ICERs that exceeded commonly cited US willingness‑to‑pay thresholds (e.g., $100,000–$150,000 per quality‐adjusted life year [QALY]), with ratios ranging from $127,461 to $1,581,276/QALY (2026 USD). Lecanemab results were mixed, with manufacturer‑funded models reporting ICERs below commonly cited thresholds, while other models generally exceeded them. The single donanemab analysis versus standard of care reported an ICER of $214,760/QALY (2026 USD), above commonly cited thresholds, and a Brazilian analysis comparing donanemab with lecanemab reported an ICER of $1,477,604/QALY (2026 USD). No independent study found any mAb cost savings.DISCUSSIONICER variability across studies, despite reliance on the same clinical trials, reflects sensitivity to assumptions and potential sponsorship bias. Evidence is limited by small numbers of CEAs, reliance on modeled rather than real‐world data, and absence of formal risk‐of‐bias scoring for individual CEAs. The cost‐effectiveness of AD mAbs in the US context remains uncertain.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, cost‐effectiveness analysis, health economics, monoclonal antibodies, quality‐adjusted life year (QALY)

INTRODUCTION

The development of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) for Alzheimer's disease (AD) represents a significant advancement in potential treatment options aimed at modifying disease progression. The introduction of these therapies has also sparked widespread debate and controversy, particularly in relation to their economic value and real‐world feasibility, regarding their cost‐effectiveness, clinical efficacy, safety profiles, and implications for healthcare systems. 1

Recently, aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab have garnered attention for their potential to influence cognitive decline in early‐stage AD by decreasing amyloid beta deposits in the brain. 2 , 3 Clinical studies have revealed that these mAbs can successfully reduce amyloid levels and modestly slow progression of the disease over the course of 18 to 36 months, representing a promising step in addressing one of the pathological aspects of AD. However, these treatments carry a substantial risk of amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIAs), which can be severe and potentially fatal. 4 These drugs are also substantially more expensive than current mainstream treatments for AD, and the clinical benefits observed in trials may not translate into noticeable improvements for daily life for patients, bringing the value of these costly interventions into question. 4 The high cost of these drugs is further compounded by the expenses associated with continuous monitoring required to prevent serious adverse reactions, such as ARIAs.

Studies regarding the cost‐effectiveness of mAbs have yielded conflicting results, with some finding these therapies to be cost‐effective 5 , 6 , 7 but others concluding the opposite. 8 , 9 Given the growing policy pressure to determine reimbursement and coverage decisions for these agents, a narrative synthesis of existing CEAs is warranted. This study is a narrative review of the cost‐effectiveness of AD mAbs that provides insights into the mixed results on their clinical and economic implications.

METHODS

Literature searches were conducted by a librarian (MGVN) across Medline (via PubMed) and EMBASE databases, as well as previous assessments by the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review, to identify published cost‐effectiveness analyses (CEAs) of AD mAbs. The search queries included terms for aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab, as well as terms consistent with economic evaluation. The search strategies are available in Appendix A. We retrieved the data on April 1, 2026.

Two reviewers (TTN and JSH) independently screened these studies to determine whether they were CEAs and whether the CEA was of mAbs for the treatment of AD. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they reported an incremental cost‑effectiveness ratio (ICER) where the health outcome was expressed in quality‑adjusted life years (QALYs); that is, studies needed to be cost‑utility analyses. Accordingly, cost‑utility analysis was an explicit inclusion criterion. Given that the United States has no explicit national cost‑effectiveness threshold, we contextualized results against commonly cited reference ranges in the US literature (e.g., $100,000–$150,000 per QALY). 10

For each article that met the inclusion criteria, we extracted the incremental effectiveness, incremental cost, and sensitivity analysis results. We measured the incremental effectiveness in QALYs and the incremental cost in US dollars (USD). Most included studies were US‐based. Four non‐US studies reported costs in their respective local currencies; for these, ICERs were converted to USD using exchange rates at the time of publication. The ICER was calculated by dividing the incremental cost in USD (ΔC) by the incremental effectiveness in QALYs (ΔE). All monetary values were inflation adjusted to January 2026 USD using the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI‐U), with each study's costs adjusted from its publication year to January.

The Risk of Bias in Systematic Reviews (ROBIS) tool was used to evaluate the search process and CEA results. 11 The study protocol was not registered. An analysis was defined as “independent” if it had no manufacturer funding and no manufacturer‐affiliated coauthors. We recognize this is an imperfect definition due to inconsistent disclosure and protocol independence and because independence does not guarantee neutrality.

RESULTS

The search yielded a total of 755 studies, 376 of which were duplicates (Figure 1). Therefore, we reviewed 379 full‐text documents. After reviewing the studies, we found 15 studies that were eligible for inclusion with no ambiguous disputes. Of the 16 CEAs, six studied aducanumab only, eight studied lecanemab alone, one compared both aducanumab and donanemab, and one compared lecanemab with donanemab (Table 1). All CEAs used lifetime horizons, except for two 12 , 13 that used a 5‐year time horizon to analyze aducanumab and one that used a 20‐year horizon to analyze lecanemab and donanemab in Brazil. 14

In contrast, the eight CEAs of lecanemab were reported divergent results: three manufacturer‐funded studies 5 − 7 reported ICERs below commonly cited US thresholds (reporting lecanemab as cost savings from both healthcare and societal perspectives), while five independent studies 8 , 9 , 14 , 16 , 17 reported ICERs ranging from $121,678 to $2,175,568 per QALY (Figure 3). All three studies that found lecanemab to be cost‐effective were funded by the drug manufacturer and employed the AD Archimedes Condition‐Event (AD ACE) simulator, a proprietary patient‐level simulation model. 18 These three studies also reported higher incremental effectiveness for lecanemab compared to the two independent studies and also assumed that the effects of treatment on clinical outcomes were mediated by amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) level.

The two donanemab analyses were not directly comparable. The US study 1 comparing donanemab with standard of care reported an ICER of $214,497 per QALY (2026 USD), whereas the Brazil study 14 comparing donanemab with lecanemab reported an ICER of $1,514,221 per QALY (2026 USD). Across all reviewed studies, only one analysis that was not funded by the drug's manufacturer found an incremental effectiveness more than 0.5 QALYs. Overall, no independent CEA found new treatment to be cost saving (i.e., having an ICER < 0; Figure 4). All industry‐funded CEAs reported ICERs below commonly cited thresholds or found the treatment to be cost‐saving. The ROBIS analysis found limited evidence to suggest significant risk of bias in this review (Figure 5). Incremental effectiveness varied substantially across studies. Among independent analyses, incremental QALYs ranged from 0.10 to 0.64 for aducanumab, 0.12 to 0.50 for lecanemab, and 0.01 to 0.41 for donanemab, depending on the comparator. Manufacturer‐funded lecanemab studies consistently reported higher incremental QALYs (0.61 from a healthcare perspective).

DISCUSSION

This review identified 16 unique CEAs focusing on mAbs for the early treatment of AD, specifically examining aducanumab, lecanemab, and donanemab. In the United States, interpreting these findings is complicated by the absence of a single, explicit national willingness‑to‑pay threshold for cost‑effectiveness. 10 The available evidence suggests that aducanumab and donanemab generally have ICERs above commonly cited US thresholds. For lecanemab, ICERs vary around these ranges, with results sensitive to model structure and assumptions. This large discrepancy highlights the importance of methodological approaches and outcomes of economic evaluations. 19

Most independent studies reported ICERs above commonly cited US willingness‐to‐pay thresholds (e.g., $100,000 to $150,000 per QALY), but one lecanemab‐versus‐aducanumab analysis reported an ICER of $121,678/QALY, and one analysis reported an ICER of $126,575/QALY in Turkey. These exceptions should be interpreted cautiously because the comparator was another expensive anti‐amyloid therapy rather than standard of care. More broadly, the modest incremental effectiveness reported in most independent studies helps explain why cost‐effectiveness remained difficult to achieve. This may be due to the low incremental effectiveness found in most of the CEAs, reporting incremental effectiveness less than 0.5 QALYs. Manufacturer‐funded studies, by contrast, consistently reported 0.61 to 0.91 QALYs. However, there are encouraging signs in the economic trends: ICER estimates have steadily decreased with each new drug introduction. ICERs have decreased from over $1 million for aducanumab to $126,575, then to $268,411 for lecanemab, and finally to $214,760 for donanemab. It is unclear whether this ICER reduction reflects genuine improvements in clinical efficacy, changes in pricing strategy, or differences in modeling assumptions. This pattern suggests that while current treatments may not be cost‐effective, future developments in disease‐modifying mAbs could yield more economically viable options.

These effectiveness measures, however, may be subject to change over time given uncertain and evolving disease‐modifying mechanism of action with anti‐amyloid treatments in general. Unlike QALYs commonly used for symptomatic treatment, other measures might be developed to assess longitudinal differences due to disease‐modifying treatment. QALYs may not adequately capture the long‐term benefits of disease‐modifying therapies, because they assign utility values to health states associated with chronic conditions, resulting in a systematic underestimation of the value of life‐extending treatments for patient populations able to adapt and accommodate to challenging health states. 20 Thus, all conclusions in this review based on QALY‐derived ICERs should be interpreted within the constraints of this metric. Alternative metrics used in other fields, such as overall survival, progression‐free survival, or time to progression in cancer treatment evaluation, 21 could provide a sense of other longitudinal impacts of these new AD treatments.

However, emerging value frameworks suggest that standard QALY thresholds may undervalue treatments for severe diseases. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence's (NICE) 2022 methods guidance applies a severity modifier that permits higher cost‐per‐QALY thresholds, and the US‐originated Generalized Risk‐Adjusted Cost‐Effectiveness (GRACE) framework similarly argues for elevated thresholds when disease severity and unmet need are high, 22 , 23 such as with AD. Under adjusted thresholds some ICERs reported here could fall within an acceptable range. Conclusions about cost‐effectiveness should therefore be tempered by the recognition that the appropriate threshold itself remains contested.

The limitations of QALY‐based assessments have sparked significant opposition from advocacy groups and raised important equity concerns. The National Council on Disability, along with more than 130 advocacy organizations, has challenged the use of QALY metrics in drug pricing policies, stating that this approach systematically undervalues treatments for people with disabilities or chronic illnesses like AD, who may have fewer “life years” or less “perfect health” ahead of them. 24 This undervaluation could be particularly pronounced for racial and ethnic minorities who may experience higher healthcare costs due to disparities in access to care and utilization of high‐cost services. 25 Distributional CEAs, which account for health inequality preferences, have been suggested as a method to mitigate these disparities and improve health equity outcomes, and one performed on a hypothetical AD‐modifying treatment found that it could reduce health inequalities. 26

Furthermore, the extreme variability in ICERs found across the CEAs is notable, especially given that these studies reference the same clinical trials for their model parameters: ENGAGE and EMERGE for aducanumab, 27 CLARITY AD for lecanemab, 3 and TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ for donanemab. 28 This variability suggests that the CEAs are highly sensitive to modeling assumptions, highlighting the need for transparency in explaining model assumptions to ensure consistency and reliability in cost‐effectiveness assessments.

Beyond funding source, other modeling characteristics also contribute to this variability. Time horizon differed markedly: Two aducanumab studies used 5‐year horizons, 1 2 , 1 3 one used a 20‐year horizon, and all others adopted lifetime perspectives, except the previously mentioned 20‐year Brazil analysis. Shorter horizons may truncate cumulative QALY gains and tend to yield higher ICERs.

Analytical perspective also varied: Manufacturer‐funded lecanemab studies incorporated societal costs, including informal caregiving, 5 , 6 whereas independent analyses generally adopted a healthcare payer perspective, 8 , 9 which excludes caregiver cost offsets and tends to yield higher ICERs. Model structure also diverged, with manufacturer‐funded lecanemab analyses using a proprietary patient‐level simulation (the AD ACE model 1 8 ) and independent studies using cohort‐based Markov models. Manufacturer‐funded studies reported an incremental effectiveness of 0.61 to 0.91 QALYs compared with 0.10 to 0.64 QALYs in independent analyses, a difference that may partly reflect structural modeling choices.

Comparator choice also varied, with most studies comparing therapy with standard care or placebo, whereas the 2026 Jiang analysis 1 7 compared lecanemab with aducanumab and the 2025 Brazil donanemab analysis 14 compared donanemab with lecanemab, limiting direct comparability across ICERs.

Drug pricing also varied: among US‐based analyses, aducanumab was evaluated at either the original annual list price ($62,000 to $67,000 in 2026 USD) or the reduced price ($29,000 to $31,000 in 2026 USD), while the Turkey‐based study assumed a lower price ($23,000 in 2026 USD).

At the component level, divergence between manufacturer‐funded and independent ICERs reflects differences in both cost and effectiveness. For cost, manufacturer‐funded analyses included broader cost offsets, such as avoiding institutionalization, reducing caregiver time and productivity losses, and lowering downstream medical costs, while independent analyses typically confined costs to drug acquisition, administration, monitoring, and adverse‐event management. On the effectiveness side, the proprietary AD ACE model produced larger incremental QALY gains than Markov‐based independent analyses, potentially owing to differences in health‐state utility mapping, treatment discontinuation assumptions, and extrapolation of trial‐period efficacy over a lifetime horizon. Together, these cost‐ and effect‐side differences compound to produce the wide range of ICERs observed.

Across the included studies, tornado and one‐way sensitivity analyses identified several variables as the largest drivers of ICER variability. In the manufacturer‐funded lecanemab analyses, 5 − 7 the discount rate and treatment discontinuation rate exerted the greatest influence on the ICER, whereas independent lecanemab analyses 8 − 9 were most sensitive to the hazard ratio for slowing disease progression. Among the aducanumab studies, drug cost and treatment efficacy were the dominant drivers, with time horizon also playing a critical role in the 5‐year analyses. 1 2 − 1 3 These patterns suggest that uncertainty in treatment efficacy and in final drug pricing, which may change after CEAs are published, are principal sources of ICER instability across the literature. A limitation of this review is the relatively small number of published studies, particularly for donanemab, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. For example, the limited number of CEAs for the mAbs, especially donanemab, restricts the ability to draw broad conclusions about their economic attractiveness across different healthcare settings. Moreover, the reliance on a single major clinical trial per drug introduces additional uncertainty regarding the robustness of effectiveness estimates used in these analyses. This limitation is compounded by the variability in ICERs across studies, which suggests that cost‐effectiveness estimates are highly sensitive to assumptions. The variability in ICERs highlights the need for more comprehensive research (e.g., through real‐world evidence) and standardized methodologies to better assess the cost‐effectiveness of these treatments.

Future research should prioritize independent, non‐manufacturer‐funded CEAs of mAbs for early AD treatment, alongside long‐term, real‐world data collection on clinical effectiveness and safety. To improve reproducibility and comparability, future CEAs could report whether prices reflect list or net values, preregister model protocols and publish full parameter files, report the threshold price at which each drug would become cost‐effective at commonly cited US reference values, include scenarios for treatment discontinuation and long‐term durability of disease‐modifying effects, and report ICERs using severity‐adjusted thresholds to address concerns that standard QALY benchmarks undervalue treatments in populations with pre‐existing disability. Such data could provide comprehensive assessments reflecting these treatments' true impact across diverse healthcare settings, including healthcare delivery, caregiver support, and residential care. With 6.9 million Americans aged 65 and older living with AD dementia 29 and approximately 30% residing in nursing homes 30 at an annual cost of up to $89,000 per person, 31 delaying institutional care by just 1 year could save billions annually. On the other hand, the sheer number of potential patients using the expensive drugs may make budget impact as important a question as cost‐effectiveness. Future economic analyses should integrate both cost‐effectiveness and budget impact perspectives to inform payer decisions. Future studies could offer crucial guidance for healthcare providers and policymakers in evaluating both the clinical use and economic implications of these therapies. These conclusions should be tempered by the recognition that severity‐adjusted thresholds, such as those applied under NICE's QALY shortfall approach or the GRACE framework, could yield more favorable assessments.

CONCLUSION

This review indicates that the cost‐effectiveness of mAbs for the treatment of early AD is highly uncertain based on currently available modeled evidence. Existing CEAs appear to be quite sensitive to assumptions and potentially influenced by biases, particularly those associated with manufacturer funding. As a result, healthcare providers and decision‐makers must exercise caution when interpreting the available evidence. Continued research, particularly the impact of mAb treatment on long‐term outcomes, and more robust independent analyses are necessary to provide clearer insights into the economic viability of these treatments and to guide their integration into clinical practice effectively. 22 , 23
