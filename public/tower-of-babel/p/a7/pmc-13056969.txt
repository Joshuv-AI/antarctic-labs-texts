Religiosity spirituality and nonreligious spiritual practices linked to anxiety and depressive symptoms
Authors: Patryk Główczyński, Paweł Dębski, Karina Badura-Brzoza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpirituality and religiosity are increasingly examined as factors relevant to mental health, yet less is known about individualized nonreligious forms of spiritual practices. In this cross-sectional study, we investigated associations between spirituality, religious affiliation, non-religious forms of spiritual practices (NFSP) and symptoms of anxiety and depression in 1240 young adults aged 18–25 in Poland. Participants were categorized as Catholics, Catholics engaging in NFSP, atheists, or atheists engaging in NFSP. Spirituality was assessed using the Interfaith Spirituality Scale and anxiety and depressive symptoms using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale. Group differences were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn post hoc tests and associations were examined with Spearman rank correlations. Higher total spirituality and the domains Direct Connection with the Creator Asceticism and Divine Love were inversely associated with depressive symptom severity in the total sample. Anxiety levels differed between groups and were highest among participants engaging in NFSP regardless of religious affiliation. In group specific analyses, anxiety correlated positively with the Meditation domain in Catholics and atheists and correlated negatively with Divine Love in Catholics engaging in NFSP and with Asceticism in atheists engaging in NFSP. These findings suggest that spirituality may function as a protective correlate for depressive symptoms whereas links with anxiety are more heterogeneous and may depend on how spirituality is expressed and integrated with worldview.Keywords: Spirituality, Young adults, Catholic, Atheist, SBNR, ReligiositySubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Spirituality and religiosity are increasingly recognized as relevant factors in mental health, particularly in the context of emotional regulation, meaning-making, and coping with psychological distress1. Although the two concepts are closely related, they are not synonymous. Religiosity typically refers to affiliation with a specific religious tradition and participation in institutional practices, whereas spirituality encompasses broader existential orientations, including the search for meaning, transcendence, connectedness, and inner coherence2. Importantly, individuals may identify as spiritual without being religious, religious without engaging in personal spirituality, or combine elements of both. In the present study, spirituality is treated as an umbrella construct that may be expressed through institutional religiosity as well as through nonreligious forms of spiritual practices (NFSP)—that is, individualized practices oriented toward meaning-making, perceived transcendence, or personal agency, conducted outside formal religious structures. NFSP describes a mode of practice rather than a coherent belief system, and its boundaries may overlap with religious traditions, for example in the case of meditation.

Existing research suggests that spiritual and religious experiences may have measurable biological and neurophysiological correlates, including activity within neural systems involved in affect regulation, self-referential processing, and social cognition3–5. Neuroimaging and neurochemical studies have associated spiritual or religious engagement with activation in regions such as the medial prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and temporal lobes, as well as with serotonergic functioning4,5. However, this body of evidence remains heterogeneous and does not support deterministic conclusions regarding belief or spirituality. Rather, current findings indicate that individual differences in neurocognitive and affective systems may shape how people experience, interpret, and engage in spiritual or religious practices2–5. Beyond biological correlates, spirituality is strongly embedded in social and cultural contexts, which influence both its forms of expression and its psychological functions.

Across contemporary societies, traditional forms of institutional religiosity have undergone substantial transformation, particularly among adolescents and young adults. Declining religious affiliation and ritual participation have been accompanied by the diversification and individualization of spiritual practices6–10. Alongside established religious traditions, many individuals increasingly engage in spiritual practices such as affirmation or manifestation techniques, astrology, tarot, ritual cleansing, or non-institutional meditation11. These practices often emphasize autonomy, personal experience, and self-regulation rather than doctrinal authority. At the same time, they may coexist with formal religious identification, resulting in hybrid or internally plural spiritual profiles. Such patterns have been described sociologically as the “privatization of beliefs,” reflecting a shift from collective, institutionally mediated religion toward individualized and situational spirituality. Within this broader context, atheists engaging in NFSP may also be situated within the heterogeneous category often described as “religious nones” or „spiritual but not religious” (SNBR)—individuals who do not identify with any religious institution but may still express spiritual needs or engage in spiritual practices12–14.

These transformations are particularly salient during young adulthood, a developmental period characterized by identity formation, increased uncertainty, and heightened vulnerability to mental health problems15,16. Anxiety and depressive symptoms are among the most prevalent psychological difficulties in individuals aged 18–25 years and represent a major public health concern15–17. Previous studies examining the relationship between spirituality, religiosity, and mental health have yielded mixed results. Many report that higher religiosity or spirituality is associated with lower depressive symptom severity, often interpreted as a protective effect linked to meaning, hope, or social support18,19. In contrast, associations with anxiety appear less consistent and may depend on the type of spiritual engagement, worldview coherence, and cultural context20–22. Notably, empirical data addressing NFSP remain limited, particularly in Central and Eastern Europe, and few studies have simultaneously examined institutional religiosity and nonreligious spiritual practices within the same analytical framework.

Given this conceptual plurality and empirical ambiguity, the present study addresses the following research question: How are different expressions of spirituality—religious and nonreligious (NFSP)—associated with anxiety and depressive symptoms among young adults in Poland? We further explore whether combining institutional religious affiliation with NFSP corresponds to distinct mental health profiles compared to more internally consistent spiritual or worldview orientations. Based on prior empirical findings and theoretical considerations, we formulated the following hypotheses: (1) higher spirituality expressed within an institutional religious framework will be associated with lower depressive symptoms and potentially lower anxiety symptoms; (2) spirituality expressed through NFSP will show non-uniform associations with anxiety and depressive symptoms, reflecting both potential protective and risk-related pathways; and (3) individuals combining Catholic identification with NFSP will exhibit a distinct pattern of anxiety and depressive symptoms compared to individuals whose declared worldview and spiritual practices are more internally consistent.

Aim

Although numerous studies have examined spirituality and religiosity in the context of mental health, there remains a lack of analyses simultaneously addressing both traditional and non-religous forms of spirituality among young adults in Poland. Equally important is the evaluation of sociodemographic factors, religious upbringing, and the influence of socio-political events on spiritual practices and mental well-being.

The aim of this study was to analyze the relationship between spirituality and the severity of depressive and anxiety symptoms among young adults in Poland, taking into account variations in the type of spiritual practices—both religious and non-religious—as well as the broader sociodemographic context.

Material

The study was conducted between January 2024 and January 2025. Participants included students from secondary schools, post-secondary institutions, and universities. Exclusion criteria comprised current psychiatric or psychological treatment, age below 18 years or above 25 years.

A total of 1,545 individuals participated in the study. Of these, 284 were excluded due to not meeting the age criteria. Given the small number of respondents, non-binary individuals (n = 10) were excluded, as were participants representing minority religious affiliations: Buddhism (n = 3), Orthodox Christianity (n = 1), Protestantism (n = 2), Islam (n = 1), and Jehovah’s Witnesses (n = 4). The final analysis included 1,240 participants aged 18–25 years.

Based on the participants’ self-reported data, four groups were distinguished:

Group 2: Catholics engaging in NFSP—participants identifying as Catholic but also practicing at least one form of NFSP (n = 354),

Group 3: Atheists (n = 212),

Group 3: Atheists (n = 212),

Group 4: Atheists engaging in NFSP—participants identifying as atheist but also practicing at least one form of NFSP (n = 225).

Group 4: Atheists engaging in NFSP—participants identifying as atheist but also practicing at least one form of NFSP (n = 225).

According to data from the Central Statistical Office (GUS, 2024), the population of individuals aged 18–25 years in Poland is estimated at approximately 3 million. Based on this estimate, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be approximately 384 participants23,24.

Methods

Three standardized research questionnaires were used in the study:

Author’s sociodemographic questionnaire

This tool was divided into two parts, A and B. Part A included questions on gender, age, place of residence (city size), education, relationship status, and parenthood. Part B focused on religious affiliation, frequency of religious practice (participation in rituals), upbringing within a religious tradition, and engagement in NFSP. Additionally, the questionnaire contained an open-ended question allowing respondents to specify spiritual practices not listed in the inventory. Engagement in non-religious forms of spiritual practice was assessed using a structured set of survey items referring to specific practices commonly described in the literature as individualized or alternative forms of spirituality (e.g., astrology, tarot, crystal therapy, affirmation, meditation, numerology, palo Santo rituals). For each practice, participants were asked three dichotomous (Yes/No) questions addressing different levels of involvement: (1) whether they practiced the given activity, (2) whether they believed in its effectiveness or validity, and (3) whether they used it as a source of guidance in everyday life or decision-making. Participants were allowed to endorse more than one practice. In addition, an open-ended question enabled respondents to report other spiritual practices not listed in the questionnaire. These responses were subsequently reviewed and categorized when they met the operational criteria of NFSP, defined as spiritual activities practiced outside institutional religious contexts and based primarily on individualized belief systems rather than formal doctrine or community affiliation. For analytical purposes, participants were classified as engaging in NFSP if they endorsed at least one practice at any of the three levels (practice, belief, or guidance).

Interfaith spirituality scale (ISS)

Interfaith Spirituality Scale (ISS)—by Kira et al., Polish adaptation by Surzykiewicz et al. The questionnaire consists of 22 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = definitely no, 4 = definitely yes). The Polish version of the scale demonstrated high internal consistency (α = 0.96), while in the present study Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was α = 0.93. The ISS conceptualizes spirituality as a multidimensional construct divided into four domains: Direct Connection with the Creator (DRC—6 items): the subjective sense of personal relationship with the “Creator” (understood broadly as a higher power or force), characterized by inner peace, closeness, and the pursuit of transcendence. Asceticism—Self-Discipline and Virtues (ASTC—6 items): self-regulation and virtuous behaviors viewed as expressions of spirituality, including moderation, gratitude, honesty, and kindness. Meditation and Spiritual Knowledge (MED—5 items): reflective or meditative practices aimed at understanding existence, creation, and personal meaning, encompassing both private contemplation and meditative appreciation of life. Divine Love (DL—5 items): the experience of love directed toward the Creator, as well as the perception of divine love through relationships with others. According to the authors, “divine love” represents the essence of spirituality—a profound sense of love for the Creator regarded as central to one’s spiritual life25,26.

Hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS)

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)—by Zigmond and Snaith. The HADS is a self-report screening instrument consisting of 14 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), designed to measure symptoms of anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D), with 7 items per subscale.

Subscale scores range from 0 to 21, with 8–10 indicating a moderate level of symptoms and scores ≥ 11 reflecting a clinically significant level of anxiety or depression27.

In the present study, internal consistency reliability was α = 0.83 for HADS-A and α = 0.77 for HADS-D.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel and Statistica version 13.3. Normality of variable distribution across the study group was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test with Lilliefors correction. As the distribution of variables deviated from normality, non-parametric statistical methods were applied. Differences between independent variables were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. Associations between continuous variables were analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. The level of statistical significance was set at α ≤ 0.05.

Results

Description of the study group

The mean age of the participants was 21.71 ± 2.45 years. Among the 1,240 respondents included in the analysis, 858 were women and 382 were men.

A total of 803 participants (64.75%) identified as Catholics. In response to the optional question regarding being raised in a religiously practicing family, 1,202 participants provided an answer, of whom 990 (82.36%) responded affirmatively.

Regarding the practice of other, non-religious forms of spirituality (NFSP), 46.7% of respondents confirmed engaging in at least one such activity. Reported practices included astrology, numerology, tarot reading, meditation, affirmation and manifestation techniques, palo santo rituals (energy cleansing using incense), and crystal therapy (belief in the metaphysical properties of stones and crystals). The most frequently reported forms of non-religious spiritual practice were: manifestation/affirmation—371 respondents (64.08%), meditation—311 respondents (53.71%), astrology—279 respondents (48.18%). Respondents also had the option to specify other spiritual practices not listed in the questionnaire. Individual declarations included Reiki (bioenergetic or spiritual healing), mindfulness, dream interpretation, Slavic beliefs, and reading Nordic runes. Based on the collected data, the following study groups were defined: Group 1—Catholics: 449 respondents (271 women, 178 men). Among them, 264 (58.79%) reported active participation in religious practices (e.g., attending religious services or rituals). Group 2—Catholics engaging in NFSP: 354 respondents (301 women, 53 men). Within this group, 187 respondents (52.82%) confirmed regular religious practice. The most common NFSP were manifestation/affirmation (233; 65.81%), meditation (184; 51.98%), and astrology (166; 46.89%). Notably, 203 participants (57.3%) in this group reported practicing two or more NFSP activities. Group 3—Atheists: 212 respondents (108 women, 104 men). Group 4—Atheists engaging in NFSP: 225 respondents (178 women, 47 men). The most frequent NFSP in this group were manifestation/affirmation (138; 61.33%), meditation (127; 56.44%), and astrology (113; 50.22%). Additionally, 144 participants (64%) reported engaging in at least two NFSP.

Hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS)

No statistically significant differences, taking into consideration results of Kruskal–Wallis test, were observed between the study groups regarding depressive symptoms (p = 0.161). Statistically significant differences between Groups 1–4 were noted for the intensity of anxiety symptoms (p = 0.001). Post hoc analysis using Dunn’s test revealed that anxiety scores were significantly higher in groups engaging in NFSP (Groups 2 and 4) compared with Groups 1 and 3 (Group 2 > Group 4 > Group 1 > Group 3; p ≤ 0.05). Correlations between variables

Due to the absence of statistically significant differences between Groups 1–4 in the assessment of depressive symptoms (HADS-D), correlations with the use of Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient were calculated for the entire sample. The total spirituality score (Spirituality Total) correlated negatively with depressive symptom severity (p = 0.001). The DRC domain also showed a negative correlation with depressive symptoms (p = 0.003). Similarly, negative correlations were observed for the ASTC domain (p = 0.001) and the DL domain (p = 0.001) with HADS-D scores. No statistically significant correlations were found between the MED domain and depressive symptom severity.

Because statistically significant differences were identified between groups in anxiety symptom intensity (HADS-A), correlation analyses using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient between the studied parameters were conducted separately for each group.

Discussion

The present study examined associations between different expressions of spirituality—religious and nonreligious forms of spiritual practices (NFSP)—and symptoms of anxiety and depression among young adults in Poland. The findings indicate that spirituality is related to mental health in a differentiated manner: while higher spirituality was consistently associated with lower depressive symptom severity, its relationship with anxiety was more complex and varied depending on the form of spiritual engagement and worldview configuration.

Across the entire sample, depressive symptom intensity remained below the clinical threshold, and no significant differences were observed between the study groups. However, correlational analyses revealed that higher overall spirituality and specific domains—Direct Connection with the Creator, Asceticism, and Divine Love—were associated with lower levels of depressive symptoms. These associations were independent of formal religious affiliation and suggest that the protective potential of spirituality for depressive symptoms may be linked less to denominational identity and more to experiential dimensions such as meaning-making, perceived support, self-regulation, and affective connectedness1,18,19. This finding aligns with functional models of spirituality, which emphasize the psychological role of coherence, hope, and value-based orientation rather than institutional belonging1.

In contrast to depression, anxiety symptoms differed significantly between groups and reached the highest levels among participants engaging in NFSP, regardless of religious affiliation. This pattern suggests that anxiety is more sensitive to the form and context of spiritual engagement. Group-specific analyses further illustrated this complexity. Among Catholics and atheists without NFSP, higher anxiety was positively associated with the Meditation domain of the Interfaith Spirituality Scale. This finding may reflect the fact that reflective or introspective practices can intensify self-focus and existential concerns, particularly when not embedded within a stable framework of meaning or communal validation21. In such cases, spiritual reflection may function as a source of cognitive or emotional strain rather than relief.

Participants combining Catholic affiliation with NFSP exhibited a distinct profile. In this group, anxiety symptoms were negatively associated with Direct Connection with the Creator and Divine Love, suggesting that experiential aspects of relational spirituality—such as trust, closeness, and perceived benevolence—may buffer anxiety. At the same time, this group showed the highest overall anxiety levels. One possible explanation is that individuals who combine institutional religion with NFSP may experience internal tension arising from the coexistence of divergent belief systems, potentially undermining worldview coherence. Alternatively, heightened anxiety may precede and motivate spiritual exploration, leading individuals to seek multiple spiritual resources simultaneously1,19. These interpretations are consistent with sociological accounts of belief privatization and individualized spirituality12,28.

Among atheists engaging in NFSP, anxiety was negatively associated with Asceticism, indicating that self-discipline, moral orientation, and value-based regulation may serve as adaptive resources even in the absence of religious belief. This group corresponds to the increasingly recognized category of individuals often described as “spiritual but not religious”29. Despite their lack of institutional affiliation, these individuals appear to maintain spiritual needs that may be addressed through individualized practices. However, their elevated anxiety levels suggest that NFSP may not uniformly function as a protective factor and may instead reflect ongoing processes of searching, experimentation, or emotional dysregulation30,31.

The findings concerning atheists without NFSP further complicate simplistic assumptions about spirituality as a biologically determined universal need. This group exhibited the lowest levels of spirituality and limited engagement in spiritual practices while also reporting relatively low anxiety levels. It is possible that non-religious individuals rely on alternative psychological or social resources—such as rational meaning-making, interpersonal support, or secular coping strategies—to regulate distress19,22. These results suggest that spirituality may represent one of several possible adaptive pathways rather than a universal requirement for mental well-being.

Taken together, the results support the view that spirituality may function as a relatively consistent protective correlate for depressive symptoms, whereas its relationship with anxiety is more heterogeneous and context-dependent. Anxiety appears particularly sensitive to issues of worldview coherence, cultural context, and the function that spiritual practices serve for the individual21. Importantly, the present findings do not allow causal conclusions. Elevated anxiety may both result from and contribute to engagement in NFSP, and longitudinal designs are required to disentangle these pathways.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. The study was conducted within a specific cultural context and focused on young adults in Poland, limiting generalizability to other populations6–10. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although individuals reporting current psychiatric or psychological treatment were excluded, the sample was non-clinical and relied on self-report measures. Meditation was treated as a single category within NFSP, despite its diverse forms and functions, which may have influenced the observed associations32,33. Women were overrepresented relative to the general population, and minority religious affiliations were excluded due to small group sizes. Another limitation is the lack of standardized instruments measuring the intensity and centrality of religious beliefs and nonreligious spiritual beliefs. The use of scales such as the Centrality of Religiosity Scale or the Paranormal Belief Scale could provide a more nuanced assessment of belief intensity beyond self-declared affiliation and practice34,35,36,37. Finally, although the online survey was distributed through educational institutions, full control over response conditions was not possible.

In conclusion, the present study highlights that spirituality is not a uniform construct with uniformly beneficial effects on mental health. While higher spirituality appears to be associated with lower depressive symptoms among young adults, its links with anxiety are more complex and depend on how spirituality is expressed and integrated with individual worldviews. Nonreligious forms of spiritual practices may serve both adaptive and maladaptive functions, particularly in the context of anxiety. These findings underscore the importance of nuanced conceptualization of spirituality in mental health research and suggest that assessment of spiritual beliefs and practices—religious and nonreligious—may be relevant in preventive psychiatry and mental health promotion among young adults.
