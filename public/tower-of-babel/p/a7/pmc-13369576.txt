BCR‐ABL1 Transcript Variants in Chronic Myeloid Leukemia Patients in Africa: A Systematic Review
Authors: Martine Emeline Oloume, Anatole Pieme, Cedric Kekuine Ngalle, Ransir Tapouli, Claude Tayou Tagny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe BCR‐ABL1 fusion gene is present in more than 95% of cases of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), the disease in which it was first described. There are different BCR‐ABL1 fusion transcripts depending on the location of the breakpoints during chromosomal translocation. Identifying these transcripts is important for treatment monitoring. The objective was to determine the frequency and distribution of BCR‐ABL1 transcripts in African countries.MethodsThe articles were sourced from scientific databases such as PubMed, Google Scholar, and the BioMed Central databases. The systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines. Data extraction was performed using Excel 2016.ResultsEight original articles, representing seven African countries and involving a total of 1258 patients with CML, were selected based on the established criteria. The mean age of patients at diagnosis was 40.55 years, ranging from 9 to 87 years. The two main transcripts were e14a2 and e13a2. In most African studies, e14a2 was more prevalent than e13a2 (51.35% vs. 38.55%). The Nigerian population showed a relatively high frequency of co‐expression of e14a2 and e13a2. The other transcripts identified were e19a2, e1a2, e13a3, and e6a2, accounting for 1.74% of the total number of identified transcripts. Age and sex did not appear to have an effect on transcript types in most countries, with the exception of Sudan and Tunisia. Certain associations were observed between BCR‐ABL variants and hematological parameters, particularly white blood cell and platelet counts.ConclusionThere are few studies in Africa on the frequency of BCR‐ABL1 gene variants. The main transcripts identified were e14a2 and e13a2. In most cases, the e14a2 variant is more common than the e13a2 variant. Some studies have reported an association with white blood cell and platelet counts in the complete blood count. There is a significant gap in molecular diagnostics in Africa.

Introduction

The Philadelphia chromosome or BCR‐ABL1 fusion gene is one of the first genetic abnormalities associated with human cancer. This gene encodes a protein with deregulated tyrosine kinase activity, the BCR‐ABL1 protein, which provides a cell growth advantage and abnormal differentiation capabilities. The BCR‐ABL1 fusion gene is detectable by conventional cytogenetics in approximately 95% of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) cases, where it was first described, while the remaining cases may still harbor the BCR‐ABL1 fusion detected by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or polymerase chain reaction techniques [1]. Depending on the exact location of the breakpoints during translocation, the molecular weight of the resulting abnormal protein varies, leading to the formation of distinct fusion isoforms or transcripts. The main regions where breakpoints cluster are the major (M‐BCR), minor (m‐BCR), and micro (u‐BCR) regions. In the BCR gene located on chromosome 22, breakpoints are generally found in exons e13 (b2) or e14 (b3) of the M‐BCR region, generating two different transcripts, e13a2 (b2a2) and e14a2 (b3a2), respectively [2].

The breakpoint in the ABL1 gene is typically located between exons a1 and a2. These breakpoints give rise to various BCR‐ABL rearrangements, the most common being e13a2 (b2a2) and e14a2 (b3a2), which encode a 210 kDa protein: p210. In some patients, both transcripts may be co‐expressed: e13a2 (b2a2) with e14a2 (b3a2). More rarely, a break in the BCR gene occurs between exons 1 and 2, generating the e1a2 transcript, which encodes a 190 kDa protein, or between exons 19 and 20, generating the e19a2 transcript, which encodes a 230 kDa protein. Other rare variants, such as the e14a3 (b3a3) and e8a2 transcripts, have been reported [3]. The global incidence of CML ranges from 0.6 to 2.0 cases per 100,000 people per year. It accounts for approximately 15% of all new cases of leukemia diagnosed each year. It is considered a rare disease according to the definition provided by the RAREcare group, meaning fewer than 6 new cases per year per 100,000 people [4]. In Africa, CML accounts for 27.47% of all leukemia cases, representing the highest overall prevalence. This type of leukemia primarily affects older adults, but approximately 5% of cases occur in people under the age of 20 [5]. The BCR‐ABL1 oncoprotein interacts with numerous cellular substrates in the cytoplasm, leading to the activation of abnormal signaling pathways characterized by elevated protein kinase activity, reduced apoptosis, and malignant transformation [6]. The diagnosis of CML typically begins with a complete blood count, which reveals a very high white blood cell count. Morphological examination of thin blood smears shows an increase in granulocytes of all types, generally including mature myeloid cells and their precursors. Basophils are almost always present in excess. Detection of the Philadelphia chromosome confirms the disease and can be performed by conventional cytogenetics or by FISH. Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) can be used to determine the gene variant where the translocation occurs and is necessary for molecular monitoring by quantitative PCR [7]. Patients are classified as having a complete hematologic response (CHR) if their white blood cell and platelet counts are normal and no immature granulocytes cells are detected in the blood smear [8]. In patients with CHR, CML cannot be detected by hematological tests, but only through cytogenetic and molecular tests. A better understanding of the molecular processes involved in the pathogenesis of CML has led to the development of new therapeutic options called tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs). In general, tyrosine kinases phosphorylate specific amino acids on substrate enzymes, which alter signal transduction and leads to downstream changes in cellular biology [9]. The mechanism of action of ITKs involves first inhibiting the activity of the BCR‐ABL1 tyrosine kinase by blocking the ATP‐binding site, and then preventing phosphorylation and downstream signaling via pathways such as RAS/MAPK, PI3K/AKT, and JAK/STAT, which leads to the inhibition of leukemic cell proliferation [10].

The management of CML in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) faces numerous challenges, particularly regarding local diagnosis and access to treatment. Furthermore, molecular diagnostic infrastructure is limited in many African countries. Regional data on the distribution of BCR‐ABL1 transcripts appear to be scarce. Molecular monitoring of patients is essential, as treatment response can be highly heterogeneous. Indeed, monitoring residual disease is important for accurately assessing treatment response, detecting resistance, and optimizing the therapeutic strategy. It is therefore necessary to determine the type of transcript in order to ensure optimal molecular monitoring and to implement the most appropriate treatment based on the therapeutic response [7, 8, 11]. This is a systematic review assessing the frequencies of BCR‐ABL1 transcripts observed in patients with CML in African countries.

Methods

The recommendations of the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyzes) model were used for this review [12]. The target papers were original articles published on BCR‐ABL variants or transcripts in African countries.

Study Search and Selection Strategy

The literature search was conducted in scientific databases such as PubMed and Google Scholar. The search terms used to identify articles were “BCR‐ABL1 in African countries.” The search strategy used the following keywords: BCR‐ABL1, BCR‐ABL1 variants, CML, and/or BCR‐ABL1 transcripts in each African country. Separate searches were performed for every country individually. For example, the complete search query used to find a study in Cameroon was “BCR‐ABL1 Cameroon; CML Cameroon.” The studies were conducted over a 20‐year period.

Selection of Studies and Data Analysis

The published articles were selected based on key search terms. An assessment of which articles to include was then conducted. The full texts of all references that potentially met the inclusion criteria were subsequently reviewed for inclusion. The inclusion criteria were as follows: a study conducted in an African country aimed at identifying BCR‐ABL1 variants in a population with CML; the identification and frequency of BCR‐ABL1 variants, rather than solely of final proteins, such as p210, which is the result of two different transcripts; the required diagnostic method was molecular biology using RT‐PCR or quantitative PCR. The minimum sample size was 30, with no age restriction, including for pediatric populations. The collected data were reviewed by three authors to verify accuracy and consistency; data extraction was performed using Excel 2016.

The following data were extracted from the selected studies: study title, country, and year of publication; sample characteristics (population size, age, and sex); study design; and main results. The chi‐square test for proportions and Fisher's exact test (where applicable) were used to compare the distribution of transcript variants across countries. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The proportions of the different transcripts were summed by transcript category to obtain the overall frequencies and percentages for African countries by transcript type. Transcripts with very low frequencies were grouped into the “rare transcripts” category.

Ethical Statement

Not applicable for this study.

Results

Initially, 32 studies were extracted from various databases (22 from PubMed and 10 from Google Scholar). Figure 1 shows the flowchart of the literature search. After removing duplicates, 25 articles were selected based on their titles and abstracts; of these, 22 were selected for a thorough review of the full text. In accordance with the criteria described in the methodology, articles for which the full text was not available were excluded, as were those for which the transcripts were not clearly identified. Eight of these studies were ultimately analyzed and included in the present study.

Discussion

The African countries with access to at least one TKI in Africa are Algeria, Benin, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Republic of the Congo, Ivory Coast, Ethiopia, Gabon, Ghana, Kenya, Madagascar, Mali, Morocco, Namibia, Niger, Nigeria, Senegal, South Africa, Rwanda, Sierra Leone, Sudan, Tanzania, Togo, Tunisia, Uganda, Zambia, and Zimbabwe. Most of these countries have entered into partnerships with the Max Foundation through the Glivec International Patient Assistance Program (GIPAP) [13]. Even in these countries where ITKs are free thanks to this valuable collaboration, few use molecular diagnostics to clearly distinguish between the different transcripts. In some countries, such as Tanzania, molecular detection of the gene focuses on the p210 protein, which combines the two most common transcripts, and each variant has not been clearly identified [14].

According to the selected articles, there were a total of 1258 patients. Table 1 shows the number and proportion of transcript types across the different studies. The mean age at diagnosis was 40.55 years, ranging from 9 to 87 years. CML can affect all age groups, although it is rare in children, and its incidence increases with age. Most patients with CML in developed countries are diagnosed at a median age between 60 and 65 years [23], whereas in Africa, the median age is considerably lower among younger populations. The prevalence of CML is steadily increasing due to the substantial prolongation of survival achieved through targeted therapies. In the majority of studies, CML appears to occur more frequently in men than in women, with a mean sex ratio of 1.2. Age and sex did not appear to influence transcript types in most countries. However, Bennour et al. [21] reported that patients with the e14a2 transcript type were significantly older than those in the e13a2 group (mean age of 56 vs. 26 years [p = 0.001]). Among Sudanese patients, men tended to express the e13a2 type, while women tended to express the e14a2 type [19].

The Figure 2 shows the frequencies of BCR‐ABL transcripts in African countries, represented by seven countries. With regard to methodological differences between studies, some variations in PCR methods were observed. Multiplex RT‐PCR is useful and saves time in the detection of different types of BCR‐ABL1 fusion transcripts. The primer combinations used in multiplex RT‐PCR allow for the simultaneous detection of all known types of BCR‐ABL1 and BCR transcripts in a single reaction. This technology has been implemented in Algeria, Nigeria, Tunisia, and Burkina Faso. In other studies, researchers used the p210 and p190 primers to perform different reactions, as well as for gene sequencing. Most studies used 2% agarose gel electrophoresis to visualize the PCR products.

Frequencies and proportions of BCR‐ABL1 transcripts in African countries represented by seven countries.

The two main types of BCR‐ABL1 transcripts, e14a2 and e13a2, are the most common worldwide, as they are present in more than 90% of patients with CML [24], including in African countries. The proportions of these variants may vary by country, but in most African studies, the e14a2 variant is more prevalent (51.35%) than the e13a2 variant (38.55%). In contrast, the e13a2 variant is more common in Sudan (53.7%) and Burkina Faso (47.4%). When comparing these results with those from other countries, we find that in the United States, a study of 481 patients with chronic‐phase CML showed that 200 patients expressed the e13a2 transcript (42%), 196 (41%) expressed the e14a2 transcript, and 85 (18%) expressed both transcripts [25]. In Germany, the frequency of transcripts among 1105 patients at the time of diagnosis was as follows: e13a2, n = 451; e14a2, n = 496; e13a2 + e14a2, n = 158 [26]. In Pakistan, 70 newly diagnosed patients with chronic‐phase CML were tested by RT‐PCR to detect different BCR‐ABL 1 transcripts. Expression of the e14a2 or e13a2 transcript was detected in 38 (54%) and 30 (43%) patients, respectively. Co‐expression of e13a2 + e14a2 was observed in 2 (3%) patients [27].

The other transcripts were e1a2, e13a3, e6a2, and e19a2, which are generally classified as atypical or rare transcripts. Most patients with CML express either the b2a2 or b3a2 variant of the BCR‐ABL1 gene. These two transcripts may be expressed in approximately 5%–10% of patients due to alternative splicing [28]. In Burkina Faso, this coexpression of these genes accounts for 18.4%, whereas in Sudan, it was 2.3%. The Nigerian population had a co‐expression frequency of 35.7%. According to the international review by Baccarani et al. [29], the frequencies of this combined expression were 6.5% in Africa, 2.4% in Asia, 9.3% in Europe, 18.7% in Australia, and 8.9% in South America.

The co‐expression rate is significantly higher in Nigeria than the rate generally reported internationally, which typically ranges between 5% and 10%. This discrepancy may be difficult to explain, as the methods used to detect transcripts appear to be the same as those used in several other countries. It could be attributed to potential biological or ethnic differences.

The other variants identified were e19a2 in Algeria and Nigeria, which accounted for less than 2% of the population. Nigeria also reported one case of the e1a2 variant. Samia et al. reported 15% of other variants in Tunisia, including e1a2, e13a3, e6a2, and e19a2 [22].

The prognosis of these variants based on disease severity and response to treatment remains controversial. Table 2 presents parameters related to white blood cells, platelets, and hemoglobin according to the main variants of the BCR‐ABL1 gene at the time of diagnosis. In Togo, abnormalities were more pronounced in patients with both types of transcripts, followed by patients with the e14a2 variant and finally those with the e13a2 variant with regard to white blood cells (p = 0.03). In Nigeria, a significantly higher platelet count was detected in patients with the e13a2 variant (531.1 × 109/L) than in those with the e14a2 variant (488.2 × 109/L) or the e13a2/e14a2 variant (320.7 × 109/L); p = 0.03. Table 2 shows no major differences between the transcripts with regard to age, sex, total white blood cell count, and neutrophil count. A significant correlation was observed between platelet counts and the major BCR‐ABL1 transcripts in Tunisia. The platelet count was higher in patients with the e14a2 transcript (681.2 × 109/L vs. 207 × 109/L; p = 0.001). In Burkina Faso, a significant difference between the main transcripts was observed only in white blood cells. Other studies conducted in Europe have reported certain disparities regarding white blood cell count, which was higher in patients with the e13a2 subtype, and platelet count, which was lower in these same patients [30, 31]. In Germany, no differences were observed with regard to age, sex, or the Euro risk score. A significant difference was observed between the e13a2 and e14a2 subtypes when comparing white blood cell counts (88 and 65 × 109/L, respectively; p < 0.001) and platelet counts (296 and 430 × 109/L, respectively; p < 0.001) at the time of diagnosis [26]. Regarding response to treatment with TKIs, various authors have reported that the e13a2 subtype was associated with a lower rate and a longer time to achieve a complete cytogenetic response [32], as well as a lower rate of major molecular response (MMR) and a longer time to achieve MMR in patients treated with imatinib in many studies worldwide [33, 34]. In many African settings, the diagnosis of CML still relies primarily on conventional cytogenetics or FISH, as PCR‐based molecular techniques are not widely available. However, cytogenetics and FISH can detect the Philadelphia chromosome or the BCR‐ABL1 fusion, but cannot identify transcript variants such as e13a2, e14a2, or e1a2. Identifying these variants requires PCR‐based molecular techniques. Furthermore, PCR‐based tests are also essential for molecular monitoring during TKI treatment, and their limited availability may affect patient management in resource‐limited settings.

Study Limitations

These findings are based on a limited number of countries and studies, which is a significant limitation. Indeed, while Africa has 54 countries, few studies met the inclusion criteria. Furthermore, some studies involve a small number of patients, which does not allow for definitive conclusions. This is likely due to the lack of molecular diagnostic tests in many countries and the heterogeneity of healthcare infrastructure across African nations. The limited availability of PCR may therefore introduce selection bias, as only a few specialized centers are capable of characterizing transcript variants. The molecular diagnostic method used is not standardized. Some methods, such as Gene Expert, do not allow for the precise identification of the different transcripts, but rather of the final protein, p210, which corresponds to two main transcripts, e13a2 and e14a2. Another limitation lies in the fact that the reports from several studies are incomplete.

Conclusion

There are few studies in Africa on the frequency of BCR‐ABL1 gene variants. The main transcripts were e14a2 and e13a2. The proportions of these variants vary by country, but in most cases, the e14a2 variant is more prevalent than the e13a2 variant. Some studies have reported that the e14a2 variant is associated with a less favorable prognosis and survival than the e13a2 variant. Age and sex did not appear to have an effect on transcript types in most countries. Certain associations were observed between BCR‐ABL1 variants and hematological parameters, particularly white blood cell and platelet counts. Access to treatment does not guarantee access to the necessary molecular monitoring of BCR‐ABL1. Many regions lack the infrastructure required to perform regular testing, which is crucial for managing the efficacy of TKIs.
