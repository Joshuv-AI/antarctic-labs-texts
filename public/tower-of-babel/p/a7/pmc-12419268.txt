The causal relationship between childhood obesity and adult oral health: A 2-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Hang Zhang, Libo Jin, Jun Wang, Haixia Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Childhood obesity is an escalating global public health concern with potential long-term implications for various health outcomes, including oral health. While the association between childhood obesity and systemic diseases is well-documented, its specific impact on adult oral health remains underexplored. This study utilized a 2-sample Mendelian randomization approach to explore the causal relationship between childhood obesity and several adult oral health conditions, including gingivitis, chronic periodontitis, dental caries, temporomandibular joint disorder, and malocclusion. Genetic instruments associated with childhood obesity were applied to assess their potential causal effect on these oral health outcomes. The analysis revealed a significant inverse association between childhood obesity and the risk of developing chronic periodontitis (P = .040, odds ratios [OR] = 0.871), temporomandibular joint disorder (P = .002, OR = 0.796), and malocclusion (P = .019, OR = 0.879) in adulthood, suggesting a protective effect. However, no significant associations were found between childhood obesity and gingivitis (P = .799, OR = 0.968) or dental caries (P = .603, OR = 0.970). This study provides novel insights into the relationship between childhood obesity and adult oral health, indicating a complex and potentially protective role of childhood obesity against certain oral health conditions. These findings highlight the importance of a nuanced approach when considering the long-term health implications of childhood obesity in public health strategies.

1. Introduction

Childhood obesity has become a critical global public health problem, with its long-term effects on individual health drawing widespread attention. In addition to being closely associated with metabolic diseases such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, childhood obesity may significantly impact adult oral health. Oral health issues, including gingivitis, chronic periodontitis, dental caries, temporomandibular joint disorder (TMD), and malocclusion, not only affect quality of life but also have adverse effects on overall health.[1] Previous epidemiological studies suggest that obesity may increase the risk of oral diseases through mechanisms such as systemic inflammation and metabolic disorders.[2] However, observational studies often struggle to eliminate confounding factors and reverse causality, limiting the accurate understanding of the causal relationship between obesity and oral health.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a genetic epidemiology method that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to overcome confounding factors and reverse causality issues in observational studies, thus providing more reliable inferences about causal relationships between exposure and outcomes.[3] The core of MR is to use genetic variants associated with the exposure factor (e.g., single nucleotide polymorphisms, or SNPs, related to obesity), which are randomly allocated in nature, similar to randomization in controlled trials, thereby reducing the influence of confounding factors.[4] In recent years, MR has been widely used to study the causal relationships between obesity and related diseases, yielding significant findings. For example, MR studies have revealed causal relationships between obesity and cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and certain cancers, providing new perspectives on the health impacts of obesity.[5]

However, research on the causal relationship between childhood obesity and adult oral health is relatively scarce, necessitating further exploration and validation. Childhood is a critical period for growth and development, and obesity during this time may significantly affect the development and health of oral tissues, potentially increasing the risk of oral diseases in adulthood. Using the MR method, we can more accurately evaluate the potential causal impact of childhood obesity on adult oral health and uncover the underlying biological mechanisms, providing scientific evidence for public health policy-making and individual health management.

This study aims to use the MR method to explore the causal relationship between childhood obesity and adult oral health issues, including gingivitis, chronic periodontitis, dental caries, TMD, and malocclusion, filling a gap in current research and providing references for future studies.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and data sources

This study employs the 2-sample MR method to investigate the causal relationship between childhood obesity and adult oral health. The research flowchart is shown in Figure 1.

Three key instrumental variable assumptions of 2-sample Mendelian randomization.

The study is based on several assumptions[6,7]: first, genetic variants are closely related to childhood obesity; second, genetic variants are unrelated to confounding factors; finally, genetic variants influence adult oral health solely through childhood obesity and not through other pathways. The GWAS data used in this study are from the IEU Open GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk). Oral health issues including gingivitis, chronic periodontitis, dental caries, TMD, and malocclusion were included as outcome traits, and these data were also based on the IEU Open GWAS database. 2.2. Selection of instrumental variables

SNPs meeting the following criteria were selected as instrumental variables: SNPs should be significantly associated with childhood obesity (P < 1 × 10−6); SNPs should be independent of each other to avoid the effects of linkage disequilibrium, that is, R2 < 0.001 and genetic distance kb > 10000; SNPs should not be associated with any confounding factors and outcomes. We calculated the F-statistic to assess the strength of the instruments, with an F-statistic > 10 indicating sufficient strength.[8] The formula for calculation is: F = beta2/SE2.[9]

2.3. Mendelian randomization analysis

The Mendelian randomization analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.4.0), with the “TwoSampleMR” and “MendelianRandomization” packages. The primary method employed for assessing causal effects was the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method. Additionally, 4 complementary methods—MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode—were utilized to ensure robustness of the findings. The results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). For sensitivity analyses, Cochran Q test was applied to evaluate heterogeneity, and the MR-Egger intercept was used to detect potential pleiotropy.[10] A leave-one-out analysis was also performed to assess the robustness of the results.

3. Results

This study assessed the causal relationship between childhood obesity and 5 adult oral health issues using the MR method. These health issues include chronic periodontitis, TMD, dental caries, malocclusion (including occlusal misalignment), and chronic gingivitis. The analysis methods used include MR-Egger, weighted median, IVW, simple mode, and weighted mode.

We identified 6 SNPs from the GWAS data of the exposure factors, all of which were strongly associated with childhood obesity (P < 1 × 10−6) and were independent of each other. We used the LDtrait Tool (https://ldlink.nih.gov/?tab=ldtrait) to search for phenotypes related to the SNPs, and no confounding factors associated with childhood obesity and adult oral health were found. All the F-statistics for the outcome data were >10, indicating they were not weak instruments (Table 2).

4. Discussion

Childhood obesity is becoming an increasingly serious issue worldwide. Research has shown that obesity not only directly affects health during childhood but also has long-term impacts on various health conditions in adulthood.[11] However, studies on the causal relationship between childhood obesity and adult oral health are relatively rare. This study used the 2-sample MR method to investigate the causal relationship between childhood obesity and adult oral health, providing new insights into this field.

The results indicate that childhood obesity is significantly negatively associated with adult chronic periodontitis, TMD, and malocclusion, suggesting a protective effect of childhood obesity on these conditions. This finding is consistent with some studies suggesting that obesity may influence chronic inflammation through immune system and metabolic pathway regulation.[12,13]

Childhood obesity has the potential to reshape adult immune responses via the endocrine function of adipose tissue. In obese children, adipose tissue secretes a range of cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α and interleukin-6, among others.[14] These inflammatory factors initiate a chronic, low-grade inflammatory state in the immune system during childhood. As individuals age, this inflammatory state persists and can exert a reshaping effect on the local immune microenvironment of the oral cavity.[15] For instance, in the onset and progression of chronic periodontitis, a normal immune response is crucial for resisting bacterial infections. Alterations in inflammatory cytokine levels due to childhood obesity may modify the immune response of periodontal tissue to bacteria in adulthood, potentially enhancing resistance to periodontal pathogens and thereby reducing the risk of chronic periodontitis. Furthermore, obesity may also impact the development and function of immune cells, such as T cells and B cells, bolstering their immune surveillance and defense capabilities against oral diseases in adulthood.[16]

From the perspective of skeletal development, childhood obesity can potentially impact the bone structure and function in adulthood by influencing the secretion and function of hormones such as growth hormone and insulin-like growth factors (IGFs). Obese children frequently exhibit insulin resistance, which disrupts the normal secretion and signaling pathways of IGFs.[17] IGFs are pivotal in the growth, remodeling, and maintenance of bones.[18] In the context of TMD syndrome and dental malocclusion, abnormal bone development stands out as a significant causative factor. Hormonal alterations resulting from childhood obesity may give rise to abnormalities in the growth of oral-related bones, including the maxilla and mandible, such as alterations in growth rate and direction. Consequently, these changes can affect the structure and function of the temporomandibular joint in adulthood, potentially lowering the risk of developing TMD syndrome.[19]

Regarding the association between childhood obesity and the development of adult gingivitis and dental caries, our study did not observe significant causal relationships. The impact of obesity on these conditions appears to be mediated predominantly through indirect pathways, such as dietary patterns and oral hygiene practices.[20,21] While children with obesity may exhibit differences in oral health maintenance behaviors, our findings suggest no substantial direct causal effect of obesity itself on gingivitis or dental caries pathogenesis.

Our research findings reveal that childhood obesity may have a protective effect on certain oral health problems in adulthood, particularly in chronic periodontitis, temporomandibular joint disorder syndrome, and dental malocclusion. Therefore, when formulating public health strategies, childhood obesity cannot be viewed solely as a single negative health factor. For oral health prevention, it is necessary to reassess the potential impact of current interventions targeting childhood obesity on oral health. For example, some widely implemented interventions for childhood obesity may focus on limiting calorie intake and increasing exercise to lose weight, but when considering the protective effects of childhood obesity on certain oral diseases, it is necessary to weigh the oral health risks that weight loss may bring when formulating these policies. We can explore more precise intervention strategies that can control other health risks associated with childhood obesity, such as cardiovascular disease risk, while also minimizing its beneficial effects on oral health. In addition, the content of public health education should also be adjusted accordingly. In addition to promoting the harm of childhood obesity, the public should also be informed of its possible protective effect on oral health, and the comprehensive understanding of the relationship between childhood obesity and health should be improved.

5. Limitations

This study relies on data from the IEU Open GWAS database, which might not represent all populations. The identified genetic variations may not be applicable to different ethnic groups. Although SNPs were carefully selected as instrumental variables, there may still be unaccounted confounding factors, and the assumption that these variants affect oral health solely through childhood obesity does not fully capture the complexity of genetic influences. The study did not account for the timing and duration of childhood obesity, which could influence the results. Additionally, while associations were identified, direct mechanistic insights were not provided, necessitating further biological research to elucidate these pathways.

6. Conclusion

This 2-sample MR study provides significant evidence that childhood obesity may have a protective effect on certain adult oral health issues. However, it seems to have no significant impact on gingivitis and dental caries. These findings highlight the need for further research into the impact of childhood obesity on adult health and suggest that intervention strategies should consider its multifaceted health effects.

Future research should include longitudinal and mechanistic studies to better understand the relationship between childhood obesity and oral health and validate these findings across diverse populations. Investigating early intervention measures’ effects on adult oral health will provide a basis for comprehensive public health policies, promoting targeted interventions that address both obesity prevention and oral health improvement.
