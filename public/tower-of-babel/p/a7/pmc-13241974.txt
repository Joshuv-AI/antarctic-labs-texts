Risk prediction models of compassion fatigue among nurses: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yuxin Yang, Liyang Chang, Tianyu Huang, Xiaodan Pei, Yanjuan Li, Haifang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCompassion fatigue has serious consequences for nursing staff, patients and healthcare organizations. The development of predictive models specifically designed for nurses' compassion fatigue has received considerable attention in recent years, the diversity of high-risk factors makes them difficult to measure and use in clinical settings, and the quality and applicability of these models have not been systematically reviewed.ObjectiveThis systematic review evaluates existing risk prediction models for nurse compassion fatigue, assesses their performance and clinical utility to guide early risk identification and model optimization.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies.MethodsThe Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Embase, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, PubMed, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Database, China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP), and Chinese Biomedical Database (CBM) were searched from inception to January 21, 2026. Data from selected studies were extracted, including study design, data source, outcome definition, sample size, predictors, model development and performance. The Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) checklist was used to assess the risk of bias and applicability.ResultsA total of 1268 studies were retrieved, and after the selection process, ten prediction models from seven studies were included in this review. Seven models used logistic regression, while the remaining respectively utilized a nomogram, decision tree, and random forest. Only one study conducted both internal and external validation. Social support, job satisfaction, job stress and working years were important predictors of compassion fatigue among nurses. The incidence of compassion fatigue among clinical nurses ranged from 49.41 % to 83.88 %. The reported area under the curve (AUC) ranged from 0.704 to 0.939. Most studies were found to have a high risk of bias, primarily due to poor reporting of the statistical analysis domain. The pooled AUC value of the six validated models was 0.819 (95 % confidence interval=0.746∼0.892), indicating a fair level of discrimination.ConclusionsAlthough the included studies reported a certain level of predictive performance in the compassion fatigue prediction model for nurses, most of them were found to have a high risk of bias according to the PROBAST checklist. Future research should prioritize the development of new models with larger sample sizes, rigorous study designs, and multi-center external validation.RegistrationThe study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42024504226).Keywords: Compassion fatigue, Nurses, Risk prediction model, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Background

The term compassion fatigue originated in trauma psychology and refers to a coexisting state of secondary traumatic stress (STS) and burnout (BO) experienced by caregivers due to prolonged empathy for patients' physical and psychological suffering (Figley, 1995). Although its symptoms overlap with STS and BO, compassion fatigue has distinct conceptual foundations. Initially described by Joinson (1992) in the context of emergency nurses, it was characterized as physical, emotional, and spiritual exhaustion resulting from chronic workplace stress and repeated exposure to patients' grief, anxiety, and distress (Joinson, 1992). Figley later conceptualized it as "the cost of caring" (Figley, 2002). Over time, the definition has evolved, allowing clearer differentiation from STS and BO. For instance, Coetzee reframed compassion fatigue as a progressive decline in empathetic capacity, marked by accumulating empathetic strain—yet balanced by compassion satisfaction, a positive counterforce that enhances resilience and facilitates recovery (Coetzee and Klopper, 2010).

By its very nature, nursing is a profession that demands both clinical expertise and significant emotional labor, placing nurses at heightened risk for compassion fatigue (Cavanagh et al., 2020). Recent studies estimated its prevalence among nurses to range from 23 % to 60 % (Missouridou et al., 2022; O'Callaghan et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2023), with particularly high rates (40 %∼62.8 %) in high-pressure departments such as emergency, intensive care unit, palliative care unit, oncology, and psychiatry (Algamdi, 2022; Duarte and Pinto-Gouveia, 2017; Wijdenes et al., 2019). Substantial evidence links compassion fatigue to severe health consequences, including insomnia, depression, emotional exhaustion, and impaired cognitive function (e.g., poor concentration and judgment), as well as diminished professional fulfillment (Alruwaili et al., 2024; Baqeas et al., 2021; Bilgiç et al., 2022). Critically, compassion fatigue also compromises patient care quality, manifesting in negative coping behaviors, strained nurse-patient relationships, and increased medical conflicts. Organizational impacts are equally concerning, with compassion fatigue correlating to higher turnover rates and reduced productivity (Alruwaili et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2023). Thus, developing robust risk prediction and early identification of compassion fatigue is imperative to inform timely interventions.

A risk prediction model may be used for individual risk stratification and clinical outcome prediction by leveraging risk factors, which is helpful for taking targeted interventions to effectively improve outcomes (Steyerberg et al., 2010). Although the development of predictive models specifically designed for nurses' compassion fatigue has received considerable attention in recent years, the existing landscape of these models is characterized by significant methodological heterogeneity and limited validation. Key limitations include substantial variations in predictor selection, inconsistent data quality due to different measurement tools, and insufficient reporting of model development details, all of which hinder a systematic evaluation of their rigor, generalizability, and clinical applicability. In direct response to these issues, this study systematically reviews existing compassion fatigue prediction models for nurses, analyzing their development processes (e.g., variable selection, statistical methods), performance metrics, and validation approaches (internal/external). By synthesizing these findings, we aim to inform evidence-based clinical strategies and guide future model optimization.

Methods

The study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (registration number: CRD42024504226).

Search strategy

We performed a preliminary search of relevant literature on the risk prediction model of clinical nurses’ compassion fatigue in both Chinese and English databases to identify appropriate keywords and search terms, and found that no similar systematic review had been published before. The databases searched included Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Embase, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, PubMed, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Database, China Science and Technology Journal Database (VIP) and Chinese Biomedical Database (CBM), which were searched from the establishment of the databases until January 21, 2026. The following MeSH terms and free-text terms were applied to title, abstract, and subject heading fields, taking PubMed as an example:(Compassion Fatigue[Mesh] OR Secondary Trauma*[Title/Abstract] OR Vicarious Trauma*[Title/Abstract] OR Secondary Traumatic Stress[Title/Abstract])AND(Nurses[Mesh] OR nursing personnel[Title/Abstract] OR registered nurses[Title/Abstract])AND(forecasting[Title/Abstract] OR risk assessment[Title/Abstract] OR risk prediction[Title/Abstract] OR risk score[Title/Abstract] OR prediction model[Title/Abstract] OR nomograms[Title/Abstract]). The complete search details were provided in Appendix 1. We also identified and tracked the reference list of the retrieved literature as well as the grey literature to avoid omissions.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for studies were: (1) studies involving the target population were registered nurses on clinical practice, with no restrictions on department, gender, race or nationality; (2) an observational study design(cohort, case-control and cross-sectional study); (3) reported a prediction model; (4) the outcome of interest was compassion fatigue.

The exclusion criteria were: (1) a mixed sample of healthcare practitioners, or only nursing technicians, auxiliary nurses or nursing students were investigated, which did not clearly define the independent data from nurses; (2) the model contains <2 predictors; (3) not written in English or Chinese; (4) Duplicate literature; (5) abstracts of conferences, systematic reviews and other literature, where the original text or complete data cannot be obtained.

Study selection and screening

Two researchers (YYX and HTY) independently screened the studies based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Initially, we used the NoteExpress software to import and manage search results. After deleting duplicates, we read the titles and abstracts for a preliminary screening to determine their eligibility. Then we examined the full-texts of studies and based on the critical appraisal and data extraction for systematic reviews of prediction modelling studies(CHARMS) checklist to select all eligible studies and identify any potentially relevant research (Moons, 2014). During the screening process of the studies, the resolution of disagreements was achieved through discussion or seeking the opinions of a third researcher.

Data extraction

Two researchers (YYX and HTY) independently used Microsoft Excel datasheets to extract information from the final selected articles. The discrepancies were resolved by discussion or seeking the assistance of a third researcher (CLY). The information extracted based on the CHARMS checklist was divided into two categories (Moons, 2014): (1) Basic information: encompassed details including the author, year of publication, research design, participants, data source, and sample size. (2) Model information: included information related to the prediction model such as the model development method, model performance measures, model validation type, model presentation forms, the variable selection method, processing continuous variables method, handling of missing data and final predictors used in the model.

Quality assessment

In order to evaluate the risk of bias and applicability of the selected studies, the prediction model risk of bias assessment tool (PROBAST) was employed (Moons, 2019). The discrepancies were resolved by discussion or seeking the assistance of a third researcher (CLY). Two researchers (YYX and HTY) independently assessed the following contents: (1) the risk of bias comprised four aspects, including 20 questions such as research subjects, predictors, results and statistical analysis. Each question can be answered with five options: "Yes", "possibly", "no", "possibly not", and "no information". If any question is answered as "no" or "possibly not", the aspect to which the question belongs should be considered to be at high risk of bias. Only when all aspects are judged as low risk of bias, the overall risk of bias can be considered low risk. (2) the applicability is mainly judged based on the first three aspects (research subjects, predictors and results), each aspect is assessed as "high", "low" or "unclear". If the applicability of any one aspect is low, the overall applicability is considered low.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

A meta-analysis of the area under the curve (AUC) values from the prediction models was conducted using Medcalc 22.0 software. The AUC index provides a measure of discrimination of the model, with values of <0.7, 0.7–0.8, and >0.8 indicating low, moderate, and high discrimination, respectively (Ruscio and Mullen, 2012). If the study only reports the 95 %CI of the AUC without reporting the standard error (SE), the potential SE should be estimated by dividing the width of the 95 %CI by 3.92 (Hanley and McNeil, 1982). Heterogeneity was examined using the I2 index and the Cochrane Q test. If I2<50 % and P > 0.1, it indicates small heterogeneity and a fixed-effects model was used for analysis; conversely, a random-effects model was adopted for analysis (Higgins et al., 2003).

For predictors that were not suitable for meta-analysis, the descriptive analysis method was adopted. Publication bias was analyzed using Egger's test. The test level (α) of the meta-analysis was 0.05, and the value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 1268 studies were retrieved from various databases. After a layer-by-layer screening process, there were ultimately 8 remaining studies (Han, 2025; Liang et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023; Wang, 2020; Wang et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2022). Assessment of quality

Table 3 summarizes the risk of bias and applicability in the included studies. In terms of the risk of bias assessment, except one study was at low risk (Sun et al., 2023), the remaining seven studies were at high risk (Han, 2025; Liang et al., 2024; Wang, 2020; Wang et al., 2024; Xie, 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2022), indicating methodological problems in the development or validation processes. The statistical analysis was the main cause of high risk. Among them, three studies had insufficient sample sizes (Liang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2022), unable to meet the recommended guideline of having >20 events per variable (EPV>20), none of the studies provided information regarding the missing data, four studies may converted continuous variables into categorical variables either partially or completely (Han, 2025; Liang et al., 2024; Wang, 2020; Zou et al., 2022), seven studies did not avoid selecting variables based on univariate analysis (Han, 2025; Liang et al., 2024; Wang, 2020; Wang et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2022), and six studies did not comprehensively assess the predictive performance of their prediction models (Han, 2025; Liang et al., 2024; Wang, 2020; Wang et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2022).

In terms of the assessment of applicability risk, seven studies were classified as having a low risk (Han, 2025; Liang et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023; Wang, 2020; Xie et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2022), while one study had a high risk (Wang et al., 2024). In the predictor domain, two studies was categorized as having a high risk of applicability due to concerns about the timing and acquisition of predictor measurements. In the outcome domain, one study had a high risk of applicability as the outcomes included not only compassion fatigue but also secondary trauma and burnout, and the relationship between predictors and compassion fatigue is not clearly demonstrated.

Meta-analysis of models included in the review

Due to insufficient reporting on the development details of the models in the included studies, only six studies were eligible for synthesis (Liang et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2023; Wang, 2020; Xie et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025; Zou et al., 2022). Among these, Zhao et al.'s research involved multiple methods for model development, all of which were based on the same sample, so only the model developed using logistic regression was included (Zhao et al., 2025). The pooled AUC was calculated using a random effects model, resulting in a value of 0.819 (95 % confidence interval=0.746∼0.892) (Fig. 2). The I2 value was 98.68 % (p < 0.001), indicating a high degree of heterogeneity among the studies. After excluding the models showing extreme AUC such as Zhao et al.'s research and Sun et al.'s research (Sun et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025), the results of the random effects meta-analysis were AUC=0.803(95 %CI=0.773∼0.833), and the I2 value was 0 %(P = 0.445), suggesting that the results were stable. The Egger's test suggests no significant publication bias(p = 0.173).

Forest plot of the random effects meta-analysis of pooled AUC estimates for 6 models.

Descriptive analysis results

For predictors reported in only a single study or those with data types unsuitable for meta-analysis, we conducted descriptive analyses. Based on the Job Demands-Resources (JDR) model (Lui et al., 2018), these potential predictors of nurses' compassion fatigue in included studies were categorized as follows:(1) Job demands: work experience (years), work pressure, job satisfaction, working hours, department, willingness to re-choose nursing profession, workplace violence. (2) Job resources: personal resources including personality, psychological resilience, stress coping strategies, emotional intelligence, mindfulness; and organizational resources including social support. (3) Demographic factors: income level, marital status. (4) Health behaviors: sleep duration, sleep quality, dietary habits. In this study, social support, job satisfaction, job stress and working years were the more frequently occurring predictors of nurses’ compassion fatigue.

Discussion

This systematic review indicated that while the existing prediction models demonstrate well (AUC > 0.70), they do have certain limitations. Some studies suffer from insufficient sample sizes, simplistic variable selection methods, incomplete reporting of model calibration and validation, and a lack of dynamic predictive capability. The review further identifies key predictors such as working years and social support, their mechanisms of influence require further validation. However, all included studies are confined to the single medical-cultural context of China, which limits the generalizability of the results to some extent. Overall, research on predictive models for nurses' compassion fatigue remains in a developmental stage and necessitates ongoing refinement in methodological rigor, cross-cultural validation, and dynamic modeling.

The prediction model for nurses' compassion fatigue awaits further development and exploration

All included studies were published within the last five years, indicating that predictive models for nurse compassion fatigue are still in the preliminary exploratory stage. Evaluation revealed that the predictive models constructed in these studies demonstrated satisfactory performance, with AUC values all exceeding 0.70. However, except for one study with a low risk of bias, the remaining studies showed good adaptability and exhibited relatively high overall bias risks, particularly in statistical analysis: (1) Generally, the sample size required for model development primarily referenced the events per variable (EPV), when EPV > 20, the risk of model overfitting is reduced, and the likelihood of including spurious predictors or omitting important variables is minimized (Ogundimu et al., 2016). Nevertheless, three included studies had EPV < 20, suggesting that prospective studies with large sample sizes be conducted in the future. (2) Six studies screened variables based on univariate analysis, potentially overlooking interaction effects between variables, which may lead to the exclusion of critical data. It is recommended to employ diverse data analysis methods, optimize variable selection through cross-validation, and incorporate expert opinions to assess clinical applicability and generalizability, thereby enhancing predictive accuracy and model robustness (Aljrees, 2024). (3) Four studies failed to report model calibration methods, making it impossible to evaluate the consistency between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes. (4) Only one study conducted both internal and external validation, while the performance and generalizability of the remaining models require further verification. The current transparency in reporting model outcomes remains insufficient, hindering the assessment of predictive performance and practical utility. Researchers should adhere to established methodologies and reporting guidelines for predictive models to improve study quality (Collins et al., 2015). Furthermore, the improvement of compassion fatigue (CF) is time-dependent, yet existing models can only predict compassion fatigue probability at specific time points. No model currently exists to predict the dynamic changes in compassion fatigue probability over time.

Predictors are of great significance to the development of risk prediction models

Identifying predictors of compassion fatigue is a critical step in developing predictive models. Our systematic review included 7 studies encompassing 9 distinct models, and found no consensus in predictor selection. Firstly, the impact of certain predictors on nurses' compassion fatigue remains contentious. For instance, some studies suggest a positive correlation between longer clinical working tenure and compassion fatigue, attributing this to cumulative emotional exhaustion from prolonged exposure to traumatic events (Aslan et al., 2022). Conversely, other scholars argue that novice nurses, facing dual pressures of career development and clinical inexperience, may lack effective coping mechanisms for stressors, thereby increasing the susceptibility of compassion fatigue (Mealer et al., 2017). This discrepancy underscores the urgent need for multi-center, large-sample studies to clarify this relationship and to account for career-stage characteristics in future model development.

Second, most existing models rely primarily on univariate analysis for variable selection, an approach that may overlook interaction effects and omit key predictors. We recommend adopting multivariate selection methods such as LASSO regression and incorporating theoretical frameworks like the Job Demands-Resources (JDR) model to enhance model performance. Our review found that predictors included in current models generally align with the JDR model, wherein excessive job demands deplete nurses' emotional resources, while job resources (e.g., social support, work environment, psychological resilience) mitigate compassion fatigue (Mazzetti et al., 2023). Combining robust variable selection methods with validated theoretical frameworks can better capture the complex etiology of compassion fatigue.

Moreover, high levels of compassion fatigue are associated with unhealthy lifestyle patterns, among which sleep serves as a critical influencing factor (Vancampfort and Mugisha, 2022). Our systematic review reveals that only a few prediction models have incorporated sleep-related factors, likely because accurate sleep assessment requires both subjective perceptions and objective measures, making data collection challenging. Therefore, future research could prioritize pragmatically measurable predictors or integrate digital assessments (e.g., wearable sleep trackers) to balance accuracy with feasibility.

The risk of compassion fatigue among nurses is relatively high

This systematic review of the included prediction model indicated that the incidence of compassion fatigue among nurses is high, ranging from 49.41 % to 83.88 %. This elevated prevalence may be attributed to several factors. Most of the included studies were conducted in tertiary hospitals located in major urban centers in China, which typically manage more complex cases and higher patient volumes compared to other healthcare settings. Furthermore, the study populations largely focused on nurses working in high-stress specialties such as emergency and geriatric departments, where the risk of compassion fatigue is inherently greater. Additionally, the unique sociocultural and healthcare context in China likely contributes to this heightened risk. Nursing professionals in China operate under stringent work standards designed to ensure quality of care, often within a culture that emphasizes endurance, suppression of negative emotions in the workplace, and a tendency to adopt avoidant coping strategies in response to occupational stress. (Natália and Júlia, 2022) These factors may collectively elevate nurses' vulnerability to compassion fatigue.

It is considerable that all included studies were conducted in China. This geographical concentration may be partly explained by national policy initiatives that promote the application of big data and artificial intelligence technologies in healthcare, which have spurred a significant increase in predictive modeling research within China. On the other hand, from the perspective of our inclusion criteria, studies from other countries often involve broader healthcare populations—encompassing nurses, interns, physicians, and psychotherapists—and were therefore excluded due to our focus on clinically registered nurses only. Therefore, future research should prioritize the development and validation of prediction models across diverse cultural and healthcare systems. This approach will support the creation of more generalizable tools for the early identification of compassion fatigue risk among nurses.

Limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review of risk prediction models for clinical nurses' compassion fatigue. It systematically collected, screened, and extracted extensive data, including general characteristics, influencing factors, and study outcomes, to compare and synthesize the performance of these models. However, there were several potential limitations in this systematic review. First of all, seven included studies were conducted in mainland China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to Western populations, and adjustments may be needed when applying these models in different regions. Thus, it is significant for future research to develop risk prediction models for nurses’ compassion fatigue in diverse departments. Secondly, due to the reporting transparency and methodological differences of the included studies, only five models were included in our meta-analysis. This may lead to the issues that the sources of heterogeneity between studies could not be further discussed and the low power of publication bias test. More rigorous methodologies and more transparent reporting are needed in the future. Lastly, as only studies published in English and Chinese were included in this review, there may be issues related to language barriers, indicating a certain degree of selection bias.

Conclusion

This systematic review, comprising eight studies with ten models, demonstrated that the pooled AUC of six models suitable for meta-analysis was 0.819(95 %CI=0.746∼0.892), indicating a certain level of predictive performance. However, seven included studies were assessed as having a high risk of bias according to PROBAST, and one study raised concerns regarding applicability. The current prediction models for compassion fatigue in nurses do not meet the PROBAST standards, and the variable selection was based on univariate analysis methods, which may lead to incorrect screening of predictors. Therefore, researchers need to familiarize themselves with the PROBAST checklist and adhere to the reporting guidelines outlined in the TRIPOD statement to improve the quality of future studies. Future research should prioritize the development of new models with larger sample sizes, rigorous study designs, and multi-center external validation.
