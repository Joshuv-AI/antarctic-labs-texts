NRP1 knockdown inhibits the invasion and migration of rhabdoid tumor of the kidney cells
Authors: Bin Yamaoka, Eri Nagasaki-Maeoka, Shota Uekusa, Eri Muto-Fujita, Naoko Abe, Kyoko Fujiwara, Tsugumichi Koshinaga, Shuichiro Uehara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Rhabdoid tumor of the kidney (RTK) is an aggressive renal neoplasm that accounts for < 2% of pediatric renal tumors [1]. The origin of this tumor has not yet been elucidated [2]. A loss of function of SMARCB1 (INI1/SNF5/BAF47) is a typical genetic abnormality in rhabdoid tumors, irrespective of the anatomic origin [3]. However, there are only a few reports on the molecular pathogenesis of RTK. The main treatment approaches for patients with RTK are surgery, postoperative radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. However, currently, a uniform standard treatment for RTK has not been established [4]. The survival of patients with RTK is generally low with a 5-year overall survival rate of 26%; furthermore, disease progression and death occur early, with a median time from diagnosis to relapse of 8 months [5]. It is necessary to elucidate the molecular mechanisms involved in the development and progression of RTK to develop more effective treatment strategies.

Recent studies have demonstrated that noncoding RNAs, such as microRNAs (miRNAs), exhibit anti-oncogenic or oncogenic functions in various cancers. MicroRNAs, single-stranded noncoding RNA molecules consisting of 18–20 nucleotides [6], play a critical role in post-transcriptional regulation of genes. They function as adaptors for the miRNA-induced silencing complex to inhibit gene expression by targeting messenger RNAs (mRNAs) for translational repression and/or cleavage [7]. Considering that over 60% of human genes are predicted to be regulated by this process, miRNAs likely control all cellular, physiological, and development processes [8, 9]. Consequently, aberrant miRNA–mRNA expression is common in various cancers, and tumor-specific miRNA–mRNA networks have been useful in identifying prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for cancers, including RTK [10–13].

MicroRNAs are synthesized in cells, and some of them are then loaded into exosomes, a form of endosome-derived extracellular vesicles [14]. The miRNAs contained in exosomes (exosomal miRNAs) reflect the physiological state of host cells [15]. In our previous study, we performed miRNA sequencing (miRNA-seq) to determine exosomal miRNA expression profiles and identified RTK-specific exosomal miRNAs as novel biomarkers for RTK [16]. In the present study, we performed mRNA sequencing (mRNA-seq) to determine the mRNA expression profiles of RTK-derived cell lines. Using this profile and the above-mentioned exosomal miRNA expression profile of RTK, we performed an integrated analysis to detect candidate oncogenes of RTK and evaluated their roles in RTK development in vitro.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

The human RTK-derived cell lines WT-CLS1 and G401 were obtained from the Cell Lines Service (Eppelheim, Germany) and Health Science Research Resources Bank (Osaka, Japan), respectively. The normal human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T served as the control, was obtained from Invitrogen (Waltham, MA, USA). WT-CLS1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Nichirei Bioscience, Tokyo, Japan) and 1% N-2-hydroxyethylpiperazine-N-2-ethane sulfonic acid (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). G401 was cultured in McCoy’s 5A modified medium (Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% FBS. HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. All media contained 100 IU/ml penicillin (Nacalai Tesque) and 100 μl/ml streptomycin (Nacalai Tesque). The cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. All cell lines were authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling methods.

Integrated miRNA–mRNA sequencing analysis and bioinformatic analysis

miRNA-seq was performed following a previously reported method [16]. For mRNA-seq, the quality of the total RNA extracted from the cells was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. NovaSeq6000 sequencing (Illumina) was performed in the 100-bp paired-end read mode. The quality of the read data was assessed using the FastQC software. The mRNA sequencing data were aligned to the reference human genome sequence (Human UCSC hg38) using STAR 2.7.5c. Each mRNA was annotated based on Ensembl Genes. The quantified data were normalized with Trimmed mean of M-value (TMM) normalization method using StrandNGS 3.4. The fold-change in mRNA expression` between the samples was calculated. All analyses were performed in triplicate. mRNAs with |fold change|> 2 and corrected P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

PITA, TargetScan, and microRNAorg were used to predict miRNA-targeted mRNAs. The open-source software platform Cytoscape (http://cytoscape.org) was used to visualize the miRNA–mRNA networks. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed using the Protein ANalysis THrough Evolutionary Relationships (PANTHER) Classification System (http://www.pantherdb.org/) to determine the functions of candidate genes based on biological processes. Fisher’s exact test was used to classify GO categories, and false discovery rate was calculated to correct the P-value. We selected only GO terms with P < 0.05.

Knockdown of NRP1 using siRNA

WT-CLS1 or G401 cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/ml, and immediately transfected with NRP1 siRNA or control siRNA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The target sequence of NRP1 siRNA was 5′-AGCAAAAGAAGGTTT-3′. Silencer Select Negative Control #1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), whose target sequence information was not available, was used as the control siRNA.

Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction

The total RNA was extracted from the cells using the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For cDNA synthesis, 1000 ng of total RNA was reverse transcribed using the iScript cDNA synthesis system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Quantitative real time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using the SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM system (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. A dilution series of the cDNA mixture was prepared and used as a template for real-time PCR to obtain a standard curve for each gene. Three independent measurements were performed. Primer sets used for Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) amplification were as follows: NRP1, 5′-ATGCGAATGGCTGATTCAGG-3′ (sense) and 5′-TCCATCGAAGACTTCCACGTAG-3′ (antisense); GAPDH, 5′-TCACCAGGGCTGCTTTTAAC-3′ (sense) and 5′-TGACGGTGCCATGGAATTTG-3′ (antisense). The housekeeping gene GAPDH was used as the internal reference. Quantitative PCR was carried out with an initial denaturation step for 2 min at 94 °C followed by 40 cycles at 94 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s using the Thermal Cycler Dice Real Time System TP800 (Takara Bio Inc.).

Western blot analysis

The cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque). The protein concentration was measured using a Bio-Rad DC kit (Bio-Rad). Twenty micrograms of protein was separated on NuPAGE 4–12% Bis–Tris gels (Life Technologies) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The membranes were blocked with Blocking-One (Nacalai Tesque) for 1 h at 25 °C, and treated with rabbit monoclonal antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA) for NRP1 (catalog no. D62C6; 3725S; dilution, 1:500) or β-actin (catalog no. 13E5; 4970S; dilution, 1:1,000) at 4 °C. After 24 h of incubation, the membranes were washed with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBS-T) and subsequently incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for rabbit IgG (catalog no. NA934-1ML; dilution, 1:2,000; GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Buckinghamshire, UK) for 1 h at 25 °C. The membranes were washed with TBS-T and treated with Chemi-Lumi One Super (Nacalai Tesque) to visualize immunoreactive signals using a luminescent image analyzer (LAS 4000 Mini; Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell viability analysis

WT-CLS1 or G401 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well, and immediately transfected with NRP1 siRNA or control siRNA, as described above. After 24, 48, and 72 h of incubation, cell viability was measured using the WST8 assay with Cell Count Reagent CF (Nacalai Tesque).

Matrigel invasion assay

Matrigel invasion assay was performed to evaluate the invasive ability of RTK cells. WT-CLS1 or G401 cells were seeded in cell culture inserts (Falcon, Durham, NC, USA) coated with human fibronectin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at a density of 7.5 × 104 cells/300 μl, and immediately transfected with NRP1 siRNA or control siRNA, as described above. The inserts were placed in 24-well plates for cell culture inserts (Falcon) filled with 700 μl of FBS-free medium per well. After 48 h, the non-invading cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed and the invading cells were fixed with methanol and stained with Giemsa (Muto Pure Medicals, Tokyo, Japan) for 1 min at 25 °C, followed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline. The invading cells in each membrane were counted in five microscopic fields under a light microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) at × 200 magnification. All analyses were performed in triplicate.

Wound healing cell migration assay

Wound healing assay was performed to evaluate the migratory ability of RTK cells. WT-CLS1 or G401 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well, and immediately transfected with NRP1 siRNA or control siRNA, as described above. At 24 h after transfection, cell layers were scratched using a Cell Scratch stick (AGC Techno Glass, Shizuoka, Japan), and the medium was replaced with fresh medium. After 24 h, the cells were photographed using a Leica DM IL phase-contrast microscope (Leica), and the width of the wounded areas was measured. All analyses were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using the JMP software ver. 11.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A median test was used to determine significant differences between groups, and Wilcoxon each pair test was used to analyze multiple groups. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. In all analyses, results with P < 0.05 were considered as statistically significant.

Results

miRNA–mRNA network of RTK cells was established

The overall flow of the integrated analysis of miRNA–mRNA sequencing is shown in Fig. 1. The results of the miRNA-seq analysis revealed that the expression of 97 exosomal miRNAs in both WT-CLS1 and G401 cells was twofold or less than that in HEK293T cells. In contrast, the expression of 27 exosomal miRNAs in both WT-CLS1 and G401 cells was twofold or higher than that in HEK293T cells. These differentially expressed miRNAs were used to predict the targeted mRNAs using PITA, TargetScan, and microRNAorg. A total of 565 targeted mRNAs of the above-mentioned 97 exosomal miRNAs whose expression was low in RTK were screened. In contrast, 189 targeted mRNAs of the 27 exosomal miRNAs whose expression was high in RTK were screened out. Together with these findings, the results of the mRNA-seq analysis revealed 40 mRNAs that were highly expressed in RTK cells targeted by exosomal miRNAs, the expression of which was lower in RTK cells than in HEK293T cells (Fig. 2). These 40 mRNAs were considered to have the potential to be oncogenes of RTK. In contrast, 28 mRNAs were expressed at low levels in RTK cells targeted by exosomal miRNAs, the expression of which was higher in RTK cells than in HEK293T cells.

NRP1 was selected as a candidate oncogene of RTK

To explore the potential biological functions of the differentially expressed mRNAs in RTK cells, we performed GO enrichment analysis. The results revealed no significantly enriched GO terms in the 28 mRNAs that were expressed at low levels in RTK cells targeted by exosomal miRNAs, the expression of which was higher in RTK cells than in HEK293T cells. In contrast, the 40 mRNAs that were highly expressed in RTK cells targeted by exosomal miRNAs, the expression of which was lower in RTK cells than in HEK293T cells, were mainly related to cell adhesion (Table 1). Of these mRNAs related to cell adhesion, we selected NRP1 as a candidate oncogene of RTK because upregulated NRP1 expression had been found to be associated with a poor prognosis in several types of tumors [17–19], and we considered that it may be true for RTK as well. In addition, miRNAs that target NRP1, whose expression was lower in RTK cells than in HEK293T cells, were identified as miR-130b-3p, miR-320c, miR-320d, miR-423-5p, miR-320a, miR-320b, miR-338-3p, and miR-185-5p (Fig. 2). The results of qRT-PCR and western blotting showed that the expression of NRP1 in RTK cells was significantly higher than that in the controls at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3a, b, WT-CLS1 vs. HEK293T: P = 0.0304, G401 vs. HEK293T: P = 0.0304).

siRNA-mediated knockdown of NRP1 inhibits the invasion and migration of RTK cells

To elucidate the function of NRP1 in RTK cells, we performed siRNA-mediated knockdown of NRP1 in RTK cells (Fig. 4a, b, WT-CLS1: P = 0.0253, G401: P = 0.0253) and observed no significant differences in cell viability between the cells in which NRP1 was knocked down and the control cells 24, 48, and 72 h after transfection (Fig. 4c–e, respectively).

Viability of RTK with NRP1 knockdown. WT-CLS1 cells and G401 cells were transfected with NRP1 siRNA or control siRNA. At 24 h after transfection, the total RNA and cell lysates were prepared and analyzed using a qRT-PCR and b western blotting. Cell viability was measured using the WST8 assay 24 c, 48 d, and 72 h e after transfection with NRP1 siRNA or control siRNA. The result showed that the viability of RTK cells in which NRP1 had been knocked down was not affected. Data are presented as mean ± SD of quadruplicate measurements. *P < 0.05

Matrigel invasion and wound-healing assays were also performed to evaluate the effect of NRP1 knockdown on cell invasion and migration abilities. Forty-eight hours after cell seeding in the invasion chamber, the RTK cells in which NRP1 was knocked down exhibited significantly smaller numbers of invading cells than the control (Fig. 5a, WT-CLS1: P = 0.0120, G401: P < 0.0001). Twenty-four hours after scratching the cell layer, the wound closure rate of WT-CLS1 cells in which NRP1 was knocked down was significantly smaller than that of the control cells. For G401 cells, there was no significant difference in the migratory ability between NRP1-knockdown and control cells; however, a similar decreasing tendency was observed (Fig. 5b, WT-CLS1: P = 0.0253). These results show that NRP1 knockdown inhibits the invasive and migratory abilities of RTK cells.

Discussion

In the present study, we performed an integrated analysis of mRNA-seq and miRNA-seq and identified 40 mRNAs that were highly expressed in RTK cells targeted by exosomal miRNAs, the expression of which was lower in RTK cells than in the controls. The GO enrichment analysis revealed that these highly expressed mRNAs were primarily related to cell adhesion. Of these mRNAs related to cell adhesion, we selected NRP1 as a candidate oncogene. The invasive and migratory abilities of NRP1-knockdown RTK cells were considerably suppressed. These findings indicate that NRP1 may have an oncogenic function in RTK.

NRP1 is a transmembrane glycoprotein that functions as a co-receptor for several extracellular ligands, including semaphorins [20], certain isoforms of vascular endothelial growth factor, such as placental growth factor (PlGF), and transforming growth factor beta [21–23]. NRP1 is an important regulator of nervous system development, immunity, angiogenesis, and tumorigenesis [24–27]. It is frequently overexpressed in several cancers and its upregulated expression indicates a poor prognosis and metastasis [17–19]. Based on these findings, NRP1 has been proposed as candidate therapeutic target for malignancies. Therefore, we hypothesized that NRP1 also acts as an oncogene and is a potential therapeutic target for RTK. For pediatric cancers, a phase I trial of TB-403, targeting the PlGF/NRP1 pathway, has been conducted on relapsed medulloblastoma, neuroblastoma, Ewing sarcoma, and alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma [23].

One of the important roles of NRP1 in tumorigenesis is to promote epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT). Epithelial–mesenchymal transition is a dominant biological process by which malignant tumor epithelial cells acquire migration and invasion abilities [28]. NRP1 enhances the migratory and invasive abilities of cancer stem cells by activating RhoA-dependent p38 MAPK signaling [29]. Our results indicate that high NRP1 expression induces EMT to promote the invasion and migration of RTK cells, resulting in frequent metastases and a poor prognosis.

In contrast, our integrated analysis showed that 8 miRNAs including miR-320 family members were expressed at low levels in RTK among the miRNAs that target NRP1 (Fig. 2). The results indicate that the aberrant expression of these miRNAs led to high expression of NRP1. Some of these miRNAs have been identified as tumor-suppressive miRNAs. For example, miR-320 downregulates the expression of NRP1 by binding to the 3′-UTR of the NRP1 promoter, serving as a tumor suppressor miRNA in cholangiocarcinoma [30]. To elucidate the mechanisms through which the above-mentioned miRNAs regulate the expression of NRP1, we are currently studying whether there is a crosstalk between these miRNAs and NRP1 signals.

The present study has some limitations. First, the expression of miRNAs was not validated using qRT-PCR. Second, the interactions between miRNAs and NRP1 were not validated in in vitro experiments. Third, we did not investigate whether NRP1 inhibition affects signaling molecules such as RhoA and cell invasion and migration. Finally, the effects of NRP1 inhibitor and in vivo experiments should be studied to evaluate the clinical usefulness of RTK-specific miRNAs and mRNAs to develop molecularly targeted therapy of RTK in the future.

In conclusion, we found that the expression of NRP1 was significantly upregulated in RTK-derived cell lines and that NRP1 knockdown suppressed the invasive and migratory abilities of RTK-derived cells. These results indicate that NRP1 acts as an oncogene by promoting the invasion and migration of RTK cells and that it would serve as a therapeutic target for RTK. Our study provides insights into the clinical applicability of NRP1 inhibitors for the treatment of RTK in the future.
