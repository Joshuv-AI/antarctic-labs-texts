De novo neurofibromatosis type 2-related schwannomatosis in a child presenting with intraocular signs as initial manifestations
Authors: Aliénor Vienne-Jumeau, Jacques Bijon, Sacha Waintraub, Georges Caputo, Florence Metge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo report a rare pediatric case of neurofibromatosis type 2-related schwannomatosis (NF2-SWN) diagnosed based on ocular findings, with multimodal imaging and surgical follow-up, highlighting the importance of early ophthalmologic evaluation in NF2-SWN.ObservationsAn 8-year-old girl with no prior medical history was referred for bilateral pre-retinal lesions noted on routine eye exam. Introduction

Neurofibromatosis Type 2-related schwannomatosis (NF2-SWN) is a rare autosomal dominant genetic disorder caused by a mutation on chromosome 22, resulting in the loss of function of a 69 kDa tumor suppressor protein known as merlin, also called schwannomin.1 Merlin regulates cell proliferation and mediates contact inhibition, primarily through its role in several key signaling pathways.2,3 Approximately 50 % of cases are due to germline mutations inherited from an affected parent, while the remaining cases result from de novo mutations. The hallmark of NF2-SWN is bilateral vestibular schwannomas, often accompanied by additional intracranial or spinal tumors.3 Clinical severity is generally greater when NF2-SWN presents in childhood, with earlier onset typically indicating a more aggressive disease course.3,4

Although less common than otovestibular findings, ocular manifestations are frequent in these early-onset forms of NF2-SWN,4 and visual complaints are often the first presenting signs or symptoms in early childhood.5 As such, they can serve as valuable early diagnostic indicators.6,7 Ocular features may include posterior subcapsular or cortical cataracts (particularly in pediatric patients), retinal hamartomas with or without involvement of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), epiretinal membranes (ERMs), and optic nerve sheath tumors. These findings are more prevalent in patients with severe genotypes.4,8, 9, 10 Management of juvenile cataracts focuses on the prevention of amblyopia and typically requires surgical removal when the visual axis is compromised. Posterior segment complications remain more challenging to manage and should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis. Surgical intervention may be beneficial in cases involving combined hamartomas,11 and high-grade ERMs.12 For cases involving tufts or low-grade ERMs, the decision-making process is more complex and should be individualized.13

Here, we present a pediatric case in which ocular findings preceded systemic symptoms and ultimately led to the diagnosis of NF2-SWN. This case highlights the potential value of early recognition in improving long-term prognosis and discusses the role of vitrectomy.

Case report

An 8-year-old girl with no prior medical history was referred for evaluation of bilateral whitish pre-retinal lesions discovered incidentally during a routine eye exam. Her parents reported no systemic symptoms, but she had begun to show reading difficulties at school. On initial examination, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was 20/40 in both eyes. Anterior segment examination revealed clear corneas and bilateral posterior subcapsular and cortical lens opacities, all sparing the visual axis, although one opacity in the left eye was located close to it. (Fig. 1A and B). Dilated fundus examination revealed a bilateral pale foveal reflex and normal optic discs (Fig. 1C and D). Spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT) revealed identical findings in both eyes: premacular, flame-shaped condensations of varying reflectivity adherent to the posterior hyaloid, localized inner retinal disorganization and thickening with loss of the foveal depression (Fig. 1E–H). Additionally, early ERM formation was noted, characterized by hyperreflective linear dots above the internal limiting membrane (Fig. 1G). Finger-shaped structures isoreflective to the ganglion cell complex and projecting into the vitreous —suggestive of retinal tufts—were also identified (Fig. 1H). The constellation of bilateral posterior subcapsular and cortical lens opacities, flame-shaped ERMs, retinal hamartomas and tufts raised suspicion for NF2-SWN. Brain and spinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were performed and revealed a left peri-optic meningioma, bilateral vestibular schwannomas, and multiple spinal schwannomas in the thoracolumbar region, confirming the diagnosis (Fig. 2). Although genetic testing was considered, it was ultimately deferred by the parents given the patient's age. Two months later, BCVA in the right eye was measured at 20/50, a change of one line from baseline. While this difference falls within the normal variability of visual acuity testing and OCT findings remained relatively stable, the decision for surgery was made after discussion with the family, who were more in favor of intervention given the child's reading difficulties and the presence of premacular changes. A 25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy was performed, including removal of the premacular vitreous condensations and peeling of the internal limiting membrane. Retinal hamartomas were more distinctly visualized intraoperatively and were left in situ. The cataract was not visually significant and was therefore left untouched. At four months postoperatively, BCVA in the right eye measured 20/30. Although this represents only a one-line change compared with baseline, it was accompanied by resolution of traction and partial restoration of foveal contour on OCT (Fig. 3, supplementary material). The left eye remained anatomically stable and functionally unchanged, and conservative management was continued for the time being.

Discussion

This case describes a pediatric patient whose ophthalmologic findings—including flame-shaped ERMs, retinal hamartomas, and posterior lens opacities—led to the diagnosis of NF2-SWN in the absence of initial neurological symptoms. These early ocular signs were the key factor prompting systemic evaluation and ultimately confirmed the diagnosis according to established clinical criteria.

Classical ophthalmologic manifestations of NF2-SWN, although often subtle, can be highly specific and should prompt further investigation when identified in children. Indeed, they may precede the classic neurological hallmarks, such as bilateral vestibular and spinal schwannomas.14 In our patient, posterior lens opacities were detected on slit-lamp examination, and funduscopy revealed white preretinal and intraretinal formations. Spectral-domain OCT played a key role in providing detailed structural insights into these formations: it enabled the characterization of ERMs as well as retinal hamartomas, two signs recently added to the diagnostic criteria for NF2-SWN.14 According to these updated criteria, a diagnosis can be established by (1) bilateral vestibular schwannomas; (2) the same NF2 pathogenic variant identified in ≥2 distinct NF2-related tumors; or (3) a combination of two major criteria, or one major plus two minor criteria. Major criteria include unilateral vestibular schwannoma, a first-degree relative (other than sibling) with NF2-SWN, ≥2 meningiomas, or a pathogenic NF2 variant in blood. Minor criteria include ependymoma, non-vestibular schwannoma, juvenile subcapsular or cortical cataract, ERM before the age of 40 years, or retinal hamartoma. In our patient, the following features were present: bilateral vestibular schwannomas (sufficient alone to establish the diagnosis), along with several minor criteria: juvenile posterior subcapsular and cortical cataracts, ERMs, retinal hamartomas, a peri-optic meningioma, and multiple spinal schwannomas. This constellation fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for NF2-SWN without the need for genetic confirmation. Of note, the updated criteria specify that cataracts and ERMs are considered diagnostic only when they occur before the age of 40 years.14 This restriction helps to avoid false positives due to age-related lens opacities or idiopathic ERMs. In our pediatric patient, the presence of both posterior subcapsular and cortical cataracts and bilateral flame-shaped ERMs was therefore particularly significant.

The ERMs exhibited a flame-like configuration, a pattern described as characteristic and likely pathognomonic for NF2-SWN, as such lesions have not been reported in other conditions.8,15 These flame-shaped ERMs differ from conventional idiopathic membranes by their distinctive morphology with a flame-like extension toward the vitreous. Another lesion recognized in NF2-SWN is the inner retinal “tuft”: small, discrete elevations arising from the retinal nerve fiber layer, containing GFAP-positive glial tissue. They represent focal inner-retinal proliferations or elevations rather than preretinal membranes.8,15,16 The presence of tufts is regarded as highly indicative of NF2-SWN, with a reported diagnostic specificity of ∼100 %, although their sensitivity is limited to 43 % in the appropriate clinical context.15 Thus, OCT provides critical structural insights that can strongly support a diagnosis of NF2-SWN in the appropriate clinical context and may serve as a valuable imaging biomarker. As OCT becomes increasingly accessible in pediatric settings and continues to improve in resolution,17 its role in NF2-SWN diagnostic pathways is likely to expand and may warrant greater emphasis in future published diagnostic criteria.

Prompted by these ophthalmologic findings, brain and spinal MRI were performed and revealed additional abnormalities consistent with the diagnosis of NF2-SWN. According to recently published consensus guidelines, our patient met one major and two minor diagnostic criteria.14 The diagnosis was therefore established without the need for genetic confirmation. Following a detailed discussion with the patient's parents regarding the diagnostic certainty, management options, and implications of testing, a shared decision was made to defer molecular analysis at this stage. Although not essential for diagnosis or immediate clinical care in this context, genetic testing may offer future utility. It could aid in prognostic stratification, clarify recurrence risk for family counseling, or determine eligibility for research protocols and emerging therapies—particularly those targeting specific NF2-SWN variants, such as gene-based interventions or molecular inhibitors.18

A detailed understanding of the disease's pathophysiology is essential to inform these personalized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. In this context, the flame-shaped configuration of the ERMs, coupled with the absence of cystoid macular edema or outer retinal disruption on OCT, supports a slowly progressive, non-inflammatory mechanism. This phenotype is more consistent with gliotic proliferation than with acute inflammatory or ischemic etiology.8,19 Histopathological findings reinforce this hypothesis: McLaughlin et al. described ERMs in a patient with NF2-SWN composed of cuboidal and spindle-shaped cells consistent with a mixed glial origin—namely Müller cells and astrocytes.19 Retinal tufts similarly exhibit glial differentiation.8,16 Juvenile cataracts, which affect approximately 60–80 % of patients with NF2-SWN,3,4,20 typically involve the posterior subcapsular or capsular regions. McLaughlin et al. hypothesized that these opacities may result from disrupted adherens junctions in NF2-SWN-deficient posterior lens vesicle cells, which fail to differentiate into primary lens fiber cells and instead accumulate anterior to the posterior capsule.19

The presence of tractional maculopathy in this patient illustrates the potential for progressive but initially silent visual decline in pediatric NF2-SWN. Pars plana vitrectomy with internal limiting membrane peeling may offer anatomical and functional improvement in selected cases. However, visual recovery can be limited by underlying retinal dysplasia or coexisting hamartomas, and postoperative outcomes remain unpredictable. Continued follow-up in this case, as well as in similar pediatric presentations, will be essential to guide future recommendations regarding surgical indications and timing in this unique clinical context.

Conclusion

This case underscores the increasingly central role of ophthalmologic assessment—including multimodal imaging with OCT—in the early detection of NF2-SWN. As imaging techniques evolve, their diagnostic yield may continue to grow, potentially justifying their inclusion in revised diagnostic criteria. Early identification is particularly critical in pediatric cases, where neurological signs may appear later and early management decisions can alter long-term outcomes.
