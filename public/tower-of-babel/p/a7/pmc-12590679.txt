Gestational age-dependent clinical characteristics of necrotizing enterocolitis-associated intestinal perforation: a 10-year cohort study
Authors: Hong Wei, Weihong Yue, Gege Liu, Feng Chen, Xia Liu, Ya Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo delineate gestational age (GA)-dependent pathophysiology of necrotizing enterocolitis-associated intestinal perforation (NEC-IP) and establish precision management protocols.MethodsA single-center retrospective cohort study (2013–2023) included 66 preterm (< 37 weeks) and 38 term (≥ 37 weeks) neonates with NEC-associated perforations. Outcomes included anatomical distribution, microbiological profiles, management disparities, and prognoses.ResultsPreterm infants exhibited significantly higher rates of twin gestation (43.9% vs 7.9%, p = 0.003), antenatal steroid exposure (43.9% vs 2.6%, p < 0.001), and preoperative fasting rate (33.3% vs 7.9%, p = 0.009) compared to term infants. Preterm infants demonstrated Gram-positive bacteremia (83.3%) with Gram-negative peritoneal predominance (83.9%), alongside significantly lower leukocyte counts (Stage 2:12.6 vs 14.9 × 10⁹/L, Stage 3: 9.1 vs 11.1 × 10⁹/L, both p < 0.05), platelet levels (all stage), and hemoglobin levels (Stage 1:125.1 vs 141.6 × 1012/L, p = 0.004). Term infants showed Gram-positive peritoneal dominance (76.2%) with classic peritonitis signs (hematochezia 68.4%, abdominal tenderness 55.3%). Lleal perforations predominated in preterms (69.7% vs 21.1%, p < 0.001), whereas colonic involvement was prevalent in terms (63.1%). Prolonged parenteral nutrition in preterms (27.0 vs 20.0 days, p = 0.009) correlating with prolonged hospitalization (38.4 ± 9.7 vs 23.5 ± 8.1 days; p < 0.001), achieved higher enteral tolerance (151.7 vs 134.2 ml/kg/d, p = 0.009). There was no case dead in initial admission. Rehospitalization and mortality rates in readmission were comparable (term 73.7 vs preterm 60.6%, p = 0.177;1% vs 2%; p = 0.653). Although weight at discharge in term group was higher compared to preterm infants (2.5 ± 0.4 vs 3.5 ± 0.6 kg; p < 0.001), while weight velocity was similar between two groups (18.3 ± 7.5 vs 16.6 ± 9.6 g.kg⁻1·d⁻1; p = 0.312).ConclusionGA-specific NEC-IP mechanisms mandate: (1) preterm-focused ileal exploration & Gram-negative coverage, (2) term-focused retroperitoneal debridement & Gram-positive control, and (3) GA-stratified diagnostic framework integrating clinical signs and imaging. This precision approach reduces missed perforations and surgical delays.

Introduction

Neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is the most devastating gastrointestinal emergency in neonates [1]. Reports indicate that its incidence in extremely low birth weight infants is approximately 4.5%–8.7%, with a mortality rate ranging from 20 to 30% [2]. Intestinal perforation represents a hallmark complication of stage III NEC [3], with an overall incidence of 26.67% [4] and an associated mortality rate of 45.46% [1]. Although preterm infants (gestational age [GA] < 37 weeks) constitute the predominant high-risk group due to immature intestinal barriers, term neonates with NEC frequently present with concurrent congenital anomalies such as Hirschsprung disease, resulting in distinct differences in pathogenesis, therapeutic approaches, and prognosis [5, 6]. Nevertheless, systematic comparisons of clinical profiles and evidence-based postoperative management protocols stratified by GA remain scarce.

We conducted a single-center retrospective cohort to (1) identify GA-specific disparities in clinical characteristics, (2) provide novel insights into etiology-driven diagnostic screening, and (3) establish a stratified management framework to optimize precision medicine for this critical condition.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

Eligible participants included neonates born at 26⁰/₇ to 43⁶/₇ weeks' gestation surgically confirmed NEC with intestinal perforation admitted to a tertiary neonatal intensive care unit between May 2013 and December 2023. Participants were stratified based on gestational age into the preterm (GA < 37 weeks) and the term (GA ≥ 37 weeks) cohorts. Inclusion criteria required all: (1) stage III NEC per modified Bell’s criteria [7], (2) operative confirmation of perforation (free intraperitoneal gas on imaging + surgical verification), (3) postnatal age < 28 days at admission, (4) GA 26–44 weeks, (5) survival to discharge after initial surgery, and (6) completed 1-year follow-up (via outpatient visits or telephone interviews). Exclusion criteria included any of the following: (1) gestational age unknown, (2) inherited metabolic diseases, (3) congenital malformations or alternative etiologies of intestinal perforation (e.g., Hirsch sprung disease, intestinal atresia, spontaneous perforation unrelated to NEC), (4) incomplete clinical data, and (5) treatment refusal or postoperative care withdrawal.

Data collection

Clinical data were collected from our institution's medical data platform. The study data encompassed maternal factors (gestational diabetes mellitus, maternal hypertension, meconium-stained amniotic fluid, prenatal use of hormones or antibiotics); neonatal parameters (sex, gestational age at delivery, postnatal age at admission, age at symptom onset, interval from symptom onset to hospital admission, birth weight, weight at admission, et al.); presenting signs and symptoms (e.g., hematochezia, abdominal distension, vomiting); abdominal imaging findings (radiographic findings such as pneumoperitoneum or portal venous gas; ultrasonographic findings such as bowel wall thickening or ascites); laboratory parameters (white blood cell count [WBC], platelet count [PLT], C-reactive protein [CRP], procalcitonin [PCT], neutrophil count [NEUT], hemoglobin [Hb] measured within 24 h preoperatively, prior to enteral feeding initiation, and prior to discharge) and microbiological data (blood and peritoneal fluid culture results); surgical parameters (interval from admission to surgery, perforation site, operative duration); postoperative parameters including nutritional management (type of feeding initiated [e.g., human milk, formula], feeding advancement rate, feeding volumes at initiation and discharge) and adjunctive therapies (duration of antibiotic therapy and invasive mechanical ventilation); and outcomes (in-hospital mortality, reoperation during initial hospitalization, gastrointestinal-related readmissions, surgical complications [e.g., intestinal obstruction, stricture, short bowel syndrome, or atresia confirmed during subsequent surgery]. For the purpose of longitudinal hematological analysis, three key clinical time points were defined as follows: Phase 1: Preoperative- the preoperative period immediately preceding surgical intervention for perforation; Phase 2: Pre-enteral feeding-the period preceding the establishment of full enteral feeding; Phase 3: Pre-discharge-the period preceding discharge from the neonatal unit.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Continuous variables underwent Shapiro–Wilk normality testing. Parametric data: mean ± SD (independent t-tests with Levene's variance verification); nonparametric data: median(IQR) (Mann–Whitney U). Categorical variables: counts (%) [χ2/Fisher's exact per cell thresholds]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Given the retrospective design of this study, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted for the primary outcomes using the PROC POWER procedure in SAS. This analysis will be based on the observed effect sizes, actual sample sizes from the study, an α-level of 0.05, and will specify the Fisher's exact test as the underlying statistical method to evaluate the reliability of these comparative findings given the current sample size.

Results

During the 10-year study period (May 2013–December 2023), 356 neonates with necrotizing enterocolitis and intestinal perforation were screened. Following rigorous eligibility assessment, 252 cases (70.8%) were excluded, yielding a final cohort of 104 neonates (29.2%). Comparative analysis showed no significant baseline differences between the included NEC-IP cohort (n = 104) and excluded NEC-IP patients (n = 252) in gender distribution (60 [57.7%] vs 147 [58.3%] male, p = 0.911), age at presentation (6.59 ± 5.7 vs 6.9 ± 5.9 days, p = 0.642), birth weight (2.3 ± 0.9 vs 2.3 ± 0.9 kg, p = 0.854), or gestational age (35.1 ± 3.6 vs 34.1 ± 6.2 weeks, p = 0.114). The between-group comparisons of all variables showed p > 0.05, demonstrating baseline comparability between the cohorts.

Clinical phenotypes exhibited GA-dependent variation (Table 2). Term neonates presented higher frequencies of hematochezia (68.4% vs 21.2%; p < 0.001), vomiting (21.1% vs 4.5%; p = 0.012), and abdominal tenderness (55.3% vs 28.8%; p = 0.008). Conversely, abdominal varices manifested exclusively in preterm infants (15.2% vs 0%; p = 0.008). No intergroup differences were observed in abdominal distension or bowel motility patterns. Imaging analyses demonstrated radiologic concordance across modalities (Table 2). Pneumoperitoneum prevalence showed modality-dependent variation (X-ray: term 60.5% vs preterm 56.1%; ultrasound: 5.7% vs 0%) without statistical significance (all p > 0.05). Etiological results and surgical findings revealed different pathogens and distinct lesion topography (Table 4). Between-group comparison showed no significant difference in culture positivity rates (p = 0.506). No positive blood cultures were observed in the term group when there were 6 positive blood cultures in the preterm group (p = 0.084). The preterm group yielded 32 positive cultures of peritoneal fluid, dominated by Gram-negative bacteria (81.3%, 26/32), primarily Escherichia coli (31.3%, 10/32) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (43.8%, 14/32). The term group had 22 positives with Gram-positive predominance (72.7%, 16/22), largely Enterococcus faecium (54.5%, 12/22). Ileal perforations predominated in preterm infants (78.8% vs 21.1%; p < 0.001), accounting for 82.7% of small intestinal lesions in this group. Term infants exhibited preferential colonic involvement (Total 78.9%: transverse colon: 76.5%; ascending colon: 64.7%). Compared with term infants, preterm neonates required longer duration of invasive mechanical ventilation (median, 2.0 vs 1.5 days; p = 0.032) and later surgical intervention timing (median age at surgery, 10.5 vs 6.0 days; p = 0.006). To address potential confounders influencing the microbiological findings, we conducted a comparative analysis of antibiotic exposure between preterm and term infants. This evaluation included both the spectrum of broad-spectrum agents and the total duration of therapy, assessed for two distinct periods: the overall hospitalization courses and the specific preoperative period preceding the diagnosis of perforation. No significant differences were found between the two groups in either the types of antibiotics used or the duration of antibiotic therapy, both for the entire hospitalization and the preoperative period (all p > 0.05).Nutritional management diverged markedly, with preterms requiring more frequent nasogastric tube initiation (34.8% vs 10.5%; p = 0.006), slower feeding advancement (median 6.8 vs 11.2 ml/kg/day; p = 0.025), yet ultimately achieving higher enteral volumes at discharge (151.7 ± 34.8 vs 134.2 ± 30.7 ml/kg/day; p = 0.009). While antimicrobial duration and spectrum breadth showed no intergroup differences, preterms exhibited extended parenteral nutrition dependence (27.0 vs 20.0 days; p = 0.009) correlating with prolonged hospitalization (38.4 ± 9.7 vs 23.5 ± 8.1 days; p < 0.001). Discussion

This decade-long cohort study systematically identifies, for the first time, gestational age-dependent pathophysiological characteristics and clinical management disparities in neonates with necrotizing enterocolitis-associated intestinal perforation (NEC-IP). Our findings provide critical evidence for developmental mechanisms in NEC progression and establish actionable insights for optimizing clinical protocols.

The significant gestational age divergence (preterm: mean 32.8 weeks; term: 39.0 weeks) underpins fundamental developmental vulnerabilities. Preterm infants exhibit compromised gut barrier integrity and dysregulated mucosal immunity, creating permissive conditions for NEC-IP pathogenesis. This aligns with Zhao et al.'s case–control evidence identifying gestational age < 34 weeks as an independent predictor for intestinal perforation [8]. Conversely, term infants with mature intestinal defenses develop NEC-IP primarily through perinatal stressor-induced pathways (e.g., congenital heart disease, sepsis) [9], necessitating distinct risk stratification protocols. This suggests clinicians should pay special attention to these populations when diagnosed with NEC, due to their higher risk of developing intestinal perforation.

Preterm and term cohorts demonstrated clinically significant divergence in perinatal risk exposure. The substantially elevated preadmission fasting rates in preterms (33.3% vs 7.9%, p < 0.001) suggest iatrogenic hypoperfusion may compound feeding intolerance pathophysiology [10], highlighting the delicate equilibrium between enteral advancement and intestinal protection. Notably, clinically significant perinatal differences include a 5.6-fold higher twin gestation incidence in preterms (43.9% vs 7.9%; p < 0.001), corroborating epigenomic evidence of placental insufficiency-mediated intestinal dysprogramming [11]. Furthermore, the exclusive occurrence of intrauterine distress in preterms (12.1% vs 0%) coupled with differential antenatal steroid exposure (43.9% vs 2.6%) substantiates a novel paradigm wherein fetal hypoxia and glucocorticoid activation synergistically disrupt mesenteric vascular ontogeny [12], with Lindner et al. confirming glucocorticoids suppress Muc2 expression and RegIIIβ/γ-IL-22 signaling to impair epithelial restitution.

Preterm infants exhibited a unique compartmentalization of microbiological profiles, characterized by concurrent Gram-positive bacteremia (83.3%) and a predominance of Gram-negative organisms in the peritoneum (83.9%), suggesting the potential involvement of dual invasion pathways [13]. One may involve hematogenous dissemination, potentially facilitated by gut dysbiosis-induced downregulation of occludin promoting enterococcal translocation; the other may involve transmural invasion, possibly mediated through LPS-activated TLR4–MyD88 signaling leading to IL-1β-induced impairment of the epithelial barrier [14] — though the precise role of TLR4-mediated mechanisms in preterm intestinal perforation requires further validation. In contrast, term infants showed a clear predominance of Gram-positive organisms in the peritoneal cavity, consistent with known gestational microbiota succession patterns. To evaluate potential confounding influences such as antibiotic exposure and sampling timing, we performed intergroup comparisons. Our analysis revealed no significant differences between preterm and term infants in either the spectrum of broad-spectrum antibiotics used or the total duration of therapy—assessed for both the entire hospitalization and the preoperative period (all p > 0.05). Furthermore, all peritoneal samples were collected during initial surgical intervention under a standardized protocol, minimizing the potential for sampling time-related bias. Together, these findings strongly suggest that the divergent microbiological features observed at the time of intestinal perforation are not artifacts of differential antibiotic exposure or sampling inconsistency, but are more likely indicative of intrinsic, developmental stage-specific pathophysiological mechanisms. Concurrently, preterm neonates demonstrated attenuated leukocyte responses (Phase 2: 12.6 vs 14.9 × 10⁹/L; Phase 3: 9.1 vs 11.1 × 10⁹/L) and immature hematopoiesis (persistently low platelet counts and Phase 1 hemoglobin levels), reflecting developmental lag in bone marrow microenvironment maturation (< 32 weeks: hematopoietic stem cell pool capacity merely 10%−20% of term infants with impaired CXCL12/SDF-1-mediated homing) caused progenitor retention and attenuated leukocyte responses [15].

Surgical findings revealed ileal perforations predominated in preterms (78.8% vs 21.1%; ileum accounting for 82.7% of small bowel lesions), contrasting with colonic involvement in terms (78.9% total, ascending colon 64.7%, transverse colon 76.5%). This anatomical divergence reflects preterm vulnerability from inadequate α-adrenergic receptor expression (30% of term levels) impairing hypoperfusion compensation in ileal watershed zones [16], versus term susceptibility where NEC inflammation suppresses interstitial cells of Cajal function, triggering dysmotility and luminal hypertension. Supporting this, retroperitoneal fat plane obliteration (63% of term infants; CT attenuation > − 30 HU) confirms occult abscess formation along Toldt’s fascia [17], necessitating gestational age-directed surgical approaches: ileal prioritization in preterms versus meticulous retroperitoneal exploration in terms.

Despite comparable pneumoperitoneum rates (radiography: 56.1% vs 60.5%; ultrasonography: 0.0% vs 5.7%), clinical manifestations diverged significantly. Term infants predominantly exhibited classical peritonitis signs (hematochezia 68.4%, abdominal tenderness 55.3%) stemming from TLR4/MyD88-mediated IL-6/TNF-α release [18], while preterms showed non-specific abdominal varices (15.2%) attributable to α-adrenergic receptor deficiency, pneumatosis intestinalis compression, and Treg-mediated endothelial damage [18, 19]. Gestational age-specific radiographic mechanisms further explain false negatives: preterm cases [3, 20] demonstrate minimal leakage (< 5 mL) due to muscular hypoplasia (< 1.5 mm thickness) and disproportionately large peritoneal capacity, whereas term infants develop TLR4-mediated fibrinous adhesions that localize pneumoperitoneum [21]. Consequently, implementing gestational age-specific diagnostic frameworks that integrate high-risk clinical signs with negative imaging findings significantly enhances detection and expedites intervention.

Nutritionally, although preterm enteral advancement was slower (6.8 vs 11.2 mL/kg/day), prolonged parenteral support (27.0 vs 20.0 days) enabled superior feeding volumes at discharge (151.7 vs 134.2 mL/kg/day) despite similar weight velocity (18.3 vs 16.6 g·kg⁻1·d⁻1). Aligned with consensus guidelines [10], individualized gestational age-based protocols featuring dynamic volume adjustment prevent feeding intolerance, while extending parenteral nutrition to 3–4 weeks provides critical metabolic reserve for microvilli restitution and functional intestinal recovery in preterm infants.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design may introduce selection bias. Second, although the overall cohort for NEC-IP is substantial, the sample size of the term infant group (n = 38) limits the power for statistical comparisons of secondary outcomes, such as re-hospitalization rates and long-term mortality. Post-hoc power analysis confirmed that these comparisons were underpowered. Therefore, these particular findings should be interpreted as preliminary and descriptive in nature, warranting verification in larger, multi-center studies.

Conclusion

NEC-associated perforations demonstrate gestational age-specific pathophysiology. Preterms require prioritized ileal evaluation& Gram-negative coverage, while terms necessitate comprehensive colonic exploration& Gram-positive control. Radiologically negative cases should be assessed via gestational age-specific signs (e.g., abdominal venous congestion in preterms, hematochezia with tenderness in terms). Preterm infants need prolonged parenteral support and hospital stay compared with term infants. This study provides evidence for stratified management to reduce missed perforations and surgical delays, though multicenter prospective validation is warranted to address limitations of its single-center retrospective design.
