New power in cancer immunotherapy: the rise of chimeric antigen receptor macrophage (CAR-M)
Authors: Jiajie Jing, Yueming Chen, Enjie Chi, Sisi Li, Yilin He, Bingyan Wang, Hongqiang Shen, Liming Fan, Jiadong Wang, Tianli Shangguan, Xinyang Ge, Yuhao Jiang, Yangyu Chen, Chunjing Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) therapy represents an innovative form of targeted treatment that employs genetically engineered effector cells to selectively target tumor cells. CAR-T immunotherapy, which uses T cells as effector cells, has demonstrated remarkable efficacy in hematological malignancies. However, its clinical application in solid tumors remains limited due to challenges such as poor tumor infiltration, cytokine release syndrome (CRS), neurotoxicity, off-target effects, and other adverse events. To address these limitations, CAR-engineered natural killer (CAR-NK) cells and macrophages (CAR-M) have been developed. Macrophages, as crucial components of innate and adaptive immunity, exhibit superior tumor microenvironment infiltration and long-term persistence, making them a promising tool for next-generation cancer immunotherapy. This review highlights the construction strategies and preclinical and clinical studies of CAR-M, comprehensively introduces the anti-tumor mechanisms, discusses their advantages and disadvantages compared with other CAR-engineered effector cells, and explores the challenges and prospects for CAR-M in treating solid tumors.

Introduction

In recent years, the immunotherapy for hematological malignancies based on chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell technology has witnessed rapid advancements and exhibited remarkable targeting, cytotoxicity, and persistence in diverse clinical trials [1]. The CAR-T therapy targeting CD19 can lead to an initial complete remission rate of 92% in patients with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) [2]. Nevertheless, the clinical application of CAR-T confronts severe toxicity limitations: approximately 70–90% of patients develop CRS, among which 8–30% progress to life-threatening severe cases; 20–50% of the subjects suffer from neurotoxicity [3]. Furthermore, issues such as off-target effects and T cell exhaustion further undermine the treatment safety [4].

Given the significant toxicity issues associated with CAR-T therapy, CAR-NK therapy, as a potential alternative, is highly anticipated. It can significantly reduce the risk of CRS, and in current clinical trials, severe CRS (≥ grade 3) has rarely been observed [5–7], with an incidence rate of approximately 5.6% (1/18) when FT596 is administered alone [8]. However, it is worth noting that CAR-NK therapy is still limited by short survival time in vivo, insufficient tumor homing ability, and the difficulty in standardizing production, which restricts its potential for wide application [9].

To address the limitations of CAR-T and CAR-NK therapies in solid tumors—such as poor infiltration, antigen heterogeneity, and the suppressive TME—researchers have turned to macrophages as alternative effector cells with lower toxicity and enhanced tissue penetration [10–13]. CAR-engineered macrophages (CAR-M), leveraging their natural ability to traverse stromal barriers via chemokine receptor-mediated migration, have demonstrated superior infiltration in tumors like pancreatic cancer and glioma compared to T cells [14–17]. Beyond direct phagocytosis of tumor cells, CAR-M also reprogram immunosuppressive M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) into pro-inflammatory M1 phenotypes by secreting IL-12 and IFN-γ, thereby enhancing antigen presentation and promoting endogenous T cell activation [18–20]. In a first-in-human trial, CAR-M therapy reduced over 40% of immunosuppressive TAMs in the TME [21]. These multifaceted mechanisms highlight CAR-M as a next-generation immunotherapeutic platform for overcoming solid tumor resistance. Design of CAR

Unlike T cells and NK cells, macrophages possess the ability of phagocytosis and antigen presentation, making them particularly suitable for CAR modification. These characteristics enable CAR-M not only to kill target cells but also to recruit other immune cells to reshape the TME. Generally speaking, traditional CARs mainly consist of three domains: extracellular domain, transmembrane domain, and intracellular activation domain [22]. Among them, the extracellular domain mainly contains the antigen-binding region, usually composed of single-chain variable fragments (scFv) [23, 24], which can specifically recognize and bind to the target antigen; the transmembrane domain anchors the CAR molecule on the cell membrane and plays a key role in maintaining stable expression of CAR; while the intracellular domain consists of co-stimulatory region and signal transduction region, responsible for the complete activation of effector cells. The choice of intracellular domain has a significant impact on the function of CAR-M. For example, the phosphorylated immunoreceptor tyrosine activation motif (ITAM) in CD3ζ domain can bind to Syk kinase, activate phagocytic signal transduction, and thereby trigger phagocytosis, while the FcRγ and Megf10 domains also have ITAM phosphorylated by Src family kinases, initiating phagocytosis through local signal cascade reactions, which can enhance antigen-specific phagocytosis [25]. On the contrary, the design based on MerTK domain shows limited cytotoxicity, but it can enhance the uptake of viral particles by macrophages without affecting the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, still having advantages in immune regulation [26]. After scFv recognizes the antigen, the transmembrane fragment of CAR transmits the ligand recognition signal to the intracellular region, activates downstream pathways, thereby promoting the activation of effector cells and killing target cells [27]. As shown in Fig. 1, the design of CAR-M integrates scFv with optimized signal domains that promote phagocytosis and cytokine secretion, making a CAR structure suitable for macrophages, thereby achieving precise targeting of tumors and regulation of TME. More details of this mechanism will be elaborated in the discussion on the killing mechanism of CAR-M.

Macrophage source

Macrophages naturally reside in the TME and exhibit inherent plasticity, allowing them to polarize into pro-inflammatory (M1) or immunosuppressive (M2) phenotypes. This adaptability makes them particularly suitable for CAR engineering to target solid tumors [28]. For CAR-M production in preclinical research, commonly used sources include human monocytic cell lines (e.g., THP-1), murine macrophage lines (e.g., RAW 264.7, J774A.1), and primary or immortalized mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs). For clinical translation, macrophages can be derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), or human peritoneal macrophage cell lines [15, 29, 30].

Among these, iPSCs offer a renewable and scalable source of human macrophages. CAR-M derived from iPSCs exhibit enhanced functional stability and phenotypic homogeneity, making them ideal for clinical-grade manufacturing [15]. Moreover, initiating CAR gene editing at the iPSC stage—prior to differentiation—confers notable advantages over editing terminally differentiated monocytes or macrophages. iPSCs are highly proliferative and more amenable to efficient gene editing methods (e.g., electroporation, lentiviral/CRISPR-Cas9-based delivery), enabling precise CAR integration and clonal selection before macrophage differentiation [31]. Such early editing ensures uniform CAR expression across derived cells and avoids the mosaicism often seen in editing mature cells. A single gene-edited iPSC clone can also serve as a master cell bank for large-scale, consistent CAR-M production. Additionally, this approach avoids the activation or stress responses sometimes triggered during ex vivo editing of primary monocytes/macrophages.

Nevertheless, iPSC-based workflows are associated with longer manufacturing timelines (typically 4–6 weeks) and require standardized, GMP-compliant differentiation protocols to ensure macrophage functionality and M1-like polarization. Functional validation remains essential to confirm that iPSC-derived CAR-Ms maintain robust phagocytosis, tumor-killing capacity, and TME-modulatory effects comparable to primary-derived counterparts [30, 32].

In contrast, PBMC-derived monocytes remain a more accessible source, particularly for autologous or patient-specific applications. The yield of monocytes can be increased approximately threefold by administering hematopoietic mobilizing agents such as filgrastim or sargramostim prior to PBMC collection. Further processing advancements—such as incorporating cytokine cocktails (e.g., GM-CSF with IFN-γ)—have improved M1 polarization efficiency, shortened differentiation timelines, and increased yield. These CD14⁺ monocytes are subsequently cultured with GM-CSF to induce differentiation into pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages [33, 34]. While GM-CSF alone drives M1 features, its combination with IFN-γ further upregulates activation markers (CD80, CD86) and pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α), establishing it as an optimal protocol for generating functional CAR-M [35].

CAR gene introduction and expression

Similar to other CAR therapies, the introduction and expression of the CAR gene in CAR-M can be achieved through either viral or non-viral methods. However, due to the high detection and response capabilities of myeloid cells to heterologous nucleic acids, macrophages and monocytes exhibit resistance to conventional gene manipulation techniques [36].

Viral transduction

The Bobadilla team developed a new type of HIV-1 derived lentiviral particles using the HIV-1 accessory protein Vpx, which can infect bone marrow cells. The restriction factor SAMHD1 in myeloid cells blocks the HIV-1 reverse transcription process by reducing the concentration of deoxynucleotides (dNTP) inside the cells, thereby inhibiting the slow virus infection. However, the HIV-1 accessory protein Vpx can counteract this restriction. It effectively enhances the transduction efficiency of the HIV-1 derived lentiviral vector in myeloid cells by targeting and degrading the SAMHD1 protein [36, 37].

Monocytes and macrophages express high levels of CD46, which enables B group adenoviruses (such as Ad35) to mediate gene transduction through CD46. Replicon-deficient chimeric adenovirus vectors Ad5f35 have been proven to stably infect macrophages, activate inflammasomes, and provide beneficial pro-inflammatory priming signals, thereby generating CAR-M cells with M1 phenotype [38–41].

Non-viral transduction

For non-viral methods, reducing the toxicity associated with nucleic acid transfection remains a key challenge in preparing CAR-M [42, 43]. Plasmids lacking unmethylated cytosine-guanine dinucleotide phosphodiester (CpG) can evade TLR9 detection and achieve gene expression in RAW 264.7 cells and primary mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) [44]. Moreover, the recently discovered PiggyBac (PB) transposon system can promote the transfer of genes to mammalian cells, providing a promising approach for the production of non-viral CAR-M [45]. To overcome the low efficiency of non-viral transfection methods, methods such as optimizing electroporation parameters or developing new delivery systems can be adopted.

Overall, the continuous improvement of CAR-T and CAR-NK technologies has provided a valuable foundation for the design and preparation of CAR-M cells and other CAR-engineered immune cells. The wide availability of macrophage-derived cells has further facilitated the production of CAR-M cells. However, unlike CAR-T or CAR-NK cells, the current gene editing methods may not be fully compatible with the preparation of CAR-M cells, which requires further technological progress.

Anti-tumor effects of CAR-M

Preclinical studies

CD19 [46–49] and CD22 [50–52] are classic targets for B-cell malignancies, frequently studied in CAR-T and CAR-NK immunotherapies against hematological tumors. Morrissey’s team conducted the first preclinical study of CAR-M [25], designing CAR-M cells targeting B-cell antigens CD19 and CD22. They observed that CAR-M could induce complete or partial phagocytosis of target cells, including Raji B cells, CD19 or CD22-positive cells, and even CD19-expressing SiO2 beads. However, CAR-M expressing an anti-CD19 scFv without an intracellular activation domain failed to mediate targeted phagocytosis via adhesion. Furthermore, combining CAR-M with CD47 blockade (a negative regulator of phagocytosis) increased the phagocytic activity of CAR-M against Raji cells by 2.5 times, highlighting the therapeutic potential of combining CAR expression with CD47 inhibitors. This work also introduced a novel direction for CAR engineering.

Subsequently, Liu M’s team [53] explored the synergistic effects of CD19-targeted CAR-M and CAR-T. Their results demonstrated that the CAR-T + CAR-M combination significantly enhanced cytotoxicity compared to either CAR-T or CAR-M alone, surpassing the additive effect of the individual therapies. Remarkably, even at half the dose, the combination therapy exhibited greater cytotoxicity than either CAR-M or CAR-T alone, achieving an effect of ‘1 + 1 > 2’. They found that CAR-T-derived cytokines enhanced the killing activity of CAR-M, upregulated CD86 and CD80 expression, and downregulated CD206 expression on CAR-M. These changes polarized CAR-M toward the M1 phenotype, further promoting the synergistic effects between CAR-T and CAR-M.

Michael Klichinsky's team validated the phagocytic activity of CAR-M using first-generation anti-CD19 CAR-transduced human macrophage THP-1 cell lines equipped with the CD3ζ intracellular domain. Additionally, they developed HER2-targeted CAR-M cells using a defective adenoviral vector (Ad5f35) to transduce human primary macrophages and polarized them into M1 macrophages using GM-CSF. These HER2-CAR-M1 cells eradicated HER2-positive SKOV3 ovarian cancer cells in vitro in a dose- and time-dependent manner and demonstrated potent anticancer activity in SKOV3-constructed lung metastases and abdominal cancers in vivo. This led to a significant improvement in the overall survival rate of the CAR treatment group.CAR-M persisted in tumor-free NSGS mice for at least 62 days after systemic administration [34].

Interestingly, CAR-M exhibited robust indirect antitumor effects by inducing phenotypic reprogramming of M2 macrophages (M2A, M2C, and M2D) into M1-like states, activating pro-inflammatory pathways such as interferon signaling, the TH1 pathway, and iNOS signaling. CAR-M also promoted the activation and maturation of immature human dendritic cells, facilitated the recruitment of resting and activated human T cells, and retained antitumor activity in the presence of human M2 macrophages. These findings provide a valuable strategy to address the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [43].

CD47, a ‘don't eat me’ signal highly expressed on the surface of malignant tumor cells, is associated with poor prognosis [54, 55]. Yizhao Chen’s team designed two types of CAR-M cells targeting CD47 and HER2, respectively, which exhibited antigen-dependent phagocytosis of ovarian cancer cells in vitro and in vivo. The signaling complex CD47-SIRPα transmits inhibitory signals to prevent macrophage-mediated phagocytosis; however, CD47-targeted CAR-M cells bypass this limitation, making CD47 a promising target for CAR-M immunotherapy [26, 56]. However, in clinical studies, treatments targeting CD47 have shown worrying side effects. Due to the expression of CD47 on the surface of red blood cells [57, 58], magrolimab (a CD47 monoclonal antibody) has been observed to cause red blood cell damage [59], even grade 3 or higher anemia [60] in multiple clinical trials. Therefore, although CD47-targeted CAR-M demonstrated encouraging anti-tumor effects in the laboratory, the particularity of CD47 and the existing risks in clinical trials caution us that the clinical application of CD47-targeted CAR-M still requires very careful consideration. Chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4 (CSPG4) is a ~ 300 kDa transmembrane proteoglycan that regulates cancer cell migration, invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and proliferation [61] CSPG4 is frequently highly expressed in melanoma tumors, and expression in non-malignant cells is low, making it an ideal target antigen [62, 63]. In Rob-Johnson’s investigation of chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan4 (CSPG4)-targeted CAR-M therapy against melanoma, Minna Roh-Johnson and colleagues also employed this strategy. They combined CSPG4-specific CAR-Ms with CD47/SIRPα signaling blockade, which synergistically enhanced CAR-M-mediated phagocytosis and significantly suppressed the growth of 3D melanoma spheroids [64].

Building on the first-generation HER2-CAR backbone, Bing Yang's team combined intracellular domains from iCasp9, CD3ζ, and CD147 to construct and evaluate a series of CAR-M designs with varying arrangements. After co-culture with SKOV3 for 24 h, the specific phagocytosis rate was measured. Based on the intracellular segment of HER2-targeting CAR-M generated by iCasp9-THP1, the CAR-CD3ζ-CD147 configuration was determined to be the best combination (the average specific phagocytosis effect was close to 40%, while the others were approximately CAR-CD147-CD3ζ: 31%, CAR-4-1 BB-CD3ζ-CD147: 33%, CAR-4-1 BB-CD147-CD3ζ: 30%, and CAR-CD147-4-1 BB-CD3ζ: 29%). This design can achieve rapid and efficient cell suicide under the regulation of iCasp9, thereby controlling the proliferation of CAR-THP1 cells. During the co-culture of these CAR-THP1 cells with SKOV3 cells in vitro, they showed a much higher targeted killing ability than ordinary THP1 cells, and significantly and specifically inhibited tumor growth in three subcutaneous tumor occurrence mouse models of SKOV3, MDA-MB-453, and NCI-N87, without relying on T cells.Transcriptomic analyses revealed that CAR-THP1 cells tend to differentiate into the M1 phenotype and effectively link innate and adaptive immunity. Additionally, combining autologous RetroNectin-activated killer (RAK) cells with CAR-M promoted intratumoral immune cell infiltration, particularly of CD8 + T cells, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy and antitumor immune responses [65]. And in the treatment model of peritoneal metastasis of gastric cancer in mice, HER2-targeted CAR-M has also been successfully used to treat peritoneal metastases of gastric cancer, showing improved efficacy when combined with oxaliplatin [29].

For most tumors, angiogenesis is critical for growth and metastasis [66, 67]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), the most potent angiogenesis-promoting cytokine, is upregulated under hypoxic conditions and binds to VEGFR2 on vascular endothelial cells, triggering tumor-associated angiogenesis. VEGF and VEGFR2 are key regulators of vascular development and permeability [68–72], making them promising targets for solid tumor therapy. Duan’s team developed VEGFR2-targeted CAR-M using intracellular domains from TLR4 or the IFN-γ receptor. These CAR-M cells exhibited higher CD86, MHC-II, and TNF-α expression in vitro and demonstrated strong antitumor effects in vivo, significantly inhibiting 4T1 breast cancer progression without notable toxicity [73].

Furthermore, as one of the most prominent and frequently overexpressed targets in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), c-MET demonstrates significantly distinguishable expression levels between tumor and peri-tumoral tissues. Studies have shown that inhibition of the HGF/c-MET signaling pathway combined with chemotherapy enhances cytotoxic T cell infiltration while suppressing pancreatic tumor growth and metastasis [74]. Based on this evidence, researchers developed CAR-M through genetic modification of both human monocyte-derived macrophages (hMDMs) and the THP-1 macrophage cell line. These CAR-Ms exhibited remarkable anti-tumor capabilities: Quantitative analysis of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release in co-culture supernatants revealed significantly higher levels in CAR-THP-1 groups compared to untransduced THP-1 controls (p < 0.01), indicating enhanced tumor cell killing activity. Confocal microscopy confirmed increased phagocytic events of tumor cells by CAR-THP-1 cells, further validating their potent anti-tumor effects. Transcriptome sequencing demonstrated significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with immune synapse formation and membrane transport processes in CAR-THP-1 cells. Also in PDAC mouse models, c-MET-targeted CAR-Ms specifically inhibited tumor progression. Additionally, The quantitative analysis of the tumor burden in the final bioluminescence images of the in situ pancreatic transplantation tumor model showed that the tumor burden in the CAR-M treatment group was approximately four times lower than that in the control group (5 × 107 VS 2 × 108). Furthermore, hMDM-derived c-MET-targeting CAR-Ms showed target expression-dependent cytotoxicity across various cell lines. This was validated in an orthotopic pancreatic cancer model using BxPC-3-Luc cells in immunodeficient mice, where intraperitoneally administered macrophages maintained detectable activity for over 20 days [75].

Similar to the NK cell source in CAR-NK technology, CAR-M can also be induced by CAR-iPSC differentiation [76, 77]. In 2020, Jin Zhang's team generated CAR-expressing macrophages (CAR-iMacs) from iPSCs derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using lentiviral transduction. These CAR-iMacs exhibited antigen-dependent phagocytosis and killing of tumor cells, pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, and M1 polarization when co-cultured with lymphoma cells expressing CD19 or ovarian cancer cells expressing mesothelin. They also demonstrated significant tumor-inhibitory effects in preclinical models of hematologic malignancies and solid tumors [30]. Recently, Zhang's team engineered a novel generation of CAR-iMacs incorporating the TIR domain of TLR4, which significantly enhanced antitumor efficacy compared to CD3ζ-based CAR-M. These M1-polarized CAR-iMacs demonstrated superior inflammatory factor secretion and antitumor activity in glioblastoma (EGFRvIII) and liver cancer (GPC3) models (For detailed information, please refer to 4.2) [78]. It is easy to observe that iPSC-derived CAR-M has its unique advantages, such as a stronger tendency towards M1 polarization. For instance, the characteristic of generating CAR-M from CAR-iPSC solves the problem of difficulty in obtaining macrophage sources. iPSC is easier to preserve and has a stronger amplification ability compared to macrophages, enabling more streamlined and standardized production. Moreover, it can freely control the differentiation direction of CAR-iPSC, such as the multi-directional differentiation of CAR-NK/T/M, facilitating more diverse anti-tumor research [30, 79–81].

Disialoganglioside GD2 (GD2) is highly expressed in neuroblastoma and many melanomas, as well as in various other tumors, including sarcomas, small-cell lung cancer, and brain tumors. GD2 has become a key immunotherapy target [82]. Although FDA-approved anti-GD2 monoclonal antibodies like dinutuximab and naxitamab show clinical efficacy, their administration is often accompanied by severe pain requiring opioid analgesia [82–84]. Clinical trials of GD2-targeted CAR-T cells have shown reduced toxicity but limited efficacy [85, 86], highlighting the need for alternative effector cells. Jue Zhang's team utilized CRISPR-Cas9 to integrate anti-GD2 CAR into the AAVS1 locus of human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs). Through a serum- and feeder-free differentiation protocol, they generated CAR-M cells with potent cytotoxic activity against GD2-expressing neuroblastoma and melanoma in vitro, as well as neuroblastoma in vivo [87]. Additionally, Shifaa M. Abdin’s team derived CAR-M cells targeting CD19 from primary hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, as well as iPSCs, demonstrating efficacy against CD19-positive leukemia [79].

In summary, CAR-M has shown promising antitumor effects in preclinical models of both hematologic and solid tumors. Its antigen-presentation capability bridges innate and adaptive immunity, enhancing immune responses and forming an effective antitumor defense. CAR-M’s potential to cooperate with other CAR-engineered immune cells further underscores its clinical promise. However, challenges remain, including the inhibitory effect of tumor-surface molecules like CD47 on CAR-M activity. Combining CAR-M with immune checkpoint inhibitors may help mitigate these effects. Evaluation of the anti-tumor mechanism and application of CAR-M

Although there is still a lack of key clinical trial data, it is not difficult to find that CAR-M, like other cells modified by CAR technology, can specifically kill targeted malignant cells. Understanding the killing mechanism of CAR-M can help strengthen the anti-tumor effect of CAR-M and may improve the problems existing in the use of other CAR-immune cells (such as CRS in CAR-T, short duration in CAR-NK, etc.); In addition, the antigen presentation of macrophages may provide the possibility for the combination of a variety of CAR-immune cells.

Relationship between M cell differentiation and CAR-M killing

The TME is a complex ecosystem composed of diverse immune cells, cytokines, and other components [88]. Within this environment, macrophages exhibit remarkable plasticity, dynamically adjusting their transcriptional profiles and functions in response to environmental, tissue-specific, and inflammatory stimuli [89, 90]. Traditionally, TAMs have been dichotomized into M1 (pro-inflammatory, anti-tumoral) and M2 (immunosuppressive, pro-tumoral) subsets based on in vitro polarization models. However, this binary framework has increasingly been recognized as an oversimplification that fails to capture the full diversity of TAMs in vivo [91].

Recent single-cell transcriptomic and multi-omics studies have unveiled substantial TAM heterogeneity across cancer types. For instance, in non-small cell lung cancer, at least eight transcriptionally distinct TAM clusters were identified, many co-expressing canonical M1 and M2 markers, indicating functional plasticity [92]. Similarly, in breast cancer, macrophages have been classified into subsets involved in antigen presentation, chemokine secretion, lipid metabolism, and wound healing [93, 94]. Notably, these studies demonstrate that TAMs often exist along a phenotypic continuum shaped by metabolic gradients, hypoxia, and tumor-derived factors, rather than fitting neatly into M1 or M2 categories.

Beyond cellular phenotypes, the spatial immune landscape also influences tumor behavior. Tumors can be broadly classified as immune-inflamed, immune-excluded, or immune-desert, based on the distribution of cytotoxic lymphocytes in the TME [95]. Immune-inflamed or ‘hot’ tumors, characterized by high T cell infiltration, IFN-γ signaling, PD-L1 expression, and tumor mutational burden (TMB), are typically enriched in M1-type macrophages. In contrast, ‘cold’ tumors often display reduced MHC-I expression, low TMB, and higher densities of M2 macrophages [96].

Despite the nuanced understanding of TAMs, the M1/M2 paradigm remains a pragmatic framework for designing and evaluating CAR-M therapies. M1-like macrophages synergize with T and NK cells to enhance antigen presentation, secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, and suppress tumor growth. Conversely, M2-like macrophages contribute to tumor progression, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression through tissue remodeling and anti-inflammatory signaling [97, 98].

Consequently, many CAR-M engineering strategies aim to either select M1-like macrophages as therapeutic effectors or reprogram M2-like TAMs into a pro-inflammatory phenotype. Surface markers such as CD80 and CD86 (M1), or CD206 and CD163 (M2), are routinely used to monitor these phenotypic transitions [99, 100].

Anti-tumor studies of CAR-M1

Recent studies have adopted diverse strategies to induce M1 polarization in CAR-M cells and enhance their antitumor activity, which can be broadly categorized into three approaches: genetic engineering, tumor microenvironment modulation, and exogenous stimulation.

Genetic engineering strategies include the use of lentiviral transduction and chimeric viral vectors. For instance, Klichinsky et al. utilized a non-replicating Ad5f35 adenoviral vector to transduce CARs, leading to durable M1 polarization independent of the CAR construct. Transcriptomic analysis confirmed activation of M1-related pathways, including interferon signaling and iNOS activation, with effects persisting for over 40 days in human donor-derived macrophages [34]. Similarly, Zhang’s team engineered iPSC-derived macrophages expressing CARs with CD3ζ-TIR dual signaling domains, resulting in enhanced phagocytosis, sustained M1 polarization, and resistance to M2 reprogramming, as validated by single-cell RNA sequencing [78].

Microenvironment-targeted strategies focus on overcoming immunosuppressive barriers. Huo et al. demonstrated that CD47 blockade via SIRPα suppression significantly augmented phagocytic function and inflammatory cytokine production in HER2-targeted CAR-Ms [101]. In Fig. 2, the mechanism by which blocking CD47 enhances tumor-targeted killing is described in detail. Similarly, a targeted short hairpin RNA (shRNA) cassette designed to inhibit SIRPα can also achieve a similar effect [102]. Additionally, CD47 inhibition also supports dendritic cell priming of CD8⁺ T cells, enhances NK-mediated ADCC/CDC, and promotes direct caspase-mediated tumor killingntation to CD8 + T cells, triggering adaptive anti-cancer immune response [103–105]. Exogenous stimuli such as ginseng-derived nanoparticles (GDNPs) resembling extracellular vesicles, a low-lactate tumor environment, and BCG-induced expression of NMAAP1 also show potential in promoting pro-inflammatory phenotypes in CAR-Ms [106–108].

These integrated strategies collectively promote a phenotypic shift toward M1 macrophages, characterized by enhanced phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), ultimately improving the therapeutic efficacy of CAR-M cells in solid tumors (Fig. 2).

Anti-tumor studies of CAR-M2

M2-type macrophages are generally considered pro-tumoral, as they interact with cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) to promote tumor progression. CAF-derived chemokines (e.g., CCL2, CXCL12) recruit monocytes and induce their differentiation into M2 macrophages, which in turn facilitate fibroblast activation and mesenchymal transformation, creating a self-reinforcing immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [109]. Consequently, studies directly employing M2 macrophages for anti-tumor purposes remain limited.

Instead, most strategies aim to inhibit M2 polarization to disrupt the tumor-supportive niche. For example, zoledronic acid inhibits M2 polarization and the activity of prostate cancer-associated fibroblasts, thereby remodeling the microenvironment and restraining tumor growth [110]. Nevertheless, M2 macrophages are not devoid of therapeutic value. In certain contexts, such as in monocyte-derived macrophages, M2 subsets have demonstrated superior phagocytic capacity compared to M1 cells [111]. While M1 type macrophages can more effectively engulf tumor cells [112], M2 type macrophages exhibit a strong ability to engulf apoptotic cells and debris [113]. This characteristic suggests potential applications beyond cancer immunotherapy, particularly in conditions requiring efficient clearance without strong inflammatory activation—such as the phagocytosis of pathogenic antibodies or cellular debris in autoimmune diseases.

Supporting this notion, studies in models of multiple sclerosis (MS)—a chronic inflammatory demyelinating disease—have shown that betulin and clofibric acid can induce M2 polarization in BV2 microglia, leading to reduced inflammation, enhanced phagocytosis, S-phase cell cycle prolongation, decreased apoptosis, and improved mitochondrial morphology [114]. These findings highlight the context-dependent utility of M2 macrophages and suggest avenues for CAR-M2 development in non-oncologic diseases.

Relationship between intracellular segment and killing effect of CAR-M cells

Similar to CAR-T and CAR-NK, the CAR structure of CAR-M consists of three key components: the extracellular region, the transmembrane domain, and the intracellular activation domain. The extracellular region includes antigen-binding domains that recognize tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and hinge regions that provide flexibility, enabling the antigen-binding domains to overcome spatial barriers and access epitopes of interest. Common extracellular domains, such as CD8α and CD28, utilized in CAR-NK designs, have also been incorporated into CAR-M hinge regions [78, 115]. The transmembrane domain connects the extracellular region to the intracellular activation signaling domain, anchoring the CAR molecule on the cell surface. Typical transmembrane regions are derived from CD3ζ, CD8, and CD28TM [76].

The intracellular domain plays the most critical role in activating the vector cell after recognizing the target antigen. The number of intracellular activation signals determines the generation of CAR cells. For instance, first-generation CAR-NK and CAR-T cells contain only the CD3ζ signal, while second- and third-generation constructs include one or two additional co-stimulatory signals. These co-stimulatory molecules are often derived from families such as CD28 (CD28, ICOS), the tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) family (4-1BB, OX40, CD27), or the signaling lymphocyte activation molecule (SLAM) receptor family (e.g., 2B4). The addition of IL-15, which enhances tumor-killing activity and prolongs cell lifespan, represents a fourth-generation CAR structure [27, 116–118].

When designing CAR-M, signal transduction domain selection is pivotal. Morrissey’s team initially adopted the CD3ζ intracellular domain containing the immune receptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM), as used in CAR-T cells. They also tested cytoplasmic domains from mouse phagocytic cell receptors such as multiple EGF-like domain protein 10 (Megf10), FcRγ, adhesion G protein-coupled receptor B1 (Bai1), and tyrosine protein kinase Mer (MerTK). CAR-Ms incorporating ITAM-based domains (CD3ζ, FcRγ, or Megf10) demonstrated strong phagocytic efficiency, whereas those containing Bai1 or MerTK intracellular domains failed to bind antigen-functionalized beads [25].

Niu et al. developed anti-CCR7 CAR-Ms targeting CCR7-expressing immunosuppressive cells by replacing the scFv with CCL19, the natural CCR7 ligand. Testing activation domains from MerTK, toll-like receptors (TLR2, TLR4, TLR6), and the second-generation CAR-T domain (4-1BB-CD3ζ), the study found that MerTK activation domains provided the highest cytotoxicity in RAW264.7 macrophages. Surprisingly, anti-CCR7 CAR-M with MerTK domains showed efficient expression and antigen binding, suggesting target-specific intracellular domains may optimize CAR-M efficacy [119].

The α1β1 integrin-mediated Fcγ receptor I (FcγRI) signaling components were also applied to the specific activation of chimeric antigen receptor macrophages (CAR-M) and their antitumor potential. Through evaluating in vitro activated macrophages and antitumor responses, it was found that compared with CD3ζ CAR-M, the involvement of α1β1 integrin-mediated FcγRI signaling was associated with enhanced macrophage activation and specific phagocytosis in primary human macrophages, and significantly improved tumor control and survival rates in multiple cancer models. RNA sequencing showed that α1β1 integrin-mediated FcγRI engagement enhances antitumor immunity by promoting pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype-related pathways, such as Toll-like receptor signaling, tumor necrosis factor signaling, and IL-17 signaling [120].

In addition, CAR-Ms incorporating CD147 activation domains have been used for tumor extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling rather than direct phagocytosis. These CAR-Ms regulate matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) expression to exert cytotoxic effects (The detailed mechanism will be discussed in 4.3) [121]. Jin Zhang’s team introduced a CD19-targeted CAR-M design integrating traditional CAR-T signaling domains (41BB-CD3ζ) with macrophage-specific activation domains (CD86-FcγRI), potentially enhancing antitumor activity but with a higher risk of CRS [30]. To further improve CAR-M efficacy, they incorporated the intracellular Toll/IL-1R (TIR) domain from TLR4 into a second-generation CAR-M design. This design of integrating CD3-TIR-CAR maintains the M1-like polarization while, compared with TIR-CAR, it can also promote the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6: 180 pg/mL (average value of TIR-CAR) VS 340 pg/mL (average value of CD3-TIR-CAR), IL-12: 100 pg/mL (average value of TIR-CAR) VS 180 pg/mL (average value of CD3-TIR-CAR), IL-23: 4000 pg/mL (average value of TIR-CAR) VS 6000 pg/mL (average value of CD3-TIR-CAR), TNF: 150 pg/mL (average value of TIR-CAR) VS 300 pg/mL (average value of CD3-TIR-CAR), etc. This design can significantly enhance the in vitro and in vivo anti-tumor effects of CAR-M without the need for LPS pre-activation [78].

In conclusion, the intracellular domains of CAR-M are the core of its anti-tumor activity, such as CD3ζ, FcRγ or Megf10, MerTK, Toll-like receptors (TLR2, TLR4, TLR6), the second-generation CAR-T domains (4-1BB-CD3ζ), α1β1 integrin-mediated Fcγ receptor I (FcγRI) pathway, etc. These intracellular segments can enable CAR-M to acquire strong phagocytic ability. The integration of the CD147 activation domain can enable macrophages to obtain better tumor extracellular mechanism degradation ability rather than stronger phagocytic action. From another perspective, it breaks down the solid fortress of the tumor. It is worth noting that the combination of any intracellular domain may indeed enhance phagocytic function, but it may also accompany an increase in the risk of side effects, such as the strategy of combining the traditional CAR-T signaling domain (4-1BB-CD3ζ) with the macrophage-specific activation domain (CD86-FcγRI) may exacerbate cytokine release, and even further lead to severe CRS. A strict risk assessment is required. The future improvement direction may include introducing secreted small molecule gene fragments, imitating the design of CAR-T/NK to enhance M1 polarization, inhibiting the function of M2 macrophages, or recruiting T, NK and B cells, thereby optimizing the therapeutic potential of CAR-M. At the same time, while optimizing the killing potential, carefully monitor the indicators related to the increase in adverse event risks, such as cascading or explosive cytokine release, in order to achieve the goal of high killing power and low risk in clinical treatment.

CAR-M induces T cell differentiation to promote tumor killing

As antigen-presenting cells (APCs), macrophages can enhance tumor cell killing by T cells [122]. Macrophages also mediate costimulatory signaling and provide cytokines essential for T cell activation [123], a function that is also applicable to CAR-M. The Yizhao Chen research team found that degraded cell debris from both HER2-CAR-M and CD47-CAR-M was presented to T cells as tumor-associated antigens, significantly promoting the differentiation of CD3 T cells into cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). Following CAR-M induction, the expression of CTL-related killing factors, such as GzmB, TNF-α, IL-2, IFN-γ, perforin, LT-α, and FasL, was upregulated and this was verified in mice as well. Additionally, flow cytometry results showed a significant increase in CD40 and CD40L expression after co-culturing CD3 T cells with CAR-M cells, indicating a direct interaction between the two [26]. In a study by Jin Zhang's team, CD3ζ-TIR-CAR-iMACs were tested alongside target cells for 48 h. Jurkat T cells expressing the NY-ESO-1-specific T cell receptor (TCR) were then added to the co-culture system for 24 h. This co-culture activated T cells, and the activation level was further increased in the presence of tumor cells overexpressing NY-ESO-1. Furthermore, in anti-tumor treatments using syngeneic tumor-bearing mice with intact immune systems, CAR-BMDMs containing CD3ζ-TIR were shown to fully mobilize T cells and NK cells within the immune microenvironment while maintaining their own anti-tumor efficacy and M1 polarization. This strategy effectively transitioned solid tumors from ‘cold’ to ‘hot’ tumors, demonstrating the potential of second-generation CAR-iMACs to enhance immune response in solid tumors [78].

Other killing mechanisms of CAR-M

In addition to the simple phagocytosis of tumor cells, macrophages are also capable of CAR-antigen-mediated local phagocytosis, a process figuratively referred to as "bites" [25]. This process relies on intracellular signaling domains carrying ITAMs. Remarkably, the CAR module can also induce endocytosis in non-specialized phagocytic cells, such as human NIH 3T3 fibroblasts. This ability could potentially be leveraged for the phagocytosis of invading pathogens after infection, as well as for constructing non-immune cell phagocytosis networks to transport small-molecule drugs. The extracellular matrix (ECM) is crucial in malignancy progression as it serves as a physical barrier to various anti-cancer therapies. Mice with defective expression of the ECM protein tenascin exhibit a higher rate of effector immune cell infiltration to tumors compared to wild-type mice [124, 125]. The density and orientation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) affect the localization and migration of T cells in human non-small cell lung cancer tumors. More T cells are observed in the loose regions of fibronectin and collagen, suggesting that a compact and intact ECM usually indicates less T cell infiltration, which typically also implies a poorer prognosis [126]. Additionally, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma with abundant ECM, the vascular density is significantly reduced, and the blood vessels are embedded in the tight ECM matrix, which also prevents therapeutic agents from reaching the tumor site [127]. The synthesis and degradation of the ECM are primarily regulated by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and their inhibitors, tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs). MMPS can degrade almost all ECM components. In the aforementioned study by Jin Zhang's team, the phagocytosis of apoptotic tumor cells by CAR-iMACs was also observed. Upon further investigation of the cytolytic mechanism of CAR-iMACs, the team detected the expression of apoptosis-related genes in target cells. These included genes such as ASP3, CASP6, CASP7, CASP8, and APAF1, as well as cell death transduction signals like FAS, TNFRSF10A, and TNFRSF25. The process was summarized into three steps: (1) tumor cell antigens bind to CAR, activating CAR-iMACs; (2) CAR-mediated M1 polarization and TNF secretion induce tumor cell apoptosis; and (3) the apoptotic tumor cells are cleared via cytosis [78].

Similar to CAR-T and CAR-NK, metabolites in the TME profoundly influence the sustained antitumor efficacy of CAR-M. A new study demonstrates that CAR-M engineered with a chimeric IL-2 receptor can continuously activate intracellular inflammatory signaling pathways via IL-2, thereby inducing a phenotypic shift toward an inflammatory/antitumor state in CAR-M [129].

Furthermore, the IL4I1 of amino acid oxidase can recognize tryptophan (Trp) and generate metabolites such as indole and uric acid, thereby activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) [130, 131]. IL4I1 + CD274 (PD-L1) + macrophages aggregate around the tumor in a T-cell-dependent manner, exerting immunosuppressive effects by degrading tryptophan, and promoting regulatory T cells to enter the tumor area [132]. Knocking out IL4I1 in macrophages can enhance the efficacy of CD19 CAR-T combined with PD-1 inhibitor in the treatment of DLBCL [133]. However, it must be acknowledged that generally, it is difficult to knockout the expression of the IL4I1-Trp-AhR axis (usually terminally differentiated cells) in the microenvironment. Therefore, other metabolites also need to be discussed, such as the mTOR-HIF-1α axis, which participates in glycolysis to promote the M1 polarization of macrophages, and can be achieved through TLR4 agonists. This may provide new ideas for the anti-tumor effect of CAR-M and reversing the inhibitory effect of the tumor microenvironment [134].

Overall, research on the antitumor effects of CAR-M is progressing rapidly. Strategies such as intracellular domain optimization of CAR, targeted degradation of tumor extracellular matrix, and mitigating or leveraging the effects of metabolites have all shown promising results. Meanwhile, experiences from non-tumor immunotherapy are also worth learning from. For example, in the progression of intervertebral disc (IVD)-related diseases, macrophages undergo phenotypic transformation and reduced phagocytic capacity after phagocytosing apoptotic nucleus pulposus cells (NPC). However, upregulation of brain-specific angiogenesis inhibitor 1 (BAI1) restores macrophage endocytic capacity. A CAR-M system with enhanced BAI1 expression, combined with an IVD circular microneedle (MN) delivery system, further clears apoptotic NPC, improves the inflammatory microenvironment, and repairs damaged IVDs. This suggests that enhancing BAI1 expression and developing specialized CAR-M delivery pathways could also be methods to enhance antitumor mechanisms [135].

Furthermore, excellent scalability significantly affects the anti-tumor efficacy of different CAR-immune cells. For instance, a mouse study demonstrated that compared to CAR T cells harvested after a longer culture period, CAR T cells cultured for only 3 days exhibited superior anti-tumor activity at a sixfold lower dose, suggesting that prolonged in vitro culture of cells may affect their efficacy. This further highlights the importance of excellent scalability [136]. Due to the relatively late start, there are currently no clear reports on the amplification efficiency and optimization methods of CAR-M. However, due to the specific growth characteristics of NK cells (as mentioned above), in the preparation of CAR-NK, merely culturing NK cells on a large scale alone requires at least 14–21 days [137]. The addition of IL-15 can accelerate the amplification of CAR-NK and extend their proliferation lifespan (from 24 to 42 days) [138], although the time is still relatively long. With more sufficient development time, CAR-T therapy has many techniques to accelerate the amplification, expansion and infusion of CAR-T, such as the Fast CAR platform. This platform simultaneously activates and transduces resting T cells with high gene transduction efficiency using the XLenti vector. After transduction, one or more CARs integrate into the T cell genome and are stably expressed, allowing the production to be completed and infused back into the patient's body the next day (9–14 days after normal CAR-T production [139]. This Fast CAR-T has excellent amplification capacity, a younger and less exhausted phenotype, and higher efficacy in eliminating B-ALL both in vitro and in vivo [140]. With the development of technology, this platform is expected to provide reference significance for the growth (scalability) of CAR-NK and CAR-M. In addition to the preparation speed, the in vivo amplification and duration of CAR-T/NK have been confirmed through experience in clinical and laboratory research over the past several years to be directly linked to the therapeutic effect of tumor treatment. Therefore, researchers have tried various methods to ensure sufficient CAR-T/NK load in the body. The simplest method is to infuse multiple times to maintain high cell load. The differentiation of CAR-iPSC to prepare engineered materials has well solved the problem of time from acquisition to preparation and infusion, forming an off-the-shelf product model [141]. Although there are currently no registered clinical trials of CAR-iPSC-derived CAR-M, this technology is still expected to bring surprises to clinical CAR-M treatment. Moreover, different exhaustion phenotypes-related target blockers, such as blocking CD38 to enhance glycolytic metabolism [126] to prevent CAR-T cell exhaustion, have been frequently mentioned. Or, the previously overlooked cytokines, such as IL10, which is widely considered an immunosuppressive cytokine, can also help maintain a complete mitochondrial structure and function in the CAR-T tumor microenvironment and increase oxidative phosphorylation in a mitochondrial pyruvate carrier-dependent manner to promote the proliferation and effector function of CAR T cells. Although the anti-exhaustion research of CAR-M is currently still relatively thin, these various attempts have significant reference significance for the progress of CAR-M.

In terms of safety, CRS remains the most well-recognized and potentially fatal adverse event associated with CAR-T therapy for hematologic malignancies [142]. It results from excessive cytokine secretion, particularly IL-6, IFN-γ, and TNF-α, which triggers a cascade involving NK cells, monocytes/macrophages, and dendritic cells, ultimately amplifying systemic inflammation [143–146]. Macrophages are considered central mediators of this response [147, 148]. Preclinical studies have shown that CAR-M-treated mice exhibit lower circulating levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ compared to controls. In contrast, tumor tissues from these mice display elevated levels of IL-12 and IFN-γ, indicating that CAR-M activity is largely localized within the tumor and induces a relatively tumor-restricted immune response [121]. Nevertheless, current clinical data are insufficient to confirm whether CAR-M therapy can effectively prevent or significantly mitigate CRS risk. In comparison, CAR-NK therapy has demonstrated a consistently low incidence of CRS. Since CAR-NK cells primarily release IFN-γ, perforin, and granzyme B upon target recognition—without triggering the macrophage-driven cytokine amplification loop—the risk of systemic cytokine storms is minimized [149, 150]. In a CD19-targeted CAR-NK treatment, only 1 case of grade I CRS (0.27%, a total of 37 CD19-positive malignant tumor subjects) was observed [7], In contrast, among 1916 patients with large B-cell lymphoma treated with CD19-directed CAR-T therapy between 2018 and 2020, CRS occurred in 75.2% of cases, with 11.3% experiencing grade ≥ 3 events.

Enhanced ability of tumor microenvironment infiltration

TAMs in the TME play a critical and complex role in tumor initiation and progression, with their secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines influencing CAR-NK/T cells to exhibit an exhausted phenotype, leading to impaired killing activity—sometimes irreversible even with immune checkpoint inhibitors [151]. However, the immunosuppressive environment in tumors is less significant for macrophages [152]. In monocyte-derived macrophages, while M2 macrophages can inhibit the function of other immune cells, they retain phagocytic abilities, with M2 macrophages demonstrating a higher phagocytic capacity than M1 macrophages [111]. CAR-M cells exhibit greater phenotypic plasticity, and direct exposure to the target protein of the CAR or pro-inflammatory cytokines produced by TAMs can polarize CAR-M cells toward an anti-tumor phenotype, thus avoiding immune exhaustion [30]. Additionally, macrophage infiltration into the TME is often secondary to the many cytokines secreted by tumor cells. This plays an important role in the tumor-targeted migration of macrophages. For instance, it has been demonstrated that the activation of the CCL2/CCR2 axis, which helps the migration of immunosuppressive phenotype macrophages [153] and the CXCL12 secreted by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF), which promotes the M2 polarization of macrophages and their migration towards the tumor microenvironment [154], are all involved. However, since the tumor often has an immunosuppressive microenvironment, the corresponding cytokines that promote the migration of macrophages often have the potential to promote the differentiation of macrophages towards an immunosuppressive phenotype. Therefore, the efforts mentioned earlier to change the intracellular segment of the CAR structure to promote the differentiation of macrophages towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype are very necessary. Once CAR-M cells are recruited into the hypoxic TME, the receptors for these soluble factors are downregulated, effectively locking macrophages into the TME [155]. Moreover, macrophages can directly sense hypoxic conditions and metabolites such as low pH, promoting migration into the TME [156]. Consequently, in the absence of an additional increase in chemokine receptors, CAR-M's ability to infiltrate the TME is even stronger than that of CAR-NK cells.Frozen sections of fresh tumor samples revealed that macrophages were the majority of tumor-infiltrating cells in various cancer types, accounting for 50% [157] in melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, and colorectal cancer.

Attempts to enhance the tumor-killing effect of CAR-M

In addition to direct phagocytosis, CAR-M can reshape the tumor immune microenvironment by presenting antigens, upregulating co-stimulatory molecules, and secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-12 and IFN-γ, thereby promoting T and NK cell activation [158]. These features support its rational combination with other immunotherapies to overcome the limitations of single-agent approaches, particularly in solid tumors.

Combination with CAR-T: CAR-M and CAR-T cells exhibit reciprocal enhancement. Cytokines released by CAR-T cells induce M1 polarization in CAR-M and upregulate co-stimulatory molecules (CD80/CD86), boosting CAR-M phagocytosis and tumoricidal activity. Conversely, CAR-M supports CAR-T activation through antigen presentation and co-stimulation [53].

Combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors: CAR-M therapy can be synergistically enhanced by blocking immune checkpoints such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CD47–SIRPα. In immunocompetent models of HER2⁺ tumors, combining CAR-M with anti-PD-1 therapy (e.g., pembrolizumab) significantly improved tumor control, TME remodeling, and T and NK cell infiltration, overcoming resistance to checkpoint blockade alone [26]. Genetic disruption of the CD47–SIRPα axis via shRNA or co-administration of anti-CD47 antibodies (e.g., magrolimab) enhances CAR-M phagocytosis and activates inflammatory pathways such as cGAS–STING, boosting T cell infiltration and long-term antitumor immunity [159].

Combination with conventional therapies: CAR-M therapy can synergize with conventional chemotherapies to enhance antitumor efficacy. In a pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) model, CAR-M-c-MET cells exhibited strong phagocytic and tumor-killing abilities, and significantly improved the cytotoxic effects of multiple chemotherapeutic agents without increasing toxicity. Intraperitoneal administration of CAR-M-c-MET led to efficient tumor infiltration and substantial tumor growth inhibition in vivo, accompanied by increased secretion of immune-stimulatory cytokines [75].

Beyond cell-based strategies, biomaterial approaches further expand CAR-M’s therapeutic potential. CAR-M-derived exosome-drug conjugates enable precise tumor targeting and enhance T cell activation, CD8⁺ differentiation, and M1 macrophage polarization [160].

However, to achieve durable clinical efficacy, further optimization of CAR-M’s intrinsic structure and signaling domains remains essential. Although recent advances have shown encouraging preclinical outcomes, the overall therapeutic performance of CAR-M is still under active investigation. Current studies indicate that CAR-M may be less potent than CAR-T cells under identical effector-to-target ratios [53], and the potential for adverse events such as CRS and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) cannot be entirely excluded. One critical area for improvement lies in the intracellular signaling domain of CAR-M constructs, particularly by incorporating cytokine-related elements to support macrophage proliferation and persistence. For example, granulocyte/macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) can be integrated as part of the intracellular signaling module to facilitate CAR expression and enhance CAR-M expansion [161, 162]. Additionally, genetic engineering to enable the secretion of type II phospholipase A2 (PLA2) may further potentiate CAR-M proliferation by promoting GM-CSF release [163]. The tumor microenvironment is not a simple balance between M1 and M2 macrophages but rather the outcome of complex immune dynamics. Better strategies to address the inevitable immunosuppressive environment and extend the survival of CAR-M in vivo are needed. To enhance the specificity of CAR-M and avoid unintended damage, logic gates (AND, OR, NOT) can be applied in CAR design. The development of synNotch-CAR is one example of this approach [164, 165]. Mammals express Notch1-4, which binds to ligands such as Delta-like 1, Delta-like 3, Delta-like 4, Jagged 1, and Jagged 2. The intracellular region of Notch contains nuclear localization signals, transcriptional regulatory domains, and RAM domains. To target specific tumors, the extracellular segment of Notch can be modified with scFv, and the intracellular region can be replaced with Gal4-VP64, LexA-VP64, or Gal4-KRAB (Gal4/LexA being DNA-binding domains, and VP64/KRAB serving as transcriptional regulators) [166–169]. This can collaborate with the CAR pathway to create logic gates.

For the AND gate, the target cell must express A, B, and C simultaneously for the target cells to be killed: "[Target cell expression A] AND [Target cell expression B] AND [Target cell expression C] → kill target cells." An OFF gate targets antigens present on normal cells but not cancer cells, requiring a transcriptional activation domain that triggers apoptotic proteins, with tBID being the most effective protein tested. The OR gate does not necessarily require synNotch participation, as it only requires two or more targeted CARs. However, OR gates can be combined with other logic gates: for example, if a target cell expresses A or B or C, the target cell is killed. Such logic gates have been implemented in CAR-T/NK cells [165, 170–173] and can also be used to improve the precision of CAR-M phagocytosis. The off-tumor application of CAR-M

As a new powerful tool for targeted therapy, tumors are not the only battlefield for CAR-M. On the contrary, many diseases with specific targets could also be new fields for the development of CAR-M, such as improving the abnormal environment within intervertebral discs, participating in drug delivery to repair damaged intervertebral discs, or eliminating severe infections by recognizing specific antigens of foreign pathogens, and diseases caused by abnormal metabolite accumulation, etc. This section will briefly describe the new functions of CAR-M in addition to anti-tumor.

SARS-CoV-2

During the period from 2019 to 2021, the lack of targeted therapies for COVID-19 (2019 coronavirus disease) caused huge losses in human lives and property, especially for severe cases. Even with adequate symptomatic treatment and nutritional support, most of the disease progression could not be contained. Hu's team creatively developed CAR-M targeting the S protein, attempting to combat COVID-19 with the phagocytic effect unique to macrophages. Interestingly, among the multiple intracellular segments they tried, CAR-M with intracellular segments of MEGF10, FcRγ, and CD3ζ all mediated antigen-specific phagocytosis or killing effects, resulting in a reduction in the load of SARS-CoV-2. Surprisingly, CAR-M with the intracellular segment of MERTK did not trigger antigen-specific phagocytosis or killing effects, but CARMERTK-M did reduce the viral particle load without upregulating the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This team called this 'immunologically silent' scavenger effect [175].

inflammation-related depression

More and more evidence indicates that the activation of neuroinflammation induced by microglia plays a crucial role in the pathophysiology of depression [134]. Therefore, targeting the central nervous system to reduce neuroinflammation holds great promise for the treatment of depression. Wang's team developed a CAR-M targeting the aldehyde-modified cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein-4 (CTLA-4) for this disease, to deliver the optical corresponding system UZPM for treating inflammation-related depression. The CAR-M-UZPM drug delivery system can effectively penetrate the BBB, target the small glial cells in the central nervous system, thereby inhibiting the M1-type polarization of microglia, generating a sustained vaccine-like anti-inflammatory effect, and preventing the occurrence and development of inflammation-related depression [176].

Alzheimer’s disease (AD)

The deposition of β-amyloid protein (Aβ) plaques is considered to be a key initial triggering factor in the pathophysiology of AD [177]. Although the exact pathophysiology of AD is not fully understood, clinical trials targeting antibodies against the aggregated form of β-amyloid protein (Aβ) have shown that reducing amyloid plaques can alleviate the cognitive decline in early-stage AD patients [178, 179]. The Carl J DeSelm team creatively used CAR-MS targeting Aβ to target and degrade amyloid plaques. When injected into the hippocampus, the first-generation CAR-M had limited persistence and could not significantly reduce the plaque load. The team improved the intracellular segment to enable CAR-M to secrete M-CSF to self-maintain without exogenous cytokines. The "enhanced CAR-M" that secretes cytokines had a higher survival rate in the brain niche and significantly reduced the local plaque load in the body [180].

Cardiac fibrosis

It has now been proven that excessive myocardial fibrosis has a detrimental effect on the pathological remodeling after myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury (I/R), and targeted regulation of myocardial fibrosis may provide protective and therapeutic advantages. The expression of FIBROBLAST ACTIVATION PROTEIN (FAP) after I/R in the mouse heart. CAR-Ms targeting FAP specifically target cardiac fibroblasts expressing FAP in the mouse heart after I/R and exhibit significant phagocytic effects, ultimately improving the heart function of mice to a certain extent, and even providing long-term cardiac protection against I/R [181].

In addition to the aforementioned diseases, CAR technology is also widely used to address cellular aging [182] and other diseases. It is not difficult to observe that in attempts outside of tumors, CAR-Ms not only participate in treatment as direct disease killers, but can also undertake the role of drug targeted delivery carriers. This has significant implications for the use of CAR-Ms in anti-tumor treatment and also enriches the usage scenarios of CAR-Ms and innovates the methods of precise drug delivery.

Conclusions and prospects

This review briefly outlines the preparation method for CAR-M cells and reviews recent research on CAR-M in tumors, focusing primarily on preclinical studies and its anti-tumor mechanisms. Additionally, CAR-NK and other cell types may offer unexpected benefits when used in combination for CAR-NK-mediated triple immunotherapy, which integrates specific recognition immunity, innate immunity, and adaptive immunity. Moreover, combining CAR-M with monoclonal antibodies could further enhance its anti-tumor activity. Despite the promising potential of CAR-M therapy, immune cell inhibition within the suppressive tumor microenvironment remains a challenge. Further development of targeted CAR-M strategies is necessary to overcome this obstacle. Approaches used in CAR-T/NK therapies to disrupt inhibitory immune factor pathways, such as targeting TGF-β [183], may also be applicable to CAR-M therapy. Additionally, cancer cell-derived exosomal miRNAs have been shown to contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and inhibit T cell activation in colorectal cancer (CRC) [184]. Specially designed miRNA-targeted CAR-M could potentially address this issue through endocytosis, offering a unique anti-tumor mechanism. Ultimately, CAR-M may bring transformative advancements to tumor immunotherapy. Furthermore, summarizing the entire review, we believe that in the upcoming preclinical studies of CAR-M, the design of multi-targeted CAR-M logic gates, more convenient CAR-M generation techniques, as well as the comparison of various CAR-immune cell anti-tumor functions and the research on combined anti-tumor effects, may lead to surprising breakthroughs.
