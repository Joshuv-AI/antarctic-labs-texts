Novel clinical and genetic insights into Gitelman syndrome from 95 Chinese patients
Authors: Xiaomeng Shi, Lu Zhang, Xing Chen, Peijin Bai, Leping Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGitelman syndrome (GS) is a rare tubulopathy with clinical and genetic heterogeneity. This study aimed to investigate the characteristics of Chinese GS patients.MethodsThe diagnosis of GS was established by combining clinical phenotypes with genetic testing, after which the clinical, biochemical, and genetic data were statistically analyzed.ResultsWe reported 95 Chinese GS patients aged 2–52 years. The younger group (≤ 16 years) had more frequent febrile episodes (20.4% vs. 4.3%, P = 0.028) and nausea/vomiting (12.2% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.027) but fewer paresthesia/numbness (20.4% vs. 43.5%, P = 0.026) and palpitations (8.2% vs. 37.0%, P = 0.001), along with higher serum potassium and magnesium levels (2.86 ± 0.45 mmol/L vs. 2.67 ± 0.38 mmol/L, P = 0.034; 0.65 ± 0.14 mmol/L vs. 0.58 ± 0.16 mmol/L, P = 0.031) than the older group (> 16 years). Moreover, serum potassium and magnesium levels were positively correlated and both were negatively correlated with age. Additionally, Among 170 detected SLC12A3 variants, 73 distinct variants were identified, including six novel ones. The compound heterozygous group exhibited higher serum magnesium levels compared to the heterozygous and homozygous groups (0.65 ± 0.17 mmol/L vs. 0.56 ± 0.09 mmol/L, P = 0.015; 0.65 ± 0.17 mmol/L vs. 0.51 ± 0.07 mmol/L, P < 0.001). Age at diagnosis was associated with variant types.ConclusionThe study characterized the phenotypic and genotypic features of Chinese GS patients, highlighting age and mutation genotype as key factors influencing phenotype, underscoring the importance of standardized potassium and magnesium supplementation, and expanding the known mutation spectrum with novel variants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40246-025-00828-4.

Introduction

Gitelman syndrome (GS) is an autosomal recessive salt-losing tubulopathy that is characterized by renal potassium wasting, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypocalciuria, metabolic alkalosis, and hyperreninemic hyperaldosteronism [1, 2]. Although rare, GS might be the most common inherited tubulopathy, with a prevalence of 1 to 10 per 40,000 and potentially more in Asia [3]. GS was previously thought to occur mainly in adolescents and adults [1], but with the development of sequencing technology, more children and even neonates have been diagnosed [4]. However, there is a lack of research on the relationship between age and this disease, so whether there is a difference between younger and older patient populations remained unknown. In addition, there is a wide variety in severity of this disease ranging from asymptomatic to severe symptoms such as muscle weakness, cramps and paralysis [5]. Severe hypokalemia can lead to fatal arrhythmias, and long-term electrolyte disorders may cause growth retardation in children with GS. The chronic nature and potential serious complications pose significant challenges to the clinic, necessitating consideration of long-term consequences such as chronic kidney disease, chondrocalcinosis, secondary hypertension and management during pregnancy [6].

GS, also known as familial hypokalemia-hypomagnesemia, is caused by mutations in genes encoding the sodium, chloride, and magnesium transporters in the apical membrane of the distal convoluted tubule. The changes in channel activity can lead to varying degrees of electrolyte abnormalities [7]. Among them, the SLC12A3 gene encodes the thiazide-sensitive sodium chloride cotransporter (NCCT), which is responsible for the reabsorption of 5–10% of filtered Na+ and Cl− [8]. The major clinical characteristics of GS is electrolyte disturbances, particularly hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia, which are critical for the diagnosis and management of this syndrome. Most patients present with subtle clinical manifestations or show no significant abnormalities on physical examination, only showing persistent hypokalemia with or without hypomagnesemia in laboratory tests. Notably, the presence of hypomagnesemia is highly predictive for the clinical diagnosis of GS [9]. The accurate measurement and interpretation of these electrolyte imbalances are essential for understanding disease progression and developing effective treatment strategies. The sensitivity and specificity of electrolyte abnormalities as diagnostic criteria highlight their critical role in the clinical assessment of GS.

The SLC12A3 gene is located on chromosome 16 at band q13 and spans approximately 29 kb, containing 26 exons. As sequencing technology evolves, more than 500 SLC12A3 mutations have been discovered [10, 11]. GS exhibits genetic heterogeneity, with diverse genotypes and mutations widely distributed, showing variability among patients of different ethnicities and regions. Identifying novel pathogenic mutations could broaden the genetic spectrum of GS, offering deeper insights into its molecular mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets. Furthermore, exploring genotype-phenotype correlations is essential to enhance the understanding of this rare disease [12].

This study collected clinical and genetic data from 95 Chinese GS patients with a considerable age span and balanced gender distribution, aiming to analyze their phenotypic and genotypic characteristics, explore the relationship between age and disease, investigate genotype-phenotype correlations, and identify novel pathogenic variants. Our findings may provide a framework for understanding the complexity of GS, providing valuable insights into diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for the disease.

Subjects and methods

Research subjects

This study enrolled 95 patients with GS between September 2019 and June 2024, including 51 males and 44 females from 89 different families, all of Han Chinese ethnicity. These patients were diagnosed at the Affiliated Qingdao Municipal Hospital of Qingdao University or recruited nationwide via clinical records collected through an online platform (QQ group ID: 372770972), according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) Controversies Conference criteria [1]. All patients were screened to exclude other renal and extrarenal causes of hypokalemia, such as Bartter syndrome, Cushing syndrome, primary hyperaldosteronism, inadequate potassium intake, and abnormal potassium distribution. None of the patients had a history of long-term use of potassium-sparing diuretics, laxatives or nephrotoxic drugs.

Phenotype analysis

General information, age at diagnosis, chief complaints and first symptoms (e.g., fatigue, muscle weakness, cramps, paresthesia, numbness, paralysis, dizziness, palpitations, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and abdominal pain) were collected from GS patients. Their laboratory findings at initial presentation were retrospectively analyzed, including serum and urinary biochemical indexes, urinary electrolytes excretion fraction and other relevant indicators.

Mutation analysis

Genetic analysis included three main steps: mutation screening, pathogenicity assessment, and validation of suspected pathogenic mutations. Peripheral blood samples were collected from patients and their family members, and genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China) according to the instructions. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) was used to analyze the entire SLC12A3 gene in GS patients. Sequencing reads were then aligned to the reference human genome (UCSC hg19) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA) [13], and variant annotation was performed with Privar toolkit to identify their clinical significance [14]. Additionally, the pathogenicity of novel variants was predicted by web-based programs such as MutationTaster, PolyPhen-2, PROVEAN and PANTHER, and variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines [15]. The variants identified through NGS were validated by Sanger sequencing in probands and their immediate relatives. The detected variants and their flanking regions were amplified by PCR and directly sequenced using the 3500XL Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA). When heterozygous copy-number variant (CNV) was suspected, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) was performed to analyze the SLC12A3 gene using the SALAS-MLPA Probemix P136 kit (MRC-Hollad, Netherlands).

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± SD and differences among groups were analyzed by Student’s t test (comparison of two groups) or one-way ANOVA (three groups or more). Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test for two-group comparisons and the Kruskal-Wallis test for comparisons among multiple groups. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess associations between age and serum potassium levels, age and serum magnesium levels, and serum potassium and magnesium levels. A regression line was plotted using simple linear regression to illustrate the trend. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 28.0, and graphics were plotted with GraphPad Prism 9.0.

Results

Phenotypic characteristics

A total of 95 patients with GS were enrolled in the study, with their clinical and biochemical characteristics summarized in Table 1 and S1. The Median age at diagnosis was 16 (interquartile range 7–28) years, ranging from 2 to 52 years. The male-to-female ratio was 1.16 to 1 (51 vs. 44). The clinical presentations were various, with fatigue being the most common symptom (60/95, 63.2%), followed by paresthesia and numbness (30/95, 31.6%), palpitations (21/95, 22.1%), febrile episodes (12/95, 12.6%), cramps (11/95, 11.6%), and muscle weakness (9/95, 9.5%). Using the median age at diagnosis as the cutoff for analysis, the patients were divided into the younger group (≤ 16 years, median age at diagnosis 7 years, 49 cases) and the older group (> 16 years, median age at diagnosis 28 years, 46 cases). The results indicated that the older group was more likely to present with paresthesia/numbness (43.5% vs. 20.4%, P = 0.026) and palpitations (37.0% vs. 8.2%, P = 0.001), whereas the younger group exhibited a higher frequency of febrile episodes (20.4% vs. 4.3%, P = 0.028) and nausea/vomiting (12.2% vs. 0.0%, P = 0.027) (Table 1). No statistically significant differences were observed in other clinical characteristics between the two groups (Table S1). Additionally, there were no differences in clinical presentations between males and females (Table 1 and S1).

The main electrolyte abnormalities of interest for this study were hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia. As shown in Table 1 and S1, the incidence of hypokalemia was 95.8% (91/95), and the average serum potassium level was 2.77 ± 0.42 mmol/L. Moreover, hypomagnesemia was identified in 69.6% (64/95) of patients, with the average serum magnesium level of 0.61 ± 0.15 mmol/L. The analysis demonstrated significantly higher serum potassium levels in the younger group compared to the older group (2.86 ± 0.45 mmol/L vs. 2.67 ± 0.38 mmol/L, P = 0.034). Similarly, the younger group had higher serum magnesium levels than the older group (0.65 ± 0.14 mmol/L vs. 0.58 ± 0.16 mmol/L, P = 0.031). There were no significant differences in serum sodium or chloride levels between the two groups, and no significant differences in electrolyte levels were observed between males and females. Furthermore, based on the GS clinical guidelines recommending a serum potassium treatment target of 3.0 mmol/L, patients were classified into Group 1 (serum potassium ≥ 3.0 mmol/L, 31 cases) and Group 2 (serum potassium < 3.0 mmol/L, 64 cases), and intergroup differences in electrolyte levels were analyzed. The reported electrolyte levels of all patients were shown in Fig. 1. Serum magnesium, sodium and chloride levels were significantly higher in Group 1 than in Group 2 (magnesium: 0.68 ± 0.14 mmol/L vs. 0.58 ± 0.15 mmol/L, P = 0.003; sodium: 138.91 ± 2.14 mmol/L vs. 137.65 ± 3.75 mmol/L, P = 0.043; chloride: 98.91 ± 3.65 mmol/L vs. 96.00 [93.00, 98.00 mmol/L, P < 0.001). Moreover, our study demonstrated that serum potassium and magnesium levels were negatively correlated with age at diagnosis (r = -0.233, P = 0.023 and r = -0.250, P = 0.016, respectively), while a positive correlation was observed between serum potassium and magnesium levels (r = 0.285, P = 0.006), as shown in Fig. 2.

Analysis of variants

Pathogenic variants in the SLC12A3 gene were identified in ninety-two patients, with 170 variants detected. As shown in Fig. 3A, missense variants were the most common type (71.8%), followed by splicing variants at 8.8%. Among these variants, the high-frequency variants were c.179 C > T (17.1%), c.1456G > A (12.4%), c.965-1_976delins ACCGAAAATTTT (5.3%) and c.1077 C > G (4.7%), with all recurrent variants displayed in Fig. 3B. Seventy-three kinds of variants were identified, consisting of 54 (74.0%) point variants and 19 (26.0%) non-point variants. Figure 3C showed the proportion of these variants, including 47 (64.4%) missense variants, 3 (4.1%) nonsense variants, 8 (11.0%) frameshift variants, 4 (5.5%) deletion variants, 2 (2.7%) insertion variants, 1 (1.4%) indel variants, 4 (5.5%) splicing variants and 4 (5.5%) intronic variants. In addition, six novel variants were found and predicted to be ‘disease causing’ by the bioinformatics software MutationTaster (Table 2). According to comprehensive pathogenicity predictions and ACMG standards and guidelines, three frameshift variants (c.381delG, c.2420-2446delGTGCCAGGCCATCAGTCTCTGGCGCTT and c.2492delT) and one splicing variant (c.742–2 A > G) were classified as ‘pathogenic variants’, while two missense variants (c.953T > G and c.1850 A > G) could only be classified as ‘likely pathogenic variants’.

Genotypic characteristics and their correlation with phenotype

Among the ninety-two genetically confirmed patients, sixteen carried a single SLC12A3 variant, two harbored three missense variants, and the remaining possessed two variants, resulting in the identification of 19 genotypes (Table 3). The list of detailed variants was shown in Table S2. Based on different genotypes, patients were classified into the heterozygous group (16 cases), homozygous group (12 cases), and compound heterozygous group (64 cases), as shown in Table 3. Statistical analysis revealed that serum magnesium levels in the compound heterozygous group were significantly higher than those in the heterozygous and homozygous groups (0.65 ± 0.17 mmol/L vs. 0.56 ± 0.09 mmol/L, P = 0.015; 0.65 ± 0.17 mmol/L vs. 0.51 ± 0.07 mmol/L, P < 0.001; Fig. 4). However, no significant differences were observed in other serum electrolyte levels (serum potassium, sodium and chloride) among the groups (Table S3). Additionally, this study analyzed the relationship between variant types and age at diagnosis. As shown in Table 4, missense variants were the predominant type in both the younger and older groups, accounting for 67.8% (61/90) and 76.3% (61/80), respectively. The 122 missense variants were evenly distributed between the two groups (50% each), whereas other variant types exhibited uneven distribution. Specifically, frameshift (66.7%, 8/12), deletion (100%, 5/5), indel (1 case) and splicing variants (73.3%, 11/15) were predominantly found in the younger group, while nonsense (66.7%, 4/6), insertion (100%, 3/3) and intronic variants (66.7%, 4/6) were more common in the older group.

Discussion

GS often presents insidiously with mild symptoms, leading to frequent misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis. Typical clinical features include hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, metabolic alkalosis, hypocalciuria, and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system [3, 16]. In this study, 95 GS patients were enrolled, of whom 92 received genetic testing. The results revealed that 76 patients had biallelic variants in SLC12A3, confirming the molecular diagnosis of GS, while 16 patients carried a single variant, with the diagnosis established based on clinical and genetic findings. The remaining 3 patients declined genetic testing and were diagnosed based on typical clinical indicators according to the guideline [1]. The typical clinical indicators of these 19 patients were detailed in Table S4. These enrolled patients had a wide age range (2–52 years), with a median age at diagnosis of 16 years and a balanced gender distribution, which facilitated the creation of age groups (younger group, ≤ 16 years; older group, >16 years) and provided high-quality clinical data for analyzing the phenotypic characteristics and their relationship with age. The majority of patients initially presented with fatigue and sought medical attention only when more obvious symptoms emerged, such as numbness, palpitations, febrile episodes, cramps or muscle weakness. Notably, older patients were more likely to exhibit numbness and palpitations, whereas younger patients commonly presented with febrile episodes, nausea and vomiting. GS should be highly suspected in the presence of persistent unexplained hypokalemia. Further analysis of age and electrolyte levels revealed that older patients had significantly lower serum potassium and magnesium levels compared to younger patients, with both potassium and magnesium levels showing a downward trend as age increases. These findings emphasized the need for long-term electrolyte monitoring and standardized supplementation in GS patients to prevent both under- and over-supplementation.

As a salt-losing tubulopathy, GS treatment primarily focuses on electrolyte supplementation, aiming for serum potassium ≥ 3.0 mmol/L and serum magnesium ≥ 0.6 mmol/L, as recommended by guidelines [1]. The study results indicated that when serum potassium levels were ≥ 3.0 mmol/L, the levels of other electrolytes (magnesium, sodium, and chloride) were generally higher compared to when potassium levels were below target. This suggested that achieving target potassium levels should be prioritized during treatment to maintain electrolyte balance, thereby facilitating disease control. Irregular treatment due to mild symptoms should be avoided. In daily management, electrolyte loss due to excessive sweating, urination, and diarrhea should be prevented, and excessive potassium supplementation should be avoided to reduce the occurrence of side effects such as abdominal pain and diarrhea. Furthermore, the correlation analysis between serum potassium and magnesium levels revealed that elevated serum potassium levels were associated with increased magnesium levels, supporting the notion that magnesium supplementation facilitated potassium absorption [17]. Therefore, GS patients with hypomagnesemia should pay attention to monitoring serum magnesium levels and supplementing magnesium as needed. Additionally, dietary management plays a crucial role [9], and patients are advised to consume foods rich in potassium (e.g., bananas and oranges), magnesium (e.g., nuts and leafy greens) and sodium (e.g., moderate amounts of salt) to support electrolyte balance [18, 19].

Pathogenic variants in the SLC12A3 gene is the molecular standard for diagnosing GS. This study identified 73 SLC12A3 variant sites, including missense, nonsense, frameshift, deletion, insertion, indel, splicing, and intronic variants, with missense variants being the most common, consistent with the previous study [20]. The high-frequency variants (c.179 C > T and c.1456G > A) were consistent with those previously reported in the Chinese population [21, 22], while showing no overlap with the seven high-frequency variants found in European populations (c.938 C > T, c.1108 + 1G > T, c.2221G > C, c.2576T > C, c.2581 C > T, c.2883 + 1G > T, and c.2981G > A) [10], suggesting potential racial differences in gene mutations, which may be attributed to founder effects, population genetic background or sample size. Additionally, six novel variants were identified, with their pathogenicity assessed based on ACMG guidelines, thereby expanding the GS-related variation database and providing valuable reference for genetic counseling.

GS is an autosomal recessive disorder, but only a single heterozygous variant was found in 17.4% of patients, suggesting the likely presence of a second undetected variant. Previous studies have reported that approximately 8–30% of patients carry only a single detectable variant, possibly because the other variant is located in a deep intronic region, involves a large fragment deletion or duplication, or occurs outside the SLC12A3 gene [23, 24]. Therefore, genetic screening should employ advanced techniques, including MLPA, whole-exome sequencing (WES), and whole-genome sequencing (WGS), to identify potential variants and enhance diagnostic accuracy [25]. With informed patient consent, copy-number variation screening (MLPA) is recommended for all patients with single variants in order to more comprehensively clarify the disease and investigate the diversity of genetic variations.

GS is phenotypically and genetically heterogeneous, with phenotype-genotype correlations remaining a research focus. However, varying conclusions have been reported, likely due to sample size limitations. The majority of GS patients in this study were compound heterozygotes, consistent with previous reports [1, 21]. We found that patients in the compound heterozygous group presented a milder degree of hypomagnesemia compared to those in the homozygous and heterozygous groups, but no significant differences were observed in serum potassium, sodium or chloride levels among the three groups. We also found all patients with deletions (5 cases) and indel variants (1 case), as well as the majority of those with frameshift and splicing variants, were diagnosed at a younger age. In contrast, all patients with insertions (3 cases) and most with nonsense or intronic variants were diagnosed at an older age. These findings suggested a potential correlation between age at diagnosis and variant types.

Although we provided a relatively comprehensive description of demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations and serum electrolyte levels, gaps remain in other laboratory parameters, such as urinary electrolytes and plasma renin activity. While the correlation between phenotype and genotype in GS was explored, the limited sample size prevented a full investigation, emphasizing the need for a larger cohort in future studies.

As we enter the age of precision medicine, the understanding of GS has improved, but challenges in diagnosis and management remain. First, comprehensive clinical evaluation of patients must be strengthened. Second, differential diagnosis from Gitelman-like syndrome is crucial, as it shares clinical features with GS and involves multiple causative factors, including genetic (mitochondrial mutations) and acquired (drug-induced, chronic diarrhea and vomiting) components. In addition, fundamental research is needed to investigate the molecular pathogenesis of GS and identify novel therapeutic targets.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study analyzed the phenotypic and genotypic characteristics and their correlation in Chinese GS patients, explored the impact of age and genotype on clinical phenotype, and identified six novel SLC12A3 variants, providing valuable insights for the diagnosis, treatment, and genetic counseling of GS patients. We advocate for the establishment of a nationwide research platform to promote the development of precision medicine for rare kidney diseases.
