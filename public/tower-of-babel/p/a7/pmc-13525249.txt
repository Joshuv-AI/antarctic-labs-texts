Associated Reconstruction Of Acl And Lcl Tears Plus Proximal Tibiofibular Joint Instability With Asymmetric Hamstring Graft
Authors: Alejandro Espejo-Reina, Giovanni Fruner, María Josefa Espejo-Reina, Maximiano Lombardo-Torre, Enrique Sevillano-Pérez, Alejandro Espejo-Baena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Proximal tibiofibular joint (PTFJ) dislocation is a rare injury, and if missed or misdiagnosed, it can lead to chronic PTFJ instability, especially in the case of a multiligamentous knee injury. Optimal treatment is poorly understood and includes a vast arsenal of surgical techniques.Indications:Surgical treatment is indicated in cases of PTFJ dislocations that are irreducible by closed means, chronic symptomatic dislocations/instability, and first-time dislocations with bony fracture. Risk of persistent symptoms has been reported to be higher than acceptable with conservative management. Anatomical reconstruction is necessary to restore the optimal PTFJ biomechanics and to improve clinical outcomes. The case of a patient with an uncommon set of multiligamentous knee injuries, that is, PTFJ dislocation, anterior cruciate ligament (ACL), and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) tear, is presented, along with a surgical approach not previously described in the literature.Study Design:Case report and a surgical technique; Level of evidence, 4.Technique Description:A surgical technique is described for the combined reconstruction of ACL, LCL, and PTFJ injuries using asymmetrically prepared hamstring autografts. The procedure includes an extra-articular lateral reinforcement through a modified Lemaire procedure, utilizing a single femoral tunnel. Fixation is achieved with a tibial suspension device for ACL reconstruction, a shared femoral fixation for both the ACL and LCL grafts, and a combined tibial fixation for the LCL and PTFJ reconstruction.Results:The present technique provides a suitable option for the rare combined injury involving ACL and LCL tears along with PTFJ disruption, using a single hamstring graft, a modified Lemaire procedure, and only 3 fixation points for the reconstruction.Discussion/Conclusion:The reconstruction of multiligament knee injuries often requires multiple grafts—including allografts—along with a high risk of tunnel convergence and loss of bone stock. Video Transcript

This video demonstrates a technique for proximal tibiofibular joint reconstruction in the context of a chronic multiligament injury.

Background

The proximal tibiofibular joint (PTFJ) represents the synovial articulation between the proximal aspects of the posterolateral tibia and the anteromedial fibula. It is stabilized by thicker anterior ligaments and thinner posterior ligaments. Additionally, the anterior ligamentous complex is biomechanically stronger. 5 This explains why anterolateral dislocations are the most common (85% of PTFJ dislocations) and are frequently the result of a fall onto a flexed knee with foot inversion and plantar flexion, which leads to the disruption of the posterior ligamentous complex. 4

PTFJ dislocation may occur in multiligamentous knee injuries, 5 where it may be missed or misdiagnosed. The true incidence of PTFJ instability is likely higher than previously reported. It can present as frank dislocations, subtle symptoms of lateral knee pain, discomfort during activity, or irritation of the common peroneal nerve, and can be confused with posterolateral corner, iliotibial band, or lateral meniscal injury. 4 Surgery indication includes irreducible PTFJ dislocations, chronic symptomatic dislocations/instability, and first-time dislocations with bony fracture. 6

In this video, a combined reconstruction technique is described for anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) tears with PTFJ dislocation using an asymmetric hamstring autograft and a modified Lemaire procedure, with a single femoral tunnel. The fixation strategy consists of using a tibial suspension for ACL reconstruction, a shared femoral interference screw for ACL and LCL grafts, and combined tibial fixation for LCL and PTFJ grafts. The level of evidence of the present work is 4, as it consists of a case report and a surgical technique.

Indications

Anamnesis

A 53-year-old female doctor presented to the clinic with chronic symptoms after having a ski accident on her right knee 18 years before, with the diagnosis of partial ACL tear, which was treated nonoperatively.

She experienced occasional instability, and her main symptom was pain over the posterolateral aspect of the knee, with occasional radiating discomfort along the lateral aspect of the leg.

The patient had experienced temporary improvement with a pronating insole, but the symptoms returned afterward. She had been having recurrent sprains of the LCL for the past 5 to 6 years. Two months before coming, she had a new episode of “giving way” sensation, and she was walking on crutches at the time of the consultation.

Physical Examination

The patient's body mass index was 26.5 kg/m2. She had a 50° valgus alignment and a full range of motion. She felt pain with pressure at the PTFJ; slight lateral laxity with forced varus stress and low-grade positive anterior drawer, Lachman, and pivot-shift tests were found.

Imaging Studies

The patient had preserved joint lines, with the Mickulicz line passing tangentially over the medial tibial spine on long-leg radiographs, and moderate lateral opening on varus stress radiographs. On the magnetic resonance imaging, an ACL tear (proximal desinsertion) with intact menisci and minor PTFJ irregularities could be found. The PTFJ showed minor morphologic alterations, including small osteophytes, on the computed tomography scan. The final diagnosis included ACL and LCL tears and PTFJ instability.

Preoperative Planning

The preoperative planning was designed as follows:

ACL reconstruction with asymmetrically folded hamstring graft, drilling 1 tunnel on the tibia and 1 on the femur.

ACL reconstruction with asymmetrically folded hamstring graft, drilling 1 tunnel on the tibia and 1 on the femur.

LCL reconstruction with hamstring graft remnant, using the same femoral tunnel from ACL reconstruction and drilling 1 tunnel on the fibular head.

LCL reconstruction with hamstring graft remnant, using the same femoral tunnel from ACL reconstruction and drilling 1 tunnel on the fibular head.

PTFJ reconstruction with the remaining graft using the same fibular head tunnel from LCL reconstruction and drilling a tibial tunnel different from ACL reconstruction.

PTFJ reconstruction with the remaining graft using the same fibular head tunnel from LCL reconstruction and drilling a tibial tunnel different from ACL reconstruction.

Modified Lemaire procedure with no additional tunnels, using the same femoral tunnel from ACL reconstruction.

Modified Lemaire procedure with no additional tunnels, using the same femoral tunnel from ACL reconstruction.

The fixation strategy consists of using a tibial suspension for ACL reconstruction, a shared femoral interference screw for ACL and LCL grafts, and combined tibial fixation for LCL and PTFJ grafts.

Technique Description

The present technique is adapted from a procedure previously described by the authors and published in 2018 for combined ACL and LCL reconstruction, which utilized a tibial suspension device and the remaining hamstring graft for LCL reconstruction. 2

Patient Positioning

The patient is placed in the supine position under regional anesthesia. A pneumatic tourniquet is applied around the thigh, and the limb is secured in a leg holder with the knee flexed to 90°.

Arthroscopic Examination

The authors typically use a transtendinous anterior portal for visualization during cruciate ligament surgery. The anteromedial portal is used for instrumentation. With the aid of a probe, the ACL rupture is confirmed along with the integrity of the remaining intra-articular structures, and lateral compartment gapping is assessed under varus stress. The ACL remnant and the medial wall of the lateral femoral condyle are prepared using a radiofrequency probe and a motorized shaver.

Lateral Compartment Approach

A curved lateral incision is made on the lateral aspect of the knee, extending from approximately 8 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle to the level of the fibular neck. The PTFJ is exposed, and abnormal joint mobility is verified. Then, 2 longitudinal incisions are made over the iliotibial band (ITB), and a 10-mm-wide strip is harvested for anterolateral reinforcement. The proximal end is detached approximately 3 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle, while the distal insertion at Gerdy's tubercle is left intact. A traction suture is placed on the free end of the strip.

Graft Harvest and Preparation

A 3-cm oblique incision is made anteromedially to the tibial tuberosity. The semitendinosus and gracilis tendons are located and harvested using a standard tendon stripper.

Both tendons are prepared on a side table, and traction sutures are placed at each end to allow the construction of an asymmetric graft with 2 arms:

A short arm for ACL reconstruction, composed of 4 tendon strands, approximately 9 to 10 cm in length, and no <8 mm in diameter.

A short arm for ACL reconstruction, composed of 4 tendon strands, approximately 9 to 10 cm in length, and no <8 mm in diameter.

A long arm for the LCL and PTFJ reconstruction, using the 2 remaining tendon strands, approximately 6 to 7 cm in length and 5 to 6 mm in diameter.

A long arm for the LCL and PTFJ reconstruction, using the 2 remaining tendon strands, approximately 6 to 7 cm in length and 5 to 6 mm in diameter.

The graft is mounted on an adjustable cortical suspension device (ProCinch; Stryker).

Tunnel Creation

Using an ACL guide, the femoral tunnel (9 mm in diameter) is created using an outside-in technique. This tunnel will accommodate both the ACL graft and the ITB strip for anterolateral reinforcement. Then, a blind tibial socket is created using an outside-in technique with an 8 mm retrograde reamer.

Graft Passage

A nitinol wire with an eyelet is passed in caudo-cranial direction through the tibial socket and the femoral tunnel, exiting the joint through the femoral tunnel. Simultaneously, the traction suture of the ITB strip is passed through the femoral tunnel and exits via the anteromedial portal. By pulling it, the ITB strip is drawn into the femoral tunnel, completing the anterolateral reinforcement. Next, the shuttle suture of the suspension device is passed through the eyelet of the nitinol wire and pulled in a craniocaudal direction, advancing the asymmetric graft into the joint. The long arm of the graft, which remains extra-articular, is reserved for the reconstruction of the LCL and the PTFJ.

ACL Graft Fixation

Tibial fixation of the graft is secured using the adjustable suspension device. Once the device is tensioned, proximal traction is applied to the ACL graft through the femoral tunnel, and distal traction is applied to the ITB strip suture via the anteromedial portal. Both grafts are then simultaneously fixed in the femoral tunnel using an interference screw of the same diameter as the femoral tunnel, with the knee flexed at 30°. This provides a single femoral fixation point for the ACL graft, the anterolateral reinforcement, and the LCL reconstruction.

LCL Reconstruction

The remaining portion of the asymmetric hamstring graft is used for LCL reconstruction. The fibular head is exposed, and a tunnel is drilled (6 mm diameter in this case) in anteroposterior, caudocranial, and lateromedial direction, using an ACL guide. The traction suture is passed under the ITB and then through the fibular tunnel in the reverse direction (posterior to anterior and proximal to distal).

Proximal Tibiofibular Joint Fixation

After the graft passes through the fibular head, a tunnel of the same diameter as the graft (same diameter as the fibular head's tunnel) is drilled in the tibia, just anterior to the proximal tibiofibular joint line. The graft is passed through this tunnel and fixed with an interference screw 1 mm larger than the tunnel diameter, with the knee at 30° of flexion.

Potential Complications (How to Avoid)

One of the main concerns when approaching the lateral aspect of the knee is the proximity of the common peroneal nerve and the popliteal neurovascular bundle.1,7 Injury to these structures can be avoided by careful dissection, placement of a retractor posterior to the tibia when drilling, and maintaining constant visual control during tunnel preparation. Additionally, this technique can only be performed if the harvested hamstring autograft provides sufficient length and diameter to reproduce the ACL, LCL, and PTFJ reconstructions; if the graft dimensions are inadequate, alternative strategies should be considered.

Postoperative Management

Weeks 0 to 2: brace in knee extension. Isometric quadriceps contractions.

Weeks 2 to 6: passive range of motion exercises. Partial weightbearing ambulation with crutches

Weeks 6 to 16: free range of motion. Full weightbearing ambulation without crutches

Since week 16: starts running

Since the 9th month: the patient has been cleared for return to sport. Resumed skiing

1 year postoperatively: medical discharge

Discussion

PTFJ instability is a rare and often underdiagnosed condition that may coexist with cruciate and collateral ligament injuries in the setting of multiligamentous knee trauma. 4 Because of its low incidence, the optimal management strategy remains poorly defined. The present case illustrates a combined reconstruction of the ACL, LCL, and PTFJ using a single asymmetric hamstring autograft, providing a reproducible alternative for multiligament knee injuries.

Conservative management of proximal tibiofibular joint instability has been associated with persistent symptoms, particularly in active patients or in the setting of multiligament knee injuries. Surgical options described in the literature include screw fixation, suspensory fixation devices, or anatomic reconstructions using independent graft constructs.4,6 Although most published reports describe satisfactory short-term clinical outcomes, the available evidence is limited to small case series and technical descriptions, without comparative or high-level data to define an optimal strategy.

Previous reports have described several surgical strategies to restore PTFJ stability. Regarding isolated reconstruction, Kanakamedala et al 3 recently proposed a minimally invasive stabilization technique using an adjustable-length suspensory fixation device, reporting its technical feasibility for isolated PTFJ instability but emphasizing the scarcity of long-term outcome data. Burger et al 1 increased the complexity of the reconstruction employing the biceps femoris and ITB autografts reinforced with suture-tape augmentation, highlighting improved biomechanical stability and smaller incisions compared with earlier methods. Regarding associated procedures, Tollefson et al 7 combined anatomic popliteus tendon and PTFJ reconstruction using separate hamstring grafts to re-establish posterolateral stability. While these techniques report encouraging technical feasibility and early clinical results, they require separate graft constructs and additional tunnels. In multiligament knee reconstruction, this may increase surgical complexity and the theoretical risk of tunnel convergence or unnecessary bone-stock sacrifice. To our knowledge, the present technique is the first to integrate PTFJ reconstruction into a single-graft multiligament construct, minimizing graft harvesting, tunnel number, and risk of convergence.

The main advantage of the current technique lies in the use of an asymmetric hamstring autograft that provides sufficient length and strength to reconstruct 3 distinct structures—ACL, LCL, and PTFJ—through a single femoral tunnel. This configuration preserves bone stock, simplifies fixation, and allows for an anatomic anterolateral reinforcement via a modified Lemaire procedure without additional grafts or implants. The shared fixation strategy reduces the need for allografts, a relevant benefit in multiligament surgery where autograft availability is limited.

Rehabilitation followed standard ACL protocols, and the patient achieved full recovery and returned to skiing within 9 months, supporting the construct's functional validity.

Conclusion

Combined ACL, LCL, and PTFJ reconstruction using an asymmetric hamstring autograft is a safe and feasible technique in selected multiligament knee injuries. By integrating 3 reconstructions within a single graft construct and minimizing tunnel number, it represents a rational alternative in scenarios where graft availability and bone preservation are critical considerations.
