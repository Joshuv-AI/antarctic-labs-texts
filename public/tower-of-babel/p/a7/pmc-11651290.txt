Prion protein pathology in Ubiquilin 2 models of ALS
Authors: Nhat T. Le, Nam Chu, Gunjan Joshi, Nicole R. Higgins, Ouada Nebie, Niyi Adelakun, Marie Butts, Mervyn J. Monteiro
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Mutations in UBQLN2 cause ALS and frontotemporal dementia (FTD). The pathological signature in UBQLN2 cases is deposition of highly unusual types of inclusions in the brain and spinal cord that stain positive for UBQLN2. However, what role these inclusions play in pathogenesis remains unclear. Here we show cellular prion protein (PrPC) is found in UBQLN2 inclusions in both mouse and human neuronal induced pluripotent (IPSC) models of UBQLN2 mutations, evidenced by the presence of aggregated forms of PrPC with UBQLN2 inclusions. Turnover studies indicated that the P497H UBQLN2 mutation slows PrPC protein degradation and leads to mislocalization of PrPC in the cytoplasm. Immunoprecipitation studies indicated UBQLN2 and PrPC bind together in a complex. The abnormalities in PrPC caused by UBQLN2 mutations may be relevant in disease pathogenesis.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive and fatal neurodegenerative disorder. Some ALS patients also develop frontotemporal dementia (FTD) (Kiernan et al., 2011). The hallmark clinical symptoms observed in ALS result from progressive degeneration and loss of motor neurons (MNs). Synaptic loss, which starts well before symptoms begin, is evident in mice and humans in a variety of neurodegenerative conditions (Subramanian and Tremblay, 2021) including familial ALS (Hammer Jr. et al., 1979; Horoupian et al., 1984; Udaka et al., 1986; Kato et al., 1987; Fogarty et al., 2016a; Fogarty et al., 2016b). The abnormalities in synaptic structure can be observed before diagnosis in certain ALS patients (Vucic et al., 2008), suggesting that these alterations are a contributing factor in disease pathogenesis (van Zundert et al., 2012) (Eisen, 2014). However, the underlying neurotoxic mechanisms remain to be established.

Missense mutations in UBQLN2 were identified in 2011 to cause ALS, sometimes with accompanying frontotemporal dementia (FTD) (Deng et al., 2011). Ubiquilin-2 (UBQLN2) is one of five ubiquilin (UBQLN) proteins found in humans (Deng et al., 2011), differing from the other UBQLN proteins by containing a proline-rich-repeat (PRR) domain where most ALS mutations are found. The cellular role of UBQLN proteins is to facilitate the clearance of misfolded proteins through the autophagy and proteasome degradation pathways (Lin et al., 2022). Consistent with their important role, UBQLN2 mutations cause major disturbances in proteostasis leading to a buildup of ubiquitinated protein aggregates in tissues (Deng et al., 2011; Le et al., 2016; Higgins et al., 2021), which may be linked to pathogenesis.

The physiological cellular PrPC form is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchored polypeptide present on the outside leaflet of the cellular membrane of most cell types in mammals. In neurons, PrPC is predominantly located in axons and dendrites (Mironov Jr. et al., 2003). PrPC is involved in several cellular processes, including neuritogenesis, neuronal homeostasis, cell signaling, cell adhesion, and in protection against stress (Wulf et al., 2017). PrPC serves as a cellular receptor for β-sheet-rich neurotoxic proteins (Wulf et al., 2017; Gomes et al., 2019; Scialo et al., 2021; Urrea et al., 2017; Lauren et al., 2009). The mislocalization of PrPC into the cytoplasm is correlated with increased cell death (Grenier et al., 2006). Possible functions of PrPC are suggested by its interaction partners (Wulf et al., 2017). PrPC regulates ion channels and neurotransmitter receptors at the pre- and postsynaptic levels. Stress-induced protein 1 (STI1) protein is known as a cell surface ligand for PrPC and their interaction triggers neuroprotection (Lopes et al., 2005). Interestingly, several STI-1-like motifs are found in UBQLN2, which have been implicated in recruitment of heat-shock proteins and autophagy mediators (Aitio et al., 2010).

Animal models and immortalized cell lines of the ALS-linked UBQLN2 are useful for understanding the mechanisms of pathogenesis and for therapeutic studies. However, most of the studies on them have been done under conditions of protein over-expression including our previous studies with transgenic (tg) mice expressing human mutant UBQLN2 proteins (Le et al., 2016). The advent of induced pluripotent stem cells (IPSCs) technology provides unlimited source of human cells for investigations. There have been numerous studies using human IPSC-derived neurons to study mutant genes involved in ALS (Du et al., 2023), including SOD1, C9ORF7, FUS and TARDBP. However, there is no information available on the properties and neurotoxicity of IPSC-derived neurons carrying ALS-linked UBQLN2 mutations.

Neuropathological analysis of the disease in ALS patients (Deng et al., 2011) and our mutant UBQLN2 (UBQLN2mut) mouse models (Le et al., 2016) revealed the accumulation of ubiquitinated inclusions in the brain and spinal cord (Sc), and TAR-DNA binding protein 43 (TDP-43) pathology. Here, we show PrPC is associated with UBQLN2 pathology in both mouse and human IPSCs derived neuronal models of the disease. We found PrPC accumulates and co-localizes with UBQLN2 inclusions, including TDP-43 in both models. Furthermore, cycloheximide-chase studies indicated mutant UBQLN2 proteins slow the turnover of PrPC, which we presume leads to its mislocalization in the cytoplasm. In addition, we provide evidence that dendritic spine number is reduced in both mouse and neuronal cell models of UBQLN2. Our data thus suggest a potential involvement of PrPC in UBQLN2 pathology and validates the potential of IPSCs-derived neuronal cultures for modeling disease caused by UBQLN2 mutations.

Materials and methods

Mouse brain and SC staining

Brain and SC tissues were obtained from Non-tg B6C3/C57BL/6 J, tg WT UBQLN2, and the ALS/FTD P497S mutant UBQLN2 mouse lines (Le et al., 2016). Tissue lysates were prepared using the protocol described previously (Le et al., 2016). Different thickness cryosections of the brain and SC were cut using a Leica CM3050 cryostat, transferred to glass slides and stained using the procedure described previously (Le et al., 2016). 10 to 14 μm thick sections were cut for all the examinations except for dendritic analysis, when 34 μm sections were cut. The sections were stained with primary antibodies against the following proteins: UBQLN2, ubiquitin, TDP-43 and PrP. Binding of primary antibodies was detected with the appropriate Alexafluor-labeled secondary antibodies. Images of the staining were captured using a Nikon Eclipse Ti A1R confocal microscope using a S Fluor 40×(1.30 oil) objective using either 405, 488, or 561 nm laser lines (Le et al., 2016). Images were analyzed and merged using iVision software (BioVision Technologies). The structure of the dendritic arbor decorated by YFP fluorescence in the CA1, and cortex regions of the brain were captured as a progressive series of 6 to 20 Z-step 0.34 μm images with the Nikon confocal microscopy using an Apo 60×(1.40 oil) objective. The Z-step images were then compressed into a single JPEG image. Dendritic spine density and spine length were measured using NIH Image-J software. The measurements were done for at least 10 to 15 different dendrites in each of three different animals per genotype.

The number of inclusion bodies in different areas of mouse brain and the anterior horn of spinal cord were estimated using software to highlight puncta with a staining intensity above a certain threshold. The inclusions were then filtered according to size and their number counted using iVision software. The number shown is the average of three animals with the same genotype.

Human IPSC cultures

We accessed the iNDI resources from the Jackson Laboratory for Genomic Medicine where the isogenic human IPSC lines carrying UBQLN2mut including P497H and P506T were engineered by an improved Cas9 ribonucleoprotein editing method that yields high rates of biallelic homology-directed repair (Skarnes et al., 2021). Both mutant lines and the control WT KOLF 2.1 line were obtained from Jackson Laboratory. IPSC lines were used for a maximum of 10 passages to avoid chromosomal and genetic aberrations that may appear during long-term passaging. Accordingly, we periodically checked the human IPSC cultures to ensure they did not possess abnormal karyotype. The human IPSC lines were cultured in mTeSR™ medium (STEMCELL Technologies, 85,850) in a Matrigel (BD Matrigel™, hESC-qualified Matrix, 354,277) coated plate. Enzyme-free passaging reagents, ReLeSR™ (STEMCELL Technologies, 100–1438) were used for routine passaging of cells as cell clumps. The media of the IPSC cultures were changed daily for optimal growth.

Neuronal differentiation

Neuronal differentiation was carried out according to the published procedure (Fernandopulle et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2017). Briefly, we generated stable iPSC lines expressing the transcription factors neurogenin-2 (NGN2), SL LIM Homeobox 1 (ISL1), and LIM Homeobox 3 (LHX3) through Lipofectamine Stem-mediated transfection of DNA plasmids. For NGN2-expressing lines, we used the K4-PB-TO-hNGN2 and K13-EF1a vectors, kindly provided by Dr. Michael Ward at NINDS/NIH. For NGN2, ISL1, and LHX3-expressing lines, we utilized the donor pUCM-CLYBL-hNIL (Addgene, plasmid #105841) along with TALENs, including pZT-C13-R1 (Addgene, plasmid #62197) and pZT-C13-L1 (Addgene, plasmid #62196). Puromycin 1–3 μg/ml was used for selection and enrichment of transgenic IPSC cell (tIPSCs) populations. Cortical and motor neuron differentiations were performed using the established procedure (Fernandopulle et al., 2018). Briefly, tIPSCs were cultured as single cells in neuronal induction medium (IM) with doxycycline (Sigma D9891) 2 μg/ml on matrigel coated plates for 3 days. Cortical neuron and MN differentiation were then induced with the appropriate medium (Table S2). The different days after differentiation are labeled numerically (D1 for day 1). At D4, cells were finally replated onto dishes coated with poly-L-ornithine (PLO) for neuronal maturation. Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK) inhibitor Y-27632 (Tocris Bioscience, 1254) 10 μM was used in the cultures on D1 and D4 for splitting. Based on the type of interrogation, the cells were either plated at low-density for immunofluorescent imaging or at high-density for preparation of cell lysates. Cells were split using Accutase (Gibco, A1110501). Following D4 final plating, neurons were cultured in neuronal maintaining medium (CM) for 14, 21, 28, and 35, and 60 days. Half of the medium was changed every day with fresh pre-warm CM. At experimental endpoints, neurons were either fixed for immunofluorescence microscopy or lysed to make protein lysates.

Immunocytochemistry and Thioflavin S staining

Cells grown on coverslips were washed with PBS and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, then blocked in blocking buffer (1 % BSA in PBS) for 30 min. In case of intracellular proteins detection by staining, permeabilization step with Triton-X100 0.1 % for 5 min were performed before the blocking step. For Thioflavin S staining, fixed cells were incubated with 0.025 % of the fluorophore (Millipore Sigma, T1892) for 8 min and washed three times with 80 % ethanol, one time with 50 % ethanol, one time with water for 5 min each time, prior to the antibody incubations. Fluorescence staining was performed using the primary and secondary antibodies listed in Table S1. Primary antibodies were made up in 1 % blocking buffer and PBS. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS; secondary antibodies were incubated in 1 % blocking buffer. Finally, cells were washed 5 times, 5 min/time with PBS, and counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen, D1306) to reveal nuclei, then mounted in Vectashield Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories, H-1900). Cell coverslips were stored at 4 °C for confocal fluorescence microscopy. The fluorescence was measured in at least 10 randomly chosen observation fields for each experimental condition using Olympus FV 3000 confocal system. Fluorescence intensity of region of interest (ROI) was measured using the ImageJ Software. Quantification experiments were carried out independently at least three times. Individual differences were assessed using individual student’s t-tests in GraphPad Prism software. Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Dendritic spine quantification

The number of dendritic spines in neuron cultures was quantified by staining with phalloidin to assess synaptotoxicity through visualization of spine morphology and quantification of spine numbers, as previously described. (Fang et al., 2018; Fang et al., 2016). Phalloidin staining specifically enriches for F-actin in dendritic spines of IPSC-derived neuron staining (Fig. 4B). Neurons cultured on coverslips were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and stained with either Alexa 488-phalloidin or rhodamine-phalloidin to visualize dendritic spines. Images were acquired using an Olympus FV 3000 confocal microscope with a 63× objective (N.A. = 1.4). The number of dendritic spines was determined using ImageJ software. Briefly, 4–5 isolated dendritic segments were chosen from each image, and the images adjusted using a threshold that had been optimized to include the outline of the spines but not nonspecific signals (Srivastava et al., 2011). The number of spines was normalized to the measured length of the dendritic segment to give the number of spines/μm. For each experiment, 15–24 neurons from 3 to 4 individual experiments were imaged and quantitated.

Western blot

Tissues were dissected from mice euthanized by asphyxiation with CO2. Protein lysates were made from spinal cord and brain regions by homogenization in protein lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 6.8, 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 0.5 % SDS, 0.5 % NP40, sarkosyl 0.5 %, 1 mM Prefabloc (AEBSF), 10 mM orthovanadate, 2.5 mM sodium fluoride) (Monteiro and Mical, 1996). Cell and neuron lysates were collected from culture in RIPA buffer or IP buffer. The total protein content of samples was measured by bicinchoninic acid assay (BCA) (Pierce, 23,225). Equal amounts of protein lysate was mixed with appropriate volume of loading dye (Monteiro and Mical, 1996) and heated for 5 min at 99 °C prior to separation on Tris-Glycine SDS-PAGE gels. For native-PAGE Western Blots, protein lysate was mix with appropriate volume of Native Sample Buffer (Novex, LC2673) prior to separation on native-PAGE Bis-Tris protein 3 to 10 % gels (BN1003BOX). Gels were run in native condition with Native Running Buffer (Novex, LC2672). The proteins were then transferred onto Immobilon PVDF membranes (Immobilon-P, Millipore) and the membranes were blocked for 30 min with 5 % BSA in TBS buffer and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Antigen-antibody complexes were detected using fluorescent goat anti-mouse IRDye 800 (green) or 680 (red) and goat anti-rabbit IRDye 680 (red) secondary antibodies, respectively, and visualized with the LI-COR Odyssey Classic Infrared Imaging System.

Cell line cultures

Human HeLa cells and mouse neuroblasts cells Neuro2a (N2a) were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, 11,965,118) with high glucose and 5 mM Glutamine containing 10 % FBS. Cells were grown at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 up to 80–90 % confluency then were passaged at 1 in 10 and grown for up to a week. The medium was changed every 3–4 days.

For the turnover, and immunoprecipitation experiments, cells were plated at equal densities and transfected with equal amounts of plasmid cDNAs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000001) for 2 to 3 days using the protocol provided by the manufacturer.

Plasmids

The pCMV HA-tagged UBQLN2 WT, P497H, P497S, P506T, P509S and P525S expression plasmids used for transfections were described previously (Chang and Monteiro, 2015). pcDNA3.1(+) Hygro plasmids (Invitrogen) encoding WT PrP which was a kind gift from Dr. David Harris (Wu et al., 2017).

PrP immunoprecipitated assay

Antibody-bead conjugates were prepared by adding 2 μg of anti-HA or-UBQLN2 antibodies to 500 μl of Pierce IP Lysis Buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 % NP-40 and 5 % glycerol) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 87,788) in a 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube, together with 30 μl of Dynabeads protein A/G (Invitrogen). Tubes were rotated for 2 h at 4 °C followed by two washes with IP Lysis Buffer to remove unbound antibodies. The washed antibody-bead mixture was incubated with cell lysate prepared as described below.

Cell cultures plated at similar densities were either mock transfected or transfected with WT or mutant UBQLN2 and/or PrP cDNAs. 2–3 days after transfection the cultures were washed in ice-cold 1× PBS and the cells were scraped from the dishes in IP buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor (Millipore Sigma, #P8340). The lysates were then sheared by repeated passage through Tuberculin syringes with 25G needle and clarified by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min. The protein concentration of the supernatants was quantified by the BCA assay. We incubated 0.5 ml at 2.0 mg/ml of lysate with the washed antibody-bead mixture for 2 h at 4 °C in a rotator at 10 rpm speed. The unbound fractions were collected, and beads were subsequently washed three times with 1.0 ml of IP lysis buffer. SDS-sample buffer was then added to the beads and the mixture was then heated at 100 °C for 5 min. Equal portions of the supernatants were then separated by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotted for PrP or UBQLN2.

Cycloheximide-chase experiments

The turnover of PrP was measured by immunoblotting equal amounts of protein lysate that were collected at various time intervals, as indicated in the figures, from N2a cultures to which cycloheximide (Sigma, 66,819) was added to a final concentration 100 μM to block new protein synthesis.

Proteinase K experiments

Proteinase K (PK) resistant ability of lysate samples was detected as previously described (Fang et al., 2016, 2018). For exposure of tissue lysates or neuronal lysates to PK (Thermo Scientific, EO0491), 50 μg of total protein was incubated with 2.5 μg/ml PK for 1 h at 37 °C with orbital shaking at 750 rpm. Western blot was performed with the anti-PrP antibodies. The multiple immunoreactive bands seen in the blots likely reflect the different glycosylation states of PrP.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All reported data were typically presented as mean ± s.e.m. and unpaired comparisons were analyzed by two-sided Student’s t-test unless otherwise mentioned. Multiple comparisons were analyzed by ordinary one-way ANOVA. GraphPad Prism 10 software program was used for all statistical analysis. The n values are either separate biological replicates or numbers of cells or mice or ROI, as indicated. We note that throughout this paper *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001, **** p < 0,0001; ns, not significant (p ≥ 0.05).

Results

Identification of PrP in UBQLN2 inclusions in the P497S mouse model of ALS/FTD

Because PrP often becomes mislocalized in neurodegenerative diseases we examined if the same occurs for UBQLN2 inclusions that are deposited in the P497S UBQLN2 mouse model of ALS/FTD (Le et al., 2016). Indeed, double immunofluorescence staining of P497S brain sections revealed good colocalization of PrP with UBQLN2 inclusions that were deposited in the hippocampus (dentate gyrus and CA1 regions), striatum, motor cortex, cerebellum, and brain stem in end-stage P497S mice (Fig. 1A and S1 and S2). The PrP inclusions were rarely seen in the brains of equivalent age non transgenic (Non-tg) or wild-type (WT) mice, where instead PrP staining was more uniform (Fig. 1B and S1). In general, there was a good match in the colocalization of PrP staining with the UBQLN2 inclusions, although rare examples of PrP inclusions devoid of UBQLN2 staining were found (Fig. S1 open arrowhead). We quantified the number and size of the PrP inclusions in the motor cortex, dentate gyrus, and CA1 regions of the brain and found significantly higher number of PrP puncta in all three regions in the P497S animals compared to Non-tg and WT UBQLN2 Tg animals (Fig. 1C and S1). An analysis of the size of the PrP inclusions in different regions of the brain in P497S animals revealed slight variation, except in the hippocampus, where more larger aggregates were found (Fig. 1C).

Similarly, staining of Sc sections of the lumbar L4 regions of P497S animals revealed an abundance of UBQLN2-positive inclusions in the gray matter that counterstained positive for PrP (Fig. 2A–B and Fig. S3A). Similar structures were rarely seen in the Sc of equivalent age Non-tg or WT UBQLN2 Tg mice (Fig. 2A–B and Fig. S3A). Triple staining of the Sc sections for PrP, UBQLN2, and TDP-43 revealed that some of the cytoplasmic inclusions in motor neurons (MN) were positive for all three proteins (Fig. 2B, white arrowhead). Interestingly, many UBQLN2-positive puncta were negative for both TDP-43 and PrP, while others were positive for PrP but not TDP-43 (Fig. 2B). Some UBQLN2-positive puncta were observed in the nucleus, where TDP-43 was excluded and translocated to the cytoplasm (Fig. S3B–D). This suggests that TDP-43 cytosolic deposition may occur in conjunction with UBQLN2 accumulation and aggregation in the nucleus. We immunoblotted Sc lysates from 5- and 8-month-old mice for PrP protein using the anti-PrP D18 antibody and found slightly higher levels of PrP protein in 8-month-old P497S mice compared to Non-tg and WT UBQLN2 mice, but the increase was not significant (Fig. 2D–E).

Age-dependent reduction in dendritic spine length and density in P497S animals

Because the P497S UBQLN2 mutation is associated with FTD, we examined whether dendritic spine length and/or density, the number of which is important for synaptic function, is altered in mutant P497S animals. To examine this, we crossed the P497S animals with Thy1-YFP-H mice (Feng et al., 2000) and examined the double transgenic mice for changes in spine length and density in 24 and 48-week-old animals. Thy1-YFP-H mice are particularly useful for such quantification because only a subset of neurons express the YFP protein, enabling ease in tracing the spine properties of individual dendrites from the conglomerate mixture of interweaving neurites in the brain. Staining of cryo-sections from the double transgenic mice for UBQLN2 revealed a high concentration of UBQLN2 inclusions surrounding the DG and CA fields (Fig. S4), as previously noted (Le et al., 2016; Higgins et al., 2021). Close examination of the disposition of the inclusions in these confocal images showed good overlap of several of the UBQLN2 puncta with GFP fluorescence in the neuronal cell bodies and neurite projections suggesting that the UBQLN2 inclusions are intracellular (Fig. S4). Quantification of the average length and number of dendritic spines in the CA1 and cortex fields revealed an age-dependent reduction in the P497S animals in both areas compared to single Thy1-YFP-H Tg animals (Fig. 3A and B). Interestingly, some CA1 dendrites in the 48-week-old double Tg mice had unusual enlargements with localized clustering of GFP in them, suggesting entrapment or exclusion of the protein from segments of the neurites, compared to the more regular bead-like chain appearance seen in age-matched single Thy1-YFP-H animals (Fig. 3A). This analysis confirmed that aged P497S mice have a major reduction in the number of dendrites in the brain.

Modeling UBQLN2 mutations using IPSCs

We employed a neuronal differentiation protocol (Fernandopulle et al., 2018) in which human IPSCs (hIPSCs) can be inducibly differentiated to either cortical neurons or lower MNs in a rapid (within 14 days), efficient, and scalable manner (Fig. 4A) Neurons differentiated from hIPSCs develop functionally active synapses (Fernandopulle et al., 2018). We first generated stable transgenic lines of hIPSCs with doxycycline-inducible transcription factors at safe-harbor loci. This was achieved by transfecting dornor:transposase/TALENs vectors with flouresence protein markers (blue fluorescence protein marker, Fig. 4A, a). These vectors enable the overexpression of neuronal transcriptional regulators neurogenin-2 (NGN2), ISL LIM Homeobox 1 (ISL1), and LIM Homeobox 3 (LHX3) under the control of a doxycycline-inducible promoter. Three days after induction, most of the IPSC had transformed into premature neurons or neuronal progenitors (Fig. 4A, b). More prolonged incubation of the cultures resulted in induction of axons and dendrites that were decorated with spines (Fig. 4A, d). We charaterized the neuronal identity of the neuron culture by immunostaining with neuronal-specific markers including Map2 (Microtubule-associated protein 2), Vglut1 (Vesicular Glutamate Transporter 1) and Tuj1 (class III beta-tubulin). After 14 days of differentiation the cells stained positive for the neuronal markers, indicative of mature neurons (Fig. 4A, e-g). Likewise, cells transformed into cortical and lower MNs stained positive for Special AT-rich sequence-binding protein 2 (Satb2) and Choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) markers, repectively (Fig. 4A, g and h).

We successfully differentiated both wild-type (WT) hIPSCs (KOLF 2.1, the parental line) and hIPSCs carrying either the P497H or P506T UBQLN2 mutations to cortical and MNs. The neuronal cultures were then characterized by immunoblotting and immunostaining for changes in expression and localization of UBQLN2, PrP, and TDP-43 proteins.

The immunoblots revealed little change in the levels of UBQLN2, PrP, and TDP-43 proteins in the neuronal cultures at 14, 21, and 28 days of differentiation, within and between the WT- and mutant-UBQLN2-expressing lines. (Fig. 4B and C). However, significant higher levels of all three proteins (including phospho-TDP-43 forms) were found for both of the UBQLN2 mutant lines differentiated for 35 days compared to the WT-UBQLN2-expressing line (Fig. S5C–G).

Increased mislocalization of PrP in IPSC neurons carrying ALS/FTD UBQLN2 mutations

Double staining of the differentiated cultures for UBQLN2 or TDP-43 proteins and PrP revealed increased colocalization of the proteins in MN expressing mutant UBQLN2 proteins that were differentiated for 35 and 60 days (Fig. 5A and FigS5A and B). For example, UBQLN2 staining of the neuronal cultures differentiated for 60 days was detected in numerous puncta in the cytoplasm for both P497H and P506T mutant neurons, many of which counter-stained positive for PrP by double immunofluorescence microscopy (Fig. 5A). By contrast, most of the UBQLN2-containing puncta in the WT neurons that had been differentiated for the same period lacked detectable PrP staining, suggesting that colocalization of PrP with UBQLN2 is governed by expression of the mutant UBQLN2 proteins (Fig. 5A).

We next exaimined whether the UBQLN2 inclusions that stained positive for PrP could be aggregates. To analyze this, we stained the neurons for ubiquitin presence and Thioflavin S (ThS) binding, positive detection of which is characteristic of protein aggregates and amyloid formation, respectively. Indeed we found that many of the PrP positive structures in the mutant neurons were positive for ThS binding (Fig. 5B). Additionally, the ubiquitin staining identified more concentrated localization in puncta that were more prominent in the differentiate neurons expressing either the P497H or P506T compared to WT UBQLN2 expressing neurons (Fig. 5C). Immunoblots of lysates from the MN and cortical neuron cultures probed with the anti-ubiquitin antibody further revealed stronger reaction of protein bands in the IPSC expressing the mutant UBQLN2 proteins than WT protein (Fig. 5D). Taken together these results suggest that the mislocalization of PrP is triggered by expression of ALS-mutant UBQLN2 proteins.

Mislocalization of TDP-43 in IPSC neurons carrying UBQLN2 mutations

We next examined whether TDP-43 protein becomes mislocalized in the IPSC neurons as it was a signature alteration seen in spinal MN during end-stage in our mutant UBQLN2 mice (Le et al., 2016). Accordingly we stained the IPSC neurons at different days after differentiation with antibodies that specifically recognize either TDP-43 or phospho-TDP-43409/410, the latter of which is typically associated with TDP-43 mislocalization.

The results of the TDP-43 staining of the cultures revealed abnormal decoration of multiple puncta in the cytoplasm in both cortical and MNs made from the P497H and P506T mutant lines, which were rarely seen in equivalent neuronal types made from WT UBQLN2 IPSCs. Furthermore, double immunofluorescence staining revealed a subset of these TDP-43 puncta were also positive for phospho-TDP-43409/410 staining (Fig. 6A).

Because TDP-43 aggregation is associated with an increase in phospho-TDP-43409/410 forms, we stained the cultures for ubiquitin, and indeed, found decoration of similar-size puncta in the mutant, but not the WT line (Fig. 5C). To validate the alterations in phospho-TDP-43409/410 and ubiquitin in the mutant lines we conducted immunoblots and consistent with the immunofluorescence findings found increased immunoreactivity with both antibodies in the mutant lines. Interestingly, the increase in phospho-TDP-43409/410 reactivity was more evident in the MN than in the cortical neuron cultures (Fig. 5D and Fig. S5C).

It is important to note that besides the increase in TDP-43 staining in the cytoplasm we did not detect any evidence of its clearance from the nucleus in the mutant IPSC neuronal cultures (Fig. 6), unlike what is observed in the UBQLN2 mouse models (Le et al., 2016).

Besides TDP-43 mislocalization, another feature prominantly seen in the mutant IPSC neurons was accumulation of UBQLN2 in large puncta in the cytoplasm, which was especially prominent in 60-day old cultures for both P497S and P506T mutants. Such large UBQLN2-foci were not seen in WT UBQLN2-expressing neurons differentiated for a similar period (Fig. 6B). Interestingly, double immunofluorescent staining revealed weak colocalization of TDP-43 staining with the UBQLN2 foci in the mutant cells (Fig. 6B). Similar weak colocalization of UBQLN2 and TDP-43 was observed previously in spinal MN with TDP-43 pathology (Le et al., 2016).

Synaptic alterations in UBQLN2 mutant IPSC neurons

Accumulation of misfolded proteins in neurons can adversely affect the health of neurons, particularly by interfering with the trafficking of synaptic proteins. We therefore examined lysates from the induced IPSC neurons for altterations in different synaptic proteins. Indeed, we found major changes in the levels of key synaptic proteins in IPSC-differentiated neurons expressing the P497S and P506T UBQLN2 mutations. For example, immunoblotting of lysates made from the cultures revealed major increases in the levels of subunit 1 and 2a of the NMDA receptor, as well as the GluN2a subunit of AMPA receptors in both sets of neurons expressing the mutant UBQLN2 proteins compared to neurons expressing the WT protein (Fig. 7A–C). Additionally, PSD-95 levels were increased in the mutant cultures.

We next examined whether the IPSC neurons have alterations in spine density. To examine this we stained the neuron cultures after differentiation for 28 days for F-actin using fluorescent-labeled phaloidin, which can be used to visualize the spines. Quantification of the spine number revealed a dramatic and significant decrease in the neurons expressing P497H or P506T UBQLN2 mutations compared to the cells expressing WT UBQLN2 (Fig. 7D–E).

Coimmunoprecipitation of UBQLN2 and PrP proteins

We next investigated whether PrP and UBQLN2 bind together in a complex. To examine this possibility, we co-transfected HeLa cells with PrP and HA-tagged WT or ALS/FTD mutant UBQLN2 expression constructs (expressing either the P497H, P497S, P506T, or P525S mutations) and then immunoprecipitated the expressed UBQLN2 proteins and examined the precipitates for presence of PrP by immunoblotting with the anti-PrP D18 antibody. The results revealed coimmunoprecipitation of PrP with both WT and ALS-mutant UBQLN2 proteins. Interestingly, more PrP was coimmunoprecipitated with the P497H and P506T UBQLN2 mutants than the WT or other UBQLN2 mutants (Fig. 8A).

To obtain additional evidence for their interaction, we conducted the converse analysis examining UBQLN2 precipitated from IPSC WT or the P497H human neuron cultures for PrP coimmunoprecipitation. The immunoblots revealed slightly more PrP protein was coimmunoprecipitated with P497H UBQLN2 than with WT UBQLN2 (Fig. 8B). Taken together, the results indicates that PrP binds UBQLN2 in a complex and that more of it may bind with certain UBQLN2 mutants.

PrP turnover is slowed by the P497H UBQLN2 mutation

Because PrP binds UBQLN2 we examined whether PrP turnover is altered by UBQLN2 mutations. To examine this possibility, we co-transfected N2a cells with cDNAs encoding PrP and either WT or the P497H UBQLN2 mutant. The transfected cultures were then treated with cycloheximide for different times to block new protein synthesis. Lysates were then prepared from the cultures and blotted for PrP. The blots revealed that the rate of PrP turnover was significantly slower in the cells transfected with the P497H UBQLN2 mutant compared to the cells transfected with WT UBQLN2 (Fig. 8C–D). These results suggest that PrP is a client of UBQLN2 that regulates its degradation.

We also stained the co-transfected cells to determine if there was a difference in PrP localization. Indeed, the staining revealed that the majority of PrP immunofluorescence was mainly localized to the plasma-membrane in the cells transfected with WT UBQLN2 compared to an increase in staining in the cytoplasm in the cells transfected with the P497H UBQLN2 mutant (Fig. 8E–F). Moreover, examination of the extent of colocalization of PrP and UBQLN2 staining in the cells revealed a higher ponderance of UBQLN2 inclusions that were positive for PrP staining in P497H transfected cells than those transfected with WT UBQLN2 (Fig. 8E).

Discussion

Here we present evidence that PrPC becomes mislocalized with UBQLN2 inclusions in cell and mouse models of UBQLN2 mutations. This mislocalization may be a contributing factor in the neurodegeneration found in UBQLN2 cases.

There is ample evidence linking PrP pathology in different neurodegenerative diseases. The most compelling is via prion transmission of PrPSc causing templating and propagation of PrPC into misfolded neurotoxic PrPSc strains (Prusiner and DeArmond, 1994; Prusiner, 1991). We found no evidence that PrPSc accumulates in the UBQLN2 mice, ruling out involvement of this mechanism. Another less-well understood mechanism is via PrPC functioning as a receptor that binds misfolded β-sheet-rich proteins that are commonly found in neurodegenerative diseases, such as Aβ (Kessels et al., 2010; Chen et al., 2010; Resenberger et al., 2011), Tau (Gomes et al., 2019), α-synuclein (Urrea et al., 2017), TDP-43 (Scialo et al., 2021) and PrPSc (Fang et al., 2018) leading to the internalization of the bound ligands, which has been linked to toxicity. It is possible that the colocalization of PrP with UBQLN2 inclusions occur via the latter mechanism. Consistent with this idea we found that the UBQLN2 inclusions in the UBQLN2 mutant IPSC cultures and in the P497S UBQLN2 mouse model (Le et al., 2016) stained positive for Thioflavin S, a dye that binds most amyloid fibrils. The binding suggest UBQLN2 inclusions are enriched in β-sheets, which is compatible with ligands that can bind PrPC. Such a mechanism implies that PrPC binds UBQLN2 aggregates extracellularly. Although we cannot rule out this possibility, we believe this is less likely due to the high correspondence of PrP staining with the UBQLN2 inclusions and because most UBQLN2 inclusions can be traced to having an intracellular location. Instead, we favor the possibility that PrPC gets trapped with UBQLN2 inclusions due to disturbances in proteostasis induced by the UBQLN2 mutations, such as interference in proteasome degradation and autophagy (Lin et al., 2022; Chang and Monteiro, 2015; Wu et al., 2020).

UBQLN2 has a UBA domain that binds ubiquitin moieties that are typically tagged onto proteins for a variety of functions, including for cell signaling, protein sorting, endocytosis, and protein degradation (Lin et al., 2022; Raasi et al., 2005; Phung et al., 2023). Not surprisingly, UBQLN proteins due to their capacity to bind different ubiquitin moieties play a key role in regulating most, if not all, of these processes (Lin et al., 2022). Conversely, ubiquitin modification regulates PrP function by the aforementioned processes (Goold et al., 2015; Foot et al., 2017). Therefore, ALS mutant UBQLN2 could interfere with PrP biology due to interaction with ubiquitin moieties tagged onto PrP. This binding may be unproductive because mutant UBQLN2 proteins aggregate and/or disturb proteostasis. Indeed, we obtained strong evidence that ubiquitin binding may be central in the PrP pathology. For example, we previously demonstrated that UBQLN2 inclusions in the P497S mouse model are highly enriched for ubiquitin (Le et al., 2016; Higgins et al., 2021). Furthermore, we found good correspondence between UBQLN2, PrP, and ubiquitin staining in inclusions seen in the neuronal cultures. Finally, through immunoprecipitation studies we found PrP is coimmunoprecipitated with WT and ALS mutant UBQLN2 proteins, suggesting the proteins bind to one another.

The slower turnover of PrP found upon expression of the P497H UBQLN2 mutant supports involvement of disturbances in proteostasis in the PrP pathology. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing UBQLN2 mutations stall protein degradation through the proteasome and autophagy pathways (Deng et al., 2011; Chang and Monteiro, 2015; Wu et al., 2020). The disturbance in autophagy may be particularly relevant for PrP degradation as it is the main route for its clearance from cells (Goold et al., 2015; Kovacs et al., 2007; Goold et al., 2013; Shim et al., 2016). The expression of mutant UBQLN2 could potentially alter levels of endogenous proteins, like UBQLN2 itself as well as PrP. For example, despite the similarity in transgenic protein expression in the WT and mutant P497S lines, based on the analysis of the total and soluble pool of UBQLN2 protein assessed in animals of 1–2 months of age (see, Le et al., 2016; Wu et al., 2021; Higgins et al., 2021), the level of the mutant UBQLN2 protein was significantly elevated in end-stage (8 months) P497S animals compared to Non-tg animals. A similar elevation was not observed for the WT UBQLN2 protein in the WT-356 line (Le et al., 2016; Higgins et al., 2021). Coincident with the increase of the mutant protein, endogenous mouse UBQLN2 levels were elevated in the end-stage P497S animals but not in the WT 356 line. This suggests that mutant UBQLN2 may have a slower turnover rate compared to WT UBQLN2, potentially leading to a slower turnover of endogenous mutant UBQLN2 as well. A similar mechanism may lead to altered PrP accumulation in the mutant mice.

An important finding of the current study is the demonstration that neuron-derived IPSC lines carrying either the P497H or P506T UBQLN2 mutation display many features associated with disease, including accumulation of UBQLN2 inclusions in the cytoplasm, TDP-43 pathology in MN and PrP pathology. Both mutant lines had similar survival times compared with non-carrier lines. The mean survival time of the neuronal cultures was around two months, before degeneration was observed. Mature neurons in both mutant lines had a dramatic reduction in dendritic spines as well as abnormal changes in key synaptic proteins, pathological features found in most neurodegenerative disease including ALS and FTD (Gelon et al., 2022). A similar reduction of spine number and density was seen in aged P497S mutant animals. The high degree to which the human IPSC models recapitulate the pathological features of UBQLN2 cases makes them extremely useful for understanding the mechanisms underlying ALS/FTD and for testing therapies to prevent disease.

Although we do not know the underlying reason for the synaptic defects that are induced by UBQLN2 mutations we are intrigued by the possibility that PrP pathology may be involved in the process. PrP directly interacts and modulates glutamate receptors, including the NMDAR and subsequently regulates cellular calcium signaling (Matsubara et al., 2020). PrP and NMDAR mediate pathogenic effects and protect against excitotoxicity (Black et al., 2014). The alteration of both PrPC and subunits of these receptors was found in mutant UBQLN2 neurons. It is therefore possible that that synaptic defects arise due to dysfunction that arise from the PrP pathology.

In summary, our results suggest that UBQLN2 mutations are associated with PrP pathology, which may be tied to ALS/FTD pathogenesis.

Conclusion

Our study presents evidence of novel PrP pathology in both mouse and human models of another progressive and fatal neurodegenerative disorder, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) with UBQLN2 mutations. We describe a potential human neuronal model for studying UBQLN2 mutations that cause ALS/FTD. We show the model successfully recapitulates key pathological features, including UBQLN2, TDP-43, and PrP mislocalization, as well formation of pathological protein aggregates that are accompanied by synaptic abnormalities. Because of the profound similarities of the mechanistic underpinnings of prion protein, our finding and disease model may have much broader implications for other neurodegenerative diseases.
