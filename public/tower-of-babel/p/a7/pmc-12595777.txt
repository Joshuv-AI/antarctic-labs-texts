Diagnostic accuracy of E-FAST examination performed by newly trained emergency physicians and its impact on clinical outcomes
Authors: Çağrı Safa Buyurgan, Akif Yarkac, Seyran Bozkurt, Ataman Köse, Gülhan Akverdi Akar, Ruken Mete, Buğrahan Güldür, Selva Oflas, Necmettin Kaan Kanevetçi, Çiğdem Öncü Güldür, Rohad Sadık Koyuncu, Hasan Hüsnü Yüksek, Gülhan Orekici Temel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (E-FAST) is considered a critical assessment tool for patients with trauma. This study aimed to investigate the diagnostic accuracy of the E-FAST examination performed by newly trained emergency medicine residents and its effect on clinical outcomes.MethodsPatients with multiple trauma presenting to the adult emergency department of a university hospital were prospectively evaluated. Senior emergency medicine residents with no prior ultrasonography experience were included in the study after completing a 2-day official and nationally certified theoretical and practical course, which also covered E-FAST training, and performing at least 50 E-FAST examinations. Computed tomography (CT) results were accepted as the gold standard, and the sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV), and accuracy of E-FAST were calculated. Clinical outcomes were defined as the need for emergency surgery and, in patients with false-negative (FN) E-FAST evaluations, chest tube placement, hospitalization, and need for surgery.ResultsA total of 158 patients were included. For hemoperitoneum, sensitivity was 81.25%, specificity 100%, PPV 100%, NPV 95.45%, accuracy 96.2%, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) 0.91; for hemothorax, sensitivity was 35.71%, specificity 100%, PPV 100%, NPV 94.12%, accuracy 94.3%, and AUC 0.68; and for pneumothorax, sensitivity was 59.38%, specificity 100%, PPV 100%, NPV 90.65%, accuracy 91.77%, and AUC 0.80. None of the six hemoperitoneum patients assessed as FN required a treatment plan for this reason. Chest tubes were placed in 5 of the 9 hemothorax patients assessed as FN due to subsequent hemodynamic instability, and in 4 of the 13 pneumothorax patients due to the need for mechanical ventilation. E-FAST assessment was positive in 7 of the 8 patients requiring emergency surgery.ConclusionsIn patients with a positive E-FAST assessment who require emergency surgery, CT scanning should not delay surgical procedures or lead to time loss. Our study demonstrates that when performed by clinical personnel with training and experience, E-FAST provides accurate results with high specificity and correctly identifies patients requiring surgical intervention. Furthermore, in patients with FN results, serial E-FAST assessments can enhance diagnostic accuracy and contribute to improved patient management.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Emergency, Emergency surgery, E-FAST, Trauma, Ultrasound

Introduction

Traumatic injuries are among the leading causes of morbidity and early mortality in young adults, with their incidence continuously increasing [1–3]. Computed tomography (CT) is considered the golden standard in the evaluation of patients with trauma [4], but its time-consuming nature, its application in hemodynamically stable patients, and the potential risks associated with radiation and contrast agents may limit its use [5].

Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST), which has enabled rapid bedside assessment of trauma patients in emergency departments (EDs) over the last three decades, is now considered an important complement to the clinical examination and is referred to as Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (E-FAST) after being extended to include the thoracic cavity [4]. Adult Trauma Life Support (ATLS) guidelines recommend E-FAST as the first imaging technique for patients with trauma [6, 7]. E-FAST is used to localize bleeding, and it is generally accepted that a positive E-FAST requires emergency laparotomy in patients with trauma and hemodynamic instability [8].

Today, many organizations worldwide require ED physicians, intensivists, and surgeons to be competent in bedside ultrasonography evaluation of critically ill patients [9–12], and certified trainings are provided for ultrasonography evaluations, which are increasingly used in emergency medicine clinics in Türkiye.

This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of E-FAST performed by emergency medicine residents with no prior ultrasonography experience, who were newly trained in the procedure, by comparing the results with CT scan findings, and to define the role of E-FAST in the clinical outcomes of patients.

Methods

Study design

This study is a prospective study conducted on trauma patients in the ED of a tertiary care university hospital. Patients were enrolled consecutively.

Inclusion criteria

Patients aged older than 18 years who presented to adult ED with multiple traumas between October 1, 2023 and October 1, 2024, underwent thoracoabdominal CT and had complete data were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with cardiopulmonary arrest at admission to ED, patients with chest tube placement and intubation prior to ED admission, pregnant patients, patients with contrast material allergy, patients with anatomical defects in the area to be evaluated by E-FAST, patients with missing data (incomplete data in patient files and unfinished E-FAST assessments) and patients with known pre-existing conditions that could cause false-positive findings, such as ascites or heart failure with pleural effusion, were excluded from the study.

Data analysis

All sonographic examinations were performed according to the E-FAST protocol by emergency medicine residents (GAA, RM, BG, SO, NKK, and ÇOG) with an average of 2 years of clinical experience, but no prior experience in ultrasonography. The residents completed a 2-day formal, nationally certified course that included E-FAST training prior to applying the protocol. Following theoretical training, residents received additional hands-on training on volunteer patients, during which the techniques and procedures for each aspect of the protocol were thoroughly explained. After training, residents were asked to perform E-FAST evaluations as part of their routine in trauma patients and to assess the patients in the study after completing at least 50 E-FAST evaluations. Sonographic evaluations were performed during daytime shifts under the supervision of a staff member who was an ultrasonography instructor and did not interfere with the results.

The E-FAST evaluation was performed using the 12 L-RS linear (5.0–13.0 MHZ frequency range) and 4 C-RS abdominal (2.0–5.0 MHz frequency range) probes on a GE Healthcare Versana Premier R2 ultrasound machine (Shenyang Liaoning, China) with procurement support from Mersin University Scientific Research Projects Coordination Unit (Project code: 2023-1-AP5-4782). In the supine position, the presence of fluid in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen was defined as intraperitoneal free fluid in the perihepatic and/or hepatorenal space; the presence of fluid in the left upper quadrant was defined as intraperitoneal free fluid in the perisplenic and/or splenorenal space; and the presence of pelvic free fluid was defined as intraperitoneal free fluid in the pelvic space. The presence of intra-abdominal free fluid (hemoperitoneum) was defined as the detection of fluid in at least one of these three regions. The presence of pericardial fluid was considered the detection of pericardial effusion and/or tamponade in the subxiphoid region evaluation, which might require urgent pericardiocentesis. The presence of hemothorax was considered pleural fluid detected during diaphragm evaluation in the right and left upper quadrants. The presence of fluid in the evaluated areas was considered posttraumatic pathologic fluid accumulation. The pneumothorax evaluation was performed between the second and fourth intercostal spaces along the midclavicular line, and between the sixth and eighth intercostal spaces along the midaxillary line on both sides of the lungs. The reciprocating motion of the visceral and parietal pleura during normal respiration was evaluated, and pneumothorax was considered to have the absence of pleural sliding motion and the appearance of a barcode mark on the M-mode. The lung point was not specifically scanned for pneumothorax, but if found, it was considered an indicator of pneumothorax.

Indications for thoracoabdominal CT were determined according to the ATLS guidelines following E-FAST evaluation by residents. E-FAST assessment was not permitted to cause any delay in resuscitative interventions and CT scanning in any patient. All patients were scanned in the supine position using a Toshiba Aquilion 64-detector multi-slice CT scanner (Toshiba Medical Systems, Otawara, Japan). Consecutive sections were obtained with 1 mm isotropic voxels in the axial plane for each patient. A peak X-ray kilovoltage of 120 kVp and automatic tube current modulation were used. The scans were enhanced with intravenous contrast material. All the CT scans were evaluated by a radiologist blinded to the patients and the results of the sonographic examinations, and CT scan results were used as the golden standard for diagnosis and management of patients.

Demographic data, vital signs at presentation to ED, types and areas of injury, ED outcome status, E-FAST and CT scan results were recorded on a pre-developed data form. Clinical outcomes were defined as the need for emergency surgery, and chest tube placement, hospitalization and/or need for surgery in the follow-up of patients with negative E-FAST evaluation but pathological findings on CT scan (false negative [FN]). The present study was performed upon approval of the Clinical Research Ethics Committee, Mersin University Rectorate (Mersin/Türkiye; dated September 14, 2022; No. 2022/632).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used for continuous variables, with arithmetic mean and standard deviation, and for categorical variables, frequency and percentage values were provided. Diagnostic performance statistics for E-FAST evaluation, including sensitivity, specificity, negative and positive predictive values (NPV, PPV), accuracy, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC), were reported with confidence intervals. A sample size of at least 138 cases was planned to achieve statistical significance with a 5% Type I error and 90% power, to detect the expected 0.90 agreement between E-FAST and CT results. Interobserver agreement among the trained practitioners was evaluated using the kappa statistic. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 171 patients were reviewed within the specified date range, and 158 patients with complete data were included in the study. 7 patients were excluded due to incomplete data, and 6 patients were excluded because they were admitted to the ED with cardiopulmonary arrest.

The mean age of the patients in the present study was 43.8 ± 19.4 years, and 71.5% of the patients were male. Of the patients, 91.8% sustained blunt trauma. E-FAST evaluation was positive in 27.2% of the patients. A total of 26.6% of patients required admission to the ward, 27.2% required admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), and 5.1% required emergency surgery. The mortality rate was 0.6%. Following FN E-FAST evaluations, CT identified hemoperitoneum in 6 patients, hemothorax in 9, and pneumothorax in 13 (Table 3). Hemoperitoneum was minimal in all cases; no abdominal surgeries were required, and patients were admitted to other wards for follow-up of associated injuries. Among FN hemothorax cases, 4 were monitored without intervention, while 5 required chest tube placement after developing hemodynamic instability. Of the FN pneumothorax cases, 4 underwent chest tube placement before intubation for planned surgery due to other injuries, whereas the remaining 9 with small pneumothorax were admitted for observation without intervention.

8 patients with unstable hemodynamics after penetrating abdominal trauma were evaluated for emergency surgery. E-FAST detected hemoperitoneum in 7 of them. CT, requested by surgical consultants, revealed additional findings in these patients: splenic injury (n = 2), combined liver and splenic injury (n = 1), and combined liver, splenic, and renal injuries (n = 1). The single patient with a negative E-FAST was found to have pneumoperitoneum indicating bowel injury. All 8 patients underwent emergency laparotomy following CT imaging.

When the time between E-FAST and CT imaging was evaluated according to emergency surgery status, the median interval between the two examinations was 28 [15–39] minutes in patients who required emergency surgery and underwent early CT, and 38.5 [9–168] minutes in those who did not require emergency surgery and underwent delayed CT.

As shown in Table 4, in patients undergoing E-FAST, the AUC values for hemoperitoneum were higher in those who required emergency surgery and underwent early CT imaging (ES +) compared with patients without emergency surgery (ES –) [1.000 vs. 0.880, respectively]. Discussion

It should be noted that the primary cause of death in trauma patients is hypovolemic shock, that E-FAST evaluation is designed to detect the presence of hemorrhage, and that FN results are possible. Patients whose hemodynamics are stable should be considered for CT evaluation [13]. Our study provides important findings regarding the diagnostic accuracy of E-FAST and its role in the clinical outcomes of trauma patients.

An analysis of the diagnostic accuracy of E-FAST

In this study, a sensitivity of 81.25%, a specificity of 100%, a PPV of 100%, an NPV of 95.45%, an accuracy of 96.2%, and an AUC of 0.91 were calculated for hemoperitoneum. A meta-analysis by Netherton et al. of 52 different studies, which investigated the diagnostic accuracy of the E-FAST assessment, reported high heterogeneity between studies with a pooled sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of 0.742 (95% confidence interval [CI]), 0.976 (95% CI) and 0.942, and an AUC of 0.931 for the detection of intra-abdominal fluid, respectively. Among these studies, the lowest sensitivity and specificity were reported as 0.26 and 0.75, while the highest sensitivity and specificity were 1.0 [14]. Akoğlu et al. reported an AUC value of 0.71, a sensitivity of 42.9%, and a specificity of 98.4% for hemoperitoneum [5].

For hemothorax, a sensitivity of 35.71%, a specificity of 100%, a PPV of 100%, an NPV of 94.12%, an accuracy of 94.3%, and an AUC of 0.68 were calculated in the present study. Some studies have reported that E-FAST detected pleural effusion with 100% sensitivity and specificity [5], while others found no statistically significant difference compared to CT, detecting hemothorax in 94.3% of patients [4].

For pneumothorax, the calculated values of sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, and AUC were 59.38%, 100%, 100%, 90.65%, 91.77%, and 0.80, respectively. In the meta-analysis by Netherton et al., the pooled sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy for pneumothorax were calculated as 0.694 (95% CI), 0.990 (95% CI), and 0.943, respectively, with an AUC value of 0.994, indicating high heterogeneity between studies. Among these studies, the lowest sensitivity and specificity were 0.40 and 0.94, respectively, while the highest sensitivity and specificity were 1.0 [14]. Akoğlu et al. reported the sensitivity, specificity, and AUC values for pneumothorax as 75%, 99.2%, and 0.87, respectively [5].

In light of this information, which aligns with the present study, most of the previous studies on E-FAST reported high specificity and moderate sensitivity, with results spanning a wide range of sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. In the present study, high accuracy rates were observed for hemoperitoneum, hemothorax, and pneumothorax, with specificity values exceeding sensitivity values and PPV values higher than NPV values. Based on AUC values, E-FAST demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for hemoperitoneum, good performance for pneumothorax, and moderate diagnostic performance for hemothorax, highlighting the presence of FN results. Therefore, while a positive E-FAST evaluation generally confirmed thoracoabdominal injuries, a negative E-FAST evaluation might not rule out the presence of injury.

A review of patients based on clinical outcomes

Patients with false negative E-FAST evaluation

In the present study, none of the patients with minimal hemoperitoneum on FN E-FAST evaluation required a treatment plan. 5 patients with hemothorax, who were not detected on admission but whose hemodynamics deteriorated during follow-up, were monitored for hemorrhage with the placement of a chest tube. Additionally, 4 patients with FN pneumothorax required chest tube placement due to the need for mechanical ventilation.

Von Kuenssberg et al. reported that the minimum amount of pelvic fluid detectable by ultrasonography should be at least 157 mL [15]. Therefore, it should be noted that the primary objective of the E-FAST examination is not to rule out minimal hemoperitoneum, but to identify significant amounts of blood that may lead to hemodynamic instability in trauma patients. In our clinic, E-FAST evaluation is performed once at the time of admission, while CT scanning is generally carried out later. Therefore, due to intravenous treatments, the higher fluid levels detected on subsequent CT scans may explain the initial negative evaluation. In addition, the high rate of positive findings for hemoperitoneum observed in patients who required emergency surgery and underwent early CT imaging supports the high AUC values obtained in our study. In contrast, in patients whose CT scans were performed later, the prolonged interval between an initially negative E-FAST for hemoperitoneum and subsequent CT imaging may have allowed minor hemorrhages to progress to a level detectable by CT, which may explain the lower AUC and accuracy values observed in this group. Conversely, interpreting the diagnostic performance of E-FAST for hemothorax and pneumothorax across subgroups is more challenging. In patients who required emergency surgery and underwent early CT imaging, the AUC values for these conditions included 0.50 within their confidence intervals, indicating uncertain discriminatory performance. Moreover, the wide confidence intervals observed in these subgroups may reflect the limited number of positive cases. Therefore, reanalysis of these parameters in larger datasets or validation in future prospective studies would contribute to a more reliable assessment of the diagnostic accuracy of E-FAST for thoracic pathologies.

The placement of a chest tube in some patients with FN hemothorax was due to the deterioration of their vital signs, resulting in hemodynamic instability during follow-up. Repeating the E-FAST evaluation in these patients could have increased our accuracy rate by detecting the hemorrhage upon reevaluation. This finding is also consistent with previous reports indicating that serial ultrasonography significantly improves diagnostic performance in trauma patients. Blackbourne et al. reported that the accuracy of two consecutive ultrasonography evaluations in trauma patients within 24 h after admission was 92.1% and 96.7%, respectively, with no patient having a negative second ultrasonography result who later developed clinically significant hemorrhage during follow-up [16]. Davoodabadi et al. demonstrated that in blunt abdominal trauma, the sensitivity of ultrasound increased from 19.8% at admission to 75.2% at 12 h and 82.2% at 24 h [17]. More recently, Yazıcı et al. showed that repeated E-FAST improved the detection of hemothorax and hemoperitoneum in hemodynamically stable thoracoabdominal trauma patients, raising the sensitivity to 100% while maintaining high specificity [18]. Considering these results, and acknowledging that the accuracy of ultrasonographic evaluation may vary depending on the individual performing it, as well as patient-related factors such as positioning on the trauma board, immobilization, or agitation, and technical limitations including probe selection, bowel gas, or obesity, which may contribute to FN results, we advocate for serial E-FAST assessments in critically injured trauma patients in the ED.

Brook et al. reported that E-FAST detected patients with small pneumothorax with a sensitivity of 32%, and these patients did not require any treatment during hospitalization [19]. In contrast, Abbasi et al. found that ultrasonography detected 9 out of 14 small pneumothorax cases (64.2%), and only one of these patients, similar to those in our study, required the placement of a chest tube due to the need for mechanical ventilation [20]. It is well established that there may be reasons leading to FN assessments of pneumothorax by ultrasonography [13] and that the lung point provides 100% accuracy in detecting pneumothorax [21]. The small size of the pneumothorax that was not detected and the lack of direct search for the lung point or lung pulse might explain the FN results in our study. Overall, our findings are consistent with studies indicating that most small pneumothorax cases assessed as FN can be successfully managed with close observation.

Patients evaluated for emergency surgery

In the present study, although most patients requiring emergency surgery at the time of ED admission had a positive E-FAST evaluation, CT scans were performed following surgical consultation. As a result, solid organ injuries were identified in some of these patients, and bowel injury was detected in one patient. Although CT imaging provided additional information, it did not alter the clinical management, and all eight patients underwent emergency laparotomy upon imaging.

The literature reports that E-FAST is used as part of its protocol to detect free fluid indicative of bleeding, and has become an established “point of care” modality. It has been emphasized that attempting to estimate the amount of blood should not alter the outcome, and that patients with intra-abdominal bleeding and hemodynamic instability should be promptly taken to the operating room [13]. Similarly, it has been argued that E-FAST evaluation is insufficient in detecting solid organ injuries in most cases, with a sensitivity range of 40–63%, and that the practitioner should avoid wasting valuable time on aspects that do not alter the medical management of the patient [22–26]. Studies have reported that pneumoperitoneum should be sought in the diagnosis of intestinal injury on sonographic imaging [27], and that CT scanning should be performed in hemodynamically stable patients with high suspicion of intestinal and retroperitoneal injuries, as small renal injuries can be difficult to detect [28]. The results of the present study are consistent with previous studies, which highlighted challenges associated with E-FAST in evaluating intra-abdominal organs, noted that this evaluation is not a routine part of E-FAST, emphasized that management of patients requiring laparotomy should not be delayed if pathological fluid is detected on E-FAST, and recommended that CT imaging be ordered for patients with stable vital signs in the presence of clinical suspicion.

In a study conducted by Ollerton et al. [8], a statistically significant reduction in CT scan requests for trauma patients was observed, decreasing from 47% to 34% following FAST examination. Although 18 years have passed since this study, the continued preference for CT imaging in patients with a positive E-FAST assessment and indications for emergency laparotomy highlights the impact of the increasing defensive medicine approach in surgical practices in our country. While this reflects a more cautious approach in surgical decision-making and the surgeons’ efforts to minimize potential risks, we believe that rapid and effective diagnostic methods, such as E-FAST, should play a larger role in this process.

As a final remark, the present study was conducted in accordance with studies that reported emergency medicine residents with no ultrasonography experience achieved acceptable accuracy rates after approximately 35 examinations [29], as well as studies by the American College of Emergency Physicians, which suggested that at least 25–50 examinations were required for FAST training [30]. Consistent with these reports, the substantial to almost perfect interobserver agreement demonstrated in our study supports the reliability and reproducibility of E-FAST assessments performed by newly trained residents. It is clear that the standardization and repetition of ultrasound training across countries and regions would enhance diagnostic accuracy.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations. First, it was conducted by a heterogeneous group of residents after a single ultrasound training session. To increase reliability, a similar study could be conducted with a homogeneous group following repeated training. Second, the E-FAST assessment was performed only once at the time of admission. Third, no direct lung pulse or lung point was examined for pneumothorax, which could have potentially increased the diagnostic accuracy of the study. Fourth, although the time intervals between the E-FAST assessment and CT imaging varied among patients, this limitation was partially addressed by performing a subgroup analysis based on emergency surgery status, which reflected early versus delayed CT imaging. Fifth, in our study, the clinical outcomes of patients with FN results were evaluated, but case-level analyses of the reasons for misinterpretation were not performed.

Conclusion

Emergency medicine clinicians should be aware of the strengths and limitations of the E-FAST assessment and must not overlook the possibility that an injury may not be completely excluded in a trauma patient with a negative E-FAST. In trauma patients with a positive E-FAST assessment who require emergency surgery, CT scanning performed to detect additional organ injuries should not delay surgical intervention or lead to critical time loss for the patient. Our study demonstrates that when performed by clinically trained personnel with sufficient education and practical experience, including newly trained residents, E-FAST provides results with high specificity, effectively identifying patients requiring emergency surgery. Additionally, serial E-FAST assessments in trauma patients with FN results can enhance diagnostic accuracy and significantly contribute to patient management. Moreover, subgroup analysis based on emergency surgery status indicated that shorter time intervals between E-FAST and CT imaging may enhance diagnostic agreement, particularly for hemoperitoneum. Our findings should be confirmed by further large-scale, multicenter studies.
