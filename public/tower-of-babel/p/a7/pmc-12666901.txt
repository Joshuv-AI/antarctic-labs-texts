Ventricular fibrillation during left atrial pulsed field ablation: A case report
Authors: Stefano Valcher, Lucio Addeo, Koen De Schouwer, Peter Peytchev, Peter Geelen, Tom J.R. De Potter
Published: 2025

Introduction

Pulsed field ablation (PFA) represents an innovative approach to catheter ablation procedures, leveraging ultrarapid electrical impulses to induce irreversible electroporation of myocardial cells. Distinguished from radiofrequency (RF), PFA operates without thermal energy generation, demonstrating exceptional specificity for myocardial cells.1,2 Consequently, it has exhibited favorable safety profiles, significantly reducing the incidence of major complications such as atrioesophageal fistula, pulmonary vein stenosis, and phrenic nerve injury.3 A randomized trial has demonstrated the noninferiority of PFA compared with conventional thermal ablation methods (RF or cryoballoon) regarding efficacy.4 Consequently, its application in paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF) ablation, particularly for pulmonary vein isolation (PVI),1 and the left atrial posterior wall (LAPW) in cases of persistent AF (PeAF)5 is increasingly popular.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old woman without cardiovascular risk factors or comorbidities had been experiencing symptomatic PeAF since August 2021. She initially responded to amiodarone and flecainide, but amiodarone was discontinued owing to thyroid dysfunction, and flecainide owing to symptomatic premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) and brief runs of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (VT), causing palpitations. She was referred to our center in November 2023 to undergo catheter ablation PVI with PFA. Preintervention transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), performed at another center in September of the same year, revealed severe left atrium (LA) dilation (index volume 54 mL/m2), absence of significant valvulopathies, and normal biventricular function. The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation, per our standard protocol. On the day of the procedure, the patient was in AF, and blood tests showed normal liver, thyroid, and renal function and normal hematology and coagulation profiles. A single transeptal puncture was performed under fluoroscopic and transesophageal echocardiographic guidance. An effective electrical cardioversion was performed. The 3-dimensional anatomy of the LA was reconstructed using rotational angiography, which was then overlaid onto fluoroscopic images. The FARAWAVE 35 mm ablation catheter (Boston Scientific) was introduced into the LA via the FARADRIVE introducer (Boston Scientific). The 35 mm size was selected owing to the presence of a common ostium in the left pulmonary veins. Eight applications were delivered for each pulmonary vein, 4 with the basket configuration and 4 with the flower configuration, as per the manufacturer’s recommendations, successfully achieving PVI. No signs of abnormalities were present on the electrocardiogram (ECG). Owing to the history of PeAF, additional LAPW ablation was planned (Figure 1). A few seconds after the third application, in the transition position between the right superior pulmonary vein and the LAPW, the patient developed ventricular fibrillation (VF) (Figure 2). A 200 J direct current shock was delivered, effectively restoring sinus rhythm. A 12-lead ECG after defibrillation showed no ST-segment elevation (Figure 3). On transesophageal echocardiography, there was no pericardial effusion, and vital signs quickly returned to normal. Three additional applications, achieving isolation of the LAPW, were performed. The following day, a TTE showed global hypokinesis of the left ventricle, which appeared mildly dilated; severely depressed contractile function (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF], 28%; global longitudinal strain, −7.5%); normal right ventricular function (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion, 19 mm; tissue Doppler S’ velocity, 10 cm/s); and signs of mild elevation in pulmonary arterial pressure (33 mm Hg). On the same day, the patient underwent an electrophysiological study. During programmed ventricular stimulation at different cycle lengths (600 ms, 500 ms, 430 ms) up to 3 extrastimuli, brief runs of nonsustained polymorphic VT were induced, but no malignant arrhythmias were observed. The following day, the patient underwent coronary angiography and cardiac catheterization, which showed no coronary artery disease, depressed left ventricular function with increased ventricular filling pressures, and mild postcapillary pulmonary hypertension. Guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure was initiated, and the patient was discharged with plans for a 1-month follow-up visit.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging after 1 month showed moderate left ventricular dysfunction (LVEF 37%) with global hypokinesis and circumferential midmyocardial gadolinium uptake consistent with dilated cardiomyopathy. A 24-hour Holter monitor 1 month after discharge, on therapy with bisoprolol 5 mg daily, showed a persistent sinus rhythm but 18,000 monomorphic PVCs (burden of 19.5%), with left bundle branch block morphology, inferior axis, and transition in V2–V3, occasionally with phases of bigeminy and a single 6-beat run of nonsustained VT. The patient was asymptomatic and in good general condition at the follow-up visit.

At the 6-month follow-up, left ventricular function had normalized (LVEF 50%) and PVCs had decreased to 6000 (burden of 8,8%), with no episodes of VT. The patient was referred to the dedicated clinic for genetic testing.

Discussion

In addition to demonstrating noninferiority to RF in terms of efficacy,4 PFA has shown a favorable safety profile. Notably, PFA’s tissue-specific properties have resulted in a low incidence of atrioesophageal fistula, pulmonary vein stenosis, and phrenic nerve injury.3,6

However, a higher incidence of coronary spasm is reported, which can cause transient ST-segment elevation.3,7,8 In most cases, spasm occurs in the right coronary artery during cavotricuspid isthmus ablation or in the left circumflex coronary artery during mitral isthmus ablation and typically resolves with the intracoronary or intravenous administration of nitroglycerin.9,10 Coronary spasm can also cause malignant arrhythmias,7 and a few cases of VF have been reported.11

In our case, the patient developed VF immediately after application to the LAPW. As shown on the 12-lead ECG before and after the application, no ST-segment elevation was observed. Moreover, the site of energy delivery is not typically associated with coronary vasospasm; thus, this etiology seems unlikely. Upon closer analysis of tachyarrhythmia induction, it can be noted that, after the end of the application, there were 3 sinus beats followed by a PVC on the T wave (“R on T” phenomenon), which then triggers VF.

Although preprocedural data indicated no ventricular heart disease, post-PVI discovery of dilated cardiomyopathy occurred. However, no malignant arrhythmias were induced during the aggressive electrophysiological study performed the following day. Hence, despite the arrhythmic risk not being high, the application of high-voltage electrical impulses may have contributed to the induction of malignant arrhythmia. It is interesting to note that the PVC that triggered the arrhythmia was morphologically similar to the PVCs observed during follow-up ECG-Holter monitoring.

Currently, PFA has also begun to be used for the ablation of PVCs and VT, and the first published studies have shown promising results.12,13 In vivo studies in animals testing PFA on ventricular myocardium (endocardial and epicardial) have noted a possible proarrhythmic activity, the mechanisms of which are not yet well understood. The theories involve differing permeability of cardiomyocyte membranes around the application zones and the possibility of damaging conduction fibers, causing blocks that can trigger reentry or focal arrhythmias.14 However, in our case, the application was performed on the LAPW, away from the ventricular myocardium. It is possible that, after the application of high-voltage impulses, there is a phase of repolarization inhomogeneity in the ventricular myocardium, which could favor the initiation of malignant arrhythmias such as VF, particularly in the presence of a pathologic tissue substrate, as in our case.

Because no apparent causes of dilated cardiomyopathy were identified, the patient was referred for genetic investigations. A possible explanation for the reduction in ventricular function could be an AF-mediated cardiomyopathy, given that ventricular function improved after ablation, even though the TTE performed 2 months earlier showed a normal LVEF. Alternatively, in the context of a dilated cardiomyopathy still in its subclinical phase, the severe reduction in left ventricular function observed after the procedure might have been caused by postcardiac arrest stunning. After the initiation of guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure, the left ventricular function returned to normal.

Conclusions

This case highlights a rare but severe proarrhythmic event that could be associated with PFA, demonstrating the potential for malignant ventricular arrhythmias even when applications are performed outside the ventricular myocardium. The occurrence of VF after LAPW ablation suggests that high-voltage electrical impulses may induce transient repolarization abnormalities, particularly in patients with an underlying myocardial substrate. Further studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying PFA-related ventricular arrhythmias and refine strategies for risk stratification and prevention.
