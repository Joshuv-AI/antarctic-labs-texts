Hemorrhagic oculomotor nerve schwannoma mimicking a completely thrombosed posterior circulation aneurysm in an adolescent: illustrative case

Schwannomas of the oculomotor nerve are exceedingly rare intracranial tumors, accounting for a small fraction of nonvestibular cranial nerve schwannomas. Their deep location within the interpeduncular cistern and close proximity to the posterior circulation arteries create considerable diagnostic uncertainty, especially when hemorrhagic components are present. Distinguishing these tumors from completely thrombosed aneurysms of the superior cerebellar artery (SCA) or posterior cerebral artery (PCA) is essential because management strategies differ substantially.1–3

We present the case of an adolescent patient with a hemorrhagic oculomotor nerve schwannoma initially suspected to represent a completely thrombosed posterior circulation aneurysm.

Illustrative Case

A 15-year-old previously healthy female presented with a 10-day history of progressively worsening headache, followed by sudden onset of left eyelid ptosis and diplopia. Neurological examination revealed an alert patient (Glasgow Coma Scale score 15). There was partial left third cranial nerve palsy characterized by partial ptosis and anisocoria. Extraocular movements were partially limited. No additional focal neurological deficits were identified. Visual acuity was within normal limits. No peripheral stigmata suggestive of neurocutaneous syndromes were observed. The patient had no relevant past medical or family history.

Initial Imaging

Brain MRI demonstrated a well-defined but heterogeneous lesion within the left interpeduncular cistern measuring approximately 12 mm. The lesion showed prominent susceptibility artifact (blooming) on susceptibility-weighted imaging, suggesting blood products. It lacked a clear cleavage plane from the left oculomotor nerve and was closely related to the left SCA-P1 complex. No definite aneurysm was detected on time-of-flight 3D MRA or contrast-enhanced MRA. The differential diagnosis included a completely thrombosed aneurysm of the SCA or PCA or hemorrhagic tumor (schwannoma, dermoid cyst, cavernous malformation). An additional small intraparenchymal lesion within the left cerebral peduncle, suggestive of cavernous malformation, was also noted (Fig. 1). Given the mild neurological deficit, deep-seated location, and potential risk of worsening oculomotor dysfunction, conservative management with close radiological follow-up was chosen. The option of proceeding with cerebral digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was discussed with the patient and her family; however, considering its invasive nature in a pediatric patient, serial follow-up with MRI and MRA was selected. Clinical Deterioration

Two months later, the patient was readmitted due to progression of symptoms. Neurological examination revealed complete left third nerve palsy, including complete ptosis, fixed and dilated pupil, limitation of elevation, depression, and adduction of the left eye. There was no chemosis, ocular injection, or pain with eye movement. Fundoscopic examination showed normal optic discs bilaterally. Thyroid function tests and routine laboratory investigations were unremarkable.

Repeat MRI demonstrated interval enlargement of the lesion to approximately 16 mm. The mass exhibited intrinsic T1 hyperintensity consistent with subacute hemorrhage and persistent susceptibility artifact on susceptibility-weighted imaging. Increased mass effect on the adjacent cerebral peduncle was also noted. No vascular filling was identified on repeat MRA. Subsequent DSA demonstrated normal basilar apex, SCA, and PCA anatomy without evidence of aneurysm or vascular irregularity (Fig. 2). Surgical Management

Given the interval enlargement of the lesion on serial imaging together with progression to complete oculomotor nerve palsy, surgery was considered indicated both for definitive diagnosis and for neural decompression. An orbitozygomatic approach with extradural anterior clinoidectomy was performed. The basilar trunk and SCA-PCA complex were first exposed to secure proximal vascular control in case a completely thrombosed aneurysm was encountered. Intraoperatively, the lesion showed no continuity with the cerebral arteries. Instead, it was found within the left interpeduncular cistern in direct continuity with the oculomotor nerve (Fig. 3). Based on these findings and the clinical presentation, an oculomotor nerve schwannoma was suspected. Gross-total resection was achieved with meticulous effort to preserve as many intact nerve fascicles as possible. The patient tolerated the procedure well and experienced no new neurological deficits. She was discharged on postoperative day 3. Histopathological analysis confirmed schwannoma (WHO grade 1) with Antoni A and B areas and diffuse S100 positivity. At the 3-month follow-up, oculomotor nerve function demonstrated minimal recovery. Follow-up MRI confirmed gross-total resection of the lesion, with no evidence of residual tumor or postoperative complications (Fig. 4). Discussion

Observations

Schwannomas account for approximately 8% of all intracranial neoplasms and most commonly arise from the vestibular nerve, followed by the trigeminal and lower cranial nerves. These tumors typically originate from sensory nerves. In contrast, schwannomas involving the oculomotor nerve are exceedingly rare.2,3

Clinically, oculomotor schwannomas characteristically present with insidiously progressive third cranial nerve palsy. Acute neurological deterioration due to intratumoral hemorrhage is distinctly uncommon. The pathophysiology of spontaneous hemorrhage in schwannomas remains incompletely defined. Proposed mechanisms include rapid tumor expansion leading to relative vascular insufficiency, central necrosis, cystic degeneration, and secondary bleeding. Fragility of intratumoral vessels and venous congestion have also been suggested as contributing factors.4,5 In the present case, the patient demonstrated abrupt progression from partial to complete third nerve palsy, temporally associated with imaging evidence of interval intralesional hemorrhage.

The differential diagnosis of an isolated space-occupying lesion within the interpeduncular cistern encompasses a broad spectrum of neoplastic and vascular entities, particularly when hemorrhagic components are present.1,6,7 In the present case, several imaging features raised concern for a completely thrombosed aneurysm, including the lesion’s location at the basilar apex, its close relationship to the SCA-P1 complex, the presence of susceptibility artifact consistent with blood products, and interval enlargement associated with hemorrhagic evolution. Completely thrombosed aneurysms may manifest as heterogeneous masses with blooming artifact on susceptibility-weighted imaging and may remain angiographically occult on vascular studies. Accordingly, negative findings on MRA or DSA do not definitively exclude an aneurysmal lesion. Nevertheless, the absence of vascular continuity at surgery confirmed a nonvascular origin. Intraoperatively, the lesion was found to be continuous with the cisternal segment of the oculomotor nerve, a finding that strongly supported the diagnosis of schwannoma and was subsequently confirmed by histopathological examination.

Oculomotor schwannomas are benign, indolent tumors for which gross-total resection provides definitive treatment. However, aggressive resection must be weighed against the risk of permanent third nerve dysfunction, particularly in patients with preserved or only mildly impaired preoperative function. In cases with dense adhesion to critical neurovascular structures or in older patients, subtotal intracapsular debulking is a reasonable strategy to reduce the risk of iatrogenic nerve injury.3,7–9 In the present case, the initial suspicion of a thrombosed aneurysm prompted establishment of proximal vascular control prior to tumor manipulation. An orbitozygomatic approach afforded wide exposure of the basilar apex and posterior circulation with minimal brain retraction. At the 3-month follow-up, recovery of oculomotor nerve function was minimal. This finding is consistent with prior reports indicating that postoperative improvement of third nerve palsy is variable and often limited, particularly in patients presenting with complete preoperative deficits.7–9 Nevertheless, the relatively short duration of follow-up represents an important limitation, and longer-term observation will be necessary to more accurately determine the extent of potential neurological recovery.

Lessons

Hemorrhagic oculomotor nerve schwannoma can closely mimic a completely thrombosed posterior circulation aneurysm on imaging, particularly in the interpeduncular cistern. Multimodal imaging including DSA, careful radiological interpretation, and meticulous surgical planning is crucial for accurate diagnosis and safe management. Despite complete resection, recovery of oculomotor function may be limited in cases presenting with complete palsy.
