Ectopic expression of safflower NAC transcription factor CtNAC78 enhances drought tolerance in Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Jialing Yang, Mukaddas Sai, Ziteng Zhao, Xinyu Meng, Qiaoyu Wang, Keer Xiao, Linna Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDrought has become a key abiotic stress factor that seriously affects crop yield, including that of safflower, an important crop grown worldwide. Our previous work showed that drought stress markedly upregulated CtNAC78 expression in safflower. Hence, to investigate CtNAC78‘s function in drought tolerance, transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana with ectopic expression of CtNAC78 was treated with drought in the present work. It was shown that the transgenic A. thaliana exhibited better growth status, higher antioxidant enzyme activity, greater accumulation of chlorophyll and osmolytes, and lower levels of MDA and H2O2, indicating that ectopic expression of CtNAC78 enhanced drought tolerance of A. thaliana. Furthermore, a yeast two-hybrid cDNA library was constructed from drought stress-treated safflower. The library capacity and titer of the constructed cDNA library were 3.232 × 108 CFU and 1.616 × 108 CFU/mL, respectively. Then, 38 candidate CtNAC78-interacting proteins were identified using a bait expression vector fused with CtNAC78 gene fragment (pGBKT7-CtNAC78A). Further yeast spotting analyses and bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays showed that CtE3 (ubiquitin ligase) was the CtNAC78-interacting protein. These findings can assist us in further elucidating the mechanism by which CtNAC78 regulates drought tolerance in safflower.KEYWORDS: Drought, NAC, safflower, transcription factor, yeast two-hybrid

Introduction

With ongoing climatic shifts, drought has emerged as a predominant abiotic stress that severely constrains global plant growth and agricultural productivity, and further leads to serious social problems and economic losses.1 Studies indicate that drought-induced crop yield reduction is a critical issue requiring urgent attention.2 Research has shown that global maize yields decreased by 40% from 1980 to 2015.3 Considering rice as another example, its yield was recorded as decreasing by 31% to 85% due to varying degrees of drought.4 Drought conditions in China are particularly severe, with approximately 17% of the cultivated area affected annually.5 Multiple drought events have been recorded throughout Chinese history. For instance, the drought that occurred in southwestern China in 2010 resulted in a loss of USD 2.5 billion in crop production and affected 27 million people.6 In 2022, China experienced its most severe heatwave for the past 60 years, which is also the most severe heatwave on record in the world.7 Notably, it is estimated that by 2050, plant growth on the majority of the world’s arable land ( > 50%) will be affected by drought.8 Thus, it is important to find efficiency strategies to enhance plant drought tolerance.

Safflower (Carthamus tinctorius L.), an ancient oilseed crop widely distributed in Asia, Australia, and elsewhere, has the potential for application in various fields, including dye, pharmaceutical, and food industries.9–11 Numerous studies have revealed the antioxidant,12 anti-inflammatory,13 anti-atherosclerosis, cardiovascular protective,14 and other biological functions of various metabolites found in safflower15. Of particular note, various tissue components of safflower have exhibited significant potential for diverse applications16. For instance, the antibacterial properties of hydroxysafflor yellow A, isolated from the petals of safflower, have been demonstrated by many researchers17. Moreover, oleosomes, subcellular organelles derived from safflower seeds, have received extensive attention due to their excellent transdermal ability; this ability has also demonstrated the potential application value of safflower in the cosmetics field18. Additionally, the high level of unsaturated fatty acids in the oil extracted from safflower seeds makes it widely popular19. There are also records on the utilization of safflower byproducts (buds and stems). In a previous study, the buds and stems collected from safflower were used in the preparation of silver nanoparticles with anticancer, antibacterial, and antioxidant activities20. Therefore, given the many beneficial effects of safflower, the market demand for safflower is gradually increasing. However, the growth of plants, including safflower, is undoubtedly influenced by various environmental factors, especially drought stress. Compared to other drought-sensitive plants, safflower has a relatively strong tolerance to drought; however, drought can also significantly affect its growth and yield21–23. Hence, understanding the gene expression regulation mechanism of safflower under drought stress is particularly important for further improving safflower drought tolerance.

In case of insufficient conditions, plants can improve their tolerance to water deficiency through a series of biochemical and physiological changes, such as closing stomata, increasing osmoregulatory substances levels, and reducing leaf area 24; 25. Obviously, these physiological changes involve a complex gene expression regulatory network within plants. The beneficial roles of functional genes in biotic stress response have been extensively demonstrated, such as MxNAS326, MxCS127, MxFRO628, etc. In safflower, several genes have been reported to be positively regulate drought tolerance, including flavonol synthase gene (CtFLS-1)29, cytochrome P450 family gene (CtCYP71A1)30, hydroxycinnamoyl-CoA shikimate/quinate hydroxycinnamoyl transferase (CtHCT061)31. In addition to functional genes, transcriptional regulation of target genes represents another strategy to improve plant tolerance to drought32. As regulatory proteins, transcription factors (TFs) modulate gene expression by binding to different cis-elements of target genes33. Extensive evidence confirms the roles of TFs in cell differentiation, organ development, external stress response, etc.34. To date, multiple genes in the plant genome have been found to encode TFs. In the Arabidopsis genome, TF coding genes account for as much as 7%35. To date, many TFs from different families have been found in plants (myeloblastosis-related proteins (MYB), WRKYGQK (WRKY), basic leucine zipper (bZIP), etc.), some of which play important roles in abiotic tolerance of plants36–38. Among them, the NAC gene family has attracted considerable research attention in plants. NAC (NAM, ATAF1/2, CUC2), one of the largest families of plant-specific TFs, has been widely found in multiple plants39. The NAC family possesses a highly conserved NAC domain at the N-terminus, in contrast to a more divergent C-terminal region known as the transcriptional activation region (TAR)40. Moreover, NAC TFs are considered key regulators of plant growth and stress responses41. Different NAC TFs (ANAC019, JUNGBRUNNEN1, ONAC066, VvNAC17, etc.) related to the drought stress response have been found in various plants42–46. For instance, virus-induced silencing of RcNAC091 was shown to impair drought tolerance of rose47. NAC TFs enhance plant drought resistance through multifaceted mechanisms, including the transcriptional activation of stress-responsive genes, induction of stomatal closure, and accumulation of osmoregulatory compounds48. However, limited research has focused on transcription factors associated with drought tolerance in safflower, particularly those of the NAC family. In the study performed by Tan et al., quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis revealed that CtWRKY56 and CtWRKY62 might be responsive to drought stress in safflower49. Moreover, it was found that CtbHLH12, CtbHLH20, and CtbHLH26 were significantly upregulated in safflower under PEG6000 treatment50. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the role of NAC transcription factors in the drought stress response of safflower.

Our previous genome-wide analysis identified the safflower NAC gene family, with CtNAC78 showing significant upregulation under water deficit conditions51. These data suggest that CtNAC78 likely mediates drought stress in safflower. Nevertheless, the functional role of CtNAC78 in the safflower drought response remains unknown. On the basis of obtaining Arabidopsis thaliana with ectopic expression of CtNAC78, this study further compared its drought tolerance with wild A. thaliana. Moreover, after generating a safflower cDNA library, the yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay and bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) were introduced to identify the proteins that interact with CtNAC78. The experimental data presented herein provide empirical evidence for further elucidating the regulatory networks of CtNAC78 during drought adaptation in safflower.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The safflower seeds (cultivar “Jihong 01”) used in the present work were bought from Honghuayuan Co., Ltd (TaCheng, China). Wild A. thaliana (Columbia-0), yeast cells (AH109), Agrobacterium tumefacians (EHA105), and Escherichia coli (TOP10) were provided by our laboratory.

Generation of Transgenic A. thaliana with Ectopic Expression of CtNAC78

An overexpression vector ligated with CtNAC78 (pCAMBIA3301-CtNAC78), constructed in our previous work, was transferred into the A. tumefacians (EHA105) and subsequently transformed into wild A. thaliana, in line with a previous study52. BASTA spray53 and PCR amplification using specific primers of the CtNAC78 gene (forward primer: 5′-ATGGGGATTCCGGTGAC-3′, reverse primer: 5′-TCACAGCCTATACCCGGC-3′) were carried out to detect the T1-T3 generations of the transgenic A. thaliana. Then, quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) detection (forward primer: 5′-AAGCACCGATCAGAGCCAC-3′, reverse primer: 5′-GGAAGCTCAGGAACTTGTCG-3′) was conducted to investigate the relative expression levels of CtNAC78 in the wild and all transgenic A. thaliana lines.

Performance of Wild and Transgenic A. thaliana Under Drought Stress

To identify the drought tolerance of the wild (WT) and T3-generation transgenic A. thaliana ectopicing the CtNAC 78 gene (three lines: OE1, OE2, OE6), a 21-day drought stress treatment was carried out. Briefly, the A. thaliana plants were sown in nutrient-rich soil (a mixture prepared by mixing nutrient soil and vermiculite in a mass ratio of 1:1) and allowed to grow in an artificial climate chamber (23°C, 60% relative humidity, and a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod) for one month. Then, the control and transgenic plants were treated with drought by withholding irrigation for 21 days, in line with another study54.

Observation of Morphology and Detection of Plant Physiological and Biochemical Indexes

After three weeks of natural drought, the phenotype of the wild and transgenic A. thaliana was photographed. Then, the leaves were immediately collected from the wild and transgenic A. thaliana. Furthermore, referring to another study, the relative water content (RWC), fresh weight (FW), and dry weight (DW) of the plants were recorded55. Additionally, the levels of common osmoregulatory substances (soluble sugars, soluble proteins, proline), antioxidant capacity (catalase (CAT), peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD)), chlorophyll content (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and total chlorophyll), and levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in the drought-treated A. thaliana were also detected using commercial kits purchased from Solarbio (Solarbio, Beijing, China)56.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR) Analysis

To identify the expression levels of selected genes, the qRT-PCR technique was employed. Briefly, total RNA was prepared from plant materials using RNAiso Plus reagent purchased from TakaRa (Dalian, China) and then reverse transcribed into cDNA using commercial kits (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). Subsequently, qRT-PCR was conducted using SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ (TaKaRa, Dalian, China), and the actin-2 was selected as an internal control. Finally, the relative expression of selected genes was calculated by referring to another study57. The primers of the tested genes are listed in Table S1.

Construction of the Safflower cDNA Library

The safflower cDNA library was constructed following a previously described method with slight modifications58. The leaves from the drought-stressed wild safflower were used for RNA isolation, following the method outlined in Section 2.5 using RNAiso Plus reagent (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). The integrity and purity of the isolated RNA were further detected using a NanoDrop 2000 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, respectively. Then, the mRNA was obtained using a MagBeads® Oligo(dT) magnetic bead kit (Nanjing Dongna Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Subsequently, the cDNA homogenization and small fragment removal were performed using Hieff NGS ® Smarter DNA Clean Bead and Clontech CHROMA SPIN™+TE-1000 Columns kit provided by Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and TaKaRa. After the homogenization treatment and removal of small fragments, the purified cDNA was homologously recombined with a linearized pGADT7 vector and further co-transformed into E. coli (TOP10) by electroshock (Bio-RAD, Inc., USA). The E. coli suspension was diluted 10,000-fold, plated on LB agar supplemented with ampicillin (100 μg/mL), and incubated overnight at 37°C. Subsequently, twenty-four randomly selected clones were subjected to PCR screening using pGADT7 vector universal primers in order to check the insert fragment size and recombination rate of the yeast two-hybrid library.

Auto-Activation Analysis of Bait Vectors

The auto-activation of the bait vectors was assessed according to established methods, with slight modifications59. To screen the regions of CtNAC78 without transcriptional activation activity, full-length and truncated fragments of the target transcription factor (CtNAC78A, 447bp) at the N-terminal were obtained. Then, the purified and amplified CtNAC78 gene and its fragments were ligated into pGBKT7 plasmids, and the recombinant plasmids (pGBKT7-CtNAC78, pGBKT7-CtNAC78A) were then transformed into yeast receptor cells (AH109) using the Yeastmaker™ Yeast Transformation System 2 kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). Meanwhile, pGBKT7-p53 and pGADT7-large T (positive control), as well as pGBKT7-laminC and pGADT7-large T (negative control), were also co-transformed into yeast. Following a 4-day incubation period at 30°C on YPDA medium, eight single colonies were randomly picked to identify whether the transformation was successful using PCR detection. Then, three single colonies from the correctly detected colonies were incubated on plates containing SD-TL (SD/-Trp/-Leu), SD-TLH (SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His), SD-TLHA (SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade), and SD-TLHA+X-α-gal media. After incubation for 3–5 d at 30°C, the color and size of the colonies were observed and photographed. Furthermore, a suitable concentration of 3-AT (3-amino-1,2,4-triazole) was selected to inhibit the transcriptional activation activity of the selected bait vectors.

Screening for CtNAC78-Interacting Proteins

To identify the potential CtNAC78-interacting proteins, a yeast two-hybrid assay (Y2H) was implemented59. In brief, the cDNA library plasmids (25 μg) were first introduced into the yeast AH109 harboring the bait vector pGBKT7-CtNAC78A. Then, the yeast was plated on SD-TLH medium containing 0.5 mM 3AT. Moreover, to ensure the accuracy of the screening results, pGBKT7-p53 + pGADT7-large T and pGBKT7-laminC + pGADT7-large T were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. Positive clones exhibiting blue coloration were collected from the screening plates and subjected to colony PCR amplification using the Yeast Colony Rapid Detection Kit provided by ProNet Biotech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). Afterward, PCR-amplified products were purified, sequenced, and analyzed by a BLAST search against the GenBank database to identify interacting proteins.

Furthermore, to avoid false positive results, the positive transformants obtained from the above process were resuspended in sterile water and inoculated into different defective plates (SD-TL, SD-TLH +0.5 mM 3AT, SD-TLH +0.5 mM 3AT+X-α-gal, SD-TLHA +0.5 mM 3AT and SD-TLHA +0.5 mM 3AT+X-α-gal) at a constant temperature of 30°C for 3–4 d. The color and size of the colonies were observed.

Y2H Spotting Analysis of the Interaction Between CtNAC78 and Three Candidate Proteins

To verify the interaction between CtNAC78 and three selected proteins (protein phosphatase 2C9 (PP2C9), NDR1/HIN1-like protein 3 (NHL3), ubiquitin ligase (E3)), point-to-point analyses were carried out as described in a previous study with minor modifications60. Briefly, as mentioned above, the CtNAC78 fragment (CtNAC78A) without auto-activation activity was fused into the bait vector (pGBKT7). Meanwhile, CtPP2C9, CtNHL3, and CtE3 were fused to the prey plasmid (pGADT7). Then, yeast AH109 cells were co-transformed with both the bait plasmid (pGBKT7-CtNAC78A) and the prey plasmid following the manufacturer’s protocol (Clontech, Beijing, China). Similarly, the positive and negative controls mentioned in the above experiments were also selected for this section. The empty plasmid (pGBKT7) was co-transformed with three prey plasmids into yeast cells; the bait vector and empty prey plasmid (pGADT7) were co-transformed into yeast cells; and the transformants were coated on different plates (SD-TL, SD-TLH +0.5 mM 3AT, SD-TLH +0.5 mM 3AT+X-α-gal, SD-TLHA +0.5 mM 3AT, SD-TLHA +0.5 mM 3AT+X-α-gal), for 4–5 d at 30°C. Then, the spot growth status of yeast was observed. Finally, the positive clones were identified by sequencing and Blastx.

Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (Bifc) Assay

To further verify the interaction between CtNAC78 and CtE3, the BiFC assay was introduced in Nicotiana benthamiana, following the method described in a previous study, with minor modifications61. Briefly, recombinant vectors (pCAMBIA1300-nYFP-CtNAC78 and pCAMBIA1300-cYFP-CtE3) were constructed using homologous recombination. Then, these two vectors were co-transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and further infiltrated into N. benthamiana GV310 leaves. Meanwhile, nYFP-CtNAC78 + cYFP and nYFP + cYFP-CtE3 were employed as negative controls, and HAI1-MPK6 was introduced as a positive control. After 48 h, leaf fluorescence was detected using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon C2-ER). In this test, N. benthamiana was allowed to grow in an artificial climate chamber (20-25°C, 14 h/10 h light/dark cycle).

Statistical Analysis

The mean±standard deviation (S.D.) was employed to represent all the data in this work. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by multiple comparisons test using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California), with p < .05 defining statistical significance.

Results and Discussion

The Drought Tolerance of Transgenic Arabidopsis Harboring the CtNAC78

To investigate the function of the CtNAC78 gene in the drought tolerance of safflower, transgenic Arabidopsis harboring the CtNAC78 were developed through A. tumefaciens-mediated transformation. Their identification and the expression level analysis of CtNAC78 are shown in Figure S1. Then, WT and three T3 homozygous transgenic Arabidopsis plants (OE1, OE2, OE6) were selected and subjected to a 21-day natural drought treatment, after which their phenotypes were photographed. As shown in Figure 1A, under normal irrigation conditions, all the Arabidopsis lines showed a similar appearance. Following the 3-week drought exposure, it was found that the transgenic and wild plants exhibited varying degrees of leaf wilting and yellowing, indicating that water loss occurred across all plants leaf62. It is well known that the impairment of cellular components caused by drought stress manifests in distinct morphological changes, especially plant leaves63. Under water deficit, leaves often lose turgor, resulting in wilting, curling, and yellowing64. Thus, as the primary organ for transpiration, plant leaves are considered a vital indicator of plant water deficit. However, compared with the transgenic Arabidopsis, more severe leaf wilting was observed in the wild Arabidopsis. In addition to morphological alterations, morphological indicators also serve as widely used measures of plant drought tolerance. In the present study, markedly decreased FW, DW, and RWC were observed in all Arabidopsis lines after drought treatment (p < .001), suggesting that the growth of plants was inhibited by drought. The drought-induced reduction in RWC is driven by an imbalance between transpiration and water absorption, culminating in cellular dehydration, reduced turgor, and a broad impairment of metabolism65. More importantly, stomatal closure impairs photosynthesis, resulting in insufficient energy production that further severely affects plant growth and development66. As dry weight is a robust measure of plant growth, the observed decline demonstrates that drought stress inhibited overall plant growth and development. Additionally, under the drought-treated condition, there were significant differences in the FW, DW, and RWC of the plants between the wild and transgenic Arabidopsis lines (p < .05) (Figure 1B-D). Obviously higher FW, DW, and RWC values were observed in the three transgenic Arabidopsis lines. Consistent with our findings, Ma et al. reported similar results in TaNAC5D-2-overexpressing A. thaliana67. Collectively, these findings suggest that the drought tolerance of A. thaliana was improved by ectopic expression of CtNAC78.

(B-D) changes in fresh weight (B), dry weight (C), and relative water contents (D) of wild and CtNAC78 transgenic Arabidopsis after drought treatment. * and *** mean significant differences at p<.05 and p<.001, respectively.

Physiological Responses to Drought Stress in Wild and CtNAC78-Ectopically Expressing Arabidopsis

Changes in Photosynthetic Pigment Levels

As is well known, photosynthetic pigments in chloroplasts are critical for light capture and electron transfer during photosynthesis. Water stress adversely affects this process by inhibiting chlorophyll synthesis and reducing pigment content, thereby diminishing photosynthetic efficiency68. Given its positive correlation with drought tolerance, chlorophyll content is a valid indicator for assessing this trait in plants69. Therefore, photosynthetic pigment profiles (Chl a, Chl b, total chlorophyll) were analyzed in the drought-stressed WT and CtNAC78-ectopically expressing Arabidopsis to assess drought adaptation. As shown in Figure 2A-C, under non-stress growth conditions, all the measured indicators were comparable between the WT and transgenic Arabidopsis lines. However, the levels of Chl.a, Chl.b and total chlorophyll markedly decreased in both the WT and transgenic Arabidopsis lines post-treatment (p < .001), consistent with the findings reported by Wang et al.68. The decline in chlorophyll under water stress may result from the disruption of its biosynthetic pathway or enhanced degradation70. Moreover, in comparison with the WT group, markedly higher levels of Chl.a, Chl.b and total chlorophyll were found in the three transgenic lines under drought stress (p < .001). Consistent with our results, previous research provides evidence that elevated chlorophyll levels contribute to the delayed yellowing observed in the leaves of transgenic Arabidopsis71. These findings demonstrate that Arabidopsis lines ectopically expressing the CtNAC78 gene maintained higher chlorophyll content under drought stress. This not only potentially enhances their photosynthetic capacity directly but, more importantly, is also associated with improved transpiration efficiency under water stress, as reported in previous studies72.

Changes in Lipid Peroxidation and Antioxidant Enzyme Activities

Accumulated evidence suggests that reactive oxygen species (ROS) mediate plant adaptation to water deficiency73. As key signaling molecules, ROS play key roles in plant adaptation to water deficiency. At low concentrations, ROS can elevate the cytosolic free Ca2 + level, thereby regulating plant growth and activating essential stress response pathways 74; 75. However, under drought stress, ROS often accumulate excessively, leading to extensive cellular damage and impairing photosystems, including lipid peroxidation, organelle dysfunction, electrolyte leakage, metabolic decline, and programmed cell death 76; 77. Additionally, drought-induced oxidative stress imposes a burden on genomic integrity by inducing DNA damage, including mutations, strand breaks, and adducts. This interference with replication and transcription processes thereby disrupts essential metabolic processes and stress response78. To alleviate the damage caused by ROS under normal or abiotic stress, plants have developed different defense mechanisms, particularly the antioxidant system composed of enzymes, which mainly include SOD, POD, and CAT 73; 79. Taking SOD as an example, Zhang et al. reported that the overexpression of Tamarix albiflonum TaMnSOD confers enhanced drought tolerance in transgenic cotton78. It is worthy noting that activity of antioxidant enzymes also play a critical role in maintaining RWC under drought stress. For example, SOD catalyzes the dismutation of superoxide into hydrogen peroxide, which is subsequently detoxified to water and oxygen by CAT and POD. This system directly supports the maintenance of cellular water content65. Thus, a comparative analysis of oxidative stress markers (H2O2 accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and activities of antioxidant enzymes) was conducted in the WT and transgenic Arabidopsis. To investigate the degree of cell membrane damage, quantifying malondialdehyde (MDA) was carried out. As a terminal product of membrane lipid peroxidation, MDA mediates further cellular damage by inactivating proteins and nucleic acids, thereby compromising both membrane integrity and intracellular components80. Thus, MDA level is usually used as a well-established biomarker for membrane lipid peroxidation81. As illustrated in Figure 3A, the level of malondialdehyde (MDA) in the transgenic Arabidopsis plants was similar to the WT plants before drought treatment. However, post-drought exposure, the MDA accumulation in the three transgenic lines was clearly lower than that of the WT plant (p < .001). Moreover, consistent with the MDA results, the transgenic plants exhibited lower H2O2 levels, indicating attenuated oxidative membrane damage (Figure 3B). These data indicate that the ROS accumulation and membrane damage were alleviated by ectopical expression of CtNAC78. Moreover, previous studies reveal that the water scarcity-induced oxidative burst constitutes another key factor responsible for the reduction in chlorophyll contents82. Thus, the observed higher chlorophyll levels in transgenic Arabidopsis after drought stress are likely linked to their reduced accumulation of ROS. Additionally, the antioxidant capacity was evaluated by quantifying the CAT, POD, and SOD activities in the plants (Figure 3C-E). Results show that under drought treatment, all Arabidopsis lines exhibited markedly enhanced activities of CAT, POD, and SOD (p < .001). Consistent with our findings, the upregulation of antioxidant enzyme activities under drought stress is well-established in the literature82. Additionally, the transgenic lines exhibited significantly higher CAT, POD, and SOD activities than the wild-type plants (p < .05), indicating that CtNAC78 ectopic expression enhances drought tolerance of Arabidopsis by mitigating oxidative damage through increased the activities of antioxidant enzymes. Similar to our results, the beneficial roles of multiple NAC TFs in ROS homeostasis during drought stress have been reported, such as MdNAC183, SNAC384, NtNAC285.

Changes in the Levels of Osmoregulatory Substances

A primary consequence of drought stress to plants is cellular dehydration. Faced with the negative impact of this environmental factor, plants accumulate osmoregulatory substances to reduce osmotic potential, thereby enhancing water uptake and maintaining cellular hydration to mitigate drought damage86. Among them, soluble protein, soluble sugars, and proline are widely studied osmoregulatory substances in plants. Beyond its established role as a key osmolyte, the proline also functions as a metal chelator, antioxidant, and signaling molecule under abiotic stress87. As a signaling molecule, proline regulates a spectrum of mitochondrial functions, cell proliferation and death, and stress-responsive gene expression, which are critical for plant stress response88. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that proline alleviates oxidative damage by enhancing cellular antioxidant capacity, both directly as an electron acceptor and indirectly by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities89. Similarly, stress tolerance of plants is promoted by soluble sugars and proteins through their roles in scavenging ROS, enhancing membrane integrity, and stabilizing enzyme activity90. Therefore, accumulating osmoregulatory substances represents an effective strategy to enhance plant drought tolerance. Here, the osmoregulatory substance levels (soluble protein, soluble sugars, and proline) between wild-type and three transgenic lines were detected, respectively. Drought stress significantly increased the osmoregulatory substance contents in all the Arabidopsis lines, with the transgenic plants accumulating markedly higher levels than the wild-type plants (p < .05). (Figure 4A-C). These data demonstrate that CtNAC78 ectopic expression enhances drought tolerance in Arabidopsis by promoting these osmoregulatory substance accumulation. Similarly, multiple NAC transcription factors have been demonstrated to promote the accumulation of osmolytes under drought conditions (eg. ONAC02291, SlNAC692, ONAC06644).

CtNAC78 enhances the levels of osmoregulatory substances of Arabidopsis with ectopic expression of CtNAC78, including proline (A), soluble sugars (B), and soluble protein (C). * and *** mean significant differences at p<.05 and p<.001, respectively.

The Expression of Stress-Related Genes in A.thaliana Under Drought Conditions

It is well known that drought-responsive gene expression profiles serve as molecular markers for drought tolerance mechanisms in plants93. Hence, to further understand the molecular basis underlying the CtNAC78-mediated improvement of drought tolerance in plants, the expression of several stress-related genes, including proline metabolism-related genes (AtPDH1, AtP5CDH, AtP5CS1), ABA (abscisic acid) pathway-related genes (AtABI4, AtSnRK2.2, AtSnRK2.3, AtSnRK2.6), and drought stress-responsive genes (AtRD22 and AtRD29A), was further investigated using qRT-PCR (Figure 5). Previous studies have demonstrated that these genes are involved in plant drought response45. For example, P5CS1 (delta-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase gene), PDH1 (pyrroline dehydrogenase), P5CDH (delta-1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate dehydrogenase) are three genes encoding the key enzymes involved in the proline biosynthesis (P5CS1) and degradation pathways (PDH1 and P5CDH), respectively94. It is evident that the expression of these genes plays a role in proline accumulation and, consequently, in plant drought tolerance. Meanwhile, under drought conditions, the overexpression of several NAC genes has been shown to regulate the expression of these proline metabolism-related genes, including HaNAC195, ANAC05596, ONAC6644. Additionally, previous studies have demonstrated the regulating roles of RD29A and ABI4 in drought tolerance through the ABA pathway97; 98. In the present study, under normal growth conditions, no obvious difference was found in the expression of all the selected genes between the WT and transgenic lines (p > .05). However, all the target genes showed significantly higher expression in the transgenic versus WT plant under drought (p < .05). It is noteworthy that the expression of AtPDH1 and AtP5CDH was also up-regulated under drought conditions in the transgenic Arabidopsis compared to the wild Arabidopsis The relationship between this up-regulation and the observed increase in proline accumulation in the transgenic plants warrants further investigation. These data suggest the important role of CtNAC78 in regulating the signaling pathways related to the drought stress response.

Construction and Evaluation of the cDNA Library

It is well established that gene products frequently interact with other proteins or DNA molecules to modulate a plant’s adaptability to environmental stresses. Given the important role of CtNAC78 in the drought response of safflower, high-throughput screening to identify its putative interacting proteins is of great interest. However, the construction of a high-quality cDNA library from safflower is the experimental basis for employing the yeast two-hybrid system in this endeavor99. As mentioned above, the RNA of safflower was isolated, and agarose gel electrophoresis detection showed no obvious degradation of the RNA (Figure 6A). A diffuse band was observed in the mRNA electrophoresis figure, suggesting successful mRNA isolation (Figure 6B). Afterward, the first and second strands of cDNA were obtained using SMART technology. The electrophoresis results are displayed in Figure 6C. Then, the cDNA was ligated into the linearized pGADT7 vector, resulting in a cDNA library with a titer of 1.616 × 108 CFU/mL. Generally, for a cDNA library to fully capture the transcriptome, it is essential that its size exceeds 1 × 106 CFU100. The library capacity of the constructed cDNA library were 3.232 ×108 CFU. Thus, the established cDNA library in this work was reliable. Meanwhile, as illustrated in Figure 6D, all the randomly selected colonies displayed electrophoretic bands, and the recombination rate was estimated to be 100%. Meanwhile, the insert size of the cDNA library varied from 600 bp to 2000 bp, indicating the low empty loading rate and abundant polymorphism of the constructed library. Overall, the above data suggest that the cDNA library is suitable for the screening of CtNAC78-interacting proteins.

Screening and Sequencing of CtNAC78-Interacting Proteins by the Yeast Two-Hybrid System

Owing to its simplicity, sensitivity, and efficacy, the yeast two-hybrid assay has been established as an increasingly popular technique for studying protein-protein interactions99. It has been successfully employed to screen for interacting partners of many NAC transcription factors (eg. CrNAC036101, CmNAC34102). In the present work, the auto-activation and toxicity of bait proteins were investigated. As shown in Figure 7A, the yeast cells co-transformed with pGADT7-large T and pGBKT7-laminC (negative control) grew exclusively on the SD-TL medium. Conversely, the transformants expressing interacting pairs of pGADT7-large T and pGBKT7-p53 (positive control) grew normally on the SD-TL, SD-TLH, and SD-TLHA media. Moreover, a blue colony was observed in the positive control group. Similar results were also found in the yeast harboring the pGBKT7-CtNAC78 and pGADT7 vectors (Figure 7A). Although the concentration of AT in the medium was increased from 0 mM to 100 mM, the yeast colonies were also observed (Figure S2). These findings indicate that CtNAC78 contains the autonomously activate domain. Based on the structural region, the C-terminus of the CtNAC78 gene may be the transcriptional activation region. Thus, truncation of the C-terminus of the CtNAC78 gene, followed by the 447 bp gene sequence at the N-terminus of CtNAC78, was inserted into the pGBKT7 vector, and no auto-activating activity of pGBKT7-CtNAC78A was determined (Figure 7B).

Auto-activation assay of bait vector pGBKT7-CtNAC78 (A) and pGBKT7-CtNAC78A (B).

Furthermore, a total of 96 positive colonies were observed on the SD-TLH plate supplied with 0.5 mM 3AT. All these colonies were amplified by PCR and sequenced via high-throughput. Then, a BLAST analysis of the sequencing results was carried out in the NCBI database. Finally, after yeast rotary validation (Figure S3), 48 positive yeast clones grew on the selective SD-TL, SD-TLH + 0.5 mM 3AT, and SD-TLH + 0.5 mM 3AT + X-α-gal plates. They also produced a light blue color on the SD-TLH + 0.5 mM 3AT + X-α-gal plates. After BLAST analysis, proteins interacting with CtNAC78 were obtained and listed in Table S2. These proteins are involved in various biological processes, such as E3 ubiquitin ligases, NDR1/HIN1-like protein 3, and protein phosphatase 2C9. Furthermore, among these clones, 38 of them both grew and turned light blue on SD/-TLHA + 0.5 mM 3AT + X-α-gal plates.

Identification of the Interaction Between CtNAC78 and CtPP2C9, CtE3, and CtNHL3 Using Yeast Spotting Analysis and the bifc BiFC Assay

One-to-one Y2H assays were introduced to verify the interaction between the target protein and three putative proteins (CtPP2C9, CtE3, and CtNHL3). Figure 8A shows that the yeast cells harboring pGBKT7-CtNAC78A+pGADT7-CtPP2C9 or pGBKT7-CtNAC78A+pGADT7-CtE3 grew on the SD-TL, SD-TLH +0.5 mM 3AT, and SD-TLH +0.5 mM 3AT+X-α-gal selective media, where yeast cells containing pGBKT7-CtNAC78A+pGADT7-CtE3 were especially prominent. These findings indicate that CtNAC78 might interact with CtPP2C9 and CtE3, especially CtE3. However, no yeasts transformed with pGBKT7-CtNAC78A+pGADT7-CtNHL3 grew on SD-TL, SD-TLH +0.5 mM 3AT, or SD-TLH +0.5 mM 3AT+X-α-gal. These data suggest that no interaction between CtNHL3 and CtNAC78 occurred.

Verification of CtNAC78-interacting proteins (A: CtE3, B: CtPP2C9, C: CtNHL3) using the yeast two-hybrid assay.

The BiFC assay was introduced to further investigate the interaction between CtNAC78 and CtE3. As illustrated in Figure 9, YFP fluorescence (yellow) was detected in the nucleus of tobacco epidermal cells harboring nYFP-CtNAC78 and cYFP-CtE3, while no fluorescence signal was observed in the negative cells containing nYFP-CtNAC78/cYFP or nYFP/cYFP-CtE3. This result also confirms the interaction between CtNAC78 and CtE3.

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assays revealing the interactions between CtNAC78 and CtE3. YFP: yellow fluorescent protein; RFP-NLS: red fluorescent protein nuclear localization signal; Y+R: merge of YFP and RFP-NLS; Y+R+B: merge of YFP, RFP-NLS, and bright. Bar, 50 μm.

In other studies, many proteins have been found to interact with NAC TFs, such as NOR (Non-ripening)102, isochorismate synthase 1103, cytochrome oxidase 11104, and E3 ubiquitin ligases. As a versatile post-translational modification, ubiquitination plays a pivotal role in modulating growth and developmental processes in all eukaryotic species105. E3 ubiquitin ligase is responsible for recognizing and selecting specific target proteins for ubiquitination, which has been demonstrated to be associated with various biological processes106. For instance, during the drought treatment of Arabidopsis, the E3 ligase MREL57 was found to have a positive regulatory effect by degrading the microtubule-associated protein WDL7107. Similarly, Lin and his coworkers found that the drought tolerance of A. thalinana overexpressing PbrATL18, a subfamily of E3 ubiquitin ligase genes cloned from Pyrus breschneideri, was improved108. Similar to other articles, an interaction between E3 ubiquitin ligases and CtNAC TFs was also found in the present work109–111. Another study identified an interaction between the MaNAC029 protein and the E3 ligase MaXB3106. Additionally, OsSNAC2 was found to interact with OsSADR1, a member of the E3 ubiquitin ligase family112. Our Y2H results revealed that CtNAC78 interacts with E3 ubiquitin ligase. However, it remains essential to further confirm the interaction between CtNAC78 and CtE3 using other methods (co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, pull-down assay) in subsequent studies. Previous studies have widely reported that the stability of many TFs is tightly controlled at the post-translational level. Notably, some of these TFs are inherently unstable and prone to degradation under normal conditions but rapidly accumulate upon drought stress105. Taken DREB2A (Dehydration Responsive Binding Element 2A) as an example, Qin et al. demonstrated that under normal conditions, DREB2A is degraded by DRIP1/2 (RING-type E3 ligases, DREB2A Interacting Protein 1) via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway, leading to the suppression of drought signaling113. However, under drought stress, this degradation process is suppressed, allowing DREB2A to accumulate and subsequently activate downstream drought-responsive genes114. Thus, we hypothesize that under normal conditions, CtNAC78 interacts with CtE3 and is targeted for degradation. However, upon drought stress, this degradation is inhibited, leading to the accumulation of CtNAC78. Furthermore, accumulated CtNAC78 may upregulate the expression of antioxidant enzyme genes, thereby augmenting cellular ROS scavenging capacity. The primarily proposed model of CtNAC78-mediated drought stress is shown in Figure 10. However, further confirmation of the interaction between CtNAC78 and CtE3, and direct validation of CtNAC78’s binding to the promoter regions of specific antioxidant enzyme genes through experiments, remain a priority for future research.

Conclusions

In the present study, CtNAC78 ectopic expression enhanced the drought tolerance of Arabidopsis by reducing ROS accumulation, elevating photosynthetic pigments and osmoregulatory substances, increasing antioxidant enzyme activities, and upregulating drought-responsive genes. Furthermore, after establishing a high-quality cDNA library of safflower, 38 putative proteins that interact with CtNAC78 were identified by Y2H. In addition, the interaction between CtNAC78 and CtE3 was demonstrated using yeast spotting analyses and the BiFC assay. In summary, this work demonstrated that CtNAC78 positively regulates the safflower drought response. These findings provide a foundation for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying CtNAC78-mediated drought tolerance in safflower. While this study establishes CtNAC78 as a key regulator of drought tolerance, the upstream signaling events that control its expression, as well as its detailed downstream molecular mechanisms, require further elucidation. Moreover, re-watering experiments should be incorporated to investigate the recovery ability of the plant after drought stress.
