Machine learning-based Bayesian optimization of tuned inerter dampers for enhanced seismic response control in high-rise base-isolated structures
Authors: Shuai Huang, Kesong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA Bayesian optimization-based design framework for Tuned Inerter Dampers (TIDs) is presented in this paper. The TIDs are used to high-rise base-isolated buildings that are subjected to ground motions that are far-fault, near-fault non-pulse, and near-fault pulse. The suggested approach clearly takes into account long-period dynamic characteristics and inertance-oriented parameterization, in contrast to traditional analytical tuning techniques. To effectively determine the ideal inertance, frequency ratio, and damping ratio that minimize seismic response metrics, a probabilistic Gaussian-process surrogate model is utilized. The optimized TID typically reduces mean square displacement by 20–25% under far-fault and near-fault non-pulse excitations and by 10–18% under near-fault pulse-type ground motions, according to thorough numerical studies performed on 30-, 40-, and 50-story base-isolated structures. The reduction of peak absolute accelerations at the top stories can reach 22.8%, which is a substantial improvement over traditional TMD, TMDI, and MTMDI systems. The suggested approach works best for mid-to high-rise buildings (30–40 stories), but because of higher-mode dominance, it eventually loses effectiveness for very tall structures. The current framework’s primary drawbacks stem from its idealized inerter modeling and simplified single-degree-of-freedom representation, which may underestimate multi-modal and nonlinear effects in extremely tall buildings. However, under long-period and pulse-type earthquake excitations, the suggested Bayesian-optimized TID provides a reliable and computationally effective design method for enhancing the seismic resistance of high-rise base-isolated buildings.Keywords: Tuned inerter damper, Bayesian optimization, Seismic isolation, High-rise buildings, Machine learning, Vibration control, Ground motion modeling, Clough–Penzien spectrum, Structural resilienceSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Seismic events present considerable problems to the structural integrity and safety of edifices, especially in earthquake-prone areas1. The damaging capacity of ground motions, defined by varied spectrum content and intensity, requires effective vibration control measures to reduce dynamic reactions and guarantee occupant safety2. High-rise base-isolated structures, increasingly prevalent in urban settings due to their architectural and functional benefits3, present distinct seismic issues relative to low- and mid-rise buildings4. These issues stem from their intricate dynamic behavior, encompassing higher-mode effects and sensitivity to long-period ground vibrations, particularly those associated with near-fault pulse-like excitations. Effective seismic management is essential for limiting structure damage, mitigating economic losses, and improving resistance against far-fault, near-fault non-pulse, and near-fault pulse ground motions.

Apart from the traditional passive control methods such base isolation, viscous dampers, and tuned mass dampers (TMDs), new research has investigated hybrid and multi-mechanism control approaches to improve seismic resilience. These consist of inerter-based mechanisms, semi-active and active control systems, and base isolation combined with additional dampening devices. Configurations with inerter enhancements, including TMDI and TID systems, have shown better results in lowering acceleration and displacement responses without requiring a sizable increase in mass. Additionally, it has been demonstrated that hybrid control strategies that incorporate several energy dissipation methods improve robustness under broadband and near-fault seismic excitations, especially for long-period and flexible structures.

Base isolation systems, which use flexible and energy-dissipating interfaces to separate the superstructure from ground motion, make up a basic class of passive seismic protection techniques. Advanced sliding-based isolation systems with re-centering capabilities have been shown in recent experimental experiments to dramatically reduce residual deformations and structural damage under intense seismic excitations5. At the same time, inerter-based dampening devices have gained more and more interest because of their capacity to produce significant apparent inertial effects without adding an excessive amount of physical bulk. In flexible constructions exposed to severe dynamic excitations, comparative studies have shown the improved vibration mitigation performance of multiple tuned mass damper–inerter (MTMDI) combinations and inerter-based dampers6,7.

To further improve robustness and adaptability under various loading situations, new active TMDI techniques have been put forth8. Complementary advancements in dual-functional metamaterial-based isolation systems and nonlinear vibration isolation mechanisms have also demonstrated remarkable potential for response modulation and broad-band vibration suppression9,10. For dependable performance evaluation of isolation and damping systems, precise characterization of seismic input characteristics and damping calibration are still necessary, especially in the presence of intricate soil–structure interactions and spatially fluctuating ground motions11,12. It is crucial to clarify that the ‘inertance (b)’ linked to the TID does not correspond to a physical gravitational mass, but rather to the inertance (represented by the symbol , with units of kg) supplied by the inerter device. , are the accelerations at the terminals, is the mathematical expression for the inerter, a two terminal mechanical element that generates a resisting force proportionate to the relative acceleration between its terminals. TIDs differ from conventional TMDs, which depend on physical oscillating masses, in that this inertance produces a perceived mass effect without adding actual weight. For high-rise base-isolated structures, where reducing additional structural load is essential for practical and economical implementation, this lightweight feature is especially beneficial12,20. In line with earlier research on inerter-based devices, the inerter’s contribution is measured in this study using the inertance ratio (where Mis is the equivalent mass of the base-isolated structure).

Previous research has thoroughly examined TIDs in base-isolated structures, predominantly concentrating on low- and mid-rise buildings represented as Single-Degree-Of-freedom (SDOF) systems13–15. These investigations refined TID parameters to reduce mean-squared displacement or acceleration under simplified loading circumstances, such white-noise excitations or harmonic loads16. Nevertheless, high-rise base-isolated edifices present supplementary difficulties17, encompassing higher-mode effects, extended natural periods, and heightened sensitivity to the spectral properties of ground motion. The foundational work18 assessed TID performance in 5-, 10-, and 15-story edifices subjected to far-fault and near-fault ground motions, demonstrating reduced control efficacy in higher structures, especially under near-fault pulse-like motions. This highlights the necessity for sophisticated optimization methods specifically designed for high-rise structures.

Conventional optimization methods, such as numerical integration or algebraic solutions, often struggle to account for nonlinear interactions and spectral variability in high-rise structures. These methods may require extensive computational resources or fail to capture the full range of dynamic responses across diverse seismic scenarios. To address these limitations, this study proposes Bayesian Optimization, a machine learning-driven approach that efficiently explores the parameter space to identify optimal TID configurations. Bayesian Optimization employs a probabilistic surrogate model, typically a Gaussian Process, to balance exploration and exploitation, minimizing the number of costly objective function evaluations. Here, Bayesian Optimization (BO) is used as a probabilistic, sample-efficient optimization technique that uses a surrogate model, usually a Gaussian Process (GP), to approximate the objective function and direct the search for optimal TID parameters. This helps to clarify the role of machine learning in this study. Although BO makes use of machine learning concepts (such as probabilistic modeling and uncertainty quantification using GPs), it does not entail predictive inference in the traditional sense or building a supervised machine learning model on sizable datasets. Rather, it functions as a potent black-box optimization tool that is especially well-suited for costly assessments, like those seen in structural dynamics simulations with uncertain ground motions.

The main aim of this research is to create an optimal design framework for TIDs in high-rise base-isolated structures, concentrating on minimizing seismic reactions to far-fault, near-fault non-pulse, and near-fault pulse ground motions. This study enhances the SDOF model from19 by integrating the Clough–Penzien spectrum to precisely depict ground motion frequency content, guaranteeing that optimization reflects site-specific seismic attributes. The suggested methodology seeks to address the shortcomings of conventional TID applications in high-rise buildings, where extended natural periods and intricate modal interactions diminish control efficacy. The study employs Bayesian Optimization to ascertain the ideal frequency ratio and damping ratio of the TID, customized to the dynamic qualities of high-rise structures and the spectral characteristics of ground motions. Although our methodological advancements are incremental rather than groundbreaking, the study significantly contributes by applying Bayesian Optimization-based design of TIDs to high-rise base-isolated buildings. These structures are especially susceptible to higher-mode effects and long-period near-fault ground motions, aspects that have been inadequately explored in the literature. Consequently, the suggested framework offers pragmatic design guidance and improves seismic resilience for high-rise structures. The dynamic response of a base-isolated high-rise building, utilizing a TID, is represented through a SDOF model to encapsulate the principal vibrational mode that predominantly influences the seismic performance of such structures. Because the isolation layer greatly lengthens the fundamental period and causes first-mode dominance in the seismic response, the use of an SDOF model is warranted for base-isolated high-rise structures. Higher modes contribute very little to the total displacement and base shear, especially at the isolation level, but the fundamental mode usually accounts for a significant fraction of the modal mass participation (sometimes surpassing 80–90%) in base-isolated buildings20. Higher modes may be more important in fixed-base high-rise constructions, in contrast to this tendency. This domination is supported by the modal participation factors for the 30-, 40-, and 50-story models that were studied in this study (detailed in Sect. Structure information and Fig. 10). With an emphasis on the dominant mode governing isolation-layer displacements, this supports the SDOF approximation for effective parametric optimization of TID parameters. Advantages of bayesian optimization

In comparison to heuristic search approaches like Genetic Algorithms (GA), Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), or Differential Evolution (DE), Bayesian Optimization presents unique advantages for the current issue. Genetic algorithms and analogous methods generally necessitate thousands of function evaluations for convergence, which becomes impractical when each evaluation entails computationally demanding seismic reaction simulations. Conversely, Bayesian Optimization utilizes a probabilistic surrogate model (Gaussian Process) to direct the search, attaining equivalent or superior solutions with markedly fewer evaluations. Furthermore, Bayesian Optimization directly quantifies uncertainty in uncharted areas of the parameter space, achieving a more effective balance between exploration and exploitation compared to heuristic approaches. These attributes render BO especially appropriate for high-rise base-isolated edifices, where the analysis of intricate seismic responses under various ground motions incurs substantial processing expenses.

Bayesian Optimization provides notable benefits compared to conventional numerical and algebraic methods, especially in tackling the intricacies of high-rise base-isolated buildings subjected to seismic forces. Its efficacy is attributed to necessitating fewer assessments of the objective function, rendering it particularly suitable for computationally intensive simulations39. The Gaussian Process model adeptly manages uncertainty in the optimization process, guaranteeing resilience against fluctuating ground motion characteristics. The approach offers versatility by tolerating nonlinear and multi-modal objective functions, prevalent in tall structures affected by higher-mode effects. Moreover, Bayesian Optimization exhibits versatility, facilitating the future incorporation of supplementary elements such as nonlinear isolation bearings or higher-mode interactions, thereby augmenting its relevance in sophisticated seismic engineering research.

However, because the TID mainly targets the primary isolation-mode response, higher-mode effects may restrict its impact on top-floor accelerations in very tall structures.

The attenuation effect is further enhanced by increasing inertance ratios, especially in the higher stories where dynamic amplification is more noticeable. The usefulness of the suggested TID in reducing extreme acceleration demands in tall base-isolated buildings is demonstrated by the most notable benefits seen under near-fault pulse excitations.

In 40- and 50-storey buildings, despite significant reductions (shown in Fig. 16), peak accelerations on top floors still approach or surpass serviceability thresholds during near-fault pulse ground movements. This suggests that a solitary TID situated at the isolation layer may inadequately meet the acceleration requirements in very tall buildings.

Consequently, while increasing TID inertance ratio enhances vibration reduction, it is not a complete solution for high-rise buildings. Alternative or complementary strategies such as distributed inerter-based devices across multiple stories, or hybrid systems combining TID with semi-active control may be more effective in mitigating acceleration responses.

Comparative performance evaluation with existing inerter-based control systems

A thorough comparison with popular mass- and inerter-based vibration control systems under the same structural and seismic conditions was carried out in order to verify the efficacy of the suggested Bayesian-optimized TID. Among the most well-known passive vibration control techniques for earthquake reduction in tall buildings are the traditional Tuned Mass Damper (TMD), the Tuned Mass Damper Inerter (TMDI), and the Multi-Tuned Mass Damper Inerter (MTMDI).

For the same high-rise base-isolated structural model with an inertance ratio µ = 0.1 and a fundamental isolation period T = 4 s, each system was constructed using the optimal tuning formulae found in the literature. The displacement reduction ratio J, which is the ratio of the controlled system’s mean square displacement response to that of the uncontrolled structure, and the peak acceleration reduction ratio at the top story were used to measure the performance.

The suggested optimized TID consistently outperforms all benchmark control schemes across all categories of seismic excitation, as shown by the comparison results in Table 7. The optimized TID obtains a displacement reduction ratio of J = 0.63 for far-fault ground motions, which is 37% better than the uncontrolled scenario. In contrast, the conventional TMD and TMDI only achieve reductions of 17% and 24%, respectively. This demonstrates how the suggested inertance-oriented design has better tuning efficiency.

Conclusion

The main conclusions of the study are outlined in this part along with their application to the seismic design of high-rise base-isolated buildings. It provides future research paths targeted at expanding the applicability and robustness of inerter-based vibration control systems, highlights the quantitative performance improvements attained by the optimized TID, and discusses the primary constraints of the proposed framework.

A Bayesian optimization-based inertance-oriented design framework for tunable inerter dampers (TIDs) applied to high-rise base-isolated buildings exposed to near-fault and far-fault seismic excitations was presented in this paper. The optimized TID typically reduces mean square displacement by 20–25% under far-fault and near-fault non-pulse ground motions, while near-fault pulse-type excitations the most critical loading conditions for tall buildings reduce it by 10–18%, according to thorough numerical studies conducted on 30-, 40-, and 50-story buildings. The upper stories’ peak absolute accelerations were also decreased by as much as 22.8%, which was a substantial improvement over traditional TMD, TMDI, and MTMDI arrangements. The findings also show that the effectiveness of control is greatest for mid- to high-rise buildings (30–40 stories) and progressively declines for ultra-tall buildings because of the growing dominance of higher vibration modes. This highlights the advantages and intrinsic drawbacks of single-location inerter-based control strategies.

The suggested structure has a number of drawbacks despite its benefits. The structural models may underestimate higher-mode interactions in extremely tall buildings since they are mostly based on single-degree-of-freedom and first-mode-dominant representations. Furthermore, practical nonlinearities related to actual inerter mechanisms and isolation bearings are not specifically taken into account because the inerter device is treated as an ideal linear component. Furthermore, a small number of recordings and Clough–Penzien spectrum models are used to characterize the seismic input, which might not fully capture the variety of site-specific ground motions.

To explicitly account for higher-mode and torsional effects, future research should expand the suggested Bayesian optimization framework to multi-DOF and three-dimensional structural models. For practical implementation, actual inerter device models and nonlinear isolation systems must also be included. To further improve seismic performance, especially for ultra-tall buildings exposed to near-fault pulse-type earthquakes, distributed and multi-location inerter configurations, hybrid passive semi-active control strategies, and large-scale validation using extensive real ground motion databases should be studied.
