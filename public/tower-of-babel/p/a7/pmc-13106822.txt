Lipid nanoparticle delivery of circle RNA vaccine induces potent immune responses
Authors: Ruohan Yang, Lin Jia, Jiuwei Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircle RNA (circRNA) is a closed-loop nucleic acid resistant to exonuclease degradation. Their enhanced stability and potential for efficient protein expression through circRNA modification render them promising novel therapeutic agents for cancer treatment. In this study, we systematically investigated the antitumor and anti-inflammatory activities of circRNA vaccines in mice, as well as their effects on activating antigen-specific immune responses, through a meta-analysis. Subsequently, we further validated the immune-activating effects of circRNA using monocytes and T cells isolated from human peripheral blood mononuclear cells. We found that circRNA induces the activation and maturation of dendritic cells, as well as the responses of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells. These findings demonstrate the potential of circRNA to exert significant effects in therapeutic cancer vaccines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46871-6.Keywords: circRNA, Lipid nanoparticles, Immunotherapy, Malignant tumors, Antigen-presenting cellsSubject terms: Cancer, Immunology

Introduction

Circle RNA (circRNA) constitutes a distinct RNA subtype featuring a covalently closed single-stranded structure1. circRNA exhibit non-coding functions such as transcriptional regulation, splicing regulation, signal transduction, and molecular sponge activities. These regulatory roles enable them to promote the onset and progression of various diseases, thereby influencing the intrinsic mechanisms of disease2–5.

In recent years, the use of in vitro-synthesized RNA to express neoantigens for disease treatment has become a focal point for many researchers6. This approach enables the precise and efficient expression of specific proteins within cells and tissues7. Currently, the use of mRNA to encode COVID-19 antigens represents one of the most prominent applications of this method. However, mRNA vaccines suffer from poor stability, imposing stringent requirements on transport and storage conditions8. Concurrently, mRNA faces the risk of rapid degradation, resulting in short-lived neoantigen expression and necessitating repeated dosing for therapeutic efficacy9. Our prior research has demonstrated that, compared to mRNA, circRNA possesses a unique circular structure that confers resistance to RNase R digestion, thereby enhancing its stability and positioning it as a novel vaccine candidate10. Studies indicate that circRNA has a half-life approximately 2.5 times longer than mRNA, thereby prolonging the duration of protein expression11. As illustrated in Fig. 1, circRNA can mediate ribosomal initiation and protein translation via the internal ribosomal entry site (IRES) element, even in the absence of a free 5′ end, thereby enabling efficient antigen presentation12. Moreover, unlike DNA vaccines, circular RNAs carry no risk of host genome recombination13 and do not require endosomal processing and cleavage, which can reduce antigen presentation efficiency in peptide vaccines14. Established research demonstrates that circRNAs can elicit potent T- and B-cell responses, resulting in complete tumor regression in mouse models of melanoma15. Wei et al. confirmed that circRNA vaccines provide robust protection against SARS-CoV-2 variants in both mice and rhesus monkeys, exhibiting excellent tolerability with no significant adverse effects16.

Mechanism of action diagram for circRNA vaccines.

circRNA holds immense potential as a next-generation RNA vaccine platform. Its unique advantages of efficient synthesis and stable high expression have demonstrated promise in vaccines, immunotherapies, and protein replacement therapies, offering innovative and effective treatment prospects for human diseases17. Concurrently, integrating lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) with RNA vaccines significantly enhances their therapeutic applications18. LNP efficiently encapsulates RNA, enabling large-scale and cost-effective production.

Therefore, building upon these findings, this study investigates the extent to which LNP-encapsulated circRNA induces immune responses in mice, thereby exerting antitumor and anti-inflammatory effects. This research provides valuable insights and perspectives for future investigations.

Materials and methods

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines19. Internet searches were conducted across the PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, Springer, and ScienceDirect, and OVID databases to retrieve relevant publications from 2020 to the present (October 2025). The following search terms were used to identify articles: ‘circle RNA’, ‘circRNA’,’ circular RNA’, and ‘vaccine’. (PROSPERO ID: CRD420251242287).

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for meta-analysis studies: (1) circRNA therapy for tumors or infectious diseases delivered via LNP; (2) Detailed documentation of intervention measures, experimental groupings, and dosage administration; (3) Inclusion of at least one of the following metrics: tumor volume, mouse body weight, and immunological parameters; (4) Full-text articles available.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) non-mouse animal models or in vitro studies; (2) Data failing to demonstrate tumor changes in mice before and after treatment; (3) Reviews, communications, case reports, presentations, letters, expert opinions, and editorials; (4) Relevant data unavailable for extraction; (5) Failure to meet inclusion criteria.

Data extraction and quality assessment

The current studies primarily comprised preclinical research using animal models. We analyzed data on whether mice with tumors or infectious diseases benefit from treatment with a circRNA vaccine. Extracted content included: (a) basic study details, including first author and publication year; (b) study characteristics, including tumor volume, weight, inhibition rate, mouse body weight, and adverse reactions; (c) elements relevant to risk of bias assessment. Using tools developed by the SYRCLE20, the authors assessed the methodological quality and risk of bias in the included trials across domains: selection, performance, detection, attrition, reporting, and other biases.

Plasmid construction

The vector was purchased from MIAOLING Biotech (IRES-Circ-GFP-FLAG). Immunofluorescence detection was subsequently performed using an anti-Flag antibody (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804). circRNA was synthesized using the HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB E2040S). IVT templates were amplified via PCR before RNA synthesis (Q5 Hot Start High Fidelity 2x Master Mix). Each 20 µL IVT reaction utilized 1 µg of circRNA PCR template. Reactions proceeded at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, IVT templates were degraded by incubating with 2 µL DNase I (NEB M0303S) at 37 °C for 20 min. Digestion was performed using 1 U RNase R per µg RNA at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by RNase R inactivation at 70 °C for 10 min. Circularization efficiency was assessed via RT-qPCR (forward primer: TCTGAACCACACAAATGGTC; reverse primer: ATGGTGGGGTACCCATCCGCT).

Cell lines

Healthy human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were commercially purchased from Beijing VITONLIVIA (D176368). Cell line origin: human. Gender: male. Source tissue: peripheral blood. The cell lines used were not misidentified, and there was no contamination during the experiment.

CD14 + magnetic beads (MicroBeads, Miltenyi, 130-050-201) were added at a ratio of 20 µl per 10⁷ PBMCs and incubated at 4 °C in the dark for 15 min. Following incubation, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated using LS Columns (MASC, 130-042-401). Cells were cultured in 10% 1640 medium supplemented with cytokines IL-4 (50 ng/ml) and GM-CSF (20 ng/ml) to induce dendritic cell differentiation.

For in vitro T-cell activation assays, healthy PBMCs were also commercially purchased from Beijing VITONLIVIA(D176368). CD3 + magnetic beads (MicroBeads, Miltenyi, 130-097-043). For every 10⁷ PBMCs, add 10 µl CD14+ magnetic beads and incubate at 4 °C in the dark for 15 min. Subsequently, isolate CD3⁺ T cells using an LS Column and culture them in 1640 medium, promptly adding activated dendritic cells to maintain activity. The culture medium contains 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 2% penicillin, 1% streptomycin, and 1% levofloxacin, and cells are grown in a humidified environment at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. All cell lines are monitored for mycoplasma infection using the Impact III PCR method.

Flow cytometry analysis

The following antibodies target surface markers: Human CD4 (FITC, BioLegend, 300505, 1:100); Human CD8 (APCy7, BioLegend, 300908, 1:100); human IFN-γ (BD, PE, 344117,1:100; human CD80 (APC, BioLegend, 305219, 1:100); human CD86 (FITC, BioLegend, 344704, 1:100).

Immunofluorescence

In immunofluorescence experiments, transfected dendritic cells were centrifuged and smeared onto microscope slides. Sections were incubated overnight with monoclonal anti-FLAG M2 mouse antibody (Sigma, F1804, 1:500 dilution), followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 647-labelled secondary antibody. DAPI was used for nuclear counterstaining. Images were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with NIS-Elements Viewer imaging software.

RT-qpcr

RT-qPCR analysis was performed in triplicate using Brilliant II SYBR Green qRT-PCR Master Mix (Agilent, 600825) and a LightCycler 480 (Roche). The relative RNA level was calculated by the ddCt method compared to the B-Actin control.

Primer sequences:

Reverse: CAGGGGTGGTTATTGCATCT.

Reverse: AAAGACACAGGTAGCTGCCA.

Preparation and purification of in vitro transcribed circRNA

To eliminate double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) contaminants, the crude transcription reaction was directly treated with RNase III at 37 °C for 30 min to specifically degrade dsRNA. The reaction was then purified using a silica-membrane-based RNA cleanup column to remove proteins, salts, and unincorporated nucleotides. And the absence of dsRNA contamination was verified using a dsRNA-specific fluorometric quantification kit and ELISA with a four-parameter logistic (4PL) standard curve. (Yesaen, 36717ES96).

ELISpot

As mentioned above, ELISpot T cells were collected from peripheral blood and then restimulated with dendritic cells (DCs) transfected with circleRNA for 24 h. IFN‑γ ELISpot assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Dakewe, 2110005, Human) to detect IFN‑γ‑secreting T cells, and the colored spots were counted.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using Review Manager 5.4 software. Forest plots displayed pooled risk ratios with their respective 95% confidence intervals. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the inconsistency index (I2). Data exhibiting substantial heterogeneity were analyzed using random-effects models, whilst those with minimal heterogeneity were evaluated using fixed-effects models.

Prism software version 9.2.0 was used to perform statistical analyses. One-way and two-way ANOVA tests and an unpaired two-sided Student’s t test were used to compare more than two groups, and differences between groups with P-values below 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Systematic screening

The study screening process is illustrated in Fig. 2. A total of 427 articles met the inclusion criteria. Among these, duplicate and irrelevant articles (e.g., studies with inconsistent subjects) were excluded following an abstract review. Subsequently, articles with inconsistent methodologies (e.g., solely in vitro cellular experiments or the absence of tumor-related endpoints) were excluded after full-text review. Ultimately, ten tumors15,21–29 and eleven infectious16,30–39 disease model preclinical studies were selected for meta-analysis.

Data extracted from the included studies are presented in Table 1. All selected studies were randomized controlled trials (RCTs) utilizing mice as experimental subjects. Overall, the animal strains employed were predominantly BLAB/C or C57BL/6, with administration primarily via intramuscular or subcutaneous injection. Notably, within infectious disease mouse models, three instances involved intranasal administration, and one case employed tracheal instillation. Among animal tumor models, ovalbumin served as the neoantigen in four instances, representing its most prevalent application.

Body weight changes in all mice before and after treatment

As shown in Fig. 3, we compared the body weight of mice before and after treatment according to different administration routes. Subgroup analyses were performed for subcutaneous injection, intramuscular injection, intranasal injection, intravenous injection, and other administration routes (including intraperitoneal injection, intramuscular injection, and intratracheal instillation). Compared with the control group, there was no statistically significant difference in body weight in the vaccine group across different administration routes (overall standardized mean difference (SMD) = -0.05, 95% CI = -0.42 to 0.32, P = 0.80). Subgroup analyses showed that the pooled effect sizes of different administration routes did not reach statistical significance, and the overall between-study heterogeneity was low (I2 = 0%).

Changes in T-cell proportion before and after treatment in all mice

The results demonstrated that the vaccine group exhibited a significantly increased proportion of activated T cells compared with the control group (overall SMD = 4.14, 95% CI = 2.67 to 5.61, P < 0.00001). Subgroup analyses indicated that the vaccine conferred significantly superior effects relative to the control via subcutaneous, intramuscular, and intravenous administration (all P < 0.05), whereas no statistically significant effect was observed in the subgroup of other administration routes (Fig. 4).

Assessment of tumor-related parameters in mice

Subsequently, we conducted separate efficacy evaluations on mice within the tumor model, including measurements of tumor volume changes. We also documented alterations in immune cells playing pivotal roles in tumor treatment, such as macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs).

Tumor volume changes

Figure 5 summarizes tumor volume alterations in mice before and after circRNA treatment. Compared with the control group, the vaccine immunization group exhibited a significantly reduced tumor volume in mice (overall SMD = -5.36, 95% CI: -7.52 to -3.20, P < 0.00001), although high between-study heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 70%, P = 0.002). Subgroup analyses stratified by administration route showed statistically significant effects in the subcutaneous injection subgroup (SMD = -6.22, 95% CI: -11.39 to -1.04, P = 0.02) and intravenous injection subgroup (SMD = -7.90, 95% CI: -10.94 to -4.86, P < 0.00001). The intramuscular injection subgroup showed a borderline statistical trend (SMD = -4.77, 95% CI: -9.70 to 0.15, P = 0.06), whereas no statistical significance was reached in the other administration routes subgroup (SMD = -1.67, 95% CI: -3.93 to 0.60, P = 0.15). Notably, significant differences in effect size were detected among different administration route subgroups (P = 0.01), suggesting that administration route represents an important source of heterogeneity.

Thus, serum antibody titers and viral load were selected to assess treatment outcomes, while cytokines IFN-γ and IL-4 were chosen to evaluate the effects on the immune system response.

In vitro experiments validate circRNA’s capacity to induce immune responses

To further validate that circRNA can induce robust immune responses, we designed a circRNA vaccine capable of expressing a FLAG tag and encoding GFP. Following circularization and digestion, circRNA constituted over 80% of the product (Fig. 12A). To minimize the immune-stimulatory effect of dsRNA contaminants generated during in vitro transcription (IVT), we performed an additional digestion with RNase III, followed by silica column purification. The dsRNA content in the circRNA preparation was then determined by ELISA. The results showed that the dsRNA level was significantly reduced after digestion (Fig. 12B). Following transfection, dendritic cells exhibited significant upregulation of the cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α (Fig. 12C). As anticipated, immunofluorescence revealed that circRNA-transfected dendritic cells successfully expressed Flag (Fig. 12D).

Discussion

circRNA is a covalently closed circular RNA molecule that has garnered significant attention due to its exceptional durability and stability, offering promise as a stable platform for gene expression delivery40,41. In this study, we first performed a systematic meta-analysis to investigate the therapeutic efficacy of lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-delivered circRNA in mouse tumor models. Consistent and marked tumor regression was observed across all included mouse models, and notably, no significant alterations in body weight were detected throughout the treatment period. This finding not only confirms the potent antitumor activity of circRNA vaccines but also indicates a favorable safety profile, supporting LNP-circRNA as a safe and effective therapeutic strategy for tumor intervention. Accumulating evidence has established that the efficacy of therapeutic tumor vaccines is predominantly dependent on efficient antigen presentation and the subsequent activation of dendritic cells (DCs)—the key professional antigen-presenting cells in the immune system42. Consequently, we analyzed immune cell states to elucidate the mechanism by which circRNA vaccines exert their effects. Results revealed marked proliferation and activation of T cells, DCs, and other immune cells following vaccination, suggesting that circRNA exerts potent antitumor effects by modulating the innate immune system. These results collectively suggest that circRNA vaccines exert their potent antitumor effects by modulating the immune system to initiate a cascade of adaptive immune responses, ultimately eliminating tumor cells. Subsequently, we examined circRNA vaccine efficacy against infectious diseases. Findings demonstrated a significantly reduced viral load in mouse lungs post-vaccination, with sustained high antibody titers over extended periods The concurrent activation of innate and adaptive immune cells in infectious disease models further corroborated the broad and potent therapeutic efficacy of circRNA vaccines against both malignant tumors and infectious pathogens16.

To further validate the potent anti-tumor and immune-activating effects of circRNA vaccines, we conducted in vitro verification experiments. To prepare high-purity circRNA and exclude non-specific immune interference, we combined RNase R-mediated digestion (to eliminate linear RNA precursors and fragments) with RNase III treatment (to specifically degrade dsRNA impurities), followed by secondary purification using a general silica column. Results demonstrated that purified circRNA successfully induced neoantigen expression in dendritic cells (DCs), while promoting DC activation and maturation, consistent with the findings of Huang et al.43. We further confirmed that circRNA-primed mature DCs could effectively present antigens to T cells and mediate the activation of antigen-specific T lymphocytes. These activated T cells subsequently secrete proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ. ELISpot experiments validate that antigen-specific T cells can be generated, which in turn orchestrate a robust antitumor immune microenvironment and promote tumor cell killing, consistent with the results of Amaya et al.15.

Collectively, our systematic meta-analysis and in vitro experimental validation consistently demonstrate that circRNA vaccines possess the capacity to elicit robust and sustained innate and adaptive immune responses, as well as to shape a proinflammatory tumor microenvironment. Endowed with multiple inherent advantages—including a covalently closed circular structure conferring superior stability, sustained and efficient neoantigen expression, and excellent in vivo tolerability—circRNA-encoded neoantigen vaccines represent a novel and promising therapeutic approach for the treatment of both malignant tumors and infectious diseases.

Notwithstanding these promising findings, several notable limitations and sources of substantial statistical heterogeneity must be acknowledged, which temper the interpretation and generalizability of our results. First, the meta-analysis revealed extreme statistical heterogeneity across the included studies for key immune and therapeutic outcome measures. Although we attempted to reduce heterogeneity through subgroup analysis, high heterogeneity persisted in some datasets. Comprehensive subgroup analysis indicated that this variability was primarily attributable to three interrelated factors: different routes of vaccine administration (e.g., intramuscular, subcutaneous, intravenous, intranasal), heterogeneity of disease models (e.g., different solid tumor types, viral pathogens), and diverse murine strains (C57BL/6, BALB/c, NOD/SCID) across the included studies. For instance, intranasal administration was associated with more pronounced local mucosal immune responses but relatively modest systemic immune effects, whereas intravenous injection elicited robust systemic immune activation.

Second, the current study is predominantly based on preclinical murine models, and the meta-analysis was restricted to animal experimental data due to the paucity of available clinical research on circRNA vaccines to date. There are inherent biological differences between murine models and humans, including disparities in immune system regulation (e.g., DC subsets, T cell receptor repertoires) and tumor microenvironment, which may result in inconsistent efficacy and safety profiles when translating circRNA vaccines from preclinical models to clinical applications. Although we supplemented our findings with in vitro validation using human PBMCs, this simplified system cannot fully recapitulate the complex in vivo microenvironment of the human body.

Finally, the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying the immune modulation of circRNA vaccines remain incompletely elucidated. While our results confirm that circRNA vaccines activate DCs and initiate adaptive immune responses, the specific molecular pathways by which LNP-circRNA is internalized by DCs and the potential crosstalk between circRNA and other innate immune sensors require further in-depth mechanistic investigation.

Despite these limitations, this study provides comprehensive and rigorous preclinical evidence for the therapeutic potential of LNP-delivered circRNA vaccines against both tumors and infectious diseases through systematic meta-analysis and in vitro validation with high-purity circRNA. Future research should focus on addressing the aforementioned limitations and heterogeneity to advance the development and clinical translation of circRNA vaccines. Priority areas include: conducting in-depth mechanistic studies to elucidate the molecular basis of circRNA uptake, cytoplasmic translation, and immune activation, thereby identifying potential molecular targets for enhancing vaccine efficacy; establishing more clinically relevant humanized mouse models to bridge the gap between preclinical and clinical research; and conducting large-scale, well-designed randomized controlled trials to validate the efficacy and safety of circRNA vaccines in human subjects. In conclusion, our study highlights the great potential of circRNA vaccines as a novel next-generation RNA vaccine platform, and with further optimization and in-depth validation, circRNA vaccines are expected to provide innovative and effective therapeutic options for human diseases.

Conclusion

This article demonstrates that LNP-delivered circRNA vaccines exhibit potent anti-tumor and anti-inflammatory effects, with minimal adverse reactions. Further experiments validate the capacity of circRNA to encode neoantigens, promote dendritic cell maturation, activate T cells, and stimulate the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, positioning it as a promising novel therapeutic approach for cancer treatment.
