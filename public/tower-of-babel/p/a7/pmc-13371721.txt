A staged Bayesian framework for longitudinal prediction of adolescent depression using add health data
Authors: Niloofar Ramezani, Ehiremen Adesua Azugbene, Jeffrey R. Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurately predicting adolescent depression from longitudinal behavioral data requires methods that incorporate feedback between prior mental health and substance use while remaining reproducible and interpretable. The objective of this study is predictive rather than explanatory or causal.MethodsUsing Waves I–IV of the U.S. National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health), we developed a novel staged Bayesian framework for longitudinal prediction: (Stage 1) multivariate logistic models with correlated random intercepts for depression, smoking, and alcohol use; (Stage 2) a composite substance-use indicator jointly modeled with depression; and (Stage 3) a temporal feedback model including lagged depression and lagged substance use. Posterior predictive checks and internal validation were used to assess model adequacy. Discrimination (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC]) and calibration (Brier score) were summarized across stages. The models are predictive rather than explanatory and are not intended to estimate causal effects.ResultsLagged depression (posterior mean = 0.98; 95% highest posterior density [HPD] interval: 0.45–1.52) and lagged substance use (posterior mean = 0.69; 95% HPD: 0.12–1.25) were associated with increased odds of future depression. Predictive performance improved from Stage 1 to Stage 3: AUC increased from 0.78 to 0.84, and Brier score decreased from 0.185 to 0.152. Posterior predictive checks supported model adequacy.ConclusionsThe staged Bayesian pipeline provides a generalizable template for longitudinal prediction with temporal feedback, improving discrimination and calibration while preserving interpretability.

Background

Depression and substance use are leading contributors to morbidity among adolescents and young adults, often co-occurring and following recurrent courses that disrupt education, social functioning, and long-term health [1, 2]. Despite their joint burden, many predictive models under-represent the temporal complexity in longitudinal behavioral data [3, 4].

A growing body of work suggests these conditions can be mutually reinforcing: depressive symptoms may increase vulnerability to substance use, while substance use may exacerbate depressive symptoms through neurobiological and social pathways [5, 6]. This feedback complicates prevention and screening during adolescence, when behavioral patterns are forming and access to treatment may be limited.

Methodologically, capturing feedback requires models that incorporate lagged states and recursive dependencies without sacrificing interpretability. Yet common approaches treat time-varying exposures as static or omit explicit lagged predictors, risking underestimation of cumulative and recursive effects [7, 8].

To address these gaps, we developed a structured staged Bayesian logistic regression framework that (i) quantifies co-occurrence between depression and substance use, (ii) consolidates behavioral risk into a composite measure, and (iii) introduces temporal feedback via lagged depression and lagged substance use. We apply the framework to Waves I–IV of the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health), a nationally representative U.S. cohort with repeated measures of mental and behavioral health, and evaluate whether a staged, modular specification improves predictive performance while remaining interpretable for adolescent depression risk [1, 3]. This approach builds on established developmental and behavioral research demonstrating stable co-occurrence and dynamic processes in adolescent substance use and mental health [9, 10]. The methodological contribution of this work is the development of a structured, reproducible modeling pipeline that integrates multivariate modeling of co-occurring behaviors, construction of a composite risk indicator, and a temporal feedback model for longitudinal prediction. The goal is not to introduce a new estimator, but to demonstrate how these components can be combined into a coherent Bayesian framework that supports interpretability, modularity, and diagnostic evaluation. Consistent with contemporary distinctions between predictive and explanatory modeling, the goal of this study is to develop a longitudinal prediction framework rather than to draw causal inferences.

This framework further contributes by integrating a staged Bayesian pipeline that accommodates feedback loops, correlated behavioral predictors, and posterior predictive validation [11]. Each stage—joint modeling, composite risk construction, and lagged feedback—offers a flexible and modular template for longitudinal prediction in behavioral health. The approach balances interpretability and statistical rigor, providing a reproducible strategy for longitudinal prediction in behavioral health.

Methods

Study population and data source

We used data from the National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health (Add Health), a nationally representative cohort study of U.S. adolescents enrolled in grades 7–12 during the 1994–95 school year [1]. Public-use data from Waves I through IV (1994–2008) were included. Each wave collected information on behavioral health, mental health, substance use, and sociodemographic characteristics.

We restricted the analytic sample to participants with complete information on depression, smoking, alcohol use, age, body mass index (BMI), and parental education across at least two consecutive waves, to support lagged predictor construction and ensure temporal continuity. The final sample comprised 4,820 individuals, each contributing two to four observations across time.

All variables were harmonized across waves to ensure consistency in measurement and coding. Complete-case analysis was used to preserve temporal continuity and avoid imputation bias in lagged models. Add Health survey weights, primary sampling unit (PSU), and strata were not incorporated; future sensitivity analyses may explore pseudo-Bayesian weighting or survey-aware extensions to assess robustness.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they contributed data for at least two consecutive waves of Add Health with non-missing measures of depression, smoking, alcohol use, age, BMI, and parental education. We excluded individuals who had missing data on any of these required variables or who participated in only a single wave, as temporal alignment is necessary for constructing lagged predictors. After applying these criteria, the final analytic sample included 4,820 participants, each contributing between two and four observations.

A complete-case approach was used to preserve the temporal ordering required for lagged depression and lagged substance-use variables. Because imputation across irregularly spaced waves can distort temporal dependencies, complete-case analysis ensured that all predictors and outcomes were observed in the correct temporal sequence. We acknowledge that complete-case restriction may introduce selection bias if missingness is informative, and we note this as a limitation in the Discussion.

A substantial proportion of participants were excluded due to missingness in one or more required variables or lack of consecutive waves, resulting in a final analytic sample of 4,820 participants. This exclusion is acknowledged in the Limitations, along with the potential for selection bias.

Measures

Depression was assessed at each wave using the 9- or 10-item version of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), with a score ≥ 10 indicating elevated depressive symptoms [2].

Smoking was defined as self-reported use of cigarettes within the past 30 days. Alcohol use was defined as five or more drinking occasions in the past 30 days or any binge drinking (≥ 5 drinks for males, ≥ 4 for females) [3]. A binary composite indicator was constructed to represent the presence of either smoking or alcohol use. This choice reflects a parsimonious representation of co‑occurring behavioral risk and is consistent with a prediction‑focused objective. Rather than estimating a latent construct, which would require additional modeling assumptions regarding measurement structure, the composite captures the presence of substance‑related risk exposure in a directly interpretable form. This simplification also stabilizes estimation in the presence of correlated behaviors and supports the staged modeling framework.

Two time-varying covariates were included: Age (centered within-individual) and BMI (based on self-reported height and weight). We also included one time-invariant covariate: Parental education, collected at Wave I and dichotomized as high school diploma or less versus some college or more [4].

Modeling strategy

We developed a structured three-stage logistic regression framework to examine how depression risk evolves over time in relation to co-occurring behavioral risk and prior mental health. All models incorporated subject-specific random intercepts to account for unmeasured individual-level heterogeneity. Latent dependencies among depression, smoking, and alcohol use are captured through the multivariate normal distribution of the random intercepts. The multivariate logistic likelihood and random-effects covariance structure are detailed explicitly in the Model Specification section.

In Stage 1, we jointly modeled depression, smoking, and alcohol use using a multivariate logistic regression with correlated random effects.

In Stage 2, we simplified the behavioral risk structure by constructing a composite binary indicator of substance use (coded as 1 if either smoking or alcohol use was reported), modeled jointly with depression using bivariate logistic regression.

Estimation and evaluation

All models were estimated via Bayesian inference using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) procedures in SAS 9.4 (PROC MCMC) [12]. Regression coefficients were assigned independent Normal (0, 100) priors. Variance components were assigned log-normal priors. The covariance matrix of the multivariate random effects was assigned an inverse-Wishart prior with scale matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S$$\end{document} and degrees of freedom \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\nu\:$$\end{document}, consistent with standard implementations in PROC MCMC, which does not support LKJ priors. Bayesian estimation was selected because it enables full posterior inference for both fixed and random effects, supports flexible modeling of correlated latent structures, and provides a natural framework for incorporating lagged predictors in longitudinal settings with sparse or irregular follow-up.

Compared to traditional approaches such as generalized estimating equations (GEE) or fixed-effects models [13, 14], Bayesian estimation offers greater flexibility in modeling latent dependencies and allows direct quantification of uncertainty through posterior distributions.

We used four parallel chains with 5,000 iterations each, discarding the first 1,000 as burn-in and applying thinning every 5 iterations, yielding 3,200 posterior samples per parameter. PROC MCMC was selected because the analysis was conducted within an established and reproducible SAS-based workflow and the model structure, while hierarchical, did not require high-dimensional parameter spaces or complex posterior geometries that would necessitate Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC). The models involved moderate-dimensional random effects and standard logistic likelihoods, for which Gibbs and Metropolis–Hastings updates in PROC MCMC provided stable and efficient estimation [15]. This choice ensured reproducibility within the analytic environment while remaining fully adequate for the inferential objectives of the study. Effective sample size (ESS) and Monte Carlo standard error (MCSE) were monitored for key parameters.

We evaluated model performance using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) to assess discrimination and the Brier score to assess calibration [16, 17]. Model performance was evaluated using apparent validation within the analytic sample. K-fold cross-validation or external validation was not conducted in the original analysis and has been removed from earlier drafts to ensure consistency. Posterior predictive checks were conducted to assess model adequacy. Watanabe-Akaike Information Criterion (WAIC) and Pareto-Smoothed Importance Sampling Leave-One-Out cross-validation (PSIS-LOO) metrics were not computed due to software constraints; model comparison was based on predictive performance (AUC, Brier score).

Convergence was confirmed by visual inspection of trace plots and using Gelman-Rubin diagnostics (target < 1.05) [16]. Posterior summaries were based on the final 3,200 iterations. Posterior predictive checks included comparing observed and replicated outcome proportions, examining replicated distributions of depression and substance-use indicators, and evaluating posterior predictive residuals. These checks followed standard Bayesian model-checking procedures [16].

Results

Participant characteristics

The analytic sample included 4,820 participants who contributed data from at least two waves of the Add Health study. Baseline characteristics of the analytic sample are summarized in Table 1. Participants were aged 12–18 years at baseline and were followed into early adulthood. At the wave level, 21.2% met the threshold for depressive symptoms (CES-D ≥ 10), 19.8% reported current smoking, and 27.3% reported alcohol use consistent with binge or frequent drinking. The co-occurrence of smoking and alcohol use was observed in 9.1% of wave-level records.

These descriptive statistics provide context for the behavioral risk landscape modeled in subsequent stages. Elevated depressive symptoms were more prevalent among female participants and those whose parents had lower educational attainment [1, 2].

Multivariate associations between depression and co-occurring risk behaviors

In the first modeling stage, depression, smoking, and alcohol use were jointly modeled using multivariate logistic regression with random intercepts. Depression was positively associated with both age (posterior mean = 0.71; 95% highest posterior density [HPD]: 0.15–1.28) and BMI (posterior mean = 1.31; 95% HPD: 0.58–2.07). Smoking and alcohol use were also positively associated with age and inversely associated with parental education.

Correlations among the random effects for depression and substance use (range: 0.38–0.44) suggest shared latent behavioral risk, supporting the rationale for a composite indicator in Stage 2.

Depression and composite substance use

In the second modeling stage, we constructed a binary indicator for substance use (coded as 1 if either smoking or alcohol use was reported). In a joint model of substance use and depression, the presence of substance use was significantly associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms. The posterior odds ratio was 1.82 (95% HPD: 1.22–2.75), controlling for age, BMI, and parental education.

The correlation between random intercepts for depression and substance use (ρ = 0.36) remained consistent, indicating stable latent behavioral risk across individuals.

Feedback effects on depression

In the final stage, we modeled depression at time t as a function of lagged depression and lagged substance use from the prior wave. Both lagged predictors were significantly associated with increased odds of current depression. The posterior mean for lagged depression was 0.98 (95% HPD: 0.45–1.52), and for lagged substance use, 0.69 (95% HPD: 0.12–1.25).

These findings support the presence of temporal feedback between depression and substance use, reinforcing the value of incorporating behavioral history into longitudinal prediction models. The effects of BMI and parental education were attenuated in this model, suggesting that behavioral history partially mediates demographic influences.

Model performance

Model performance improved across stages of the structured framework. Table 2 shows the predictive performance across three modeling stages based on AUC and Brier score. AUC measures discrimination while Brier score measures calibration. The AUC increased from 0.78 in Stage 1 to 0.84 in Stage 3. Correspondingly, the Brier score decreased from 0.185 to 0.152, indicating better discrimination and calibration in the feedback model. PROC MCMC does not natively compute uncertainty estimates for AUC or Brier score; future work may incorporate bootstrap or Bayesian predictive intervals to quantify uncertainty around these metrics.

Performance metrics reflect apparent validation on the analytic sample. Cross-validation or external validation was not conducted; future work should incorporate these to assess generalizability.

Calibration was assessed using the Brier score. Calibration slope and intercept metrics were not computed due to software constraints in PROC MCMC, which does not natively estimate these quantities. We note this as a limitation and recommend their inclusion in future implementations.

Uncertainty estimates for AUC and Brier scores were not derived; posterior intervals are reported only for fixed effects, and future work should extend uncertainty quantification to performance metrics.

These results suggest that incorporating lagged behavioral states enhances predictive accuracy and model calibration in longitudinal mental health modeling. AUC and Brier score are reported consistently across all stages to facilitate comparison of predictive performance.

Discussion

In this longitudinal study of U.S. adolescents and young adults, we found that prior depressive symptoms and substance use significantly increased the likelihood of subsequent depression. Using a staged modeling framework applied to Add Health data, we demonstrated that incorporating lagged behavioral predictors and modeling behavioral feedback meaningfully improves the prediction of depression trajectories. This approach illustrates how temporal structure and behavioral history can be integrated into longitudinal risk models to enhance predictive accuracy. This study’s primary contribution is methodological: a novel staged Bayesian workflow that can be adapted to other longitudinal behavioral health settings. This staged Bayesian framework differs from generalized estimating equations (GEE), static risk models, and black box machine learning approaches. Unlike GEE, which provides marginal population-average estimates, our approach models individual-level temporal feedback. Unlike static risk scores, it incorporates lagged predictors and evolving behavioral patterns. And unlike black-box ML models, the staged structure preserves interpretability and allows each behavioral mechanism to be examined independently.

Although the Add Health waves span 1994–2008, the primary purpose of this study is methodological rather than to estimate contemporary prevalence. Add Health remains one of the most comprehensive U.S. longitudinal cohorts with repeated measures of depression, smoking, alcohol use, and sociodemographic factors, and comparable contemporary datasets with this level of behavioral detail are not available. Importantly, empirical research indicates that the associations between adolescent depression and substance use have remained stable across cohorts, even as absolute prevalence has shifted (e.g [6, 18, 19]), . Thus, the temporal feedback mechanisms examined here continue to reflect well-documented behavioral processes, supporting the relevance of the modeling framework despite the historical data.

The feedback effects observed in this study are consistent with theoretical models of reinforcement and comorbidity in behavioral health [20]. Depression may exacerbate substance use through impaired coping and social withdrawal, while substance use may heighten depression through neurobiological and environmental mechanisms. By modeling these relationships longitudinally, we demonstrate how recursive behavioral patterns can be captured within a flexible and interpretable Bayesian framework.

Our staged approach further illustrates how model structure can influence both interpretability and performance. While the fully multivariate model identified shared latent behavioral risk, the feedback model improved both discrimination and calibration [21]. This progression underscores the value of staged modeling in balancing complexity, interpretability, and predictive utility. Incorporating behavioral history—not just current exposures—may support more accurate risk stratification in longitudinal health research.

Although the application here centers on adolescent depression, the framework is generalizable to other domains where prior states influence future outcomes—such as chronic disease progression, relapse prediction, or treatment response. The staged structure also facilitates modular adaptation, allowing researchers to tailor model components to specific behavioral or clinical contexts.

This study has some cautionary notes. Depression and substance use were assessed via self-report, which may introduce misclassification due to recall or reporting bias. The spacing of Add Health waves also limits the resolution of feedback timing. Because the analysis required temporally aligned lagged predictors, we used a complete-case approach. This may introduce selection bias if missingness is informative, and we note this as a limitation of the analytic design. Survey weights and design variables from Add Health were not incorporated into the Bayesian models. Because our aim was methodological rather than population-representative inference, we prioritized model structure over weighted estimation; however, this may affect generalizability, and future work could incorporate pseudo-Bayesian weighting or survey-aware extensions. Ignoring the complex survey design may also introduce bias in marginal prevalence estimates, reinforcing the need for survey‑aware extensions in future work.

Future research could extend this framework to multivariate or continuous outcomes and explore interaction effects between behavioral domains. Methodological extensions might include dynamic priors, time-varying covariates, or marginal structural models to clarify causal pathways. More frequent longitudinal measurement or administrative health data could allow finer-grained modeling of feedback effects and behavioral relapse.

While the current analysis focused on a three-stage Bayesian logistic framework, future work should evaluate the robustness of findings under alternative model specifications, including frequentist generalized mixed models, sensitivity to prior distributions, and simulation-based comparisons with traditional methods such as GEE or fixed-effects regression.

In conclusion, this study highlights the role of behavioral feedback in shaping depression risk across adolescence and early adulthood. The staged Bayesian framework offers a reproducible and interpretable strategy for modeling longitudinal behavioral data. Incorporating lagged mental and behavioral health indicators improves risk prediction and may inform future methodological approaches to adolescent health research.

Conclusions

This study highlights the role of behavioral feedback in shaping depression risk across adolescence and early adulthood. The staged Bayesian framework provides a reproducible and innovative strategy for modeling longitudinal behavioral data, integrating lagged mental and behavioral health indicators to improve risk prediction. By explicitly capturing recursive behavioral patategterns and temporal structure, the framework offers a flexible modeling template for adolescent health research and other domains where prior states influence future outcomes.
