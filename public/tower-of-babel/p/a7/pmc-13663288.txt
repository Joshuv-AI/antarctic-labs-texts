Interpretable machine learning for cardiovascular disease risk prediction in cancer survivors: development and internal validation
Authors: Guoxing Zhang, Xueying Zhang, Chunyi Jia, Huanyu Zhou, Xiaojia Sun, Haifeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) is a major concern among cancer survivors. However, the intersection of cancer and CVD has only recently gained broader attention, and substantial evidence gaps remain. This study aimed to identify risk factors associated with incident CVD in cancer survivors and to develop a machine learning model for CVD risk prediction.MethodsIn this retrospective study, we included 2,500 patients receiving systemic antitumor therapy at Jilin Cancer Hospital; 188 incident CVD events were observed. Variables spanning demographic, clinical, tumor- and treatment-related, and laboratory domains were collected. The dataset was randomly split into a training set (75%) and an internal validation set (25%). Feature selection was performed using LASSO regression. Six prediction models were developed, including five machine learning algorithms (GBM, CoxBoost, XGBoost, SVM, and RSF) and a traditional Cox proportional hazards model. Model performance was evaluated using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves (AUC), calibration analyses, and decision curve analysis. Model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsIn univariate analyses, 48 variables were associated with CVD risk (P < 0.20). LASSO regression identified 19 predictors for model development. Key predictors included elevated systolic blood pressure, specific cancer types, anthracycline use, and a history of hypertension. The XGBoost model demonstrated the best predictive performance, with an average AUC of 0.666, sensitivity of 69.8%, specificity of 60.7%, and overall accuracy of 61.4% in the internal validation set. The model showed good calibration and yielded a positive net benefit across a range of clinical thresholds. SHAP analysis indicated that cancer type, lack of anti-HER2 therapy, elevated systolic blood pressure, advanced T stage, advanced TNM stage, and higher uric acid levels were the most influential predictors.ConclusionThis study developed and internally validated interpretable machine learning models to predict CVD risk among cancer survivors. The models demonstrated good discrimination and calibration and outperformed traditional methods. By enabling individualized risk quantification and providing transparent interpretation of key predictors, this approach offers a practical tool to support personalized surveillance and prevention strategies in cardio-oncology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40959-026-00547-2.

Background

Advances in oncology have significantly improved cancer survival rates [1, 2], shifting clinical attention toward the management of treatment-related complications and long-term health outcomes among cancer survivors [1, 3]. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) has emerged as a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in this population. CVD risk is influenced by cancer type and stage, specific anticancer therapies (including drug class and cumulative dose), pre-existing comorbidities, and the accumulation of traditional CVD risk factors during or after treatment [4–6]. Studies indicate that patients with cancer face increased risks of major cardiovascular events, bleeding, and both cardiac and noncardiac mortality [7–10]. Notably, acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is being diagnosed more frequently in patients with cancer, who now account for approximately 3% of large-scale ACS registries [8]. The reported incidence of treatment-related cardiac ischemia ranges from 0.6% to 5%, depending on the therapeutic exposure [6, 11]. Consequently, integrating CVD risk assessment into cancer care through personalized surveillance, accurate prediction models, and timely preventive strategies is essential to improve long-term outcomes.

In response, risk prediction tools have attracted growing interest for individualized prevention and monitoring. The 2022 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines recommend the Heart Failure Association-International Cardio-Oncology Society (HFA-ICOS) risk assessment tool for patients scheduled to receive potentially cardiotoxic therapies [6, 12]. However, this tool is largely based on expert consensus and provides a qualitative checklist rather than a validated quantitative prediction model. Similarly, the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guidelines propose a risk-stratification framework based on treatment type, age, and CVD risk factors, but do not include an integrated scoring system [13]. Although several attempts have been made to apply existing CVD risk prediction models, such as the Framingham risk score, to cancer survivors, their performance has been suboptimal [14]. A recent systematic review [15] highlighted that most existing predictive models have been developed and validated primarily in breast cancer populations [16–18] or childhood cancer survivors [19–21], with limited generalizability to other oncology cohorts. Moreover, many current tools categorize patients into broad risk groups rather than providing individualized risk estimates, which limits their ability to identify high-risk individuals who may benefit from targeted interventions.

Therefore, a robust, generalizable, and individualized prediction model for CVD risk in cancer survivors remains critically needed. This study aimed to identify independent risk factors associated with CVD and to clarify associations between different anticancer therapeutic regimens and specific cardiotoxicity phenotypes among cancer survivors who received systemic anticancer therapy. Building on these findings, we then developed and validated a machine-learning–based model to predict CVD risk in this population.

Methods

Study population and data source

This retrospective study used data from the electronic medical record system of Jilin Cancer Hospital. Cancer survivors were defined as patients who had survived for more than 3 years after their initial cancer diagnosis and had been followed for at least 2 years. Between December 2012 and July 2023, 5000 cancer survivors were enrolled. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) available and accurate contact information; (2) age 18–80 years; (3) receipt of systemic antitumor therapy; and (4) agreement to follow-up, with follow-up duration exceeding 30 days. Patients with pre-existing CVD or incomplete baseline cardiac assessments were excluded. The final analysis included 2500 participants, of whom 188 (7.5%) developed incident CVD. A flowchart of participant selection is shown in Supplementary Figure 1.

Patients were randomly allocated in a 3:1 ratio, with 75% assigned to the training set and 25% reserved for internal validation. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jilin Province Cancer Hospital (approval number: 202603-009-01) and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. Patient data were anonymized and kept confidential in accordance with ethical guidelines.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the first occurrence of a major adverse cardiovascular event after cancer therapy. CVD events included heart failure, cardiomyopathy, arterial or venous thrombosis, myocarditis, or clinically significant arrhythmia. The date of the first qualifying CVD event was defined as the date of the initial clinical diagnosis. Follow-up ended on the date of the first CVD event, death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period.

Covariates

Predictors were collected across three domains: demographic characteristics, health status, and tumor- and treatment-related factors. These included age, sex (male or female), body mass index (BMI), cancer type, TNM stage, age at diagnosis, treatment details (agent, dose, and number of cycles), baseline cardiovascular status (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF], cardiac troponin I [cTnI], N‑terminal pro‑B‑type natriuretic peptide [NT‑proBNP]), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia), serial cardiac function assessments, and cardiovascular medication use.

Pre-processing of machine learning features

The dataset initially contained 19 features: 9 continuous and 10 categorical. Continuous variables were Winsorized, with values capped at the 1 st and 99th percentiles. Missing values (<5% for key variables) were handled using multiple imputation. All features were subsequently standardized to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 to ensure comparable scaling across variables during model training.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequency (%), and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation (for normally distributed data) or median (interquartile range). Associations between predictors and incident CVD were assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression, with results reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

For predictive modeling, patients were randomly split into training (70%) and internal validation (30%) sets. Feature selection was performed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression in the training set, retaining variables with nonzero coefficients. Multiple predictive algorithms were trained and optimized using 10-fold cross-validation, including a Cox proportional hazards model (as a traditional benchmark), gradient boosting machine (GBM), CoxBoost, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), support vector machine for survival analysis (survival SVM), and random survival forest (RSF). Model performance was evaluated in the internal validation set using the time-dependent area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), Harrell’s C-index, calibration plots with the Brier score, and decision curve analysis (DCA). An ensemble model was constructed by averaging predictions from the top-performing individual models, with weights optimized on a held-out validation subset of the training data.

Model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which quantifies global feature importance and explains individual predictions. To evaluate time-specific predictive performance, separate models were developed for 1‑, 2‑, and 3‑year follow‑up intervals, in addition to an overall model using the full follow-up period. Patients were stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups based on tertiles of predicted risk from the optimal model. Kaplan–Meier curves were plotted, and between-group differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Subgroup analyses were performed by tumor type and treatment regimen. Sensitivity analyses included complete-case analyses and varying the multiple imputation parameters.

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.0, and a two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Risk factors associated with CVD incidence

The results of the univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses are shown in Figure 1. A total of 48 variables met the prescreening threshold (P < 0.20). The most statistically significant factors (P < 0.01) included Cys-C (HR=2.98, 95% CI: 1.51, 5.89), followed by lung cancer (HR=2.03, 95% CI: 1.60, 2.58), hypertension (HR=1.92, 95% CI: 1.42, 2.61), gender (HR=1.67, 95% CI: 1.30, 2.14), chemotherapy (HR=1.82, 95% CI: 1.80, 2.80), platin use (HR=1.77, 95% CI: 1.32, 2.38), use of fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy (HR=1.77, 95% CI: 1.37, 2.29), chest radiotherapy (HR=1.63, 95% CI: 1.25, 2.13), age (HR=1.03, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.05), systolic blood pressure (HR=1.02, 95% CI: 1.02, 1.03), diastolic blood pressure (HR=1.02, 95% CI: 1.01, 1.04), Crea (HR=1.02, 95% CI: 1.01, 1.02), uric acid level (HR=1.00, 95% CI: 1.00, 1.01), and breast cancer (HR=0.56, 95% CI: 0.44, 0.71). Other predictors with P< 0.05 included ALP, eosinophil count, gastric cancer, taxane, eosinophil percent, MPV, Mono, EGFR-TKI, and Anti-HER2. Before model development, the cohort was randomly split into training (n=1750) and validation (n=750) sets. Baseline characteristics were comparable between the training and validation sets, with no significant differences. Model interpretation by SHAP analysis

SHAP analysis was used to interpret predictions from the optimal XGBoost model (Figure 4). The summary plot (Figure 4A), which ranks the top 15 features by mean absolute SHAP value, identified the most influential predictors. Key factors contributing to a higher predicted CVD risk included non-lung cancer type, absence of Anti-HER2 therapy, higher systolic blood pressure, advanced T stage (e.g., T3/T4), advanced TNM stage (e.g., Stage III/IV), and higher uric acid levels. Female gender and receipt of fluoropyrimidine chemotherapy were associated with lower predicted CVD risk. The waterfall plot (Figure 4B) illustrates the individualized prediction for a representative high-risk patient, showing how each feature contributes additively to shift the model output from the base log-odds value to the final predicted risk. Discussion

This study developed and validated a machine learning model to predict CVD risk in cancer survivors after systemic antitumor therapy. This model provides a data-driven tool for individualized risk quantification, complementing existing qualitative guidelines and supporting tailored surveillance strategies.

Our model demonstrated moderate discriminative performance (AUC ≈ 0.666). While not exceptionally high, this level is comparable to existing cardio-oncology tools, many of which are qualitative checklists without formal validation of discriminative performance. The decision curve analysis supported a positive net benefit across clinically relevant threshold probabilities. The moderate AUC likely reflects the complex, multifactorial nature of CVD in cancer survivors, and we view this tool as a decision-support aid to be used alongside clinical judgment. Our findings align with and extend current knowledge regarding CVD risk factors in cancer survivors. Confirmation of established risk factors, including hypertension (elevated systolic and diastolic blood pressure), male sex, older age, and impaired renal function (elevated serum creatinine and cystatin C), reinforces the biological plausibility of the model [13, 22, 23]. Moreover, the model captured distinct cardiotoxic risks associated with specific anticancer therapies, including anthracyclines, fluoropyrimidines, and chest radiotherapy [24–28], consistent with known mechanisms of myocardial injury, coronary vasospasm, and vascular damage, respectively [29–31]. The results align closely with key clinical risk elements emphasized in consensus tools, such as the HFA-ICOS checklist [6, 12]. Notably, the analysis also identified several significant predictors that extend beyond conventional risk assessment frameworks, including anti‑HER2 therapy and taxane-based chemotherapy, elevated inflammatory markers, and cancer-related prognostic indicators such as advanced T stage and specific cancer subtypes. Incorporation of these variables, spanning therapy‑specific CVD hazards [6, 32, 33], host inflammatory response in cancer‑related CVD [34], and tumor biology [34, 35], provides a more nuanced and multidimensional risk profile than currently available qualitative tools and may enable earlier identification of at-risk patients who could be overlooked by guideline-driven checklists alone.

The model directly addresses key methodological and clinical gaps highlighted in recent systematic reviews of CVD prediction tools. As synthesized by Gomes et al. [4], the current landscape of CVD risk prediction models for cancer survivors is characterized by a frequent lack of external validation, which limits generalizability beyond the original development cohorts [14, 16, 17]. This concern is reinforced by methodological studies emphasizing that rigorous external validation is essential before clinical adoption in other populations. Furthermore, many widely used tools, including the consensus-based HFA-ICOS checklist, provide qualitative risk categories (e.g., low, medium, high) rather than individualized quantitative risk estimates [6, 12]. This approach contrasts with the need for precise, personalized risk stratification in clinical decision-making. In addition, many established models were developed primarily using data from traditional chemotherapies and do not fully incorporate CVD risks associated with the broader spectrum of modern anticancer therapies [36]. Similarly, the review by Altena et al. [15] highlighted the urgent need for evidence-based, generalizable tools to predict CVD morbidity in the heterogeneous adult cancer population, moving beyond consensus-driven checklists. This study responds to these calls by developing and validating a model in a mixed-cancer cohort, thereby enhancing generalizability across oncology subtypes. Crucially, the model provides a quantitative, continuous 3-year risk score that enables more granular risk stratification than the binary or categorical outputs of many existing tools. The robust performance in both internal and external validation cohorts supports potential reliability across diverse clinical settings. By integrating a broad set of predictors spanning demographics, traditional CVD risk factors, specific anticancer drug exposures, and novel inflammatory biomarkers, this tool represents a practical and meaningful advance toward personalized risk assessment and preventive care in cardio-oncology.

The application of SHAP provided data-driven insights into the hierarchy of risk contributors, revealing a distinct pattern in which cancer-specific factors outweighed traditional CVD variables in predictive importance in this oncology cohort. Notably, SHAP values are expressed on the log-odds scale, and their magnitude reflects the marginal contribution of each feature to the model's prediction rather than directly quantifying effect size in clinically familiar units. Specifically, the SHAP summary plot identified cancer type as the most influential feature, followed by Anti-HER2 therapy, whereas established factors such as hypertension ranked third. This ordering suggests that overall risk is primarily defined by the biology of the primary malignancy and the specificity of its treatment, with conventional CVD risk factors acting as potent modifiers within that context. The strong contributions of T stage and TNM stage further support that advanced tumor burden is a key determinant of CVD risk, potentially mediated through systemic inflammation, hypercoagulability, and increased metabolic demand [37, 38]. Importantly, the prominent contribution of Anti-HER2 therapy illustrates the model's ability to quantify therapy-specific risk and to distinguish the reversible HER2-signaling blockade mechanism from other cardiotoxic pathways [6, 32, 33]. The inclusion of uric acid as a major contributor suggests a potential role of purine metabolism and oxidative stress in cancer-related CVD. This pathway is increasingly recognized in cardio-oncology literature [39, 40]. Further, a modest SHAP value does not necessarily indicate negligible clinical relevance, particularly when multiple features act additively to shift an individual's predicted risk. The clinical significance of these findings is best evaluated by their biological plausibility, their alignment with established mechanisms of cardiotoxicity, and their potential to inform actionable clinical decisions. This data-driven prioritization of oncological variables over purely cardiological factors represents a nuance that is not captured by current generalized risk stratification frameworks. Importantly, the use of SHAP for model interpretation aligns with a growing body of literature on explainable machine learning in oncology [41–43]. These studies collectively underscore a paradigm shift toward model transparency in clinical oncology, where interpretability is increasingly recognized as essential for translating machine learning innovations into actionable clinical tools.

This study has several strengths. It integrates multimodal clinical, treatment, and biomarker data with interpretable machine learning to develop a dynamic, time-dependent prediction model. Model performance was rigorously validated using independent multicenter data, supporting potential generalizability across diverse clinical settings. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, despite the inclusion of a broad range of predictors, some potentially relevant factors were not available (e.g., physical activity, diet, smoking, socioeconomic status). This omission may result in residual confounding and limit the comprehensiveness of the model [44]. Second, our model was developed and internally validated using data from a single regional cancer center in Northeast China, which may limit its generalizability to other healthcare systems and ethnic groups. Although the training and validation splits were randomly allocated and baseline characteristics were well balanced, we acknowledge that the lack of an independent external cohort to test model performance remains a major constraint. Moreover, the single-center design may have introduced selection bias, potentially overrepresenting patients with more advanced disease or complex treatment histories. Regional variations in treatment practices, supportive care standards, and cardiovascular risk factors may also affect model performance. Future multicenter studies are needed to assess the transportability of our findings. Third, the pathway for clinical translation remains to be defined. Although the model may support risk stratification, prospective interventional studies are needed to determine whether acting on its predictions improves patient outcomes (e.g., through targeted monitoring or preventive therapy) and whether such an approach is cost-effective.

Conclusions

This study developed and validated an interpretable machine-learning model to predict CVD risk in cancer survivors after systemic antitumor therapy. The XGBoost model demonstrated good discrimination and calibration, providing a quantitative, individualized risk estimate that can inform discussions regarding cardiotoxic treatment, patient surveillance, and preventive interventions. By elucidating the contributions of both oncological and traditional CVD risk factors, the model offers a practical tool to advance personalized risk assessment in cardio-oncology and provides a framework for developing more sophisticated, cancer-type-specific models in the future.
