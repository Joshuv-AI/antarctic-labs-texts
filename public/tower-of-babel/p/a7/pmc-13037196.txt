Hirsutine mitigates high glucose-induced cell injury via autophagy activation and NRF2/GPX4-mediated ferroptosis inhibition
Authors: Boran Ni, Yuchang Yang, Bai Yu, Wenyang Tian, Liwei Shi, Qing Ni, Jian Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHirsutine, a potent indole alkaloid extracted from Uncaria rhynchophylla (Miq.) Miq. ex Havil., family Rubiaceae., exhibits antioxidant and cytoprotective activities. However, its potential role in diabetic nephropathy (DN) remains unclear. DN is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease, and podocyte injury is a key event in the progression of DN. MPC5 cells were exposed to 30 mM HG with or without hirsutine (5–20 μM). Hirsutine significantly improved cell viability. Hirsutine significantly improved cell viability, accompanied by a marked reduction in intracellular ROS levels and apoptosis, collectively indicating that hirsutine exerts a pronounced protective effect and effectively enhances podocyte viability under hyperglycemic stress. Autophagy-related proteins LC3 and p62 were examined by Western blotting and immunofluorescence, showing that hirsutine significantly modulated autophagy-related protein expression under hyperglycemic conditions. Apoptosis and ROS formation were evaluated using the Western blotting, and immunofluorescence, indicating that hirsutine attenuated high glucose -induced apoptosis and oxidative stress. LC3 and p62 expression patterns were altered in a manner consistent with autophagy-associated changes. Hirsutine increased the markers of NRF2 pathway activation and also reduced the biochemical signatures consistent with ferroptosis.This study provides new evidence that hirsutine protects against HG-induced cell injury by simultaneously modulating apoptosis, autophagy and ferroptosis, highlighting its potential as a candidate for future DN therapy. However, these results are limited to an in vitro model and lack direct autophagic flux assessment and genetic validation of NRF2 dependency, which warrant further investigation.

Introduction

As a microvascular complication of chronic hyperglycemia in diabetes, diabetic nephropathy (DN) poses a significant clinical challenge and represents the primary cause of proteinuria in end-stage renal disease (ESRD) patients. Studies have shown that 15–40% of individuals with diabetes mellitus (DM) may develop DN during their lifetime [1,2]. With the global prevalence of diabetes expected to reach 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045, the number of DN patients and DN-related ESRD cases is projected to rise substantially, further increasing mortality and socioeconomic burden. These epidemiological trends highlight the urgent need to develop new therapeutic strategies [3]. In the last twenty years, DN, a critical global health problem, has shown substantial increases in both incidence and mortality rates [4]. DN presents not only serious health challenges for patients but also a considerable socio-economic burden. Current clinical management primarily targets modifiable risk factors, including hyperglycemia, hypertension, and proteinuria [5].

There have been clear advances in clinical management. Although this includes strategies like strict glycemic control, RAS inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, and GLP-1 receptor agonists, their primary focus remains on systemic metabolic risk factors, Their protective effects on renal structure, especially podocytes, remain limited, and many patients continue to progress to ESRD [6]. High cost, adverse effects, and variable patient response also restrict their widespread application. Therefore, there is an unmet need for therapeutic agents capable of directly modulating intracellular injury mechanisms in renal cells.

Excessive ROS production and subsequent oxidative stress, major contributors to DN pathogenesis, have been shown to be driven by abnormally high glucose and lipid concentrations [7].

Notably, increased ROS levels induce lipid peroxidation, leading to podocyte damage and the activation of inflammatory pathways, thereby exacerbating renal injury [8]. Consequently, ROS overproduction triggers apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis, and autophagy—key forms of programmed cell death [9]. Autophagy plays an essential role in maintaining podocyte homeostasis, and its suppression under diabetic conditions is associated with podocyte loss [10]. In DN models, suppressed autophagic activity is evidenced by the marked accumulation of the autophagy substrate p62 and reduced expression of LC3-II. Apoptosis, which plays a central role in DN progression, is induced by various stimuli, including lipotoxicity, angiotensin, and advanced glycation end products [11]. In podocytes, these stimuli activate multiple pro-apoptotic signaling pathways. The NRF2/GPX4 signaling pathway, central to cellular antioxidant responses, governs oxidative stress mitigation and ferroptosis regulation [12]. Ferroptosis, an iron-driven cell death process marked by lipid hydroperoxide buildup, has been identified as a key driver of DN progression [13]. Autophagy, ferroptosis, and apoptosis, distinct forms of regulated cell death, may be initiated by shared upstream signals and can either occur concurrently, such as in autophagy-associated apoptosis, or operate in a mutually exclusive manner.

Chinese herbal medicine has shown important therapeutic potential in chronic metabolic diseases, including DN [14]. Hirsutine, a monoterpenoid indole alkaloid isolated from Uncaria rhynchophylla, exhibits multiple pharmacological activities, particularly its antioxidant and Anti-cell death effects [15]. Accumulating evidence suggests that hirsutine can regulate ROS production, maintain mitochondrial function, and modulate cell death pathways; however, its role and underlying mechanism in DN remain largely unexplored [16–18].

Recent studies have reported renoprotective effects of hirsutine in DN-related contexts, including modulation of oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling pathways [19,20]. However, whether hirsutine directly regulates the coordinated interplay among autophagy, apoptosis and ferroptosis in podocytes under hyperglycemic stress remains unclear. In particular, its potential involvement in the NRF2/GPX4 axis and autophagy-associated signaling within podocyte-specific injury models has not been systematically investigated.

In this study, the conditionally immortalized MPC5 podocyte cell line was selected because MPC5 cells are widely used to investigate hyperglycemia-induced podocyte injury and closely recapitulate key physiological characteristics, including slit diaphragm structure and cytoskeletal organization. Using this model, we aimed to evaluate whether hirsutine exerts protective effects against high glucose–induced injury and whether its action involves apoptosis suppression, autophagy regulation, and ferroptosis inhibition through pathways such as PI3K/Akt/mTOR and NRF2/GPX4. This work provides new evidence supporting the potential renoprotective role of hirsutine and lays a mechanistic foundation for future in vivo validation.

Materials and methods

Reagents and chemicals

The indole alkaloid hirsutine (purity ≥98%, Shanghai Standard Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; Cat# ST17300105) was commercially sourced. DMEM (low glucose, Gibco, USA; Cat# 11885-084) and fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA; Cat# 10099-141), along with CCK-8 viability assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat# CA1210), were used. For oxidative stress quantification, standardized kits manufactured by Beyotime Biotechnology were employed, including MDA assay kit (Cat# S0131S), GSH assay kit (Cat# S0053), and SOD activity assay kit (WST-8 method, Cat# S0101S). Antibody panels targeting (i) apoptosis regulators (e.g., Bax/BCL-2), (ii) autophagy markers (LC3-I/II), and (iii) ferroptosis mediators (GPX4/SLC7A11) were purchased from Abcam, UK and Proteintech, China (full antibody list with RRID numbers is given in supplement materials).

Cell cultures and treatment

The conditionally immortalized MPC5 mouse podocyte cell line (Mus musculus, CVCL_A4T8; iCell Bioscience Inc., Shanghai, China; Cat# CL-0485) was used. Cells were maintained in low-glucose DMEM (Gibco, USA; Cat# 11885-084) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, USA; Cat# 10099-141) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat# P1400) under standard culture conditions. For proliferation, cells were incubated at 33 °C in the presence of recombinant interferon-γ. Terminal differentiation was induced by temperature shift to 37 °C following 10–12 days of culture in interferon-γ-free medium. Differentiated podocytes (passages 3–5) were exposed to high glucose conditions (30 mM) in 1% FBS-containing medium for 24 h, with or without hirsutine treatment. The compound was dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich, USA; Cat# D8418) at a final concentration <0.1% and stored at 4 °C prior to use.

Cell viability assay and morphology examination

The cytoprotective potential of hirsutine was evaluated in MPC5 cells through CCK-8 viability assessment. Following seeding in 96-well plates, cells were incubated with hirsutine (5–20 μM concentration range) for 24 h prior to high glucose exposure (24 h). Viability was quantified by measuring 550 nm absorbance after CCK-8 reagent addition, utilizing a Thermo Scientific Multiskan GO plate reader. Morphological evaluation was conducted via Olympus IX71 (Olympus, Japan) inverted microscopy, with documentation of characteristic cellular changes.

Measurement of ROS levels

ROS detection was performed through three sequential steps: (1) Cellular loading with 10 μM DCFH-DA (Beyotime, Beijing, China; Cat# S0033S) for 30 min at 37 °C; (2) Enzymatic dissociation into single-cell suspensions using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat# T1300); (3) Quantitative fluorescence measurement by flow cytometry (FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences, USA). Data analysis was conducted using FlowJo software (v10.0), with gating strategies established using unstained and hydrogen peroxide-treated positive controls.

Measurement of GSH, MDA, and SOD levels

We quantified malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) concentrations using commercial assay kits according to manufacturers’ protocols((Beyotime, Beijing, China; Cat# S0131S for MDA, Cat# S0053 for GSH, Cat# S0101S for SOD). Sample processing and reagent application followed kit-specific guidelines, with final data normalized to total protein content.

ELISA

ELISA kits (Proteintech, Wuhan, China; Cat# KE10007 for IL-6, Cat# KE10008 for IL-10, Cat# KE10002 for TNF-α) were utilized to quantify inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α) in MPC5 cell lysates per manufacturer guidelines. Briefly: standards, controls, and samples (100 μL/well) underwent 90-min incubation at 37 °C. After triple washing, 100 μL of specific antibody was added for 1 h (37 °C). Following another wash, 100 μL enzyme conjugate was incubated in darkness (30 min, 37 °C). Substrate solution (100 μL) was then added for 15 min (37 °C) before reaction termination with 100 μL stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit

JC-1 (Beyotime, Beijing, China; Cat# C2006), a carbocyanine derivative, displays differential fluorescent behavior based on mitochondrial status. While normal MMP promotes its aggregation into red-emitting complexes within mitochondria, MMP dissipation causes cytoplasmic dispersion of green-fluorescent monomers. This characteristic enables clear discrimination between apoptotic cells (predominantly green) and viable cells (dual red/green fluorescence) through fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry.

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was detected using Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (Beyotime, Beijing, China; Cat# C1062S) and analyzed by flow cytometry (FACSCanto II, BD Biosciences, USA).

Transmission electron microscopy

MPC5 cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat# P1126) and 1% osmium tetroxide (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat# G1146) After uranyl acetate staining (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat# S2096), samples were epoxy-embedded (EPON resin), polymerized in gelatin capsules (60 °C, 48 h), and ultrathin-sectioned for TEM (Hitachi, Japan).

Mitochondrial damage scoring

Mitochondrial ultrastructural damage was evaluated using a semi-quantitative scoring system based on established TEM criteria. Individual mitochondria were manually assessed using the ImageJ “Cell Counter” plugin and assigned a damage score from 0 to 3 according to cristae integrity and membrane morphology(0 = normal, 1 = mild injury, 2 = moderate injury, 3 = severe injury).

Immunofluorescence

MPC5 cells on coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat# P1110), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, Beijing, China; Cat# T8200), and blocked. Primary antibodies (4 °C, overnight) and rhodamine-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, UK; Cat# ab150077) were sequentially applied. Fluorescence images were acquired using an Olympus inverted microscope.

Western blotting

Cell lysis was performed on ice (30 min, RIPA + protease inhibitors). After centrifugation (10,000 ×g, 15 min), supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were quantified, and samples denatured in loading buffer (5 min, 100 °C). Equal protein amounts underwent SDS-PAGE separation and PVDF (Millipore, USA; Cat# IPVH00010) membrane transfer. Membranes were blocked (5% skim milk, RT, 2 h), probed with primary antibodies (4 °C, overnight), then incubated with HRP-secondary antibodies (RT, 2 h). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to GAPDH (or β-actin) as the housekeeping loading control. Protein bands were visualized by ECL and quantified via ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times independently. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n ≥ 3) from three independent biological replicates unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses used GraphPad Prism 9.5: one-way ANOVA (Dunnett’s test) or unpaired t-test. Significance thresholds: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Hirsutine alleviated HG-induced cellular damage in MPC5 cells

Consistent with previous reports [21], the optimal concentration for high-glucose (HG) induction in MPC5 cells was identified as 30 mM (Figure 1(a)). Based on this, three concentrations of hirsutine (5, 10, and 20 μM) were selected to evaluate its effects on HG-stimulated MPC5 cells (Figure 1(b)). Cell viability under hirsutine treatment was quantified via CCK-8 assay. The results demonstrated that 5 μM hirsutine partially alleviated HG-induced cytotoxicity, while 10 and 20 μM treatments led to a more pronounced restoration of cell viability after 24 h (Figure 1(c)). Importantly, a 24-h treatment period was selected according to previous MPC5 studies, which commonly employ 24 h HG exposure and drug intervention to assess cytotoxicity, inflammation, and autophagy responses [22]. The protective effect of hirsutine was further evidenced by its ability to alleviate HG-induced morphological abnormalities, including cell shrinkage, loss of podocyte process-like extensions, and cytoplasmic vacuole formation (Figure 1(d)). The inflammatory response to hyperglycemic conditions was evidenced by elevated cytokine production (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) in MPC5 cells (Figure 1(e)). Hirsutine intervention significantly suppressed HG-induced cytokine overexpression (p < 0.05), suggesting its therapeutic potential may involve inhibition of NF-κB or MAPK inflammatory pathways.

Hirsutine pretreatment attenuated HG-induced oxidative stress in MPC5 cells

To further investigate the effects of hirsutine on HG-induced oxidative stress and cellular damage in MPC5 cells, we examined oxidative biomarkers, including MDA, GSH, and SOD, to evaluate both oxidative injury and antioxidant capacity. MDA levels were markedly elevated under hyperglycemic conditions, increasing from 5.41 ± 2.81 μmol/L in the control group to 32.62 ± 4.84 μmol/L in the HG group. Hirsutine treatment significantly reduced MDA levels to 14.91 ± 1.07 μmol/L. Conversely, intracellular GSH content decreased from 65.27 ± 11.44 μmol/L in the control group to 16.33 ± 5.72 μmol/L in HG group and was partially restored following hirsutine administration. Similarly, SOD activity declined markedly under HG stimulation (15.32 ± 4.38 U/mL) and recovered to 55.38 ± 8.06 U/mL after hirsutine treatment (Figure 2(a)).

Flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy detected cellular ROS accumulation. ROS relative fluorescence intensity increased to 2.17 ± 0.05 in the HG group and was reduced to 1.31 ± 0.04 following hirsutine intervention. This result demonstrated that hirsutine markedly attenuated HG-induced ROS accumulation (Figure 2(b,c)). Since oxidative stress indicators such as MDA, GSH, and SOD are closely linked to programmed cell death pathways, and given that autophagy plays a critical role in ROS clearance, we hypothesized that hirsutine’s cytoprotective effects might involve the modulation of autophagy, as well as the suppression of ferroptosis and apoptosis.

To validate this hypothesis, specific pharmacological modulators were employed: ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) as a ferroptosis inhibitor, Z-VAD-FMK as a pan-caspase inhibitor for apoptosis, and rapamycin as an autophagy activator. Notably, all three interventions led to improved cell viability under HG stimulation. These findings indicate hirsutine protects podocytes via coordinated regulation of autophagy, ferroptosis and apoptosis (Figure 2(d–f)).

Hirsutine suppressed HG-induced apoptosis in MPC5 cells

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of apoptosis, with the loss of MMP serving as a key indicator of mitochondrial damage [23]. The integrity of MMP in MPC5 cells post-HG exposure was assessed using the JC-1 staining method. HG treatment led to a marked reduction in MMP, indicative of mitochondrial depolarization, when compared to control cells. Notably, hirsutine treatment significantly attenuated the HG-induced loss of MMP (Figure 3(a)). To further investigate hirsutine’s ability to inhibit apoptosis, Annexin V/PI dual staining was performed. This analysis revealed a marked elevation in the proportions of both early and late apoptotic cells following HG stimulation. However, hirsutine administration effectively mitigated HG-induced apoptosis in a dose- and time-dependent manner, with the most pronounced effect observed at 20 μM after 24 h (Figure 3(b)). We additionally analyzed apoptosis-associated proteins. Hirsutine administration dose-dependently reduced pro-apoptotic BAX and cleaved caspase-3 levels while enhancing anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 expression (Figure 3(c)).

Hirsutine-regulated autophagy-associated signaling in HG-treated MPC5 cells

Autophagy dysfunction is widely recognized as an indicator of podocyte injury and apoptosis both in vitro and in vivo [24]. Given the observed protective effect of hirsutine against apoptosis in MPC5 cells, it remained unclear whether this protection involves the regulation of autophagy-related pathways, particularly those contributing to apoptosis induced by autophagy downregulation. Further investigation was therefore warranted. LC3 and p62 are widely used as molecular markers for monitoring autophagosome formation and autophagic flux [25]. Autophagy inhibition is typically characterized by a reduced LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and elevated p62 accumulation, both of which contribute to podocyte apoptosis. Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the expression patterns of autophagy markers LC3 and p62. Compared with the model group, hirsutine treatment significantly downregulated p62 expression and upregulated LC3-II levels (Figure 4(a)), indicating autophagy-associated alterations in HG-treated MPC5 cells. In addition, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, which is closely associated with impaired autophagy and inflammation, was further examined to explore the potential regulatory mechanism of hirsutine [26]. To elucidate whether the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is involved in hirsutine-induced autophagy, the expression levels of key signaling proteins—including PI3K, AKT, and mTOR—were evaluated. Phosphorylation levels of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR were markedly suppressed by hirsutine treatment in HG-stimulated MPC5 cells, as evidenced by reduced p-PI3K/PI3K, p-AKT/AKT, and p-mTOR/mTOR ratios (Figure 4(b)). These findings suggest that hirsutine may regulate autophagy-related signaling at least in part through inhibition of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway.

As mitochondrial ultrastructural alterations are another key feature of ferroptosis, TEM was employed for morphological assessment. (Figure 5(c)). HG stimulation caused mitochondrial swelling, cristae disruption, and outer-membrane rupture. These abnormalities were clearly reduced after hirsutine treatment. To elucidate the mechanistic basis of ferroptosis, we examined the protein levels of key regulatory factors involved in this cell death pathway. HG exposure led to significant downregulation of NRF2, HO-1, NQO1(NAD(P)H quinone dehydrogenase 1), GPX4, and SLC7A11(Solute carrier family 7 member 11), alongside a marked increase in ACSL4 expression, compared with the NG group (Figure 5(d)). These alterations confirmed the occurrence of ferroptosis under HG conditions. Importantly, hirsutine treatment restored the expression of NRF2/GPX4 pathway components, suggesting that its protective effect may involve the modulation of the NRF2–GPX4 axis to suppress ferroptosis.

Discussion

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) is primarily characterized by progressive podocyte injury, a pivotal early event that drives glomerular filtration barrier disruption. Although DN involves complex whole-kidney pathology, the present study specifically focuses on podocyte-intrinsic mechanisms responsible for cytotoxicity under hyperglycemic conditions [4]. A large body of evidence identifies oxidative stress as the central pathological driver in podocytes, where excessive ROS production promotes inflammation, disturbs redox homeostasis, and triggers multiple regulated cell death pathways. While both apoptosis and ferroptosis involve lipid peroxidation, their upstream regulators and execution mechanisms are distinct [27,28]. Autophagy, a vital cellular mechanism for maintaining homeostasis, alleviates oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby contributing to renal protection [29]. Furthermore, oxidative stress orchestrates crosstalk between apoptosis and ferroptosis, two evolutionarily distinct regulated cell death pathways now acknowledged as central mediators of diabetic renal injury [30]. Apoptosis is caspase-dependent and mitochondria-regulated, whereas ferroptosis is iron-dependent and marked by catastrophic lipid ROS accumulation. Therefore, distinguishing oxidative stress–induced apoptosis from ferroptosis is essential to accurately interpret podocyte injury mechanisms.

Beyond its core bioactivities in inflammation suppression, apoptosis modulation, and oxidative stress attenuation, hirsutine demonstrates pleiotropic regulatory capacities across diverse pathological cascades [18]. Hirsutine significantly reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in MPC5 cells. Although cytokine measurements in podocyte lysates primarily reflect cell-intrinsic inflammatory responses, these findings remain biologically meaningful because podocytes are active participants in the local inflammatory microenvironment during DN progression. It is important to note that, in vivo, macrophages, T cells, and other immune cells also contribute to the overall cytokine milieu; therefore, our data mainly capture the podocyte-specific component of this process while broader immune-cell interactions warrant further investigation.

Although the precise pathogenic mechanisms underlying DN remain unclear, accumulating evidence suggests that oxidative stress is a key driver of disease progression. Elevated ROS levels contribute to renal cell injury, ultimately resulting in renal insufficiency [31]. The antioxidant defense system is incapacitated by ROS overproduction, leading to suppressed activity of pivotal redox regulators, specifically SOD and GSH, along with parallel escalation in malondialdehyde levels, the principal biomarker of lipid peroxidation [32]. Previous studies have reported that hirsutine attenuates oxidative stress induced by various pathological stimuli [18]. In this study, hirsutine markedly reduced intracellular ROS levels and enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities in MPC5 cells. Although the magnitude of ROS reduction did not completely mirror the degree of SOD and GSH restoration, such non-parallel patterns are commonly observed in redox biology. They may reflect differences in the temporal dynamics of ROS scavenging versus enzymatic regulation, as well as the distinct sensitivities of biochemical assays to oxidative changes. These observations highlight the multilayered nature of redox homeostasis in podocytes and suggest that hirsutine may act at several regulatory nodes along the oxidative stress cascade. Further kinetic studies would help delineate how these pathways are coordinated under hyperglycemic conditions. As a highly regulated cellular suicide mechanism, apoptosis performs critical physiological functions including tissue homeostasis maintenance, developmental patterning coordination, and cell population control [33]. Dysregulated apoptosis serves as a key molecular driver in numerous disease states, with well-documented involvement in diabetic kidney disease development [34]. Additionally, TEM revealed ferroptosis-related ultrastructural changes, including mitochondrial condensation and cristae loss under HG stimulation. These morphological alterations were consistent with biochemical evidence of enhanced lipid peroxidation. Because TEM and biochemical assays were performed on separate preparations, the correspondence between structural and molecular indicators reflects overall rather than single-sample correlations. Even so, the convergence of both datasets supports ferroptotic involvement in podocyte injury, and future integrated approaches may help refine these associations. Cellular homeostasis under basal conditions is maintained through the precisely orchestrated interplay of autophagy-mediated cellular renewal and apoptosis-regulated programmed cell death. However, in DN, reduced protective autophagy alongside increased podocyte apoptosis may contribute significantly to renal injury [21].

Furthermore, we examined the role of autophagy in HG-induced injury of MPC5 cells. Autophagy dysfunction is recognized as a contributing factor to podocyte damage, and previous studies have shown that HG conditions suppress autophagic activity [35]. LC3 operates as a central regulator in autophagosome formation, with its proteolytic processing and lipid modification constituting a key regulatory node. The conversion from soluble LC3-I to phosphatidylethanolamine-anchored LC3-II serves as a definitive biochemical marker for monitoring autophagy induction [36]. Additionally, autophagy activation induces the degradation of p62, a substrate of autophagy [37]. Our findings demonstrated that hirsutine treatment induced coordinated changes in autophagy-associated markers, including altered LC3 processing and reduced p62 levels. However, these observations reflect static autophagy-related alterations rather than definitive autophagic flux. The study additionally interrogated the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, an evolutionarily conserved metabolic hub that orchestrates critical cellular decisions ranging from cycle progression and differentiation programming to survival pathway activation [38]. Hirsutine treatment reduced the phosphorylation levels of Akt and mTOR, suggesting that hirsutine may promote autophagy in MPC5 cells by suppressing the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway.

Although p62 reduction can indicate enhanced autophagic flux, it may also reflect ferritinophagy-mediated degradation of ferritin, which increases the labile iron pool and promotes ferroptosis. However, in our study, hirsutine consistently reduced lipid ROS, preserved GPX4 expression, and did not increase NCOA4 levels, suggesting that ferritinophagy was not activated. Instead, hirsutine appears to promote general, cytoprotective autophagy that decreases ROS burden and mitigates ferroptosis. This dual relationship between autophagy and ferroptosis highlights the importance of pathway context [39].

The transcription factor Nrf2 serves as a principal orchestrator of cytoprotective responses, governing both oxidative stress adaptation pathways and regulatory networks that determine cellular vulnerability to ferroptosis [40]. Transcriptional upregulation of Nrf2 establishes a robust defense mechanism against ferroptotic cell death. Current research consensus confirms that Nrf2-mediated GPX4 signaling activation promotes the biosynthesis of radical-scavenging enzymes, creating a dual protective effect against ROS-mediated damage and iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation [41]. In the present study, Experimentally, HG suppressed core ferroptosis suppressors (GPX4/SLC7A11/Nrf2/HO-1/NQO1) while upregulating ACSL4, a key regulator of lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. Hirsutine treatment rectified these pathogenic alterations and protected against HG-induced nephrotoxic effects. However, in the absence of functional validation experiments (such as NRF2 knockdown or pharmacological modulation), these findings should be interpreted as correlative evidence rather than definitive proof of NRF2-dependent ferroptosis inhibition. Future studies incorporating loss-of-function approaches will be required to determine whether NRF2 activation is necessary for the protective effects of hirsutine.

Although previous reports have demonstrated beneficial effects of hirsutine in DN models, our study extends these findings by systematically evaluating apoptosis, autophagy-associated signaling alterations, and ferroptosis-related molecular signatures within the same podocyte-specific hyperglycemic model. Unlike earlier studies that primarily focused on inflammatory or oxidative pathways, the present work integrates multiple regulated cell death-associated mechanisms and highlights a potential link between NRF2/GPX4-related antioxidant responses and ferroptosis-associated biochemical changes [42].

Unlike previous studies primarily focusing on anti-inflammatory or antioxidant properties of hirsutine in renal models, our study systematically evaluated apoptosis, autophagy-associated signaling, and ferroptosis-related biochemical signatures within the same podocyte model, highlighting a coordinated regulatory pattern.

We did not compare hirsutine with SGLT2 or GLP-1 drugs because these agents primarily exert systemic glucose-lowering effects, whereas the present study was specifically designed to investigate direct podocyte-specific cellular mechanisms; such comparisons will require in vivo DN models. Limitations of the current study also include the absence of direct autophagic flux assays and the lack of functional NRF2 validation. While LC3/p62 expression, TEM morphology, and pharmacological modulators provide convergent mechanistic evidence, we acknowledge these gaps. Planned follow-up work will incorporate autophagic flux evaluation, ARE-luciferase and NRF2 genetic manipulation, and in vivo studies assessing renal function and albuminuria to further strengthen the mechanistic and translational significance of hirsutine.

Conclusions

Collectively, our findings indicate that hirsutine alleviates HG-induced podocyte injury, as reflected by quantifiable improvements in cell viability, reductions in ROS and apoptotic markers, and modulation of ferroptosis-related lipid peroxidation alongside enhanced autophagy-associated protein changes. However, these observations are limited to an in-vitro MPC5 model and reflect pathway-associated alterations rather than definitive mechanistic causation. Therefore, in vivo studies assessing renal function, inflammatory responses, and systemic metabolic regulation are required to validate these podocyte-specific effects within the broader context of diabetic nephropathy.

Future directions of the study should prioritize translating the present in-vitro findings into physiologically relevant models by validating the renoprotective effects of hirsutine in well-established in vivo diabetic nephropathy models, with comprehensive assessment of renal function, albuminuria, histopathological changes, and podocyte integrity. Mechanistically, future work should incorporate dynamic autophagic flux assays (e.g. tandem fluorescent LC3 reporters) and genetic or pharmacological manipulation of NRF2 to definitively establish causal links between autophagy activation, NRF2/GPX4 signaling, and ferroptosis suppression. Given the complex crosstalk among apoptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis, systems-level approaches such as transcriptomics and metabolomics may further elucidate upstream regulatory networks modulated by hirsutine under hyperglycemic stress. In addition, comparative studies with current standard-of-care agents, including SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists, are warranted in animal models to determine potential additive or synergistic effects. Finally, pharmacokinetic, safety, and dose-optimization studies are essential to support the translational potential of hirsutine as a novel therapeutic candidate targeting podocyte-specific injury mechanisms in diabetic nephropathy.
