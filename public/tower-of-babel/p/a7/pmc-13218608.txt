The limits of language
Authors: Rafael Loch Batista
Published: 2026

She walks into my clinic armed with humour.

“I think I got the worst deal in the lottery of periods,” she says, half-smiling. “Everyone complains about them, and I didn’t even get one.”

She is in late adolescence, bright, ironic, perfectly composed. She has been referred for primary amenorrhea after several unsuccessful attempts to induce a period with progestins. On examination, she has breast development and female external genitalia. Ultrasonography shows no uterus. Hormone levels and, later, the karyotype will point us to a diagnosis I know well: complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS).

The lab work is the easy part.

I am an endocrinologist, trained to assemble “sex” from pieces: chromosomes, gonads, hormones, receptors. Most days this feels precise, almost mechanical. But then someone like her sits in front of me and the neat diagrams in the textbook are inadequate.

A few weeks earlier, I had been at a museum, standing behind a marble woman draped in fabric. From that angle, her figure appeared familiar — the curve of the hip, the hair falling over one shoulder. Only when I walked around did the sculpture reveal something unexpected: male genitalia. One body, two views; whichever way I started, some part of the truth remained hidden.

I do not mention art or myth in the consultation. But the memory lingers as I try to find words that will hold what my patient’s test results now show.

After a pause, she asks the question that matters most.

“So … who does that make me?”

It is a short sentence, but a huge question. The literature offers options: “46,XY difference of sex development,” “female with complete AIS,” “intersex.” None of them provide the answer she is looking for. She is not requesting a technical label. She wants to know if she can still call herself a woman.

I feel the weight of my answer.

I explain slowly that her chromosomes are XY; that her body made androgens but the receptors inside her cells did not respond to them as expected. But none of that changes the fact that she has lived as a girl, that she is a young woman. A diagnosis of AIS doesn’t erase that. It adds another layer to it.

No sentence can fully settle the question underneath: What kind of story does this condition force her to tell about herself? Her life has been spent as someone’s daughter, a female friend, a classmate — and it will remain so — even as she learns that her body diverges from the diagrams we were both taught.

Practical decisions lie ahead. We discuss estrogen replacement if she undergoes a gonadectomy, and we talk about fertility. Guidelines help with those topics. They are almost silent, however, on how to talk about who she is.

I know that once she leaves my office, she will have to decide when and how to tell this story to others, if at all: partners, future clinicians, friends. She will hear casual remarks about what a woman is expected to be and, at least for a while, she may wonder where her own body fits in that discourse.

Androgen insensitivity syndrome has changed my practice. Not in the medical therapy I offer, but rather in exposing the limits of my language. At the start of my training, I subconsciously regarded conditions like AIS as belonging to the edges of a male–female binary. Over time, I have come to see it is this assumption of a binary that is narrow.

Most people with complete AIS look like and are regarded by others as women. They rarely resemble the mythic “hermaphrodite” of our history books. Instead, they reveal something quieter and more subversive: that chromosomes, gonads, and receptors do not line up neatly for everyone, and authentic lives are built anyway.

When I think back to the sculpture in the museum, I no longer see it as an emblem of being between two sexes. I see it as a reminder that any single point of view hides something important. From one angle, my patient’s body seems entirely ordinary; from another, it unsettles old certainties about how sex and gender are supposed to work. Neither view is wrong. Neither is complete.

As my patient leaves that day, prescription and follow-up arranged, she thanks me in a tone I cannot read. Gratitude? Relief? The beginning of grief? I know that whatever words I used will travel with her, echoing long after the numbers on her lab report are forgotten.

The stubborn receptor in AIS does more than resist androgens. It quietly resists our urge to draw hard lines around bodies and call them simple. I am learning that my task is not to provide a definitive label, but to sit long enough with the complexity and help someone like my patient begin to write her own version of who she is.
