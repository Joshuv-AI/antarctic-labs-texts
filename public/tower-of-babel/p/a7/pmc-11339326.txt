Automatic prediction of obstructive sleep apnea in patients with temporomandibular disorder based on multidata and machine learning
Authors: Yeon-Hee Lee, Seonggwang Jeon, Q.-Schick Auh, Eun-Jae Chung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is closely associated with the development and chronicity of temporomandibular disorder (TMD). Given the intricate pathophysiology of both OSA and TMD, comprehensive diagnostic approaches are crucial. This study aimed to develop an automatic prediction model utilizing multimodal data to diagnose OSA among TMD patients. We collected a range of multimodal data, including clinical characteristics, portable polysomnography, X-ray, and MRI data, from 55 TMD patients who reported sleep problems. This data was then analyzed using advanced machine learning techniques. Three-dimensional VGG16 and logistic regression models were used to identify significant predictors. Approximately 53% (29 out of 55) of TMD patients had OSA. Performance accuracy was evaluated using logistic regression, multilayer perceptron, and area under the curve (AUC) scores. OSA prediction accuracy in TMD patients was 80.00–91.43%. When MRI data were added to the algorithm, the AUC score increased to 1.00, indicating excellent capability. Only the obstructive apnea index was statistically significant in predicting OSA in TMD patients, with a threshold of 4.25 events/h. The learned features of the convolutional neural network were visualized as a heatmap using a gradient-weighted class activation mapping algorithm, revealing that it focuses on differential anatomical parameters depending on the absence or presence of OSA. In OSA-positive cases, the nasopharynx, oropharynx, uvula, larynx, epiglottis, and brain region were recognized, whereas in OSA-negative cases, the tongue, nose, nasal turbinate, and hyoid bone were recognized. Prediction accuracy and heat map analyses support the plausibility and usefulness of this artificial intelligence-based OSA diagnosis and prediction model in TMD patients, providing a deeper understanding of regions distinguishing between OSA and non-OSA.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common chronic heterogeneous disorder characterized by the repetitive collapse and/or intermittent complete obstruction of the upper airway that disrupts normal ventilation during sleep1, causing recurrent apnea and hypopnea, which leads to intermittent hypoxia, sleep fragmentation, and impaired sleep quality. These conditions may exacerbate an individual’s pain perception. The anatomical structures where obstruction occurs are mainly the uvula, soft palate, tonsils, tongue base, and oropharynx2–4, but the exact cause of this collapse is unknown. OSA is estimated to occur in 4% of men and 2% of women and accounts for 95% of all apnea cases5. OSA is the most common form of sleep-related breathing disorder, accounting for up to 85% of cases6,7. Patients experience symptoms such as daytime drowsiness, difficulty concentrating, fatigue, decreased work or academic efficiency, and decreased quality of life8. Considering the enormous impact of OSA on patients’ health and quality of life, a tool is needed to diagnose it precisely and easily.

The immediate effects of OSA include sleep disruption, oxyhemoglobin desaturation, blood pressure and heart rate fluctuations, persistent sympathetic arousal, and cortical arousal9. The long-term effects include systemic hypertension, cardiovascular disease, neurocognitive disorders, and metabolic syndrome10. Increased body mass index (BMI), obesity, inflammatory response, and sleep disturbance are factors that link OSA and chronic pain11. Anatomical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) technology has also demonstrated structural and functional changes in brain regions of patients with OSA12. In particular, the anterior cingulate, hippocampus, and frontal cortical regions, which are brain areas related to the regulation of memory and planning functions, and the anterior cingulate, cerebellar, and brainstem areas, which regulate autonomic outflow, are known to be altered in OSA patients12,13.

Accumulating evidence suggests that OSA is associated with chronic pain disorders, including temporomandibular disorder (TMD)14–16. TMD is a very common disease, affecting up to 25% of the population, with a peak prevalence between the ages of 20 and 40 years and occurring 1.5 to 2 times more often in women than in men17. TMD signs and symptoms vary and include temporomandibular joint (TMJ) noise, TMJ pain, jaw opening limitation, ear pain or fullness, neck pain, headaches, psychological problems, and sleep disturbance. In previous studies, 57–90% of patients with TMD had poor sleep quality based on the well-constructed Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index18,19. In addition, the prevalence of TMD in patients with OSA is 52%20. Recently, OSA in patients with TMD has been considered a persistent and/or aggravating factor of TMD symptoms, and the prediction or diagnosis of OSA in patients has received great attention.

Human sleep has a complex relationship with pain. In sleep research, the gold standard for measuring sleep quantity and quality is polysomnography (PSG). However, the cost of PSG still poses an economic burden to patients, and there are practical difficulties in using PSG easily. Attaching various channels to the body, feeling uncomfortable in a laboratory-based environment, and repeated testing for several days can disturb sleep21. Consequently, PSG is limitedly available in everyday clinical practice7. Therefore, in this study, we used a portable PSG device that allows home sleep testing. TMD and OSA are heterogeneous and ambiguous diseases. Evaluating OSA in patients with TMD requires a comprehensive perspective and multidata-based judgment. We also introduced a new machine learning algorithm to process this complex data and enable automatic OSA prediction or detection. Machine learning has already proven useful in the interpretation of multidata in the medical field22,23.

The hypothesis of this study was that the presence of OSA in patients with TMD is related to the severity of symptoms and that OSA diagnosis in these patients by a useful machine learning algorithm will be possible based on multimodal data. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the onset of OSA in patients with TMD, predict OSA using machine learning based on clinical data together with portable PSG data of patients with TMD, and determine whether the prediction accuracy for the diagnosis of OSA increases when additional MRI data are included. To prove this, we compared the demographics, clinical characteristics, and portable PSG data of TMD patients with and without OSA. In addition, we performed two tasks with machine learning algorithms: Task 1, an algorithm to predict OSA using the demographics, clinical characteristics, and portable PSG parameters of 55 patients with TMD, and Task 2, an algorithm to predict OSA in 16 patients with TMD who underwent MRI. By providing additional lateral MRI images, we derived an algorithm to predict OSA and estimated whether the prediction accuracy of Task 2 was higher than that of Task 1.

Materials and methods

Methodology

Participants

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University Dental Hospital (KH-DT23007). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study population

In this observational study, a total of 55 patients (37 females and 18 males; mean age, 45.87 ± 18.60 years) with TMD who visited the Department of Orofacial Pain and Oral Medicine, Kyung Hee University Dental Hospital, Seoul from January 1, 2020 to July 31, 2023 and agreed to participate in the study were eligible.

Subject selection was based on a standardized clinical examination. All subjects underwent a physical examination according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) Axis I by two TMD specialists24,25 and were aged 18 years or older. The inclusion criteria for patients with TMD were as follows: completed a set of routine TMJ assessments, performed portable PSG, completed sleep reports and constructive questionnaires, and underwent no other treatment for the current episode, except medication25,26. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) systemic inflammatory connective tissue disease; (2) craniofacial malformations (e.g., micro- or retrognathia) or genetic disorders; (3) history of facial tumor or surgery; (4) pregnancy; (5) occult neuralgia in the orofacial area; (6) local facial infection; and (7) psychiatric or psychological disorders2.

For sample size calculation, we used G*Power software (ver. 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) and determined that 46 TMD patients (α level = 0.05, power = 0.95) were suitable for statistical analysis. Aiming for this target, a total of 55 TMD patients were ultimately included. To develop a machine learning-based prediction algorithm, clinical characteristics, portable PSG, X-ray, and MRI data were obtained from 55 TMD patients with self-reported sleep problems.

Data description

The presence of OSA

The presence of OSA was determined based on self-reported OSA during a clinical interview or a history of a clinical diagnosis of OSA27. Briefly, patients were labeled as having OSA if they had a history of OSA diagnosed by a physician, or if OSA was observed by themselves, their sleep partner, or an optional sleep monitor. TMD patients with OSA in the clinical interview were labeled OSA_TMD, and TMD patients without OSA were labeled non-OSA_TMD.

Clinical evaluation of TMD patients

Clinical evaluation procedures included oral examination, interview, panoramic radiography, and a comprehensive questionnaire on the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD) Axis I diagnostic algorithms for TMD. Two experts in TMD and orofacial pain with over seven years of experience analyzed the clinical, imaging, and PSG data. Intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs) of 0.82 and 0.85 for TMD diagnosis and OSA evaluation were estimated in intra-examiner reproducibility measurements. Inter-examiner ICCs were from 0.80 to 0.87 for TMD diagnosis and OSA evaluation. When there was a disagreement, a unified conclusion was reached through several discussions.

Characteristics of pain

Characteristics of pain

Characteristics of pain

Patients reported the duration of symptoms related to the TMJ and adjacent structures based on the number of days since they first experienced TMD-related symptoms. TMD-related pain and/or discomfort was subjectively scored by the patients using a visual analog scale (VAS) ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain imaginable).

(2)Contributing factors or comorbidities for TMD

(2)Contributing factors or comorbidities for TMD

Contributing factors or comorbidities for TMD

We investigated self-reported parafunctional activities, including bruxism, using an Oral Behavior Checklist28. Self-assessment of bruxism, headache, psychological stress, and tinnitus were also reported. Self-reported macrotrauma experience was evaluated using the dichotomous question, “Do you have any macrotrauma experience associated with current TMD?” Each parameter was recorded as a binary answer (yes or no).

(3)Psychological aspects with SCL-90R

(3)Psychological aspects with SCL-90R

Psychological aspects with SCL-90R

Psychological characteristics were assessed using the Symptom Checklist-90-Revised (SCL-90-R), which consists of nine symptom subscales: somatization, obsessive-compulsiveness, interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility, phobic anxiety, paranoid ideation, and psychosis29,30.

(4)BMI

(4)BMI

BMI

Overweight and obesity were assessed based on BMI (in kg/m2) using self-reported weight and height. The main adult BMI categories are underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), and obesity (≥ 25 kg/m2)31,32.

(5)Narrowing of the airway

(5)Narrowing of the airway

Narrowing of the airway

Narrowing of the airway was judged to exist when it was observed at the oropharynx level on panoramic radiography and the Mallampati score was 3 or more (3 or 4). The Mallampati score (range 1–4) is a simple test, and a higher score may be a sensitive predictor of OSA33.

(6)Application of portable PSG

(6)Application of portable PSG

Application of portable PSG

We investigated OSA-related parameters using the Alice OneNight (Philips, Amsterdam, Netherlands) sleep testing device, which is a portable level III PSG device used to test sleep apnea at home. It measures oxygen saturation (saturation of percutaneous oxygen [SpO2]) and pulse rate (from a finger oximeter probe), airflow (pressure-based airflow with detection of snoring through a nasal cannula and thermistor), thoracic and abdominal effort (inductance plethysmography), and body position (Fig. 1). The patient installed this device on their body for several days, and the operator selected representative data from one day without data loss and used them for analysis.Fig. 1Airflow blockage and application of portable PSG in OSA. (7)OSA-related parameters

(7)OSA-related parameters

OSA-related parameters

The respiratory event index (REI) was used to predict OSA. The REI represents the number of apneas and hypopneas detected by the portable monitoring device per hour of elapsed recording time. The central apnea index (CAI) and obstructive apnea index (OAI) were added, and each index was used to calculate the number of events per hour. Apnea was defined as a 90% reduction in airflow for at least 10 s, and hypopnea was defined as a ≥ 30% reduction in airflow for at least 10 s, associated with a ≥ 3% reduction in oxygen saturation34. In general, OSA is defined as an REI ≥ 5/h, whereas a normal REI is < 5/h35. Sleep studies were considered unacceptable for data analysis if none of the following criteria were met: (1) portable monitoring device with elapsed time < 2 h, or (2) poor-quality PSG recording (defined as a substantial portion of the PSG not interpretable for the scoring of sleep and respiratory events).

(8)MR image acquisition

(8)MR image acquisition

MR image acquisition

MRI data from 16 patients were included, and we investigated whether the prediction accuracy of the multimodal data increased with the addition of these images. High-resolution MR images were obtained in the supine position using a 3 T MRI system (Signa™ Genesis, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) with a 6-cm × 8-cm diameter surface coil. MRI examination was dependent on the MR sequences and protocol of Kyung Hee University Medical Center. All scans involved sagittal oblique sections (section thickness, 3 mm or less; field of view, 15 cm; matrix dimensions, 256 × 224), and spin-echo sagittal MR images were obtained on the axial localizer. Lateral MRI images in which the airway was clearly visible were selected for analysis.

(9)OSA prediction with convolutional neural network (CNN) model

(9)OSA prediction with convolutional neural network (CNN) model

OSA prediction with convolutional neural network (CNN) model

To train and measure the performance of the CNN model, proton density MR images were preprocessed with visual geometry group (VGG) 16, a CNN with 16 deep layers. The pre-trained three-dimensional VGG16 models were used for image classification. As one of the top vision model CNN architectures to date, VGG16 can reduce the error rate by half and is twice as deep as the AlexNet 8-layers model36. VGG16 comprises a convolutional layer, three fully connected layers, a 3 × 3 convolutional filter, stride, padding, 2 × 2 max pooling, and a rectified linear unit (ReLU)37. The risk of overfitting is reduced by training the model and augmenting the data. To train all three schemes, a learning rate of 1e-4 in 20 epochs was used. The best model for each scheme was derived by considering the lowest training loss per epoch. MRI was performed in only 16 of 55 patients, and airway narrowing was examined in lateral images of the head and neck area. Statistics and data analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as percentages, means, and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables. The Student’s t-test for non-normally distributed variables was used to compare the OSA_TMD and non-OSA_TMD groups. Differences in the means of continuous variables between the independent groups were examined using the Student’s t-test. To analyze the distribution of discontinuous data, we used χ2 tests for equality of proportions, Fisher’s exact tests, and Bonferroni correction. The Spearman’s correlation test was used to analyze bivariate correlations between categorical and continuous variables. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was expressed as Spearman’s rho (r), with an absolute value closer to 1 indicating a stronger correlation. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 24.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), R Version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and Python Version 3.9.7 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). The performance of the prediction model for OSA at the defined threshold was demonstrated by plotting a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated for each model. To determine which factors are significant predictors for OSA and estimate performance accuracy, a logistic regression algorithm, multilayer perceptron, and the AUC scores were used. AUC values were interpreted as AUC = 0.5 (no discrimination), 0.6 ≥ AUC > 0.5 (poor discrimination), 0.7 ≥ AUC > 0.6 (acceptable discrimination), 0.8 ≥ AUC > 0.7 (excellent discrimination), and AUC > 0.9 (outstanding discrimination)38. Ten-fold cross validation was used to evaluate the machine learning models through a resampling procedure on a limited data sample. Kappa statistics were used to measure the degree of agreement between the two examiners who evaluated and rated the same subjects. A generalized linear model (GLM) was used with OSA as the dependent variable and demographics, clinical characteristics, SCL-90R parameters, and PSG parameters of the patients with TMD as independent variables. In statistics, a GLM is a flexible generalization and a way of unifying various other statistical models, including linear regression, logistic regression, and Poisson regression39. The coefficients (B) of the GLM with Wald 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p values were calculated. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics of TMD patients

There was no statistically significant difference in mean age between the non-OSA_TMD group and the OSA_TMD group (49.01 ± 18.20 vs. 43.07 ± 18.81 years old, p = 0.241). The average age of TMD patients in this study was considerably higher than the average age of TMD patients in previous studies40,41. Since the study was conducted in patients with TMD, the female-to-male ratio of all TMD patients was 2.05:1, with a female predominance. Specifically, the ratios were 1.89:1 (17 females and 9 males) in the non-OSA_TMD group and 2.22:1 (20 females and 49 males) in the OSA_TMD group. There was no significant difference in the female-to-male ratio between the two groups (p = 1.000). Generally, OSA is predominant in men, with a male-to-female ratio of 3:142. However, since we investigated OSA in TMD patients, who are predominantly female, the OSA_TMD group had a higher proportion of females than males. As for symptom duration, the average value of the non-OSA_TMD group was higher than that of OSA_TMD group, but the standard deviation was also large, so there was no statistically significant difference (913.92 ± 1588.14 vs. 369.97 ± 736.01 days, p = 0.103). There was no difference in the rate of chronicity with a symptom duration > 180 days between the non-OSA_TMD and OSA_TMD groups (61.5% vs. 41.4%, p = 0.180). Psychological aspects

Considering the psychological aspects, no significant differences were observed in the mean scores of the nine SCL-90-R subscales—somatization (45.86 ± 8.58 vs. 48.85 ± 9.65), obsessive-compulsiveness (47.03 ± 9.50 vs. 47.50 ± 9.39), interpersonal sensitivity (47.48 ± 10.33 vs. 48.58 ± 11.12), depression (46.52 ± 9.09 vs. 50.12 ± 11.16), anxiety (48.38 ± 8.20 vs. 50.58 ± 11.95), hostility (47.93 ± 9.33 vs. 47.12 ± 7.99), phobic anxiety (44.55 ± 11.87 vs. 48.85 ± 14.06), paranoid ideation (45.38 ± 7.83 vs. 44.96 ± 6.13), and psychosis (45.83 ± 7.13 vs. 48.46 ± 8.80)—between the non-OSA_TMD and OSA_TMD groups (all p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Prediction accuracy when MRI data was added

We performed learning and analysis of the VGG16 model using MRI (lateral images where the airway was clearly observed). Because only 16 out of 55 patients underwent MRI, a machine learning model was trained, and the performance was analyzed at the ratio of train: 9, validation: 3, and test: 4. Because the n number was small, it was impossible to measure the 95% CI value; therefore, only the AUC value and confusion table were obtained. In other words, the number n contributing to the test was small, making it difficult to analyze the performance using the AUC values (there was no data corresponding to false positives and true negatives). Surprisingly, when MR images were added to the demographics, clinical characteristics, and portable PSG data of the 16 patients, the prediction accuracy improved and the AUC increased (AUC = 1.00) (Fig. 8). Grad-CAM of anatomical structures for OSA determination

The anatomical structures used to determine OSA were visualized in lateral MR images using Grad-CAM. In the case of OSA-positive labeling, several structures such as the nasopharynx, oropharynx, uvula, larynx, and epiglottis were recognized, and the occipital region of the brain was also primarily recognized. In the case of OSA-negative labeling, not only the tongue, but also the nose, nasal turbinate, and hyoid bone were identified. Based on the visualization results examined with Grad-CAM, it was confirmed that the machine learning algorithm mainly recognized not only the oropharynx, but also the oral cavity, nasal cavity, airway, and brain regions (Fig. 8). The number of patients who showed Grad-CAM activity in the brain was 11 out of 16 (68.8%).

Discussion

The results of this study support the prediction accuracy and heat map analyzes that artificial intelligence-based OSA diagnosis and prediction using multidata in TMD patients is plausible and beneficial. The developed machine learning algorithm achieved outstanding diagnostic performance for OSA in TMD patients. Additionally, the incorporation of MRI data significantly improved the prediction accuracy for OSA, elevating the AUC score to 1.00. Furthermore, the findings of this study provided a deeper understanding of the most informative regions distinguished between OSA and non-OSA. The hypothesis that with useful machine learning algorithms, the diagnosis of OSA in patients with TMD would be possible based on multimodal data was proven. However, the hypothesis that the presence of OSA in patients with TMD is related to symptom severity was rejected, and TMD symptom severity was not a valid predictor of OSA. Regarding the VAS score, symptom severity of TMD was only correlated with aging but not with the presence of OSA.

In the GLM, only the OAI, a portable PSG parameter, was statistically significant in predicting the presence of OSA in patients with TMD. With the machine learning algorithm, the OAI threshold for predicting OSA was 4.25 events/h in patients with TMD. Using portable PSG parameters, OSA was identified as an REI ≥ 5/h, whereas a normal REI was < 5/h35. The criterion of an REI ≥ 5/h for diagnosing OSA has undoubtedly been used for a long time26,35, but modifications may be necessary based on underlying diseases and/or comorbidities. REI represents the number of apneas and hypopneas detected by the portable monitoring device per hour of elapsed recording time and is the sum of OAI and CAI26. However, in this study, the CAI and REI were not predictors of OSA, and only the OAI was a useful predictor. Generally, apnea is defined as a > 90% reduction in airflow for ≥ 10 s, and hypopnea was defined as a ≥ 30% reduction in airflow for ≥ 10 s, associated with a ≥ 3% reduction in oxygen saturation43. In full PSG conducted in the laboratory, the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), which is the combined number of apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep, is commonly used to diagnosing OSA44. According to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, sleep apnea is categorized as mild (5–15 events/h), moderate (15–30 events/h), or severe (> 30 events/h)35. Many studies define OSA as an AHI of ≥ 5 events/h45,46. In children, OSA is defined by an AHI of ≥ 1 event/h47. Given the complex etiopathophysiologies of OSA, there is a potential need to establish personalized AHI cutoff values for diagnosis. As part of such efforts, additional research based on patient multidata needs to be conducted to determine the cutoff values by sex, age, race, underlying disease, patient customization, disease subtype, and phenotype.

Factors that significantly correlated with the presence of OSA were BMI, airway narrowing, bruxism, REI, and OAI. In other words, in a simple correlation analysis between OSA and another indicator that did not consider the interaction between parameters, there were significant indicators related to OSA, in addition to OAI. To date, risk factors for OSA identified in the literatures include PSG parameters, male sex, aging, hypertension, daytime sleepiness, witnessed apnea, increased BMI, neck circumference, tonsil size, palatal height, overjet, overbite, and decreased cricomental space48–50. According to Li et al., sleep bruxism is observed in 50% of patients with OSA51. The co-occurrence of bruxism and OSA can suggest an underlying link. However, given that the prevalence of bruxism itself reaches 8–10%52, it is problematic to recognize bruxism as a diagnostic indicator for OSA due to its commonality. Clearly, bruxism is considered an aggravating factor for TMD symptoms. Sleep bruxism may be associated with the underlying etiopathogenesis of OSA, and consequently, the etiology of TMD53. Interestingly, obesity is strongly associated with poor sleep quantity and quality; therefore, weight loss may improve sleep problems54,55. Obesity is clearly a key risk factor for OSA development and progression11. Although there is strong evidence that weight loss improves OSA, recent research has suggested that OSA itself may cause weight gain56,57. Narrowing of the airway is a typical symptom of OSA and is mainly caused by the tongue and soft palate58. Ghrelin, a hormone produced by the cells lining the stomach, stimulates appetite and is a counter-regulator of leptin59. Interestingly, in obese patients, ghrelin levels increase at night, and sleep problems may increase ghrelin production, ultimately stimulating appetite, leading to obesity and worsening OSA59. Additionally, genetic factors related to energy homeostasis and the balance between energy use and conservation may influence its role in OSA. Accordingly, there is a need to expand and study machine-learning algorithms to include these factors in patients with TMD.

Using Grad-CAM to visualize the recognized area, it was confirmed that the machine learning algorithm mainly recognized not only the oropharynx but also the oral cavity, nasal cavity, airway, and brain regions. The oropharynx and nasopharynx are the anatomical locations where airway blockage mainly occurs in OSA patients60,61. Airway collapse is mainly caused by an enlarged tongue, soft palate, uvula, inflammation in the nasal cavity, an increase in soft tissue, and a decrease in muscle tonicity62. In the present study, MRI analysis of the upper airways was performed. In recent OSA studies using neck MRI, MRI was suitable for investigating the prognostic role of various anatomical factors in upper airway obstruction4,63–65. Notably, in this study, Grad-CAM activity was also found in the brains of 11 of 16 (68.8%) patients with TMD using MRI data. In other words, in addition to the airway, which is the anatomical location that machine learning algorithms generally consider major in patients with OSA, the brain region was also used to determine OSA. Although the activity was lower than that in the anatomical airway, various patterns of activity were observed in the frontal, temporal, and occipital regions of the brain. With more sensitive anatomical MRI techniques, structural changes or decreased nerve density in brain regions have been observed in patients with OSA, and structural and functional changes have been observed in regions that regulate memory and planning functions (hippocampus and prefrontal cortex) and in regions basic to autonomic regulation (anterior cingulate, cerebellum, and brainstem)12,66–68. However, studies of the hippocampus, parieto-occipital white matter, and cortex have been limited by the poor spatial resolution of this technique69,70. In addition, while human studies on brain structure to date have provided some evidence of damage or atrophy, they do not fully explain the extent. Exploration of the brain using MRI and machine learning in a larger number of patients is needed when investigating OSA in patients with TMD.

Research on OSA diagnosis based on machine learning or its deep learning subsets is rapidly increasing. Levy et al. presented a deep-learning algorithm using single-channel oximetry data71. The prediction accuracy of the machine learning algorithm that classified OSA into mild, moderate, and severe OSA based on the AHI was 88–91%72. When using a machine learning algorithm with an electrocardiogram signal, the accuracy of the algorithm was reported to be 99%73. In a recent systematic review, the main predictors in a logistic regression algorithm were age, waist circumference, Epworth Sleepiness Scale score, and oxygen saturation, with an AUC of 0.98 (0.96–0.99)74. However, OSA-related sleep parameters are diverse and complex, and it is necessary to develop a machine-learning algorithm that comprehensively handles multiple indicators rather than a model based on a single sleep indicator. In machine learning, which deals with multidata, prediction accuracy can increase as the type of data increases22,75. In general, a sufficient amount of data and the quality of the homogenized data determine the derivation of useful results from machine learning76. However, the extent to which increasing the number of data points leads to meaningful results remains unclear. Additionally, clinical studies that target diseases with low prevalence do not have established objective testing methods or require high economic costs to conduct; hence, it is practically impossible to increase the amount of patient data infinitely. Currently, the development of machine learning algorithms that can predict or explain results with small amounts of data has begun77. Undoubtedly, the integration of artificial intelligence into the diagnosis and treatment of OSA is emerging as a pivotal factor in advancing the precision and effectiveness of OSA management, while also enhancing cost efficiency78–81. The future of research will be bright if it is based on these cutting-edge technologies when investigating OSA in patients with TMD.

Clinicians and researchers must consider the significant decline in quality of life in patients with concurrent TMD and OSA to genuinely help patients. In patients with TMD, quality of life is notably compromised by orofacial pain, psychological distress, and physical ailments82. Additionally, OSA exacerbates quality of life deterioration, not only through its intrinsic onset and aggravation mechanisms but also due to associated factors such as obesity, insomnia, and daytime sleepiness83. Delayed treatment of both TMD and OSA can lead to further reductions in quality of life. Typically, TMD treatment includes conservative modalities like cognitive therapy, pharmacotherapy, physical therapy, and injection therapy, with occlusal stabilization splints used to restore orthotopic stability to the TMJ84. For the treatment of OSA, a mandibular advancement device (MAD) is utilized to advance the mandible forward, thereby securing a stable airway and minimizing airway collapse85. However, the use of MADs can lead to tooth movement and bite changes, provoke xerostomia, and induce pain in the teeth, TMJ, and masticatory muscles86. Consequently, the application of MADs is restricted in patients with TMD due to these potential adverse effects. The coexistence of OSA and TMD in a patient presents a complex challenge, necessitating a balance between expert opinions and patient needs. This scenario necessitates a highly specialized and professional approach to ascertain the appropriate types and sequence of interventions.

This study had several limitations. First, because sleep parameters were investigated based on portable PSG, a home sleep test device, additional research based on full PSG is required. It is necessary to set the OSA diagnosis result based on in-laboratory PSG as the ‘correct answer’ and apply the developed machine-learning algorithms. According to Massie et al., home sleep apnea tests do not match the diagnostic accuracy of PSG87. However, portable PSG is a valuable tool for initial screening, appreciated for its accessibility, convenience, and cost-effectiveness. As a home-based test, it allows patients to feel more comfortable than with full PSG, potentially leading to results that better reflect their typical sleep patterns88. Second, some parameters in the predictive model were based on patient self-reported data rather than objective, scientific tests. Despite the superior capability of cephalography to estimate airway diameter, panoramic radiography remains the easiest and most commonly used X-ray imaging technique in dental hospitals. While this approach allowed for a comprehensive collection of data, it also introduced potential biases that could affect the model’s predictive performance. Thus, results from this study should be interpreted with caution and clinically implemented only after more objective data robustly support the findings. Third, although the results showed that increasing the type of data by adding MRI data increases the prediction accuracy, because of practical and economic problems, not all patients with TMD have MRI data; therefore, only some analysis results are available. However, because MRI is performed with the patient lying down, this study has the advantage of simultaneously identifying anatomical structures related to OSA in the patient’s sleeping position, compared to other imaging techniques that are mainly performed while standing. Despite the inherent challenges in perfecting materials and methods in clinical research, efforts to minimize bias must continue. Nevertheless, this study stands out as it integrates clinical characteristics, imaging data, and portable PSG data from TMD patients and utilizes innovative analyses through machine learning.

Conclusion

This study is the first to automate the prediction of OSA in patients with TMD based on multidata using a machine learning algorithm. The prediction accuracy and heat map analyses support that this artificial intelligence-based OSA diagnosis and prediction model for patients with TMD is plausible and useful. Furthermore, we provided a deeper understanding of the most informative regions distinguished between OSA and non-OSA. These results can be helpful not only to TMD specialists and general dentists but also to physicians, otolaryngologists, and researchers interested in this topic in various fields.
