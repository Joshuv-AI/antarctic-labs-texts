Engineering thiamine pyrophosphate metabolism improves consolidated bioprocessing for cellulosic ethanol with inorganic nitrogen in Myceliophthora thermophila
Authors: Jiaxing Chen, Jia Liu, Tao Sun, Rui Pang, Jinyi Yu, Defei Liu, Jingen Li, Chaoguang Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConsolidated bioprocessing (CBP) by cellulolytic fungi offers a direct route for cellulosic ethanol production, but the process still relies heavily on costly complex nitrogen sources. Replacing complex nitrogen with inorganic nitrogen is a potential strategy to reduce the cost of the medium, yet it often leads to impaired fermentation performance, and the metabolic basis for this limitation remains unclear.ResultsReplacement of yeast extract with inorganic nitrogen was evaluated in the engineered thermophilic fungus Myceliophthora thermophila YL913, a CBP strain capable of producing ethanol directly from cellulose. Ammonium sulfate supported higher ethanol production than nitrate, but still resulted in a lower titer than yeast extract. Under ammonium sulfate, transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed altered amino acid metabolism together with reduced carbohydrate metabolic and cellulose catabolic functions, while supplementation with selected amino acids provided only limited improvement. Supplementation with 5 mg/L thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) increased ethanol production under ammonium sulfate from 14.3 g/L to 23 g/L, reaching a level comparable to that obtained with yeast extract. Moreover, α-ketoglutarate was identified as a responsive metabolic node associated with the altered fermentation state. Stepwise strengthening of the endogenous TPP biosynthetic pathway progressively improved ammonium-based ethanol fermentation, and additional overexpression of pyruvate decarboxylase further enhanced ethanol production. In a 5-L fermenter, the final engineered strain produced 49.5 g/L ethanol under ammonium sulfate without exogenous TPP, close to the 51.2 g/L obtained with the TPP-supplemented parental strain.ConclusionsThis study establishes ammonium sulfate as a feasible low-cost alternative to yeast extract for CBP-based cellulosic ethanol production by the engineered M. thermophila strain. These findings provide a practical strategy for developing low-cost fungal CBP processes for cellulosic ethanol production supported by inorganic nitrogen.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13068-026-02786-y.Keywords: Myceliophthora thermophila, Consolidated bioprocessing, Cellulosic ethanol, Nitrogen source, TPP

Introduction

Consolidated bioprocessing (CBP) is considered an attractive route for cellulosic ethanol production because it integrates cellulose hydrolysis, sugar utilization, and ethanol fermentation into a single process, thereby avoiding the separate production and external addition of cellulases [1, 2]. Thermophilic filamentous fungi such as Myceliophthora thermophila have been developed as promising CBP ethanol producers capable of directly converting cellulose into ethanol [3, 4]. Despite this advantage, the process still depends heavily on complex organic nitrogen sources to sustain growth and extracellular enzyme production, and the cost of these nitrogen inputs remains a major obstacle to economical production [5]. This limitation suggests that improving the carbon conversion pathway alone is not sufficient for process optimization, and that the physiological dependence of CBP fungi on medium composition, particularly nitrogen supply, must also be addressed.

Replacing costly organic nitrogen with inorganic nitrogen appears to be a straightforward strategy for reducing medium cost. In practice, however, nitrogen-source substitution reshapes much more than nitrogen availability [6]. In cellulolytic filamentous fungi, nitrogen supply is tightly coupled to extracellular enzyme production, intracellular amino acid homeostasis, and central carbon metabolism [7, 8]. Nitrogen-source substitution therefore changes more than nitrogen availability, which rewrites the balance between secretion and intracellular metabolism. Fungal nutrient utilization is organized through activating and repressing transcriptional programs that discriminate between preferred and nonpreferred nutrients, and this regulatory crosstalk extends to secretory phenotypes [9]. In Trichoderma reesei, nitrogen-source identity dictates cellulase output via mechanisms distinct from growth-rate inhibition, highlighting a specific metabolic constraint on the secretory apparatus [7, 10]. This is particularly important in cellulosic ethanol fermentation, where reduced secretory capacity directly weakens substrate deconstruction and thereby limits carbon flux into glycolysis.

The biochemical consequences of inorganic nitrogen depend on which source is supplied. Nitrate and ammonium are often grouped together as cheap alternatives to complex nitrogen, but they impose very different metabolic demands [6]. Nitrate cannot enter glutamate/glutamine metabolism directly, which must first be reduced to nitrite and then to ammonium [11]. That requirement introduces an additional reductive burden before nitrogen becomes biosynthetically useful. Ammonium assimilation avoids that first cost, yet it proceeds through glutamate and glutamine synthesis, pivotally using α-ketoglutarate as the carbon skeleton and consuming reduced cofactors. In yeast, the NADP-dependent GDH route explicitly consumes ammonium and α-ketoglutarate to produce glutamate, and GS/GOGAT provides an alternative route that becomes relevant under different ammonia regimes [12]. The result is a predictable carbon–nitrogen stoichiometry problem. When ammonium becomes the dominant nitrogen source, carbon must be retained in the TCA-associated network to maintain the α-ketoglutarate and glutamate/glutamine pools needed for nitrogen distribution. Under cellulolytic conditions, that retention can limit the pyruvate fraction available for ethanol formation. In this context, replacing complex nitrogen with inorganic nitrogen is not simply a matter of nitrogen supply, but a problem of carbon allocation at the interface of pyruvate metabolism, the TCA cycle, and ammonium assimilation.

Removal of complex nitrogen exposes a second constraint, as cofactor supply can no longer be buffered by the medium and must instead be sustained endogenously. Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) is especially relevant here because it sits at the central carbon branch points that determine whether pyruvate is fermented or oxidatively retained. Pyruvate decarboxylase, pyruvate dehydrogenase, and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase are all TPP-dependent, so a change in TPP availability can simultaneously alter ethanol formation, acetyl-CoA entry, and TCA progression, illustrating the breadth of TPP control over both fermentative and respiratory carbon allocation [13]. Fungal TPP homeostasis is further complicated by the architecture of its biosynthetic pathway. Enzymes of THI4 and THI5 families occupy the upstream branch and are unusually expensive catalysts. THI4 operates through a suicide mechanism in fungi [14], and THI5 family pyrimidine synthases are also single-turnover enzymes [15]. Sustained de novo TPP production therefore imposes a real proteostatic cost, because pathway flux requires continued protein replacement rather than ordinary catalytic recycling. This burden is overlaid by RNA-level feedback control. In fungi, TPP-responsive riboswitches regulate alternative splicing of THI4 and NMT1/THI5 transcripts [16], which means that increasing intracellular TPP can rescue cofactor limitation while simultaneously suppressing further biosynthetic gene expression.

In this study, we used the engineered cellulolytic strain Myceliophthora thermophila YL913 [4], which is capable of directly producing bioethanol from cellulose via CBP, to examine how replacement of yeast extract with inorganic nitrogen reshapes fermentation performance and intracellular metabolic state. By combining nitrogen source comparison with transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, we identified metabolic features associated with the decreased ethanol production under ammonium-based fermentation, with particular attention to the interface between central carbon metabolism and nitrogen assimilation. We then tested whether this constraint could be relieved through TPP supplementation and by engineering endogenous TPP-dependent carbon allocation. Through this approach, we sought to define the metabolic basis of inorganic nitrogen adaptation in the process of cellulosic ethanol production and to develop a lower-cost nitrogen strategy for fungal CBP.

Materials and methods

Strains and growth conditions

Myceliophthora thermophila YL913 [4] and its mutants were cultured on Vogel’s minimal medium (VMM) with 2% glucose at 35 °C for 10 days to produce mature conidia. Antibiotics were added as needed for transformant selection.

Escherichia coli DH5α was used for plasmid construction and amplification, and cultured at 37 °C in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) or ampicillin (100 μg/mL) for plasmid selection.

Vector construction and strain engineering

The TPP biosynthesis pathway genes THI5 (MYCTH_2308456) and THI20 (MYCTH_2303459) amplified from M. thermophila genomic DNA under control of the strong constitutive promoter of eno (Mycth_2295989), were inserted between the BglII and BamHI sites of pAN52-PtrpC-neo-PMtgpdA [17] to generate the vectors Peno-THI5-neo and Peno-THI20-neo. The genes THI4 (MYCTH_2315910), THI6 (MYCTH_2304176), PHO8 (MYCTH_2304549), and THI80 (MYCTH_2305592) were cloned into pAN52-MtPtef-TtrpC at the BcuI and BamHI sites using the NEB Gibson assembly kit, to generate the corresponding plasmids Ptef-THI4-bar, Ptef-THI6-bar, Ptef-PHO8-bar, Ptef-THI80-bar, respectively.

Polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated transformation of M. thermophila protoplasts was performed using the method described previously [18]. Briefly, 15 μg of linearized plasmid was transformed into M. thermophila protoplasts. Putative transformants were selected with 80 μg/mL G418 or phosphinothricin, followed by sequential identification via PCR with paired primers.

Media for cellulosic ethanol production

In shake-flask experiments, conidia of M. thermophila strains were inoculated into 100 mL fermentation medium in a 250-mL Erlenmeyer flask at a final concentration of 2.5 × 105 conidia/mL. The culture was incubated at 45 °C with shaking at 150 rpm. The medium consisted of 75 g/L Avicel, 0.15 g/L KH2PO4, 0.15 g/L K2HPO4, 0.15 g/L MgSO4⋅7H2O, 0.1 g/L CaCl2⋅2H2O and 1 mL/L trace element solution. Yeast extract (10 g/L), (NH4)2SO4 (3 g/L), or KNO3 (3 g/L) was used as the nitrogen source. For cultures with an inorganic nitrogen source (NH4)2SO4, TPP was supplemented at a final concentration of 5 mg/L.

For fed-batch fermentation of cellulosic ethanol, the cultivations were performed with 3.3 L medium in 5-L reactors. Seed flask inoculations were carried out in 1-L Erlenmeyer flasks containing 300 mL medium with agitation speed of 150 rpm at 45 °C for 28 h. The medium (per liter) contained 30 g glucose, 0.15 g KH2PO4, 0.15 g K2HPO4, 0.1 g MgSO4⋅7H2O, 0.1 g/L CaCl2⋅2H2O, 8 g/L yeast extract, and 1 mL trace element. The initial medium for fermenter comprised (per liter) 60 g Avicel, 0.15 g KH2PO4, 0.15 g K2HPO4, 0.1 g MgSO4⋅7H2O, 0.1 g/L CaCl2⋅2H2O, 9 g/L (NH4)2SO4, and 1 mL trace element of Vogel’s salt. TPP was supplemented at 5 mg/L where indicated. The initial Avicel loading was therefore 198 g per reactor. Fed-batch Avicel supplementation was performed at defined time points: 102 g Avicel was added at 48 h, followed by 100 g Avicel at 72 h, 96 h, and 120 h, respectively. The total Avicel input throughout the fermentation was therefore 600 g per reactor. Fermentation temperature was controlled at 45 °C and agitation was automatically adjusted to maintain dissolved oxygen (DO) at 10%. pH was monitored during fermentation but not actively controlled. Samples were withdrawn at defined time intervals for analysis.

Assay of residual biomass and mycelium dry weight

Quantification of mycelium dry weight and residual Avicel was performed using a previously described method [19]. In brief, the fermentation broth was mixed thoroughly, and two 10-mL aliquots were transferred into separate 15-mL centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 15 min. After removal of the supernatant, the pellet in one tube was dried directly and weighed as W1. The pellet in the other tube was resuspended in 5 mL of glacial acetic acid:nitric acid:H2O (8:1:1, v/v), mixed thoroughly, and incubated in a boiling water bath for 1 h. This treatment was repeated until the mycelia were completely removed. The remaining solids were then collected by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 15 min, dried, and weighed as W2. W2 was defined as the residual cellulose content, and the difference between W1 and W2 was used to calculate mycelial dry weight.

Metabolite analysis

To evaluate intracellular metabolites in M. thermophila grown on Avicel, mycelia were harvested after 3 d of cultivation in ethanol production medium. Mycelia were ground to a powder in a prechilled mortar, and then intracellular metabolites were extracted as previously described [20]. An ABSciex 5600 TripleTOF liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometer (LC‒MS/MS) equipped with a UHPLC LC-30A system using a zic-HILIC column was employed for intracellular metabolite analysis. Solvents were composed of water/acetonitrile/ammonium acetate (A: 100%/0%/10 mM, B: 0%/100%/0 mM). Equal amounts of each sample were used for determination of protein concentration to normalize the weight of mycelia.

The concentration of ethanol in the supernatant of the culture was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with an e2695 instrument (Waters, Manchester, United Kingdom) equipped with a Waters 2414 refractive index detector and an Aminex HPX-87H column (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) at 35 °C. The mobile phase was 5 mM H2SO4 with a constant flow rate of 0.5 mL/min.

Assay of enzyme activities

To determine lignocellulolytic enzyme activities, 1 mL of M. thermophila cultures grown on Avicel was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. To minimize proteolytic degradation, PMSF was added to each sample to a final concentration of 1 mM.

Total secreted protein in supernatants was determined using a Bio-Rad protein assay kit. Exoglucanase activity in the culture supernatant was determined using p-nitrophenyl β-D-cellobioside (PNPC) as the substrate. Briefly, all reagents were preincubated at 45 °C for 5 min. Then, 200 μL of 1 mg/mL PNPC was mixed with 200 μL of culture supernatant and incubated at 45 °C for 10 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 500 μL of 1 M Na2CO3. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 420 nm. One unit (U) of exoglucanase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 μmol of p-nitrophenol (pNP) per minute from PNPC at 45 °C and pH 4.8. The activity of endoglucanase in the culture supernatant was measured using an Azo-CM-Cellulose assay kit (Megazyme, Wicklow, Ireland). The activity of intracellular pyruvate decarboxylase (PDC) was determined using a Pyruvate Decarboxylase Activity Assay Kit (Solarbio).

RNA extraction, sequencing, and data analysis

For transcriptomic analysis, M. thermophila strains were cultivated in ethanol production medium with Avicel as the sole carbon source, and mycelia were harvested after 3 days of cultivation. At the sampling point, cultures were filtered through sterile filter paper and washed with sterile distilled water. The collected mycelia were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until further analysis. Total RNA was extracted as previously described [21], and library construction and sequencing were performed on the Illumina HiSeq™ 2000 platform. Sequencing data were processed and analyzed as previously described [20]. Genes with an RPKM value > 20, fold change > 1.0, and a DESeq-adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed between the tested conditions. The RNA-seq raw data were deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (accession number: GSE327034) at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI).

Results

Ammonium sulfate was the preferred inorganic nitrogen source for ethanol production by CBP

To explore cost-effective alternatives to expensive organic nitrogen, we compared the efficiency of cellulose-to-ethanol fermentation by the previously engineered strain M. thermophila YL913 [4], using various nitrogen sources. Specifically, inorganic salts KNO3 and (NH4)2SO4 were evaluated against yeast extract during growth on Avicel to determine their impact on cellulosic ethanol production in the shake flask. Ethanol accumulation increased throughout cultivation in all three groups, but the trajectories clearly diverged after the initial phase. Strain YL913 produced approximately 6.2 g/L and 14.3 g/L of ethanol using KNO3 and (NH4)2SO4 as the nitrogen source after 7 days of fermentation, respectively, whereas ethanol production reached a maximum of 23 g/L with yeast extract as the nitrogen source on the 5th day (Fig. 1A). Thus, ammonium sulfate supported an endpoint titer that was 1.30-fold higher than that with nitrate, whereas yeast extract remained 60.8% higher than ammonium sulfate. Consistently, the utilization of Avicel was most rapid in the yeast extract group and most slow in the nitrate group (Fig. 1B). Meanwhile, the secreted protein and the cellulolytic activities of endoglucanase and exoglucanase in the supernatant under KNO3 and (NH4)2SO4 conditions were much lower than those with yeast extract (Fig. 1D–F). Interestingly, while (NH4)2SO4 supported substantial biomass accumulation (peaking at 7.7 g/L on day 5), this growth did not translate into proportional ethanol production (Fig. 1C). This decoupling suggests that the primary bottleneck is not growth per se, but rather a disruption in the coordination between nitrogen assimilation and fermentative carbon flux.

To further compare the nitrogen assimilation modes supported by different nitrogen sources, we analyzed the transcriptional profiles of key genes involved in nitrate and ammonium assimilation (Fig. 1G and Table S1). Under the KNO3 condition, the nitrate transporters Nrt-1 and Nrt-2, together with the nitrate reductase NR and nitrite reductase NiR, were markedly upregulated relative to the (NH4)2SO4 and yeast extract conditions. This expression pattern indicates that YL913 actively mobilized the nitrate assimilation pathway when nitrate was supplied as the sole nitrogen source. In this route, nitrate must first be reduced to nitrite and then further reduced to ammonium before entering glutamate/glutamine-centered nitrogen metabolism, and these two reduction steps require substantial reducing power in the form of NADH. In stark contrast, under the ammonium sulfate condition, nitrogen can be incorporated more directly through the GS/GOGAT and GDH branches, thereby bypassing the reductive conversion of nitrate. Consistently, genes associated with direct ammonium assimilation, including glutamine synthetase (GS1 and GS2) and glutamate synthase (GOGAT), remained highly expressed under inorganic nitrogen conditions. Glutamate dehydrogenase GDH2, which catalyzes the conversion of glutamate to α-ketoglutarate and NH4+ with concomitant NADH generation, exhibited its highest expression under the yeast extract condition. These data indicated that KNO3 imposes an additional reductive burden for nitrogen assimilation, which likely contributes to its inferior fermentation performance relative to ammonium sulfate, and ammonium sulfate was clearly the better starting point for further engineering.

Ammonium feeding produced an amino acid imbalance that was only partially relieved by amino acid supplementation

Next, we examined why ammonium sulfate still underperformed yeast extract. Transcriptomic comparison between the ammonium sulfate and yeast extract conditions revealed a pronounced shift in cellular priorities. Under ammonium sulfate, genes involved in amino acid biosynthetic process, nitrogen compound metabolic process, and branched-chain amino acid biosynthesis were enriched among the upregulated categories, whereas genes associated with carbohydrate metabolic process and cellulose catabolic process were downregulated (Fig. 2A and Table S2). This pattern suggested that cells supplied with ammonium sulfate were allocating more carbon and energy to assimilatory and biosynthetic balancing than to cellulose utilization and ethanol formation. The intracellular amino acid profile supported this interpretation. Several amino acids showed a markedly altered abundance profile under ammonium relative to yeast extract, with glutamate, histidine, and valine standing out as the most responsive metabolites (Fig. 2B). Because glutamate and related amino acids are closely tied to ammonium assimilation and transamination, these changes point to a rewiring of the TCA-linked nitrogen assimilation network rather than to an isolated deficiency of one metabolite.

To test whether amino acid insufficiency itself explained the fermentation defect, glutamate, histidine, and valine were supplemented individually or in combination under ammonium conditions. Histidine, valine, and the mixed supplementation increased early ethanol production, raising the day-3 titer from 4.6 g/L in the ammonium control to ~ 6.1–7.3 g/L, whereas glutamate exerted only a minor effect. However, by day 7 all supplemented cultures converged at roughly 13.2–14.1 g/L ethanol, still well below the yeast extract condition. Thus, amino acid supplementation transiently enhanced ethanol accumulation, but did not restore the full fermentation phenotype observed with yeast extract.

TPP supplementation rescues ethanol synthesis and cellulase secretion under inorganic nitrogen

To further elucidate which non-nitrogen component might account for the performance gap between ammonium sulfate and yeast extract, we tested the effect of thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) supplementation. Because TPP-dependent enzymes occupy key branch points in central carbon metabolism, we first evaluated the ethanol response to different TPP concentrations under ammonium sulfate. Under ammonium sulfate, ethanol production responded to TPP in a clear dose-dependent manner. The titer of ethanol remained low at very low TPP levels at day 3, increased sharply at intermediate concentrations, and then declined slightly at higher concentrations. The highest ethanol production was observed at 5 mg/L TPP, which was therefore selected for subsequent experiments.

Under ammonium sulfate, TPP supplementation markedly improved the time-course fermentation profile. Ethanol accumulation was significantly enhanced throughout cultivation, and the final ethanol titer (23 g/L) became comparable to that observed under the yeast extract condition at day 5. In parallel, secreted protein concentration and the activities of endoglucanase and exoglucanase were markedly elevated throughout fermentation and Avicel consumption was substantially accelerated after TPP addition, with a much steeper decline in the remaining substrate over time (Fig. 3C–F). By contrast, fungal biomass exhibited a different time-course pattern. Fungal biomass was higher at day 3 after TPP addition, but became lower than the control at days 5 and 7 (Fig. 3G). Thus, the higher cellulolytic activity after TPP addition could not be explained simply by increased fungal biomass.

To distinguish whether the increase in cellulase activity mainly reflected increased protein secretion or altered enzyme activity per unit secreted protein, we further analyzed the day-5 samples. Normalization of cellulase activity to secreted protein showed that TPP supplementation did not uniformly increase the specific activity of all secreted cellulases (Fig. 3H). Rather, the effect was enzyme-dependent, indicating that the elevated volumetric cellulase activities were not merely a consequence of direct enzyme activation or generalized improvement of enzyme stability. In contrast, secreted protein normalized to fungal biomass was strongly increased after TPP addition (Fig. 3I), demonstrating that TPP enhanced the protein-secretion output of the culture on a per-biomass basis. Together, these data indicate that TPP supplementation restores ammonium-based CBP performance through a broader physiological reconfiguration that improves secretion-coupled cellulose deconstruction and ethanol formation, rather than through a simple increase in biomass or a nonspecific increase in cellulase catalytic efficiency.

TPP-associated metabolic remodeling highlighted α-ketoglutarate as a responsive node during ammonium-based fermentation

Building upon the marked improvement in ammonium-based fermentation after TPP supplementation, we next examined whether this phenotypic shift was accompanied by changes in intracellular central metabolism. Metabolomic analysis showed that TPP supplementation substantially altered the intracellular TCA-associated metabolite profile, with several intermediates exhibiting pronounced decreases (Fig. 4A, B). Among them, α-ketoglutarate showed one of the most prominent changes, decreasing from approximately 37.8 to 4.8 μg/g protein. In parallel, amino acids directly linked to the TCA network, including glutamate, glutamine, and alanine, were also clearly reduced (Fig. 4C), whereas several amino acids not directly connected to TCA intermediates remained relatively stable (Fig. 4D). Consistent with these metabolite changes, transcriptomic GO analysis showed increased expression of genes associated with carbohydrate metabolism and cellulose catabolism, together with decreased expression of TCA cycle-associated genes after TPP supplementation (Fig. 4E and Table S3). Notably, the concurrent decrease in intracellular pyruvate, citrate, and α-ketoglutarate, together with the enhanced ethanol accumulation observed (Fig. 3), was consistent with reduced carbon partitioning into the TCA-associated network and increased carbon flow toward ethanol formation. This trend is also in line with the known cofactor role of TPP in α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase.

Because α-ketoglutarate lies at the interface between the TCA cycle and ammonium assimilation and displayed a particularly strong response to TPP addition, we further tested the fermentation phenotype after exogenous α-ketoglutarate supplementation. In stark contrast to TPP treatment, exogenous α-ketoglutarate attenuated ethanol accumulation throughout fermentation, reducing the final ethanol titer from approximately 23 to 15.3 g/L at day 5 (Fig. 5A), while fungal biomass, secreted protein, and endoglucanase activity increased (Fig. 5B–D). Together, these results identify α-ketoglutarate as a highly responsive metabolic node associated with the redistribution of carbon between ammonium assimilation-related metabolism and ethanol production.

Reinforcement of the TPP biosynthetic pathway progressively improved ammonium-based ethanol fermentation

Given the clear benefit of exogenous TPP supplementation, we next asked whether intracellular TPP supply had become limiting under ammonium-based fermentation and whether this constraint could be relieved by pathway engineering. As shown in Fig. 6A, TPP biosynthesis proceeds through formation of the thiazole and HMP-P branches and then through a series of downstream conversion steps, involving thiazole synthase (THI4), thiamine biosynthesis enzyme (THI5), hydroxymethylpyrimidine kinase (THI20), thiamine-phosphate pyrophosphorylase (THI6), alkaline phosphatase (PHO8), and thiamine pyrophosphokinase (THI80). Of these, THI4 and THI5 are particularly notable because they function as suicide enzymes [15]. This feature suggested that the upstream part of the pathway might represent a vulnerable step under ammonium conditions. Moreover, the transcriptional analysis showed that THI4 and THI5 exhibited the highest transcript levels in the ammonium sulfate group, whereas their expression was clearly lower in the yeast extract and ammonium-plus-TPP groups (Fig. 6B and Table S4). This pattern was consistent with the reported TPP feedback repression pathway in fungi [16]. In contrast, the downstream genes THI20, THI6, PHO8, and THI80 remained at comparatively low expression levels across all tested conditions. Based on this expression pattern, we sequentially reinforced the pathway by overexpressing THI4/THI5 in YL913 to generate JC2653, then THI20/THI6 in JC2653 to generate JC1137, and finally PHO8/THI80 in JC1137 to generate JC3235.

We then compared the fermentation performance of these engineered strains under ammonium sulfate. The time-course profile showed a stepwise improvement in ethanol accumulation along the engineering trajectory (Fig. 6C). Relative to the parental strain YL913, ethanol production was increased in JC2653, further enhanced in JC1137, and reached the highest level in JC3235. By day 7, the ethanol titer increased from approximately 14.3 g/L in YL913 to 16.7 g/L in JC2653, 17.4 g/L in JC1137, and 18.8 g/L in JC3235, corresponding to increases of about 17.6%, 22.5%, and 31.5%, respectively. The divergence among strains became more apparent after day 5, indicating that the benefit of pathway reinforcement accumulated progressively over the course of fermentation.

To further assess whether the improved ethanol phenotype was accompanied by enhanced cellulolytic performance, we compared the parental strain YL913 and the best-performing engineered strain JC3235 during fermentation (Fig. 6D–F). In parallel with its higher ethanol production, JC3235 showed a clear increase in extracellular protein secretion and cellulase activities, particularly at the late stage. At day 7, extracellular protein and endoglucanase activity, and exoglucanase activity were increased by 60.8%, 56.9% and 105%, respectively. Taken together, these results showed that strengthening the endogenous TPP biosynthetic pathway improved ammonium-based cellulose ethanol fermentation, and that the most complete pathway reconstruction gave the strongest overall phenotype.

Enhancing the TPP-dependent ethanol branch further improved ammonium-based cellulose ethanol fermentation

To further improve cellulosic ethanol synthesis, the branch of pyruvate to ethanol was subsequently strengthened. As shown in Fig. 7A, both PDC and PDH are TPP-dependent enzymes positioned at key branch points of central carbon metabolism. Among them, PDC directly converts pyruvate toward ethanol formation, whereas PDH directs carbon flow into the TCA pathway. Based on this relationship, pdc was overexpressed in JC3235 to generate JC3802, and the resulting strain was evaluated under ammonium sulfate without exogenous TPP.

The engineered strain showed a clear increase in PDC activity (Fig. 7B). Compared with strain JC3235, PDC activity in JC3802 was increased by 44.3%. This increase was accompanied by higher ethanol production in shake-flask fermentation (Fig. 7C). Using Avicel as the carbon source, the time-course profiles of JC3235 and JC3802 were similar at the beginning of cultivation, but the difference became progressively larger during fermentation, and JC3802 maintained a consistently higher ethanol titer thereafter. By the end of fermentation, JC3802 produced more ethanol than JC3235 under the same ammonium condition without TPP addition.

This improvement was further validated in 5-L fermenter cultures (Fig. 7D). Under ammonium sulfate without TPP supplementation, the parental strain YL913 produced 37.6 g/L ethanol, corresponding to an ethanol yield of 0.19 g/g consumed cellulose and an overall productivity of 0.196 g/L/h. When 5 mg/L TPP was added, the final ethanol titer of YL913 increased to 51.2 g/L, with a yield of 0.26 g/g consumed cellulose and an overall productivity of 0.268 g/L/h. Under the same ammonium sulfate condition but without exogenous TPP, JC3802 produced 49.5 g/L ethanol, corresponding to a yield of 0.25 g/g consumed cellulose and an overall productivity of 0.258 g/L/h. Residual cellulose at the end of fermentation was approximately 3 g/L, indicating extensive cellulose utilization under the fed-batch process. The corresponding process profiles, including fungal biomass, dissolved oxygen, and pH during the 5-L fermentations, are provided in Fig. S1. Therefore, JC3802 largely reproduced the TPP-rescued fermentation phenotype through endogenous TPP-pathway reinforcement and PDC overexpression, achieving an ethanol titer, yield, and productivity close to those of the TPP-supplemented parental strain without requiring exogenous TPP addition. Taken together, these results show that increasing PDC activity in the TPP-engineered background further improved ethanol production and largely reproduced the effect of external TPP addition under fermenter conditions.

Discussion

A central conclusion of this study is that the limitation imposed by replacing yeast extract with inorganic nitrogen is not simply poorer fungal growth, but a broader redistribution of carbon and cellular resources away from cellulose deconstruction-coupled ethanol production. M. thermophila has already been established as a promising fungal CBP chassis for direct conversion of cellulose to ethanol [2–4, 22]. What the present results add is a physiological dimension to that engineering framework. Under ammonium sulfate, YL913 still accumulated substantial biomass, yet ethanol titer, extracellular protein secretion, and cellulase activities remained clearly below those observed with yeast extract. In a fungal CBP system, this divergence is consequential because secretory performance directly determines how much cellulose is converted into fermentable carbon. The inferior ammonium phenotype is therefore better interpreted as a failure to maintain coordination among nitrogen assimilation, secretory output, and fermentative carbon flux than as a simple defect in growth.

The comparison between nitrate and ammonium helps sharpen this interpretation. Nitrate was clearly the poorer inorganic nitrogen source, which is consistent with its additional reductive cost before nitrogen can be incorporated into metabolism [6, 9]. Ammonium bypasses that first reduction step, but its apparent advantage comes with another demand: efficient assimilation still requires sustained provision of α-ketoglutarate and glutamate-centered nitrogen carriers [11, 23, 24]. The transcriptomic pattern observed here fits that logic well. Under ammonium sulfate, genes associated with amino acid biosynthesis and nitrogen metabolism were elevated, whereas carbohydrate metabolism and cellulose catabolism were weakened, indicating that the cells were committing more metabolic effort to assimilatory balance than to substrate deconstruction and ethanol formation. This is also consistent with current understanding of filamentous fungi, where carbon utilization, nitrogen response, and secretion are tightly intertwined rather than independently regulated [7, 25, 26].

The amino acid supplementation experiments reinforce this point because they argue against a simple deficiency model. If the inferior ammonium phenotype mainly reflected the absence of one or a few metabolites normally contributed by yeast extract, then supplementation would be expected to restore the fermentation state more substantially. Instead, the benefit was transient and incomplete. This is more consistent with a persistent network-level imbalance than with a discrete shortage of individual amino acids. The marked responsiveness of metabolites associated with the α-ketoglutarate/glutamate/glutamine axis further supports that view. What appears to be perturbed under ammonium is not amino acid abundance in a general sense, but the central carbon node that couples nitrogen distribution to biosynthetic demand. Temporary relief of local metabolic pressure can improve the early phase of fermentation, but it does not by itself restore the integrated secretory and fermentative program sustained by complex nitrogen.

The effect of TPP is best understood as a redistribution of central metabolic flux rather than a narrow stimulation of pyruvate decarboxylase. TPP-dependent enzymes occupy several decisive branch points in carbon metabolism, including PDC, PDH, and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase [27, 28]. Altering TPP availability therefore has the potential to change how carbon is apportioned among ethanol production, oxidative metabolism, and TCA-associated biosynthetic support. The phenotypic response observed here is consistent with such a shift. TPP supplementation not only increased ethanol accumulation, but also enhanced cellulose consumption, extracellular protein secretion, and cellulase activity, indicating that its effect extended beyond the ethanol branch itself. In this context, TPP appears to relieve the ammonium-associated tendency to retain carbon in assimilatory buffering and instead favors a physiological state in which carbon is more efficiently directed toward secretion-coupled ethanol production.

Among the metabolites responsive to TPP, α-ketoglutarate emerged as the most informative node. Reduced intracellular α-ketoglutarate accompanied recovery of the ethanol-producing phenotype after TPP supplementation, whereas exogenous α-ketoglutarate shifted the system in the opposite direction, promoting growth and secretion-related traits while attenuating ethanol formation. This reciprocal behavior suggests that α-ketoglutarate functions as a metabolic partition point at which nitrogen assimilation and fermentative carbon release compete. A larger α-ketoglutarate pool is expected to support ammonium incorporation and anabolic maintenance [29, 30], but that benefit comes at the cost of retaining carbon within the TCA-associated network. Conversely, when carbon is less strongly committed to this node, the fermentative state becomes more accessible. Taken together, these data indicate that the ammonium-associated limitation is organized primarily around TCA-centered carbon allocation rather than around the ethanol branch alone.

The genetic analysis of thiamine metabolism further suggests that endogenous TPP supply becomes limiting under ammonium-based fermentation. This conclusion is supported not only by the beneficial effect of exogenous TPP, but also by the expression pattern of thiamine biosynthetic genes and by the progressive improvement obtained through pathway reinforcement. This interpretation is mechanistically plausible in fungi, where thiamine biosynthesis is unusually costly. THI4 functions as a suicide thiazole synthase, and THI5-family enzymes behave as single-turnover catalysts, meaning that continued pathway output requires continued protein replacement rather than conventional catalytic cycling [14–16, 27, 31]. Under ammonium conditions, where central metabolism is already under pressure to sustain nitrogen assimilation, the burden of maintaining de novo TPP biosynthesis may become especially relevant. The improvement achieved by strengthening successive segments of the pathway therefore points to a genuine physiological bottleneck in cofactor supply rather than to a merely correlative transcriptional response.

The additional benefit of pdc overexpression further clarifies the engineering hierarchy revealed by this study. Strengthening the ethanol sink became most effective only after the cofactor constraint had been partially relieved. This is important because amplification of the terminal product-forming pathway often underperforms when the dominant limitation lies upstream in cofactor availability or competing branch demands. Here, the ability of pdc overexpression to further improve ethanol production indicates that the pyruvate-to-ethanol step became more limiting only after TPP supply was reinforced. The near-recapitulation of the exogenous TPP phenotype at fermenter scale supports the same conclusion. Thus, the limitation imposed by inorganic nitrogen appears to be layered rather than singular: cofactor supply defines one boundary, and ethanol-branch pulling defines another. A hierarchical engineering strategy that first relieves cofactor-associated constraints and then strengthens the ethanol-producing branch is therefore more effective than direct amplification of the terminal pathway alone.

To further place this performance in the context of fungal CBP and direct cellulose-to-ethanol systems, we compared representative fungal platforms reported for direct ethanol production from cellulose or cellulosic materials (Table 1). Although several native cellulolytic fungi can directly convert cellulose into ethanol, most reported systems remain limited by low ethanol titers or dependence on complex medium components. In this context, the engineered M. thermophila strain developed here achieved a high ethanol titer under inorganic ammonium sulfate conditions, thereby extending fungal CBP toward a lower-complexity nitrogen regime.

Conclusions

In this study, we established ammonium sulfate as a feasible low-cost alternative to yeast extract for production of cellulosic ethanol by M. thermophila. Under ammonium sulfate, ethanol production and cellulolytic performance were both reduced, whereas TPP supplementation largely restored the fermentation phenotype to the level obtained with yeast extract. Multi-omics analysis and node perturbation further indicated that α-ketoglutarate was closely associated with the metabolic shift under ammonium conditions. On this basis, strengthening endogenous TPP biosynthesis progressively improved ethanol production, extracellular protein secretion, and cellulase activity, and additional overexpression of pyruvate decarboxylase further raised ethanol production from cellulose. In fermenter culture, the engineered strain produced 49.5 g/L ethanol under ammonium sulfate without exogenous TPP, approaching the level of the TPP-supplemented parental strain (51.2 g/L). These results provide a practical route to reduce nitrogen-source cost in fungal cellulose ethanol fermentation and highlight TPP-dependent carbon allocation as an effective engineering target for inorganic-nitrogen-based bioprocesses.
