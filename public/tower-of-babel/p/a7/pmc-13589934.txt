Targeting the nervous system: Mechanistic insights into neuro–tumor communication and therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Tong Wu, Yaguang Zhang, Junhong Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The nervous system has emerged as an active regulator of the tumor ecosystem. Neural inputs from the central, peripheral, and enteric nervous systems influence tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic response through innervation and signal transmission. In addition to regulating tumor cell states, neural signaling reshapes stromal and immune compartments, promoting stemness, invasion, angiogenesis, metabolic adaptation, and immune evasion. Tumors, in turn, actively remodel their neural niche through neurogenesis, axonal remodeling, and tumor–nerve feedforward circuits. The biological effects of neuro–tumor crosstalk are highly context-dependent and are jointly determined by neural origin, receptor distribution, tumor state, and the local microenvironment. Accordingly, neural signaling has become a promising axis for therapeutic intervention. In this review, we summarize key advances in the molecular mechanisms of neuro–tumor communication over the past five years. We also highlight neurotransmitter and neuropeptide receptor axes with pharmacological and translational relevance, related targeted agents, and therapeutic strategies for targeting these pathways, as well as recent progress in neuro-immune combination therapy and clinical studies. Together, these advances provide an updated framework for understanding the role of neural signaling in tumor biology and its therapeutic potential.

Introduction

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a dynamic ecosystem that critically shapes tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance1. Beyond malignant cells, the TME comprises immune, stromal, vascular, and neural components that collectively influence disease evolution. Among these, the nervous system has emerged as an active and previously underappreciated regulator of tumor biology, giving rise to the rapidly expanding field of cancer neuroscience. Increasing evidence indicates that neural inputs are not merely secondary responses to tumor growth, but integral components of the tumor ecosystem that influence multiple stages of cancer development.

Bidirectional communication between tumors and the nervous system has been documented across a broad range of solid malignancies. Both the central nervous system (CNS) and the peripheral nervous system (PNS) participate in neuro–tumor crosstalk. This includes the sympathetic, parasympathetic, sensory, and enteric branches. They influence tumors through direct innervation, release of neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors, neuroendocrine regulation, and indirect effects on immune and stromal cells2, 3, 4.

Through these pathways, neural signaling can shape proliferation, invasion, metastatic dissemination, angiogenesis, stemness, immune evasion, and therapeutic response. Functionally, the CNS mainly acts through systemic neuroendocrine programs5, whereas the PNS exerts more direct local effects within the tumor niche6,7. In gastrointestinal malignancies, the enteric nervous system (ENS) provides an additional regulatory layer by integrating neural, immune, and microbiota-derived signals within the local tissue environment8. Despite the rapid progress of cancer neuroscience, the field remains conceptually fragmented. Although the importance of neural signaling in tumor biology is now widely recognized, related findings are often presented separately across tumor types, neural branches, and signaling molecules. This fragmentation makes it difficult to explain why similar neural inputs may exert distinct biological effects in different tumor ecosystems. Thus, a central challenge is no longer whether nerves regulate tumors, but how the biological consequences of neural signaling are shaped within specific tumor ecosystems.

In this review, we examine neuro–tumor communication from an integrated and context-aware perspective. We first summarize the anatomical and functional interfaces between the nervous system and the TME. We then discuss the major molecular mediators that govern neuro–tumor crosstalk. Next, we examine how tumors remodel their neural niche through neurogenesis, axon guidance, and feedforward innervation circuits. Finally, we highlight the therapeutic implications of these interactions, including receptor-targeted pharmacology, drug repurposing, neuroimmune combination strategies, and emerging translational opportunities. Through this review, we aim to provide an integrated view of how the nervous system shapes tumor biology and therapeutic response.

Anatomical interactions between the nervous system and the tumor microenvironment: from neural innervation to tumor evolution

CNS-mediated tumor regulation: neuroendocrine, neuroimmune, and barrier-associated interfaces

The CNS regulates tumor progression through several interconnected routes, including direct synaptic and neurotransmitter-mediated communication in brain tumors, systemic neuroendocrine and neuroimmune pathways, and specialized CNS interfaces with the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Synaptic and neurotransmitter-mediated CNS regulation of tumor progression

The CNS regulates tumor biology through both direct and indirect mechanisms. In brain tumors, neurons and glial cells directly modulate the tumor microenvironment via neural circuits, neurotransmitters, and neurotrophic factors, thereby promoting tumor proliferation and invasion. In peripheral tumors, CNS influences are more often exerted indirectly through systemic neuroendocrine signaling.

In gliomas, tumor cells form functional synapses with neurons, receiving excitatory inputs such as glutamate through synaptic signaling, which enhances tumor proliferation and invasion9,10. In addition, specific neuronal subpopulations, such as callosal projection neurons in the contralateral hemisphere, can further promote tumor malignancy by driving activity-dependent infiltration and hyperactive brain networks through bidirectional synaptic communication with tumor cells11. This synaptic integration drives malignant progression and establishes a “proliferation–synaptic integration–hyperactive brain” vicious cycle12. Neurotrophic support further strengthens tumor responsiveness to neural input. For example, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) activates CAMKII through TrkB, promotes AMPA receptor trafficking to the glioma cell membrane, and increases the amplitude of glutamate-induced currents, thereby enhancing malignant synaptic strength and glioma proliferation13. Together, these findings indicate that CNS neural activity can directly shape tumor behavior by enabling functionally integrated neuron–tumor communication.

CNS-associated neuroendocrine and neuroimmune regulation of tumor progression

Beyond direct synaptic communication, the CNS also influences tumor progression through neuroendocrine and neuroimmune pathways. Within brain tumors, CNS-associated immune regulation contributes to the formation of an immunosuppressive niche, characterized by macrophage polarization14 and reduced T-cell activity15, which supports tumor growth and immune escape.

A representative systemic route is the HPA axis, through which the CNS modulates peripheral immunity. Intracranial tumors increase plasma corticosterone, activate glucocorticoid-receptor signaling, and upregulate CCR8 in T cells, while also elevating CCL1 and CCL8 levels in the bone marrow. Together, these changes retain T cells in the bone marrow and reduce their infiltration into brain tumors. Blocking the glucocorticoid receptor–CCR8 axis restores T-cell distribution and antitumor immunity16. These findings illustrate how CNS-derived neuroendocrine signals can reshape systemic immune allocation and thereby influence antitumor responses.

CSF as a key interface in CNS-associated tumor regulation

CSF serves as a CNS-specific regulatory interface that shapes tumor dissemination, immune surveillance, and therapeutic accessibility. In brain metastasis, CSF is not merely a transport medium but an active microenvironmental component that supports tumor survival and metastatic progression. In leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) from breast or lung cancer, tumor cells interact with the choroid plexus epithelium, upregulating key proteins such as GAD67 and Bestrophin-1. This results in elevated levels of GABA in the CSF, which helps sustain tumor cell survival and facilitates metastatic spread17.

Beyond its role in tumor dissemination, CSF also functions as a distinct immune niche that can influence antitumor activity and metastatic progression. For example, in lung cancer-associated LM, lipid-associated macrophages are regulated by Midkine (MDK), which is significantly elevated in both CSF and plasma, driving macrophage polarization towards an M2 phenotype and creating an immunosuppressive environment18. Similarly, in breast cancer LM models, CD8+ T cells in the CSF show senescence/exhaustion markers (e.g., downregulation of VLA-4), which reduces their anti-tumor activity and accelerates LM progression19. These findings indicate that CSF and barrier-associated interfaces are not merely anatomical constraints, but active components of CNS-associated tumor regulation.

The mechanisms discussed above indicate that CNS-associated tumor regulation is mediated through several interconnected routes, including neuroendocrine signaling, synaptic communication, and CSF- or barrier-related regulation. In contrast, peripheral neural regulation is distinguished by its closer anatomical proximity to the tumor ecosystem. Local neural projections and neurotransmitter release can act more directly on tumor cells, stromal components, and the immune microenvironment and can therefore influence tumor progression and microenvironmental remodeling.

Peripheral neural programs in the tumor ecosystem

The PNS, comprising motor, sensory, autonomic nerves (sympathetic and parasympathetic) and the ENS, has traditionally been regarded as a passive conduit for signal transmission. However, accumulating evidence indicates that the PNS functionally interacts with the TME through neural projections and the release of neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors. This interaction modulates tumor progression. Neural–TME crosstalk involves neurotransmitter-driven oncogenic signaling, immune microenvironment remodeling, and tumor cell-intrinsic epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming, with these mechanisms potentially exhibiting specificity among different PNS components.

Sensory nerves

Sensory nerves in tumor progression

Sensory neurons detect external and internal stimuli through diverse receptors and convert these cues into electrical or chemical signals, allowing them to regulate pain perception, mechanosensation, and signal transmission within the tumor microenvironment (Fig. 3). Sensory neurons use several receptor types to detect and transmit signals. Transient receptor potential (TRP) channels (TRPV1, TRPM8, and TRPA1) detect thermal and chemical cues. PIEZO1 and PIEZO2 sense extracellular matrix (ECM) tension and pressure. CGRP receptors regulate proliferation and inflammatory signaling. CB1/CB2 and other ion channels control neuronal excitability and immune responses. During bone cancer growth and colonization, upregulation of TRPV1 receptors on sensory neurons promotes persistent and movement-evoked pain, and their antagonism or deletion significantly alleviates these pain behaviors20,21. This suggests that sensory denervation or inhibition of sensory neuron excitation may reduce both bone pain and cancer progression. TRPM8 channels in prostate cancer cells similarly regulate Ca2+ permeability, influencing tumor cell function22,23. Mechanosensory neurons expressing PIEZO1 and PIEZO2 detect ECM tension and pressure changes, and in breast cancer, elevated PIEZO1 expression correlates with malignant phenotypes, promoting epithelial-to-mesenchymal plasticity through Ca2+ influx-mediated activation of pro-migratory signaling pathways24.

Neuropeptides released from sensory neurons also play critical roles in tumor progression. Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) density correlates with tumor incidence in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), suggesting a direct tumor-promoting effect25. In gastric cancer, tumor cells recruit CGRP + sensory neurons via NGF–Trk signaling and facilitate tumor dissemination through the CGRP–RAMP1 axis, while CGRP antagonists markedly suppress tumor growth and extend survival26.

The endocannabinoid/exocannabinoid system (CB1/CB2) induces apoptosis and inhibits invasion in osteosarcoma27. Sensory neurons further modulate host immunity by regulating intestinal barrier integrity and microbiota composition28, while stimulator of interferon genes (STING) controls nociception in sensory neurons via type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling and counteracts tumor-promoting chronic inflammation29.

Sensory neuroimmune interactions in the tumor ecosystem

Sensory neurons promote tumor immune evasion not only through direct neuro–immune interactions but also by secreting factors that regulate immune cell functions, shaping an immunosuppressive microenvironment and further exacerbating immune escape and tumor progression (Fig. 3).

In breast cancer models, TRPV1+ sensory neurons promote metastasis through neuroimmune signaling. They enhance neuro–immune crosstalk in the tumor microenvironment and accelerate tumor cell migration and invasion30. Mechanistically, these neurons secrete substance P and activate the extracellular RNA–TLR7 axis. This signaling cascade directly drives breast cancer metastasis31.

In HNSCC, increased densities of CGRP+ sensory nerves correlate with immunosuppressive TME profiles; conversely, sensory denervation enhances T cell activity and inhibits tumor growth32.

In melanoma, CGRP inhibits T cell receptor (TCR) signaling through RAMP1, leading to CD8+ T cell exhaustion; sensory denervation or pharmacological blockade of CGRP restores T cell function and significantly prolongs survival in mouse models33.

In PDAC, regions with high nerve infiltration form an immunosuppressive microenvironment composed of NLRP3+ macrophages, CAFs, and TGF-β-induced, migration-promoting TGFBI+ Schwann cells at the nerve–tumor interface, and are associated with poor patient prognosis34. Nociceptor neurons interact with CAFs via CGRP and NGF. This interaction downregulates IL-15 expression in CAFs, reduces the infiltration and cytotoxicity of NK cells, and ultimately promotes PDAC progression and cancer-related pain35. Hirth et al.36 identified the key signaling axes CCL21–CCR7 and CXCL10–CXCR3 in sensory neuron–cancer cell interactions through unbiased screening36. This axis drives the chemotaxis and invasion of PDAC cells toward sensory nerves, serving as a crucial mechanism mediating PDAC-related pain and providing a novel potential target for targeted analgesic therapy. These findings suggest that sensory neurons not only contribute to immune evasion in tumors but may also serve as potential therapeutic targets in immunotherapy.

Sympathetic nerves

Adrenergic signaling in tumor progression and microenvironmental remodeling

The autonomic nervous system, composed of the sympathetic nervous system and parasympathetic nervous system, plays a pivotal role in tumor initiation, progression, and immune regulation through multilayered neurotransmitter signaling and cell–cell interactions. The SNS exerts its tumor-promoting effects primarily through the release of norepinephrine (NE) and epinephrine (Epi), which activate adrenergic receptors (ARs) expressed on tumor and stromal cells, triggering downstream oncogenic signaling pathways. In neuroblastoma (NB), isoliquiritigenin or α1B receptor antagonists enhance the antiproliferative and prodifferentiation effects of 13-cis-retinoic acid, effectively suppressing tumor growth and improving control of residual disease by targeting the α1B adrenergic receptor37. Pharmacological α1-AR blockade has been shown to inhibit angiogenesis and EMT, effectively suppressing tumor growth38.

In colorectal cancer (CRC), Epi upregulates β-AR expression, activating the CEBPB/TRIM2 axis to promote epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and stemness39. Epi promotes enhanced glycolysis and tumor growth in CRC through the β2-adrenergic receptor/PKA/CREB1 pathway40. In breast cancer, chronic stress-induced Epi promotes tumor progression and lung metastasis. It activates AKT-mediated phosphorylation and stabilizes USP22. USP22 then stabilizes FOXO1, which enhances adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL)-mediated lipolysis41. In gastric cancer, depressive states can drive β-AR/MACC1-mediated neuroendocrine transdifferentiation, increasing tumor aggressiveness42. In oral cancer, sympathetic nerves activate tumor cell β-AR by releasing NE, promoting TNF-α secretion and worsening pain perception. Meanwhile, the use of the β-blocker propranolol or chemical sympathectomy not only significantly alleviates cancer-induced pain but also inhibits tumor growth43. NE promotes the survival and micro-metastasis of fallopian tube epithelial pre-neoplastic lesions to the ovary via β-AR, enhancing spheroid formation and hypoxia resistance. These changes collectively support the early progression of high-grade serous ovarian cancer44.

Within the TME, NE acts not only on tumor cells but also on stromal components such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to create a pro-metastatic network. In breast cancer, sympathetic innervation contributes to bone remodeling and regulation of the bone vasculature, which increases the ability of tumor cells to colonize the bone niche45. In CRC, NE induces CAFs to secrete NGF. NGF activates ADRA2A and the PI3K/AKT pathway in tumor cells, which promotes tumor growth. NE also increases sympathetic innervation and intratumoral norepinephrine accumulation. Together, these events form a CAF-nerve positive feedback loop that may be targeted with TRK inhibitors46. In hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), NE induces SFRP1 expression, activating a WNT16B/β-catenin positive feedback loop that drives EMT and CSC-associated gene expression, ultimately promoting tumor invasion and migration47.

Adrenergic regulation of tumor immunity

In the TME, immune cells expressing AR, including T cells, MDSCs, and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), respond to signals from NE and Epi, forming potent immunosuppressive circuits48 (Fig. 4).

In CRC, NE maintains myeloid cell maturation via α-AR; disruption of this signaling leads to the accumulation of immature MDSCs and Tregs in the tumor microenvironment, with marked suppression of anti-tumor immunity and accelerated tumor progression49. Furthermore, NE promotes the expansion of MDSCs by activating the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling pathway. This signaling event reinforces the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and facilitates breast cancer metastasis50. In HCC, NE drives MDSC recruitment through the CXCL5/CXCR2/ERK pathway, further inhibiting T-cell proliferation and promoting immune escape51. This series of effects underscores the complex role of NE in regulating immune cell behavior and shaping the immunosuppressive nature of the TME. Catecholamines released by the sympathetic nerves directly drive CD8+ T cells into a state of functional exhaustion via the β1-adrenergic receptor ADRB1. Blocking this signaling pathway not only reverses T cell exhaustion and enhances their anti-tumor function but also synergizes with immune checkpoint blockade to improve therapeutic efficacy52. This discovery establishes a direct link between the sympathetic stress response and T cell exhaustion, providing a novel target for improving tumor immunotherapy.

Targeting NE may help overcome resistance to anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody therapy. In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), NE regulates the secretion of CXCL9 and adenosine by tumor cells. This suppresses the chemotaxis and effector functions of CD8+ T cells and promotes resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy through the WNT7A/β-catenin pathway53. In renal clear cell renal cell carcinoma, NE drives an immunosuppressive microenvironment that restricts the effector function of CAR T cells; inhibition of NE signaling alleviates CD8+ T cell exhaustion and M2 macrophage polarization. This enhances the antitumor efficacy of CAR T cells54.

From a therapeutic perspective, emerging strategies targeting neuroimmune pathways are gaining traction. For instance, NE antagonists and immunosympathectomy exhibit significant antitumor effects. These findings highlight the promise of targeting neuroimmune interactions in cancer therapy, offering new strategies to overcome tumor immune tolerance.

In tumors, nAChRs are frequently overexpressed, activating downstream oncogenic pathways to drive tumor cell proliferation, EMT, and CSC properties55,56. Specifically, in LUAD, ACh engages α5-nAChR to modulate the DNMT1/FHIT axis, promoting migratory and invasive capabilities57. In NSCLC, α7-nAChR is activated by tobacco component-induced signaling, such as nicotine- or NNK-induced signaling, promoting tumor progression58. In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), nicotine activates α7-nAChR and subsequently upregulates the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, promoting tumor malignancy and tumor-initiating capacity7. In breast cancer, α9-nAChR activation promotes angiogenesis59.

In gastric cancer, DCLK1+ tuft cells and nerves release ACh, which induces NGF expression through activation of ACh muscarinic receptor-3 (m3-AChR). This response expands enteric nerve innervation and promotes YAP-dependent tumorigenesis60. In prostate cancer, ACh activates m1 and m3-AChRs to regulate the FAK/YAP pathway, promoting castration-resistant growth of prostate cancer61. In renal cancer, upregulation of SLC18A3 increases ACh uptake, activating the protein kinase A (PKA)/cAMP-response element binding protein (CREB) pathway to promote tumor growth62.

Notably, the tumor-promoting effects of parasympathetic ACh signaling are often cancer type-specific, highly dependent on receptor subtype expression and the TME state. This suggests that the ACh-driven neuro–tumor interaction network is emerging as a promising target for precision therapeutic intervention.

Acetylcholine signaling in tumor immunity

α7nAChR on macrophages plays a key role in mediating cholinergic anti-inflammatory effects through the downregulation of NF-κB signaling. Recent studies further show that cholinergic signaling via α7nAChR regulates the migration of monocyte-derived macrophages during acute inflammation, contributing to the control of inflammation63. Similarly, in PDAC, ACh has been reported to inhibit the ERK signaling pathway, reduce TAM infiltration, and lower serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, which exerts a tumor-suppressive effect64.

Collectively, these mechanisms underscore the pivotal role of parasympathetic neural pathways in regulating immunosuppression, EMT, stemness maintenance, and tumor metastasis, providing a solid theoretical basis for targeting cholinergic signaling in therapeutic interventions for tumors.

Enteric nervous system

ENS-mediated gut–nerve–tumor interactions

The ENS, often referred to as the “second brain”, forms a complex neural network within the submucosal and myenteric plexuses, orchestrating gut motility, secretion, barrier integrity, and immune homeostasis65. Developmental defects of the ENS can disrupt gut structure and function, creating a microenvironment conducive to tumorigenesis. As a conserved regulator of cell fate, aberrant activation of the Notch signaling pathway impairs intestinal homeostasis, induces structural and functional abnormalities of the ENS, and further increases the risk of tumorigenesis68. In contrast, systemic iron overload can trigger oxidative stress and ferroptosis; by suppressing the activity of the Notch signaling pathway, it impedes the proliferation of intestinal stem cells, alters their differentiation trajectory, and consequently perturbs intestinal homeostasis to a greater extent69. In Hirschsprung disease (HSCR), NOTCH1 and its ligand JAG2 function as the core signaling mediators for ZEB2 to promote the proliferation and differentiation of enteric neural precursor cells, which alleviates HSCR-associated enterocolitis70. Clinically, in CRC patients, upregulation of NOTCH4 correlates positively with proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) expression, vascular invasion, and poor differentiation, and negatively with overall survival, indicating its role as a prognostic marker71. Embryonic ENS developmental defects, such as mutations in the transcription factor Sox10, can lead to disrupted ganglion distribution and congenital megacolon-like structures, potentially fostering a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment72. In adulthood, pathological activation of enteric glial cells (EGCs) can release ROS and pro-inflammatory mediators under hyperglycemic stress, damaging the mucosal barrier and increasing tumor susceptibility. Upon carcinogen exposure, such dysfunction markedly elevates gastrointestinal cancer risk73,74. Loss of NDRG4 in the enteric nervous system promotes intestinal adenoma formation and colorectal cancer progression by releasing neuron-derived factors enriched in Nidogen-1 and Fibulin-2, which enhance the migratory capacity of CRC cells75.

The ENS also directly promotes malignant phenotypes by releasing neurotransmitters that act on tumor cell surface receptors. These neurotransmitters activate downstream signaling pathways and enhance proliferation. Specifically, enteric serotonergic neurons produce 5-HT that activates HTR1B/1D-mediated Wnt/β-catenin signaling to sustain colorectal CSC self-renewal, a process further enhanced by CRC-enriched microbiota-derived isovalerate and ultimately promoting colorectal tumorigenesis76. In CRC, the substance P (SP)–neurokinin 1 receptor (NK1R) signaling axis promotes tumor cell growth and survival by inducing ER stress, triggering calcium release and thereby representing a key pro-tumorigenic mechanism77.

Beyond direct tumor–neuron interactions, the ENS collaborates with CAFs and the gut microbiota to remodel the TME. In CRC, NGF secreted by CAFs under the influence of NE enhances intratumoral sympathetic innervation, forming a CAF–nerve feedforward loop that promotes tumor cell proliferation and cancer progression46. In CRC, Netrin-1 is highly expressed in tumor cells and is also upregulated in CAFs. It promotes CAF–tumor cell signaling and enhances cancer stemness, changes that drive tumor progression and plasticity78. Enteric glial cells indirectly shape the gut microbiota by modulating intestinal stem cell behavior and local immune responses, thereby cooperatively regulating the tumor microenvironment79.

Pro-tumor immune–neural circuits in the ENS

Aberrant activation of the ENS can initiate chronic intestinal inflammation via the Eph/ephrin signaling pathway, triggering the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α. This inflammatory milieu fosters the accumulation of genetic mutations and induces EMT, creating a pro-inflammatory and tumor-promoting microenvironment80.

Moreover, the ENS can shape gut microbiota composition via the gut–brain axis, where microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and tryptophan derivatives modulate ENS neuronal development and function, establishing a “microbiota–neural–tumor” positive feedback loop81,82. For instance, indole-3-lactic acid derived from Lactobacillus plantarum can modulate the proportion of CD8+ T cells, fine-tuning immune responses and inhibiting colorectal tumorigenesis83.

In addition, the gut microbiota can indirectly influence tumor progression by regulating ENS activity. Metabolites from Lactobacillus plantarum OC01 modulate CRC cell secretions (TGF-β) to inhibit tumor-associated M2 macrophage polarization, promote a pro-inflammatory macrophage phenotype, and consequently suppress CRC proliferation and migration84. Schwann cells, a glial cell type within the PNS, can be activated by ENS-derived signals in the TME. Once activated, Schwann cells enhance colorectal cancer cell migration and invasion through the NF-κB/IL-8 signaling axis85 (Fig. 6). These findings indicate that tumor-associated neural remodeling is not solely dependent on neuronal axonal guidance but also requires the cooperative involvement of non-neuronal components such as Schwann cells and microbiota.

Molecular mediators of neuro–tumor communication: neurotransmitters, neuropeptides, neurexins and neuroligins

Beyond classical neurotransmitters covered in CNS and PNS projections, additional molecular mediators such as glutamate, GABA, dopamine, neuropeptides, and synaptic adhesion molecules including neurexins and neuroligins further regulate tumor cell behavior and the TME.

Neurotransmitters

Neurotransmitters are better understood as dynamic signaling mediators within broader neural communication networks, in which receptor-dependent and context-specific signaling shapes tumor progression, immune regulation, and therapeutic response.

Neurotransmitters.

In GBM, glutamate not only maintains redox homeostasis through glutathione (GSH) synthesis, but also induces CypB secretion and activates p-AKT signaling. This promotes glycolysis and suppresses mitochondrial function, helping tumor cells adapt to glutamine deprivation89. Glutamate also promotes immune evasion in gliomas. The acidic microenvironment impairs immune cell function, creating a pro-tumor immune niche91. Glutamate released from nerve injury can activate oligodendrocytes to secrete IL-1β, inducing CCL5 expression, accelerating tumor progression, and driving optic glioma formation92.

Glutamate also plays key roles in other solid tumors. In HCC, approximately 30% of glutamine is metabolized to glutamate in the cytosol, supporting ATP production via the TCA cycle to sustain tumor growth93. Liver fibrosis is a critical process in the development of hepatocellular carcinoma. Glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) catalyzes the conversion of glutamate to α-ketoglutarate, enhancing mitochondrial energy metabolism to promote fibrosis, whereas GDH inhibition reduces ATP production and slows fibrosis and tumor progression94. SLC7A11-mediated glutamate export acidifies the extracellular environment, which can also exacerbate the occurrence of seizures90. In PDAC, sensory nerve endings form pseudo-synapses with cancer cells. GRIN2D-type NMDAR on tumor cells senses neuron-derived glutamate and activates calcium signaling. This enhances tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis. These pseudo-synapses also convert some co-cultured cancer cells into calcium-responsive cells, creating a feedforward loop that further promotes tumor progression95.

Tumor cells export glutamate via the cystine-glutamate antiporter (xCT) to take up cystine for GSH synthesis. In breast cancer, xCT upregulation similarly increases extracellular glutamate, activating mGluR3 to enhance Rab27-dependent extracellular vesicle (EV) release, remodeling endosomal trafficking and generating a pro-invasive microenvironment96. In melanoma, xCT-mediated PD-L1+ exosomes drive M2 macrophage polarization, reducing the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint blockade therapy97.

Glutamate further regulates immune cell phenotypes and cytokine release, reshaping the TME to promote immune evasion. In PDAC, CAF-derived Netrin G1 (NetG1) induces immunosuppression through regulation of glutamate metabolism, and NetG1 inhibition restores anti-tumor immunity98. In HCC, glutamate promotes TAM infiltration by upregulating CCL2 expression in hepatoma cells via the NMDAR2B receptor. This reshapes the immune microenvironment and facilitates tumor progression99. In hepatocellular sarcoma and fibrosarcoma tumors, activation of metabotropic NMDAR also induces an immunosuppressive phenotype, inhibiting T cell and NK cell activity; NMDA antagonists combined with anti-PD-1 therapy can enhance anti-tumor immunity100. In melanoma, GRM4 signaling modulates anti-tumor immunity, and its inhibition enhances NK and T cell responses by promoting their activation, proliferation, and functional differentiation and strengthening anti-tumor immune efficacy101.

Collectively, these findings indicate that glutamate and its receptors influence the TME across multiple tumor types. Pharmacological inhibition of glutamate receptors has been shown to suppress tumor growth and metastasis, highlighting glutamate receptors as potential therapeutic targets.

γ-Aminobutyric acid

γ-Aminobutyric Acid (GABA) is primarily synthesized from glutamate via glutamate decarboxylase (GAD), a reaction that requires the coenzyme pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), and functions as the principal inhibitory neurotransmitter in both the CNS and peripheral tissues133. GABA exerts its effects through three classes of receptors: GABAA, GABAB and GABAC, which mediate either rapid or prolonged inhibitory signaling134. Emerging evidence indicates that GABA signaling exhibits multifaceted pro-tumorigenic roles, including intracellular signaling regulation, invasion and metastasis, immune modulation, and metabolic adaptation (Table 1).

Tumor cells can upregulate GAD1 or GPT2 to reprogram glutamine metabolism, resulting in enhanced autocrine or paracrine GABA production. Activation of GABA receptors subsequently activates the Ca2+/PKC/CREB signaling pathway. This promotes tumor growth and metastasis. The metabolic and signaling axis has been closely associated with invasion and poor prognosis in CRC102 and breast cancer103. In NSCLC brain metastases, GABA enhances brain colonization adaptability through the FOXA2/ABAT/GABA/NF-κB pathway and interactions with astrocytes104.

GABA signaling also profoundly influences the tumor immune microenvironment. In renal cancer, B cells synthesize and secrete GABA, which drives monocytes to differentiate into IL-10+ anti-inflammatory macrophages and suppresses CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity. This creates an immunosuppressive microenvironment105. In parallel, tumor-intrinsic GABA–β-catenin signaling reduces CD8+ T-cell infiltration and weakens responses to immune checkpoint blockade106.

Although some in vitro studies have reported that exogenous GABA inhibits CRC cell proliferation, in vivo evidence predominantly supports its pro-tumorigenic role, which likely reflects differences in receptor subtype expression and tissue microenvironment135. Overall, GABA coordinates a pro-tumorigenic network that links intracellular metabolism, invasion, and immune regulation. Targeting key nodes such as GAD1 or specific GABA receptor subtypes may enhance immunotherapy efficacy and inhibit tumor progression across multiple cancer types136.

5-Hydroxytryptamine

In the CNS, 5-HT is primarily synthesized by dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN) neurons through the rate-limiting enzyme tryptophan hydroxylase 2 (TPH2), regulating mood, cognition, and neuronal activity137. In the periphery, enterochromaffin (EC) cells in the gastrointestinal mucosa are the principal source of 5-HT production138. Beyond its role as a neurotransmitter in CNS function, 5-HT participates in tumor initiation, progression, and immune regulation139 through multiple mechanisms (Table 1).

In tumors, 5-HT binds to its GPCRs and activates multiple oncogenic signaling pathways. For instance, in CRC, the serotonin transporter (SERT) mediates cellular uptake of 5-HT, triggering the RHOA/ROCK/YAP signaling axis to drive CRC initiation and invasion. Inhibition of SERT or blockade of YAP significantly suppresses tumor growth and metastasis107. Also in CRC, upregulation of the HTR2B receptor enhances ERK pathway activation, leading to increased tumor-cell proliferation and migration and faster tumor progression108. Elevated HTR2B expression activates the AKT/mTOR/S6K1/CREB1/ZEB1 axis to drive EMT and metastasis, which further promotes tumor progression109. In gastric cancer, 5-HT binds to the HTR2B receptor, activating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway to promote tumor proliferation and invasion110. In neuroendocrine prostate cancer (NEPC), elevated secretion of 5-HT induces neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation and promotes liver metastasis111. In NSCLC, NGF-driven neural infiltration increases intratumoral 5-HT levels. This activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, induces metabolic reprogramming and immune suppression, and accelerates tumor progression112.

At the epigenetic level, 5-HT mediates serotonylation of histone H3 at glutamine 5 (H3Q5ser), promoting the conversion of CAFs into an inflammatory phenotype (iCAFs). This process enhances tumor cell invasiveness, macrophage M2 polarization, and other immunosuppressive features. Notably, silencing SLC22A3 can reverse these 5-HT-driven tumor-promoting effects140.

Dopamine

Dopamine synthesis depends on the sequential activity of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) and aromatic l-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC) and occurs predominantly in the substantia nigra pars compacta, which regulates motor control, and the ventral tegmental area, which is involved in reward and emotional processing141,142. Increasing evidence indicates that dopamine exerts complex, context-dependent effects in cancer, functioning as both a tumor promoter and an immune activator (Table 1).

Dopamine can exert anti-tumor effects through specific receptor subtypes. In breast cancer, D1DR activation inhibits the NF-κB–AKT pathway, downregulating EMT programs and CSC phenotypes and suppressing metastatic dissemination113. In HCC, D1DR activation enhances tumor cell proliferation and metastasis by activating the cAMP/PI3K/AKT/CREB signaling pathway, which accelerates cancer progression and predicts poor prognosis114. In melanoma, DRD2 activates the oxygen-independent HIF-1α pathway, enhances EMT in tumor cells, and promotes malignant progression115. DRD1/2 signaling has also been implicated in long-range regulation of brain tumors through the LRP5–CCN4 axis, increasing their growth capacity116. In melanoma, high DRD3 expression in CD8+ T cells stimulates IL-2 secretion, boosting anti-tumor immunity and enabling effective clearance of tumor cells117. In CRC, DRD4 activation induces transcription of SNAI1 and JAG1, driving EMT and enhancing migratory and invasive potential118. DRD5 activation enhances the JAK1–STAT1 pathway in B cells, promoting antigen presentation and cytotoxic T cell responses, which collectively inhibit tumor progression119.

Histamine

Histamine is synthesized primarily by neurons of the tuberomammillary nucleus (TMN) in the hypothalamus via histidine decarboxylase (HDC) and is released in a circadian rhythm143,144. Acting through four distinct histamine receptors (HRH1–HRH4), histamine participates in diverse physiological processes, including sleep–wake regulation, immune-inflammatory responses, and cancer modulation (Table 1).

In GBM, high expression of histamine promotes angiogenesis through the HRH1/Ca2+/NF-κB pathway, with elevated HRH1 expression correlating with pro-angiogenic tumor microenvironment remodeling and poor patient prognosis120,145. In breast cancer and melanoma, histamine promotes M2 polarization of tumor-associated TAMs through the HRH1 signaling pathway, suppressing CD8+ T cell function and promoting immune evasion and tumor progression121. In NSCLC, HRH3 overexpression activates the PI3K–AKT–mTOR and MEK–ERK pathways, enhancing tumor cell invasiveness122. In breast cancer, HRH4 activation promotes EMT and tumorsphere formation via TGF-β signaling, supporting cancer stemness123. Additionally, histamine signaling regulates pH-dependent metastatic and drug-resistant phenotypes in lung cancer throughw carbonic anhydrase 12 (CA12)-mediated endocyto jit3is, which reverses acidity via CaMKII signaling124.

Pharmacological inhibition of histamine receptors has demonstrated anti-tumor effects: HRH1 antagonists can inhibit tumor cell proliferation146, HRH2 antagonists can influence cancer progression in various tumor types by modulating multiple mechanisms, including proliferation, apoptosis, immunity, and the tumor microenvironment147, while HRH3 antagonists effectively suppress the growth of triple-negative breast cancer148.

Nitric oxide

Nitric oxide (NO) is an atypical gaseous neurotransmitter synthesized from l-arginine via nitric oxide synthase (NOS), and is expressed in diverse cell types including neurons, endothelial cells, immune cells, and tumor cells149. NOS comprises three isoforms: neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS), endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). NO transduces signals primarily through activation of soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC) or by inducing protein S-nitrosylation, and exhibits concentration-, spatiotemporal-, and tissue-specific dual effects in cancer biology (Table 1).

nNOS mainly regulates tumor metabolic reprogramming via S-nitrosylation and other mechanisms, promoting cancer cell proliferation, metabolic adaptation, and metastasis. In ovarian cancer, nNOS-mediated S-nitrosylation of PFKM at Cys351 stabilizes its tetramer and promotes glycolytic metabolic reprogramming, which enhances tumor-cell proliferation and metastasis125. eNOS mainly promotes angiogenesis, improves tumor blood supply, and modulates the redox state of the tumor microenvironment, supporting tumor growth and invasion. In prostate cancer, for example, the upregulation of eNOS activates the NO/sGC/cGMP/PKG signaling axis, driving CSC self-renewal and promoting tumor growth126. iNOS may induce DNA damage, promote tumor progression, or mediate immunosuppression150. In cervical cancer, iNOS is upregulated to promote tumor proliferation, migration, and survival, and andrographolide inhibits iNOS, suppressing cell proliferation, inducing G1–S cell cycle arrest, and promoting apoptosis127. In metaplastic breast cancer, iNOS promotes S-nitrosylation of the JNK/c-Jun complex, maintaining tumor stemness and EMT, enhancing chemoresistance and malignant progression128. Additionally, in breast cancer, NO activates the NF-κB/Snail signaling pathway, inducing EMT and enhancing the metastatic potential of tumor cells129.

Despite its role in promoting tumor progression, NO can exert anti-tumor effects under certain conditions. Endogenous NO inhibits tumor cell-induced platelet aggregation (TCIPA), reducing the adhesion and extravasation of circulating tumor cells and consequently suppressing metastasis. Moreover, the co-delivery of NO donors and chemotherapy drugs via nanomaterial-based platforms has been shown to effectively inhibit metastatic colonization130.

As a gasotransmitter, NO can enhance tumor thermosensitivity. NO-releasing micro/nanoplatforms downregulate HIF-1α, improve antigen presentation, and induce immunogenic cell death and prevent tumor metastasis and recurrence131. In melanoma and lung carcinoma models, low-dose NO donors modulate T cell and macrophage activity to inhibit tumor growth132. Broad-spectrum NO pathway inhibitors, such as L-NMMA, have shown anti-metastatic potential in clinical studies, supporting NOS inhibition as a viable therapeutic strategy for tumor metastasis151. Therefore, targeting NO signaling pathways may present a promising approach for combating tumor progression152.

Neuropeptides

Neuropeptides are better understood as components of broader neuropeptidergic systems, in which coordinated ligand–receptor networks shape tumor progression, immune regulation, and therapeutic response153.

GnRH also exerts significant effects in other solid tumors. For instance, variants in GnRH signaling pathway genes have been associated with improved survival in patients with NSCLC, potentially involving altered ITPR3 expression and downstream GnRH-related intracellular signaling155. In castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), GnRH activates the Gαi/cAMP inhibitory signaling pathway through its receptor. This counteracts the persistently active androgen/AR axis and exerts anti-proliferative, pro-apoptotic, anti-metastatic, and anti-angiogenic effects176. GnRH analogs also trigger apoptosis in androgen-independent prostate cancer cells by inhibiting the PI3K/PKB pathway and activating the c-Src/MLK3/JNK signaling cascade, which exerts a direct antitumor effect156. In endometrial cancer, GnRH and its receptor regulate cell proliferation, cell cycle, and apoptosis through an autocrine system and inhibit tumor growth157,177.

GnRH and its receptors are expressed in various solid tumors and regulate tumor cell proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis through autocrine or paracrine mechanisms. Clinically, GnRH agonists are widely used for hormone-dependent tumors. Continuous stimulation of pituitary GnRH receptors causes receptor desensitization. This suppresses LH and FSH secretion, lowers circulating sex hormone levels, and indirectly inhibits hormone-dependent tumor growth. For example, GnRH-a can effectively provide reversible ovarian function suppression in premenopausal breast cancer patients, and clinical trials have shown that combining GnRH-a with aromatase inhibitors or tamoxifen improves patient survival178. In prostate cancer, GnRH-a (such as the oral drug relugolix) can achieve rapid and complete suppression of testosterone levels, leading to a faster decline in prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and providing patients with a more controllable and optimized androgen-deprivation therapy179.

In cancer therapy, the GnRH system is regarded as a promising therapeutic target, with applications extending beyond reproductive-related diseases to non-reproductive conditions. Future research directions may include combining GnRH-based treatments with other therapies, such as androgen receptor antagonists, to further enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce adverse effects.

Kisspeptin

Kisspeptin is predominantly localized to the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus and the anteroventral periventricular nucleus (AVPV), functioning as an upstream regulator of GnRH rhythmicity180,181. It exerts dual roles in tumor biology, with its effects largely dependent on tissue type, receptor expression status, and splice variant composition (Table 2).

In solid tumors, Kisspeptin signaling demonstrates pro-tumorigenic potential. In breast cancer, high KISS1R expression has been exploited for targeted liposomal drug delivery, prolonging median survival in murine models158. In breast cancer brain metastases, KISS1 dysregulation is associated with impaired E-cadherin function, potentially facilitating loss of cell–cell adhesion and accelerating brain colonization159. In metastatic prostate cancer, the KISS1/KISS1 receptor (KISS1R) axis recruits β-arrestin1/2 to activate ERK and NF-κB pathways, leading to proliferation, migration, invasion, and metastatic potential of prostate cancer cells160.

Conversely, Kisspeptin has been reported to exhibit tumor-suppressive functions. Early studies in melanoma identified KISS1 as a metastasis suppressor gene, capable of inhibiting cell migration and preventing distant dissemination182. The functional duality of Kisspeptin is likely linked to its splice variant diversity, such as kisspeptin-10 and kisspeptin-54, which can activate distinct downstream pathways in different tissue contexts to elicit either oncogenic or tumor-suppressive effects183.

Dynorphin

Dynorphin, a member of the endogenous opioid peptide family, is widely distributed across multiple regions of the CNS, including the ventral pallidum and hypothalamus184. It is primarily involved in pain perception, stress response, and emotional regulation. Emerging evidence indicates that dynorphin and its cognate opioid receptors play pivotal roles in tumorigenesis, metastatic dissemination, and immune evasion. Dynorphin is primarily the endogenous ligand for the κ-opioid receptor (KOR), but it can also act on μ-opioid receptor (MOR), δ-opioid receptor (DOR) and non-classical receptors (Table 2).

KOR activation may inhibit proliferation and induce apoptosis. Specifically, KOR exerts tumor-suppressive effects in glioma by inducing receptor internalization, activating the p38 pathway, and regulating downstream gene expression to inhibit proliferation and promote apoptosis161. In estrogen receptor positive breast cancer, KOR agonists exert anti-tumor effects by activating the KOR/ER/XBP1 signaling axis, suppressing proliferation and promoting apoptosis162. In triple-negative breast cancer, the KOR agonist U69593 suppresses tumor growth and metastasis by disrupting urea cycle function, causing mitochondrial dysfunction and ATP depletion, which induces cancer cell apoptosis163.

MOR and DOR promote tumor progression. In breast cancer, activation of the MOR triggers the JAK/STAT3 signaling cascade, inducing EMT and facilitating breast cancer metastasis164. In HNSCC, the use of opioid analgesics, particularly MOR agonists, has been associated with accelerated disease progression165. In urological malignancies, DOR expression correlates with poor prognosis185.

Clinically, the administration of MOR agonists for analgesia in cancer patients may, while alleviating pain, inadvertently promote tumor progression in certain settings. From an immunomodulatory perspective, opioid peptides, including dynorphin, can suppress natural killer (NK) cell and T cell function, attenuating host antitumor immunity186,187. This mechanism may contribute to immune escape and distant metastasis in malignancies. These findings highlight the need for cautious opioid use in oncology and support the development of selective, non-pro-tumorigenic opioid-based analgesics as a critical goal in cancer pain management.

Vasoactive intestinal peptide

Vasoactive Intestinal Peptide (VIP) is a neuropeptide secreted by neurons of the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus, functioning as a master regulator of circadian rhythmicity. In the PNS, particularly within the ENS, VIP modulates smooth muscle contractility, epithelial secretion, and local immune homeostasis188,189. Recent evidence indicates that VIP, via its receptors VPAC1 and VPAC2, exerts pro-proliferative and immunosuppressive effects in tumors (Table 2).

VIP acts on tumor-infiltrating T cells, inducing the expression of exhaustion markers and attenuating immune responses. In PDAC, VIP engages VPAC2 in an autocrine signaling loop to activate the TGF-β1 pathway, suppress T cell function and promote cancer cell growth166. VIP/VIPR signaling can also suppress anti-tumor immunity by impairing T cell recruitment, activation, and function167. Beyond T cell regulation, VIP also modulates group 3 innate lymphoid cell (ILC3) recruitment and function, stimulating IL-22 secretion to maintain intestinal barrier integrity and antimicrobial defense via VPAC1168. Conditional deletion of Vipr2 in gut-resident ILC3 in mouse models significantly reduces IL-22 production, leading to hepatic steatosis and systemic metabolic dysregulation169, further underscoring the central role of VIP as a neuro–immune–metabolic nexus.

Neurotensin

Neurotensin (NTS) is a tridecapeptide neuropeptide secreted primarily by neurons of the lateral hypothalamic area and by enteroendocrine cells of the gastrointestinal tract, acting as a key regulator of the neuro–gut–immune axis. In multiple solid malignancies, NTS exerts potent oncogenic effects through its high-affinity receptor neurotensin receptor 1 (NTSR1), orchestrating cell signaling and tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) remodeling (Table 2).

In LUAD, IGF2BP3-mediated m6A methylation stabilizes NTSR1 transcripts170, promoting malignant progression and irinotecan resistance by enhancing proliferation, migration and invasion while inhibiting apoptosis. TOX4 also promotes cancer cell migration and invasion by mediating cytoskeletal remodeling downstream of NTSR1 in LUAD171. In PDAC, neuron-derived NTS promotes tumor invasiveness, recruitment, and gemcitabine resistance via NTSR1-mediated activation of the PI3K pathway172. Pharmacological blockade of NTSR1 has demonstrated robust anti-proliferative and anti-metastatic activity in diverse preclinical models, underscoring the NTS–NTSR1 axis as a pivotal regulatory hub in solid tumors and a promising target for translational intervention.

Substance P

Substance P (SP) is a prototypical neuropeptide synthesized and released by sensory neurons in the dorsal root ganglia (DRG), exerting its biological effects primarily via the NK1R. As a central mediator of pain transmission, inflammation, and stress responses, SP orchestrates a broad spectrum of protumorigenic processes within the TME by modulating neuro–immune–vascular interactions, including enhanced cell proliferation, migration and distant metastasis (Table 2).

In breast cancer, tumor-activated sensory neurons release SP, which induces ssRNA/TLR7 signaling in tumor cells to promote metastatic progression. SP also promotes tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis by activating TACR1 on breast cancer cells, inducing death of TACR1-high cells, and triggering ssRNA/TLR7-mediated pro-metastatic gene programs in neighboring tumor cells31. In HCC, peripheral nerve-derived SP activates hepatic stellate cells, promoting tumor invasion, metastasis, EMT, and drug resistance174.

Given its multifaceted role in tumor progression, NK1R has emerged as a potential therapeutic target. Notably, certain NK1R antagonists (such as aprepitant, already clinically approved for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting) are currently being evaluated for anticancer indications, underscoring the translational potential of targeting the SP–NK1R axis175.

Neurexins and neuroligins

Neurexins (NRXNs) and neuroligins (NLGNs) are two closely related families of neuronal proteins that play a critical role in the nervous system. NRXNs are located on the presynaptic membrane and, as axonal adhesion molecules, pair with postsynaptic NLGNs to form the trans-synaptic neurexin–neuroligin signaling axis. These proteins are primarily involved in the formation and regulation of synaptic connections between neurons. However, their functions also extend to tumor biology, where they participate in processes such as tumor progression, metastasis, and differentiation. By mediating neuronal signaling and neuro–tumor interactions, they influence cancer cell growth, migration, and therapeutic resistance (Table 2).

In GBM, NRXN1 regulates tumor cell fate toward a neuron-like lineage, suppresses malignant phenotypes, and improves prognosis, serving as a key neuroligin-related factor in tumor differentiation control190. In prostate and pancreatic cancers, NLGN1 cooperates with the neurotrophic factor GDNF to promote cancer cell migration and invasion along nerves. In GBM, NLGN3 functions as a Wnt/β-catenin-upregulated secreted oncogenic factor that induces and sustains cancer stem cell properties, promoting tumor progression and therapeutic resistance191. In gliomas, neuronal activity drives tumor progression through NLGN3. NLGN3 also drives tumor growth, proliferation, and migration by recruiting and activating Gαi1/3 to mediate downstream AKT/mTORC1 and ERK signaling192. In melanoma, downregulation of NLGN4X activates HIF1A signaling through suppression of its downstream target VBP1, which promotes invasion and metastasis and drives malignant progression193.

ADAM10 inhibition by GI254023X abrogated all cellular sources of NLGN3 and attenuated tumor proliferation and growth in mouse models bearing pediatric glioblastoma or diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma orthotopic xenografts194. These findings imply that anti-neurogenic drugs targeting NRXN–NLGN signaling may offer novel strategies to block tumor progression, representing a potential therapeutic approach for cancer treatment195,196.

Tumor-induced modulation of neurons and neural circuits

Tumors actively shape their surrounding neural microenvironment, establishing pro-tumor neural circuits through multiple mechanisms, including induction of neurogenesis, modulation of neuronal and glial plasticity, secretion of neurotrophic and axon guidance factors, enhancement of neuronal excitability, and regulation of synaptic coupling. These processes remodel pre-existing nerve fibers and form supportive “neural niches” that promote tumor growth, immune evasion, and metastasis197. They also enable tumor cells to acquire neuron-like phenotypes with electrophysiological excitability and synapse-dependent invasive behavior198. Furthermore, long-range signaling and the gut microbiota–gut–brain axis provide systemic feedback that reinforces neural excitation, immune suppression, and tumor dissemination (Fig. 7). Tumor-driven neurogenesis and neural plasticity

Cancer progression drives neurogenesis and stimulates neural plasticity199. In gastric and colorectal cancers, a subset of cancer stem cells can differentiate into tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-expressing sympathetic neurons and vesicular acetylcholine transporter (VAChT)-expressing parasympathetic neurons, forming functional neural networks involved in tumor neurogenesis200. In oral cancer, studies have shown that TP53 loss drives adrenergic transdifferentiation of tumor-associated trigeminal sensory nerves by downregulating the microRNA miR-34a, resulting in the formation of newly generated adrenergic nerve fibers. These transdifferentiated nerve fibers can release signaling molecules such as noradrenaline, markedly promoting tumor growth201. In GBM, tumor cells can regulate neuronal activity to induce synaptic input onto themselves, triggering Ca2+ signaling that promotes the de novo formation of tumor microtubes and accelerates invasion, indicating that tumors exert multi-level control over their own dissemination and heterogeneity by manipulating neurons202. In Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) mouse models, tumor-prone Nf1 mutations enhance neuronal excitability by reducing HCN channel function, leading to increased secretion of tumor-promoting paracrine factors that stimulate Schwann cell proliferation and tumor formation203. Epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming shape Schwann tumor cell states and remodel the immune microenvironment by activating neural crest or nerve injury-related pathways, and can be induced by radiotherapy to drive reversible conversion between tumor subtypes204.

Tumor-driven epigenetic and neural remodeling

Tumors can manipulate epigenetic and metabolic regulatory pathways to reprogram transcription and induce phenotypic changes in neurons or glial cells. This increases their plasticity to support tumor growth and invasion.

In Schwann cells, the loss of Bap1 leads to aberrant spreading of Polycomb-associated epigenetic marks such as H2AK119ub1 and H3K27me3, which prematurely activates nerve injury and neural crest-related gene programs, disrupts the myelination transcriptional network, and ultimately results in myelin instability and axonal degeneration205. In PDAC, perineural invasion-associated cancer-associated fibroblasts (pCAFs) enhance glycolysis to produce high levels of lactate, creating a lactate-rich tumor microenvironment. Lactate absorbed by pancreatic cancer cells induces histone H3K18 lactylation, an epigenetic modification that activates neural invasion-related genes such as L1CAM and SLIT1 and promotes perineural invasion. Meanwhile, acetylation of the glycolytic enzyme GAPDH in pCAFs increases its enzymatic activity and glycolytic flux, further amplifying lactate production and providing metabolic and epigenetic support for neural invasion206.

Furthermore, in pediatric ependymoma (EPN), histone serotonylation during tumorigenesis converts neuronal signals into epigenetic changes in tumor cells, activating core developmental transcription factors and remodeling chromatin states to promote tumor progression. ETV5 suppresses NPY expression by enhancing repressive chromatin states. In contrast, neuropeptide Y (NPY) upregulation inhibits tumor progression and excessive neural network activity through synaptic remodeling. These findings reveal how neuronal signaling, histone modification, and developmental programs interact to drive brain tumor malignancy207.

This perspective suggests that targeting specific epigenetic modifications could effectively inhibit tumor progression driven by neural plasticity, providing a potential strategy for precision therapy.

Tumor-driven neurotrophic and axon guidance signaling

Tumor cells can modulate intratumoral neural remodeling by secreting neurotrophic factors and axon guidance molecules, which enhance the growth, branching, and plasticity of pre-existing nerve fibers within the tumor microenvironment and form a supportive “neural niche” that facilitates tumor proliferation, immune evasion, and metastasis.

In oral and salivary gland tumors, NGF promotes tumor cell scattering and migration by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and enhances perineural invasion208. In pancreatic cancer, tumor cells secrete NGF, which promotes the growth and proliferation of pancreatic nerve plexus fibers, forming dense neural networks that exacerbate pancreatic neuropathy and facilitate tumor progression209. The HGF/c-Met signaling pathway promotes pancreatic tumor secretion of NGF through activation of the mTOR/NGF axis and TrkA and p75NTR receptors, recruits nerve fibers to form PNI, accelerates nerve growth, and facilitates tumor progression210,211. In gliomas, BDNF secreted by neurons acts on TrkB receptors on the tumor cell surface, activating downstream signaling pathways such as CAMKII, regulating the membrane localization of AMPAR and synaptic plasticity. This enhances neuron-tumor synaptic function and promotes tumor proliferation and progression13. In intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), acetylcholine activates the CAMKII/GSK3β/β-catenin signaling pathway through the CHRNA5 receptor and simultaneously induces BDNF expression, promoting nerve infiltration and forming an acetylcholine/BDNF feedforward axis that drives tumor metastasis and enhances chemoresistance212.

In precancerous lesions of pancreatic cancer, the axon guidance molecule Netrin-1 is secreted by pancreatic cells and promotes sympathetic axon growth and branching through the DCC receptor, driving neural plasticity and reshaping local cellular dynamics, with major effects on the tumor microenvironment and early cancer progression213.

Tumors also secrete neuroregulatory factors (Gal3 and LIF) to activate the HPA axis and PVN neurons, inducing systemic and local immunosuppression and reinforcing a positive feedback loop between nerves and the tumor. For example, in lung cancer, prostate cancer, colorectal cancer, and triple-negative breast cancer, tumors secrete LIF and Gal3 to activate the central nervous system and induce sympathetic nerve-mediated immunosuppression, thereby collectively exploiting the nervous system to promote tumor progression214. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) serve as long-range signaling mediators, enhancing neural plasticity and facilitating tumor dissemination215. In CRC, tumor-derived exosomal miR-21-5p induces Schwann cells to secrete NGF, and the tumor-derived NGF both promotes Schwann cell proliferation and nerve fiber expansion and activates tumor cells via the TrkA/ERK signaling pathway, forming a positive feedback loop that jointly drives neural infiltration, tumor growth, and metastasis216.

Tumor-induced neuronal excitability and synaptic coupling

Increasing evidence indicates that tumors can modulate neuronal activity and electrical signaling, either by altering the plasticity of neighboring neurons or by acquiring intrinsic electrical excitability. These reciprocal interactions promote tumor proliferation, invasion, and metastasis.

In gliomas, tumors drive pathological plasticity in neighboring neurons by enhancing mTOR signaling, including decreased dendritic spine density, altered expression of synapse-related genes, and functional neuronal hyperexcitability, which exacerbates neural network abnormalities and may contribute to tumor-associated symptoms; these neuronal changes can be reversed by mTORC1/2 inhibitors217. In SCLC, NE cells acquire electrical excitability, and their action potential firing directly drives tumor malignancy, accompanied by a reliance on oxidative phosphorylation and metabolic support from non-NE cells, forming a tumor–autonomous vicious cycle that enhances proliferative capacity and metastatic potential218. In gliomas, neurons form bona fide neuron-glioma synapses with tumor cells mediated by AMPA receptors, allowing electrical signals to propagate synchronously through tumor microtube networks and promoting tumor proliferation and invasion9.

Overall, these results demonstrate the active role of tumors in regulating neuronal activity and electrical signaling, suggesting that targeting neuron–tumor electrical interactions may represent a novel strategy to inhibit cancer progression.

Gut microbiota–gut–brain axis feedback in tumorigenesis

The gut microbiota profoundly influences neuro–tumor interactions via the microbiota–gut–brain axis. Metabolites produced by gut microbes, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), glutamate, and glutamine, can cross the blood–brain barrier or act on the ENS to modulate neuroinflammation, immune responses, and TME formation.

For example, SCFAs can regulate glial cell maturation and influence neuroinflammation and nociceptive sensitivity219,220. In dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)-induced colitis models, inflammatory gene expression in microglia is elevated, increasing colorectal cancer susceptibility221. Furthermore, intestinal tryptophan maintains gut homeostasis by regulating metabolic pathways, preserving the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier, shaping the gut microbiota composition, and modulating AMPK–SIRT1-mediated autophagy, which confers anti-inflammatory and protective effects222.

Overall, the gut microbiota modulates the inflammatory environment through neuro-immune regulatory mechanisms mediated by microbial metabolites. This highlights potential intervention points at the level of metabolite production, neural signaling, or immune modulation, suggesting that microbial metabolic pathways may serve as novel targets for precision cancer therapy223.

Pharmacological agents targeting neuropeptide signaling in cancer and translational implications.

Among classical neurotransmitter pathways, ADRB1/ADRB2 is currently the most extensively studied and the most advanced in translation. β-Blockers such as propranolol, originally used for cardiovascular disease, are now being re-evaluated for their antitumor potential284. Beyond adrenergic signaling, CHRM antagonists such as darifenacin285, 5-HT receptor- or transporter-targeting agents such as sertraline286, and modulators of GABA, histamine, and glutamate signaling have also provided pharmacological support. For pathways such as DRD2/3, development is not limited to repurposed neuropsychiatric agents, as newer compounds such as ONC201 have also entered investigation287.

A similar pattern is seen in neuropeptide pathways. NK1R antagonists such as aprepitant288, initially developed as antiemetics, have been incorporated into anticancer studies. Recently, Han et al.289 reported that magnolol acts as a novel natural NK1R inhibitor289. At the same time, receptor-directed small molecules, blocking antibodies, and radioligands targeting NTSR1, the CGRP axis, VPAC, KISS1R, GnRH receptor, and opioid receptor-related signaling are also under active development. Together, these findings indicate that both classical neurotransmitter receptors and neuropeptide receptors now have a substantial preclinical basis for therapeutic intervention.

Overall, therapeutic targeting of neurotransmitter and neuropeptide receptors has progressed from mechanistic investigation to a translational stage supported by pharmacological intervention and clinical testing. At present, the ADRB1/ADRB2, DRD2, and GnRH receptor directions are advancing most rapidly, whereas pathways involving GABA, histamine, glutamate, and certain sensory neuropeptides remain less developed clinically. Nevertheless, these pathways already have corresponding pharmacological tools and therefore remain important directions for further investigation.

Neuro–tumor communication across the tumor ecosystems: toward precision intervention

Interpreting divergent neural effects across tumor ecosystems

Neurotransmitters and other neuroactive mediators do not exert uniform biological effects across different tumor types. Instead, their functions are shaped by the tumor ecosystem, and their biological effects depend on how neural inputs are decoded within specific cellular and microenvironmental contexts. Sympathetic adrenergic signaling is more consistently associated with tumor-promoting programs, mainly including enhanced stemness maintenance, increased angiogenesis, stromal activation, and immune suppression. In contrast, parasympathetic-, sensory-, and enteric nerve-associated signals, as well as neurotransmitter systems such as acetylcholine, GABA, dopamine, and histamine, more often display tumor type-specific, receptor-dependent, and microenvironment-dependent activities.

Neuropeptide signaling in tumors is likewise heterogeneous and can be organized into several relatively stable dominant output patterns (Table 3, Table 4 and 4). Specifically, the CGRP/VIP axis is more closely associated with neuro-immune suppression. The SP axis is more strongly linked to inflammation-driven invasion and metastatic dissemination. The NTS axis is more prominently involved in malignant progression and therapy resistance. In contrast, dynorphin and kisspeptin reflect the bidirectional and context-dependent nature of neuropeptide regulation in different tumor settings. Taken together, the functional output of neurotransmitters and neuropeptides is highly context-dependent, and their biological consequences are shaped less by ligand identity alone than by receptor subtype usage, the lineage identity of responding cells, and the local microenvironment.

Therapeutic stratification of neuro–tumor communication

These observations indicate that therapeutic targeting of neuro–tumor communication should be stratified according to the dominant mode of neural regulation, rather than applied uniformly across tumor types or neural pathways. This view is also consistent with recent efforts to frame neuro–tumor crosstalk as a therapeutically reprogrammable process, further supporting stratified intervention across distinct tumor ecosystems290. Pathways with relatively consistent tumor-promoting effects, particularly sympathetic adrenergic signaling, may represent broader candidates for intervention through pharmacological blockade, drug repurposing, or combination treatment291. By contrast, parasympathetic, sensory, and neuropeptide-related programs show greater tumor type specificity and stronger dependence on receptor distribution and local microenvironmental architecture. These pathways are therefore more likely to require receptor-selective targeting, local denervation or neural ablation strategies, microenvironment-informed intervention, or the development of more precise inhibitors.

Accordingly, precision strategies should move beyond histological classification alone and incorporate neural features that reflect functional dependency, including nerve density, neurotransmitter receptor expression, neuropeptide pathway activation, and states of neural remodeling292. Such features may help guide different therapeutic approaches, including direct pathway blockade, repurposing of clinically available neuroactive drugs, neuroimmune combination strategies, and the development of new agents targeting defined neural receptor axes293. In this framework, the key challenge is to identify which neural program is functionally dominant in a given tumor ecosystem, so that different modes of intervention can be matched to different forms of neural dependency.

Resolving neural dependency with emerging models and technologies

Meeting this challenge will require experimental models and analytical tools that can resolve neural dependency with greater spatial and temporal precision.

As noted above, Trace-n-Seq, which combines retrograde axon tracing with single-cell transcriptomics, provides a paradigm for dissecting the subtype-specific roles of neurons in the tumor microenvironment, although systematic neuronal atlases remain limited for most tumor types294. This is particularly important because neuro–tumor and neuro–immune interactions are intrinsically dynamic, involving neurotransmitter plasticity, axonal regeneration, synaptic remodeling, and circuit reprogramming, whereas many current in vivo and in vitro systems still capture only terminal states.

To address this limitation, newly developed platforms can be incorporated into a more mechanistic workflow. Brain organoid-based neural co-culture systems295 and microfluidic neural chips provide tractable platforms for modeling tumor–neural networks, monitoring neuronal migration, and analyzing synapse-like connectivity at higher resolution296,297. In parallel, multimodal neuroimaging approaches integrating MRI298, DTI, computer-aided diagnosis (CAD)299, and metabolic tumor markers300 may help visualize neuro–tumor interactions in vivo for diagnosis, innervation mapping, and therapeutic monitoring, although their spatial resolution and dynamic tracking capacity remain limited, especially for deep-seated tumor-associated nerves.

High-resolution imaging innovations such as light-microscopy-based connectomics (LICONN)301 and ARCHmap-blockface-VISoR302 further expand the toolkit for reconstructing neural circuitry and whole-body neural architecture, creating new technical opportunities to study tumor-associated neural complexity.

Taken together, future progress in cancer neuroscience will depend on integrating mechanistic biology with high-resolution models and imaging platforms. Rather than treating neural regulation as a static background feature of the tumor microenvironment, the field should move toward defining when, where, and in which cell populations neural signaling becomes functionally dominant. Such a shift may improve biomarker-informed stratification, refine mechanism-based intervention, and strengthen the translational value of targeting neuro–tumor crosstalk.

Conclusions

Cancer neuroscience is redefining the role of the nervous system in the tumor ecosystem. Rather than functioning as a passive background component, neural regulation is increasingly recognized as an integral determinant of tumor progression, immune remodeling, and therapeutic response. Neuro–tumor communication does not conform to a single biological pattern, but varies across tumor contexts. This view provides a coherent basis for understanding the heterogeneity of neural effects in cancer and how distinct neural programs are incorporated into tumor evolution. Future progress will depend on more precise identification of neural dependencies across distinct tumor ecosystems and on translating this knowledge into actionable therapeutic strategies. Continued integration of mechanistic studies with emerging experimental models and spatiotemporally resolved technologies should further refine neuro–tumor dependency and support more precise neural-targeted intervention in cancer.
