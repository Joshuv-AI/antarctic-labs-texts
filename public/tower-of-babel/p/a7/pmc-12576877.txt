Moral Opposition to Genetically Engineered Food in the United States, France, and Germany
Authors: Yoel Inbar, Sydney E Scott, Paul Rozin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhen people are morally opposed to a practice, they often profess to be consequence‐insensitive—that is, they say that they think it ought to be prohibited regardless of the risks and benefits. We investigate consequence‐insensitive opposition to genetically engineered (GE) food in France, Germany, and the United States. Using nationally representative samples (total N = 1599), we find that most GE food opponents in all three countries are consequence‐insensitive (France: 93.1%; Germany: 87.4%; United States: 81.3%). Consequence‐insensitive opponents differ from other opponents in other ways consistent with their holding moral beliefs. They are more likely to display other properties of sacred moral values, like quantity insensitivity and universalism. They also see GE food as more personally important, are less willing to consume it, are more in favor of policies restricting it, and are more willing to engage in activism against it.Keywords: consequence insensitivity, genetically engineered (GE), moral judgment, protected values, sacred values, science communication

Introduction

Genetic engineering of plants and animals for human consumption is controversial. Proponents of genetically engineered (GE) food point to many promising applications, including drought and pest resistance, enhanced nutrition, and resistance to spoilage [1]. Moreover, nearly all scientists agree that GE food is not riskier to consume than conventionally produced food [1, 2], and the additional environmental risk posed by GE crops seems to be minimal [2]. Nonetheless, public opposition to GE food is widespread in the United States and even more prevalent in Europe [3]. This is reflected in government regulation, with growing GE crops or selling GE food for human consumption severely restricted in the European Union [4].

Researchers studying GE food opposition commonly work from the assumption that laypeople rationally use information (primarily perceptions of risks and benefits) to arrive at a considered evaluation of GE food (for a review, see Ref. [4]). Indeed, risks seem to play a large role in laypeople's GE food attitudes. People see GE food as quite risky, especially to human health [5, 6], and seeing GE food as risky is consistently associated with opposition to it [7, 8, 9]. Risk perceptions often explain the largest share of variance in GE attitudes [7, 10], and some other predictors of GE attitudes (such as trust or sociodemographic variables) may be related to attitudes indirectly, via risk perceptions [7].

Laypeople may also dislike GE crops because they think there are minimal (or no) benefits for consumers, perhaps because the most common GE crops are engineered for traits (such as herbicide and pest resistance) that directly benefit producers, not consumers [11]. Indeed, greater perceived benefits are associated with more positive attitudes toward GE food [8, 9, 12, 13] and greater willingness to pay for it.

Consequence‐Insensitive GE Food Opposition

Despite the extensive research showing relationships between risk and benefit perceptions and GE food attitudes, many people assert that their opposition depends neither on risks nor benefits—a stance that Baron and Spranca [14] called “consequence insensitivity.” In the United States, for example, most GE food opponents (71%) agree that GE food “should be prohibited no matter how great the benefits and minor the risks from allowing it” [15]. Taken literally, consequence insensitivity is an extreme position that would prohibit the sort of trade‐offs that policymakers and risk analysts routinely make [14]. Fortunately, though, for many people, consequence insensitivity seems to be more expressive than literal. Even consequence‐insensitive GE food opponents often give responses to follow‐up belief probes that are inconsistent with unconditional opposition [15, 16], and they sometimes change their minds in response to morally framed pro‐GE food arguments [17]. In this respect, GE food seems to be like other values for which people express consequence insensitivity, in that they are amenable to change under the right circumstances [18].

Consequence insensitivity is one characteristic of what researchers have called “sacred” or “protected” moral values [14, 19]. People often say that these moral values should not be traded off against other considerations (particularly material and financial considerations) and react with outrage at such trade‐offs [19, 20, 21]. However, although consequence insensitivity is a correlate of one kind of moralization, it is not synonymous with it. (We are using “moralized” here as defined by Rozin [22] [p. 220]: “[T]he conversion of an object or activity preference into something with negative moral status.”). For example, people might have moral objections to GE food because of its perceived harms or lack of benefits (see, e.g., Ref. [23]), but might also be willing to withdraw those objections if the balance of risks and benefits changes (i.e., they are consequence‐sensitive). It might, therefore, be the case that both consequence‐sensitive and consequence‐insensitive GE opponents are moralizing their views, just in different ways.

Consequence‐insensitive GE food opponents do respond differently from other opponents to some other items associated with sacred values. Scott et al. [15] found that consequence‐insensitive opponents overwhelmingly agree with two other statements associated with holding sacred values (“quantity insensitivity” and “universalism”; see below), but consequence‐sensitive opponents do not. Likewise, Inbar and Waldhof [16] found that consequence‐insensitive GE food opponents report stronger moral conviction [24] about GE food compared to consequence‐sensitive opponents or supporters. Despite this suggestive evidence, a more thorough investigation of whether consequence‐insensitive opponents show other characteristics of moralization is currently lacking.

The Current Research

The aim of the current research is to more thoroughly explore the correlates of consequence‐insensitive GE opposition. We examine whether consequence‐insensitive GE opponents in France, Germany, and the United States differ from other opponents on other measures of holding sacred moral values, as well as on downstream consequences of moralization. If so, this would support the hypothesis that consequence‐insensitive opponents are the group most likely to moralize GE food in general.

Do Consequence‐Insensitive GE Opponents Agree With Other Sacred Value Questions?

In their research on moral GE food opposition in the United States, Scott et al. [15] asked participants three questions that were originally developed by Baron and Spranca [14] for their research on sacred values (which they call “protected values”). Scott et al.’s primary analyses focused on the consequence‐insensitivity question, which asked whether GE food “should be prohibited no matter how great the benefits and minor the risks from allowing it.” Likewise, we here focus on the consequence‐insensitivity item and test whether responses to other questions theoretically related to sacred values differ between consequence‐sensitive and consequence‐insensitive opponents. In the current study, we used two other items related to sacred values that were previously used in Scott et al.: “It is equally wrong to allow some of this to happen as to allow twice as much to happen. The amount doesn't matter” (quantity insensitivity); and “This would be wrong even in a country where everyone thought it was not wrong” (universalism). We also added two new exploratory items that were intended to tap moral outrage at the juxtaposition of secular (financial) considerations with sacred values [19]: “I am offended by the idea of putting a monetary price on allowing this”; and “It is morally wrong to put a monetary price on allowing this practice.” For each of these items, we test whether responses differ between consequence‐sensitive and consequence‐insensitive opponents. If consequence‐insensitive opponents are more likely to display quantity insensitivity, universalism, and moral outrage at sacred‐secular tradeoffs than consequence‐sensitive opponents, then this would provide further evidence that consequence‐insensitive opponents moralize GE food more than consequence‐sensitive opponents.

Do Consequence‐Insensitive Opponents Differ From Other Opponents on Attitudes Related to Moralization?

We also test whether consequence‐insensitive opponents differ from consequence‐sensitive opponents on other attitudes that are theoretically related to moralizing an issue. We focus on three characteristics of moralization: subjective importance, action motivation, and support for legal restriction.

First, moral beliefs are seen as subjectively more important [24]. Of course, people can see nonmoralized attitudes as important as well [25, 26], but if consequence‐insensitive GE opponents did not rate the issue as important, that would be evidence against the validity of the measure.

Second, moral beliefs motivate action [27, 28]. People are motivated to act consistently with their moral views, even if that means sacrificing their self‐interest (e.g., by paying more for certain goods [28]). Moral attitudes also motivate political activism [28, 29]. We assessed action motivation by asking participants about how willing they would be to purchase GE food that had different consumer benefits, and by asking about their willingness to participate in different kinds of anti‐GE activism.

Finally, governments often intervene to regulate or ban negatively moralized behavior. For example, the moralization of cigarette smoking in the United States since the 1950s was accompanied by a marked increase in legal restrictions on the consumption, sale, and advertising of cigarettes [22]. In the current study, we measured demand for legal restrictions by asking participants to rate their support for policies restricting or banning GE food in their country.

In short, if consequence‐insensitive (vs. consequence‐sensitive) opponents are more likely to see their GE attitudes as important, want to protest GE food, refuse to buy GE food, and want to regulate GE food, then this would provide further evidence that consequence‐insensitive opponents moralize GE food more than consequence‐sensitive opponents.

How Prevalent is Consequence‐Insensitive GE Food Opposition Outside the United States?

We also examine the degree to which consequence‐insensitive opposition to GE food and moralization of it are prevalent in two European countries: France and Germany. Consequence‐insensitive opposition is common in the United States [15], but its prevalence in Europe is less well‐understood. Europeans are even more negative than Americans toward GE food [3], but there is little evidence directly assessing consequence‐insensitive opposition in Europe. One exception is a recent study (with data collected after the present study was run), finding high levels of GE food consequence‐insensitive opposition and moralization in a German sample [16]. Our tentative hypothesis, therefore, was that consequence‐insensitive GE food opposition would be more common in Europe compared to the United States.

Method

Participants and Sampling Strategy

Data collection occurred in June and July of 2016. Participants from the United States (N = 540), France (N = 500), and Germany (N = 519) were recruited and paid by Qualtrics.com. Unless stated otherwise, we exclude no participants from analysis.

This sample was intended to be representative of each country's general population on age, gender, and income. However, due to an error during data collection, some demographic quota categories were slightly oversampled. We therefore use survey weighting to the population demographics (using Stata's “pweight” subcommand) in most analyses, though this never changes the statistical significance of results.1

Materials and Survey Flow

Here, we describe the measures relevant to our hypotheses, which are a subset of all measures collected. Any measures not described here are described in the Supplementary Materials.

Participants first completed a single‐item measure of GE food opposition, followed by the consequence‐insensitivity item. Response options for each were binary (“Agree” or “Disagree”).

After these items, participants then completed the subsequent blocks of items in block‐randomized order.

GE Food Importance

Participants were asked four questions measuring the importance of GE food to them personally: “How important is the issue of genetically modifying plants and animals for human consumption to you personally?” “How much do you personally care about the issue of genetically modifying plants and animals for human consumption?” “How much does the issue of genetically modifying plants and animals for human consumption mean to you?” and “How important would a candidate's position on the issue of genetically modifying plants and animals for human consumption be if you were voting?” These items were adapted from Krosnick et al. [26]. Participants gave their ratings on 9‐point scales where higher ratings corresponded to greater importance.

Willingness to Buy GE Food

Participants were asked to rate their willingness to buy three different GE or GE‐related foods (longer‐lasting GE tomatoes, less‐expensive meat from animals fed GE corn, and better‐tasting chocolate containing GE lecithin, adapted from Siegrist [8]). Participants gave their ratings on 9‐point scales numbered from −4 (“Certainly would not”) to 4 (“Certainly would”). The scale midpoint (0) was labeled “Maybe.” We included these items as a measure of personal behavior intentions.

Likelihood of Engaging in Political Activism

Participants were asked to rate how likely they were to engage in four different acts of anti‐GE‐food activism (writing a letter to a political figure, donating to two different nonprofits, and participating in a public demonstration). Participants gave their ratings on 9‐point scales (1 = “Very unlikely” to 9 = “Very likely”). These items were created by us. We expected that consequence‐insensitive opponents would rate themselves as more willing to engage in anti‐GE activism, compared to consequence‐sensitive opponents.

Desire to Regulate GE Food

Participants were asked to rate how much they supported five different regulations restricting GE (e.g., “Your government forbidding any sale of GM foods within the nation's borders”). These items were first used in Scott et al. [15]. Participants gave their ratings on 9‐point scales (1 = “Certainly oppose” to 9 = “Certainly support”).

Demographics

This block was always presented last. Participants were asked about their gender, year of birth, race/ethnicity, level of education, and household income. The race/ethnicity and education categories were changed to be appropriate for each country. We asked participants to pick an income range—for example, $25,000−$50,000—expressed in their local currency. They were also asked to indicate their religious affiliation (if any), religiosity, and political ideology. We do not present analyses of the demographic measures here. For privacy reasons, they are not included in the posted data, but they are available upon request from the corresponding author.

Results

As described above under “Participants and Sampling Strategy,” analyses are performed using survey weighting (via Stata's “pweight” subcommand) to the population demographics, unless otherwise specified. However, this never changes the statistical significance of results.

How Prevalent is Consequence‐Insensitive Opposition?

GE food opposition was highest in France, where 87.5% [95% CI: 84.2%, 90.1%] agreed with the statement “I oppose genetically modifying plants and animals for human consumption.” It was lowest in the United States, where 62.9% [95% CI: 58.7%, 67.0%] agreed. Opposition in Germany fell between the two, with 80.5% [95% CI: 76.9%, 83.8%] agreeing. All between‐country differences in opposition were statistically significant at p < 0.01 or less.

We next examined consequence‐insensitive opposition, that is, the proportion of opponents who agreed that GE food should be prohibited regardless of the risks and benefits. This proportion was high in all countries, but was highest in France, where 93.1% [95% CI: 90.4%, 95.2%] of opponents were consequence‐insensitive. It was lowest in the United States, where 81.3% [95% CI: 76.7%, 85.2%] of opponents were consequence‐insensitive. As with overall opposition, consequence‐insensitive opposition in Germany fell between France and the United States, with 87.4% [95% CI: 83.8%, 90.4%] of opponents agreeing with the consequence‐insensitivity item. All between‐country differences in consequence‐insensitivity were statistically significant at p < 0.03 or less.

Thus, both opposition and consequence‐insensitive opposition are higher in Europe than in the United States, and higher in France than in Germany. At least across the three countries in the current sample, opposition and consequence‐insensitive opposition (as a share of all opposition) seem to increase in tandem. In the subsequent analyses, we focus on comparisons between different types of GE food opponents (consequence‐sensitive vs. consequence‐insensitive). We return to the question of GE food supporters in the General Discussion.

These results are consistent with an account where consequence‐insensitive opponents moralize GE food more than consequence‐sensitive opponents. As predicted by this account, consequence‐insensitive opponents are more likely to exhibit properties of sacred value commitments, place more importance on GE food, show different behavioral intentions, and are more supportive of regulating GE food.

General Discussion

Most respondents in the United States, France, and Germany were opposed to GE food, and most opponents were consequence‐insensitive opponents. There were, however, substantial differences between countries. Opposition and consequence‐insensitive opposition were higher in Europe than in the United States, with the highest levels of overall opposition and consequence‐insensitive opposition in France.

Previous studies have examined consequence‐insensitive opposition to GE food in the United States [15] and Germany [16]. To our knowledge, the current research is the first to directly compare consequence‐insensitive GE food opposition between the United States and two European countries. Although it would be premature to draw broad conclusions from three countries, the evidence from these countries suggests that overall opposition and consequence insensitivity increase in tandem—the greater the share of opponents in the population, the greater the share of consequence‐insensitive opponents among opponents.

What is Consequence‐Insensitive GE Food Opposition Expressing?

In previous research, we described consequence‐insensitive GE food opponents—people who said GE food ought to be prohibited “regardless of the risks and benefits”—as “moral absolutists” [15], in part because this question was described as a measure of “absoluteness” in prior work [14]. In retrospect, “absolutist” suggested that consequence‐insensitive opponents would not change their minds under any circumstances or that they did not see GE food as harmful, which we did not intend. Understandably, other researchers objected, pointing out that, in fact, consequence‐insensitive opponents do see GE food as harmful [23] or that they are willing to set aside their objections to it under some circumstances [35]. We agree that statements of consequence insensitivity should not be taken literally, and that consequence‐insensitive GE food opponents can sometimes be persuaded to change their minds [16]. We do believe, however, that consequence‐insensitive GE food opponents are expressing a moral objection. We tested this here in two ways: by asking about other properties of sacred values and by asking about other potential downstream consequences of moralization.

Sacred Value Items

We asked a broader set of questions about sacred values, because if consequence‐insensitive opponents are moralizing GE food as inconsistent with their sacred (protected) values, then they should also agree more with other sacred value items. To our knowledge, the current research is the first time any of these items have been used to test GE food moralization with non‐United States respondents (Inbar and Waldhof [16] used the consequence‐insensitivity item but not the other four).

Two sacred value items (quantity insensitivity and universalism) were previously used in Scott et al. [15] but were not the focus of the analyses (although Scott et al. reported robustness checks with them that were consistent with the consequence‐insensitivity item). The two remaining “monetary price” items (moral outrage and trade‐off condemnation) are new. These new items asked respondents about their reactions to putting “a monetary price” on allowing GE food. Trading off sacred values against secular values (paradigmatically, money) elicits condemnation and moral outrage [19], and we wrote these two items to tap this possible reaction to financial benefits and profitability of GE (i.e., to the pro‐GE argument that the greater efficiency of GE crops will increase consumer and producer surplus). Because we had not used either of these items previously, we asked separately about outrage (Item 3) and offense (Item 4) to be able to detect any differences between the two. As shown in Figure 1, though, responses across the two items were quite similar.

We asked about price broadly because we aimed to be general. However, because the phrase “to put a price on” can also be interpreted more broadly as to express how important something is (e.g., “you can't put a price on love”), we specified “monetary” to make sure that people were thinking about a tradeoff with money specifically. This does raise the possibility that these items might have confused participants (who may not have understood what we were asking), but the data suggest otherwise. Pairwise contingency coefficients for the two “monetary price” items were similar to those for the other three items (see Table S2), which would not be the case if these items elicited more random error in responses. Alternatively, it might be that participants interpreted the items more broadly than intended (e.g., they might have interpreted “putting a monetary price on allowing” GE food as asking about bribery or corruption, or they might have interpreted being “offended” in a nonmoral sense). If this were the case, we would expect high levels of agreement with the items even from consequence‐sensitive opponents (who would be expected to object to bribery and corruption entirely aside from their views of GE food). However, as Figure 1 shows, consequence‐sensitive opponents did not agree with the “monetary price” items much more than they agreed with the other three items in the United States and France. Agreement with these items was noticeably higher only in Germany, which might reflect either true cultural differences or an issue with the translation of these two items into German.

We cannot rule out, though, that different groups of people might be interpreting these items in different ways. For example, some might interpret them as referring to bribery and corruption; some might interpret them (as we intended) as referring to higher profits or lower prices as a justification for GE, and still others might have interpreted them to refer to the idea of allowing GE foods to be sold in stores at all. Thus, the apparent consistency between the monetary price items and the other items might still mask meaningful variation.5 We suggest that future research could use more specific monetary price items in addition to—or instead of—the ones used in the current study.

Responses across sacred value items were generally highly consistent for consequence‐insensitive opponents. Of the opponents who said that GE food should be prohibited regardless of the risks and benefits, 88% also agreed with all four other sacred value items (quantity insensitivity, universalism, emotional resistance to secular trade‐offs, and moral outrage). This high agreement across items might raise the concern that participants simply adopted a response set once they had agreed or disagreed with the first (consequence insensitivity) item and, therefore, agreed or disagreed with all subsequent items. However, as Figure 1 shows, responses for consequence‐sensitive opponents did vary across items. This suggests both that many consequence‐sensitive opponents also had some moral objections to GE food, and that participants were not treating the sacred value items as the same question asked multiple times. An important question for future research is why opponents might agree with some sacred value items and not others, which is not well‐explained by current theories of sacred values.

Other Correlates of Moral Attitudes

Consequence‐insensitive GE food opponents also showed many other hallmarks of holding a moral attitude. Compared to other opponents, consequence‐insensitive opponents rated the issue of GE food as more personally important to them, said they were less willing to buy GE food, indicated a greater willingness to engage in anti‐GE activism, and supported stricter GE food regulation. The fact that consequence‐insensitive GE food opponents responded like people who have moral beliefs across a range of other measures is support for the contention that for many, GE food opposition is moral opposition.

We have been treating the difference between consequence‐sensitive and consequence‐insensitive opponents as categorical. Our approach here is based on the research on protected or sacred values, which treats these values as categorically distinct from others [14, 19]. Other definitions of moralization do not have this characteristic; for example, Skitka and colleagues’ research on moral conviction [24, 28, 29, 36] treats moral conviction as continuous rather than categorical, despite conceptualizing moral conviction as very similar to holding a sacred or protected value. We focus on consequence sensitivity as a dichotomous variable here because consequence insensitivity has such clear implications for policy and science communication. This would remain true even if the underlying psychological construct of moralization (that we argue leads to consequence insensitivity) were better thought of as continuous than categorical. What is most important for the current research is that people who hold sacred or protected values really do seem to think about those values differently than those who do not (which is not to deny that the categories may be blurry at the edges).

The Role of Perceived Risks and Benefits

Past research has found that greater perceptions of risks and lower perceptions of benefits are associated with greater GE food opposition [7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13]. In the current studies, we find similar patterns of results. We asked participants eight questions assessing their perceptions of GE food risks and benefits; analyses reported in the Supplementary Materials show that risk perceptions were higher, and benefit perceptions were lower, among consequence‐insensitive versus consequence‐sensitive opponents (see Table S4 and Figure S1). We also asked four other questions about trust in GE‐related institutions (e.g., “Agricultural companies,” “Food companies”). Consistent with past research (e.g., Ref. [8]), trust was lower among consequence‐insensitive opponents.

These differences are most likely both a cause and a consequence of moralization. On the cause side, perceived harm is reliably associated with increased moralization [22, 37, 38, 39]. On the consequence side, people are generally motivated to bring their perceptions of facts into line with their pre‐existing beliefs [40, 41]. This is also true for policy‐relevant factual questions [42, 43] and seems to be particularly pronounced among those who hold a strong moral belief [44]. Even when moralized beliefs are motivated by perceptions of harm, though, moral opponents often respond in ways that seem hard to justify from a purely consequence‐based perspective. Opponents of organ markets, for example, sometimes point to fears of a slippery slope leading to a devaluation of human life and a dystopian future in which human parts are routinely bought and sold. They are not convinced by counterarguments that these hypothetical future risks (1) are extremely speculative; (2) can be managed if they should occur; (3) should not outweigh the thousands of yearly deaths while people wait for donor organs [45]. Likewise, vaccine skeptics point to possible risks of vaccination (even when these are extremely implausible, physically impossible, or not supported by credible scientific evidence) and are unpersuaded by strong evidence demonstrating vaccine safety [46, 47]. Should these beliefs be considered “consequence‐based”? On the one hand, people who hold them (presumably) sincerely think that negative outcomes may occur. On the other hand, they do not seem responsive to evidence in the way one would expect of someone dispassionately weighing risks and benefits. It seems likely that once an issue has been strongly moralized, purely consequence‐based arguments will generally not change people's beliefs, even though perceived negative consequences may have originally motivated moralization.

Other Causes of Consequence‐Insensitive Opposition to GE Food

In addition to risk and benefit perceptions, we believe that there are other (nonconsequentialist) causes of consequence‐insensitive GE food opposition, including perceptions of unnaturalness, playing God, and novelty. People in developed countries have very positive views of the natural world, think of nature as benevolent, and value naturalness in products [48, 49, 50]. GE food is seen as unnatural [51], and perceptions of unnaturalness are correlated with more negative attitudes toward it. At the extreme, perceived violations of naturalness may lead to moral opposition. Relatedly, some see genetic engineering as “playing God,” (i.e., “taking on the role of some higher, metaphysical power to intervene in natural or human affairs,” Ref. [52] p. 2), and perceptions that genetically engineering food is playing God is associated with greater moral condemnation of it [52]. Finally, for a variety of crop production techniques (e.g., genetic engineering, irradiation, conventional breeding), more novel (i.e., more recently created) crops are evaluated more negatively, and growing them is rated as less moral [53]. If, when thinking of GE food, people call to mind very new crops (perhaps because they have been covered in the media), this may lead to consequence‐insensitive opposition.

GE Food Supporters

Our focus here is on GE food opponents, but the attitudes of supporters are of interest as well, especially since other research has found that some GE food supporters also moralize their attitudes [16]. All supporters in this study completed the binary‐response sacred value items as well as the continuous attitude items (risks, benefits, trust, importance, willingness to buy, desire to regulate, and activism). The results for all measures are reported in full in the Supplementary Materials, but overall, the responses for the continuous attitude items are as expected. Compared to opponents, supporters see GE food as less risky, more beneficial, are more willing to buy it, and have a lower desire to regulate it and engage in activism against it. They also trust the relevant institutions more and see the issue overall as less important.

Combining across countries, a minority of supporters agreed with each of the sacred value items (consequence insensitivity, 15.36%; quantity insensitivity, 26.26%; universalism, 17.60%; Trade‐off condemnation 1, 30.45%; Trade‐off condemnation 2, 31.84%). These items were always worded the same way regardless of whether the respondents were GE supporters or opponents, so agreement is more difficult to interpret for supporters. We think there are three distinct things that agreement with these items could mean. First, for the quantity insensitivity item, a supporter could agree without holding a sacred value (someone who thinks there is nothing wrong with a practice might also think quantity is irrelevant because twice as much of an acceptable practice is just as acceptable). Second, for the monetary price items, someone might be morally offended by putting a price on a practice that touches on sacred values, even when they do not disapprove of the practice per se (e.g., people might approve of sex between consenting adults but be morally offended by one adult buying it from another). In the current case, some GE supporters might likewise see supporting the technology as a sacred value because of its welfare benefits (see Ref. [16]) and thus might agree with these items.

Finally, for the remaining items, agreement seems contradictory with supporting GE food. Someone who approves of a practice should not want to ban it regardless of risks and benefits, nor should they think it wrong in a country where no one thinks it is wrong. For these items, agreement could reflect confusion, inconsistent attitudes, or perhaps a mistaken response on the previous support/opposition item. It is noteworthy that agreement was higher for the items where agreement for supporters is interpretable (quantity insensitivity and trade‐off condemnation), and lower for the ones where it is not (consequence insensitivity and universalism).

Comparing across countries, supporters were more likely to agree with these items in countries where opposition was higher (see Table S1). This might indicate overall greater moralization of GE food in these countries among both opponents and supporters. Alternatively, it might reflect that (given the greater prevalence of opponents in those countries) a greater proportion of “supporters” are opponents who mistakenly selected the wrong response on the support/opposition question. Finally, when interpreting these results, it is important to remember that the total number of supporters in France (n = 62) and Germany (n = 101) was quite low.

Limitations of Self‐Report

We asked participants several items about their subjective likelihood of engaging in different behaviors (e.g., their willingness to purchase a GE food, engage in GE‐related political activity, etc.). Though the use of such hypotheticals is ubiquitous in psychology and consumer behavior research, people have imperfect insight into their mental processes and future behavior [54, 55]. In the current case, people may mispredict what they would do, or they may struggle to answer a question that does not apply to them (e.g., someone who does not like chocolate might not know how to respond to a question that asks about their willingness to buy chocolate with GE ingredients). Future research should examine actual purchasing behavior, either recalled or in lab or field studies.

Conclusion

Consequence‐insensitive GE food opposition is prevalent in the United States, France, and Germany, and is higher in France and Germany than in the United States. Compared to consequence‐sensitive opponents, consequence‐insensitive opponents show many hallmarks of holding moral attitudes. In framing their public statements, policymakers and scientists must take into account that GE food is a moral issue for much of the public.
