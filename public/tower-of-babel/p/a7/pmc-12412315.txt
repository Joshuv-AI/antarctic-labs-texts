Impact of thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy on partial hepatectomy and Hassab’s operation in patients with cirrhosis: a multicenter retrospective cohort study
Authors: Qijun Yang, Jing Zhang, Yong Chen, Renren Wang, Shuhong Zhang, Yueyue Li, Yanjing Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThrombocytopenia (TCP) and coagulation disorders are common complications for patients with liver cirrhosis. The impact of severe TCP [30 × 109/L < platelets (PLT) ≤ 50 × 109/L] and coagulation disorder on bleeding risk after Hassab’s operation or partial hepatectomy (PH) for cirrhotic patients is unclear.MethodOur study retrospectively enrolled 724 cirrhotic patients from July 2019 to August 2024, including 189 patients who underwent Hassab’s operation and 535 patients with hepatocellular carcinoma who underwent PH without prophylactic platelet transfusions or thrombopoietin receptor agonists. Patients were categorized into severe TCP (30 × 109/L < PLT ≤ 50 × 109/L) and mild/moderate TCP (50 × 109/L < PLT ≤ 150 × 109/L) groups, and INR < 1.5 and INR ≥ 1.5 groups, respectively. The 6-week postoperative bleeding rate was recorded.ResultsSevere TCP or INR ≥ 1.5 has no significant effect on increased 6-week postoperative bleeding rate for patients with cirrhosis who not only underwent Hassab’s operation (p > 0.05), but also underwent PH (p > 0.05). Besides, elevated Child-Pugh score was linked with increased postoperative bleeding of Hassab’s operation (HR = 2.932, 95%CI 1.796–4.786, p < 0.001) and PH (HR = 2.140, 95%CI 1.588–2.883, p < 0.001), and creatinine (HR = 1.029, 95%CI 1.011–1.048, p = 0.002) and surgical procedure (HR = 0.388, 95%CI 0.163–0.919, p = 0.031 was another risk factor associated with postoperative bleeding rate for patients who underwent Hassab’s operation and PH separately.ConclusionBoth TCP and INR have no significant impact on increased bleeding rate after Hassab’s operation or PH for patients with cirrhosis. Child-Pugh score is a risk factor for 6-week bleeding after Hassab’s operation or PH. Additionally, creatinine is another risk factor associated with high 6-week postoperative bleeding of Hassab’s operation, and laparoscopy is a protective factor associated with 6-week postoperative bleeding of PH.

Introduction

Thrombocytopenia (TCP) is defined as condition in which platelet (PLT) count is <150 × 109/L, which has been reported in about 78% of patients with liver cirrhosis [1]. Thrombocytopenia can lead to coagulopathies through several pathways [2]. Similarly, coagulation disorders, such as international normalized ratio (INR) of ≥1.5, are highly common in cirrhotic patients [3]. Elevated INR and TCP are the indirect indicators of increased severity of cirrhosis or portal hypertension.

Splenectomy combined with pericardial devascularization, referred to as Hassab’s operation, is a traditional surgical treatment for portal hypertension [4], which contributes to a reduction of portal pressure [5]. Partial hepatectomy (PH) is another type of surgical treatment that is generally used for patients who have cirrhosis combined with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [6]. Previous studies on the effects of TCP and coagulation abnormalities on the risk of bleeding after surgery in cirrhotic patients have reported conflicting results [7]. Historically, moderate to severe TCP was generally regarded as a contraindication to liver surgery, and such patients received platelet transfusions either preoperatively or intraoperatively [7]. In patients with resectable hepatocellular [8,9], a low platelet count before treatment has been associated with a low survival rate. Moreover, the invasive nature of the surgical procedure, particularly the high risk of postoperative bleeding in cirrhotic patients, limits the clinical use of surgery in patients who have cirrhosis with thrombocytopenia and coagulopathies. In addition, INR is reported as a poor indicator of perioperative bleeding events [10]. However, several recent studies have suggested that severe TCP and coagulation disorders have no significant effect on the increased risk of postoperative bleeding in patients with cirrhosis who undergo invasive treatment [11,12].

Studies on the impact of TCP and coagulation disorders on postoperative bleeding in patients who have cirrhosis with portal hypertension have not reported any conclusive findings. More importantly, studies that have investigated the impact of severe TCP (30 × 109/L–50 × 109/L) and coagulation disorders (e.g. INR ≥ 1.5) on bleeding after Hassab’s operation are scarce. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether the postoperative bleeding rate was affected by TCP and INR. In addition, the risk factors associated with postoperative bleeding after these abdominal surgical treatments were also explored.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a multicenter cohort study, in which the data of patients with TCP who underwent surgical treatment (Hassab’s operation or PH) at three hospitals, namely, Qilu Hospital of Shandong University, Shandong Provincial Hospital affiliated with Shandong First Medical University, and Jinan Central Hospital affiliated with Shandong First Medical University, between July 2019 and August 2024, were analyzed retrospectively. The study protocol conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the research ethics committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (approval no. KYLL-202410-029-1). In addition, each institution also approved the study protocol. Since this research was a retrospective analysis, informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee from the Qilu Hospital of Shandong University.

Patients and groups

Patients who were diagnosed with cirrhotic portal hypertension or HCC based on the result of liver function tests, abdominal ultrasonography, computed tomography (CT) scans, and liver biopsy were included in this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged <18 years or >75 years; (2) patients with platelet counts >150 × 109/L or <30 × 109/L; (3) patients with incomplete coagulation tests and medical records; (4) patients who received anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy before surgery; (5) patients who received platelet transfusions or used thrombopoietic drugs or thrombopoietin after surgery; (6) patients with hematologic disease.

Patients who met the inclusion criteria, but not the exclusion criteria, were divided into two groups, namely, the severe TCP group (30 × 109/L ≤ PLT ≤ 50 × 109/L) and the mild/moderate TCP group (50 × 109/L < PLT ≤ 150 × 109/L) based on their preoperative platelet counts. The patients were also divided into an INR < 1.5 group and an INR ≥ 1.5 group based on their preoperative INR levels.

Data collection

The clinical characteristics, laboratory tests, and radiography results of the participants were obtained from the electronic medical record at the respective hospitals. The Child-Pugh score/class and model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score were calculated according to patients’ clinical characteristics and laboratory test results. In this study, the 6-week bleeding rate and mortality after surgical treatment were recorded.

Surgical treatment

Hassab’s operation

Hassab’s procedure was performed when the patient was hemodynamically stable. The operation was performed by a senior surgeon who has received specialist surgical training for over a decade. The procedure commenced with a splenectomy, followed by peripancreatic vessel dissection. Subsequent to standard splenectomy, the gastric branch and five to eight small branches of the gastric coronary vein and the esophageal branch were severed. Afterward, the posterior gastric vein and the left inferior phrenic vein were ligated. Finally, the arteries accompanying these veins, including the left gastric artery, the left gastric omental artery, the posterior gastric artery, and the left inferior phrenic artery, were ligated. Calcium heparin (1.0 mL, qd) was initiated three days after Hassab’s operation.

Partial hepatectomy

Patients with HCC underwent PH performed by senior surgeons who have received specialized surgical training for over a decade. PH was performed after hemodynamic stabilization. Two kinds of operation procedures were used for PH: laparoscopic partial hepatectomy and open partial hepatectomy. The procedures are explained in detail below.

Laparoscopic partial hepatectomy

The pneumoperitoneum was routinely established, and a laparoscope and other instruments were positioned appropriately to explore the abdominal cavity for ascites and tumor metastases. The tumor location and size were determined using fluorescence laparoscopy, ultrasonic knife resection, ligation of the hepatic round ligament, and free hepatic cystic ligament. A precut line was drawn about 3 cm along the edge of the tumor, after which the liver tissue was dissected along this precut line, and the blood vessels and bile ducts in the liver section were ligated and excised. The tumor and part of the normal liver tissue were resected together. The hepatic portal was blocked intraoperatively for 15 min. Finally, the abdominal cavity was flushed with warm distilled water and saline.

Open partial hepatectomy

After the successful application of anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position, and the splenic and renal areas were padded. Next, a sterile double-lumen urinary catheter was placed and disinfected. The right upper abdominal anti-L incision of about 18 cm in length was performed, sequentially cutting into the abdomen and exposing the liver, above the right or left liver pads. Along the tumor, about 2 cm above, a precut line was drawn to perform the excision along the precut line. Afterward, the blood vessels and bile ducts of the liver section were ligated and excised, respectively. The tumor was resected along with a part of the liver tissue and removed from the body. After confirming that there was no active bleeding during the operation, an abdominal drain was placed in the liver section.

Study endpoints and definition

The primary outcome evaluated in this study was bleeding within 6 weeks after the surgical treatment. The second outcome evaluated was death related to surgical operation or liver dysfunction during the 6-week follow-up.

Initially, TCP was defined as PLT < 150 × 109/L, severe TCP was defined as 30 × 109/L ≤ PLT ≤ 50 × 109/L, and mild/moderate TCP was defined as 50 × 109/L < PLT ≤ 150 × 109/L. Significant coagulopathy was defined as INR ≥ 1.5, consistent with the Baveno VII criteria [13]. Major bleeding was defined as bleeding in a critical region or organ, such as bleeding from a wound, intra-abdominal bleeding, or a decrease in hemoglobin by over 20 g/dL after surgery and requiring over 2 U of erythrocyte transfusion, as defined by the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis [14].

Follow-ups

The follow-ups began after the operation and continued until the sixth week. Bleeding events were estimated by reviewing the medical records of patients or phone calls with them. Patients were followed up until bleeding, death, or the end of the follow-up period.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of continuous variables was evaluated before analysis. The normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations and were compared between groups using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. The categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages (%) and were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to determine the surgery-related bleeding rates within 6 weeks after the surgical treatment, and group comparisons were performed using the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox hazard regression analyses were used to identify the independent risk factors associated with bleeding after the surgical treatment. The hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated for each risk factor. A two-tailed p-value <0.05 indicated a significant difference. All statistical analyses in this study were performed using IBM SPSS Version 26.0 and GraphPad Prism Version 9.5.1.

Results

Patient characteristics

In this study, a total of 1221 patients with cirrhosis who underwent abdominal surgery between July 2019 and August 2024 were included. Among these patients, 497 patients were excluded based on the exclusion criteria. Finally, 724 patients were included in this study. According to the type of surgery, 189 (26.10%) patients with LC and esophageal variceal bleeding who underwent Hassab’s operation were included in cohort 1, and 535 (73.90%) patients with LC and HCC who underwent PH were included in cohort 2 (Figure 1). Among all patients in cohort 2, 418 (78.13%) patients who underwent laparoscopic surgery, whereas the other 117 (21.89%) patients who underwent an open procedure. The majority of patients were diagnosed with HBV, including 85 patients (44.97%) in cohort 1 and 439 patients (82.06%) in cohort 2. Overall, about 99.72% of all patients were in the Child-Pugh classes A and B.

Flow diagram of this study.

Patients were initially stratified into two groups according to platelet count (Table 1). In cohort 1, there was a significant difference in the PLT between the two groups [71.50 (28.25) vs. 41.00 (11.00), p < 0.001]. The indicators of coagulation in LC patients in the severe TCP group were worse than those in the mild/moderate TCP group (PT-S: p = 0.001, INR: p < 0.001, PT%: p = 0.001). Patients with lower platelet counts had significantly lower AKP (p = 0.046) along with higher TBIL (p = 0.03) and MELD scores (p = 0.03). In cohort 2, the median platelet count was significantly different between the two groups of patients [106.00 (43.00) vs. 41.00 (12.00), p < 0.001]. Similar to the LC patients, patients with LC and HCC who had a lower platelet count presented significantly worse coagulation (PT-S: p < 0.001, INR: p < 0.001, PT%: p < 0.001, FIB: p = 0.004). Additionally, the patients in the 30 × 109/L < PLT ≤ 50 × 109/L group had significantly lower RBC (p = 0.003), HGB (p < 0.001), and GGT (p < 0.031) and higher TBIL (p = 0.04), Child-Pugh (p = 0.001), and MELD (p < 0.001) scores than those in the PLT > 50 × 109/L group.

Bleeding rate and mortality within 6 weeks after surgery

In this study, 14 (7.41%) patients who underwent Hassab’s operation in cohort 1 and 22 (4.11%) patients who underwent PH in cohort 2 experienced intra-abdominal bleeding or gastrointestinal bleeding within 6 weeks after surgery. Most of them received plasma transfusions due to the considerable reduction in hemoglobin level. Totally, only three patients died (0.41%), unfortunately, during 6-week follow-up in the two cohorts. One patient in cohort 1 died on the 35th day after Hassab’s operation due to gastrointestinal bleeding caused by portal vein thrombosis. In cohort 2, one patient died on the twenty-fifth day after PH due to an unidentified reason, while the other patient died as a result of some infection and bleeding related to postoperative complications after PH during the 6-week follow-up.

Table 2 shows that there was no significant difference between the two PLT groups in terms of the bleeding rate within 6 weeks after Hassab’s operation for patients in cohort 1 (p > 0.05). LC patients with severe TCP underwent Hassab’s operation had a slightly greater postoperative bleeding rate (8.0%) than those with mild/moderate TCP (7.0%), however, the difference in the postoperative bleeding rate during the 6-week follow-up was not significant between the two groups (p = 0.785). Similarly, platelet count reduction had no significant effect on postoperative bleeding within 6 weeks in patients with LC&HCC underwent PH (p = 0.151). This finding was confirmed by the results of the Kaplan-Meier analysis, as shown in Figure 2A (log-rank p = 0.770) and Figure 2B (log-rank p = 0.104).

In terms of the postoperative bleeding rate between the INR groups, although, the postoperative bleeding rate within 6 weeks after Hassab’s operation between two groups was not different significantly in cohort 1 (p = 0.378), as shown in Table 2, the bleeding rate of patients who received Hassab’s operation in the INR ≥ 1.5 group showed a slightly lower trend (6.4%) than those patients in the INR < 1.5 group (18.3%). Moreover, Kaplan-Meier analysis (Figure 2C) revealed a similar trend (log-rank p = 0.066). Additionally, for patients with LC and HCC, this association was also found in cohort 2. Table 2 and Figure 2D showed a slightly higher postoperative bleeding rate in the INR ≥ 1.5 group than those in the INR < 1.5 group, however, no significant difference was detected statistically (p = 0.19).

Risk factors for bleeding within 6 weeks after surgery

According to univariate analysis (results shown in Table 3), higher TBIL (p = 0.008), Cr (p = 0.0335), Child-Pugh (p < 0.001), and MELD scores (p = 0.001), and more severe coagulation disorders, such as INR (p = 0.027) and PT-S (p = 0.045), promoted the risk of bleeding risk within 6 weeks after Hassab’s operation. After adjusting for variables, Cr (HR = 1.029, 95% CI 1.011–1.048, p = 0.002) and the Child-Pugh score (HR = 2.932, 95% CI 1.796–4.786, p < 0.001) were revealed as the risk factors associated with a greater risk of postoperative bleeding within 6 weeks after Hassab’s operation according to the univariate and multivariate analyses.

Discussion

This study revealed that neither severe TCP (30 × 109/L < PLT ≤ 50 × 109/L) nor an INR ≥ 1.5 had a significant effect on the increased rate of postoperative bleeding within 6 weeks after surgery in cirrhotic patients who underwent Hassab’s operation or PH. Apart from PLT and INR, an increased Child-Pugh score was revealed as a risk factor associated with high postoperative bleeding within 6 weeks after Hassab’s operation or PH in patients with cirrhosis. Moreover, high creatinine levels and the need for laparotomy were other risk factors contributing to elevated postoperative bleeding within 6 weeks after Hassab’s operation and PH, respectively.

This study showed that the postoperative bleeding rate within 6 weeks after surgery was not significantly greater in patients in the severe TCP group than those patients in the mild/moderate TCP group. Previous studies concluded that PLT ≤ 50 × 109/L did not have a significantly higher postoperative bleeding rate [11,15] in cirrhotic patients with HCC undergoing surgical treatment, such as liver resection and radiofrequency ablation. Hassab’s operation has been widely used to prevent rebleeding of esophageal varices caused by portal hypertension in cirrhotic patients [5,16]. Many clinical studies have provided some evidence regarding the rate of rebleeding from esophageal varices and the associated mortality after Hassab’s operation [17–19]. However, no relevant studies have reported the effect of TCP on bleeding after Hassab’s operation. The present study revealed that severe TCP did not affect bleeding after Hassab’s operation.

In addition, only in patients with liver cirrhosis, several clinical studies have indicated that there was no significant difference in postoperative bleeding of invasive treatment between INR ≥ 1.5 and INR < 1.5, such as TIPS, TACE, endoscopic variceal ligation, and Liver biopsy [20,21]. However, studies that have investigated the impact of coagulation disorder on postoperative bleeding of surgical treatment for cirrhotic patients with HCC, especially those who undergo hepatectomy are scarce. Therefore, our study suggested that INR ≥ 1.5 may not increase post-procedural bleeding in cirrhotic patients undergoing surgical treatment, and our results provide strong evidence for surgeon that serve TCP or INR ≥ 1.5 cannot increase the postoperative bleeding rate within 6 weeks after Hassab’s operation or PH for cirrhotic patients.

Above all, the results of this study provide strong evidence supporting that PLT ≤ 50 × 109/L or INR ≥ 1.5 cannot increase the postoperative bleeding rate within 6 weeks after Hassab’s operation or PH for patients with cirrhosis. Apart from PLT and INR, some clinical studies demonstrate that diminished liver function (e.g. elevated MELD and Child-Pugh scores) is associated with an elevated risk of bleeding associated with esophageal variceal band ligation in cirrhotic patients with portal hypertension [22,23]. Moreover, several indicators linked with the Child-Pugh score, including elevated TBIL and prolonged PT, have been shown to be associated with an increased risk of bleeding in cirrhotic patients undergoing invasive treatments for portal hypertension [2,24]. However, the findings of our study suggested that an elevated Child-Pugh score should be regarded as a risk factor associated with high rate of bleeding for not only cirrhotic patients who underwent Hassab’s operation, but also patients with LC&HCC who underwent PH. And creatinine could be another risk factor leading to an increased bleeding rate after Hassab’s operation in cirrhotic patients, which could be related to uremic platelet dysfunction due to toxin accumulation, reduced clearance of heparin-like substances leading to hypocoagulability, or synergistic decline in both hepatic coagulation factor synthesis and renal metabolic function. However, the relative study is scarce.

Given the influence of surgical maneuver on the postoperative bleeding in patients with PH, the present clinical study indicated that laparoscopy poses a lower risk of postoperative bleeding comparing to laparotomy for LC&HCC patients undergoing PH, This observation aligns with previous clinical research demonstrating that laparoscopic surgery offers notable advantages over laparotomy in patients with LC and HCC, such as reduced intraoperative blood loss, a lower rate of postoperative complications and a significantly shorter hospital stay [25] due to its small incision. Morise et al. also demonstrated that laparoscopic liver resection has been shown to be an efficient operation for reducing blood loss and postoperative complications, especially ascites and liver failure, for HCC patients with chronic liver disease who underwent PH [26]. Moreover, compared with laparotomy, laparoscopic liver resection is a safe and feasible surgical operation for LC&HCC patients with child-Pugh B, which contributes to a significantly lower intraoperative blood loss, fewer major complications, and lower morbidity [27]. Similarly, a prediction mode relating postoperative risk and survival for LC&HCC patients with Child-Pugh B suggested that patients who underwent hepatectomy had a higher rate of morbidity, morbidity and postoperative complications than those undergoing minimally invasive resection [28]. However, the number of studies concerning the relationship between the surgical approach and postoperative bleeding rate is limited warranting further research.

However, this study also has some limitations that should be considered. First, the evaluation of the correlation between coagulation and the perioperative bleeding rate might be limited by the small sample size of the INR ≥ 1.5 group in this study. Second, the majority of patients in the study cohort were categorized in Child-Pugh classes A and B, which means that the study findings may not be generalizable for patients with poor liver function. Third, it is challenging to ascertain whether PLT ≥ 30 × 109/L can be utilized as a safe cutoff value for postoperative bleeding because of the absence of data on patients with PLT < 30 × 109/L, which restricted the analysis of their bleeding risk in this study. Further studies with more patients having PLT < 30 × 109/L or INR ≥ 1.5, who have undergone surgical treatment for liver cirrhosis, are required to investigate the effects of severe TCP and coagulation disorders on postoperative bleeding risk.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study is the first work to suggest that severe TCP or INR ≥ 1.5 is not associated with an increased postoperative bleeding rate in liver cirrhosis patients who undergo Hassab’s operation. Moreover, the postoperative bleeding rate of the PH is not affected by PLT or INR for patients with cirrhosis and HCC.
