Transoral Robotic Resection of Glossopharyngeal Neurofibroma: A Case Report
Authors: Cody L Messick, Austin R Swisher, Hannah M Gibbs, Michael T Sramek, Laura Klusovsky, Payam Entezami, James S Lewis, Brent A Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by rare oropharyngeal neurofibromas, their potential to mimic other conditions, and the novel application of transoral robotic surgery (TORS) for the management of an oropharyngeal neurofibroma. A 47‐year‐old female presented with a 1‐year history of a left tonsillar mass. Clinical evaluation, imaging, and biopsy results were inconclusive but raised suspicion for a neoplasm. The patient underwent robotic‐assisted tonsillectomy and mass resection. Subsequent histopathological evaluation confirmed a benign spindle cell tumor consistent with neurofibroma. Oropharyngeal neurofibromas may present diagnostic challenges due to their resemblance to common tonsillar pathologies. Imaging and biopsies may not always provide definitive diagnoses, necessitating surgical excision for accurate characterization. This case demonstrates the utility of TORS, offering improved visualization and access while minimizing patient morbidity.Keywords: nerve sheath myxoma, neurofibroma, obstructive sleep apnea, tonsillar mass, transoral robotic surgery

Introduction

Neurofibromas are benign nerve sheath tumors commonly associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), but also present sporadically along the course of peripheral nerves. Cutaneous neurofibromas are primarily located in the trunk (60%), in the limbs (21%), and in the head and neck region (< 10%) [1]. Limited descriptions of solitary oropharyngeal neurofibromas exist in the literature, with some cases affecting the palatine tonsil [2, 3, 4, 5]. However, none utilized TORS for management. The clinical presentation is varied, but when affecting the oropharynx, patients may have symptoms of dyspnea, dysphagia, odynophagia, dysphonia, or obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) [6]. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by oropharyngeal neurofibromas, which may mimic other conditions and lead to delayed treatment. We also demonstrate the clinical utility of TORS in managing oropharyngeal neurofibromas.

Presentation

A 47‐year‐old female presented to our outpatient clinic with a 1‐year complaint of a left tonsillar mass. Her past medical history was notable for OSA, recurrent tonsillitis as a child, and halitosis with tonsilloliths that persisted into adulthood. The patient had no complaints of dyspnea, dysphagia, odynophagia, or dysphonia. A prior outside computed tomography (CT) scan showed a well‐circumscribed and homogenous hypoattenuating mass in the left tonsil with benign morphology on biopsy. Flexible laryngoscopy of the upper airway confirmed the left tonsillar asymmetry but also revealed a firm, mass‐like component of the visualized tonsil, which was suspicious for cancer. Repeat CT with contrast demonstrated an unchanged rounded hypoattenuating mass in the left pharyngeal mucosal space measuring 25 × 30 × 30 mm (Figure 1).

Computed tomography scan taken same week as procedure with white arrow showing 25 × 30 × 30 mm, rounded, hypoattenuating, left pharyngeal mucosal space mass (white arrow).

The patient underwent tonsillectomy via TORS with the DaVinci SP robot (Intuitive Surgical Inc., Sunnyvale, California) with a Crowe‐David mouth gag, Maryland bipolar forceps arm, and a monopolar cautery arm. Removal of the left palatine tonsil was performed by incision along the anterior tonsillar pillar and dissection from the underlying constrictor muscle. As dissection progressed inferiorly, a separate smooth, encapsulated mass was visualized extending toward the submandibular gland. The bulk of the mass favored a capsular dissection approach using robotic resection, which enabled dynamic visualization of regions otherwise obscured from the direct line of sight and enhanced surgical dexterity. The tonsil was removed, and the mass dissected without capsular rupture (Figure 2). Frozen section pathology showed a benign spindle cell lesion. The right tonsil was removed for indications independent of the left tonsillar mass, and the patient was discharged. Total blood loss was estimated at 10 mL. Permanent pathology results of the left tonsillar mass showed a spindle cell neoplasm with “carrot shred”‐type collagen and central cystic change consistent with a 31 mm neurofibroma (Figure 3A) with SOX‐10 immunostaining (Figure 3B) confirming the diagnosis. No densely fascicular or infiltrative growth, nuclear pleomorphism, or mitotic activity (0 per 2 mm2) was identified. The tonsillar tissue lacked any histopathologic abnormalities. Specialized neurophysiologic testing was not performed for practical reasons, but careful history and clinical examination allowed confident assessment of lower cranial nerve function. At 9‐month follow‐up, there were no deficits in cranial nerves 9, 10, or 11, with preserved taste, no throat pain, dysphagia, or speech disturbance. A postoperative image is shown in Figure 4.

(A) Hematoxylin and eosin‐stained pathology slides of the tonsillar mass showing bland, hyperchromatic, wavy spindle cells with carrot shred‐type collagen; (B) SOX‐10 immunostaining showing strong extensive positive staining of most of the tumor cell nuclei (10× magnification) supporting the diagnosis of Neurofibroma.

Discussion

This case illustrates how a rare oropharyngeal neurofibroma can mimic the clinical presentation of common tonsillar conditions, thereby leaving the patient with an untreated mass for over a year. Her only pertinent presenting symptom for neurofibroma was OSA, without dyspnea, dysphagia, odynophagia, or dysphonia. The contrast difference between the mass and right tonsil on initial imaging did not exclude more common tonsillar diagnoses. Although magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was not obtained in this case, it is recommended when neurofibroma is suspected, as it is more sensitive than CT for distinguishing benign from malignant neurofibromas and aids in planning a low‐morbidity surgical approach [7]. In clinical practice, persistent oropharyngeal or retropharyngeal masses in the correct context should prompt consideration of neurofibromas and definitive diagnosis via surgical pathology. TORS may serve as a valuable tool in the surgical management of these lesions. Additionally, since neurofibromas can recur years later, longer‐term follow‐up is necessary. The absence of trigeminal sensory deficit with preserved vocal fold motion and palatal elevation suggests a glossopharyngeal origin, though this conclusion remains inferential. Glossopharyngeal nerve fibers are regularly encountered in this location during TORS cancer resections and provide the predominant sensory innervation [8].

Definitive management of neurofibroma requires surgical removal. While recurrence rates are cited at less than 20% with total or near‐total resection, rates of recurrence tend to be higher in the head and neck region [6]. The decision of surgery versus serial imaging must be weighed against the patient's symptomatic burden. Retained neurofibromas carry risks for malignant transformation, persistent discomfort, airway obstruction, sleep apnea, and reduced quality of life, as in our patient. Compared with open approaches in the oropharynx, TORS has yielded shorter hospital stays, reduced operative time and blood loss, and significantly fewer positive margins [9, 10]. While transoral microscopic approaches are useful, they would likely add complexity in this case due to direct line‐of‐sight requirements. TORS improved visualization and instrumentation of the tumor's inferior and deep aspect, facilitating neurovasculature protection and enabling en bloc resection.
