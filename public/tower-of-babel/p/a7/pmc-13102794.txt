Clinical Trial Comparison of Dupilumab and Nemolizumab for Prurigo Nodularis FDA-Approved Biologic Therapy Selection in Prurigo Nodularis
Authors: Anna Robertson, Craig Rohan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrurigo nodularis (PN) is a chronic, pruritic skin disorder characterized by significant morbidity and historically limited treatment options. The recent approval of biologic therapies has rapidly changed the treatment landscape, with dupilumab and nemolizumab now Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved for PN. This article provides a clinically relevant overview of completed randomized trials, highlighting differences in efficacy, timing of response, and symptom targets. Data were extracted from six compound randomized clinical trials (two dupilumab trials and four nemolizumab trials) identified on ClinicalTrials.gov through January 2025. Across phase 3 studies, dupilumab demonstrated sustained reductions in itch severity and lesional disease, with approximately 58–60% of patients achieving a ≥ 4-point improvement in Worst Itch Numeric Rating Scale (WI-NRS) scores and 45–48% achieving Investigator Global Assessment for Prurigo Nodularis–Stage (IGA PN-S) success by week 24. Nemolizumab showed a more rapid onset of antipruritic effect, with 41–56% of patients achieving a ≥ 4-point improvement in Peak Pruritus Numeric Rating Scale (PP-NRS) scores as early as week 4, along with clinically meaningful improvements in sleep disturbance by week 16. Both therapies demonstrated manageable safety profiles, with predominantly mild to moderate adverse events and low rates of serious adverse events, although nemolizumab was associated with higher overall rates of mild to moderate adverse events. These findings suggest that dupilumab may offer greater lesional clearance at later time points, whereas nemolizumab may provide faster itch relief and less sleep disturbance. These findings support a phenotype-guided approach to biologic selection while highlighting the need for further comparative studies.

Key Points

Dupilumab and nemolizumab are the first biologic therapies approved for prurigo nodularis and have significantly expanded treatment options for patients with severe disease.Clinical trial data suggest that nemolizumab may provide faster relief of itch and sleep disturbance, while dupilumab may offer more consistent improvement in skin lesions over time.Considering dominant symptoms such as itch severity, sleep disruption, and lesion burden may help clinicians choose between these biologic therapies.

Introduction

Prurigo nodularis (PN) is a chronic inflammatory skin condition characterized by intensely pruritic nodules lasting ≥ 6 weeks that is notoriously difficult to treat [1, 2]. Nodules are often symmetric and numerous. Until the past several years, PN was grouped with other pruritic conditions. It is now recognized as a distinct diagnosis, with the establishment of a unique billing code following the adoption of the International Classification of Diseases Version 10 (ICD-10) Clinical Modification coding system in the USA on 1 October 2015 [3]. It is estimated that PN affects around 72 per 100,000 people in the USA [2]. There is some evidence that PN disproportionately affects the Medicare population and older adults, with higher prevalence among women, and may be up to three times more common in Black patients [3]. Globally, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis estimated a pooled prevalence of approximately 0.08% in the general population, based primarily on registry-based studies from Europe and the USA, with higher prevalence observed in clinically examined cohorts, suggesting potential underestimation of disease burden [4]. This article provides an overview of clinical trial data for dupilumab and nemolizumab, the two biologics currently Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved for the treatment of PN and examines their roles within the evolving therapeutic landscape.

The pathophysiology of PN remains under investigation, though recent insights suggest both neural and immune dysregulation play key roles [5]. PN can develop after arthropod assault, new medications, diabetes, or peripheral neuropathy. It is especially common in patients with end-stage renal disease and those undergoing dialysis, where reactive perforating collagenases are proposed to be on a spectrum that includes PN [6]. Chronic pruritus in PN likely follows a non-histaminergic pathway involving cowhage-activated epidermal mechano-sensitive and heat-sensitive C fibers [7]. Despite decreased intraepidermal nerve fiber density, patients with PN exhibit increased epidermal nerve branching and overexpression of nerve regeneration genes, suggesting chronic scratching drives nerve injury and sprouting [8]. Scratching releases cytokines that amplify type 2 inflammation and nerve sensitization [9]. Patients with PN exhibit varying degrees of peripheral sensitization in cutaneous nerve fibers that transmit pruritic sensations [7, 9]. With sensitization, a lower threshold stimulus is needed to produce a response, and this persists longer after stimulus removal. Type 2 inflammation is a key driver of pruritus, with IL-4, IL-13, and IL-31 identified as the primary cytokines involved [10]. They signal through Janus kinase (JAK)–signal transducer and activator of transcription protein (STAT) pathways, reinforcing PN’s chronic itch [11]. While current biologic therapies target key type 2 inflammatory cytokines, ongoing research on PN pathophysiology may allow for more precise therapeutic targeting through improved characterization of disease phenotypes and the identification of additional neuroimmune mechanisms, especially for patients with refractory disease.

PN causes a high disease burden due to severe and prolonged pruritus and chronic pain, leading to impaired quality of life, disrupted sleep, and high rates of medical comorbidities [1, 2, 12–15]. Greater pruritus severity and disease burden in PN are associated with more refractory disease, characterized by prolonged treatment courses and persistent symptoms despite therapy [16]. PN is associated with higher rates of anxiety and depression, particularly in female patients [1, 2, 13, 15, 17]. It has been associated with increased IL-6 and decreased serotonin, which may contribute to decreased sleep and depressive symptoms [18]. When compared with other pruritic conditions, including atopic dermatitis and psoriasis, PN is associated with a significantly greater comorbidity burden [2, 15].

The International Forum for the Study of Itch (IFSI) and the American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) recommend a multimodal, stepwise approach, typically combining skin-directed therapies (emollients and topical anti-inflammatories), adjunctive antipruritic agents, and escalation to systemic immunomodulators or neuromodulators for severe or refractory disease [19–22]. Newer biologic therapies are expanding treatment options, particularly for patients with difficult-to-control symptoms.

Dupilumab was the first monoclonal antibody approved for PN, receiving FDA approval in 2022. It targets type 2 inflammation by binding to the IL-4 receptor alpha subunit (IL-4Rα), blocking IL-4 and IL-13 signaling [23]. Nemolizumab, approved on 13 August 2024 offers an alternative approach by binding to the IL-31 receptor alpha chain (IL-31Rα), inhibiting IL-31-induced inflammation [24]. While both drugs target type 2 inflammation, they act on different points of this immune pathway. The aim of this study is to review and compare the clinical trial data supporting the two FDA-approved biologic therapies for prurigo nodularis, with the goal of informing treatment selection in clinical practice.

Methods

All clinical trial data were obtained from ClinicalTrials.gov, which was chosen because it directly reflects FDA-relevant outcomes. The search was conducted in January 2025, and trials published after this date were not included. This review includes only completed clinical trials with available results for dupilumab and nemolizumab in the treatment of prurigo nodularis. Trials in progress or lacking full data reports were excluded. Search terms included “prurigo nodularis,” “nemolizumab,” and “dupilumab.” A total of six trials are included, two dupilumab trials and four nemolizumab trials. This review is a narrative synthesis of the data from each completed clinical trial.

Clinical Trial Results of Dupilumab

LIBERTY-PN PRIME (NCT04183335)

The LIBERTY-PN PRIME study (2019–2022) evaluated the efficacy of dupilumab in reducing pruritus in 151 patients with PN. Eligible participants were 18–80 years old, had PN for at least 2 months, a Worst-Itch Numeric Rating Scale (WI-NRS) score of ≥ 7, at least 20 lesions, and had failed a 2-week course of medium to super-potent topical corticosteroids (TCS) or were not medically advised to initiate TCS. All participants had applied topical emollients at least 5 days per week prior to study enrollment. Patients were randomized to receive either dupilumab (600 mg loading dose followed by 300 mg every 2 weeks for 24 weeks) or placebo, both in combination with background therapy of TCS or topical calcineurin inhibitors at a stable dose.

The primary outcome analyzed the percentage of participants achieving ≥ 4-point reduction in WI-NRS by week 24. A significantly higher proportion of patients treated with dupilumab (60.0%) achieved this outcome compared with placebo (18.4%). Additionally, ≥ 50% of patients in the dupilumab group met this threshold by week 13. The study also assessed skin clearance using the Investigator’s Global Assessment for Prurigo Nodularis-Stage (IGA PN-S), a 5-point scale evaluating the number and thickness of PN lesions. By week 24, 48.0% of the dupilumab group achieved an IGA PN-S score of 0 or 1 (clear or almost clear skin), compared with 18.4% in the placebo group. The combined endpoint, defined as achieving both WI-NRS improvement of ≥ 4 and an IGA PN-S score of 0 or 1, was met by 38.7% of patients treated with dupilumab versus 9.2% of those receiving placebo. All results were statistically significant. In the treatment group, serious adverse events occurred in 6.67% of participants, and 18.67% of patients experienced mild to moderate adverse events.

PRIME 2 (NCT04202679)

The PRIME 2 study (2020–2021) evaluated the efficacy of dupilumab in reducing pruritus in 160 patients with PN. The eligibility criteria was the same as LIBERTY-PN PRIME. Patients were randomized to receive either dupilumab (600 mg loading dose followed by 300 mg every 2 weeks for 24 weeks) or placebo, both in combination with background therapy consisting of previously established TCS or topical calcineurin inhibitors at a stable dose.

The primary outcome was the percentage of participants achieving ≥ 4-point reduction in WI-NRS by week 24. A significantly higher proportion of patients treated with dupilumab (57.7%) achieved this outcome compared with placebo (19.5%). Additionally, at week 12, 37.2% of the dupilumab group achieved a ≥ 4-point reduction versus 22.0% in the placebo group. The study also assessed response rates over time, showing that ≥ 50% of participants in the dupilumab group reached a ≥ 4-point WI-NRS reduction by week 19. The study further evaluated skin clearance using IGA PN-S. By week 24, 44.9% of the dupilumab group achieved an IGA PN-S score of 0 or 1 (clear or almost clear skin), compared with 15.9% in the placebo group. The combined endpoint, defined as achieving both WI-NRS improvement of ≥ 4 and an IGA PN-S score of 0 or 1, was met by 32.1% of patients treated with dupilumab versus 8.5% of those receiving placebo. All results were statistically significant. In the treatment group, serious adverse events occurred in 2.60% of participants and 14.29% of patients experienced mild to moderate adverse events.

Clinical Trial Results of Nemolizumab

A Study to Assess the Safety and Efficacy of Nemolizumab (CD14152) in Subjects with Prurigo Nodularis (NCT03181503)

This study (2017-2018) evaluated the safety and efficacy of nemolizumab in 70 participants. Eligible participants were ≥18 years old and had had PN for at least 6 months, at least 20 lesions, a Peak Pruritus Numeric Rating Scale (PP-NRS) score of ≥ 7 three days before the screening visit, and a PP-NRS score of ≥ 7 the week before the baseline visit. Female subjects of childbearing potential were required to agree to true abstinence or use of effective contraception throughout the clinical trial and for at least 120 days after the last dose. Patients were randomized to receive either nemolizumab (0.5 mg/kg injections every 4 weeks for 12 weeks) or a matched placebo.

The primary outcome measured was the percent change from baseline in the weekly average of the PP-NRS score at each visit using the Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) approach. The PP-NRS score assesses the intensity of itching from 0 (no itch) to 10 (worst itch imaginable). The nemolizumab group showed significant improvement, with a 52.6% reduction from baseline by week 4. By week 18, the treatment group achieved a 59.6% reduction from baseline. In contrast, the placebo group did not exceed a 50% reduction, achieving only a 21.7% reduction by week 18. The study also assessed the Prurigo Activity Score (PAS) at week 12, which evaluates lesion characteristics, distribution, and activity. In the nemolizumab group, the PAS score improved from a baseline mean of 84.9 to 54.8 at week 12. The placebo group showed a smaller improvement, from a baseline mean of 87.1 to 74.2 at week 12. The Investigator Global Assessment (IGA) score was used to evaluate disease severity on a 5-point scale, with 0 indicating clear skin and 4 indicating severe disease. By week 18, the nemolizumab group achieved a mean IGA score of 2, while the placebo group had a mean score of 3. The study also assessed the percentage of patients achieving IGA Success (defined as an IGA score of 0 or 1 with a 2-point improvement). In the nemolizumab group, 20.6% of participants achieved IGA Success by week 12, compared with only 2.8% in the placebo group. In the treatment group, serious adverse events occurred in 2.19% of participants and mild to moderate side effects occurred in 15.85% of patients.

A Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Study to Assess the Efficacy and Safety of Nemolizumab (CD14152) in Subjects with Prurigo Nodularis (OLYMPIA 1) (NCT04501666)

Olympia 1 (2020-2023) evaluated the efficacy and safety of nemolizumab monotherapy in patients with moderate to severe PN. A total of 286 participants were enrolled, with eligibility criteria including diagnosed PN for at least 6 months with at least 20 lesions with bilateral distribution and IGA score ≥ 3, a PP-NRS score of ≥ 7 one day before screening visit, a PP-NRS score of ≥ 7 one week before baseline visit. Female participants of childbearing potential agreed to at least one approved method of contraception throughout the study and for 12 weeks after the last injection. Participants were randomized to receive either nemolizumab or a placebo. For the treatment group, patients weighing < 90 kg received an initial dose of 60 mg of nemolizumab followed by 30 mg every 4 weeks, while patients weighing ≥ 90 kg received 60 mg every 4 weeks. The placebo group received matching placebo injections.

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 4-point reduction from baseline in the PP-NRS at week 4 and week 16. The PP NRS scale measures pruritus intensity on a 0–10 scale, with 0 indicating no itch and 10 indicating the worst imaginable itch. At week 4, 41.4% of patients in the treatment group achieved a ≥ 4-point reduction, compared with only 6.3% in the placebo group. At week 16, 56.3% of the treatment group reported a ≥ 4-point reduction, while 20.9% of the placebo group achieved the same result. Both differences were statistically significant. The study also assessed the percentage of participants achieving a PP-NRS score of < 2 at weeks 4 and 16. At week 4, 21.6% of the treatment group achieved a PP-NRS score of < 2, compared wiht 1.0% in the placebo group. By week 16, 34.2% of the treatment group had a PP NRS score of < 2, while only 4.2% of the placebo group achieved the same. In addition, the study evaluated the proportion of patients with a ≥ 4-point reduction in the Sleep Disturbance Numeric Rating Scale (SD-NRS) at weeks 4 and 16. The SD-NRS assesses the degree of sleep loss related to pruritus on a 0–10 scale, with 0 indicating no sleep loss and 10 indicating complete sleep loss due to symptoms. At week 4, 31.1% of the treatment group achieved a ≥ 4-point reduction, compared with 5.2% in the placebo group. At week 16, 50.0% of the treatment group achieved a ≥ 4-point reduction, compared with 11.5% in the placebo group. All results were statistically significant. In the treatment group, 2.19% of participants experienced serious adverse events, and 15.85% reported mild to moderate adverse events.

A Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Study to Assess the Efficacy and Safety of Nemolizumab (CD14152) in Subjects with Prurigo Nodularis (OLYMPIA 2) (NCT04501679)

Olympia 2 (2020-2024) assessed the efficacy of nemolizumab in patients with PN over a 16-week treatment period. A total of 274 participants were enrolled, with 183 in the nemolizumab group and 91 in the placebo group. Eligibility criteria was the same as in OLYMPIA 1. The study used a weight-based dosing approach, with patients weighing < 90 kg receiving 30 mg of nemolizumab at baseline followed by 30 mg every 4 weeks, and patients ≥ 90 kg receiving 30 mg at baseline followed by 60 mg every 4 weeks.

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients achieving a ≥ 4-point improvement from baseline in the weekly average PP-NRS score at week 16. In the nemolizumab group, 56.3% of participants achieved this reduction, compared with 20.9% in the placebo group. Another key outcome was the proportion of patients achieving IGA success, defined as a score of 0 or 1 (clear or almost clear skin) and a ≥ 2-point reduction from baseline. In the nemolizumab group, 37.7% of participants reached IGA success by week 16, compared with 11.0% in the placebo group. Additionally, the study assessed the Sleep Disturbance Numeric Rating Scale (SD-NRS) to measure the degree of sleep disturbance due to PN symptoms. At week 16, 51.9% of the nemolizumab group reported a ≥ 4-point reduction in SD NRS, compared with 20.9% in the placebo group. All results were statistically significant. In the treatment group, serious adverse events occurred in 8.56% of participants and other mild to moderate adverse events were reported in 32.62% of patients.

A Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled, Randomized Study to Assess the Durability of Effect and Safety of Nemolizumab for 24 Weeks in Subjects with Prurigo Nodularis (NCT05052983)

This study (2021–2023) assessed the durability of response and safety of nemolizumab in patients with PN who previously demonstrated a clinical response during long-term nemolizumab treatment and were subsequently enrolled in a randomized withdrawal trial. A total of 34 participants were enrolled, all of whom had achieved IGA success (score of 0 or 1) and ≥ 4-point improvement in weekly average PP-NRS following uninterrupted nemolizumab treatment before randomization. Of the patients, 18 received 30 mg subcutaneous nemolizumab every 4 weeks for 24 weeks, and 16 received placebo.

This study examined the percentage of participants maintaining IGA success at each scheduled visit, defined as an IGA score of 0 or 1. At week 24, the treatment group had 14 participants and 85.7% with IGA success, and the placebo group had 5 participants and 60% with IGA success. This result was statistically significant. In the treatment group, serious adverse events occurred in 11.11% of participants and 61.11% experienced mild to moderate adverse events.

Discussion

Clinical trial data show that both dupilumab and nemolizumab are effective treatments for prurigo nodularis. Each agent significantly reduces itch severity, improves skin lesions, and enhances overall disease outcomes. Both medications demonstrate manageable safety profiles, with most adverse events being mild to moderate. Across trials, dupilumab was more commonly associated with injection-site reactions and conjunctivitis, whereas nemolizumab showed injection-site reactions and gastrointestinal symptoms.

Comparing the two agents reveals important differences in clinical response. Nemolizumab appears to provide more rapid symptom relief, with 41.4% to >50% of participants achieving a ≥ 4-point improvement in Peak Pruritus Numeric Rating Scale (PP-NRS) as early as week 4. In contrast, ≥ 50% of dupilumab-treated participants reached this level of improvement in Worst Itch Numeric Rating Scale (WI-NRS) by week 16 or 19. Dupilumab may offer more substantial improvement in lesional clearance, as an average of 46.4% of participants across both phase 3 trials achieved Investigator Global Assessment for Prurigo Nodularis-Stage (IGA PN-S) success by week 24, compared with 20.6–37.7% reported with nemolizumab. Sleep disturbance data further differentiate these therapies, as sleep outcomes were not assessed in dupilumab trials, whereas more than 50% of patients treated with nemolizumab achieved ≥ 4-point reductions in Sleep Disturbance Numeric Rating Scale (SD-NRS) scores by week 16. Taken together, these findings suggest that nemolizumab may provide faster itch relief and sleep improvement, while dupilumab may offer more robust lesion clearance. Head-to-head studies are needed to clarify the relative strengths of each agent and guide individualized treatment selection.

The differences in clinical response between dupilumab and nemolizumab are biologically plausible given their distinct mechanisms of action. Nemolizumab targets the IL-31 receptor, a cytokine closely linked to neuronal itch signaling, which may help explain the rapid improvements in pruritus and sleep disturbance seen as early as week 4 across multiple trials [25]. In contrast, dupilumab inhibits IL-4 and IL-13 signaling earlier in the type 2 inflammatory pathway, processes involved not only in itch but also in chronic inflammation, tissue remodeling, and fibrosis [23]. This broader anti-inflammatory effect may underlie the greater improvements in lesional clearance observed with dupilumab at later time points. Taken together, these mechanistic differences suggest that IL-31 blockade may be particularly effective for neurogenic itch, whereas IL-4/13 inhibition may better address the chronic inflammatory drivers of persistent nodular disease.

From a clinical perspective, the indirect comparison of trial outcomes suggests that dupilumab and nemolizumab may address different features of PN. Patients whose disease is characterized by severe, sleep-disrupting itch may experience earlier symptomatic relief with IL-31 inhibition using nemolizumab, given its rapid effects on itch and sleep. In contrast, patients with extensive, long-standing, or highly nodular disease may benefit from IL-4/13 blockade with dupilumab, which demonstrated greater lesional clearance over longer treatment durations. While no validated biomarkers currently guide biologic selection in PN, these patterns suggest that clinical features could reasonably inform initial therapy choice. This supports a more intentional, phenotype-guided approach to selecting between currently approved biologics, rather than relying solely on trial-and-error prescribing. Further studies that integrate clinical characteristics with immune profiling, patient-reported outcomes, and formal indirect comparison methods will be important to confirm and refine this approach, especially given the differences in current trial designs, endpoints, and treatment timelines.

This review has several limitations. First, direct comparisons between dupilumab and nemolizumab are constrained by differences in study design, outcome measures, and treatment duration across trials. Pruritus was assessed using different scales (WI-NRS versus PP-NRS), and sleep outcomes were evaluated only in nemolizumab studies, limiting cross-trial comparability. Additionally, the use of background topical anti-inflammatory therapies across all trials may have contributed to improvements in itch and lesion severity, limiting the ability to contribute clinical outcomes exclusively to the studied biologic therapy. Second, no head-to-head randomized trials currently exist, precluding definitive conclusions regarding relative efficacy or optimal sequencing of therapy. Third, trial populations were highly selected and may not fully reflect the experiences of real-world patients with comorbidities or prior biologic exposure. Finally, long-term comparative effectiveness, durability of response, and safety beyond trial follow-up periods remain incompletely characterized.

Several investigational therapies are also being studied for PN. Tralokinumab, which targets IL-13, may have a potential benefit in PN based on the role of type 2 inflammation in chronic pruritus and emerging evidence of clinical improvement in PN-like phenotypes of atopic dermatitis [26]. Barzolvolimab, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor targeting KIT, demonstrated a 57% ≥ 4-point WI-NRS improvement at week 8 in a phase 1b trial [27]. Vixarelimab, which inhibits OSMRβ, showed a 52.2% WI-NRS reduction and 30% IGA success in a phase 2a study [28]. JAK inhibitors, including abrocitinib and povorcitinib, have demonstrated meaningful reductions in itch severity and improvements in quality of life, though dose-dependent adverse events have been reported [29]. Serlopitant, a neurokinin-1 receptor antagonist, demonstrated significant itch reduction in a phase 2 randomized trial for treatment-refractory PN [30]. Other emerging agents include apremilast and nalbuphine hydrochloride (HCL) extended release (ER), though these have demonstrated modest and inconsistent antipruritic effects in phase 2 trials [31, 32]. Upadacitinib, stapokibart, SHR-1819, rocatinlimab, ruxolitinib, and tofacitinib remain under early investigation.

Conclusions

Biologic therapies have marked a major advancement in the treatment of prurigo nodularis. Dupilumab and nemolizumab, the first FDA-approved monoclonal antibodies for PN, have demonstrated meaningful clinical benefits in well-designed trials. However, many patients do not achieve complete relief, and substantial variation exists in onset of action, symptom targets, and safety profiles. As new therapies continue to emerge, clinical trials remain essential tools to support evidence-based, individualized care. Comparative studies will help identify the most effective treatment options, especially for patients with specific comorbidities, rapid symptom progression, or sleep disturbances. Future research should also evaluate combination strategies, long-term durability, and real-world application. As the treatment landscape grows, clinicians will need clear, practical guidelines to navigate increasingly complex therapeutic choices. Continued investigation will be key to ensuring that all patients with prurigo nodularis have access to effective and lasting relief.
