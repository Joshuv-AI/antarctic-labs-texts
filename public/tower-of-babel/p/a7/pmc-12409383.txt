SND1, a novel m6A RNA regulator: Its high expression correlates with tumorigenesis and poor prognosis in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Karpakavinayakam Gopalakrishnan, Chandra Pandi, Vijayashree Priyadharsini Jayaseelan, Paramasivam Arumugam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe multifunctional protein SND1 (Staphylococcal Nuclease and Tudor Domain Containing 1) is involved in transcriptional control, RNA metabolism, and tumour development. While its role in several cancer types has been studied, little is known about its importance in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). This study investigates the expression patterns, clinical relevance, and functional role of SND1 in HNSCC.MethodsSND1 expression in the TCGA-HNSCC dataset was examined using multiple databases, including TIMER, Protein Atlas, and UALCAN. Further SND1 expression was validated using real-time PCR. The prognostic impact of SND1 in HNSCC patients was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. Protein-protein interactions and pathway enrichment analyses were conducted to elucidate the SND1 functional role.ResultsThe expression of SND1 was significantly elevated in HNSCC tissues compared to normal tissues. Higher grade, metastasis, TP53 alterations, HPV status, and advanced tumour stage were all substantially associated with elevated SND1 expression. According to Kaplan-Meier analysis, a lower overall survival rate was linked to SND1 high expression. Analysis of protein interactions identified partners such as AGO2, FXR1, and STAT5, which connect SND1 to carcinogenic pathways including JAK-STAT signalling. Functional enrichment pathways indicated SND1 role in oncogene translation regulation and head and neck carcinoma.ConclusionSND1 overexpression in HNSCC indicates a poor prognosis and unfavorable clinicopathological features. Due to its role in key cancer pathways, SND1 could serve as a useful biomarker for prognosis and a potential target for treatment. Improving patient outcomes requires further research into its molecular mechanisms and the development of treatments.Keywords: SND1, HNSCC, Overexpression, Prognosis, Therapeutic target

Introduction

Staphylococcal nuclease and Tudor domain-containing 1 (SND1), also known as Tudor-SN, TSN, or p100, is an evolutionarily conserved multidomain protein with a wide range of biological functions. Its structural composition includes four tandem staphylococcal nuclease-like (SN) domains at the N-terminus, with C-terminal domain that combines a Tudor domain and a truncated SN domain. This unique architecture enables SND1 to interact with RNA and methylated amino acids, while its intrinsic endonuclease activity allows it to degrade double-stranded RNA.1 Clinically, SND1 has gained attention for its role as an oncogene, being overexpressed in several cancers, including colon, breast, prostate, liver, lung, glioma, and ovarian cancers. It contributes to key processes such as tumor progression, metastasis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and the development of chemoresistance.2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 For example, in hepatocellular carcinoma and colon cancer, SND1 enhances RISC (RNA–induced silencing complex) activity, while in ovarian cancer, it modulates chromatin accessibility through the recruitment of histone acetyltransferases such as p300 and GCN5.2,8,9 Under standard physiological conditions, SND1 serves a vital function in modulating gene expression at both the transcriptional and translational levels. It is associated with tumor development and primarily acts as a transcriptional co-activator, while also playing a role in chromatin remodelling and pre-mRNA processing within the spliceosome.2,8,10,11 Alongside its established role in cancer progression and metabolic diseases, SND1 is recognized as a valuable biomarker for disease prognosis and a promising target for therapeutic intervention.

Head and neck squamous cell carcinomas (HNSCCs) are a group of cancers that develop in the mucosal lining of the oral cavity, pharynx, and larynx. They are the most common type of cancer found in the head and neck region. Worldwide, HNSCC poses a major health challenge, with over 500,000 new cases diagnosed each year. Its development is closely linked to environmental and lifestyle factors, such as tobacco use, excessive alcohol consumption, and infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) strains.12, 13, 14 In the last two decades, the epidemiological trends of HNSCC have changed significantly. While the incidence of cancers associated with tobacco and alcohol use, such as laryngeal and hypopharyngeal carcinomas, has declined, cases of oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma (OPSCC), particularly HPV-positive OPSCC, have risen significantly. Compared to HPV-negative HNSCC, HPV-positive OPSCC exhibits a distinct clinical profile, typically affecting younger, non-smoking individuals from higher socioeconomic backgrounds, and is associated with better treatment outcomes.15,16

Apart from genetic mutations, epigenetic and epitranscriptomic modifications also contribute significantly to the development of HNSCC. HNSCC tumors are characterized by global DNA hypomethylation as well as hypermethylation. Identifying molecular biomarkers is crucial for predicting survival, discovering new therapeutic targets, assessing drug response, and anticipating the progression of pre-malignant HNSCC lesions.

Significant biomarkers, whether diagnostic or prognostic, such as EGFR, TP53, PD-L1, or even p16/HPV, HPV DNA testing, and some immunohistochemical markers, generally suffer from very low sensitivity and specificity, thereby rendering risk stratification and prediction of therapeutic response difficult. There emerges an urgent need to establish newer, reliable, functionally relevant biomarkers for differential diagnosis, prognostication, and patient stratification. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) machinery has recently gained a reputation as a therapeutic and prognostic biomarker for many cancers. Among other targets, SND1 is an emerging candidate m6A reader with various multifunctional roles in RNA regulation. Reports prove that SND1 was overexpressed in different types of cancer and that it promotes tumor growth and metastasis. However, whether and how SND1 functions as an m6A-dependent factor and its role in prognosis in HNSCC remain largely unexplored, making it a new and untapped biomarker. This study aims to investigate SND1 overexpression in relation to HNSCC pathogenesis, prognosis, and clinical outcomes.

Materials and methods

Tissue collection and clinical data documentation in cancer patients

With prior informed consent and approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee, tissue samples were taken from patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), the most common type of HNSCC, who were undergoing surgery at Saveetha Dental College and Hospitals. The Helsinki Declaration was followed, and the reference number (IEC NO: SDC/FAC-12/19/003) was obtained. To maintain molecular stability, 34 tumour and surrounding non-tumorous tissues were collected and kept at −80 °C. Inclusion criteria for the study included patients diagnosed with primary cancer confirmed histologically. These cases were considered eligible only if the patients had not undergone any treatment at the time of sample collection and had enough tissue sample available for RNA and protein analysis. Patients with systemic disease, autoimmune diseases, second primary malignancies, or cases with insufficient tissue samples were all excluded from the study. Staging information was gathered from the medical records, and whenever possible, it was extracted from the TCGA HNSCC dataset.

RNA extraction and mRNA expression analysis

RNA extraction from tissue samples and cell lines was carried out using the TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA's purity and concentration were determined using a Nanodrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), ensuring a 260/280 nm absorbance ratio close to 2.0. For cDNA synthesis, 1 μg of RNA was utilized with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed based on the methodology described in our previous study.17 The primer sequences used in RT-qPCR are detailed in Table 1. In this study, we also utilized HNSCC cell lines obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC).

Decoding SND1 function through a bioinformatics approach

The mRNA expression levels of the SND1 gene in HNSCC were investigated using publicly available datasets. Expression data for SND1 in HNSCC and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) via the UALCAN database18 (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu), an online platform for analysing TCGA data and exploring differential gene expression in cancers. The relationship between SND1 expression and various clinicopathological parameters, including tumor stage, grade, and lymph node involvement, was also examined to assess its association with HNSCC progression. To evaluate the prognostic significance of SND1 expression, survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan-Meier plots (https://kmplot.com/). Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression value of SND1. Overall survival (OS) was compared between these groups, and statistical significance was determined using the log-rank test.19 The clinical characteristics of patients with HNSCC from the TCGA-HNSCC dataset are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blot

Protein was extracted from tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues following a standardized procedure.20 The BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to assess protein concentrations. Equivalent protein samples underwent SDS-PAGE separation and were subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes using a semi-dry blotting system (Bio-Rad). To minimize non-specific interactions, membranes were blocked with 5 % skim milk in TBST for 1 h. They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting SND1 (ABclonal) and GAPDH (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). After TBST washes, membranes were exposed to HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using the Clarity Max ECL substrate, and images were acquired using the ChemiDoc XRS + system (Bio-Rad). Protein expression levels were measured and analyzed with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

In silico-analysis

The Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/)21 was employed to assess SND1 protein expression in a wide range of cancers. This platform provides detailed immunohistochemical data, enabling visualization and quantification of protein expression across normal and cancerous tissues. To investigate the interaction of SND1 with other proteins, the STRING database (https://string-db.org/)22 was utilized. STRING is a robust resource that compiles, scores, and integrates protein–protein interaction data from a variety of public databases while also incorporating computational predictions. This database facilitates the exploration of complex interaction networks and helps identify potential functional relationships between proteins. Additionally, Metascape (https://metascape.org/)23 was used to analyze the functional enrichment pathways associated with SND1 in cancer.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance for all experiments was assessed by comparing p values obtained from GraphPad Prism software using an unpaired two-tailed Student t-test. P values are indicated on the plots (∗p < 0.05; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗∗∗p < 0.001). Results are presented as mean + standard error (SE). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

SND1 mRNA expression in HNSCC

The SND1 mRNA expression in various cancers was investigated using multiple publicly available databases. The TIMER database was used to analyze SND1 transcript levels across different types of cancer (Fig. 1A, P < 0.05), and the Protein Atlas database provided insights into the analysis of SND1 mRNA levels across various cancer cell types (Fig. 1B), revealing high expression of SND1 mRNA in cancer tissues and cell lines. Data from the UALCAN database indicated a significant overexpression of SND1 mRNA in HNSCC tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1C, P < 0.001). To further validate this, we analyzed SND1 mRNA expression in tissue samples (Fig. 1D, P < 0.01) and cell lines (Fig. 1E, P < 0.001). Our study confirmed a substantial increase in SND1 mRNA levels in tumor tissues and cancer cell lines.

SND1 protein expression in HNSCC

The expression of the SND1 protein was assessed across multiple cancer types (Fig. 2A). In HNSCC, SND1 tumor tissues showed significantly higher protein levels compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2B, P < 0.001). Immunohistochemical data from the Protein Atlas database further highlighted that SND1 protein is highly expressed in various cancers (Fig. 2C). Specifically, in HNSCC, moderate staining of SND1 was observed in tumor and metastatic samples, whereas normal tissues displayed low staining levels (Fig. 2D). To confirm these results, we conducted a Western blot analysis to assess SND1 protein levels in both tumor and normal tissues. The findings revealed a notable upregulation of SND1 protein in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2E).

Clinicopathological features and survival analysis of HNSCC associated with SND1 expression

The UALCAN database provided detailed information on the correlation between SND1 mRNA levels and the clinicopathological traits of HNSCC. Significant differences in SND1 expression were observed between HNSCC samples and normal tissues across several parameters. Specifically, elevated SND1 expression showed a strong association with tumor stage, grade, metastasis, HPV presence, and TP53 mutation status (Fig. 3A–E, P < 0.05). Additionally, the survival outcomes of HNSCC patients were analyzed in relation to SND1 expression levels (Fig. 3F, P = 0.021). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that elevated SND1 expression correlated with reduced survival in HNSCC patients, highlighting its relevance in predicting patient outcomes.

Analysis of SND1 protein interactions and functional pathways

We analyzed the subcellular localization of SND1 protein in cells to understand its functional role. Our findings revealed that SND1 is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of the cells (Fig. 4A). To explore the network of interactions between proteins of SND1, we utilized the STRING database, which provides high-confidence predictions of protein associations. The analysis identified several interacting partners of SND1, including FXR1, FMR1, STAT6, STAT5A, PIWIL1, PIWIL4, AGO2, MTDH, and STAT5B (Fig. 4B). Such linkages indicate that SND1 functions as a pivotal part of fundamental molecular systems and may play diverse roles in cellular processes, particularly in RNA regulation and signaling pathways. Further functional analysis highlighted that SND1 involves multiple critical biological pathways and processes. Notably, SND1 participates in the growth hormone receptor signaling pathway via JAK-STAT, a pathway known for its role in cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation (Fig. 4C). Moreover, SND1 participates in translation regulation, reinforcing its role in post-transcriptional gene control and protein production. Pathway enrichment analysis further identified SND1 involvement in the development of head and neck cancers, including head and neck tumors and malignant neoplasms (Fig. 4D). Such insights indicate that SND1 facilitates the advancement and aggressiveness of HNSCC by influencing key oncogenic pathways and cellular mechanisms.

Discussion

This study investigated the expression patterns, clinical significance, and functional roles of SND1 in HNSCC. Numerous studies have recently shown that abnormal gene and protein expression associated with the development of HNSCC/OSCC can serve as biomarkers and therapeutic targets.24, 25, 26, 27, 28 Our findings showed that SND1 was significantly overexpressed in HNSCC tissues compared to normal tissues, as demonstrated through analyses of the TIMER, Protein Atlas, and UALCAN databases. Experimental validation further corroborated these results, showing significantly higher SND1 mRNA and protein levels in tumor tissues and cell lines. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting SND1 overexpression in various cancers, including HCC, colon cancer, and breast cancer. In HCC, SND1 overexpression has been associated with increased RISC activity, facilitating the breakdown of tumor suppressor mRNAs through oncogenic miRNAs.8 Similarly, in colon cancer, SND1 was shown to suppress APC protein levels via post-transcriptional mechanisms, contributing to early carcinogenesis.2 These studies highlight the conserved oncogenic role of SND1 across cancer types, including HNSCC, where its elevated expression may drive tumorigenesis by influencing gene expression and RNA metabolism.

Our findings also revealed a significant connection between SND1 expression and key clinicopathological factors in HNSCC, including tumor stage, histological grade, metastasis, TP53 mutation status, and HPV presence. Higher SND1 levels were strongly associated with advanced tumor stages, higher grades, and the presence of metastases. This is consistent with prior findings in colon and liver cancers, where SND1 overexpression was linked to tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis. Notably, SND1 association with TP53 mutations in HNSCC suggests its involvement in pathways critical to genomic instability and tumor progression.

The relationship between SND1 and HPV status also underscores its potential role in viral oncogenesis, a phenomenon observed in other cancers where SND1 interacts with viral proteins to modulate host cellular processes.29 HPV infection, particularly HPV16, has been well established as an underlying cause of a certain subset of oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinomas. Overall, they have a better prognosis, are more radiosensitive, and carry certain molecular signatures in contrast to HPV-negative cancers. There is a potential link between SND1 levels and HPV-negative tumors, which could imply that SND1 is involved in non-viral oncogenic signaling. These findings therefore support investigating the potential of SND1 as a high-risk biomarker, particularly in HPV-negative HNSCC cases.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that increased SND1 expression correlates with reduced overall survival in HNSCC patients. This aligns with findings from other cancers, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and breast cancer, where high SND1 levels were associated with poor prognosis. In NSCLC, SND1 overexpression contributed to chemoresistance and inhibited ROS-dependent apoptotic signalling, enabling tumor cells to withstand therapeutic stress.6 In HNSCC, the association between high SND1 expression and poor prognosis suggests its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker, providing valuable insights for patient stratification and personalized treatment approaches.

The functional characterization of SND1 in our study showed its main location in the cytoplasm. This result aligns with previous studies that identified SND1 as a transcriptional coactivator; by disrupting stress granule aggregation, SND1 knockdown promotes apoptosis and decreases RISC activity.1,4,8,29,30 SND1 has been identified to associate with various proteins, and interaction analysis using the STRING database revealed key binding partners, such as FXR1, FMR1, and PIWIL1, reinforcing its involvement in RNA regulation and signaling networks. NF-κB activation, miR-221 induction, miR-184 expression, JAK/STAT3 suppression, and the activation of important pathways like TGFβ1/Smad, Wnt/β-catenin, ERK, and Akt are some of the mechanisms that have been suggested for SND1-driven carcinogenesis. Furthermore, SND1 interacts with vital proteins such as monoglyceride lipase and metadherin-1, underscoring its importance in the development of tumors.2,5,7,8,31, 32, 33, 34, 35 Crucially, our study's observation of SND1 participation in the JAK-STAT signalling pathway emphasises its role in tumour growth, cellular proliferation, and differentiation. These results are in line with studies on breast cancer, which showed that SND1 regulates gene expression by interacting with histone modification complexes.

SND1 roles in various malignancies align with its involvement in these pathways. For example, SND1 enhances TGFβ signaling in breast cancer promotes the transcriptional activation of SMAD genes through interactions with histone modification complexes. Similarly, SND1's role in regulating protein synthesis and gene expression likely contributes to the development of carcinogenesis and HNSCC. SND1 functional relevance and significant overexpression in HNSCC underscore its potential for therapeutic intervention. Targeting SND1 may disrupt its carcinogenic function, as previous studies have shown that suppressing SND1 nuclease activity lowers cell viability in colon cancer and HCC. Furthermore, restoring tumour suppressor gene expression may be possible by altering SND1 interactions with AGO2 and other RISC components, offering a prospective cancer therapeutic approach.8

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of our study, even though it provides insightful information about the function of SND1 in HNSCC. First and foremost, the smaller sample size. It is necessary to expand the sample size. Furthermore, although we experimentally confirmed SND1 expression, functional research is necessary to determine its precise function in the development and metastasis of HNSCC. To determine SND1 potential as a therapeutic target, preclinical research examining the effectiveness of SND1 inhibitors in HNSCC models is also required.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the significant overexpression of SND1 in HNSCC and its substantial association with poor prognosis and unfavorable clinicopathological characteristics. SND1 plays a crucial role in the development of HNSCC by affecting RNA regulatory mechanisms and oncogenic signalling pathways, according to functional and pathway analyses. These results offer strong support for investigating SND1 further as a therapeutic target and prognostic biomarker.
