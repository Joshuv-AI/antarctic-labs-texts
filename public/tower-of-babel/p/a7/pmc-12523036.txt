Lignin: the primary component responsible for endocarp sclerosis in the development of Cornus officinalis fruit
Authors: Si Yang, Yue Qin, Jing Gao, Gang Zhang, Liang Peng, Yimin Li, Xiaofei Zhang, Mingying Zhang, Ying Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sclerified endocarp formation represents a defining developmental feature in Cornus officinalis drupes. We systematically investigated cytological dynamics and lignin metabolism to define the mechanistic basis of endocarp sclerification during Cornus officinalis fruit development. Results demonstrated that endocarp sclerification coincided with a significant increase in lignin content. Stone cell density exhibited developmental variation (239–529 piece·mg-1; mean: 400 piece·mg-1), while the microscopic characteristic index of stone cells progressively increased and stabilized at fruit maturity. Critically, the activities of pivotal lignin biosynthesis enzymes (PAL, CCR, CAD, POD) peaked during early fruit development and subsequently declined with progressive lignin accumulation—a trend corroborated by transcriptomic profiling. RNA-Seq analyses revealed stage-specific expression of lignin biosynthesis genes: PAL, C4H, CSE, POD, CAD, and CCR were highly expressed in young fruit but downregulated during later development. Conversely, COMT and F5H expression peaked at ripening, aligning with maximal lignin deposition and indicating their direct regulation of terminal lignin synthesis steps. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Cornus officinalis endocarp hardening arises from synergistic stone cell differentiation and lignification, with phenylpropanoid metabolism initiated at juvenility and ripening-stage lignin deposition governed primarily by COMT and F5H.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07347-3.

Introduction

Cornus officinalis, a member of the Cornaceae family, is esteemed for its medicinal properties, predominantly derived from its dried and mature pulp. Its therapeutic use was first documented in Shennong's Herbal Classic (circa 200–250 CE), where it was classified as a medium-grade medicinal agent. The chemical composition of Cornus officinalis is highly diverse. It contains carbohydrates, organic acids, phenolic compounds, glycosides, iridoid glycosides, saponins, and other bioactive components [1, 2]. Cornus officinalis has a sour taste and mild warming properties. Pharmacologically, it is known to tonify the liver and kidneys, alleviate astringency, and stabilize dehydration. Additionally, it exhibits significant anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor, antioxidant, and anti-aging effects, highlighting its potential for therapeutic applications [3, 4]. The Cornus officinalis produced in Shaanxi has a long historical tradition. It is a key component of Taoist traditional Chinese medicine. Its dual-use status as both functional food (GB 2763–2023) and medicinal material was formally endorsed in 2023 through joint legislation by China's National Health Commission (NHC) and State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR), validating its historical Yao-Shi Tong Yuan (medicinal-food homology) applications.

The fruit of Cornus officinalis is botanically classified as a drupe, characterized by three histologically distinct pericarp layers: Exocarp, mesocarp, kernel (Fig. 3 A, B). The exocarp of Cornus officinalis fruit is thin, comprising primarily a single layer of epidermal cells or the epidermis, along with some adjacent tissues. This epidermal layer is rich in colouring substances or pigments, particularly various anthocyanins. These anthocyanins not only impart a bright color to the Cornus officinalis fruit but also serve as indicators of its maturit [5, 6]. The mesocarp contains thin-walled tannin cells that are the primary sites of tannin accumulation. This tannin concentration contributes significantly to the fruit's astringent flavor [6, 7]. The pyrenes are formed from endocarp cells in the fruit by lignification, thick walling and sclerificatio [8–10]. Lignin, the primary component of the endocarp, interacts with cellulose and hemicellulose to enhance its mechanical support and structural stability [11]. Endocarp formation is closely associated with lignin metabolism, which involves multiple enzymes. Similar processes have been observed in other species, such as apricot [12], Sichuan okra [13], and cotto [14], where enzyme activity correlates with lignin metabolism during development. The medicinal use of drupe fruits like Cornus officinalis involves an ongoing debate regarding kernel removal versus retention. While endocarp sclerification—a developmental hallmark of Cornus officinalis—forms a hardened kernel essential for pulp separation, recent evidence reveals similar bioactive potential in both tissues. Biochemical analyses confirm identical active compounds in kernels and pulp, including hepatoprotective cyclic enol ether terpenes and polyphenols [15–17]. This pharmacological equivalence underscores the significance of kernel research. As a drupe, the highly lignified stone cells are formed when the fruit matures and the number of stone cells in the endocarp increases, which further contributes to its structural characteristics [18]. This process entails parenchyma cell differentiation into stone cells through secondary cell wall formation, thickening, and progressive lignification [19–21]. The resultant lignified endocarp forms a critical physical barrier during ripening, protecting seeds from pathogens and herbivory while aiding dispersal [22]. Traditionally discarded as waste, the kernels (endocarp and seeds) are now recognized as un-tapped re-sources rich in oils, proteins, phenolics, and flavonoids [23]; Systematic characterization suggests applications in functional foods, nutraceuticals, and cosmetics, with seed oils and extracts showing dermatological and antioxidant/anti-inflammatory potential [24].

As a drupe, Cornus officinalis exhibits progressive kernel weight gain throughout fruit development, stabilizing at maturity—a process that establishes the physicospatial foundation for stone cell differentiation. Notably, lignin accumulation demonstrates tight synchrony with kernel weight dynamics, with both parameters peaking concurrently at maturity (Supplementary Data Table S1). While existing research on this traditional medicinal herb has predominantly centered on fruit phytoconstituents, critical mechanistic gaps persist regarding kernel development: specifically, the ontogeny of endocarp hardening, the role of lignification in stone cell formation, and the temporal expression patterns of lignin biosynthesis enzymes/genes during sclerification. To address these questions, we systematically investigated endocarp development across key phenological stages through integrated morpho-anatomical characterization, cellular ultrastructure analysis, lignin quantification, and transcriptome profiling. This multidisciplinary approach deciphers the cellular dynamics of endocarp development and trajectories of lignin accumulation, while establishing a spatiotemporal molecular framework for lignin deposition mechanisms. Our findings provide foundational insights for targeted regulation of medicinal fruit quality.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

Cornus officinalis fruits were harvested from March 2018 to October 2020. The fruits were collected from 15-year-old healthy Cornus officinalis plants in the Medicinal Botanical Garden of Shaanxi University of Chinese Medicine (coordinates: 108.748308°E, 34.320231°N), and all samples taken for the experiments belong to the cultivated products of the Medicinal Botanical Garden of Shaanxi University of Chinese Medicine, which are not currently deposited in the public botanical herbarium. A portion of fresh fruit was used for observing the appearance and internal structure. Another portion of the fruit pulp was separated, and the kernels were wrapped in tinfoil, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for later determination of fruit indices.

Experimental methods

Histochemical staining

Fresh Cornus officinalis fruits were used as experimental materials. The fruits were cut crosswise, and 3–4 mm thick tissue sections were taken from the center. These sections were stained with resorcinol-concentrated hydrochloric acid. Lignin accumulation in the endocarp was then observed under a Nikon SMZ745T light microscope.

Paraffin section method

The collected Cornus officinalis fruits were fixed in 50% FAA fixative. The specimens were then dehydrated step by step using a gradient alcohol series, cleared with xylene, and embedded in wax. Tissue sections were cut to a thickness of 8–10 μm using a Leica RM2135 hand-operated slicer. The slices were dried in an electric blower oven at 40 °C, stained with Senna red-fixed green, and sealed with neutral gum. The sections were observed under a Leica DM750 light microscope and photographed for documentation.

Microscopic quantification

Precision weighing of April–October Cornus officinalis kernel sample powder (80 mesh) 25 mg, refer to the method of Xu Liang [25]et al, with chloral hydrate for several times grinding transferred to 10 ml volumetric flasks, with chloral hydrate to fix the volume, fully shaking well and then precise aspiration of 0.1 ml of suspension, heating permeable, drops of dilute glycerol mounted slices in the microscope, its observation area of 18 mm × 18 mm, with the count of stone cells as the microscopic characteristics of the object The mean value of the number of stone cells in each group of 5 slices was calculated, and then the mean value of the 7 groups and the RSD value of the 7 groups were calculated. Equation 1: Microscopic feature index P = (X × V)/(V’ × W); where: P-quantitative herb microscopic feature index (one/mg); X-number of herb microscopic features under each coverslip; V-total volume of quantitative herb suspension (ml); V’-volume of herb suspension under coverslip (ml); W-weight of herb (mg, according to the dry product).

Examination of linear range: The powder of the same sample was weighed precisely into 5 portions, and then determined microscopically and quantitatively according to the above experimental methods, the endocarpolite cells were observed and counted, and the regression equation was calculated by taking the weight of the sample as the horizontal coordinate (X), and the mean value of the number of endocarpolite cells in each slice as the vertical coordinate (Y), and the standard curve was plotted. Stability investigation: 5 portions of the same number of sample powder were weighed precisely, and the microscopic quantitative determination was carried out according to the experimental method, and the number of stone cells under the same moment and the RSD of the microscopic characteristic index under the 5 different moments were observed and recorded at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 12 h, respectively. The results are shown in Table S3.

The results are shown in Supplementary Material 3: Table S4.

Content determination

Determination of lignin, cellulose and hemicellulose content

Lignin content was determined using a kit method (Bebel Reagent, BB-51026) [26]. Cellulose content was measured using the anthrone-H₂SO₄ method [27]. Hemicellulose content was determined following the method of [28].

Determination of lignin synthesis-related enzyme activities

Phenylalanine aminotransferase (PAL) activity was measured using the PAL kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A137-1–1). Cinnamoyl coenzyme A reductase (CCR) activity was determined using the CCR kit (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., m1260349). Cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD) activity was measured using the CAD kit (Beijing Solepol, BC4170). Peroxidase (POD) activity was determined using the POD activity kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A084-3–1). Excel 2016 software was used to collate data, GraphPad Prism 8.0 software to draw Line graphs, SPSS 25.0 software for correlation analysis, and Adobe Photophoto CC software to collate images. (Specific information can be found in the supplementary document).

Metabolomic analysis

Sample preparation

The YG, ZG, and SG stage samples of Cornus officinalis were freeze-dried under vacuum using a Scientz-100F freeze-dryer. The dried samples were ground into powder using a Retsch MM 400 grinder at 30 Hz for 1.5 min. A total of 100 mg of powder was weighed and extracted with 1.2 mL of 70% methanol. The samples were vortexed for 30 s every 30 min, for a total of six vortexes, and kept at 4 °C for 12 h. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min and used for UPLC-MS/MS analysis.

Ultra high performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry detection

Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC) (SHIMADZU Nexera X2) and Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) (Applied Biosystems 4500 QTRAP) were performed.

Liquid phase conditions: Column: Agilent SB-C18 1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; Mobile phase A: 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution, B: 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile solution; Gradient elution. The mobile phase A/mobile phase B (V/V) was 95:5 for 0.00 min, 5:95 for 9.00 min, 5:95 for 10 min, 95:5 for 10.10–11.10 min, and 95:5 for 14 min. The column temperature was 40 °C; The flow rate was 0.35 mL/min; The injection volume was 4 μL.

Mass spectrometry conditions: The sample was ionised by an electrospray ionisation source (ESI) at 550°C. Positive and negative ion detection modes were used, with an ion spray voltage (IS) of 5500 V (positive ion mode) / -4500 V (negative ion mode); Ionsource gas I 50 psi, Ion source gas II 60 psi and Curtain gas 25.0 psi with the Collision Induced Ionisation parameter set to High.

Data analysis

Mass spectrometry data were processed using Analyst 1.6.3 software. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to assess the overall metabolic differences between Cornus officinalis fruit samples from different developmental stages (including quality control samples). PCA also helped evaluate the variability among the YG, ZG, and SG stage groups. For differential metabolite screening, we applied the following criteria: Variable Importance in Projection (VIP), p-value from univariate analysis, and Fold Change. (Note: Metabolites with Fold Change ≥ 2, Fold Change ≤ 0.5, or VIP ≥ 1 were considered significantly different.)

Transcriptome analysis (unreferenced gene analysis)

RNA extraction and detection

Agarose gel electrophoresis: to analyse the RNA integrity of samples and the presence of DNA contamination; NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer: to detect the purity of RNA (OD260/280 and OD260/230 ratios); Qubit2.0 Fluorometer: to accurately quantify the concentration of RNA; Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer: accurate detection of RNA integrity.

Ranscriptome library sequencing

The mRNA with polyA tail was enriched by Oligo(dT) magnetic beads, and the common transcriptome library was constructed by using Illumina's NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit. After the library construction was completed, the preliminary quantification was carried out by using the Qubit2.0 Fluorometer, diluting the Library to 1.5 ng/ul, then the insert size of the Library was detected using Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer, and after the insert size met the expectation, qRT-PCR was used to accurately quantify the effective concentration of the library (the effective concentration of the Library was higher than 2 nM) to ensure the quality of the Library. After the Library was qualified, different libraries were pooled according to the effective concentration and the target downstream data volume for Illumina sequencing, and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated for subsequent analyses.

Data quality control

Raw data were filtered using fastp v0.19.3. The filtering process involved removing reads with adapters. Paired reads were discarded if the N content exceeded 10% of the total bases in the read. Paired reads were also removed if more than 50% of the bases had a quality score (Q) ≤ 20. All subsequent analyses were based on the clean reads.

Quantification and differential analysis of gene expression levels

Expression of transcripts was calculated using RSEM software, and then FPKM was calculated for each transcript based on transcript length. Biological replicates were analysed for differential expression between the two groups using DESeq2 v1.22.1, and unbiological replicates were analysed for differences using edgeR, with corrected P-values using the Benjamini & Hochberg method. Corrected P values as well as |log2foldchange| were used as thresholds for significant differential expression. In this project, the transcriptome sequencing analysis of 9 samples was completed, and a total of 62.81 Gb of Clean Data was obtained, and the Clean Data of all samples reached 6 Gb, with the percentage of O30 bases at 91% and above. The project is a non-participating gene. (Note: Differential genes were screened for |log2Fold Change|> = 1 ˈ and FDR < 0.05.)

Differential gene enrichment analysis

Enrichment analyses were performed based on hypergeometric tests, for KEGG, the hypergeometric distribution test was performed in terms of pathway; For GO, it was performed based on GO term.

Results

Developmental characteristics of the endocarp in Cornus officinalis fruit

Our research group conducted an in-depth study of the entire developmental cycle of Cornus officinalis fruits, in prior experiments [5, 29]. Consequently, this experiment focused on four key time points, which were marked by substantial changes in fruit size and significant Lignification: 30 days post—anthesis, 90 days post—anthesis, 180 days post—anthesis, and 210 days post—anthesis. The fruit of Cornus officinalis is elongated and oval—shaped. Its color undergoes notable changes during different developmental stages. From flowering to 90 days after flowering, the fruit experiences rapid growth, with both the Longitudinal and transverse diameters increasing. Throughout this period, the exocarp remains green. Between 90 and 180 days post—flowering, the fruit continues to enlarge, and the exocarp transitions from green to yellow. From 180 to 210 days post—flowering, the fruit reaches its full size, and the exocarp deepens to a bright red as the fruit matures (Fig. 1A). The endocarp originates from the inner layer of the ovary, adjacent to the seed tissue. In drupe fruits, endocarp hardening occurs as a result of secondary wall thickening and lignification. Under acidic conditions, resorcinol reacts with lignin in cell walls, producing a purplish—red color. Cross—sections of Cornus officinalis fruits stained with resorcinol—HCl exhibited this purplish—red color in the endocarp after 90 days post—anthesis (Fig. 1B), indicating that Lignification commenced between 30 and 90 days after flowering. Longitudinal observations of Cornus officinalis fruits at different developmental stages (Fig. 2) showed that endocarp heterocytes were relatively small at the early stage (from anthesis to 90 days after anthesis). Indices of microscopic characteristics of endocarp stone cells in Cornus officinalis

Lignification in plants involves the deposition of Lignin within its tissues, with stone cells serving as the primary cells undergoing Lignification. The presence of lignin in the endocarp of Cornus officinalis was demonstrated by its ability to react with resorcinol-concentrated hydrochloric acid, producing a purplish-red coloration, as illustrated in Fig. 3A and B. Further microscopic examination of the stone cells within the powdered fruit of Cornus officinalis revealed that these cells exhibited a variety of shapes, including square-like, oval, or oblong forms. Notably, these stone cells in the endocarp possessed distinct striated pores and a prominent cell lumen (Fig. 3. C, D). The count of stone cells ranged from 239–529 piece·mg-1, with an average value of 400 piece·mg-1 (Table 1). The microscopic characteristic index of these stone cells demonstrated an overall upward trend in conjunction with the development of Cornus officinalis fruits, ultimately stabilizing at maturity. Changes in enzyme activities associated with lignin biosynthesis

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) plays a crucial role in the phenylalanine metabolic pathway and lignin biosynthesis. Figure 4A shows that PAL activity increased during the early stage of fruit development (30 to 60 days after flowering). Prior to significant Lignin accumulation in the endocarp, PAL activity peaked at 845.5078 U·g⁻1 FW at 60 days post-anthesis. As Cornus officinalis fruit developed, endocarp lignification became evident, and the secondary wall surrounding heterocytes thickened. Concurrently, PAL activity declined. Notably, PAL activity decreased rapidly during the late young fruit stage (60–90 days after flowering), with an average daily reduction of 12.0558 U·g⁻1 FW. In contrast, during the ripening stage (180–210 days after flowering), the average daily reduction in PAL activity was 7.0630 U·g⁻1 FW.Fig. 4Changes in enzyme activities related to lignin synthesis. Cinnamoyl-CoA reductase (CCR) is a key enzyme in the lignin-specific biosynthetic pathway, directing metabolites from the phenylpropanoid pathway to lignin synthesis. Figure 4B shows that CCR activity increased during the early fruit stage (30–90 days after flowering), peaking at 2.33 00U·g⁻1 FW at 90 days. During the middle fruit stage (90–180 days), CCR activity fluctuated between 1.2308 and 2.3300 U·g⁻1 FW. At fruit ripening (210 days after flowering), CCR activity decreased to 1.1580 U·g⁻1 FW.

Cinnamyl alcohol dehydrogenase (CAD) catalyzes the final step in lignin monomer synthesis. Figure 4C shows that CAD activity was high during the early young fruit stage (30–60 days after flowering), peaking at 1982.0949 U·g⁻1 FW at 60 days. As the endocarp hardened and Lignin accumulated, CAD activity gradually declined, reaching approximately 128.3558 U·g⁻1 FW by 210 days after flowering.

Peroxidase (POD) works in conjunction with laccase to oxidatively polymerize lignin monomers into three types of lignin. Figure 4D shows that POD activity initially decreased and then increased during Cornus officinalis fruit development. Specifically, during the young fruit stage (30–90 days after flowering), POD activity decreased by an average of 0.0924 U·g⁻1 FW per day. In the middle fruit stage (90–180 days), POD activity followed a similar decreasing-then-increasing pattern, reaching a minimum of 11.8971 U·g⁻1 FW at 120 days. During fruit ripening (180–210 days after flowering), POD activity ranged from 14.2388 to 14.5417 U·g⁻1 FW.

The expression of structural genes involved in lignin synthesis in Cornus officinalis, including PAL, F5H, 4CL, C4H, CSE, POD, CAD, CCR, and COMT, was analyzed (Fig. 5A, B). The results showed that the expression of PAL, C4H, CSE, POD, CAD, and CCR peaked during the young fruit stage (YG). As the fruit developed and lignin content increased, the expression of these genes decreased. Specifically, PAL, POD, CAD, and CCR exhibited a similar trend to enzyme activity changes, suggesting their involvement in lignin synthesis. Correlation analysis of metabolites and structural genes involved in lignin biosynthesis

Lignin is composed of three primary monomers: P-coumaryl alcohol, Sinapyl alcohol, and Coniferyl alcohol. These monomers undergo oxidative polymerization to form three distinct lignin polymers: p-hydroxyphenyl lignin (H-lignin), Syringyl lignin (S-lignin), and Guaiacyl lignin (G-lignin) [30]. Analysis of the metabolomic data showed that the three core monomers of lignin synthesis showed significant differences in dynamic accumulation during cornelian cherry fruit development: coniferyl alcohol peaked at the young fruit stage (YG), Sinapyl alcohol accumulated the highest amount at the middle fruit stage (ZG), and p-coumaric acid was significantly enriched at the ripening stage (SG) (Fig. S1).

To explore associations between transcriptomic dynamics and lignin metabolism, we conducted correlation analyses (p < 0.05, |R|> 0.8) of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differential lignin metabolites (DAMs). The results revealed that COMT(Cluster-10622.34014、Cluster-10622.61621and Cluster-10622.63048) and F5H(Cluster-10622.70695) genes exhibited significant positive correlations with p-coumaric acid, suggesting their catalytic roles in redirecting this precursor toward coniferyl alcohol and sinapyl alcohol biosynthesis during fruit development. Conversely, CCR, CAD, POD, CSE, C4H, 4CL, and PAL genes showed positive correlations with coniferyl alcohol but negative correlations with p-coumaric acid, while 4CL, PAL, COMT, and C4H demonstrated significant negative correlations with sinapyl alcohol. Notably, a specific 4CL isoform (Cluster-10622.51504) displayed dual positive associations with both p-coumaric acid and sinapyl alcohol. These coordinated correlation patterns between core lignin monomers (p-coumaric acid, coniferyl alcohol, sinapyl alcohol) and key gene families (COMT, F5H, CCR, CAD, POD, CSE, C4H, 4CL, PAL) indicate their synergistic regulation of lignin monomer synthesis and conversion in Cornus officinalis fruit development, establishing a cohesive transcriptional-metabolic network central to the lignin biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 6).Fig. 6Correlation analysis of DEGs and DAMs related to lignin biosynthesis

Discussion

The formation of highly lignified stone cells is the primary cause of endocarp sclerosis during the development of Cornus officinalis fruit

Throughout Cornus officinalis fruit development, progressive kernel weight gain establishes the physical and spatial framework for stone cell differentiation, stabilizing at maturity. Stone cell density exhibited biphasic fluctuation—characterized by an initial rise, subsequent decline, and final resurgence—indicating stage-specific differentiation dynamics. Crucially, lignin accumulation demonstrated tight temporal coupling with kernel weight dynamics, both peaking synchronously at maturity. These patterns collectively reveal a hierarchical regulatory mechanism: (1) Kernel expansion facilitates stone cell proliferation by providing structural accommodation, thereby indirectly influencing lignification scale. (2) As primary lignin deposition sites, stone cell density fluctuations directly correlate with lignin accrual. (3) The non-significant stone cell–kernel weight correlation confirms that kernel growth modulates lignification solely through spatial provision for stone cell differentiation, without direct involvement in deposition biochemistry.

The innovation of this study is to integrate and analyse the indicators of microscopic characteristics of herbal medicines, data on herbal components and multi-omics data. The aim was to conduct a combined phenotypic-chemical-molecular study of endocarp sclerosis in the fruit of Cornus officinalis. This integrated approach provides novel experimental methods and important insights for deciphering the mechanism of endocarp sclerosis in this species. The method of microscopic characteristic index of traditional Chinese medicine is a method of quality evaluation of traditional Chinese medicine by using volumetric analysis and using the microscopic characteristic index of traditional Chinese medicine or the correlation with its indicator components and other quality factors. In the quality evaluation of traditional Chinese medicine, the correlation between the characteristic index and the index ingredient can be used to deter-mine whether the medicine meets the national quality regulations. This approach offers distinct advantages for authenticating Chinese medicinal materials, particularly species with overlapping macroscopic and microscopic morphologies. By quantifying disparities in microscopic characteristic indices between authentic and adulterated/variant specimens, it enables robust taxonomic discrimination. Exemplifying this utility, Kang's team demonstrated a significant negative correlation between the endocarp stone cell index and arctiin content in burdock (Arctium lappa L.), establishing novel quality assessment parameters that integrate structural and phytochemical metrics [25]. Similarly, calcium oxalate cluster crystal indices effectively differentiate wild forest ginseng (mean: 273 piece·mg-1,range: 172–383 piece·mg-1) from cultivated varieties (mean: 79 piece·mg-1; range: 33–144 piece·mg-1), providing a reliable morphological criterion for Panax ginseng C. A. Mey. species authentication [31]. These cases collectively validate how microscopic indexing advances quality control paradigms beyond conventional methods. Consequently, in the follow-up study of Cornus officinalis, exploring the correlation between its microscopic characteristic indices and the content or efficacy of key active ingredients (e.g., maqianoside) can provide new perspectives and new bases for the establishment of a more com-prehensive and in-depth quality evaluation system for Cornus officinalis herbs, complementing the traditional physicochemical testing methods.

Significant changes in lignin accumulation and the activities of related enzymes, including PAL, CCR, CAD, and POD, during the development of Cornus officinalis fruit

The stone endocarp of Cornus officinalis fruits is primarily composed of lignin, hemicellulose, and cellulose [12]. During fruit development, the content of these components increases significantly, with lignin accumulation peaking at maturity (51.6057 mg·g⁻1). In contrast, cellulose and hemicellulose levels decline substantially at the ripening stage, suggesting their primary functional importance occurs during earlier developmental phases.

Previous studies on apricot [12], Sichuan okra [13], and cotto [14] have shown significant correlations between the hardening process and ligninase activities. The decrease of PAL activity with lignin accumulation in Cornus officinalis fruits follows the same trend of changes during the development of peach [32] and walnut sclerotia [33]. Therefore, it is believed that the higher PAL enzyme activity before kernel hardening in Cornus officinalis is related to the opening of the lignin synthesis pathway.

CCR is a crucial enzyme in determining the type of lignin monomer synthesized. It catalyzes the redox conversion of hydroxycinnamic acid CoA esters to aldehydes [34]. It has been shown that CCR expression is directly proportional to the synthesis content of plant lignin, which is different from the results of the present study, but it can indicate that changes in CCR activity during plant development are closely related to kernel hardening and lignin accumulation [35].

CAD activity correlates with lignin accumulatio [36]. The activity of CAD in Cornus officinalis fruit decreased with lignin accumulation, and its activity stabilised during fruit maturation. This trend is consistent with the findings in lentil fruits, where lignin content was positively correlated with CAD activity during the pre-endocarp development stage, and lignin content was negatively correlated with CAD activity during the late endocarp development stage [37]. CAD gene expression is also associated with plant tissue development, postharvest maturation, senescence, mechanical damage, and external stress [38]. The initial increase and subsequent decrease in CAD activity in Cornus officinalis fruits may be linked to kernel hardening patterns.

POD, a key enzyme in the final step of lignin synthesis, influences plant tissue lignificatio [39]. In Cornus officinalis fruits, POD activity peaked during the young fruit stage, decreased to its lowest point in the middle stage (120 days post-flowering), and then stabilized during ripening. This trend aligns with previous findings in pear fruit development, where POD activity declined after peaking [40]. High initial POD activity may trigger extensive lignin synthesis, programmed cell death, and nutrient loss, which subsequently reduces POD activity [41]. Consistent with our study, Wang XH [42] found a negative correlation between POD activity and lignin accumulation during walnut sclerotia development.

In the lignin biosynthesis pathway of Cornus officinalis fruit, F5H and COMT are key genes involved in lignin synthesis, with their expression peaking at the ripening stage

In the fruit of Cornus officinalis, key monomers involved in lignin synthesis, such as Coniferyl alcohol, Sinapyl alcohol, and P-coumaric acid, as well as important genes including PAL, C4H, CSE, POD, CAD, CCR, COMT, F5H, and 4CL, exhibited distinct dynamic accumulation patterns during fruit development. Notably, P-coumaric acid, COMT, and F5H reached their highest levels at the ripening stage (SG), aligning with the trend of lignin content peaking at this stage. This suggests a synergistic role of P-coumaric acid, COMT, and F5H in lignin synthesis. In contrast, Coniferyl alcohol content and the expression of PAL, C4H, CSE, POD, CAD, and CCR were highest in the young fruit stage (YG). Although these elements showed a positive correlation with each other, they exhibited a negative correlation with lignin content. This implies their potential involvement in early lignin synthesis and possible Shifts in function towards the synthesis and metabolism of other substances in later fruit development stages. Additionally, Sinapyl alcohol showed a significant negative correlation with the genes 4CL, PAL, COMT, and C4H. Thus, the total amount of lignin accumulated is closely related to the amount of relevant metabolites and genes produced during lignin metabolism. Research indicates that upregulating PAL, C4H, and 4CL expression enhances the accumulation of lignin and other metabolites like flavonoids [43]. The COMT gene plays a crucial role in catalyzing the synthesis of G- and S-lignin. In alfalfa, COMT downregulation led to decreased lignin content, with reduced G-lignin and nearly absent S-lignin accumulation [44]. The F5H gene is vital for converting G-lignin to S-lignin and regulating the S/G ratio. A higher S/G ratio facilitates lignin degradation [45]. Heterologous F5H expression in gymnosperms resulted in S-lignin production in their cell walls [46]), and in willow jacaranda, F5H and COMT demonstrated a synergistic effect in lignin biosynthesis [47]. The expression of COMT and F5H peaked at the ripening stage of Cornus officinalis (SG), indicating their potential as key catalysts in lignin production.

Correlation analysis revealed strong associations among stone cells, lignin, F5H, and COMT during fruit development. The extremely strong correlations between stone cells and lignin, F5H (Cluster-10622.70695), COMT (Cluster-10622.61612, Cluster-10622.63048), as well as between lignin and F5H (Cluster-10622.70695) and COMT (Cluster-10622.61612, Cluster-10622.63048), further highlight the need to investigate the relationship between stone cells, lignin, and endocarp sclerosis in fruits (Fig. S2). Future studies can clarify the causal contributions to the hardening process by quantifying changes in stone cell differentiation and lignin deposition through gene function validation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that endocarp hardening in Cornus officinalis fruit is characterized by increasing stone cell formation and progressive lignin accumulation. The activities of key lignin biosynthesis enzymes (PAL, POD, CAD, CCR) peaked during the young fruit stage but subsequently declined as lignin accumulated, a trend corroborated by corresponding transcriptomic analyses. Notably, the genes COMT and F5H, exhibiting their highest expression at the ripe fruit stage, likely regulate late-stage lignin synthesis. Furthermore, correlation analyses strongly link stone cells, lignin content, and the expression of COMT and F5H to the endocarp hardening process (Fig. 7). Collectively, the synergistic interplay between stone cell development, lignin deposition, and the spatiotemporal regulation of key biosynthetic enzyme genes underpins endocarp lignification in Cornus officinalis, providing a foundation for elucidating its molecular mechanisms.Fig. 7Cornus officinalis endocarp lignification correlation diagram
