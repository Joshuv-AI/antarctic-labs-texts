Does lateralization and distalization affect revision rates and patient experience in primary stemmed reverse total shoulder arthroplasty?
Authors: David R.J. Gill, Sophia Corfield, Peiyao Du, Michael Sandow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examined the association between distalization and lateralization of implant components and revision outcomes and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) following primary total stemmed reverse shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) using data from a national arthroplasty registry.Materials and methodsAll primary total stemmed modular rTSA procedures (excluding custom implants) recorded between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2023, were identified. A total of 25,837 primary rTSA procedures were included. Procedures were classified into 6 construct groups based on humeral and glenoid component features associated with distalization and/or lateralization. Kaplan–Meier methods were used to estimate cumulative percent revision. Cox proportional hazards models estimated adjusted hazard ratios, controlling for age, sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists score, body mass index, primary diagnosis, glenosphere size, and glenoid morphology. Subgroup analyses were performed for inlay and onlay humeral configurations. PROMs collected from November 2018 were analyzed in a subset of patients.ResultsConstructs incorporating humeral distalization demonstrated higher revision rates beyond 1.5 years compared with standard components (P = .002). Augmented glenoid baseplates were associated with lower early revision rates within 3 months (P = .002). Onlay designs incorporating distalization and/or lateralization showed increased revision risk during specific post-operative periods. Instability or dislocation was the most common indication for revision, while infection was more frequent in constructs combining distalization and lateralization. All groups demonstrated significant improvements in EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale and Oxford Shoulder Score at 6 months.ConclusionHumeral distalization was associated with higher medium-term revision rates despite early improvements in PROMs, highlighting trade-offs in implant selection.

Since 2008, nationally, there has been a 284.7% increase in shoulder arthroplasty including 72.7% primary total shoulder arthroplasty. Since 2012, the most common shoulder arthroplasty is primary total stemmed reverse (rTSA).20 With this increase, there has been an expansion (through modularity) of implant sizing variations (ISV, as defined by our registry)21 to correct for glenoid deformity and add lateralization or distalization of the center of rotation or the humeral component. The extent of actual lateralization or distalization can vary with component design but is generally a combination of both directions. Early results for these changes to the glenoid baseplate,7,28 glenosphere,23,31 or humeral component5 have been promising but limited to single-arm or single-design platform prostheses (SDPP, as defined by our registry21). It has been proposed that this would reduce notching or instability/dislocation.12,32

Traditionally, implant performance has been assessed by the rate of revision due to its significant impact on the patient outcomes and healthcare costs. Orthopedic registries and health care service groups around the world are gradually expanding from routinely reported surgical data to include data on the self-reported health status of patients undergoing joint replacement surgery. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) provide an indicator of surgical thresholds and treatment effects, with the potential to improve health care outcomes within the health care sector for these increasingly common and resource-intensive procedures.13,14,24 To this point, PROMs have reported the rate of recovery after shoulder arthroplasty, or economic utility analysis.24

Our aim was to classify ISV within SDPP for rTSA to allow comparison of rates of revision and PROMs in a large national shoulder arthroplasty registry. Our hypothesis was that PROMs and revision rate will improve with increased lateralization and distalization by variations in ISV within SDPP when selected by the treating surgeon.

Materials and methods

A survivorship analysis of prospectively collected national joint arthroplasty registry data was undertaken. The Australian Orthopaedic Association National Joint Replacement Registry (AOANJRR) began data collection on September 1, 1999, for hip and knee replacements and from April 2004 for shoulder procedures. The AOANJRR has documented almost all shoulder arthroplasty procedures Australia-wide since November 2007. The data were externally validated against patient-level data provided by all Australian state and territory health departments. The processes, including data linkage, validation, matching, and data cleaning, are well described.9, 10, 11,20 The data were also matched biannually to the Australian National Death Index data to ascertain mortality.

Surgical procedure forms received from each hospital were entered into the AOANJRR database as part of routine AOANJRR processes. The patient information from the procedure form was then matched with the patient information in the purpose-built online platform, known as RAPID (Real-time Automated Platform for Integrated Data capture). This process assisted with determining the proportion of joint procedures registered at each hospital as well as identifying any data entry errors. It was also required for electronically triggering post-operative data collection reminders based on the procedure date. Reports were produced on patient registration and procedure registration rates at each hospital.

The study period was from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2023. This allowed capture of additional patient characteristics and avoid documented secular biases in rTSA revision rates.21 The study population included all primary rTSA undertaken for all diagnoses. Total stemless reverse shoulder arthroplasty, custom total stemmed reverse shoulder arthroplasty, and monoblock humeral components of rTSA were excluded. There were 25,837 primary rTSA in the study group with complete data. Of these, 9,574 were +H, 46 were lateralized glenosphere (LG), 3,600 ABG, 63 ABG/LG, 2,224 +H + B, and 10,330 rTSA standard. Procedures were classified into 6 groups: (1) plus size humeral cup (+H), (2) LG, (3) augmented glenoid base plate (AGB), (4) AGB and LG (AGB/LG), (5) plus size humeral cup with either augmented glenoid base plate, LG or both (+H + B), and (6) rTSA without additional features (rTSA standard) as per the classification of our national annual arthroplasty report.19 A glenoid baseplate is categorized as augmented (ABG) if the backside has been modified for glenoid deformity (eg, wedged, stepped, angulated, or lateralized). If the radius of curvature of the articular surface of the glenosphere is displaced laterally by design, it is categorized as a LG. The rTSA proximal humeral component metaphyseal region was classified by the following previously published criteria: inlay, where the metaphyseal component lies deep to the epiphyseal osteotomy; or onlay, where the metaphyseal component lies on top of the epiphyseal osteotomy.8 Only procedures where the prostheses were available and in use nationally in 2023 were included. There were 6 humeral stem manufacturers for H+, 4 glenosphere manufacturers for LG, 5 for ABG, 1 for ABG/LG, 6 for + H + B, and 6 for rTSA standard in eligible primary total stemmed reverse shoulder prosthesis combinations. The analyses were restricted to procedures in which the age, sex, ASA score,16 BMI,33 primary diagnosis, glenosphere size, and glenoid morphology,34 and all components used were known, that is, complete case analyses were performed throughout. The inclusions and exclusions are summarized in Figure 1. A subanalysis restricting to procedures performed from November 1, 2018, to December 31, 2023, permitted reporting of PROMs for a subset of the cohort groups when the AOANJRR commenced collection of these patient parameters. The AOANJRR operates a PROMs program, targeting all patients undergoing elective hip, knee, or shoulder replacement from participating institutions. Guidelines have been produced regarding the types of PROMs that should be collected.30,35 Due to the deficiencies associated with using paper forms (either directly or by mail) within a national registry, the AOANJRR PROMs program uses direct electronic data capture.

Data capture by telephone call using an interviewer who directly entered data electronically was also used for the pilot stage of the program, but only for patients who did not respond to direct electronic data entry following text message and email prompts for completion. The reported instruments were (1) EuroQol Visual Analogue Scale (EQ-VAS) Health, which is a validated measure on a scale from 0 (the worst health you can imagine) to 100 (the best health you can imagine). (2) The Oxford Shoulder Score (OSS) is a standardized and validated PROMs instrument developed to assess function and pain in patients undergoing shoulder surgery. The OSS provides a single summed score out of 48; the lower the score, the higher the severity of the patient's problems. (3) Expected joint pain vs. actual joint pain was also assessed by asking patients before their procedure to rate their EXPECTED joint pain at 6 months’ post-operatively on a sliding scale (from 0: no pain at all to 10: the worst pain imaginable) and then to rate their ACTUAL pain, using the same sliding scale at 6 months post-operatively. The actual pain vs. expected pain was then rated by patients as either better than expected, as expected, or worse than expected. (4) Procedure satisfaction was assessed at the 6-month post-operative collection time point by asking patients to select the statement which best described how satisfied they were with the results of their procedure from a 5-point Likert rating scale from very satisfied (5) to very dissatisfied (1). (5) A further question to assess their perceived change in problems associated with their joint using a similar 5-point rating scale from much better (5) to much worse (1). The Strengthening the Reporting of OBservational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines regarding descriptive data have been followed precluding the use of significance tests and P values of demographic data. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data statement, an extension of the STROBE guidelines for observational studies using routinely collected health data.2

Statistical analysis

Kaplan–Meier estimates of survivorship were used to report the time to revision, with censoring at the time of death and closure of the dataset at the end of December 2023. The cumulative percent revision was calculated as the complement of the Kaplan–Meier survival function estimate, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) obtained using unadjusted point-wise Greenwood estimates for the standard error. Hazard ratios (HRs) comparing the rate of revision between glenoid types were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models, adjusting for a prespecified set of confounders consisting of age, sex, ASA, BMI, primary diagnosis, glenosphere size, and glenoid morphology. Sex was defined as per the Sex and Gender Equity in Research guidelines.

The assumption of proportional hazards was checked analytically for each model. If the interaction between the predictor and the log of time was statistically significant in the standard Cox model, then a time-varying model was estimated. Time points were selected based on the greatest change in hazard, weighted by a function of events. Time points were iteratively chosen until the assumption of proportionality was met, and HRs were calculated for each selected time period. For the current study, if no time period was specified, the HR was calculated over the entire follow-up period. All tests were 2-tailed at 5% levels of significance.

In the subanalysis of procedures performed since 2018, the adjustments for the PROMs data comparisons were patient factors age, sex, ASA score, and BMI. Linear mixed model was used to calculate mean score change between pre- and post-operative EQ-VAS Health and OSSs with adjustments. Log-binomial model was used for categorical variables: Expected Joint Pain vs. Actual Joint Pain, Procedure Satisfaction, and Patient-Reported Change to calculate the event rate. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina).

Results

Of the 711 revisions, 306 were +H, 76 ABG, 50 +H + B, and 277 rTSA standard. ABG/LG and LG groups had 1 revision only and were not analyzed further. The cumulative percent revision at 6 years for rTSA with +H was 4.6% (95% CI: 4.0, 5.3), 5.7% (95% CI: 3.2, 10.1) for ABG, 3.4% (95% CI: 3.0, 3.9) for rTSA standard and at 5 years (latest year of follow-up) 6.3% (95% CI: 3.3, 11.7) for + H + B. (Fig. 2). +H had a 67% higher revision rate than rTSA standard after 1.5 years (HR: 1.67; 95% CI: 1.22, 2.30; P = .002); ABG had a lower revision rate before 3 months (HR: 0.48; 95% CI: 0.30, 0.77; P = .002) when compared to rTSA standard. The subanalysis, restricted to PROMS reported since November 1, 2018, for each instrument responded to by patients within each cohort group. Discussion

In a comprehensive minimum dataset cohort, we undertook an observational comparison of the revision rates of modern ISV in SDPP of rTSA to standard-sizing rTSA implants as reported to the AOANJRR. The (predominantly) distalization of the humeral component by larger humeral cup components increased the rates of revision of rTSA. In contrast, the positive change in PROMs was greater for lateralized and distalized constructs than conventional rTSA in the first 6 months after arthroplasty. When revised, those combinations were more likely to be infected.

Design variations in rTSA were introduced to address what may be regarded as “weaknesses” of the original Grammont configuration.6,12,19,27 Following evaluation of biomechanics and finite element analysis, these changes were predicted to increase range of motion, decrease joint load, and increase stability.5,15,25,32 However, such potential advantages have not necessarily been born out in vivo.5,1726,31 Although distalization was proposed as a method to reduce instability/dislocation,12 in primary arthroplasty, it has not been successful as revision surgery where rTSA with distalization is most commonly revised for instability/dislocation.3 More importantly, the rate of revision increased when distalization was used as an implant option in isolation in our review. This does not include any additional further surgery for stress fractures. This difference was most apparent in onlay rTSA, where the risk of overstuffing would be greatest.

Inlay vs. onlay classifications of rTSA have been validated as a method to use differing design concepts to define rTSA revision rate changes.9 Some argue the classification is not important and have instead proposed a more detailed method of assessing rTSA SDPP.4 To our knowledge this methodology has yet to be validated. Deltoid forces are more closely replicated with the medial/lateral design (glenoid/humerus) that confers an “anatomic appearance.” Equally, the lateral/medial configuration provides a more anatomic shoulder appearance that is more normal vis-a-vis medial/medial SDPP and improved rotator cuff recruitment.12,1819,22 Differing SDPP classifications, such as that of Roche,29 are not assessed directly here. The more modern lateralized designs did not change revision rates compared to standard devices in this study. In combination with distalization increased the risk of infection, but there is no apparent reason for this finding. While distalization and lateralization may reduce notching and change instability/dislocation risk biomechanically, we did not demonstrate an advantage via revision rate in our series.

Instability following reverse shoulder arthroplasty is recognized as a multifactorial complication. In addition to implant configuration, factors such as soft-tissue tensioning, subscapularis management, pre-operative deformity, correction of version and inclination, and surgeon-specific technique may all influence post-operative stability. These variables are not captured within the AOANJRR and therefore could not be accounted for in the present analysis.

The association between combined distalization and lateralization and infection-related revision cannot be fully explained by the available data. It is possible that increased surgical complexity, longer operative time, or unmeasured patient or procedural factors contributed to this finding; however, this remains speculative and should be interpreted cautiously.

This study has limitations and strengths. The findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of the limitations inherent to registry-based observational analyses. While multivariable adjustment was performed for known confounders, the use of routinely collected health data is subject to selection bias, residual confounding, and limited granularity regarding surgical technique and intraoperative decision-making. As such, the associations observed in this study do not imply causation. The study end point is revision rather than clinical performance such as range of motion or relief of pain. While the groups were well matched for BMI, ASA, and comparisons were adjusted, other unknown factors may be associated with revision rates. Furthermore, the decision on the use of nonstandard components may be related to a variable discretionary decision process intraoperatively by the surgeon to address some perceived stability or (device to bone) impingement occurrence. The 3 groups had differing sample sizes and length of observation, but as a survivorship study design, it is time independent rather than dependent. This national observational study records whole of population results, analyses associations, and does not determine the best possible outcome for any given diagnosis or prosthetic type.20 The reference group implied some sort of gold standard, but we do not know (yet) what the correct position or reference is. Standard components imply a starting point of where things are deemed to be right. Our comparator may not be accurate.

Conclusion

In this large national registry-based study, deviations from standard reverse shoulder arthroplasty components, particularly humeral distalization, were associated with higher medium-term revision rates, despite greater early improvements in patient-reported outcomes. Combined distalization and lateralization were associated with a higher proportion of infection-related revisions. These findings highlight potential trade-offs in implant selection and underscore the importance of cautious interpretation when using observational registry data to inform clinical decision-making. This study was unable to support the proposal that increased lateralization and distalization by variations in ISV within SDPP will achieve a consistent improvement in PROMs and revision rate.
