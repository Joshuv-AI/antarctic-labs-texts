Start Early, Do It Well: Implications of a National Diabetes Care Quality Assessment Program for Life Expectancy
Authors: Kyoung Hwa Ha, Dae Jung Kim
Published: 2025

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a major chronic condition linked to microvascular and cardiovascular complications, as well as premature mortality. T2DM continues to pose a major public health burden and significantly contributes to shortened life expectancy around the world, including in South Korea [1-4]. To reduce the disease burden among individuals with diabetes, many countries have implemented national-level strategies, including prevention-focused approaches, systematic screening, improved access to care, integrated service delivery, continuous quality improvement, and comprehensive monitoring systems. Among these strategies, effective incentive schemes to enhance care quality are particularly important, as the overall delivery of care, including its quality, efficiency, and equity, is largely shaped by the motivation of healthcare professionals [5]. In this context, many countries have adopted various forms of incentive-based programs. One representative example is Taiwan’s Pay-for-Performance (P4P) program, which functions as a structured quality-management system that links provider incentives to specific clinical targets [6,7].

Evaluating how implementation of diabetes quality-assessment initiatives such as P4P affects long-term health outcomes is critical to informing the design and scaling of value-based care models. In the current issue, Chen et al. [8] provide evidence supporting the long-term health benefits of performance-based programs for individuals with diabetes. Using data from a nationwide sample of 953,733 individuals newly diagnosed with diabetes in Taiwan, the authors investigated whether enrollment in the P4P program was associated with a reduction in loss of life expectancy. Individuals with diabetes were categorized based on P4P enrollment status, and those enrolled in the P4P program were further stratified according to the time from diagnosis to enrollment: <1, 1–4, 5–9, and 10–15 years. Life expectancy was estimated from survival curves extrapolated over the lifetime, and loss of life expectancy was calculated by comparing each group’s life expectancy with that of matched referent populations. Loss of life expectancy was 2.60 years in the non-P4P group and 3.55 years in the overall P4P group; however, substantial variation was observed depending on the timing of program enrollment. Among P4P participants, loss of life expectancy was minimal when enrollment occurred within 1 year (0.06 years) or 1–4 years (0.05 years) after diagnosis but was substantially higher for those enrolled at 5–9 years (10.01 years) or 10–15 years (12.77 years) post-diagnosis. Based on these findings, Chen et al. [8] suggest that early enrollment in the P4P program enables more timely and effective diabetes management, potentially leading to a meaningful reduction in loss of life expectancy.

Key strengths of this study include its use of a large, nationally representative sample and a 15-year follow-up period, enabling a robust assessment of the long-term impact of diabetes-management programs on life expectancy. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study population was restricted to adults aged 40 to 64 years, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, individuals who enrolled early in the P4P program may have had better baseline health or greater access to healthcare, introducing potential selection bias. Third, secular trends in diabetes care, such as increasing P4P participation and advancements in medical treatment, may have influenced the observed differences in life expectancy. Notably, P4P participation increased from approximately 30% in 2010 to nearly 50% by 2020 and, as a result, earlier samples likely included more non-participants, whereas later samples may have benefited from newer therapies. These temporal changes could have led to an overestimation of the observed differences in life expectancy between P4P and non-P4P groups.

In diabetes quality-assessment initiatives—especially those offering financial incentives to healthcare providers—structured care has been associated with improved long-term health outcomes for individuals with diabetes. Korea’s National Diabetes Quality Assessment Program (NDQAP), a nationwide initiative that evaluates the quality of diabetes care and provides performance-based financial incentives to healthcare institutions, has been shown to reduce the risk of diabetes-related complications such as proliferative diabetic retinopathy, end-stage kidney disease, amputation, myocardial infarction, and stroke as well as all-cause mortality [9]. Both South Korea and Taiwan operate national programs aimed at improving diabetes care through incentive-based strategies, but their approaches differ. Korea’s NDQAP primarily emphasizes process indicators to assess care quality and allocate incentives. In contrast, Taiwan’s P4P program incorporates both process and intermediate outcome indicators such as glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels, with provider payments increasingly tied to clinical improvements [8,9]. Financial incentives targeting process quality measures, which can be directly modified by providers, tend to produce greater effects than those targeting clinical outcomes, which can only be influenced indirectly [10,11]. However, each type of indicator has distinct strengths and limitations. Outcome indicators provide a more accurate reflection of clinical effectiveness but may incentivize risk selection, potentially disadvantaging patients with complex conditions or lower socioeconomic status. In contrast, process indicators are easier to implement and promote equity but may lead to ‘checkbox’ behaviors without corresponding improvements in health outcomes [12,13]. Recognizing these trade-offs, South Korea has recently begun incorporating outcome indicators such as HbA1c control rates as reference measures, signaling a gradual shift toward a more outcome-oriented evaluation framework.

In conclusion, early participation in national diabetes quality-assessment initiatives with incentive-based components may play a critical role in improving long-term health outcomes among individuals with T2DM. Accordingly, national diabetes management strategies should prioritize early access to high-quality, incentive-aligned care from the time of diagnosis.
