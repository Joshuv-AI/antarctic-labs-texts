Accelerated left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex transcranial magnetic stimulation for suicidality in patients with depressive disorders: A case series
Authors: Jithin T. Joseph, Ashok Jammigumpula, Abhiram N. Purohith, Sonia Shenoy, Amrtavarshini Radhakrishnan, PSVN Sharma, Samir K. Praharaj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Suicidality in depression presents significant management challenges, with limited treatment options such as electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and ketamine. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is an emerging intervention for such cases, particularly when ECT is contraindicated. This chart review examines the clinical outcomes of accelerated TMS (aTMS) targeting the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) for suicidality in depression patients.Cases:Six patients with depression and suicidality underwent aTMS targeting the left DLPFC between June 2024 to March 2025. Excitatory rTMS was administered using intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) at 100%–120% of RMT. Treatment sessions were delivered over 6.17 ± 1.86 days, with a 60-min intersession interval to maximize cortical excitability. Symptom severity was assessed using the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (CSSRS) as the primary outcome measure and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS). Significant reductions were observed in CSSRS scores (mean difference 11.87, 95% CI: 5.63, 18.0), corresponding to a 73.3% reduction in suicide severity. HDRS also improved significantly (mean difference 18.2, 95% CI: 13.50, 22.8), corresponding to a 73.1% reduction. All but one patient achieved a treatment response, and four patients met remission criteria. The interventions were well-tolerated, with no serious adverse effects.Conclusion:This study provides preliminary evidence supporting the efficacy, safety, and rapid reduction in suicidality with left DLPFC aTMS in depression. Randomized controlled trials are needed to validate these results, optimize treatment protocols, and assess the durability of clinical benefits.

INTRODUCTION

Suicide is a leading preventable cause of death globally, resulting in over 720,000 fatalities annually. Disproportionately, 73% of these deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, intensifying the global burden. Furthermore, for every suicide death, there are approximately 20 attempted suicides, highlighting the urgent need for preventive measures and support.[1] Mood disorders, including major depressive disorder (MDD) and bipolar disorder (BD), significantly elevate the risk of self-harm and suicide. Notably, a recent review of registry-based records revealed that individuals with MDD are 8.62 times more likely to die by suicide, while those with BD face an 8.66 times higher risk.[2] Lithium’s antisuicidal effects in mood disorders are well-established, but its delayed onset of action (6–8 weeks) limits its utility for acute intervention.[3] Although electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is a well-established treatment for acute suicide risk, its practical application is often limited by medical comorbidities, the requirement for anesthesia, and the risk of adverse effects.[4] Preliminary evidence supports the use of ketamine for suicidality, however, robust evidence is lacking.[56]

Non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS) techniques, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), have gained substantial evidence and regulatory approval for the treatment of depression.[7] TMS has also demonstrated effectiveness in reducing suicidality in patients with depression.[8] Accelerated TMS (aTMS), involving multiple sessions per day over a short period, has attracted significant attention as a rapid treatment for depression and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), and has recently received FDA approval.[910] Preliminary evidence suggests that aTMS may exert rapid antisuicidal effects, positioning it as a promising treatment option for mitigating acute suicidal risk in individuals with depressive disorders.[1112] This case series explores the use of aTMS as a therapeutic alternative for individuals with depressive disorders and acute suicidal ideation, particularly in cases where ECT was not preferred due to potential contraindications, feasibility issues, or patient preference for TMS.

METHODS

Chart review and sample

Six patients with depression and suicidal behavior (suicidal ideation or attempt), received aTMS between June 2024 and March 2025 at the Clinical Research Centre for Neuromodulation in Psychiatry, at Kasturba Medical College, Manipal, a tertiary care center in South India. Clinicians referred patients for therapeutic aTMS due to factors such as medical comorbidities, prior adverse effects, and the potential risk of cognitive adverse effects, which made ECT less preferable. Additionally, the need for swift symptom improvement also influenced this decision. All patients provided written informed consent, and the safety of undergoing TMS was assessed using the TMS Adult Safety Screen.[13] All patients continued their routine treatment, including medications.

Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (DLPFC rTMS)

We employed intermittent theta burst stimulation (iTBS) targeting the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) in patients with depression. For patients with comorbid depression and OCD, we utilized a sequential TBS protocol consisting of continuous TBS (cTBS) over the right DLPFC, followed by iTBS over the left DLPFC. Treatments were delivered using Cool-B70 Magventure Mag Pro X-100 coils with a posteroanterior direction and the coil positioned at a 45-degrees angle. The DLPFC was localized using the Beam F3 method.[14]

Resting motor thresholds (RMTs) were estimated using the Rossini-Rothwell method from the left motor hotspot corresponding to the first dorsal interosseous muscle for iTBS, and bilateral motor hotspots (right and left) for sequential TBS.[15] All patients received their sessions at 100%–120% of RMT, and side effects were monitored after each session using a TMS Side Effect Questionnaire.[16] A detailed description of each patient’s TMS protocol is provided in Table 1.

Outcome

We assessed symptom severity using the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (CSSRS)[17] and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS)[18] and monitored progress after every five sessions. For patients with comorbid OCD, changes in OCD severity were evaluated before and after treatment using the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS).[19] The primary outcome measure was the reduction in suicidal risk, as assessed by changes in CSSRS scores before and after aTMS treatment. Secondary outcome measures included the change in HDRS score before and after aTMS, as well as the percentage of responders (defined as >50% reduction in HDRS score) following treatment.

Statistical analysis

Paired t-tests were used to compare pre- and post-treatment severity of suicidality and depression, and effect sizes were calculated using the mean difference and Cohen’s d (with 95% confidence intervals). Any P value < 0.05 (2-tailed) was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

The patients (four males, two females) had a mean age of 44.7 ± 16.2 years (range: 17–61 years) and an illness duration of 11.1 ± 10.7 years (range: 6 months to 26 years). All but two patients had failed to respond to at least one antidepressant. A description of the patient demographics, failed medications, and current drug regimens is provided in Table 1.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation treatment characteristics

We administered an accelerated protocol consisting of five daily sessions of iTBS and two daily sessions of sequential TBS, totaling 20.83 ± 1.86 sessions over 6.17 ± 1.86 days. A total of 2400–3000 pulses were delivered per day, with a cumulative total of 16,500 ± 5,408 pulses over the acute treatment course of aTMS. At baseline, patients presented with severe suicidal ideation and depressive symptoms, as shown by mean scores of 16.2 ± 5.27 on the CSSRS and 24.8 ± 2.71 on the HDRS.

Clinical outcomes

The changes in CSSRS scores following aTMS treatment for individual patients are presented in Figure 1a. On average, CSSRS scores decreased by 11.87 points (95% CI: 5.63, 18.0), corresponding to a 73.3% reduction in suicide severity. Notably, all but one patient exhibited a greater than 50% reduction in CSSRS scores immediately post-intervention. A paired t-test revealed a significant difference between baseline (M = 16.2, SD = 5.27) and post-treatment CSSRS scores (M = 4.33, SD = 4.84, t (5) =4.90, P = 0.004, Cohen’s d = 2.0), with a large effect size [Figure 2a].

Following aTMS treatment, all patients exhibited the significant reduction in depressive symptoms, with a mean decrease of 18.2 points (95% CI: 13.50, 22.8) in their HDRS scores, corresponding to a 73.1% reduction. Figure 1b illustrates the changes in depression severity for individual patients from baseline to post-aTMS. This change was statistically significant from baseline (M = 24.8, SD = 2.71) to post-treatment (M = 6.67, SD = 5.13, t (5) =10.01, P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 4.09) [Figure 2b]. Notably, all but one patient achieved a treatment response (>50% reduction in HDRS), and four patients met remission criteria (HDRS < 8) after acute treatment. For the two patients with comorbid OCD, we observed a 40% and 81% reduction in YBOCS scores, respectively, following aTMS.

Adverse effects

The patients tolerated the treatment well and reported no serious adverse effects at therapeutic stimulation intensities of 100%–120% of RMT. None of the patients discontinued the treatment due to adverse effects. Most patients experienced mild side effects such as pain at the stimulation site, eyebrow twitches, coil pressure, and headaches, which resolved without active interventions.

DISCUSSION

Our results, which demonstrate a reduction in acute suicidality following aTMS, align with the growing body of evidence supporting the use of aTMS in mitigating acute suicide risk.[1112] A recent review of neuromodulation treatments for reducing suicidality identified ECT, TMS, and magnetic seizure therapy (MST) as promising treatment options.[8] We selected aTMS as a treatment option due to contraindications and patient preferences that precluded using ECT. ECT has been shown to rapidly reduce suicidal ideation, with improvements observed in 38% of patients after 1 week, 61% after 2 weeks, and 81% upon completion of treatment.[20] Although ECT has shown superior efficacy in reducing depressive symptoms and suicidality, its typical requirement of 6–9 sessions over 2–3 weeks may not be feasible in acute settings where rapid symptom reduction is necessary.[4]

Our study reveals that aTMS can substantially reduce suicidality within a week, a crucial timeframe for the acute management of suicidal risk.[21] This finding is corroborated by a recent study on fMRI-guided aTMS in depression, which also reported significant reductions in suicidality within five days, aligning with our observations.[12] We also found a significant reduction in depression severity following aTMS, with most patients achieving a treatment response. This is consistent with the established rapid efficacy of aTMS in treating depression and reflects a linear relationship between depression severity and suicidality.[22]

Our patient group, comprising individuals with unipolar and bipolar depression, as well as those with comorbid OCD, demonstrates that aTMS is a feasible option for targeting suicidality despite comorbidities. This finding is consistent with a previous review that reported the efficacy of TMS in treating depression with various psychiatric comorbidities.[23] Our choice of sequential stimulation targeting different brain regions is informed by the role of inhibitory TMS in OCD and early evidence supporting sequential and accelerated protocols that target multiple dysfunctional circuits in OCD treatment.[242526] Additionally, early evidence supports the use of aTMS targeting the left DLPFC in treating bipolar depression.[12] Our study further confirms that aTMS is a safe and feasible treatment option, even in acute crisis situations.[27]

Multiple mechanisms have been proposed for the antisuicidal effects of TMS. Neuroimaging studies implicate regions such as the ventral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, DLPFC, and insula in suicidal ideation and behavior.[28] Studies have shown that the therapeutic effects of TMS in depression are related to modulating the functional connectivity between DLPFC and the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC) and related structures.[29] These modulations also have implications for reducing suicidal ideations and behaviors also.[8] A recent Stanford Neuromodulation Treatment (SNT) study found that suicide prevention effects were correlated with enhanced connectivity between the insula and hippocampus, while antidepressant effects were linked to connections involving the sgACC, highlighting distinct neurobiological processes underlying suicidality.[12] Our series also demonstrates that administering 2 to 5 rTMS sessions per day is a feasible strategy for reducing suicidality in patients with depression.

The effect of TMS on emotional and cognitive control also plays a role in reducing suicidal ideation and heavier. Research suggests that dysregulation of GABA and serotonergic systems contributes to suicidal behavior, and TMS may help restore balance to these systems.[8] Another possible explanation for the antisuicidal effects of TMS is the reduction in depressive symptomatology. Since psychiatric disorders, particularly depression, are significant risk factors for suicide, effectively treating depression can lead to a decreased suicide risk.[8] Research has consistently shown a linear relationship between reduced depression severity and decreased suicidality scores after TMS, a trend also observed in our study.[30]

Our study has significant limitations, including its non-randomized design, lack of a comparison arm, unblinded assessment, and heterogeneous patient population. Despite these limitations, this case series supports the clinical utility and feasibility of aTMS as an alternative treatment option for acute suicide risk, particularly when ECT is not the preferred first-line treatment due to contraindications and potential adverse effects. These findings may also have implications for adolescents at acute suicide risk, as the use of ECT in minors is restricted in India and requires special permission, potentially leading to treatment delays.[31] Future research should prioritize direct comparisons between aTMS and ECT, as well as investigations into the long-term effects of aTMS, to further establish its efficacy and durability as a treatment option.

CONCLUSION

Accelerated TMS appears to be a safe and effective alternative for treating acute suicidality in depression, particularly when ECT is not feasible. It has been shown to rapidly reduce both suicidal and depressive symptoms. However, further controlled studies must confirm its efficacy and safety in a larger population and compare it with existing treatments.
