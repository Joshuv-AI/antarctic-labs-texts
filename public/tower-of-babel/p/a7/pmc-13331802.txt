Real-time contrast-enhanced ultrasound-guided percutaneous biopsy in the diagnosis of malignant solitary fibrous tumor of liver: A case report and review of the literature
Authors: Li Wang, Mengxi He, Yufei Wang, Jinzhe Wang, Haolin Shang, Li Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolitary fibrous tumor of liver (SFTL) is a rare mesenchymal neoplasm. Due to its low incidence and atypical clinical presentation, preoperative diagnosis is often challenging, making imaging features critical for its identification. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), a dynamic imaging technique, has been demonstrated to provide high diagnostic accuracy for hepatic lesions. This study reports the CEUS characteristics of a percutaneous biopsy-confirmed SFTL in an adult patient. The findings included peripheral hyperenhancement during the arterial phase, progressive centripetal filling with partial perfusion defects, and early washout commencing in the portal venous phase, which became more pronounced in the delayed phase.Keywords: Solitary fibrous tumor of liver, Case report, Contrast-enhanced ultrasound, Percutaneous biopsy

Introduction

Solitary Fibrous Tumor (SFT) is an uncommon mesenchymal neoplasm composed of spindle cells, originating from fibroblastic mesenchymal tissue that contains both spindle cells and collagen bundles [1]. SFT affects a broad age range, including young adults and the elderly, with no significant gender predominance, although some reports indicate a slight female preponderance [2]. Compared to other systemic tumors, the incidence of SFT is relatively low. It most commonly arises from serosal linings such as the pleura, peritoneum, and retroperitoneum, but can also occur in nonserosal sites including the central nervous system, orbit, lung, and mediastinum [3]. SFTL is exceptionally rare, with only approximately 117 cases documented in the medical literature to date [4]. Although most SFTLs follow a benign course, they possess malignant potential, with about 10%-20% classified as malignant and capable of local recurrence and metastasis [5]. Descriptions of the imaging features of SFTL in the medical literature are limited, with the majority of reports focusing on Computed Tomography (CT) and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) [6], leaving its characterization on CEUS rarely reported. This case report details a 53-year-old male patient diagnosed with multifocal SFTL lesions via real-time contrast-enhanced ultrasound-guided percutaneous biopsy. The CEUS features of this case were comprehensively analyzed and compared with the characteristic findings of 4 other principal hepatic mesenchymal tumors documented in the literature. This comparative analysis aims to identify key imaging clues for differential diagnosis and to discuss the potential pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these radiological presentations.

Case report

A 53-year-old male patient was found to have a hepatic space-occupying lesion during a routine physical examination at a local primary hospital fourteen days prior to admission. There were no abdominal pain, abdominal distension or other clinical symptoms. No lymph nodes were palpable. No significant change in body weight or family history of inherited disorders was noted. Laboratory findings revealed an elevated serum carcinoembryonic antigen level of 8.8 ng/mL (reference range: 0-5 ng/mL). Liver function tests showed abnormalities, including decreased total protein and albumin levels, with total protein at 64.2 g/L (reference range: 65-85 g/L) and albumin at 39.3 g/L (reference range: 40-55 g/L). In contrast, alkaline phosphatase and gamma-glutamyl transferase levels were increased, measuring 251 U/L (reference range: 56-119 U/L) and 155 U/L (reference range: 11-50 U/L), respectively.

Initial abdominal CT revealed multiple large, heterogeneous, slightly hypodense masses within the liver. The largest lesion, located in the right anterior lobe, measured approximately 13.0 × 10.9 cm with ill-defined borders. On contrast-enhanced scans, it showed marked heterogeneous enhancement during the arterial phase, with decreased enhancement in the portal venous and delayed phases (Fig. 1).

Abdominal ultrasonography was performed using a Aixplorer ultrasound system, convex transducer with frequency of 1-6 MHz were used. Conventional ultrasound imaging demonstrated multiple isoechoic masses in the liver, with well-defined boundaries and heterogeneous internal echogenicity. The largest lesion measured approximately 11.5 × 11.3 cm. Color Doppler Flow Imaging (CDFI) detected blood flow signals within the lesions. To further characterize the abnormalities, CEUS was conducted. After routine disinfection, a 20-gauge intravenous catheter was inserted into the left median cubital vein, and a 2.4 mL bolus of sulfur hexafluoride microbubble suspension was administered. CEUS revealed the presence of multiple enhancing hepatic lesions. Notably, all lesions exhibited peripheral hyperenhancement in the arterial phase, progressive centripetal filling with partial perfusion defects, followed by early washout beginning in the portal venous phase, which became more pronounced in the delayed phase (Fig. 3). These CEUS features suggested possible malignant pathology, prompting a real-time CEUS-guided percutaneous biopsy of the hepatic lesion. Prior to the procedure, the puncture site was localized using conventional ultrasound. Following standard sterilization and draping, local anesthesia was administered with 2% lidocaine. Under CEUS-guidance, a percutaneous liver biopsy was then performed on the largest lesion, with care taken to avoid major vessels and nonenhancing central areas. A total of 3 tissue cores were obtained. Throughout the procedure and during the subsequent 2-hour observation period, the patient remained hemodynamically stable, and no complications such as bleeding, pain, or vasovagal reactions were observed. The histology showed a neoplasm populated by fibroblast-like cells with short spindle or oval morphology. These cells displayed an irregular distribution in density and a disorganized growth pattern amidst a stroma abundant in collagen fibers (Fig. 4A). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed positive expression of CD34, BCL-2, CD99, STAT6, and vimentin, along with CD31 immunoreactivity confined to vascular endothelial cells. The Ki-67 proliferation index was approximately 10% (Fig. 4B–F). To exclude the possibility of an extrahepatic primary origin, particularly given the multifocal hepatic involvement, a comprehensive staging workup was performed. This included contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, as well as whole-body fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (18F-FDG PET/CT). No evidence of a primary tumor in the pleura, peritoneum, retroperitoneum, lung, mediastinum, orbit, or central nervous system was identified. Furthermore, there was no abnormal FDG uptake suggestive of an occult extrahepatic malignancy. Based on these negative findings and the histopathological confirmation of STAT6-positive spindle cell neoplasm, the diagnosis of primary multifocal solitary fibrous tumor of the liver (SFTL) was established.

Following admission, the patient was initiated on a nonsurgical treatment regimen consisting of bevacizumab (600 mg per administration) combined with temozolomide (250 mg per administration). However, re-evaluation after 2 cycles of therapy revealed that the lesions remained essentially unchanged in size, accompanied by an elevation in tumor marker levels. Consequently, the treatment strategy was switched to percutaneous hepatic arterial infusion chemotherapy combined with chemoembolization. The procedure was performed successfully. The patient tolerated the treatment well with no significant discomfort and currently maintenance therapy with regular follow-up.

Discussion

SFT is a rare mesenchymal tumor, initially documented in 1931, that commonly originates in the pleura [7]. Its occurrence in the liver is exceedingly rare. The clinical presentation of SFTL is often nonspecific. Patients are frequently asymptomatic, with tumors detected incidentally during examinations [8], as in the present case. Symptomatic presentations, such as dyspepsia or jaundice, are usually attributable to compression by a large mass [9,10]. This lack of distinctive symptoms underscores the critical role of accurate imaging analysis in achieving a diagnosis. CEUS, which utilizes intravascular microbubble contrast agents, enables real-time visualization of tumor microcirculation. This technique not only aids in differentiating benign from malignant lesions but also sensitively identifies viable tumor tissue versus necrotic areas, thereby guiding targeted biopsy for accurate diagnostic sampling and improving the biopsy positivity rate [11]. Accordingly, a retrospective review was conducted to evaluate the CEUS manifestations in a series of hepatic mesenchymal tumors (Table 1). Mesenchymal liver tumors occur more frequently in middle-aged to elderly females and commonly manifest as solitary, small, hypoechoic lesions. Hepatic Epithelioid Hemangioendothelioma (HEHE) typically demonstrates peripheral rim-like hyperenhancement during the arterial phase with an irregular nonenhancing central area, exhibiting an overall pattern of rapid enhancement followed by quick washout. Hepatic Angiosarcoma (HA) is characterized by irregular linear or patchy areas of marked arterial enhancement, which may persist into the portal venous phase, accompanied by extensive multifocal perfusion defects. Primary Hepatic Leiomyosarcoma (PHLS) often presents as a complex cystic-solid mass, with the solid components showing irregular wall enhancement and papillary projections; the enhancement tends to wash out slowly, frequently appearing isoenhancing in the portal phase, while the central region remains nonenhanced throughout all phases. Undifferentiated Embryonal Sarcoma of the Liver (UESL) classically shows intense enhancement of the peripheral rim with progressive centripetal filling and large internal areas of perfusion defect.

In this case, CEUS demonstrated early peripheral hyperenhancement during the arterial phase, with progressive centripetal filling accompanied by partial perfusion defects. This was followed by early washout commencing in the portal venous phase, which became more pronounced in the delayed phase. This “rapid washin and washout” pattern is consistent with previous reports by Lu et al. [12]. The CEUS presentation in this case shares certain similarities with but also exhibits critical distinctions from other hepatic mesenchymal tumors. Similar to HEHE and UESL, it demonstrates arterial phase peripheral enhancement with a centripetal filling tendency. The critical distinction, however, lies in the washout kinetics: both HEHE and UESL typically exhibit an early washout phase, commencing in the early portal venous phase (approximately 50 seconds), whereas washout in this SFTL case is comparatively delayed, initiating in the mid-portal venous phase (around 70 seconds). Furthermore, the morphology of the nonenhancing areas within the lesion differs: HEHE and UESL often exhibit irregular or extensive nonenhancing regions, while this SFTL lesion tends to present with small, well-defined focal nonenhancing areas. Compared to HA, which characteristically displays fine, disorganized reticular or branching arterial enhancement that may persist into the portal venous phase alongside extensive, irregular perfusion defects, this SFTL case shows relatively homogeneous peripheral enhancement, washout starting in the portal phase, and more localized, regular nonenhancing areas. The key differentiating feature from PHLS is that PHLS typically manifests as thick, rim-like arterial enhancement often remaining isoenhancing in the portal phase, whereas this SFTL presents with peripheral enhancement progressing to hypoenhancement in the portal phase. Furthermore, this case exhibits distinct features for differential diagnosis against some common hepatic conditions. Hepatic hemangiomas typically demonstrate “persistent moderate-to-marked enhancement in the delayed phase” [17], which is notably different from the rapid washout observed in our case. Primary hepatocellular carcinoma usually presents with “rapid, homogeneous enhancement throughout the entire lesion in the arterial phase” [18], in contrast to the centripetal filling with perfusion defects seen in the present case. By providing detailed hemodynamic information, these unique enhancement characteristics on CEUS serve as pivotal evidence for imaging-based differential diagnosis.

Although the CEUS findings showed a degree of specificity, definitive diagnosis still relies on histopathological and immunohistochemical examination. Microscopically, SFT is characterized by a proliferation of spindle-shaped stromal cells interspersed with collagen bands and branching vessels, typically exhibiting low to moderate cellularity. Tumor cells are distributed around thin-walled, dilated vessels, forming a hemangiopericytoma-like structure. Positive staining for CD34, BCL-2, and CD99 supports the diagnosis [1,2]. In the present case, tumor cells were positive for CD34, BCL-2, CD99, STAT6, CD31(vascular), and vimentin, with a Ki-67 proliferation index of approximately 10%. Integrating these immunomarkers, we posit that the CEUS manifestations essentially reflect aggressive biological behavior coupled with microenvironmental dysregulation. Based on the angiogenic potential and hypervascular immunophenotype conferred by positive CD34 expression, along with the presence of functional vascular channels validated by CD31 positivity restricted to the endothelium, and in conjunction with the histomorphological feature of peripherally enriched vascular distribution within this tumor entity, these factors collectively result in the preferential and abundant distribution of contrast agent to the peripheral regions during the arterial phase of CEUS, thereby giving rise to the characteristic peripheral hyperenhancement pattern [19]. Furthermore, nuclear STAT6 positivity is a marker of the NAB2-STAT6 gene fusion, which drives aberrant angiogenesis and cell proliferation, collectively establishing a biological foundation for rich but architecturally disorganized tumor vasculature [20]. Consequently, the incomplete centripetal filling and internal perfusion defects may reflect central collagen deposition, necrosis, or areas of low cellular density. Although the Ki-67 index of approximately 10% indicates that the tumor’s proliferative activity is not extremely high [21], the aberrant angiogenesis driven by the STAT6 fusion gene is sufficient to form immature tumor vessels with widened endothelial gaps. This defective vascular architecture causes the contrast agent microbubbles that entered during the arterial phase to rapidly extravasate from the hyperpermeable vessels as early as the portal venous phase, thereby manifesting as the characteristic early washout phenomenon.

CEUS-guided percutaneous biopsy allows real-time monitoring of the needle position, minimizing the risk of injuring surrounding critical structures [22]. In this case, postcontrast injection revealed centripetal filling with progressive peripheral hyperenhancement and internal perfusion defects. The relatively weaker enhancement in the central region suggested possible necrosis or liquefaction; therefore, biopsy sampling was directed toward the markedly enhancing areas, which ultimately yielded positive pathological results.

Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for SFTL. In light of the existence of multiple sizable hepatic lesions with concerning features for malignancy, the initial management for this patient involved a nonoperative approach with bevacizumab plus temozolomide. However, the response was suboptimal, with imaging showing no significant tumor regression and tumor marker levels increasing, reflecting the considerable challenge in treating SFT and the potential limitations of current medical therapies. Given these circumstances, the subsequent treatment was adjusted to Transarterial Chemoembolization (TACE). Long-term follow-up data are unavailable to evaluate the impact and efficacy of this approach for cases. Nevertheless, studies by Jin et al. [23] suggest that TACE, as a locoregional therapy, can be effective for some SFT cases. Additionally, research by Gou et al. [24], involving 42 SFT patients, confirmed that postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy can improve overall survival and local control rates.

Conclusions

This case stands out as remarkable. SFT, especially SFTL, is inherently rare. Additionally, the combination of large, multifocal lesions and malignant behavior posed a considerable diagnostic challenge. For this patient who was not a candidate for immediate surgery, CEUS-guided percutaneous biopsy proved to be a valuable diagnostic procedure. CEUS plays a complementary role in characterizing SFTLs, especially for lesions deemed indeterminate on conventional ultrasonography. CEUS offers a distinct perspective for differentiating these rare hepatic mesenchymal tumors by dynamically visualizing the sequential changes in their microvascular perfusion. The well-documented efficacy and safety profile of CEUS supports its integration as an adjunct to routine ultrasound in the evaluation and management of suspected SFTL cases.
