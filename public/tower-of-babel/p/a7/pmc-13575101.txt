Suppression of pre-monsoon tropical cyclones over the north Indian Ocean
Authors: Rongmie Emmanuel, Medha Deshpande, Radhika Kanase, Soumyajyoti Jana, Sahadat Sarkar, H Himabindu, Malay Ganai, Revanth Reddy B, Snehlata Tirkey, Piyush Ahire, Umesh Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPre-monsoon (April–June) tropical cyclones (TC) over the North Indian Ocean (NIO) pose severe coastal hazards. Surprisingly, in recent years like in 2025 and 2026 pre monsoon season there was not a single named TC reported over NIO. In this study, we explored the drivers for such a suppressed activity. We considered 46 years (1980–2025) of India Meteorological Department (IMD) best-track data and found that such situation occurred in 12 specific years during the analysis period. We considered those 12 years as non-TC years and to examine the contrast, we identified 10 TC-active years, where at least one TC occurred in both Bay of Bengal (BoB) and Arabian sea (AS) basins. Genesis Potential Parameter (GPP) composites reveal a basin-wide suppression during non-TC years, with a statistically significant difference of 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ concentrated over the BoB and AS. Logarithmic decomposition of the GPP shows that low-level vorticity is the primary driver of enhanced GPP over both basins during TC years, while reduced vertical wind shear provides additional positive support over the BoB. In general, the conditions are less favourable for the TC formation over the AS and are favourable over the BoB. But, during TC years, warmer SST, higher mid-level moisture and strong low-level positive vorticity supported the TC activity over the AS. Enhanced atmospheric moisture transport (IVT) and stronger large-scale upper-tropospheric diabatic heating during TC years reflect a pre-existing convective environment driven by organized large-scale forcing rather than TC-induced latent heat release. We further identify the MJO as the dominant large-scale modulator of pre-monsoon cyclogenesis, with TC activity systematically phase-locked to long duration and high-amplitude MJO phases 2–4 during active years, while suppressed years are characterized by weak or inactive MJO convective envelopes (RMM amplitude < 1.0). Wavenumber-frequency spectral analysis exhibits enhanced power in equatorial Rossby, Kelvin, and MRG waves alongside active MJO during TC years. Analysis of ENSO and IOD states reveals no systematic difference between TC and non-TC years. These findings suggest role of intraseasonal variability in modulating the large-scale kinematic forcing, alongside local thermodynamic conditions behind the cyclogenesis activity over the NIO.Keywords: Tropical cyclones, Genesis potential parameter, Atmospheric moisture transport, Madden Julian Oscillation, Cyclogenesis suppression, North Indian OceanSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Ocean sciences

Introduction

The North Indian Ocean (NIO), comprising the Bay of Bengal (BoB) and Arabian Sea (AS), represents a unique tropical cyclone (TC) basin that, despite contributing only 6–7% of global TC activity, accounts for over 80% of cyclone-related fatalities due to dense coastal populations and limited preparedness infrastructure1,2. These characteristics make understanding TC variability critical for seasonal forecasting, disaster preparedness, and climate adaptation strategies, particularly as ocean-atmosphere thermodynamic conditions evolve under climate change3.

A fundamental aspect of this variability is the basin’s distinctive bimodal seasonality, with peak activity during pre-monsoon (April-June) and post-monsoon (October-December) seasons, while monsoon months experience minimal cyclogenesis due to strong vertical wind shear4. Long-term records show an average of 5.5 cyclonic storms annually, with the BoB and AS contributing 4% and 2% of global TC frequency respectively5,2.

Recent research by Kim et al.6 reveals that the NIO pre-monsoon season features fewer TC seeds, but higher survival rates compared to post-monsoon periods, attributed to stronger convection and favourable moisture gradients.

Despite these favourable genesis conditions, long-term trends reveal complex basin-specific changes. Roose7 report a 43% decline in low-latitude NIO cyclones (5°N–11°N) since the 1980s, driven by weakened vorticity and increased vertical wind shear modulated by the Pacific Decadal Oscillation. In contrast, Deshpande et al.8 document a significant increase in AS cyclone frequency, lifetime maximum intensity, and duration during the recent epoch (2001–2019) relative to the earlier epoch (1982–2000), linked to enhanced basin warming, increased ocean-to-atmosphere latent heat flux, and elevated relative humidity and moist static energy.

Beyond these long-term thermodynamic changes, the dynamic interannual TC variability over the NIO is strongly modulated by large-scale climate modes9. El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) significantly influences activity, with La Niña phases enhancing and El Niño suppressing cyclogenesis, particularly over the BoB10–12. The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) has emerged as an increasingly important factor since 2005, with negative IOD years providing favourable conditions through warm eastern Indian Ocean Sea Surface Temperature (SST)13,14. Additionally, the Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO) modulates cyclogenesis through sub seasonal variations in vorticity, humidity, and wind shear, with nearly all very severe cyclonic storms associated with the convective MJO phase and favourable equatorial Rossby and Kelvin wave patterns15,16; Sharma et al.17). While these factors are individually well documented, no prior study has simultaneously diagnosed basin-wide pre-monsoon TC suppression across both NIO basins within a long-term climatological framework, quantitatively attributed suppression to basin-specific mechanisms, or established the MJO and convectively coupled equatorial wave (CCEW) as a necessary rather than merely modulating precondition for genesis. This study addresses these gaps using a 46-year record (1980–2025). Climatologically, post-monsoon season is active season with every year there is a TC activity over the NIO, whereas there are years when no TC occurs during pre-monsoon season. The 2025 and 2026 pre-monsoon season witnessed no named tropical cyclone formation over the NIO, a recurrence of the suppressed activity last observed in 2012. This prompted a broader scientific question: how frequently does complete suppression of pre-monsoon TC activity occur over the NIO, and what large-scale environmental conditions drive it?

Data and methodology

The India Meteorological Department (IMD) best-track datasets (https://rsmcnewdelhi.imd.gov.in/report.php?internal_menu=MzM=)﻿ was used for tropical cyclone observations over the north Indian ocean (NIO). Since a few tropical cyclones also form in June, the pre monsoon cyclone season was defined as 1April to 15 June for this study.

A 10-day low-pass Lanczos filter was first applied to all atmospheric and oceanic parameters prior to compositing, to remove high-frequency transient signals associated with passing TCs, depressions, and deep depressions. The seasonal mean for each year was subsequently computed from the filtered fields over 1st April to 15th June before compositing across TC and non-TC year subsets. The 10-day cutoff is consistent with established practice for isolating the large-scale background environment from synoptic-scale TC-related variability18,19.

To assess the oceanic contribution to cyclogenesis, we analyzed SST data obtained from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) daily Optimum Interpolation Sea Surface Temperature (OISST) version 2 dataset20, which has a spatial resolution of 0.25° (~ 25 km) and is available at.

https://www.psl.noaa.gov/data/gridded/data.noaa.oisst.v2.highres.html. SST anomalies were computed with respect to the 1991–2020 climatology, following the latest WMO-recommended 30-year climatological normal and its common use in climate anomaly monitoring (WMO, NCEI C3S21–24. The full 1980–2025 period was retained for TC frequency analysis to maximise the TC sample size.

The Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) data is obtained from Modern-Era Retrospective analysis for Research and Applications, Version 2 (MERRA-2). The MERRA-2 AOD dataset provides a global record of aerosol conditions with a spatial resolution of 0.625°, covering the period from 1980 to the present. The dataset is produced by combining satellite observations, ground-based measurements, and simulations from the Goddard Earth Observing System, version-5 atmospheric model. In this study, hourly datasets are used. The composite for this study is done using the variable total aerosol extinction at 550 nm. The data is available at https://disc.gsfc.nasa.gov/datasets/M2T1NXAER_5.12.4/summary. Atmospheric variables are obtained from the ECMWF Reanalysis 5th Generation (ERA5) available at.

(https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/cdsapp#!/dataset/reanalysis-era5-pressure-levels), which has a horizontal resolution of 0.25°25. The analysis uses pressure level data of 27 vertical levels from 1000 − 100 hPa and 6 hourly temporal resolution. The pressure levels data includes vertical velocity, temperature, specific humidity, relative vorticity, zonal and meridional wind components. ERA5 provides enhanced spatial and temporal resolution, enabling improved representation of convective updrafts, tropical waves, and the dynamic and thermodynamic structures associated with TCs and meso- to synoptic-scale atmospheric processes26. These parameters are used to compute different dynamical and thermodynamical fields such as vertical wind shear (VWS), genesis potential parameter (GPP), diabatic heat source (Q1), Integrated Vapor Transport (IVT).

These derived metrics are fundamental indicators of environmental favourability. For example, vertical wind shear defined here as the magnitude of the vector difference between 850 hPa and 200 hPa winds becomes prohibitive for cyclone development when it exceeds approximately 10 m s⁻¹27.

While VWS acts as a primary destructive force, cyclogenesis ultimately depends on the interplay between this shear and favourable atmospheric conditions. Their analysis showed that the first three factors favour intensification, while VWS inhibits the development of low-pressure systems. Results and discussions

First to understand if it is normal or unusual to have no cyclone activity over NIO, we studied the tropical cyclone (TC) frequency over the NIO during pre-monsoon (April-June) and post-monsoon seasons (October-December) (Fig. 1a and b). In past 46 years since 1980, there were 12 such years (2025, 2012, 2011, 2005, 1995, 1993, 1988, 1986, 1984, 1983, 1981 and 1980) when no named TC formed over the NIO during pre-monsoon as per IMD and Joint Typhoon Warning centre (JTWC) records (Fig. 1a). So, in historical perspective, no TC activity during pre-monsoon season is not a rare event though not a very frequent occurrence. The frequency distribution of TC per year shows the possibility of not having any named TC during pre-monsoon is 25% whereas it is 0% for post monsoon season.

(a) Annual tropical cyclone frequency over the North Indian Ocean during 1980–2025. (b) Frequency distribution of the number of TCs per year. (c, d) Same as (a) and (b), respectively, but restricted to the pre-monsoon season and shown separately for the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea.

The post-monsoon season exhibits a different distribution not a single year in the 46-year recorded zero TCs, confirming that TC genesis during October–December occurs every year. The post-monsoon distribution extends up to 5 TCs per year (observed twice, in 1980 and 1992, from Fig. 1a), while the pre-monsoon season has never exceeded 3 TCs in a single year (Fig. 1b), and the overwhelming majority of pre-monsoon years recorded only 1 or 2 TCs. The mean annual TC count during pre-monsoon is approximately 1.1 TCs/year compared to roughly 2.5 TCs/year during post-monsoon.

The North Indian ocean is divided into two basins AS and BoB. The BoB is the preferred basin for pre-monsoon TC formation, with BoB recording zero TCs in only ~ 18 out of 46 years (~ 39%), compared to the AS which recorded zero TCs in ~ 30 years (~ 65%) (Fig. 1c and d). A single TC per year is the most probable outcome (~ 41%) during pre-monsoon, while years with 2 or more TCs are relatively rare (~ 32% combined). Years with elevated TC counts (≥ 2) in a single basin are rare and confined to only four instances in the 46-year record BoB recorded 2 TCs in 1982, 1991, 2009, and 2017, while AS recorded 2 TCs in 2010 and 2018.

Our aim is to understand the reasons behind suppressed activity of TCs over both the basins during pre-monsoon season, we divided our datasets into two subsets, TC years and non-TC years. TC years (1985, 1994, 1998, 2004, 2007, 2010, 2019, 2020, 2021, 2023) are the years, when there is at least one named TC over both the basins and non-TC years (1980, 1981, 1983, 1984, 1986, 1988, 1993, 1995, 2005, 2011, 2012, and 2025) are the years when there is absence of TC in both the basins. This classification is physically motivated requiring TC activity in both basins ensures the large-scale NIO basin wide environment was genuinely favourable for cyclogenesis, while requiring zero TCs in both basins ensures basin-wide suppression. Years with TC activity in only one basin represent mixed environmental states and are excluded to avoid ambiguity in the composite signal. The central objective of this study is to understand seasonal-scale basin-wide suppression rather than event-scale genesis dynamics, making seasonal composites the appropriate diagnostic tool. Event-based anomaly diagnostics require the existence of TC genesis events and are therefore not applicable to the non-TC years that are central to this analysis. We present the composites of dynamic, thermodynamic and kinematic parameters during TC and non-TC years. The GPP proposed by Kotal et al.28 is an integrated metric consisting of low-level vorticity, vertical wind shear, mid-tropospheric relative humidity, and atmospheric instability. Composite analyses of GPP reveal difference between cyclone and non-cyclone years (Fig. 2). During active cyclone years, the NIO exhibits enhanced genesis potential (Fig. 2a). GPP maxima (> 0.5 × 10⁻⁵) dominate over the BoB region (8°–18° N, 82°–95° E) and AS region (8°–16° N, 65°–75° E), spatially aligned with the TC genesis locations. Non-TC years exhibit a broadly similar distribution of GPP (Fig. 2b) but with systematically reduced magnitudes across both basins. Over the BoB, GPP values is below 0.25 × 10⁻⁵, a reduction of over 50% relative to active years. Strikingly, even the southeastern BoB, typically the basin’s most active genesis region, experiences a severe drop in GPP during suppressed TC activity years, inhibiting genesis. This inhibition is even more pronounced in the AS, where GPP is less, with near-zero GPP values over the latitude range from 10° to 18° N.

We now decompose the GPP to identify which parameters contribute to the contrasting tropical cyclone activity. Atmospheric instability, measured here in terms of the temperature difference between the 850 hPa and 500 hPa levels (T850–T500), provides a direct index of the environment lapse rate governing deep convective organization essential for TC genesis (Fig. 3a-c). Higher T850–T500 values indicate a steeper tropospheric lapse rate, reflecting greater conditional instability favourable for sustained deep convection and cyclogenesis. During TC years, genesis locations in both basins are broadly co-located with regions of elevated instability (Fig. 3a). Over the Arabian Sea genesis belt (8°–16°N, 65°–75°E), T850–T500 values reach 26–28 °C, comparatively higher than BoB, while over the BoB (8°–16°N, 82°–95°E) exhibits values in the range of 24–27 °C, confirming that cyclone formation preferentially occurs where the atmosphere sustains a sufficiently steep convective lapse rate to support deep organized convection. Interestingly, in non-TC year shows similar feature (Fig. 3b). Even in these years, there is no major change in the temperature patterns across either ocean basin. The atmosphere remains just as unstable, suggesting that a lack of storms isn’t caused by a lack of atmospheric instability. The composite difference (Fig. 3c) reveals that the core AS and BoB genesis regions exhibit negative differences (− 0.10 to − 0.20 °C), indicating that non-TC years possess slightly greater thermodynamic instability over these regions compared to active TC years. Despite this marginally steeper lapse rate, cyclogenesis is completely absent during suppressed years. Positive differences exceeding + 0.10 °C emerge only over the western AS and the easternmost BoB and Andaman Sea, while remaining negligibly small across the genesis zones. This implies that thermodynamic instability favourable during non-TC years. Thus, the source of this suppression therefore lies elsewhere, motivating a systematic examination of the dynamic and moisture-related components of the GPP.

Having examined thermodynamic instability, we now turn to mid-level (averaged over 500–700 hPa layer) relative humidity (Fig. 3d-f). Mid-tropospheric moisture is essential for sustaining deep convection by limiting entrainment-driven dilution of convective updrafts, making it a critical component of the GPP. However, the humidity patterns for both active (Fig. 3d) and suppressed (Fig. 3e) TC years are remarkably similar. Both exhibit a clear southeast-to-northwest gradient, ranging from very dry conditions (below 30–40%) over the northwestern AS to very moist conditions (exceeding 70–80%) over the eastern BoB, Andaman Sea, and equatorial waters. Non-TC years maintain an equally moist mid-troposphere across the genesis zones.

The composite difference (Fig. 3f) reinforces this finding, with differences confined within ± 3.0% across the entire basin. Slight positive value (+ 1.5 to + 3.0%) appear over the AS and the equatorial belt during TC years. Strikingly, over the central and eastern BoB, the regions of cyclone formation, mid-level moisture is lower during active TC years (− 1.5 to − 3.0%) relative to non-TC years. Since both thermodynamic instability and mid-level moisture indicate the environment favourable for TC particularly over the BoB region during non-TC years, we next examine kinematic forcings like low-level vorticity and VWS.

We examine VWS of horizontal winds at 200 and 850 hPa levels (Fig. 3 g-i), an inhibitor of tropical cyclogenesis that disrupts vortex’s alignment in vertical43; Tang and Emanuel.44). Unlike the thermodynamic fields examined previously, VWS exhibits contrast between active and suppressed TC years over the genesis regions.

Both composites (Fig. 3 g and h) display broadly similar VWS, characterized by a strong latitudinal gradient across the NIO. Shear is highly unfavourable, exceeding 15–20 m s⁻¹, over the equatorial Indian Ocean and the far northern AS and coastal latitudes, while a broad belt of moderate shear (10–15 m s⁻¹) extends across the central basins encompassing the genesis zones of both the AS and BoB. The composite difference (Fig. 3i) reveals a spatially heterogeneous signal. Over the BoB, negative differences of − 0.5 to − 1.0 m s⁻¹ extend broadly across 8°–20°N, 80°–100°E, and the genesis cluster in this basin lies within this reduced-shear zone, indicating that TC years consistently experience lower deep-layer shear at the locations where cyclones form. The AS picture is less uniform: negative values of comparable magnitude are confined to the southwestern sector (5°–12°N, 52°–65°E), where the southernmost AS genesis points reside, while genesis locations in the central and northeastern AS (12°–16°N, 65°–72°E) fall within near-neutral or weakly positive difference values. Positive difference (+ 0.5 to + 1.0 m s⁻¹) is concentrated over the northern AS, the Indian coastline, and the equatorial belt regions that are dynamically unfavourable for cyclogenesis regardless of year type, so their increased shear in TC years carries no practical implication for genesis. To complete the kinematic picture, we next examine low-level relative vorticity (ζ₈₅₀; Fig. 4j–l), which is the key dynamic precursor for the development of organized rotation from disorganized convective disturbances.

During active TC years (Fig. 3j), positive vorticity values of 1.0–2.0 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ extend broadly across the central and southwestern BoB, the waters surrounding Sri Lanka, while the AS exhibits weaker but coherent cyclonic anomalies over its northeastern and central portions. The genesis locations across both basins are broadly embedded within or at the margins of these enhanced vorticity belts, confirming the physical coupling between background cyclonic rotation and preferred cyclogenesis zones. Notably, a subset of genesis events occurs in regions of near-zero or negative ζ₈₅₀, suggesting that sufficiently favourable thermodynamic conditions or transient synoptic-scale forcing can occasionally support vortex initiation even in the absence of a pre-existing positive vorticity background. Non-TC years (Fig. 3k), by contrast, show reduced ζ₈₅₀ over the open ocean, with values across the central BoBand AS is below 0.5 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ insufficient to support the low-level cyclonic organization required for vortex development. The difference of relative vorticity of TC and non-TC years (Fig. 3 L) isolates the vorticity enhancement during active years, positive value of + 0.24 to + 0.48 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ concentrated over the northeastern BoB and the central Bay genesis cluster, and weaker positive difference over the central AS. These positive differences reflect not only regions of stronger cyclonic vorticity in TC years but also regions of negative vorticity, where it is comparatively less pronounced than in non-TC years, indicating a systematic suppression of anticyclonic tendency across the genesis zones during active years. Also, it is observed that the positive difference of vorticity is comparatively higher over BoB genesis zone than that of Arabian sea. Negative difference over portions of the southern AS and eastern BoB indicate that the vorticity enhancement is spatially selective rather than basin wide. In TC years, low-level vorticity and reduced shear are systematically collocated with genesis locations over the BoB, a weaker but broadly consistent signal is also evident over the AS, suggesting that large-scale dynamical modulation, though of differing magnitudes, operates across both basins.

Next, we examine the column-integrated water vapor transport (IVT, Fig. 6a-c). Both active (Fig. 6a) and suppressed (Figu. 6b) years are characterized by a climatologically dominant moisture transport structure, with peak IVT values exceeding 300–400 kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹ concentrated over the equatorial Indian Ocean and the waters immediately south of Sri Lanka, representing the large-scale cross-equatorial moisture flux feeding both basins. Over the AS, IVT values during both TC and non-TC years range between 100 and 200 kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹ across the central and southeastern region. It is observed that in TC active years the IVT is maximum over BoB genesis zone compared to AS and the value ranges above 250 kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹.

The composite difference (Fig. 6c) reveals a physically crucial and spatially coherent redistribution of moisture transport during active TC years. Strong positive IVT differences of + 10.0 to + 20.0 kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹ dominate AS genesis belt (8°–18°N, 60°–75°E) and extend across the BoB (10°–18°N, 82°–92°E), spatially coinciding with the region of cyclogenesis clusters in both basins. Notably, a statistically significant positive IVT difference is also evident over the northeastern AS and the western Indian coastline, suggesting an enhanced poleward moisture flux channelling additional water vapor toward the genesis zones during active years. In sharp contrast, pronounced negative differences of -10.0 to -20.0 kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹ dominate the eastern BoB and the Andaman Sea, indicating that during TC years moisture transport is actively redirected away from the eastern basin and steered preferentially into the western and central genesis regions. This differential moisture routing rather than a simple basin-wide enhancement demonstrates that cyclone formation over the BoB and AS is not solely dependent on local thermodynamic conditions but is instead sustained by an enhanced large-scale moisture delivery from remote sources, compensating for the modest local SST values and slightly reduced mid-level humidity identified in the earlier analyses.

To examine how dynamically transported moisture is converted into thermodynamic energy for vortex development, we analyze spatial composites of the apparent heat source (Q1; Fig. 7a-i), a diagnostic of diabatic heating driven primarily by latent heat release from deep organized convection. Figure 7a and c present Q1 for 300 hPa for TC years, non-TC years, and their difference; Fig. 7d, f, g and i show the same for 400 hPa and 500 hPa pressure levels. It is important to note that the Q1 fields presented here are derived from 10-day low-pass filtered data. Since the TC lifecycle operates on timescales of 5–8 days, the filtering effectively removes any direct TC-induced heating contribution from the composites. The Q1 differences between TC and non-TC years therefore reflect differences in the large-scale, pre-existing convective environment specifically the background diabatic heating associated with organized large-scale moisture convergence and the MJO convective envelope rather than the heating generated by the TCs themselves.

At 300 hPa (Fig. 7a and c), TC years show moderate heating (~ 3–4 K Day⁻¹) over the BoB (5°–15°N, 80°–95°E) and strongly suppressed values (< 2 K Day⁻¹) over the northern AS. The difference field (Fig. 7c) reveals positive Q1 in TC years over the AS (10°–20°N, 58°–72°E) and over the BoB, (80°–87°E, 10°–15°N).

At 400 hPa (Fig. 7d and f), TC years exhibit elevated heating of 4–7 K Day⁻¹ over the BoB (5°–15°N, 80°–100°E), with genesis locations clustering near these maxima. Non-TC years show a modestly weaker and more spatially confined heating signal, particularly over the central and western BoB (75°–88°E, 8°–15°N). The difference field (Fig. 7f) shows the clearest basin-level contrast: a spatially coherent positive signal (0.5–1.0 K Day⁻¹) across the central AS (10°–20°N, 60°–75°E) with statistically significant stippling, alongside positive differences along the western BoB coinciding with the genesis region.

At 500 hPa (Fig. 7g and i), TC years show a pronounced heating maximum in the eastern equatorial BoB (~ 90°–100°E, 2°–12°N; up to 6–7 K Day⁻¹) and a heating zone extending westward across the southern BoB. Non-TC years exhibit a similar but spatially reduced pattern with less westward extension of the heating zone. The difference field (Fig. 7i) shows the most spatially extensive positive signal across the regions, with widespread TC-year enhancement over the central and southern AS (5°–20°N, 55°–75°E) and the western-central BoB (75°–87°E, 8°–15°N), while the northeastern BoB again shows weakly negative differences.

Overall, the Q1 composites confirm that TC years are associated with systematically enhanced mid-to-upper tropospheric diabatic heating over the central AS at all levels (300, 400, and 500 hPa) and over the western-central BoB near the genesis cluster. This spatially coherent heating enhancement indicates that the additional moisture flux identified in the IVT analysis for TC-years is effectively converted into latent heat release aloft, providing the thermodynamic forcing necessary to build and sustain the warm core structure required for organized tropical cyclone development over the NIO. The consistency of enhanced Q1 across multiple pressure levels (300, 400, and 500 hPa) during TC years, after removal of TC-induced synoptic heating through the 10-day low-pass filter, confirms that this signal represents a robust large-scale thermodynamic precondition for cyclogenesis rather than feedback from TC activity.

Further we examined the AOD at 550 nm. The composite of AOD for TC years (Fig. 8a), non-TC years (Fig. 8b) and difference (Fig. 8c) are shown. During TC years over the AS (Fig. 8a), AOD is moderate to high, ranging from 0.2 to 0.4, with values increasing towards the northern and coastal regions. Over the BoB, AOD is similarly elevated, around 0.3 to 0.5, especially in the northern bay and near the Myanmar/Southeast Asia coast. The genesis locations (black dots) cluster in areas of moderate AOD (0.2–0.35). During non-TC years (Fig. 8b), the spatial pattern looks broadly similar to TC years, but the BoB shows lower AOD in the central and southern region. The AS pattern is comparable, though the high AOD over the northern AS appears somewhat less intense than in TC years.

Composite of aerosol optical depth (AOD) at 550 nm for (a) TC years, (b) non-TC years, and (c) the difference between TC and non-TC years. Stippled regions indicate statistically significant differences at the 95% confidence level.

Active TC years exhibit higher aerosol loading over both basins compared to suppressed years (Fig. 8c), with positive AOD differences of + 0.03 to + 0.05 at 550 nm over the AS genesis region (5°–15°N, 60°–80°E) and positive differences also evident over the BoB (5°–20°N). Consistent with prior work demonstrating that anthropogenic emissions of black carbon and sulphate aerosols alter the large-scale atmospheric circulation over the AS with consequent reductions in deep-layer vertical wind shear45, the regions of significantly higher aerosol loading during active TC years broadly correspond to the regions of reduced shear. However, it is important to note that AOD is not interpreted here as a direct causal driver of TC genesis. Rather, it is treated as a co-varying indicator of the large-scale circulation state. The primary TC genesis regions over the open AS and BoB are situated away from the strongest continental aerosol outflow zones, where near-coast black carbon concentrations are highest due to continental transport46–48. The elevated AOD during TC years is therefore best understood as a proxy for the enhanced low-level south-westerly flow associated with active MJO phases, which simultaneously transports both moisture and aerosols into the basin49. We acknowledge that under certain conditions aerosols can suppress TC activity by stabilizing the lower troposphere or offsetting intensification50,51, and these effects are not mutually exclusive with the large-scale circulation signal identified here.

To identify the large-scale atmospheric driver responsible for the favourable kinematic and thermodynamic environment documented in the preceding analyses, we examine the convective signature of the MJO through outgoing longwave radiation (OLR). To quantitatively validate this intraseasonal control, we track the duration, amplitude and phase propagation of the MJO using Real-time Multivariate MJO (RMM) index phase space diagrams (Fig. 9). The RMM index characterizes the MJO through two principal components (RMM1 and RMM2), where displacement from the origin represents MJO amplitude and the angular position identifies the phase, with amplitudes exceeding unity (outside the unit circle) indicating a dynamically active MJO.

Real-time multivariate MJO (RMM) index during (a) TC years and (b) non-TC years. Blue dots mark the dates of cyclonic disturbances and their corresponding MJO phase at the time of occurrence. Red shading indicates the number of days spent in each MJO phase.

During active TC years (Fig. 9a), the background RMM density distribution reveals a broad, high-amplitude spread across multiple phases, with the maximum duration concentrated in the phases 2–4 sector corresponding to the Indian Ocean and Maritime Continent convective envelope. The MJO background state during TC years is characterized by frequent excursions well beyond the unit circle, confirming sustained periods of strong intraseasonal forcing across the pre-monsoon season. The overlaid cyclone lifecycle trajectories reveal a physically profound phase-locking relationship: cyclogenesis initiation points (blue dots) are concentrated within phases 2–4, precisely the phases during which the MJO convective envelope is positioned over the Indian Ocean and Maritime Continent, simultaneously enhancing low-level vorticity, suppressing vertical wind shear, and driving anomalous moisture transport into the NIO genesis zones.

In contrast, non-TC years (Fig. 9b) show little to no coherent MJO activity. The background RMM MJO index density is almost entirely confined within the unit circle, with the distribution tightly clustered near the origin, confirming that the MJO amplitude remains persistently weak or effectively inactive throughout the pre-monsoon season during non-TC years. No coherent phase propagation is evident, and the absence of cyclone confirms the direct correspondence between MJO inactivity and cyclogenesis suppression. It is also noteworthy that TC years are distinguished not only by higher MJO amplitude but also by longer duration of active MJO phases (Fig. 9a and b), further strengthening the role of sustained intraseasonal forcing as a necessary precondition for pre-monsoon TC genesis. Further, we performed a wavenumber-frequency spectral analysis of OLR anomalies for TC and non-TC years (15°S–15°N) following Wheeler and Kiladis36(Fig. 10, left column). During TC years, the symmetric power spectrum (Fig. 10, left column) shows enhanced Kelvin wave signal along the theoretical dispersion curve (eastward propagating, zonal wavenumbers 0–10, frequencies 0.06–0.25 cpd), along with a clear eastward propagating MJO signal at low zonal wavenumbers (0–5) and westward equatorial Rossby wave (0 to -10). The WK spectra are broadly consistent with the stronger MJO activity inferred from the RMM analysis during TC years. Non-TC years show substantially weaker Kelvin wave, MJO and equatorial Rossby wave power. The anti-symmetric spectra (Fig. 10, right column) show a clearer distinction in the mixed Rossby-Gravity (MRG) wave band. TC years exhibit a broader and more coherent westward propagating MRG signal over zonal wave numbers approximately − 10 to − 5, and frequencies between 0.12 and 0.3125 cpd, whereas non-TC years display comparatively weaker and incoherent MRG signal in the same band. This result is particularly noteworthy because previous studies have shown that MRG disturbances can provide favourable low level cyclonic vorticity and evolve into tropical depression type disturbances that contribute to tropical cyclogenesis52,33,38.

Conclusions

This study provides a process-based diagnostic assessment of pre-monsoon tropical cyclogenesis over the NIO, focussing on why certain years experience a complete cyclone suppression across both the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. The climatology confirms a strong basin asymmetry, with the BoB dominating pre-monsoon TC activity and genesis confined to a preferred latitudinal band. By contrasting years with the presence of TC activity in both basins against years with no TC formation in either basin, the study identifies basin-wide suppression of favourable large-scale conditions as the primary reason for non-TC years.

GPP analysis shows that suppressed years are characterized by a significant basin-wide reduction in genesis favourability. Among the GPP components, conventional thermodynamic controls such as lapse-rate instability and mid-tropospheric relative humidity do not strongly distinguish TC and non-TC years. In contrast, reduced vertical wind shear, enhanced low-level relative vorticity, and favourable SST anomalies contribute more clearly to active years. Quantitative decomposition identifies relative vorticity as the dominant contributor to enhanced GPP over both the BoB and AS, while reduced shear plays an important supporting role, particularly over the BoB.

The AOD at 550 nm shows that the region where cyclogenesis is dominant, the AOD is comparatively higher in TC years than in non-TC years. The spatial correspondence between enhanced aerosol loading and reduced vertical wind shear in TC years is consistent with the established role of aerosols in modulating large-scale atmospheric circulation over the NIO45. However, AOD is interpreted here as a secondary co-varying environmental indicator rather than a primary cause of cyclogenesis. The dominant suppression mechanism is instead linked to vertical wind shear, low-level vorticity, mid-tropospheric humidity, moisture transport, diabatic heating, and intraseasonal forcing.

Column-integrated vapor transport shows that active TC years are not simply wetter across the basin but are marked by targeted moisture transport into the AS and BoB genesis regions. This targeted moisture, rather than a simple basin-wide increase, provides the pre-conditioning for deep organized convection independent of local SST or humidity conditions.

Consistently, Q1 composites derived from 10-day low-pass filtered data show enhanced mid- to upper-tropospheric diabatic heating at 300–500 hPa over the central AS and western-central BoB during TC years. This heating reflects a favourable pre-existing large-scale convective environment, likely associated with MJO-organized convection, rather than direct TC-induced heating.

The MJO emerges as the key large-scale forcing that organizes the favourable chain of processes: reduced shear, enhanced vorticity, stronger moisture transport, and deeper diabatic heating. RMM phase-space analysis shows that active TC years are associated with sustained MJO amplitudes exceeding the active threshold, especially during phases 2–4, where observed genesis points are strongly concentrated during 1980–2025. In contrast, non-TC years exhibit persistently weak MJO activity throughout the pre-monsoon season. This establishes the MJO not merely as a modulating factor, but as a necessary large-scale precondition for pre-monsoon NIO cyclogenesis.

Wavenumber–frequency analysis further shows that TC years exhibit enhanced Kelvin, equatorial Rossby, and mixed Rossby–gravity wave activity, while non-TC years show suppressed convectively coupled equatorial wave power. This indicates that multi-scale tropical wave interactions provide important synoptic-scale convective precursors for cyclogenesis. ENSO and IOD analysis, however, reveals no systematic distinction between TC and non-TC years, suggesting that these interannual modes do not directly explain the observed suppression.

Overall, the study presents a physically consistent framework for understanding complete pre-monsoon TC suppression over the NIO. It advances existing knowledge by: (i) providing the first simultaneous basin-wide diagnosis of TC suppression over both the AS and BoB within a 46-year framework, (ii) identifying basin-specific suppression mechanisms through quantitative GPP decomposition, and (iii) establishing sustained active MJO forcing as a necessary precondition for pre-monsoon TC formation. The results also highlight the need for improved representation of MJO-driven circulation, moisture transport, air–sea interaction, and aerosol–radiation effects in forecast systems to enhance probabilistic early warning of tropical cyclogenesis over this vulnerable basin. Event-scale analysis of individual genesis cases within active seasons remains an important direction for future work.
