Gustave Roussy Immune (GRIm) score as a novel prognostic biomarker for gastrointestinal cancers: a meta-analysis of sixteen studies
Authors: Xiaolian Deng, Tingting Yan, Huayang Pang, Jinlai Wei, Chunxue Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Gustave Roussy immune (GRIm) score has been widely recognized as a promising prognostic biomarker in various malignancies. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the association between pretreatment GRIm score and survival outcomes in patients with gastrointestinal (GI) cancers.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted using electronic databases (Web of Science, PubMed, Cochrane Library and Embase) from inception to January 15, 2026. The primary endpoints included long-term survival outcomes such as overall survival (OS), disease-free survival (DFS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), progression-free survival (PFS), and cancer-specific survival (CSS). Pooled hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using a random-effects model. The meta-analysis was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261291292).ResultsThis meta-analysis included 16 studies comprising 4,904 patients with GI cancers. The pooled outcomes demonstrated that patients with a high GRIm score had significantly worse OS (15 studies; HR = 2.01; 95% CI: 1.69–2.40; P < 0.01; I² = 63%), PFS (8 studies; HR = 1.57; 95% CI: 1.29–1.91; P < 0.01; I² = 48%), DFS (2 studies; HR = 2.15; 95% CI: 1.35–3.42; P < 0.01; I² = 55%) and CSS (2 studies; HR = 1.68; 95% CI: 1.17–2.41; P < 0.01; I² = 4%) compared to those with a low score. However, a single study assessing RFS reported that the GRIm score lacked independent prognostic significance for this endpoint (HR = 1.69; 95% CI: 0.77–3.73; P = 0.19).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that the pretreatment GRIm score may serve as a prognostic biomarker in patients with GI cancers. However, further high-quality studies are required to robustly validate its prognostic utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04320-8.Keywords: Gastrointestinal Cancer, Gustave Roussy immune score, Overall survival, Disease-free survival, Progression-free survival, Cancer-specific survival

Background

Globally, in 2022, gastrointestinal (GI) cancers accounted for approximately 4.9 million new cases and 3.3 million deaths, representing about one-quarter and one-third of all malignant tumor incidences and fatalities, respectively [1]. Although there have been significant improvements in surgical methods, chemotherapy, radiation, targeted treatments, and immunotherapies for patients with GI cancers, their long-term survival outcomes continue to be unsatisfactory [2]. The conventional approach to anti-cancer treatment and prognosis prediction is still largely based on traditional staging systems. Nevertheless, clinical experience shows that using tumor stage alone is inadequate for effectively guiding therapy choices and predicting patient prognosis [3, 4]. Thus, there is a clear need to identify new prognostic indicators that can help achieve more personalized and accurate treatment for GI cancer patients.​​

A growing body of evidence indicates that host inflammation and nutritional status significantly influence disease progression, therapeutic efficacy, quality of life, and survival in cancer patients [5]. Building on these observations, several inflammation- and nutrition-based biomarkers—such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) [6, 7], albumin (ALB) [8], and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) [9]—have been investigated for their ability to predict survival outcomes. Nevertheless, although these large-scale meta-analyses have revealed the prognostic potential of these markers in cancer patients [6–9], their predictive accuracy remains constrained by the limitation of relying on individual or simple parameters. Consequently, the Gustave Roussy Immune (GRIm) score, integrating NLR, ALB, and LDH, was recently established to improve prognostic stratification in cancer patients [10]. Initially introduced by Bigot et al. in 2017 as a tool for assessing prognosis in cancer patients undergoing immunotherapy, the GRIm score has since been evaluated for its ability to predict long-term outcomes across various malignancies [10–13]. However, for GI cancers, previous meta-analyses have either evaluated the GRIm score briefly in upper GI cancers or reported its association with prognosis only incidentally within broader analyses of pan-cancers [10, 14].

To the best of our knowledge, no published meta-analysis has specifically and comprehensively investigated the prognostic significance of GRIm score in patients with GI cancers. Given the substantial growth in relevant peer-reviewed literature over recent years, we conducted an updated meta-analysis to quantitatively synthesize current evidence on the prognostic utility of the pretreatment GRIm score in GI malignancies.

Methods

Search strategy

This meta-analysis adhered to the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). The study protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (Registration number: CRD420261291292, available from https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420261291292). A comprehensive literature search was performed using the Web of Science, PubMed, Cochrane Library and Embase databases, covering all records up to January 15, 2026. The detailed search strategies for each database are comprehensively delineated in Supplementary file Table S1. The search process was not limited by language. Furthermore, the reference lists of all included studies were manually reviewed to identify additional relevant publications. Two independent researchers (DXL and YTT) carried out the literature search.

Study selection

Studies were included if they: (1) examined the association of pretreatment GRIm score with survival outcome in individuals diagnosed with GI cancer; (2) either directly provided or allowed for the calculation of a hazard ratio (HR) accompanied by its 95% confidence interval (CI); and (3) stratified patients into two distinct cohorts according to their GRIm score.

The exclusion criteria comprised: (1) an inability to definitively ascertain that the GRIm score was sampled prior to the initiation of therapy; (2) a classification of the GRIm score into more than two categories; (3) publications in the form of letters, case reports, conference abstracts, or review articles; and (4) reports containing duplicate or overlapping datasets.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (DXL and YTT), who subsequently cross-checked all gathered data for accuracy. Information retrieved from the primary studies included the first author’s name, year of publication, country, study period, research design, sample size, timing of blood collection, definition and stratification of the GRIm score, patient age, sex, cancer type, tumor stage, primary treatment modality, and long-term survival outcomes. In line with the methodology proposed by Bigot et al. [11], the standard GRIm score in this analysis was determined using the following criteria: LDH (0 point if within normal limits, 1 point if above the upper limit), ALB (0 for ≥ 35 g/L, 1 for < 35 g/L), and NLR (0 for ≤ 6, 1 for > 6). Certain studies employed alternative cutoff values for these parameters; such variations were classified as modified GRIm scores in the current meta-analysis. The primary endpoints assessed in this study were survival outcomes, which comprised overall survival (OS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), progression-free survival (PFS), disease-free survival (DFS), and cancer-specific survival (CSS).

The methodological rigor of the included studies was carefully evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [15], a tool consisting of eight predefined criteria. Following a thorough assessment, each study was assigned an overall score on a scale from 0 to 9. Studies achieving scores of 7 to 9 were classified as high-quality.

Statistical analysis

For the analysis of survival outcomes, HRs along with their 95% CIs served as the primary measure of effect size. For studies that presented both univariate and multivariate survival analysis outcomes, we prioritized the inclusion of results derived from multivariate analysis. When survival data were not explicitly provided in the original articles, they were reconstructed from Kaplan-Meier curves following the methodology outlined by Tierney and colleagues [16]. To evaluate statistical heterogeneity across the studies, the I² statistic was employed. HRs were combined using inverse-variance weighting, and the analyses were performed with a REML random-effects model. Furthermore, subgroup, meta-regression and sensitivity analyses were performed to explore the potential sources of heterogeneity and the stability of pooled results. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and statistical tests by Begg’s and Egger’s tests (A P-value less than 0.1 is considered to be statistically significant). For pooled results affected by publication bias, the trim-and-fill method is employed to assess the robustness of meta-analytic findings. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value less than 0.05. All analyses were conducted using the package meta in R software (version 4.2.1).

Results

Study characteristics

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 347 records were identified from the database searches. After a detailed evaluation process involving the screening of titles, abstracts, and full-text articles, 16 studies [17–32] were ultimately selected. Among these studies, the study by Li et al. [20] included both a training cohort and a validation cohort. Ultimately, a total of 17 cohorts with 4,904 participants, were incorporated into the current analysis.

The PRISMA flowchart of study selection

As presented in Tables 1 and 2, all selected investigations were retrospective and conducted in several countries, including China, Japan, Canada, Turkey, and Romania. The publication dates of these studies spanned from 2020 to 2025, with cohort sizes ranging from 58 to 1,579 subjects. A total of 9 studies reported the standard GRIm score, while an additional 7 reported a modified GRIm score (see Table S2). The reported reference ranges for modified score were as follows: ALB, 3.5–4.01 g/L; LDH, 177.5–250 U/L; and NLR, 2.445–6.6. In terms of cancer types, 7 studies concentrated on tumors of the upper gastrointestinal tract, 4 studies addressed colorectal cancers, and 5 studies explored hepatobiliary and pancreatic cancers. Regarding the primary treatments analyzed, 5 studies involved surgical intervention, 1 study utilized a neoadjuvant therapy approach, and systemic therapy was the main focus in 10 other studies. OS was reported in 15 studies, PFS in 8 studies, DFS in 2 studies, CSS in 2 studies, and RFS in 1 study.

According to the quality assessment detailed in Table 2 and Table S3, 15 cohorts were rated as high-quality (score: 7–9), whereas 2 cohorts were rated as moderate-quality—primarily owing to limited follow-up duration and reliance on univariate rather than multivariate analysis (score: 6).

Relationship between the GRIm score and OS

A total of fifteen studies (comprising sixteen cohorts) enrolling 4,532 individuals evaluated the prognostic value of the GRIm score for OS in patients with GI cancers. As presented in Fig. 2, pooled analysis demonstrated that an elevated GRIm score was significantly correlated with inferior OS (HR = 2.01; 95% CI: 1.69–2.40; P < 0.01). Given the substantial heterogeneity observed (I² = 63%), subgroup analyses were conducted according to country (China or others), sample size (> 200 or < 200), cancer site (upper gastrointestinal tract cancers, colorectal cancers, or hepatobiliary and pancreatic cancers), sampling time (within one week or others), definition of GRIm score (standard or modified), GRIm score classification (0–1/2–3 or 0/1–3), primary treatment (neoadjuvant, resectable surgery, radical surgery or systemic therapy), tumor stage (nonmetastatic, mixed, or metastatic), and use of multivariate analysis (yes or no). As illustrated in Table 3 and Figure S1, pooled subgroup analyses consistently revealed that a high GRIm score was associated with significantly poorer OS compared to the low-score group (all P < 0.05). Specifically, there exists a significant difference between the group sampled within one week and those with other sampling periods (P for interaction < 0.01), suggesting that sampling time may be a source of heterogeneity. Consistently, meta-regression analysis further confirmed that the sampling time was a statistically significant contributor to this heterogeneity (P = 0.029; Figure S2). Furthermore, sensitivity analysis, performed by sequentially excluding individual studies, confirmed the stability of the pooled HRs across all iterations (Figure S4A).

Relationship between the GRIm score and PFS

There were eight studies involving 1,215 patients evaluated the prognostic value of the GRIm score for PFS in GI cancers. As illustrated in Fig. 3, pooled results demonstrated that a high GRIm score was significantly associated with poorer PFS among GI cancer patients (HR = 1.57; 95% CI: 1.29–1.91; p < 0.01; I² = 48%). Subgroup analyses were performed according to the aforementioned variables. As shown in Table 4 and Figure S3, all subgroups demonstrated statistically significant associations between a high GRIm score and poorer PFS. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the stability of the pooled HRs (Figure S4B).

Relationship between the GRIm score and other survival outcomes

Two studies comprising 1,774 patients reported on DFS. Pooled analysis revealed that a high GRIm score was significantly associated with inferior DFS compared with a low GRIm score (HR = 2.15; 95% CI: 1.35–3.42; p < 0.01; I² = 55%; Fig. 4). Similarly, two studies including 802 patients assessed CSS, and pooled result demonstrated a statistically significant association between high GRIm score and poorer CSS (HR = 1.68; 95% CI: 1.17–2.41; p < 0.01; I² = 4%; Fig. 5). Owing to the paucity of eligible studies, subgroup and sensitivity analyses could not be performed for either DFS or CSS. Additionally, a single study (n = 430) in surgically treated colorectal cancer patients evaluated RFS and found no independent prognostic value of the GRIm score for this outcome (HR = 1.69; 95% CI: 0.77–3.73; p = 0.19).

Publication bias

The funnel plot along with Begg’s and Egger’s tests was applied to assess potential publication bias in relation to OS and PFS. As presented in Figure S5A, the funnel plot of OS exhibited bilateral asymmetry, with P values of 0.017 for Begg’s test and 0.013 for Egger’s test, suggesting the presence of publication bias. A trim-and-fill analysis was subsequently performed, and after incorporating seven potentially missing studies, the pooled result remained statistically significant (HR = 1.59; 95% CI: 1.27–1.99; p < 0.01; I² = 77.8%). For, PFS (Figure S5B), no evidence of publication bias was detected: the funnel plot was bilaterally symmetrical, and statistical tests yielded non-significant results (Begg’s test, P = 0.174; Egger’s test, P = 0.285). Publication bias was not assessed for other survival outcomes due to the limited number of available studies.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this meta-analysis is the first to exclusively and systematically evaluate the prognostic value of the pretreatment GRIm score in GI cancer patients. In present meta-analysis, we incorporated sixteen studies comprising seventeen cohorts and 4,904 GI cancer patients. Based on the fact that most of the included studies (88.2%) had a low risk of bias in terms of literature quality assessed by NOS, we revealed that an elevated GRIm score was significantly correlated with worse OS and PFS. Moreover, evidence from limited included studies suggested that patients with a high GRIm score may have worse DFS and CSS than those with a low score. Nevertheless, the prognostic value of the GRIm score for RFS was not identified, as only a single study reported this outcome.

Regarding OS, substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 63%), yet subgroup analyses across all variables consistently showed that a high GRIm score significantly predicted worse OS. Meta-regression and the between-subgroup test both identified blood sampling time as a key source of heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis confirmed result robustness. Although publication bias was detected (likely due to preferential publication of significant or favorable findings in the field of prognostic biomarker research), trim-and-fill analysis imputing seven hypothetical unpublished studies, still yielded a statistically significant adjusted HR, reinforcing the GRIm score’s robust prognostic value for OS in GI cancer patients. In terms of PFS, there was no significant heterogeneity observed, subgroup analyses revealed statistically significant associations in all subgroups. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis and publication bias assessment collectively indicated the robustness and reliability of the meta‑analytic findings. Nevertheless, for other survival outcomes—including DFS, CSS, and RFS—definitive conclusions cannot be drawn at present due to the limited number of eligible studies.

In 2023, Sun et al. [10] conducted a meta-analysis of 15 studies evaluating the prognostic value of the GRIm score in patients with solid malignancies, of which eight focused specifically on GI cancers. Their pooled analysis indicated that, among GI cancer patients, those in the high GRIm group exhibited significantly worse OS (7 cohorts; HR = 2.01; 95% CI: 1.49–2.73) and PFS (3 cohorts; HR = 1.52; 95% CI: 1.27–1.84). More recently, Guo et al. [14] published a correspondence summarizing evidence from six studies exclusively addressing upper GI cancers. Their synthesis reported markedly elevated HRs for OS (5 cohorts; HR = 2.88; 95% CI: 2.03–4.10) and PFS (3 cohorts; HR = 2.80; 95% CI: 1.42–5.52) in patients with high versus low GRIm scores, along with preliminary evidence suggesting an association with reduced CSS (HR = 2.25; 95% CI: 1.64–3.08). Relative to these prior syntheses, our meta-analysis substantially expands the available evidence base—incorporating over twice as many studies through inclusion of recently published literature. This advancement yields two key methodological and clinical contributions: first, the enlarged dataset permits more detailed, subgroup-stratified evaluation of the GRIm score’s prognostic performance for OS and PFS across diverse patient populations; second, we present the first comprehensive assessment of its association with DFS and RFS, thereby broadening the scope of its potential prognostic utility beyond previously reported endpoints. Beyond GI malignancies, the prognostic utility of the GRIm score has increasingly attracted attention across a broader spectrum of tumor types. Recently, a meta-analysis focusing lung cancer included eight studies with 1,333 patients, and showed that a higher GRIm score predicted worse OS (HR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.54–2.49) and PFS (HR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.22–2.21) [33]. For platinum-refractory metastatic urothelial carcinoma treated with pembrolizumab, a real-world study revealed that the high GRIm score was an independent and significant adverse prognostic factor of both OS and PFS (HR, 1.65 and 1.82, respectively; both p < 0.001) [34]. Additionally, in patients with advanced malignant melanoma, Oksuz et al. identified that patients with a low GRIm Score had significantly longer median PFS (21.4 vs. 6.3 months, p = 0.003) and OS (26.5 vs. 7.2 months, p < 0.001) [35].

The prognostic value of the GRIm score may be attributed to its integration of several markers linked to inflammation and nutritional status, such as NLR, ALB, and LDH. NLR, a well-established indicator of systemic inflammation in cancer patients, has been reported to be associated with increased intraoperative bleeding, intraoperative blood transfusions, and postoperative complications in cancer patients, thereby deteriorating survival [36, 37]. Additionally, neutrophils, particularly the N2 tumor-associated subtype, foster a favorable tumor microenvironment by enhancing cancer cell proliferation, stimulating angiogenesis, and suppressing T-cell activity, which collectively drive tumor progression and metastasis [38]. lymphocytes, especially cytotoxic T cells, are essential for anti-tumor immunity and surveillance [38]. A decline in lymphocyte levels within the tumor microenvironment compromises the ability to destroy cancer cells, thereby encouraging tumor growth and spread [39]. Secondly, albumin serves as a key marker of a patient’s nutritional condition. Research indicates that low albumin levels are strongly linked to impaired wound repair, a higher incidence of infections, and reduced effectiveness of adjuvant treatments in cancer patients [32, 33]. Furthermore, decreased serum albumin contributes to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which compromises cellular immunity and vascular endothelial function [34]. Thirdly, LDH, a significant inflammatory biomarker, is crucial for tumor angiogenesis and metastasis [40]. Elevated LDH expression correlates positively with pro-angiogenic factors like VEGF, enhancing the ability of cancer cells to invade and spread [40]. It also promotes tumor immune evasion by inducing lactate accumulation, which acidifies the tumor microenvironment and inhibits immune cells such as T cells and NK cells [41]. Moreover, preoperative elevation of LDH has been shown to be related to a higher risk of unplanned hospital readmission following surgery among cancer patients [42]. Additionally, a recent meta-analysis of 76 studies further verified that high plasma LDH levels are significantly associated with reduced PFS and OS in cancer patients [43].

It is important to recognize several limitations of this study. A key constraint is that all the studies analyzed were retrospective, a design that carries a potential risk of selection bias. Furthermore, the patient cohort was primarily composed of individuals from China, which may affect the applicability of the results to populations in other geographical areas. Another limitation is the scarcity of studies reporting on survival outcomes like RFS, DFS, and CSS, which indicates that these results in this study are preliminary and require further validation through large-scale studies Moreover, although the subgroup analysis of the GRIm score based on its definition did not reveal any inconsistency with the overall OS and PFS results, the differences in the definition of the GRIm score among included studies pose challenges for its application in clinical practice. Furthermore, multiple survival outcome data were extracted from survival curves, and the potential measurement bias in outcome metrics may compromise the reliability of the findings. Finally, the analysis was restricted to summary-level data, preventing further investigation with individual patient data to rule out other confounding factors on the results.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the aggregated findings demonstrate that the baseline GRIm score may serve as a potent prognostic indicator in patients with GI cancers. However, more high-quality studies are still needed to verify the prognostic predictive value of the GRIm score for these survival outcomes in different population subgroups, such as Western patients.
