Background

Delayed fracture healing may persist despite adequate mechanical stability, reflecting biological insufficiency rather than mechanical failure [1, 2]. In complex distal tibial fractures, circular external fixation provides stable fixation while minimizing additional soft-tissue disruption and is particularly useful when extensive internal fixation is undesirable [3, 4]. However, even under favorable mechanical conditions, fracture healing may remain biologically inactive.

Abaloparatide is a synthetic parathyroid hormone-related protein analog with osteoanabolic effects [5–8]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated enhanced callus formation and improved mechanical strength during fracture repair with parathyroid hormone analogs [9, 10]. However, clinical evidence supporting their role in delayed union or nonunion remains limited and inconsistent [11, 12].

Gustilo type III-C pilon fractures represent one of the most severe injuries in orthopedic trauma because of the combination of fracture comminution, soft-tissue destruction, and vascular compromise [13]. Management becomes even more challenging in patients with systemic conditions that increase procedural risk. Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) primarily influences bleeding risk and surgical decision-making rather than fracture biology itself [14].

We report a case of successful limb salvage of a Gustilo type III-C pilon fracture in a patient with severe ITP, followed by rapid fracture union after initiation of abaloparatide. This case may illustrate a treatment concept in which fracture healing is achieved through the integration of mechanical stability, individualized soft-tissue management, strict hematologic control, and delayed biological activation.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old Japanese man sustained a right open pilon fracture following a crush injury. On presentation, he had extensive soft-tissue damage around the ankle and absence of distal pulses (Fig. 1a–f). The injury was diagnosed as a Gustilo type III-C open pilon fracture.

Initial injury and imaging findings. a, b Two large soft-tissue defects over the medial and lateral aspects of the right ankle at presentation. c Anteroposterior radiograph obtained in the emergency department demonstrating a comminuted distal tibial fracture. d, e Three-dimensional computed tomography images showing fracture configuration and displacement. f Three-dimensional computed tomography angiography demonstrating vascular injury

Emergency surgery included aggressive debridement, repair of the posterior tibial artery and vein, and skeletal stabilization using a circular Ilizarov external fixator (Fig. 2a, b). This strategy was selected to minimize further soft-tissue disruption and bleeding risk. The patient had chronic ITP, with a platelet count of approximately 5000/microL. He had no history of corticosteroid therapy during the entire clinical course.

Surgical management and external fixation. a, b Emergency surgery including aggressive debridement, posterior tibial artery and vein repair, and application of a circular Ilizarov external fixator. c, d External fixator configuration optimized for fracture stability using four tensioned wires (red arrows)

Perioperative management was performed in close collaboration with hematologists. Bleeding control was carefully monitored throughout the perioperative period, and platelet transfusion support was provided when necessary. The ring fixator was applied using a minimally invasive percutaneous technique to avoid wide surgical exposure and minimize additional soft-tissue trauma.

A medial soft-tissue defect was managed with negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) after meticulous surgical hemostasis had been confirmed. Because the patient had severe thrombocytopenia and a high risk of bleeding, a lower pressure setting of − 75 mmHg was selected rather than the commonly used setting of approximately − 125 mmHg. No clinically significant bleeding occurred during NPWT, and complete epithelialization was achieved at 2 months (Fig. 3d). The external fixation construct was subsequently optimized by adding four tensioned wires to improve fracture stability (Fig. 2c, d). Full weight bearing was permitted, and daily low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) therapy was initiated.

Radiographic progression during follow-up. a, b Radiographs demonstrating progressive callus formation after initiation of systemic abaloparatide therapy. c Radiograph obtained 12 months after injury, corresponding to 3 months after initiation of abaloparatide, demonstrating bridging callus across the metaphyseal and diaphyseal components. d Healed soft-tissue envelope. e Clinical photograph demonstrating full weight bearing and functional recovery

Despite these measures, serial radiographs showed no meaningful progression of fracture healing for more than 8 months. At that stage, the fracture was not interpreted as established nonunion. Rather, it was interpreted conservatively as prolonged biologically inactive delayed healing under stable mechanical conditions, characterized by persistent absence of bridging callus despite maintained alignment and stability.

At 9 months after injury, the patient developed atraumatic low back pain. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed a sacral insufficiency fracture [15, 16]. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry demonstrated severe osteoporosis, with a lumbar spine bone mineral density of 0.553 g/cm2 and a T-score of − 3.5. On the basis of the sacral fragility fracture and severe osteoporosis, daily subcutaneous abaloparatide (80 microg) was initiated.

Sacral pain improved within 2 weeks after treatment initiation. More importantly, within 6 weeks, radiographs of the tibial fracture demonstrated abundant bridging callus formation (Fig. 3a, b). By 12 weeks after initiation of abaloparatide, cortical bridging was observed across the fracture site (Fig. 3c), without any additional surgical or biological intervention. The patient regained full function and was able to ambulate with full weight bearing (Fig. 3e). At 24 months after the initial injury, radiographs confirmed complete union with maintained alignment and no evidence of hardware failure (Fig. 4a, b).

Long-term outcome. a, b Radiographs obtained 24 months after injury, corresponding to 15 months after initiation of abaloparatide, demonstrating sustained fracture union with maintained alignment and no evidence of hardware failure

Discussion

This case demonstrates two clinically important observations: successful limb salvage of a Gustilo type III-C pilon fracture in a patient with severe ITP, and a temporal association between initiation of abaloparatide and rapid fracture union after a prolonged period of biological inactivity. The case should be interpreted cautiously because several components of care were used simultaneously and because the safety of these strategies in patients with severe thrombocytopenia has not been established.

Limb salvage in a high-risk host with severe immune thrombocytopenia

Gustilo type III-C pilon fractures represent one of the most challenging injuries in orthopedic trauma, particularly in high-risk hosts. In the present case, severe ITP had a major influence on surgical decision-making because of the substantial bleeding risk and limited tolerance for extensive reconstructive procedures. Rather than being interpreted as a direct cause of impaired fracture healing, thrombocytopenia was considered primarily as a host-related factor affecting procedural risk.

Under these circumstances, circular external fixation offered an important potential advantage. It allowed stable fracture fixation through a minimally invasive approach while avoiding wide surgical exposure and further insult to already compromised soft tissues [3, 4]. However, the safety of thin-wire circular external fixation in patients with severe thrombocytopenia or coagulation disorders has not been established. To our knowledge, reports specifically addressing circular external fixation for severe ITP-associated thrombocytopenia in Gustilo type III-C open fractures are scarce. Therefore, the present case should not be interpreted as evidence supporting routine use of thin-wire external fixation in this population.

Instead, this case represents an individualized limb-salvage strategy performed under strict multidisciplinary management. Hematologists were involved in perioperative planning and postoperative management, and platelet transfusion support was used when clinically required. The favorable course observed in this patient therefore reflects the combination of minimally invasive fixation, meticulous surgical technique, careful hemostasis, and hematologic support, rather than the intrinsic safety of ring fixation in patients with coagulation disorders.

Negative-pressure wound therapy and bleeding risk

Soft-tissue management was another important aspect of this case. NPWT is used in traumatic wounds and open fractures as an adjunctive or temporizing strategy and has been evaluated in severe open fractures [18, 19]. However, NPWT should not be regarded as a substitute for flap reconstruction when vascularized soft-tissue coverage is required. In addition, NPWT can be associated with serious bleeding complications, particularly in wounds with active bleeding, exposed vessels, vascular grafts, or inadequate hemostasis [20].

For these reasons, the use of NPWT in the present patient was approached cautiously. NPWT was initiated only after careful surgical hemostasis had been confirmed, and the wound was monitored closely. Because of the severe thrombocytopenia, a reduced pressure setting of − 75 mmHg was selected rather than the commonly used setting of approximately − 125 mmHg. Experimental work has shown that lower negative pressure levels can still contribute to wound contraction and fluid evacuation, although the optimal setting must be individualized [21]. In this patient, no clinically significant bleeding occurred during NPWT, and complete epithelialization was achieved. This outcome should be interpreted as a successful individualized application under strict monitoring, not as evidence that NPWT is generally safer than soft-tissue transfer in patients with bleeding risk.

Fracture healing dynamics and abaloparatide

Because Gustilo type III-C distal tibial fractures are high-energy injuries associated with severe soft-tissue damage, vascular injury, and a high risk of delayed union or nonunion, delayed radiographic progression during the first several months may be expected. Therefore, the present case was not interpreted as established nonunion at 8 months. Rather, the concern was prolonged biologically inactive delayed healing, characterized by minimal radiographic progression and absence of bridging callus despite stable circular external fixation and maintained alignment. Definitions of delayed union and nonunion remain heterogeneous, and the present interpretation should therefore be considered conservative [2].

After initiation of abaloparatide for sacral insufficiency fracture and severe osteoporosis, fracture healing behavior changed markedly. Abundant bridging callus appeared within 6 weeks, followed by cortical consolidation at 12 weeks and sustained union at 24 months. The abrupt transition from prolonged radiographic inactivity to rapid progression was the most notable finding in this case.

Abaloparatide exerts osteoanabolic effects through activation of the PTH1 receptor and stimulation of osteoblast activity [5–8]. Experimental studies of parathyroid hormone analogs have shown increased callus formation and improved biomechanical strength during fracture repair [9, 10]. However, clinical evidence regarding their efficacy in delayed union and nonunion remains limited and inconsistent [11]. Reports specifically describing the use of abaloparatide in impaired fracture healing are still scarce [12].

In this context, the present case adds observational clinical data suggesting a temporal association between systemic anabolic therapy and restoration of fracture healing in a biologically inactive environment. Importantly, this interpretation is based on temporal association only and should not be regarded as proof of treatment efficacy.

Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound therapy

The adjunctive biophysical treatment used in this case was LIPUS, not pulsed electromagnetic field stimulation. LIPUS was initiated as an adjunctive therapy during the fracture-healing process. However, its independent contribution cannot be determined. High-quality clinical evidence has questioned the effectiveness of LIPUS in tibial fracture healing [17]. Therefore, the rapid progression to union observed after abaloparatide initiation should not be attributed to LIPUS.

Interpretation and clinical relevance

The clinical significance of this case lies not only in the eventual achievement of union, but also in the pattern by which healing was restored. Mechanical conditions had already been optimized with stable circular external fixation, preserved alignment, and progressive weight bearing. The subsequent radiographic response after abaloparatide initiation raises the possibility that biological activation represented an additional and potentially modifiable component of fracture healing.

From a practical standpoint, this observation suggests that, in selected patients with prolonged delayed healing under stable fixation, clinicians should consider whether biological insufficiency may be contributing to persistent nonprogression. In such situations, systemic anabolic therapy may deserve consideration, especially when severe osteoporosis or a concomitant fragility fracture is present. However, this should be viewed as a hypothesis-generating observation rather than a treatment recommendation.

Conceptual implication

This case supports a treatment concept in which fracture healing is achieved through the integration of mechanical stability and delayed biological activation. While stable fixation and appropriate loading remain essential prerequisites, the present observation suggests that biological activation may represent an independent therapeutic target in selected cases of prolonged delayed healing. The abrupt transition from prolonged biological inactivity to rapid callus formation after abaloparatide initiation may be consistent with a biological activation phenomenon, although this hypothesis requires validation in controlled clinical studies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, this is a single case report, and no causal relationship can be established between abaloparatide therapy and fracture union. The temporal association is notable but does not constitute proof of efficacy.

Second, the independent contribution of abaloparatide cannot be separated from other factors that may have influenced healing, including stable circular external fixation, progressive weight bearing, and concurrent LIPUS therapy [17]. The observed union should therefore be interpreted within the context of combined mechanical and biological management.

Third, this case does not establish the safety of thin-wire circular external fixation in patients with ITP or other coagulation disorders. The treatment was performed under strict multidisciplinary management with hematology support and platelet transfusion when necessary. The findings should not be generalized to patients with uncontrolled bleeding risk or inadequate hematologic support.

Fourth, NPWT was used under carefully controlled conditions after confirmation of hemostasis and at a reduced pressure setting. This case does not demonstrate that NPWT is safer than flap reconstruction or that it can replace vascularized soft-tissue coverage when such reconstruction is indicated.

Fifth, although corticosteroid exposure is an important confounder in many patients with ITP [14], this patient had no history of corticosteroid therapy. Nevertheless, other systemic and local factors may have contributed to delayed healing, including the severity of the initial high-energy trauma, vascular compromise, extensive soft-tissue damage, and severe osteoporosis.

Finally, definitions of delayed union and nonunion remain heterogeneous in the literature [2]. In this case, the fracture was interpreted conservatively as prolonged biologically inactive delayed healing under stable mechanical conditions, not as definitive nonunion at 8 months. The findings are not generalizable, and further studies are needed to clarify the potential role of systemic anabolic therapy in fracture healing.

Patient perspective

The patient expressed satisfaction with the treatment outcome and reported a return to independent daily activities without pain or functional limitation. He was able to resume his previous lifestyle without restrictions.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates successful limb salvage of a Gustilo type III-C pilon fracture in a patient with severe ITP using a minimally invasive circular external fixation strategy under strict multidisciplinary bleeding control. It also documents a marked temporal association between abaloparatide initiation and rapid progression of fracture healing after prolonged biological inactivity. Although causality cannot be established, this case supports the concept that fracture healing may, in selected situations, be restored through the integration of mechanical stability and delayed biological activation.
