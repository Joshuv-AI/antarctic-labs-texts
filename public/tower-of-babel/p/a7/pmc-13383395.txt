Evaluation of proximal contact tightness and associated factors in posterior resin composite restorations: a cross-sectional study in Nairobi, Kenya
Authors: Umaru Kizito, Lois Keren Kisakye, Hazel Orengo Simila, Fred Odera Otieno, Lincoln Abraham Tentena, Bernina Kyale Kisumbi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global shift from dental amalgam to direct posterior resin composite restorations (RCRs), driven by aesthetic demands and the Minamata Convention, has increased the need to evaluate their clinical performance. Establishing proper proximal contact tightness (PCT) in Class II restorations is a critical, technique-sensitive parameter required to prevent food impaction, periodontal disease, and restoration failure. In Nairobi, Kenya, there is a lack of local data regarding technical failures like deficiencies in proximal contact tightness. This study aimed to evaluate the proximal contact tightness of Class II posterior RCRs placed over a five-year period and their associated factors in selected dental institutions in Nairobi.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at three sites: a private facility (UoNDP), a teaching hospital (UoNDS), and a public hospital (KNHDD). A total of 159 Class II RCRs placed between 2014 and 2019 were selected for clinical examination. PCT was assessed using a tactile method with dental floss and recorded using a modified United States Public Health Survey (USPHS) tool modified to include class II PCT categorized as either tight, loose, or open. Data were analyzed using STATA v.19, employing Chi-square tests and multivariate analysis to identify associated factors.ResultsOf the 139 assessable restorations, 56.8% (n = 79) had tight contacts, while 43.2% (n = 60) exhibited unacceptable proximal tightness (22.3% loose and 20.9% open). Disparities between facilities were significant; 75% of restorations at KNHDD had unacceptable contacts, compared to 26.7% at UoNDP and 30% at UoNDS. Multivariate analysis revealed that placement at KNHDD was associated with both open (aRRR = 6.17, p = 0.038) and loose contacts (aRRR = 38.73, p = 0.005). Secondary caries showed an association with loose contacts (aRRR = 6.78, p = 0.001). The age of the restoration was found to confound the relationship between both facility and secondary caries, and PCT.ConclusionsThe prevalence of inadequate proximal contacts in class II posterior RCRs across three Nairobi dental facilities was high (43.2%), with open and loose contacts associated with the facility and loose contacts associated with secondary caries, highlighting the need for standardized protocols, better training, improved access to materials, and future studies to investigate the impact of procedural and operator factors in resource-limited settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08629-4.Keywords: Resin composite restorations, Proximal contact tightness, Class II restorations, Nairobi, Kenya, USPHS

Background

Dental resin composite, a tooth-coloured restorative material, has been used for more than half a century in the treatment of dental caries affecting both anterior and posterior teeth [1]. In the past, dental amalgam was the preferred material for posterior restorations [2]. However, growing patient demand for aesthetic solutions, the favourable properties of composite restorations, and the global Minamata convention on mercury advocating for an amalgam phase-down by individual countries have led to an increased preference for direct posterior resin composite restorations (RCRs) [3]. This transition underscores the need for careful assessment of RCR performance, particularly in high stress-bearing posterior areas.

The placement of RCRs is highly technique sensitive as it requires the balance of multiple conditions for achieving long-lasting restorations [4]. Moisture control is essential because moisture can compromise bonding, so methods like rubber dam isolation are recommended [5]. The cavity should be modified to expose more enamel for stronger bonding, and the composite should be placed incrementally to reduce polymerization shrinkage [5]. Proper conditioning of enamel and dentine helps ensure good adhesion. Effective light curing is also critical, as the intensity and exposure time of the curing unit affect the composite’s degree of conversion and hardness. Aesthetic goals such as colour match, stable shade, smooth finish, long-term gloss and good polishability also need attention [5]. Long-term success depends on accurate anatomical contouring and well-sealed margins to prevent leakage and discoloration [6].

For Class II cavities, which involve the mesial-occlusal (MO), distal-occlusal (DO), and mesial-occlusal-distal (MOD) proximal surfaces, reconstruction of proximal contacts in a specialized manner is crucial to prevent fractures and preserve tooth structure [7–9]. Literature shows that achieving tight proximal contacts with Class II composites remains a challenge: In one systematic review of contact outcomes, only about 6% of restorations had tight contacts while open contacts occurred in 61.6% of cases [10]. Proximal Contact Tightness is one of the specialized parameters assessed for Class II restorations [11]. Matrix systems play a critical role in establishing proper proximal morphology. When correctly positioned, the matrix complex enables accurate reconstruction of the proximal wall and reduces the risk of overhangs or open contacts [12–14]. Failure to establish an adequate proximal contact is a commonly reported complication and a significant factor in RCR failure. It can lead to localized periodontitis and gingivitis due to food impaction [11]. In clinical settings, PCT is conventionally assessed using the tactile method with dental floss. The contact is ideal if the floss passes through with a “snap” [15]. Assessment includes recording whether contacts are too tight (difficult to place/withdraw), tight (reasonable placement/withdrawal), loose, or open (no resistance) [15].

Despite the growing indication for RCRs globally, there is a noted burden on healthcare systems due to the failure of posterior RCRs, often leading to replacement or complex treatments [16]. In Nairobi, Kenya, where RCR use is increasing [17], local data regarding the longevity, failure rates, and specific technical failures, such as deficiencies in proximal contact tightness, are lacking. Nairobi also provides a relevant setting for this study due to its diverse mix of teaching, public, and private dental facilities, which vary in resources, patient load, operator experience, and access to materials, thereby offering a unique opportunity to examine disparities in PCT. This study therefore aimed to evaluate the prevalence of inadequate proximal contacts in Class II posterior RCRs placed in patients who attended 3 dental facilities over a 5-year period in Nairobi and to identify factors associated with these deficiencies.

Methods

Study design and setting

The study utilized a cross-sectional study design and was conducted in Nairobi County, Kenya across 3 distinct dental clinical settings conveniently sampled for their institutional diversity: the University of Nairobi Dental School (UoNDS), a teaching hospital; the University of Nairobi Dental Plaza (UoNDP), a private facility; and the Kenyatta National Hospital Dental Department (KNHDD), a public hospital.

Study population

The study population consisted of patients with posterior RCRs placed at the three selected sites between 2014 and 2019 who met the eligibility criteria.

Sample size determination and sampling

The parent study was powered to detect a 50% prevalence of posterior RCRs. The target sample size was initially calculated using Kish Leslie’s method for a single proportion. The final calculated sample size, adjusted for a population estimate of 1000, was 278 RCRs. This total was divided equally, aiming for 93 restorations per study area and 19 per year of study per site. Ultimately, a total of 286 posterior RCRs distributed among 95 participants were examined. While the parent study initially examined 286 restorations from 95 participants including class I, II, and V restorations, we specifically investigated a subset of 159 Class II RCRs to evaluate proximal contact tightness. We acknowledge that this reduces the power of the current study, however, the sample size provided sufficient data to model the five investigated predictor variables with more than 20 observations per variable. The study sites were selected using convenient sampling. Participants were identified through patient records and daily work registers. A stratified random sampling approach was employed to select participants, based on the year of placement of the RCR and the specific facility.

Data collection instruments and techniques

After obtaining approval to access patient records, a list of individuals who received posterior composite restorations from 2014 to 2019 was compiled. Ninety-five eligible participants were recruited, contributing a total of 286 restorations evenly distributed across the study sites. Participants were contacted by phone using numbers from their dental records and scheduled for appointments based on their availability and that of the lead investigator. Each participant received a one-hour appointment. Written informed consent in English or Swahili was obtained after the study was fully explained and any questions addressed. All personal and clinical information was handled confidentially, and participation was entirely voluntary.

Clinical examination

Clinical examination was performed in a clinical setting utilizing a dental mirror, dental explorer, and a WHO periodontal probe. Examination of participants was done by the lead investigator.

Clinical data collection tool (USPHS)

The clinical performance of the posterior RCRs was evaluated using a modified version of the United States Public Health Survey (USPHS) tool for tooth-coloured restorations. This tool was modified to measure the proximal contact tightness for class II RCRs and record whether the contacts were tight (reasonable placement and withdrawal of floss), loose (minimal resistance to floss), or open (no resistance on placement and withdrawal of floss) [15]. Waxed single strand dental floss, approximately 20 cm in length, was utilized.

Assessment for secondary caries

An AdDent 110019 Microlux transilluminator (AdDent, Inc. Danbury, Connecticut, USA) was used for secondary caries detection. A shadow seen when light from the transilluminator was shone from the lingual or gingival aspect was recorded as secondary caries.

Data validity and reliability

Data validity and reliability was ensured through a pilot pre-test of data collection tools on 10 patients, who were not included in the study, to refine the data collection procedures and ensure consistency. Calibration of the examiner was done by the main supervisor of the study and involved repeated assessment of a set of restorations and comparison with a reference standard, with discrepancies discussed until agreement was achieved. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa (κ = 0.90), indicating near-perfect agreement.

Data analysis

Data was cleaned and analysed using STATA version 19 (STATA Corp, College Station, TX, USA). Categorical variables were summarized with proportions and percentages. Chi-square tests were used to explore associations between PCT and factors such as secondary caries, tooth type, dental facility and restoration surface. The data was checked for collinearity with the variance inflation factor being 1.04 therefore showing little to no collinearity. Ordinal logistic regression was considered for regression to determine factors associated with PCT. However, a significant brant test (p < 0.001) showed violation of the proportional odds assumption. Multinomial logistic regression was therefore chosen as the regression method of choice with adjustment for clustering at the participant level. Factors to be included in the multivariate analysis were selected at bivariate analysis at a p - value < 0.2. Interaction terms were formed between significant variables, and a likelihood ratio test was used to check for the presence of interaction. There was no significant interaction. Stepwise estimation was done to maintain only significant variables. Thereafter, the data was checked for confounding with a percentage change greater than 10% used to confirm the presence of a confounder. The final model was checked for goodness-of-fit (Pseudo R2 = 0.182).

Ethical considerations and consent

Written informed consent was sought from participants in either Swahili or English after thorough explanation of the study, its risks, and benefits. Ethical approval was obtained from The Kenyatta National Hospital-University of Nairobi Ethics and Research Committee (Protocol No. P760/08/2019).

This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines. The strobe checklist is provided as additional file 2.

Results

The study examined a total of 159 Class II RCRs. The USPHS clinical evaluation tool was used, with modifications made for Class II composite restorations to measure proximal tightness. This assessment focused on restorations placed on mesial-occlusal/distal-occlusal (MO/DO) and mesial-occlusal-distal (MOD) surfaces.

Proximal contact tightness findings

Proximal contact tightness analysis was conducted on 139 of the 159 class II RCRs (MO/DO and MOD), given that 20 of the class II restorations could not be assessed due to the absence of adjacent teeth. Information about whether the missing adjacent teeth were lost after the restoration was placed was not recorded. A sizable majority of these restorations, 79 (56.8%), were found to have tight contacts. However, a total of 60 (43.2%) were found to have unacceptable proximal tightness, defined as having loose or open contacts. Of these 60, 31(51.7%) were loose whereas 29 (48.3%) were open. Distribution of PCT among facilities

Of all 139 class II RCRs assessed for proximal contact tightness, 45 (32.4%) were done at UoNDP while 50 (35.9%) and 44 (31.7%) were done at UoNDS and KNHDD respectively. A significantly higher proportion (75%) of restorations at KNHDD exhibited unacceptable (loose and open) contacts. In comparison, unacceptable contacts were observed in 26.7% of class II restorations at UoNDP and 30% at UoNDS. This distribution is represented in Fig. 3 below.

Distribution of PCT among tooth type

Among the 139 class II RCRs assessed for proximal contact tightness, 67 were molars and 72 were premolars. A slightly higher proportion of molars 44.8% had unacceptable contacts, defined as loose or open while 41.7% of premolars had unacceptable contacts. These findings are shown in Fig. 4 below.

Among the 139 restorations to be assessed for PCT, 30 (21.6%) presented with secondary caries.

Discussion

The findings from this study provide novel insights into PCT among class II RCRs in a Kenyan dental setting, revealing a high prevalence of unacceptable contacts (43.2%) among 139 assessable restorations. Tight contacts predominated at 56.8%, yet the substantial proportion of loose (22.3%) and open (20.9%) contacts may indicate a potential quality gap in the placement of posterior RCRs. This observation mirrors concerns reported in the previous literature about the difficulty of consistently forming optimal contacts with composite materials. A study from Canada found that a high proportion of Class II composite restorations had open or inadequate proximal contacts depending on placement technique, in contrast to amalgam restorations that more frequently produced acceptable contacts [18]. Similarly, Abbassy et al. reported a decrease in PCT between teeth with class II RCRs and adjacent teeth over a 12 month follow up period [19]. Predominantly MO/DO surfaces (91.2%) were evaluated, reflecting common clinical practice reports from literature [20, 21], while the inability to assess 20 restorations (12.6%) due to missing adjacent teeth highlights a real-world challenge in PCT evaluation. Tooth type yielded comparable unacceptable rates (44.8% for molars and 41.7% for premolars), even with a chi-square test showing no significant difference between the two (χ²=0.74, p = 0.691), therefore suggesting that technique rather than anatomy drove outcomes here. Given that only 14 of the assessed restorations were on the MOD surface, there was no analysis done to assess whether their increased complexity would be correlated with high failure rates.

Facility-level disparities were striking, with KNHDD showing 75% unacceptable contacts compared to 26.7% at UoNDP and 30% at UoNDS. Multivariate analysis confirmed KNHDD’s association with both open and loose contacts compared to tight contacts using UoNDP as the reference category. Restorations placed at KNHDD were 6.17 times more likely to present with open rather than tight proximal contacts and 38.73 times more likely to present with loose rather than tight proximal contacts compared to those placed at UoNDP. Although these estimates should be interpreted cautiously due to the wide confidence intervals, likely reflecting sample size limitations, the overall pattern suggests meaningful differences in restorative outcomes across facilities. Evidence from similar low- and middle-income settings supports the plausibility of such disparities. A randomized clinical trial from Egypt demonstrated that proximal contact tightness varies significantly depending on the contact-forming instruments used and may deteriorate over time, underscoring the influence of technique and clinical protocols [19]. Likewise, a systematic review from India highlighted that achieving optimal proximal contact remains challenging due to variations in matrix systems, operator handling, and separation techniques [11]. Additional studies have shown that differences in restorative materials and clinical approaches can result in substantial variation in contact tightness outcomes [22–25]. Taken together, these findings suggest that disparities in proximal contact outcomes are likely influenced by differences in clinical resources, operator training, and procedural protocols, which often vary between facilities in resource-constrained settings. Further research should involve more facilities in Kenya to further investigate these findings and improve precision, while incorporating detailed assessment of procedural factors such as matrix systems, operator experience, and clinical protocols, alongside longitudinal follow-up and mixed-methods approaches, to better understand the underlying causes of facility-level disparities and evaluate whether standardization and targeted training can improve proximal contact outcomes.

Secondary caries showed a significant bivariate association with PCT and persisted for loose contacts compared to tight contacts at multivariate analysis. The secondary caries were 6.78 times more likely to be present in restorations with loose contacts rather than in tight contacts. These findings align with those reported by Sharafat et al. who observed an association between defective proximal contacts and caries development in adjacent teeth [26]. Notably, no such link was shown for open contacts. This difference may reflect variations in plaque retention, food impaction, and challenges in interproximal cleaning associated with loose contacts, or even a limited sample size. Loose contacts are associated with microbial ingress accelerating demineralization at restoration margins, a well-documented pathway in studies [27]. Inadequate contacts also allow food impaction in the interdental spaces, the consequences of which include localized gingival inflammation and periodontitis, which can exacerbate tissue destruction and ultimately precipitate restoration failure [28]. Conversely, the association between secondary caries and loose proximal contacts may reflect an underlying influence of confounders such as cavity preparation techniques, material selection and placement [29]. Consequently, the relationship between secondary caries and proximal contact quality should be interpreted within a broader multifactorial context and further research using longitudinal designs could be vital in investigating any temporal relationship between PCT and secondary caries, while controlling for key confounders such as operator experience, restorative technique, and material selection in the current study setting.

The age of the restoration was found to confound the relationship between both facility and secondary caries, and PCT. This could be because older restorations are more prone to marginal breakdown and secondary caries [30, 31]. Similarly, variations in restoration age across the different facilities may have influenced the observed association between facility and PCT. Future studies should consider stratifying analyses by restoration age to better isolate the independent impact of PCT on clinical outcomes.

Proximal contact tightness is paramount for the longevity and success of restorations as it directly influences the functional and aesthetic outcomes, as well as the long-term viability of the restoration [11]. Indeed, a longitudinal study with up to 10 years of follow-up identified loss of proximal contact as a leading cause of failure for posterior composite restorations [32].

Strengths and limitations

The research directly addresses the noted lack of local data regarding deficiencies in PCT, one of the specialized parameters and a key determinant of restoration success for Class II restorations using RCRs, in Nairobi, Kenya. However, there are notable limitations from this study. First, selection bias may have arisen from convenience sampling of institutions and recruitment of patients responsive to phone calls, potentially favoring individuals more inclined to seek dental care. Second, information bias is possible, as proximal contact tightness was assessed using a semi-quantitative, floss-based tactile method. Although floss-based tactile assessment is widely used clinically, it remains a semi-quantitative subjective method. Future studies should incorporate objective measurement tools such as pressure-sensitive strips or digital force gauges [19, 23]. The examiner’s awareness of participants’ facility origin may have introduced observer bias. Third, several potential confounders known to influence PCT such as matrix and wedge type, operator experience, rubber dam use, composite material, and tooth position were not recorded. The cross-sectional design also precludes causal inference, and the sample size, based on the prevalence of posterior RCRs in the parent study, may have limited power for subgroup analysis, contributing to wide confidence intervals. Finally, the selection of three institutions in Nairobi limits generalizability of these findings. Despite these limitations, the study provides novel insights into PCT and highlights facility-level disparities within a low-middle-income setting, offering a foundation for future research and quality improvement in restorative dental practice.

Conclusions

The study demonstrates that the prevalence of inadequate proximal contacts (43.2%) was considerably high in Class II posterior RCRs placed at 3 dental facilities in Nairobi, Kenya. There was a statistically significant association between open and loose proximal contacts and the facility, as well as between loose contacts and the presence of secondary caries. This findings highlight a possible quality gap in the clinical performance of these restorations, particularly within public hospital settings where unacceptable contacts were significantly more frequent. Given the documented clinical implications of poor proximal tightness, including its association with secondary caries and potential periodontal complications, these findings point to an urgent need for standardized restorative protocols and targeted educational interventions, especially in resource-constrained facilities. Strengthening training in adhesion techniques and proximal contact formation, alongside improved access to appropriate materials and instrumentation in Nairobi and similar settings, may help reduce the observed disparities. Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs to study the temporal relationship between proximal contact tightness and associated factors, while also incorporating key procedural and operator-related confounders such as matrix systems, material selection, clinician experience to better understand the drivers of these associations and inform evidence-based practice and policy in Kenya and similar settings.
