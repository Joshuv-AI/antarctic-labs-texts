Selective Reprogramming of Ribosome Assembly and Translational Control during Bacterial Growth Phase Transitions
Authors: Manuel Terrazas-López, Vanessa Aitken, Tonya N. Zeczycki, Eda Koculi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Most bacteria in natural environments experience prolonged nutrient limitation resembling the stationary phase and only intermittently encounter nutrient-rich conditions analogous to exponential growth. Understanding the molecular processes operating in these two states is therefore essential for elucidating bacterial adaptation to fluctuating nutrient availability. We applied a label-free proteomics workflow to characterize protein-level differences in the Escherichia coli (E. coli) proteome between exponential and prolonged stationary growth phases. We found that multiple components of the translational machinery are selectively enriched during the exponential growth. These include the DEAD-box rRNA (rRNA) chaperones DeaD, DbpA, and RlhE, the small-subunit ribosomal protein chaperone RimP, and 13 ribosomal proteins, several of which possess RNA and/or combined RNA-protein chaperone activity. In addition, enzymes responsible for post-transcriptional modification of rRNA and tRNA, RlmN, RsmI, RluB, RlmG, and TsaC, were more abundant during exponential growth, consistent with enhanced ribosome stability, translation rate, and translational fidelity under conditions of rapid proliferation. Translation initiation factors IF-1 and IF-3 and the elongation factor EF-P, which support efficient translation initiation and alleviate ribosomal stalling on challenging sequences, were also elevated during the exponential phase. Finally, a distinct set of proteins associated with antibiotic response exhibited differential abundance between exponential and stationary growth phases, indicating growth phase-dependent remodeling of cellular stress and defense pathways. Together, these findings provide a statistically supported, protein-level framework that defines how specific components of the translational machinery are selectively remodeled during the transition between exponential and stationary growth phases in E. coli, with potential relevance to other bacteria.

Introduction

Escherichia coli (E. coli) is widely used as a model organism to study bacterial physiology, metabolism, and protein production. − Identifying molecular pathways activated under diverse stress conditions in E. coli improves our understanding of the mechanisms that support bacterial survival and growth. Here, we focus on the prolonged stationary phase, a physiologically relevant condition characterized by nutrient limitation and altered metabolism that likely reflects the dominant state of bacteria in natural environments and compare it to the fast-growing exponential phase. −

To define the proteomic signatures underlying these growth phase differences, we quantified differential protein abundances between exponential and stationary cultures of E. coli BLR­(DE3) using a label-free, untargeted liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) workflow. This workflow was optimized for comparative analysis. A landmark 2016 study generated an absolute, condition-dependent characterization of the E. coli proteome across exponential and stationary growth phases as well as nutrient-limited conditions, providing important insight into bacterial adaptation at the whole cell level. A key strength of that work is its estimation of absolute protein abundances and derived protein concentrations per cell, enabling quantitative analysis of how protein copy numbers per cell change across growth conditions. Although fold-changes can be calculated from such absolute proteome data sets, those changes are model-derived, as protein abundances are inferred separately for each condition using calibration models and whole cell adjustment based on cell volume and total protein mass. In contrast, our analysis compares relative protein abundances directly across conditions using a shared normalization framework applied consistently across all samples (see Materials and Methods). Moreover, the observed protein abundance changes in our study are not model-driven but instead reflect measured differences supported by p-value statistics. Thus, the two studies have distinct objectives. The earlier work quantifies absolute protein content at the cellular level, whereas our study focuses on precise, statistically supported protein abundance changes between the exponential and stationary growth phases.

Using this comparative proteomic framework, we identified distinct proteomic signatures in ribosome maturation, RNA modification, translational control, and antibiotic response pathways between exponential and stationary growth in E. coli. Although increased ribosome biogenesis and translational activity during exponential growth have been observed previously, the specific proteins and RNA-modifying enzymes that quantitatively and statistically distinguish these processes across growth phases have not been systematically defined. ,,−

Materials and Methods

Materials

E. coli BLR­(DE3) cells, which are a recA– derivative of BL21 cells, were purchased from Novagen. The iST-Preparation and iST-Fractionation kits used for peptide generation and offline fractionation were purchased from PreOmics. All chemicals and reagents were purchased in the highest grade possible and used without further purification. Nondiet P-40 (NP-40) was originally purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. (Note: IGEPAL CA-630 (Sigma-Aldrich) can be used as a direct replacement for NP-40.) All remaining chemicals and reagents were obtained from Fisher Bioreagents.

Methods

Sample Preparation

Five colonies of E. coli BLR­(DE3) (n = 5 biological replicates) were separately cultured in 50 mL Luria–Bertani (LB) broth overnight at 37 °C with continuous shaking at 225 rpm. Subsequently, the cultures were scaled up to 200 mL and adjusted to an initial optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.03. The cultures were then incubated under the same growth conditions in the same medium for 3 h, reaching an OD600 of approximately 0.3, and for 22 h, reaching an OD600 of approximately 5. The cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 4 °C at 5000g for 15 min. The resulting cell pellets were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen prior to peptide isolation.

LC–MS/MS for Label-Free Proteomics

Peptide Isolation

Low protein-binding microcentrifuge tubes (Eppendorf Protein LoBind Tubes) were used for peptide isolation and purification steps. Biological replicates (n = 5) for each growth condition were initially resuspended in bacterial lysis buffer (500 μL, 50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 0.1% NP-40). It should be noted that IGEPAL CA-630 (Sigma-Aldrich) can be directly substituted for NP-40. HALT Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (1× final concentration) was added to the lysis buffer immediately before use. The cells were disrupted via sonication on ice (10 s burst, 30% amplitude, total of 1 min sonication). After sonication, proteins in the cell lysate were subsequently precipitated using ice-cold methanol (3:1 v/v) at −20 °C overnight and pelleted by centrifugation (5000g, 10 min). The precipitated protein pellets were washed with ice-cold methanol (500 μL ×2) and allowed to air-dry before further use.

PreOmics iST and iST-Fractionation Add-on Kits were used according to the provided instructions to prepare mass spectrometry-grade peptides. Briefly, the precipitated proteins were resuspended in the provided denaturation/alkylation buffer (150 μL) and heated at 95 °C for 10 min. After cooling to room temperature, a freshly prepared digestion solution (50 μL) was added to each sample. Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h to ensure complete digestion before adding the provided stop solution (100 μL). Samples were cooled to room temperature prior to transferring to the provided solid phase extraction columns. After 2 min of centrifugation (room temperature, 500g), the cartridges were washed with wash 1 and 2 solutions (200 μL each, 2 min centrifugation, 500g) to remove hydrophobic and hydrophilic contaminations. For reverse-phase, pH-dependent fractionation, peptides were eluted from the cartridges using elution buffers 1–3 (150 μL, 2 min centrifugation, room temperature, 500g). Each elution was collected in a separate low protein-binding tube and dried under a stream of N2 to dryness. Purified, alkylated peptides were reconstituted in 30 μL of loading buffer (98:2, v/v water/acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). Peptide concentrations in each sample were determined fluorometrically (Pierce Quantitative Peptide Assay) and adjusted to 0.25 mg/mL with loading buffer.

nLC-MS/MS for Label-Free Proteomics

Peptides were subjected to nano–liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (nanoLC-MS/MS) analysis using an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive Plus Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) via a nanoelectrospray ionization source. For each fraction, 1 μg (4 μL) of sample was first trapped on an Acclaim PepMap 100 (20 mm × 0.075 mm) trapping column (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 98:2 v/v water/acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (5 μL/min, 6 min trapping). Analytical separation was performed over 120 min with an effective linear gradient of 4–35% acetonitrile plus 0.1% formic acid (300 nL/min) using an EASY-Spray PepMap RSLC C18 column (75 μm × 2 50 mm Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a column temperature of 50 °C. MS1 was performed at 70,000 resolution, with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 1 × 105 ions and a maximum injection time (IT) of 100 ms. MS2 spectra were collected by data-dependent acquisition on the top 20 most abundant precursor ions with a charge >1 per MS1 scan. The precursor ion isolation window was 1.5 mass/charge ratio (m/z), dynamic exclusion was 20 s, and the normalized collision energy was 32. MS2 scans were performed at 17,500 resolution, maximum IT of 60 ms, and AGC target of 5 × 104 ions.

Database Searching and Analysis

FragPipe (v 19.1) was used for raw data analysis with default search parameters for Open and Label-Free Quantification Matching between Runs (LFQ-MBR) workflows. All samples were searched together with biological replicates (n = 5) for group identification. An initial open search against the canonical Uniprot E. coli BLR­(DE3) reference proteome (UP000475070, accessed 6/2024) was used to identify potential post-translational modifications for inclusion in the LFQ-MBR workflow. Precursor m/z tolerance was set to −150 to 500 Da and fragment tolerance was ±20 ppm with 3 missed cleavages for Tryp and Lys-C allowed. Peptide spectrum matches (PSMs) were validated using PeptideProphet, and results were filtered at the ion, peptide, and protein -level with a 1% false discovery rate (FDR). Based on these initial searches, the following variable modifications were included in the LFQ-MBR analysis: oxidation (+15.5995 Da on Met), deamidation (+0.98401 Da on Gln and Asn), and fixed modification carbamodiomethyl (+57.025 Da on Cys). For LFQ-MBR analysis, data were searched against the canonical Uniprot E. coli reference proteome BLR­(DE3) (UP000475070, accessed 6/2024). Precursor ion m/z tolerance was ±20 ppm with 3 missed cleavages for Trypsin/LysC allowed. The search results were filtered by a 1% FDR at the ion, peptide, and protein-level. PSMs were validated using Percolator, and label free quantification was carried out using IonQuant. Match between runs FDR rate at the ion level was set to 10% for the top 300 runs. PeptideProphet, IonQuant, and Percolator were all accessed through FragPipe. Proteins with >95% probability of ID, >2 unique peptides, and in more than 80% of a sample group (i.e., 4/5 injections) were manually validated and considered high confidence IDs. These proteins were retained for comparison analysis. Intensities were log2 transformed, quantile normalized within the entire sample run, and relative abundances for low sampling proteins were determined via knn methods (15 neighbors) in Perseus. The filtered, imputed log2 abundances for the 1819 proteins identified at both growth stages with high confidence were compared using multiple unpaired t tests with Welch’s correction (p < 0.05, GraphPad Prism v. 10.6.1) (Table S1 and Table S2).

Thresholds and Biological Process Identification

GO enrichment analysis (statistical overrepresentation test) using Panther GO was used to identify over- and under-represented GO terms. A log2 fold change threshold of ±0.585 (fold changes of 1.5 and 1/1.5) in protein abundances during the exponential and stationary growth phases was used to identify biologically significant differences in protein abundances and was included in the GO enrichment analysis. , Fisher’s exact test (p < 0.05) was used to establish statistically significant over- or under-representation. Protein–protein interactions and additional biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular locations of proteins were identified using STRING analysis with E. coli K-12 as the reference organism. A subset of protein functions was further investigated using UniProt and EcoCyc. ,

Results and Discussion

493 Proteins Showed Altered Abundance between the Exponential and Stationary Phases of E. coli BLR­(DE3), While 1326 Proteins Remained Unchanged

From approximately 4200 predicted protein-coding genes in E. coli, we identified 1819 proteins with high confidence. 230 proteins exhibited biologically and statistically significant increases in abundance during the exponential phase compared to the stationary phase, while 263 showed significant decreases. Protein levels associated with translation, ribosome biogenesis, and gene expression rose during exponential growth to support rapid cell division (Figure , Table S3).

Conversely, in the stationary phase, which is characterized by nutrient limitation, toxic stress, and increased exposure to oxidative and acidic stress, the proteins involved in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle were elevated in abundance (Figure , Table S4). , The TCA cycle supports energy generation from alternative carbon sources and supplies key biosynthetic precursors. − In addition to the TCA cycle, aerobic respiration, small-molecule biosynthesis, and carboxylic-and oxoacid-metabolic processes were also elevated in the stationary phase (Table S4). These pathways are remodeled by stress-response systems including RpoS, (p)­ppGpp, ArcA/ArcB, CRP–cAMP, FNR, OxyR/SoxRS, and CsrA. −

Since our study examines only two discrete growth conditions, exponential and prolonged stationary phase, mechanistic or sector-based analyses, such as those developed in previous multicondition studies, cannot be performed. , Instead, our work focuses on statistically robust differential analysis between these two phases, enabling us to identify specific proteins and pathways that undergo biologically meaningful changes during the transition from exponential to prolonged stationary phase (Figure , Tables S3 and S4). ,

Ribosomal Maturation Factors DeaD, DbpA, RlhE, and RimP Are More Abundant during the Exponential Phase Than during the Stationary Phase

The ribosome (70S) in E. coli consists of the small (30S) and large (50S) subunits. The small subunit possesses one rRNA molecule, 16S, and 21 ribosomal proteins, while the large subunit possesses two rRNA molecules, 5S and 23S, and 33 ribosomal proteins. In bacteria, dozens of proteins transiently interact with ribosomal intermediates and facilitate their maturation. − One class of maturation factors that facilitates ribosome assembly in most known organisms is the DEAD-box RNA helicases. − In E. coli, there are five DEAD-box RNA helicases, DeaD, DbpA, RlhB, RlhE, and SrmB. Four of these enzymes, DeaD, DbpA, RlhE, and SrmB, are involved in large subunit ribosome assembly; DeaD is also implicated in small subunit assembly. − Our data reveal that during the exponential growth phase, the abundance of the DEAD-box RNA helicases DeaD, DbpA, and RlhE increases relative to the stationary phase (Figure A). On the other hand, we observed no change in SrmB abundance between the exponential and stationary phases (Table S5).

Previous studies have shown that the number of mature ribosomes increases during the fast-growing exponential phase compared to the stationary phase. , Therefore, the increased abundance of DeaD, DbpA, and RlhE is consistent with the elevated rate of ribosome production during the exponential growth phase. ,,− Interestingly, unlike DeaD, DbpA, and RlhE, the DEAD-box protein SrmB is present at similar levels in both exponential and stationary phases, indicating that its function may be required across growth conditions (Table S5).

Our analysis shows that RimP, another protein that facilitates ribosome assembly, is more abundant during the exponential phase compared to the stationary phase (Figure A). In E. coli, deletion of the rimP gene results in a high-temperature, slow-growth phenotype, and the accumulation of small subunit intermediates. Functionally, RimP promotes the accelerated binding of small subunit proteins uS9 and uS19 to 30S intermediates, while inhibiting the association of uS12 and uS13. Taken together, our observations and prior data are consistent with a role of elevated RimP abundance in efficient 30S subunit assembly under conditions of a high translational demand.

Exponential Phase Enrichment of Specific Ribosomal and Translational Machinery Components

Thirteen large and small ribosomal subunit proteins show increased abundance in the exponential phase compared to the stationary phase (Figure A, Table S6). In E. coli, several large and small subunit proteins possess RNA and/or protein chaperone activity in addition to their structural roles. Of the 13 ribosomal proteins more abundant during the exponential phase, uL1 and uL24 exhibit RNA chaperone activity, while uL15, uL18, and bL19 exhibit both RNA and protein chaperone activities. , Therefore, similar to the ribosome maturation factors DeaD, DbpA, RlhE, and RimP, the large subunit proteins uL1, uL15, uL18, bL19, and uL24 likely also facilitate the extensive folding of rRNA and ribosomal proteins necessary for ribosome assembly and protein synthesis during the exponential phase. These processes are associated with the rapid cell growth and division characteristic of this phase. ,,−

Lastly, consistent with elevated translational activity during the exponential phase, the translation initiation factors IF-1 and IF-3, which are key regulators of initiation fidelity and efficiency, are more abundant in the exponential phase than in the stationary phase (Figure ). The elongation factor EF-P, which alleviates ribosomal stalling at polyproline motifs during elongation, is also enriched during exponential growth. During rapid growth, when translation rates are high, ribosomal pausing at difficult sequence motifs becomes particularly costly, as it can sequester ribosomes and reduce overall translational throughput. ,,, Increased EF-P abundance may help minimize such pauses, which could contribute to sustained efficient elongation under conditions of high protein synthesis demand. In parallel, ArgRS, which charges tRNAArg, is more abundant during the exponential phase compared to the stationary phase (Figure B). Because several arginine codons are among the rarest in E. coli and rely on limited tRNA pools, increased ArgRS abundance may be associated with maintenance of decoding efficiency for rare arginine codons under conditions of high translational demand. −

RlmN catalyzes the incorporation of the m2A modification at position 2507 of the 23S rRNA. ,,,, The m2A2507 modification, together with the 7-methylguanosine at position 2073 and 2-methylguanosine at position 2447 introduced by RlmKL, the 2′-O-methylcytidine (C m) at position 2505 introduced by RlmM, and the pseudouridine (Ψ) at position 2461 introduced by RluE, facilitate maturation of the 50S large ribosomal subunit and increase the translation elongation rate. ,,− All the above modifications are located within the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) of 23S rRNA.

In DH5α cells, a K-12 strain of E. coli, the level of m2A2507 is elevated during the exponential growth phase in cells cultured in LB medium, relative to those subjected to metabolic or cold stress. Thus, during the exponential phase, which is characterized by enhanced ribosome biogenesis and elevated translation rates, the incorporation of m2A2507 modification increases across multiple E. coli strains (Figure ). ,,− ,

RluB catalyzes the isomerization of U 2609 in 23S rRNA to Ψ, a modification located within PTC. The role of RluB in large subunit maturation, as well as the function or significance of the Ψ 2609 modification in ribosome maturation, stability, or translation, remains unclear. , Interestingly, our data show that RluB is enriched during the exponential phase (Figure ). Although the biological functions of RluB and the Ψ 2609 modification remain unknown, RluB’s selective enrichment during the exponential phase suggests that RluB and/or Ψ 2609 may play roles associated with ribosome structure and/or function during rapid cellular proliferation. Alternatively, RluB and/or Ψ 2609 may confer a disadvantage during the stationary phase. Future studies are needed to elucidate how RluB-mediated pseudouridylation modulates ribosome function and structure across different physiological states.

The m2G1837 modification, catalyzed by the RlmG enzyme, has been shown to influence the stability of the E. coli 70S ribosome by strengthening interactions between the 50S and 30S subunits. , Furthermore, the extent of m2G1837 modification decreases in DH5α cells under cold and metabolic stress conditions. Collectively, our data and previous studies suggest that under various stress conditions, 70S ribosomes may be less stable and tend to dissociate into 30S and 50S subunits. These free subunits are more vulnerable to degradation, potentially contributing to amino acid and nucleotide recycling during the stationary phase. The stationary phase is characterized by nutrient limitation. Additionally, during the stationary phase, a smaller fraction of ribosomes is engaged in active translation. , Thus, the reduced stability of 70S ribosomes during this phase may further increase the pool of inactive 30S and 50S subunits.

RsmI is an enzyme that catalyzes the 2′-O-methylation of the C1402 residue in 16S rRNA. Another enzyme, RsmH, modifies the same nucleotide by methylating the N4 position of C1402 (m4C). In BLR­(DE3) cells, we observed that RsmI abundance increases during the exponential phase relative to the stationary phase, whereas RsmH levels remain largely unchanged based on our biological and statistical thresholds (Figure ). These findings suggest that during the exponential phase, a greater proportion of C1402 residues in BLR­(DE3) cells are modified by both 2′-O-methylation and N4-methylation. In contrast, during the stationary phase, many C1402 residues likely carry only the N4-methylation.

The C1402 nucleotide makes direct contact with the mRNA positioned in the ribosomal A site of the ribosome. Both N4-methylation and 2′-O-methylation at this site contribute to maintaining translation fidelity. Here, we report that the abundance of the RsmI enzyme, which catalyzes 2′-O-methylation of the C1402 residue in the 16S rRNA, decreases during the stationary phase relative to the exponential phase (Figure ). These findings are consistent with the possibility of translational accuracy being reduced under stationary-phase conditions. Accordingly, during this growth phase, BLR­(DE3) cells may prioritize survival over fidelity in protein synthesis. A decline in translational accuracy under stress conditions has also been observed in other E. coli and other bacteria.

The shifts in translation-associated proteins, including decreased levels of fidelity factors such as RsmI, likely represent a regulated adjustment of translational control tailored to stationary-phase physiology. Recent elegant adaptive laboratory evolution studies demonstrate that stress-response pathways help maintain translational fidelity under chemical or metabolic perturbations. , This supports the idea that the bacterial stress-response network may contribute to the maintenance of translation accuracy under stress conditions. , Although our data cannot directly establish that stress responses drive the reprogramming of the translational machinery, the stationary-phase upregulation of metabolic pathways typically controlled by stress-response regulators is consistent with a model in which the translational changes we observe are modulated by stress-response mechanisms (Tables S3 and S4). − ,,

TsaC catalyzes the formation of l-threonylcarbamoyladenylate (t6A). Subsequently, the TsaB, TsaD, and TsaE enzymes collaboratively incorporate t6A at position 37 in tRNA. , This tRNA modification enhances translation fidelity by stabilizing codon–anticodon interactions. , In addition to its role in tRNA modification, the TsaC protein is involved in the maturation of the E. coli small ribosomal subunit. Our data show an increased abundance of the TsaC enzyme in the exponential phase compared to the stationary phase. However, no change was observed in the abundance of TsaB, TsaD, and TsaE in either growth phase (Table S5). The elevated abundance of TsaC in the exponential phase, without a corresponding increase in TsaD and TsaB, suggests that TsaC may be involved in 30S small subunit assembly or in other, yet-uncharacterized, cellular functions that do not require the activity of the TsaD and TsaB proteins.

Conclusions

A systematic, statistically supported comparison of the proteomes across the two growth phases revealed that the abundance of four rRNA and ribosomal protein chaperones, DeaD, DbpA, RlhE, and RimP, is enhanced in the exponential phase relative to the prolonged stationary phase, reflecting increased ribosome biogenesis required for active growth (Figure A). − Similarly, 13 ribosomal subunit proteins were enriched in the exponential phase, including uL1 and uL24, which possess RNA chaperone activity, and uL15, uL18, and bL19, which exhibit both RNA and protein chaperone functions (Figure A, Table S6). , Enzymes modifying 16S and 23S rRNARlmN, RsmI, and RlmGand the tRNA-modifying enzyme TsaC, which are implicated in enhanced ribosome stability, translation rate, or fidelity, were more prevalent in the exponential phase (Figure ). ,,,,− , The abundance of IF-1, IF-3, EF-P, and ArgRS, which regulate translation initiation and elongation, is also elevated in the exponential phase (Figure B). ,, Lastly, the abundance of unique proteins implicated in antibiotic response is altered between the exponential and stationary phases (Figure , Table , Tables S7–S10).

Combined, our findings provide a proteome-wide characterization of ribosome biogenesis, RNA modification, and translational regulation across exponential and prolonged stationary phases in E. coli. These data may serve as a resource for future studies of bacterial growth, stress responses, and translational controls.
