Plasma tumour necrosis factor‐alpha‐related proteins in prognosis of heart failure with pulmonary hypertension
Authors: Adam Engel Sällberg, Sara Helleberg, Salaheldin Ahmed, Abdulla Ahmed, Göran Rådegran
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsPatients with heart failure (HF) exhibit poor prognosis, which is further deteriorated by pulmonary hypertension (PH), with negative impact on morbidity and mortality. As PH due to left HF (LHF‐PH) is among the most common causes of PH, there is an urge according to the 2021 European Society of Cardiology HF guidelines to find new biomarkers that aid in prognostication of this patient cohort. Given the role of tumour necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) in HF progression, we aimed to investigate the prognostic value of plasma proteins related to TNF‐α in patients with LHF‐PH, in relation to haemodynamic changes following heart transplantation (HT).Methods and resultsTwenty TNF‐α‐related plasma proteins were analysed using proximity extension assay in healthy controls (n = 20) and patients with LHF‐PH (n = 67), before and 1 year after HT (n = 19). Plasma levels were compared between the groups, and the prognostic values were determined using Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses. Plasma levels of lymphotoxin‐beta receptor (LTBR), TNF receptor superfamily member 6B (TNFRSF6B), and TNF‐related apoptosis‐inducing ligand receptors 1 and 2 (TRAIL‐R1 and TRAIL‐R2, respectively) were higher in LHF‐PH pre‐HT vs. controls (P < 0.0001), as well as higher in pre‐HT vs. post‐HT (P < 0.001). The elevated pre‐HT levels of LTBR, TNFRSF6B, TRAIL‐R1, and TRAIL‐R2 decreased towards the levels of healthy controls after HT. Higher preoperative levels of LTBR, TNFRSF6B, TRAIL‐R1, and TRAIL‐R2 in LHF‐PH were associated with worse survival rates (P < 0.002). In multivariate Cox regression models, each adjusted for age and sex, LTBR, TNFRSF6B, TRAIL‐R1, and TRAIL‐R2 predicted mortality (P < 0.002) [hazard ratio (95% confidence interval): 1.12 (1.04–1.19), 1.01 (1.004–1.02), 1.28 (1.14–1.42), and 1.03 (1.02–1.04), respectively].ConclusionsElevated pre‐HT plasma levels of the TNF‐α‐related proteins LTBR, TNFRSF6B, TRAIL‐R1, and TRAIL‐R2 in LHF‐PH decreased 1 year after HT, displaying a normalization pattern towards the levels of the healthy controls. These proteins were also prognostic, where higher levels were associated with worse survival rates in LHF‐PH, providing new insight in their potential role as prognostic biomarkers. Larger studies are warranted to validate our findings and to investigate their possible pathobiological mechanisms in LHF‐PH.Keywords: Biomarkers, Haemodynamics, Survival, Multi‐marker panel, Cardiovascular diseases, Pulmonary hypertension

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a growing health problem with an increasing prevalence in the aging population. Although clinical and research experience has led to better prevention of most cardiovascular diseases, the 5 year survival of patients with HF remains poor. 1 A complicating condition in left HF (LHF) is pulmonary hypertension (PH), presenting in up to 75% of patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and 83% in HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). 2 In LHF, the development of PH is initiated by a passive backward transmission of the elevated left‐sided filling pressure due to diastolic and systolic left ventricular dysfunction. This results in elevated pressures in the pulmonary circulation, which if sustained may lead to endothelial dysfunction, vasoconstriction, and vascular remodelling, further increasing morbidity and mortality. 2 Therefore, PH demands clinical attention, especially in the context of HF as PH due to LHF (LHF‐PH) is the most common type of PH. 3 Despite well‐defined aetiology, the exact pathophysiological explanation to the progression of PH is complex and only partly understood.

The clinical use of blood‐borne biomarkers is emerging and may reflect different pathophysiological mechanisms involved in the progression of HF. 4 One key pathway is inflammation‐mediated cardiac remodelling and dysfunction, suggesting both pro‐inflammatory proteins and cytokines as potential biomarkers in patients with LHF‐PH. 5 For instance, elevation of the inflammatory cytokine tumour necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α) is associated with increased mortality in patients with HF, 6 reduced left ventricular systolic and diastolic function in dogs, 7 and severe cardiac disease and structural remodelling in mice. 8 , 9 Moreover, high levels of TNF‐α and its receptors are associated with apoptosis in the myocardium, aggravating HF, 10 , 11 , 12 and elevated cardiac TNF‐α levels have been found in patients with ischaemic and dilated cardiomyopathy. 13 Additionally, inflammatory cells and cytokines have been linked to pulmonary vascular remodelling in pulmonary vascular diseases such as LHF‐PH. 14

In context of heart transplantation (HT), the role of TNF‐α and related proteins is not fully established. Some studies suggest increased TNF‐α levels in the heart graft, 15 and other studies propose that risk of transplant rejection is associated with types of TNF‐α polymorphism. 16 However, the data are conflicting, and there is no consensus whether the TNF‐α system is activated or not in these patient groups. 17 , 18

There is a need of less invasive methods to improve risk stratification, predict disease progression, and provide prognostic information of LHF‐PH patients. Blood‐borne biomarkers have been proposed to aid in the clinical management of LHF‐PH. 19 , 20 Thus, it is important to evaluate potential prognostic biomarkers. Given the role of TNF‐α in HF progression, it is possible that plasma levels of TNF‐α‐related proteins may be changed in LHF‐PH, suggesting a potential role in disease prognostication. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the prognostic value of TNF‐α‐related plasma proteins in patients with LHF‐PH, in relation to haemodynamic changes following HT.

Methods

Study population

Seventy‐two patients with LHF‐PH and 20 healthy controls, all of whom were ≥18 years, were enrolled in the Lund Cardio Pulmonary Registry (LCPR) between October 2011 and February 2017 and assessed for eligibility to be included in the study. Patients without haemodynamic values confirming LHF‐PH diagnosis were excluded from the present study (n = 5). Only healthy controls without a past medical history of cardiovascular diseases were included (n = 20). The included patients with LHF‐PH suffered from either HFpEF (n = 31) with EF ≥ 50% or HFrEF (n = 36) with EF < 50%. During this period, 20 of the 67 included patients were heart transplanted and had collected blood samples available for analysis. One patient was diagnosed with PH at the routine 1 year follow‐up after HT and was therefore excluded from the pre‐ and post‐HT groups (n = 1).

The study was done in accordance with the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul. Ethical approval was obtained by the regional ethical board in Lund (Dnr: 2010/114, 2010/442, 2011/368, 2011/777, 2014/92, and 2015/270). All participants provided written informed consent before inclusion.

Plasma proteins and storage

Venous blood samples were collected from peripheral veins in healthy controls and from the venous introducers during right heart catheterization (RHC) in all patients with LHF‐PH, before and when available, 1 year after HT in 19 of the 67 patients, during the routine clinical follow‐up at Skåne University Hospital, Lund, Sweden. The samples were then centrifuged at 2000 g for 10 min at 20°C and stored at −80°C in the LCPR—a cohort of Region Skåne's biobank, Lund, Sweden, initiated by Göran Rådegran in September 2011.

Protein analysis

Plasma retrieved from LCPR were analysed with proximity extension assay (PEA), a method in which the protein levels are detected using matching oligonucleotide‐labelled antibody pairs specific to target proteins. Upon antibody–protein binding, the oligonucleotides become close and hybridize, forming a protein‐specific sequence, which is subsequently quantified with real‐time PCR. 21

TNF‐α‐related proteins were selected from the Proseek Multiplex Cardiovascular II, III, and Oncology II (Olink, proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden). The plasma protein levels are expressed in arbitrary units (AU), as linear normalized protein expression scale—a relative quantification scale corresponding to the inverted Ct value. 21

The proteins analysed were N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP) and 20 plasma proteins related to TNF‐α, that is, caspase‐3 (CASP‐3), CD27 antigen (CD27), CD40 ligand (CD40‐L), FAS‐associated death domain protein (FADD), lymphotoxin‐beta receptor (LTBR), TNF‐related apoptosis‐inducing ligand (TRAIL), TNF‐related apoptosis‐inducing ligand receptors 1 (TRAIL‐R1) and 2 (TRAIL‐R2), TNF ligand superfamily members 6 (FASLG), 13 (TNFSF13), and 13B (TNFSF13B), TNF receptors 1 (TNF‐R1) and 2 (TNF‐R2), and TNF receptor superfamily members 4 (TNFRSF4), 6 (TNFRSF6), 6B (TNFRSF6B), 10C (TNFRSF10C), 13B (TNFRSF13B), 14 (TNFRSF14), and 19 (TNFRSF19).

Haemodynamics and population characteristics

To measure the haemodynamic parameters in the 67 patients with LHF‐PH and at the routine 1 year follow‐up after HT, RHC was performed using a Swan‐Ganz catheter (Baxter Healthcare Corp., Santa Ana, CA, USA), predominantly inserted via the right internal jugular vein. Thereafter, the diagnosis of PH in patients with LHF was established by experienced cardiologists in accordance with the guidelines in effect at the time. 22 Group 2 PH was defined as a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) ≥ 25 mmHg in addition to a pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) > 15 mmHg. LHF‐PH was divided into isolated post‐capillary PH when the pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) ≤ 3 wood units (WU) and/or a diastolic pulmonary pressure gradient (DPG) < 7 mmHg, and combined post‐capillary and pre‐capillary PH when PVR > 3 WU and/or DPG ≥ 7 mmHg. 22

The haemodynamic parameters measured during RHC were diastolic and systolic pulmonary arterial pressures (dPAP and sPAP, respectively), mPAP, PAWP, mean right atrial pressure (MRAP), mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO2), and arterial oxygen saturation (SaO2). Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was measured non‐invasively and cardiac output (CO) was estimated by thermodilution.

The other parameters were calculated as follows: body surface area (BSA) = (weight0.425 × height0.725) × 0.007184, 23 cardiac index (CI) = CO/BSA, stroke volume (SV) = CO/heart rate, SV index (SVI) = SV/BSA, pulmonary arterial compliance (PAC) = SV/(sPAP − dPAP), PVR = (mPAP − PAWP)/CO, PVR index (PVRI) = PVR * BSA, left ventricular stroke work index (LVSWI) = (MAP − PAWP) × SVI, right ventricular stroke work index (RVSWI) = (mPAP − MRAP) × SVI, and arteriovenous oxygen difference (a‐vO2diff) = (SaO2 − SvO2) × plasma haemoglobin × 1.34.

The revised Lund–Malmö equation was applied for estimated glomerular filtrations rate (eGFR) calculations on patients with LHF‐PH. 24 HF medications and immunosuppressive agents were consistent with the guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology and The International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation at that time. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29

Study set‐up

After enrolment, patients with LHF‐PH, regardless of whether they were heart transplanted or not, were followed up until August 2020. Patients were censored at 96 months, with all‐cause mortality as the primary endpoint. To find prognostic proteins, the protein levels were first compared between the different study groups. Proteins with a significant change in controls vs. LHF‐PH and pre‐HT vs. post‐HT, including a change from post‐HT levels towards the levels of the controls, were eligible for further analysis. Next, protein levels were compared between the two outcomes of patients with LHF‐PH (survival vs. death) to find protein‐specific thresholds to be used in prognostication. Thereafter, prognostic proteins in LHF‐PH group were correlated with NT‐proBNP and haemodynamic parameters. Lastly, the effect size of prognostic proteins and NT‐proBNP, age, and sex on all‐cause mortality were analysed. An overview of the study set‐up is shown in Figure 1 .

In the end, four proteins predicted mortality and higher levels were associated with worse survival rates. Statistical analyses

The data were determined visually using histograms and were not normally distributed. The Mann–Whitney U tests were used for nonpaired data, whereas the Wilcoxon tests were used for paired data when appropriate. A normalization pattern from post‐HT towards controls' levels was identified using boxplots. All plasma protein‐level comparisons were corrected with false discovery rate (FDR) (Q‐value at 1%). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to compare outcomes in the LHF‐PH group. Proteins with an area under the curve (AUC) ≥ 0.5 and P value < 0.05 were considered significant and evaluated using Youden's index. Subsequently, Kaplan–Meier curves were used to assess the proteins' prognostic values, with log‐rank test to compare the outcomes. Correlations were performed using Spearman's correlation analyses (FDR, Q = 10%). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were used to evaluate the effect size of the different parameters. Assumptions of seven events (death) per variable were used in the regression analyses.

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.1.2 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA), R 4.1.0, and R studio 3.4.5 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All P values were two‐tailed and considered to indicate statistical significance if P values < 0.05 when no FDR was conducted.

Kaplan–Meier analysis and correlations of the four prognostic proteins

Patients with LHF‐PH with plasma levels of LTBR, TNFRSF6B, TRAIL‐R1, and TRAIL‐R2 above the attained thresholds from the ROC analysis of respective protein had worse survival rates vs. patients with plasma levels below the thresholds of each protein, respectively (P < 0.002) (Figure 2 E–H ). Spearman's correlations of the four proteins with NT‐proBNP and haemodynamic parameters are presented in Supporting Information, Table S4 . TRAIL‐R2 correlated with the largest number of haemodynamic parameters, namely, mPAP, PVR, MRAP, and NT‐proBNP (P < 0.03).

Cox regression analysis of the four prognostic proteins

Elevated levels of LTBR, TNFRSF6B, TRAIL‐R1, and TRAIL‐R2 and higher age in patients with LHF‐PH were significantly associated with higher mortality (P < 0.05), whereas sex and NT‐proBNP levels were not associated with increased mortality. Adjusting for age and sex, all four proteins continued to predict mortality (P < 0.002) (Table 2 ). TRAIL‐R1 displayed the highest hazard ratio per unit increase in plasma levels (AU).

Discussion

The need for additional methods to improve risk stratification, prognostication, and disease monitoring of patients with LHF‐PH is emerging. Although NT‐proBNP is currently used in prognosis of many cardiac diseases, levels of this biomarker may be impacted by several factors, including sex, renal function, age, and body mass index. 29 In addition, there is a lack of clinically relevant LHF‐PH‐specific markers in disease management. Thus, it is important to further evaluate potential biomarkers to aid in diagnosis, prognosis, and disease progression of HF, including LHF‐PH. 19 , 20 In this study, we investigated TNF‐α‐related plasma proteins, due to the association of TNF‐α with HF exacerbation. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Of the 20 proteins, we specifically found that the plasma levels of LTBR, TNFRSF6B, TRAIL‐R1, and TRAIL‐R2 were higher in patients with LHF‐PH compared with healthy controls. Higher levels of these proteins predicted mortality in LHF‐PH. Additionally, the four proteins correlated with at least one of the invasively measured haemodynamic parameters before HT.

TRAIL‐R1 and TRAIL‐R2 are similar receptors that induce apoptosis when bound to the ligand TRAIL. 31 , 32 The receptors are expressed in many cells in the body, including cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells. 33 , 34 Moreover, TRAIL can bind to three decoy receptors, which are associated with anti‐apoptotic activity, meaning that the function of TRAIL is partly dependent on the expressed receptors. 34 In the context of LHF, apoptosis is associated with disease progression. Interestingly, injection of TRAIL in mice reduces apoptosis and fibrosis in the heart, and increased soluble TRAIL is associated with improved survival in patients with advanced HF. 35 , 36 Increased levels of TRAIL‐R2 are also found in many heart conditions, which is consistent with our findings that both TRAIL‐R2 and TRAIL‐R1 were higher in patients with LHF‐PH. 34 , 36 , 37 , 38 In line with these results, we also found that higher plasma TRAIL‐R1 and TRAIL‐R2 were predictors of mortality in patients with LHF‐PH, further supported by the haemodynamic correlations of TRAIL‐R1 with CI and TRAIL‐R2 with mPAP, PVR, MRAP, and NT‐proBNP. However, whether increased levels of soluble receptors are markers of increased TRAIL receptor expression on specific cells, attributed to pathological changes due to LHF and associated PH, or is a result of other activities, remains unknown and warrants further investigations.

TNFSF14 is a ligand that binds to a number of receptors, shared with proteins like the apoptotic ligand FASLG. 39 In the present study, both LTBR and TNFRSF6B—receptors that bind TNFSF14—predicted mortality in LHF‐PH, further supported by their correlation with haemodynamic parameters (LTBR with PVR, MRAP and NT‐proBNP, and TNFRSF6B with mPAP and PVR). The function of TNFSF14 differs depending on cell environment. For instance, TNFSF14–LTBR interactions are associated with apoptosis, cytokine production, T‐cell activation, and proliferation. 39

LTBR is also linked to HF, atherosclerosis, diabetes mellitus, and inflammation in endothelial cells in patients with HFpEF. 40 Given that 21% of the patients in our study suffered from diabetes, it is possible that the elevation of LTBR levels in the LHF‐PH group, in part, is associated with comorbidities such as diabetes. In line with our results, a recent study showed that changes in LTBR levels were associated with increased mortality and re‐hospitalizations in HFpEF patients. 41 However, the prevalence of diabetes did not differ regardless of EF in their study, making it less likely that diabetes explains the elevated LTBR levels in our present study.

TNFRSF6B, also known as decoy receptor 3, is a soluble receptor, binding to ligands such as TNFSF14 and FASLG, thereby neutralizing them. 42 TNFRSF6B is considered a regulating molecule with the potential of modulating differentiation and migration of different immune cells. 43 , 44 In non‐pathological conditions, TNFRSF6B is barely detectable, in contrast with its high expression in different tumour cells. Intriguingly, TNFRSF6B predicts outcome in some inflammatory diseases, including Crohn's disease and acute respiratory distress syndrome. 39 , 45 , 46 Recently, a study showed that decreased levels of TNFRSF6B were associated with increased disease severity in patients with coronary heart disease. 47 Given that inflammation promotes HF progression and that coronary heart diseases may lead to HF, it is reasonable to hypothesize that TNFRSF6B may reflect multiple pathological pathways in LHF‐PH. 5 , 29

Clinically, our results may pave way for future investigations of TNF‐α‐related proteins as biomarker candidates. Biomarkers in addition to NT‐proBNP may be valuable to combine with already existing risk scores, creating a multidimensional assessment tool to improve, for instance, mortality prognostication. Moreover, the use of biomarkers may aid in a better characterization of the diverse LHF‐PH population and the pathophysiology behind HF progression, potentially offering guidance in the clinical management of LHF‐PH.

Strengths and limitations

Major strengths of the present study are the use of PEA to detect protein levels and RHC to measure haemodynamics. PEA offers high sensitivity and specificity, 21 but the protein levels are expressed in AU. This may limit the interpretation and the results generalizability, which is further affected by the median age difference of more than 20 years between controls and the LHF‐PH patients. It is possible that the levels of proteins differ with age, but there was no possibility to match controls with the patient group in the present study. Surprisingly, the levels of NT‐proBNP measured with PEA were not predictive in the Cox regression model, despite being generally considered a prognostic marker of cardiovascular diseases. 3 This could be due to the small study population, but probably related to multiple factors. Due to performing multiple statistical testing, the risk for type I errors may be present, even though FDR was used to correct for mass significance. It should be noted that our study is hypothesis generating and does not address causality or the origin of the proteins. Moreover, the measured proteins cannot be attributed to specific tissues, limiting the interpretability of our results. However, our study provides new insights in LTBR, TNFRSF6B, TRAIL‐R1, and TRAIL‐R2 as prognostic candidates in LHF‐PH.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that plasma levels of LTBR, TNFRSF6B, TRAIL‐R1, and TRAIL‐R2 were higher in patients with LHF‐PH compared with healthy controls. High levels of these four proteins predicted mortality in LHF‐PH patients, supported by correlations with multiple prognostic haemodynamic parameters, and may have the potential to be part of a multi‐marker panel in prognostication of LHF‐PH. Our results warrant further investigations of TNF‐α‐related proteins and LHF‐PH progression.
