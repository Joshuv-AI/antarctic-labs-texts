Introduction

Splenic hemangioma is a rare condition affecting the spleen. While imaging facilitates diagnosis, optimal management remains controversial. Current evidence indicates surgical intervention is typically warranted under any of the following conditions: lesions causing symptoms, lesions exceeding 5 cm in diameter, or presence of acute complications such as hemorrhage, torsion, rupture, or infection [1].

We report a case of a massive splenic hemangioma diagnosed prior to pregnancy, with splenectomy performed during the second trimester. Pregnancy further complicates decision-making due to fetal safety concerns. Few cases of splenic tumor management during pregnancy are reported, with this case representing the largest splenic tumor surgically resected during gestation [1]. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE Criteria [2].

Case presentation

In July 2021, a 34-year-old asymptomatic female underwent abdominal computed tomography (CT) as a part of routine health examination. The scan revealed massive splenomegaly with a maximum cross-sectional dimension of approximately 20 cm × 20 cm, and demonstrated multiple cystic lesions showing no enhancement during either the arterial or portal venous phases (Fig. 1). The patient remained asymptomatic at that time and declined further magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or intervention. She denied any recent overseas travel. Her past medical, surgical, and family history was unremarkable. She denied smoking, alcohol consumption, or use of any illicit drugs.

The images demonstrate significant splenomegaly (with white bidirectional arrows indicating measurement calipers showing a maximum cross-section of approximately 20 cm × 20 cm), displaying polycystic morphology without enhancement during either arterial or portal venous phases.

In September 2024, she was confirmed pregnant, and by October 2024, developed left upper quadrant distension. In November 2024, antenatal ultrasound revealed significant splenomegaly with distortion of the normal splenic anatomy (Fig. 2). The inferior border extended into the pelvic cavity, and the superior border crossed the midline into the right abdomen. The splenic parenchyma was filled with multiple anechoic areas of varying sizes, the largest measuring approximately 3.79 cm × 2.39 cm. And physical examination revealed an enlarged spleen with its inferior border located 25 cm below the intersection of the left midclavicular line and left costal margin. The distance from this intersection to the most distant point of the enlarged spleen measured 20 cm. The right border of the spleen was positioned to the right of the anterior midline, at +10 cm from the midline.

Preoperative ultrasound scan in November 2024. The image demonstrates an enlarged spleen with diffusely distributed anechoic areas of varying sizes. The largest one measures approximately 3.79 cm (+) × 2.39 cm (×) in cross-section, with no definite flow signals detected on Doppler imaging.

Based on multidisciplinary experts' consensus, the clinical team favored open splenectomy over conservative management. This decision was driven by concerns that the gravid uterus in advanced pregnancy could exert compression on the enlarged spleen, potentially leading to splenic rupture and even maternal mortality and fetal demise [3]. Furthermore, no high-level evidence currently supports the safety profile of second-trimester vaccination for both the fetus and pregnant patient [4]. Due to concerns regarding fetal safety, she therefore declined preoperative vaccination.

At 19 weeks + 5 days of gestation, the patient underwent open splenectomy. Intraoperatively, the spleen measured approximately 24.0 cm × 23.0 cm, the uterine fundus was at the level of the umbilicus extending inferiorly into the pelvic cavity and crossing the midline to the right. The patient recovered well postoperatively and was discharged on postoperative day 5. Final histopathological analysis confirmed a diagnosis of splenic hemangioma. Immunohistochemical findings were as follows: CD31 (+), CD34 (partially +), D2–40 (−), CD8 (partially +), AE1/AE3 (partially +), CD21 (FDC+), CA9 (−), CD3 (partially +), CD163 (partially +), and F8-R (+) (Fig. 3).

Outcomes and follow-up

The patient reported no discomfort after discharge and adhered to regular prenatal monitoring as advised by her obstetrician. All obstetric examinations showed normal fetal and maternal status. One-month post-surgery, a blood test revealed mild thrombocytosis with a platelet count of 461 × 109/L. The surgical team determined this did not meet intervention criteria and recommended ongoing observation. At the subsequent follow-up in February 2025, repeat blood testing showed a decreasing trend in the platelet count (453 × 109/L). In April 2025, the patient underwent an uncomplicated cesarean delivery at 38 weeks + 3 days gestation, delivering a male infant weighing 2840 g. The newborn achieved Apgar scores of 10 at 1 min, 10 at 5 min, and 10 at 10 min, with no neonatal complications noted. By June 2025, the patient attended her first postpartum obstetric follow-up, during which no abnormalities were identified.

Discussion

Splenic hemangioma, pathologically classified as a true splenic cyst and representing the most common benign tumor of the spleen, accounts for 0.02 % to 0.16 % prevalence in autopsy data [5,6]. These lesions typically manifest as solitary foci demonstrating solid, cystic, or mixed patterns. Larger cystic hemangiomas may develop degenerative changes, fibrosis, infarction, or thrombosis. Multifocal or diffuse involvement remains uncommon [5,7]. In ultrasound scans, splenic hemangiomas typically present as well-circumscribed hyperechoic masses that may exhibit cystic or solid components. On CT imaging, they appear as well-defined hypodense lesions within the splenic parenchyma, with enhancement patterns mirroring those of hepatic cavernous hemangiomas [8]. The CT scan in July 2021 of this patient revealed a cystic splenic lesion, underscoring the diagnostic challenge in reliably differentiating true splenic cysts from pseudocysts based solely on imaging findings–a distinction ultimately requiring histopathological confirmation. Despite the minimal radiation risk associated with CT, fetal safety considerations and patient preference led to our selection of ultrasonography for preoperative characterization of the splenic lesion [9]. Based on current reports, splenic hemangiomas requiring surgical intervention during pregnancy may be managed surgically as splenic cystic lesions [[13], [14], [15]]. Yagmur et al. recommended surgery for subcapsular masses or masses protruding from the splenic surface, particularly if the tumor diameter exceeds 2 cm, even if asymptomatic [16]. Open splenectomy remains the classic surgical approach for splenic cysts, and most cases of splenic cysts during pregnancy have also been managed with open splenectomy [17]. Majesky et al. and Varban et al. reported laparoscopic splenectomy for splenic cysts, with no severe complications and healthy full-term deliveries [13,18]. In contrast, Rotas et al. and Mahran et al. reported percutaneous aspiration for 17 cm and 20 cm splenic cysts, respectively, during pregnancy, but these cases resulted in severe complications and cyst recurrence [19,20]. Kapp et al. performed laparoscopic cystectomy on a pregnant patient with a 15 cm primary epithelial splenic cyst, successfully preserving the spleen [21].

There are currently no guidelines or consensus for managing splenic lesions during pregnancy. Treatment decisions should balance symptoms, lesion size, proximity to adjacent organs, surgical risks, and pregnancy-related complications while considering the gestational stage, fetal health, and patient delivery preferences. Emergency surgery is mandatory for complications such as splenic torsion, rupture, or infarction [1,15]. Preoperative discussions involving obstetrics, surgery, and other specialties are recommended for collaborative decision-making.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of symptomatic splenic hemangioma during pregnancy. This case highlights the importance of balancing maternal and fetal benefits and risks when making surgical decisions for pregnant patients. It also underscores the crucial role of multidisciplinary teams in managing complex clinical cases, enabling comprehensive consideration of treatment options to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes.
