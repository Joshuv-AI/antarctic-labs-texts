Defining the role of systemic autoimmune markers in adult epilepsy: A focus on autoimmune‐associated epilepsy
Authors: I‐Ting Lin, Chiehyu Shen, Chi‐Ting Chung, Chang‐Yu Edward Cheng, Pin‐Yu Chen, Tun Jao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAutoimmune mechanisms are a recognized cause of adult epilepsy, with immunotherapy offering etiology‐specific treatment. Autoimmune‐associated epilepsy is an emerging clinical entity, for which early diagnosis remains challenging and largely depends on neuronal antibody testing. This study investigates the utility of serum systemic autoantibodies as surrogate markers for positive neuronal antibodies and their potential in predicting epilepsy outcomes.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled adult epilepsy patients, excluding those with prior autoimmune diseases. All patients had baseline laboratory data, including ESR, ANA, and anti‐ENA. Further immunological evaluations included serum or CSF studies for systemic or neuronal autoantibodies. We compared the prevalence of systemic autoantibodies, seizure patterns, and EEG characteristics across immune, other, and unknown etiological groups and assessed epilepsy outcomes based on autoimmune comorbidities and systemic autoantibody presence.ResultsAmong 333 patients (immune = 12, other = 122, unknown = 199), autoimmune‐associated epilepsy showed higher rates of status epilepticus (41.7% vs. 0.5% vs. 2.5%, p < 0.001), multifocal epileptogenicity (41.7% vs. 9.1% vs. 8.2%, p < 0.01), ≥2 anti‐seizure medications (83.3% vs. 28.6% vs. 43.4%, p < 0.001), and a worse seizure outcome. Systemic autoantibodies were prevalent across subgroups (27.6%–41.7%), and autoimmune comorbidities were newly diagnosed in 5.5%–9.8% of cases, but no differences were observed across groups. The prevalence of antiphospholipid syndrome (2.1%) and Sjögren's syndrome (1.5%) was high in our study cohort. Neither autoimmune comorbidities nor systemic autoantibodies correlated with seizure types, EEG patterns, seizure frequency, number of ASMs, or outcomes.SignificanceAutoantibody testing contributes to unexpectedly high rates of new rheumatological diagnoses following epilepsy, often preceding systemic symptoms. However, systemic autoantibodies cannot reliably predict epilepsy characteristics or outcomes. Multifocal epileptogenicity on EEG and high seizure burden may be characteristic features in autoimmune‐associated epilepsy. These findings suggest that adult epilepsy patients should benefit from a tiered approach to immunological evaluation.Plain Language SummaryAutoimmune causes of epilepsy are increasingly recognized, and some patients may benefit from immunotherapy in addition to standard anti‐seizure drugs. INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is the seventh leading cause of neurological health loss worldwide. 1 Anti‐seizure medications (ASMs) are central to epilepsy management, preventing disability and improving quality of life. However, despite the development of newer‐generation ASMs, up to 36.3% of epilepsy patients encountered in neurology clinics remain drug‐refractory and experience functional disability due to uncontrolled seizures. 2

First introduced in 2010 and further refined in 2017, the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) established the classification framework for seizure etiology. 3 , 4 This classification provides insights into clinical management and paves the way for etiology‐specific treatments beyond ASMs. Following this framework, autoimmunity has emerged as a distinct etiological category of epilepsy, with immunotherapy offering a potential avenue for seizure control. Subsequent studies have conceptualized the differentiation between acute symptomatic seizures secondary to autoimmune encephalitis (ASSAE) and autoimmune‐associated epilepsy (AAE), based on their distinct therapeutic and prognostic implications. Only the latter condition is considered to have enduring epileptogenicity due to postencephalitic structural pathology or ongoing CD8‐positive T cell–mediated brain inflammation. 5 However, this differentiation remains challenging in clinical practice, especially early in the disease course. 6 , 7 The detection of specific neuronal autoantibodies is essential in both conditions. Antibodies against surface antigens are more commonly associated with ASSAE, whereas antibodies against intracellular antigens may contribute to AAE. 5 The Antibody Prevalence in Epilepsy and Encephalopathy (APE2) score is a validated tool that integrates clinical features, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings, and brain MRI results to predict the presence of specific neuronal antibodies in patients with epilepsy or encephalopathy. 8 However, this approach primarily emphasizes symptoms associated with autoimmune encephalitis, and thus is more suitable to identify patients with ASSAE. No existing tool is currently available to screen for potential AAE patients in routine epilepsy clinics. Furthermore, research and clinical interest have primarily focused on AAE patients with specific neuronal antibodies, while the relationship between rheumatological diseases and AAE has received comparatively less attention.

The reciprocal relationship between autoimmune diseases and epilepsy has been demonstrated by both large‐scale and smaller studies. 9 , 10 , 11 Systemic autoantibodies, cost‐effective and widely used in rheumatology, aid in the diagnosis of autoimmune disease. Whether these autoantibodies also serve as markers of neuroinflammation and are associated with worse epilepsy outcomes has not been rigorously examined.

In this study, we aimed to investigate (1) the characteristics and potential biomarkers of autoimmune‐associated epilepsy in adult populations using routinely available clinical tools, and (2) the epilepsy outcomes in patients with positive systemic autoantibodies compared with those with negative results.

METHODS

Study population

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital (approval number: 202211091RINC). We retrospectively enrolled consecutive, unrelated adult epilepsy patients at the epilepsy clinic of a tertiary medical center in Taiwan between April 2022 and May 2023. The diagnosis of epilepsy was made according to the ILAE 2014 diagnostic criteria. 12 Patients with ASSAE were excluded. Following the recommendation by Steriade et al., a minimum period of 2 years should elapse before a diagnosis of AAE is made. 13 Patients with autoimmune comorbidities preceding epilepsy onset were also excluded.

All included patients had at least basic immunological laboratory data, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), antinuclear antibody (ANA), and anti‐extractable nuclear antigen antibody screening (anti‐ENA), although the timing of these tests varied and was not restricted. Further immunological evaluation included either serum or CSF studies, testing for specific autoantibodies associated with systemic rheumatological diseases, as well as neuronal and onconeural antibodies. The depth of the immunological assessment was determined based on screening results, clinical severity, and diagnostic uncertainty (Figure 1). Additionally, the demographic characteristics, seizure types, electroencephalographic classifications, ASM responses, associated rheumatological symptoms, referrals to rheumatologists, and use of immunomodulating agents were also reviewed and analyzed.

Autoantibody (+): Positive result for any of the tested systemic autoantibodies (neuronal antibodies not included), Autoantibody (−): Negative for all the tested systemic autoantibodies, AAE, autoimmune‐associated epilepsy; CNS, central nervous system; NAb, neuronal antibodies.

We compared these factors across three epilepsy etiological groups: immune, other (genetic, structural, infectious, metabolic), and unknown to evaluate potential biomarkers characterizing AAE. Besides, patients were re‐categorized into four distinct groups according to their autoimmune diagnosis and the positivity of systemic autoantibodies. These groups included: (1) the immune etiological group, comprising individuals with an autoimmune disease and central nervous system (CNS) involvement; (2) the systemic autoimmune comorbidity group, consisting of patients with an autoimmune diagnosis but without known CNS involvement; (3) the positive autoantibody group, which included patients with any positive systemic autoantibodies but without a definite autoimmune diagnosis; and (4) the negative autoantibody group. For detailed cut‐off values and testing methodologies for each immune assay, please refer to Section 2.4. Using this approach, our secondary interest was to evaluate whether occult or coexisting autoimmunity could contribute to more challenging seizure management.

Seizure etiology

The etiology of epilepsy for each patient was meticulously scrutinized by two neurologists, adhering to the classification proposed by the ILAE task force in 2017. 4 In our study, immune etiology, equal to a diagnosis of AAE, was defined as the presence of 1 any positive serum or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) specific neuronal antibodies, 2 comorbid autoimmune diseases with evidence of brain inflammation demonstrated by neuroimaging or CSF studies, or 3 acute meningoencephalitis without identifiable pathogen but with CSF findings favoring immune process. An infectious etiology was defined by an encephalitis or meningitis with identifiable pathogen or with suspicious CSF profile of infection. A genetic etiology was established by identifying pathogenic genetic variants through genetic testing in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics–Association for Molecular Pathology guidelines, followed by a careful genotype–phenotype correlation analysis. 14 A structural or metabolic etiology was assigned according to correlated imaging and biochemical findings respectively. Unknown etiology was defined as (1) the absence of evidence for the etiologies mentioned above and a lack of obvious clinical features suggesting genetic causes, or (2) insufficient diagnostic studies.

Seizure presentation and electroencephalographic features

Seizure type was classified by a clinical epileptologist based on the 2025 International League Against Epilepsy definition 15 Seizure outcome was defined based on seizure frequency and the maximum number of anti‐seizure medications used during the study period for each patient. Scalp electroencephalogram (EEG) or continuous EEG was performed and interpreted according to the American Clinical Neurophysiology Society's Standardized Critical Care EEG Terminology. 16 Based on scalp EEG, multifocal epileptogenicity was defined as interictal epileptiform discharges arising independently from three or more anatomically distinct regions with at least one pattern in each hemisphere.

Autoantibodies and autoimmune diagnoses

Systemic autoantibodies were examined as part of an integrated diagnostic approach to better elucidate the etiology of epilepsy. In this study, we analyzed the level of ESR and the presence of ANA, anti‐ENA, rheumatoid factor (RF), antiphospholipid antibodies (APLAs), antithyroid peroxidase antibody (anti‐TPO), and antithyroglobulin antibody (anti‐TG). The values and positivity results of these inflammatory markers and autoantibodies were documented at the time of their initial testing.

ANA was assessed by indirect immunofluorescence using a commercially available kit at a 1:80 dilution (IIFT: HEp‐20‐10 EUROPattern, Euroimmun, Lübeck, Germany), with a positivity threshold of 1:160 to minimize false‐positives. 17 Anti‐ENA antibody screening was measured via fluorescence enzyme immunoassay (EliA CTD Screen Well, Thermo Fischer Scientific, MA, USA), with a positivity cut‐off of >1.0 antibody index. RF levels were determined using turbidimetry (RF Latex Reagent, Beckman Coulter, CA, USA), with a cut‐off of >14 IU/mL. APLAs included lupus anticoagulant, anti‐beta2‐glycoprotein, anti‐cardiolipin IgM and IgG, and antiphospholipid IgM and IgG antibodies. Lupus anticoagulant was assessed using a clotting method, while anti‐beta2‐glycoprotein was measured via fluorescence enzyme immunoassay. Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was used to quantify anti‐cardiolipin and antiphospholipid antibodies (QUANTA Lite® ACA IgM III, Inova Diagnostics, CA, USA; EliA Cardiolipin IgG Well, Thermo Fischer Scientific, MA, USA; APhL ELISA® HRP IgG and IgM Kit, Louisville APL Diagnostics, TX, USA). Anti‐TPO and anti‐TG antibodies were measured using chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (ARCHITECT Anti‐TPO and Anti‐Tg, Abbott, IL, USA), with reference ranges of <5.61 IU/mL and <14.4 IU/mL, respectively. In this study, a result was considered positive if any APLAs tested positive in a single immune sampling.

Serum and CSF neuronal autoantibodies associated with limbic encephalitis or paraneoplastic neurological syndrome were analyzed using the commercial kit Autoimmune encephalitis mosaic via cell‐based indirect immunofluorescence assays and the Paraneoplastic Neurologic Syndrome 12 Ag via EUROLINE immunoblot assays, respectively (Euroimmun, Lübeck, Germany).

The diagnosis of comorbid autoimmune diseases was made by the referring rheumatologist based on the established diagnostic criteria for each specific condition.

Statistics

Discrete variables were expressed as median and interquartile range, categorical variables as % (counts). The median values of discrete variables were compared among groups using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Fisher's exact test was used throughout the analysis to compare proportions between categorical groups. Bonferroni correction was utilized for post hoc analysis. MATLAB (version R2023b) was used for all statistical analysis.

RESULTS

Basic characteristics

A total of 374 adult patients with epilepsy were initially included, while five patients were excluded due to questionable epilepsy diagnosis, 12 patients were excluded due to known autoimmune comorbidities preceding epilepsy onset, and another 24 patients were excluded for not undergoing testing for any autoantibodies. The remaining 333 patients were enrolled for analysis (Figure 1). Overall, the etiology was classified as immune in 12 (3.6%), metabolic in 4 (1.2%), genetic in 6 (1.8%), infection in 10 (2.7%), structural in 103 (30.9%), and unknown in the remaining 199 (59.8%) patients (Figure 2). Comparison among immune, other, and unknown etiological groups

First, a comparative analysis among the immune, other, and unknown etiological groups was conducted (Table 1). The demographic features, including age at enrollment, age at epilepsy onset, and sex, showed no statistically significant differences among the three groups. Regarding seizure characteristics, a significantly higher proportion of patients in the immune etiological group had focal seizures (100%), compared with 56.8% and 76.2% in the unknown or other etiological groups, respectively. A higher proportion of seizures in the unknown etiological group were classified as unknown onset (32.7%) compared with the immune (0%) or other etiological groups (19.5%), respectively. Clinically, more patients in the immune group presented with status epilepticus than in both the unknown or other groups (41.7% versus 0.5% and 2.5%). Encephalographically, multifocal epileptogenicity was found in a significantly higher proportion of immune patients (41.7%), compared with 9.1% and 8.2% in the other two groups, respectively. Regarding seizure outcome, a significantly higher proportion of patients in the immune etiological groups demanded more than two ASMs (83.3%, 28.6%, and 43.4%, respectively). Meanwhile, a significantly lower proportion of patients in the immune and other groups achieved seizure freedom during the study period compared with the unknown group (25.0%, 62.3%, and 47.5%, respectively).

Patients across all three etiological groups—not exclusively those with an immune etiology—exhibited a high proportion of individuals with at least one positive systemic autoantibody (41.7% vs. 27.6% vs. 28.7%, p = 0.54). The positivity rates for ANA, anti‐ENA, RA, and anti‐TG were comparable among the three groups. Notably, for APLAs and anti‐TPO, the immune etiological group even demonstrated seemingly lower positivity rates compared with the other groups, although these differences did not reach statistical significance (0% vs. 19.3% vs. 28.1%, p = 0.21; and 0% vs. 11.0% vs. 8.8%, p = 0.88, respectively).

Consistent with the clinical confirmation of an immune etiology, all patients within the immune group received immunotherapy—a proportion significantly higher than that observed in the unknown (4.0%) and other etiological groups (9.8%).

Comparison between groups based on autoimmune diagnosis and nonneuronal autoantibody status

In Table 3, patients were categorized into four groups based on their autoimmune diagnosis and systemic autoantibody status. Demographic characteristics, including age at enrollment, age at epilepsy onset, and sex, showed no statistically significant differences among the four groups. The distribution of etiologies across the latter three groups was not statistically significant. Regarding seizure type, the immune etiological group exhibited a significantly higher frequency of focal onset seizures and clinical status epilepticus, whereas no significant differences were observed among the latter three groups. A significantly higher proportion of patients in the first group exhibited multifocal and generalized epileptogenicity, while the other three groups showed no difference. Patients in the first group required a significantly higher number of ASMs, whereas no significant differences were observed in ASM usage among the latter three groups. However, the 1‐year seizure freedom rate did not differ significantly across all four groups.

A significantly higher proportion of patients in the second group reported rheumatological symptoms compared with those in the third and fourth groups. Regarding exposure to immunomodulating agents, a significantly higher proportion of patients in the first and second groups received these agents compared with those in the third and fourth groups.

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to elucidate the intricate relationship between neuronal and systemic autoantibodies in adult patients with epilepsy. We demonstrate that the presence of systemic autoantibodies alone is insufficient to differentiate patients with AAE from those with other etiologies, nor can it reliably predict epilepsy outcomes. However, we also observe that patients with AAE are prone to more severe manifestations of focal seizures, including a higher prevalence of clinical status epilepticus, multifocal epileptogenicity on scalp EEG, greater use of ASMs, and a lower likelihood of achieving seizure freedom. In our study, the multifocal epileptogenicity observed in AAE is consistent with findings from previous studies, which predominantly investigated patients with ASSAE and described multiple ictal patterns or multifocal seizures. 18 , 19 In this regard, multifocal epileptogenicity may represent a pathophysiologically relevant feature associated with autoimmune‐associated seizure or epilepsy, although it may not be useful to differentiate AAE from ASSAE.

While previous studies have reported a low incidence of epilepsy in anti‐NMDAR encephalitis, anti‐NMDAR antibodies were still the most commonly detected neuronal autoantibodies in our patients with AAE. 20 , 21 Reviewing the three patients positive for anti‐NMDAR antibodies, two exhibited atypical presentations characterized by seizures as the predominant symptom, without a definitive history of encephalitis (Table S1). Notably, these two patients underwent neuronal autoantibody testing only after a significant delay—more than 5 years after seizure onset. An argument could be raised regarding the risk of false‐positive findings in these patients. However, the antibodies were detected in CSF, and neither patient had clinical features suggestive of CNS disorders typically associated with false‐positive anti‐NMDAR antibody results, as reported in a previous study. 22 Therefore, these findings suggest that in some cases, cell surface neuronal antibodies may be associated with remote or atypical presentations of encephalitis, in which seizures are the only symptom, potentially delaying immunotherapy and contributing to the development of AAE.

Our study also showed that systemic autoantibodies are not reliable for predicting immune etiology in epilepsy, due to their unexpectedly high prevalence across different etiologies. For ANA, the proportion of individuals with a positive test result ranged from 9.1% to 15.8% across the various etiological groups. In comparison to the overall rate of positive samples as 9.5% (1:160 dilution) reported by Hayashi et al. in their epidemiological study of the general Japanese population, the epilepsy patient cohort in our study exhibits a comparable to higher positivity rate across all etiological groups. 23 For anti‐ENA, the proportion of individuals with a positive test result ranged from 8.6% to 16.7%, with the highest rate observed in the immune etiological group, albeit without statistical significance. In different Asian or Caucasian general population cohorts with various sizes, the rates of positive samples have been reported from 2.7% to 7%. 24 , 25 , 26 For RA factor, the proportion of individuals with a positive test result ranged from 4.8% to 11.1%, which can be considered comparable to findings from previous studies. 27 The proportion of APLAs positivity varied across the three etiological groups. Patients under the other etiological group had the highest positivity rate (28.1%), followed by those in the unknown etiological group (19.3%). In contrast, none of the patients in the immune etiological group with available data tested positive for APLAs. Previous studies evaluating the APLAs prevalence rate in American and European general population demonstrated a positive rate between 14.5% and 15.1%. 28 , 29 Compared with these results, patients in the unknown and especially those in the other etiological group may exhibit higher positivity rates. These observations may reflect that antiphospholipid autoantibodies are more relevant to nonimmune mechanisms, such as cerebrovascular events, infection or even metabolic factors, which all can result in epileptogenesis. 30 , 31 Regarding thyroid autoantibodies, the proportion of patients tested positive for anti‐TPO antibodies showed no significant difference across the three etiological groups, ranging from 0% to 11.0%. Similarly, the proportion of patients tested positive for antithyroglobulin antibodies followed the similar trend, ranging from 13.0% to 20.0%. Consistent with previous studies reporting the presence of circulating thyroid autoantibodies in ~ 10–20% of asymptomatic individuals, our patient cohort did not exhibit a higher positivity rate compared with the general population. 32 , 33

The higher prevalence of autoantibodies was linked to higher rates of autoimmune comorbidities in our study. The overall prevalence of antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) in this epilepsy patient cohort was 2.1% (7/338), including a case of presumed seronegative APS from the immune etiological group. Compared with the globally reported prevalence rate of up to 50 cases per 100,000, the prevalence of APS in our cohort was exceedingly high. 34 A nationwide population‐based study also reported the incidence of APS at only 6.49 per 10,000 person‐years in the Taiwanese general population in 2013. 35 The overall prevalence of Sjögren syndrome in our study was 1.5% (5/338), which was also markedly elevated compared with the prevalence of 0.0058%. 36 The overall prevalence of autoimmune thyroid disease in our cohort was 1.8% (6/338), which is lower than the 5.8% (95% CI: 2.8–9.9%) reported for Hashimoto's thyroiditis in Asia in a meta‐analysis by Hu et al. (2022). 37 Several other autoimmune diseases were also identified. Interestingly, no patients in our cohort were newly diagnosed with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), underscoring the rarity of seizures as the initial clinical manifestation of SLE. 38 , 39

We thus aimed to investigate whether these autoimmune comorbidities or potential autoimmune traits, as indicated by the presence of positive autoantibodies, influence seizure presentation and the difficulty of epilepsy control. However, as shown in Table 3, we did not identify a significant difference in electroclinical presentation or epilepsy outcomes when comparing the second (autoimmune comorbidity), third (positive autoantibody), and fourth (none) groups. In contrast, the first group (immune etiological group) remained a distinct entity compared with the other three groups. These observations may suggest that these autoimmune comorbidities or autoimmune traits are more likely bystanders and contribute to merely systemic but not CNS inflammation. However, due to the cross‐sectional study design, the seizure outcome in these patients may have already been modified by immunomodulating agents that were prescribed in most cases. Nevertheless, the immunotherapy used in treating systemic autoimmune diseases usually follows a stepwise rule, initiating from low‐potency immunomodulating agents. 40 , 41 , 42 From this perspective, whether epilepsy should be considered a sentinel event for systemic autoimmune disease in these patients and whether earlier aggressive immunotherapy could improve epilepsy outcome remain unresolved questions.

Based on our study, we advocate for a tiered approach to managing epilepsy patients with suspected autoimmunity (Figure 3). For patients presenting with additional features suggestive of autoimmune encephalitis, timely testing for specific neuronal antibodies followed by aggressive immunotherapy is mandatory. Suspicious encephalitis history of unknown cause, status epilepticus, drug resistance, and multifocal epileptogenicity on EEG may serve as potential indicators of AAE. In epilepsy patients with concomitant systemic rheumatological symptoms, testing for systemic autoantibodies and disease‐specific autoantibodies is suggested. When a definitive autoimmune comorbidity is identified, the initiation of immunotherapy tailored to the specific autoimmune disease is required. Testing for neuronal antibodies may also be considered in such cases. Additionally, screening for systemic autoantibodies might be considered even in patients without overt systemic rheumatological symptoms.

Our study had several limitations. First, as a single‐center study, biases may exist due to physician experience, EEG interpretation, and laboratory workflows. Second, a certain degree of missing autoantibody serological data and variation in the timing of immunological test sampling was noticeable due to the retrospective study design. Third, as most systemic or neuronal antibodies were tested by a single method without confirmatory testing, the possibility of false‐positives should not be overlooked. Fourth, the cross‐sectional design limited our ability to track seizure frequency in relation to associated treatments, particularly immunotherapy, over time. Lastly, the small sample size may have reduced statistical power and led to an underestimation of differences between groups.

In summary, our study reveals that the presence of systemic autoantibodies alone is insufficient to determine the coexistence of specific neuronal antibodies, as it does not reliably distinguish immune etiology from other groups. Notably, patients with AAE exhibit a higher frequency of focal seizures and status epilepticus, along with an increased tendency toward drug resistance; multifocal epileptogenicity may therefore represent a relevant electroencephalographic feature associated with this entity. Overall, epilepsy patients in our cohort exhibit a high positivity rate for ANA, anti‐ENA, and APLAs. Consistent with these findings, they also have a high prevalence of Sjögren syndrome and APS. However, seizure characteristics and epilepsy outcomes are not influenced by autoimmune comorbidities or the presence of positive systemic autoantibodies. Based on these findings, we propose a tiered diagnostic and therapeutic approach for epilepsy patients in whom autoimmunity is concerned.
