Effectiveness of a Single Dose of Pneumococcal Conjugate Vaccine Against Invasive Pneumococcal Disease in Children: A Systematic Literature Review
Authors: Eileen M Dunne, Linge Hong, Benjamin M Althouse, Kyla Hayford, Luis Jodar, Bradford D Gessner, Christian Theilacker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWhile pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) are typically administered to infants using a three- or four-dose regimen, children may receive less immunogenic regimens due to missed doses or alternative schedules. The level of direct protection in children vaccinated with a single dose of PCV remains unclear.MethodsWe performed a systematic review of observational studies published during 2000–2024 on vaccine effectiveness (VE) of a single dose of PCV7, PCV10, or PCV13 against vaccine-type invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) in children. Results were stratified by vaccine and age at administration, and meta-analysis performed to generate pooled VE estimates.ResultsTwenty-seven studies met the inclusion criteria: nine reported VE for PCV7, four for PCV10, seven for PCV13, and seven reported VE separately for more than one PCV. For PCV7, pooled VE was 64.6% (95% CI 47.3, 76.2) when administered < 12 months or age unspecified and 81.6% (95% CI 72.5, 87.7) when given ≥ 12 months. For PCV10, pooled VE was 73.0% (95% CI − 29.4, 94.4) when age at vaccination was unspecified, and one study reported 68.0% (95% CI 17.6, 87.6) VE when administered ≥ 12 months. For PCV13, pooled VE was 56.8% (95% CI 44.1, 66.6) when administered < 12 months or age unspecified, and 79.2% (95% CI 65.5, 87.5) when given ≥ 12 months.ConclusionsAvailable evidence demonstrates that a single dose of PCV provides protection against vaccine-type IPD, especially when administered after age 12 months. While complete vaccination according to licensed schedules provides optimal protection, our findings support single-dose catch-up programs for toddlers. Potential single-dose strategies for infants in humanitarian emergencies warrant further exploration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-025-01211-5.Keywords: Pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, Invasive pneumococcal disease, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Vaccine effectiveness, Dosing

Key Summary Points

We conducted a systematic literature review to compile evidence on the level of protection provided by a single dose of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) in children.Twenty-seven studies that reported the effectiveness of a single dose of PCV7, PCV10, or PCV13 against vaccine-type invasive pneumococcal disease were included.Results demonstrated that a single dose of PCV provides some protection against invasive pneumococcal disease, particularly when administered after 12 months of age.While complete vaccination according to recommended schedules is needed for optimal protection, results support single-dose vaccination strategies in certain circumstances.

Introduction

Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs) protect against disease caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, the leading cause of bacterial deaths in children aged under five years worldwide [1]. The 7-valent PCV (PCV7) was licensed for use in children in 2000, with PCV10 and PCV13 subsequently developed to include additional serotypes [2]. PCV dosing schedules vary by vaccine and country, and typically consist of a two- or three-dose primary series given in infancy followed by a booster dose (2 + 1 or 3 + 1 schedules, respectively) [3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends a three-dose schedule (2 + 1 or 3 + 0) for PCV10 and PCV13 [2]. The licensed posology for PCV13 in the United States and Europe is summarized in Supplementary Table 1. In some circumstances, children may receive only a single dose of PCV according to the approved schedule (only old enough for the first dose, or as part of a catch-up program for older children). Children might also be inadvertently vaccinated with only a single dose because of missed or delayed vaccination. In Germany, only 31.5% children receive their second PCV dose on time, leaving them protected by only a single dose during an age of high risk for pneumococcal disease [4].

Despite substantial reductions in pediatric invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) following widespread use of PCVs, a high burden of disease due to non-PCV13 serotypes remains, prompting the development of expanded valency vaccines PCV15 and PCV20 [5]. These new PCVs were licensed based on safety and immunogenicity compared to a licensed PCV, in line with WHO guidance [6]. The addition of serotypes to PCV formulations has led to slight reductions in immunogenicity when compared to earlier PCVs [7]. The totality of data supported licensure for higher valency PCVs, including PCV20, however, the interpretation of lower immunogenicity, particularly for serotypes that missed non-inferiority comparisons following a two-dose primary series, is unclear [8].

The effectiveness of PCV7, PCV10, and PCV13 against vaccine-type IPD has been well documented through observational studies, such as case–control studies and the indirect cohort (Broome) method, which compares the vaccination status of patients with vaccine-type IPD to those with non-vaccine-type IPD [9–12]. There is evidence that a single dose of PCV can provide some protection against pneumococcal infection. For example, the multicountry European SpIDnet study found that the vaccine effectiveness (VE) of a single dose of PCV13 given < 12 months of age against vaccine-type IPD was 60.6% (95% CI 29–78) [12]. Although lower than the VE for fully vaccinated children (88.7%, [95% CI 83–93]), their findings support that a single dose of PCV can provide some protection. The effectiveness of a single dose of PCV13 suggests that the lower immunogenicity generated by two infant doses of PCV20 might still provide adequate immunity against IPD, as IgG levels were higher than for a single dose of PCV13 [8]. In certain situations, administering a single dose of PCV might be pragmatic, such as during catch-up campaigns for toddlers or in humanitarian emergency settings where timely administration of multiple doses is not feasible. Despite the potential value of these data, VE of a single dose of PCV against IPD in children has not been evaluated by a systematic literature review to date. Therefore, we aimed to summarize the evidence on the effectiveness of a single dose of PCV7, PCV10, and PCV13 against vaccine-type IPD in children.

Methods

Search Strategy

We conducted a systematic literature review according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Eligibility criteria were developed using PICOS (population, intervention, comparator, outcomes, study design) framework (Supplementary Table 2) to identify observational studies that reported the effectiveness of a single dose of PCV against vaccine-type IPD in children aged ≤ 18 years [13]. We included studies on PCV7, PCV10 (Synflorix), and PCV13 effectiveness. More recently licensed vaccines such as PCV10 (Pneumosil), PCV15, and PCV20 were not included as no effectiveness data are available. Exclusion criteria were randomized controlled trials (due to low likelihood of incomplete vaccination), vaccine impact studies (e.g., pre-post studies comparing IPD incidence before and after PCV introduction), reviews, editorials, and studies on pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine or other outcomes such as pneumococcal carriage or otitis media. A systematic search was conducted in July 2024 using the PubMed database for relevant literature published in English since 2000, with search terms detailed in Supplementary Table 3. We also conducted hand searching to identify additional relevant studies by checking the references of three published systematic literature reviews on PCV effectiveness [14–16].

Study Selection and Data Extraction

For all search results, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts to exclude studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria. Next, two independent reviewers evaluated the full text using the inclusion and exclusion criteria. For included studies, data extraction was conducted in Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) by one reviewer with a quality check by a second reviewer. We extracted the following information: country, time frame, study design, age group, sample size (total number of IPD cases and controls), number of vaccine-type IPD cases, vaccine assessed, routine infant immunization schedule, how IPD cases were identified, and how VE was calculated, including covariates used in adjusted analysis. For the outcomes, we extracted VE and 95% confidence intervals, and outcome definition. Some studies of PCV13 VE reported results separately for the six additional PCV13 nonPCV7 serotypes, and some studies classified certain vaccine-related serotypes, such as 6C as vaccine-type IPD due to evidence of cross-protection. VE results for a single dose of PCV were extracted, as well as VE for any other number of doses/exposure definitions reported. We report adjusted VE unless only the crude VE was provided. Risk of bias assessment was conducted by a single reviewer using a modified Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (Supplementary file) [17].

Data Analysis

We summarized data descriptively, stratified by vaccine and exposure definition. We conducted meta-analyses stratified by vaccine and timing of administration (≥ 12 months, or < 12 months or unspecified). Meta-analyses included case–control and indirect cohort studies (Broome method), which are a type of case–control study, and excluded studies of high-risk children and retrospective cohort studies. We log-transformed VE estimates to stabilize variance and assume normality, thereby permitting standard meta-analysis methods for multiplicative effects. Consequently, the final meta-analyzed VE estimates can differ slightly from those reported in source manuscripts after back-transformation to the percent scale. Pooled VE estimates were generated in R (version 4.4.2) [18] using the metafor package [19] using random effects models. We conducted sensitivity analyses to exclude publications with overlapping IPD cases, to exclude studies with age at vaccination unspecified, and for PCV13, to exclude studies that reported VE only for serotypes unique to the vaccine (PCV13-nonPCV7 serotypes), and to separate meta-analyses by study type (indirect cohort or case–control), when there were sufficient studies for stratification. Publication bias for studies included in meta-analyses was assessed using a funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Ethical Approval

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. Therefore, ethical approval was not required.

Results

The search yielded 241 publications, of which 27 met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Publications included 21 studies from high-income countries and six from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) (Table 1). Study designs included indirect cohort (n = 15), case–control (n = 11), and retrospective cohort (n = 2), with one publication containing both an indirect cohort and case–control analysis. Nine studies evaluated VE of PCV7, four for PCV10, seven for PCV13, and seven studies for more than one vaccine (with VE results stratified by vaccine for all except one). Most studies focused on younger children, with only two including children aged > 5 years [10, 20]. Two studies were conducted in at-risk populations, including one that evaluated VE of PCV7 or PCV13 in transplant recipients and did not find any protection following a single dose of either vaccine (VE – 111.1%, 95% CI – 1648.9, 74.5), although sample size was small (14 vaccine-type IPD cases) [20]. Two studies in South Africa stratified analyses of PCV7 or PCV13 VE by human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status; however, VE results for a single dose of PCV were only reported for HIV-uninfected children [21, 22]. In an Australian cohort study that evaluated the effectiveness of PCV7 and PCV13, VE results for a single dose were only reported for non-Aboriginal children [23]. Risk of bias assessment found that included studies scored between 6 and 9 of a maximum 11 points (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). No studies were considered to have a high risk of bias.

PCV7

Fourteen included studies examined the effectiveness of PCV7 against vaccine-type IPD, with VE reported for a range of vaccine exposure definitions (Supplementary Table 6). VE point estimates for a single dose of PCV7 ranged from − 11 to 96%, with most studies reporting a VE above 50% (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 6). For several studies, confidence intervals were wide and included zero. Within individual studies, effectiveness typically increased with increasing number of doses (Supplementary Table 6). For example, a case–control study in Quebec, Canada reported VE of 77% (95% CI 46, 90), 93% (95% CI 82, 98) and 95% (95% CI 85, 98) for one, two, and ≥ three doses of PCV7, respectively [24]. A cohort study in Australia evaluated PCV7 effectiveness in medically at-risk children and reported a VE of 81.9% (95% CI – 38.6, 97.6) for the first dose and 88.1% (95% CI 2.1, 98.6) for the third dose [25]. A larger cohort study in Australia calculated VE during two time periods, and found that VE for a single dose of PCV7 was 87.1% (95% CI 65.5, 95.2) during the pre-universal vaccination period and 23.7% (95% CI − 190.3, 79.9) during the universal vaccination period [23]. Jayasinghe et al. conducted both a case–control and indirect cohort analysis using the same IPD cases and different controls and reported higher VE point estimates in the case–control analysis compared with the indirect cohort analysis for a single dose of PCV7 (58.7% and 12.2%, respectively), however results were similar for children who received multiple PCV7 doses (Supplementary Table 6).[26].

In studies that reported VE of a single dose of PCV7 by age at administration, VE was generally higher when children were vaccinated at 12 months of age or older. For example, a United States case–control study found that effectiveness of single dose of PCV7 was 73% (95% CI 43, 71) when given ≤ 7 months of age and 93% (95% CI 68, 98) when given between 12 and 23 months of age [27]. One study conducted in South Africa found no evidence of protection from a single dose of PCV7, however the first dose of PCV7 was recommended at 6 weeks of age compared with two months for the other included PCV7 studies [21]. A study in Quebec, Canada, found no protection of a single dose of PCV7 when evaluating patients with IPD aged 2–4 months, however a single dose of was protective in the full study population aged 2–59 months (73% VE (95% CI 30, 89) [28].

We conducted a meta-analysis of VE of a single dose of PCV7 stratified by age at vaccination. When administered at < 12 months of age or age unspecified, the pooled VE of single dose of PCV7 was 64.6 (95% CI 47.3, 76.2) (Fig. 2A). For a single dose of PCV7 given at ≥ 12 months of age, pooled VE was 81.6% (95% 72.5, 87.7) (Fig. 2B). Sensitivity analyses to exclude publications with overlapping IPD cases or with age at vaccination unspecified yielded similar results to the main analyses (Supplementary Fig. 2). When meta-analyses were stratified by study type, results were similar for PCV7 given ≥ 12 months of age (Supplementary Fig. 3). When PCV7 was given at < 12 months or age or age unspecified, the pooled VE point estimate was slightly higher for indirect cohort studies, possibly due to the low VE from the South African case–control study [21].

PCV10

Five included studies reported VE for PCV10 against vaccine-type IPD for a range of exposure definitions (Supplementary Table 7). VE point estimates for a single dose of PCV10 ranged from 37 to 95%, with wide 95% CIs for some studies (Supplementary Table 7, Fig. 3). Baqui et al. conducted a case–control study in Bangladesh using two different control groups and reported VE for the first dose of PCV10 using a dose–response analysis of 40.6% (95% CI − 2.41, 65.6) and 57.8% (95% CI 4.39, 81.4) for community and clinic controls, respectively [29]. One study from Brazil found that a single catch-up dose of PCV given at 12–23 months of age had a VE of 68.0% (95% CI 17.6, 87.6) [30]. Three studies evaluated VE of a single dose of PCV10 with age of administration unspecified (Fig. 3). VE point estimates were lower for the studies conducted in Pakistan and Bangladesh, where the first dose of PCV10 is administered at 6 weeks of age [31, 32]. Following meta-analysis, the pooled VE was 73.0% (95% CI − 29.4, 94.4) (Fig. 3).

PCV13

Thirteen included studies evaluated the effectiveness of PCV13 against vaccine-type IPD, and VE results for a single dose ranged from − 18 to 91.3%, with all but one study reporting a positive point estimate (Supplementary Table 8, Fig. 4). Some studies evaluated VE against IPD due to all PCV13 serotypes whilst other studies evaluated IPD caused by the 6 additional non-PCV7 serotypes included in PCV13. One cohort study from Australia evaluated VE for a single dose of PCV13 against IPD due to all PCV13 serotypes (86.6%, 95% CI 45.2, 96.7) as well as for the 6 non-PCV7 serotypes (82.2%, 95% CI 43.4, 94.4), yielding similar results [23]. A study from Quebec specifically focused on serotype 3 and reported 79% (95% CI − 25, 96) VE for a single dose of PCV13 against this serotype [33]. Within individual studies, effectiveness of PCV13 tended to increase with the number of doses, with some exceptions (Supplementary Table 8).

Effectiveness of a single dose of PCV13 was typically higher when given to children aged ≥ 12 months. When administered to children aged < 12 months or when age of vaccination was not specified, the pooled VE of a single dose of PCV13 was 56.8% (95% CI 44.1, 66.6) (Fig. 4A). The only study that reported a negative VE was conducted in South Africa, where the first dose of PCV13 was administered at 6 weeks of age [22]. Six studies evaluated the effectiveness of a single dose of PCV13 in preventing vaccine-type IPD during the first year of life, with a pooled VE of 56.1% (95% CI 36.1, 69.9) (Fig. 4B). For children who received a single dose of PCV13 at ≥ 12 months of age, the pooled VE was 79.2% (95% CI 65.5, 87.5) (Fig. 4C). Results from sensitivity analyses to exclude studies with duplicate IPD cases or those that only evaluated IPD due to the non-PCV7 PCV13 serotypes were similar to the main results (Supplementary Fig. 4). When meta-analyses were stratified by study type, results were similar for PCV13 given < 12 months or age or age unspecified (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Discussion

This is the first systematic literature review to compile evidence of effectiveness of a single dose of PCV against vaccine-type IPD in children. Overall, available data indicate that a single dose of PCV7, PCV10, and PCV13 given in the first year of life provides clinically meaningful protection against vaccine-type IPD, with pooled VE estimates more than 50%, with little variation by PCV product. Effectiveness of a single PCV dose increases with age, with pooled VE estimates of a single dose of PCV7 or PCV13 given ≥ 12 months around 80%. High levels of protection in toddlers following a single PCV dose are consistent with clinical trial data showing that immune responses to PCV are more robust in older children compared to infants [34]. Our results showing high VE of a single dose of PCV administered after 12 months of age are consistent with findings from a systematic literature review on the impact of PCV administration in older children that evaluated immunogenicity, disease incidence, and vaccine-type carriage [35]. Additionally, these children have been identified as key transmitters of pneumococci to other age groups [36]. While a single dose of PCV given before 12 months was effective in most studies, the timing of the first dose might be a factor, as the two studies that did not show evidence of protection were conducted in South Africa, where the first dose of PCV is recommended at 6 weeks of age, as opposed to 2 months in most of the other study settings. However, other differences, such as the high IPD burden in young infants in South Africa, might play a role [3].

Demonstration of partial protection following a single dose during infancy is important as IPD incidence is high during the first year of life, when infants may not be fully vaccinated [37]. Real-world evidence on the population-level effects of a reduced-dose PCV schedule is available from the United Kingdom (UK), which transitioned to a 1 + 1 schedule for PCV13 in 2020 based on sustained reduction of PCV13-type disease, strong indirect protection, evidence from clinical trials and modeling studies, and the UK government’s capacity to rapidly detect changes in disease rates [38]. The incidence of vaccine-type IPD in children aged < 1 year, who would have only been eligible for a single PCV13 dose, did not significantly increase, although these infants would have also benefited from indirect protection [38]. Although a single dose of PCV in the first year of life is only weakly immunogenic, results from our analysis indicate that meaningful, direct protection can still be expected. This finding, along with real-word data from the UK 1 + 1 schedule, is relevant to the interpretation of immunogenicity trials for next-generation PCVs, which tend to be less immunogenic than the preceding generation [39]. PCV20 includes 7 additional serotypes beyond those in PCV13, and while immunogenic, serotype-specific IgG responses for the shared 13 serotypes tended to be lower compared to PCV13, particularly after a two-dose primary series [8]. In a phase 3 clinical trial, serotype-specific IgG geometric mean concentrations were much higher in infants who received two doses of PCV20 compared to one dose of PCV13, although formal statistical comparisons were not conducted (see Korbal et al. [8], Supplemental Digital Content 8). As our review demonstrated effectiveness of a single dose of PCV13, these results indicate that immune responses induced by two infant doses of PCV20 will provide substantial direct protection against IPD prior to the booster dose in a 2 + 1 schedule, even if non-inferiority criteria for the respective serotypes were not met. Pooled VE estimates for a single dose of PCV13 were similar to PCV7, indicating that slight immunogenicity decrements that accompany the addition of serotypes to PCV formulations have not resulted in reduced protection to date. Postlicensure studies are needed to confirm the effectiveness of higher valency PCVs, including PCV20.

While complete vaccination according to recommended schedules is needed for optimal protection, evidence of effectiveness of a single dose of PCV has public health implications. Our results support the use of a single dose of PCVs in older children who missed their primary infant series or for whom vaccination history is unknown. Results also support the use of higher valent PCVs to complete a child’s vaccination series during transition of a national immunization program to a new PCV product. Our findings further support the use of catch-up campaigns that administer a single PCV dose to toddlers and older children to accelerate reductions in IPD [2, 40]. The WHO recommends administration of PCV according to age-appropriate schedules in humanitarian settings and health emergencies [2]. However, in some emergency settings multi-dose schedules may be difficult to implement. Findings from this review, along with real-world data collection and modeling approaches could be used to inform PCV strategies for humanitarian settings [41].

A strength of our study is the use of meta-analysis to synthesize evidence. Individual studies varied by setting, timing of administration of PCV doses, IPD serotype distribution, and design. VE confidence intervals for smaller studies were often wide and included zero, making interpretation difficult. Evaluation of VE for a single dose of PCV was not the primary analysis in any included study, and in settings with high PCV uptake, relatively few IPD cases would have occurred in children who only received one dose, thus reducing sample size for this exposure definition. Nevertheless, pooled VE estimates provided strong evidence of PCV effectiveness for a single dose, particularly for PCV7 and PCV13 due to the larger number of included studies. The robustness of the pooled VE estimates is supported by the fact that sensitivity analyses yielded similar results. While our primary analyses included both indirect cohort and case–control studies, sensitivity analyses conducted for each study type yielded broadly similar results.

Our review is not without limitations. Relatively few studies were conducted in LMIC settings or in children with risk conditions. Certain IPD risk factors, such as malnutrition or other infections, such as HIV and tuberculosis are important in LMIC settings and could potentially influence PCV effectiveness [42]. Fewer studies evaluated PCV10, leading to wide confidence intervals in the pooled VE estimate for this vaccine. Several studies did not specify age at vaccination when reporting VE for a single dose of PCV. We grouped these age-unspecified results with VE for a single dose given < 12 months of age, assuming that most children who received a single PCV dose outside of a catch-up campaign would be younger, incompletely vaccinated infants. A sensitivity analysis limited to studies that evaluated VE at < 12 months of age provided similar results. Lastly, we used fairly narrow criteria for our literature search for efficiency and searched English-language literature only. However, as we cross-checked our results against published systematic reviews that used broader search criteria, we believe that it is unlikely that we missed any eligible publications.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our review and meta-analyses provide evidence that a single dose of PCV13 or lower valency PCV provides protection against vaccine-type IPD in children, particularly when administered after 12 months of age. Our findings support vaccination strategies based on the use of a single toddler dose in transition schedules or catch-up programs. The potential use of single-dose PCV campaigns in humanitarian emergency settings warrants further investigation. Future studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of newer, higher valency PCVs in both fully vaccinated and incompletely vaccinated children.
