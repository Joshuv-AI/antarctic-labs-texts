Bailout Left Atrial Appendage Closure implantation following Left atrial appendage perforation—a case report
Authors: Hannes Alessandrini, Jan Per Wenzel, Charlotte Eitel, Darko Trajanoski, Roland Richard Tilz
Published: 2025

Clinical case

Catheter-based electrical isolation of the left atrial appendage (LAA) is an emerging strategy for patients with symptomatic atrial fibrillation (AF) that persists despite pulmonary vein isolation (PVI).1 Because electrically isolated LAAs carry an increased risk of thromboembolism, endovascular LAA occlusion (LAAO) has become an important adjunctive therapy.2

We report the case of a 76-year-old woman scheduled for elective LAAO following successful electrical isolation. Her medical history included persistent AF (EHRA class III) and hypothyroidism treated with thyroxine. After ultrasound-guided transseptal puncture, a steerable coronary sinus catheter (WEBSTER® CS Catheter) was introduced to verify isolation. Within minutes of catheter placement, the patient developed severe hypotension (50/30 mmHg), and the suspicion of pericardial tamponade was confirmed by contrast extravasation from the LAA and transoesophageal echocardiogram (Figure 1A and B). Emergency pericardiocentesis evacuated 400 mL of haemorrhagic effusion, which was autotransfused.

After stabilization, several management strategies were considered, including surgical repair or a conservative approach. As the perforation was most likely located at the apical portion of the LAA, the Heart Team decided to attempt interventional management first. Echocardiographic 2D sizing guided implantation of a 20 mm Watchman FLX device (Boston Scientific, USA). Final deployment was achieved after confirming all PASS criteria, and post-deployment imaging demonstrated complete hemostasis (Figure 1C and D). Protamine was administered, and the patient recovered uneventfully. She was discharged on day five. Due to a very small pericardial effusion, post-interventional anti-inflammatory therapy with ibuprofen and colchicine was prescribed for three weeks. During telephone follow-up, the patient reported no complaints, and outpatient TEE confirmed stable device position and complete closure.

LAA perforation is rare but potentially life-threatening during catheter-based interventions.3 In this case, iatrogenic rupture caused by diagnostic catheterization was successfully managed with immediate implantation of an LAA occluder, thereby avoiding surgery. The occluder’s semi-permeable membrane likely promoted thrombus formation and rapid hemostasis. The apical location of the perforation facilitated effective sealing, highlighting the potential of prompt device-based rescue in similar complications.
