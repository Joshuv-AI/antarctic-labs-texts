Immunotherapy with B28, an antibody to Aβ oligomers, potently decreases amyloid plaques, microgliosis, and memory decline in APP knock-in mice
Authors: Ting Yang, Yi Ran Xu, Shaomin Li, Jean-Pierre Bellier, Youqi Tao, Anna E. Francis, Andrew M. Stern, Shanxue Jin, Beth L. Ostaszewski, Trebor L. Lawton, Laurent Pradier, David Reczek, Cynthia A. Lemere, Dominic M. Walsh, Dennis J. Selkoe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYImmunotherapy against amyloid β-protein (Aβ) for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) has been widely approved. Breakthrough disease-modifying treatments such as lecanemab and donanemab are often followed by drugs with improved efficacy. By immunizing Trianni mice with aggregated synthetic Aβ, we obtained B28, a fully human antibody specifically selected for its neutralization of tau neuritic dystrophy induced by AD brain-derived oligomers. In a blinded trial in mutant human APPNL-G-F knock-in mice, weekly infusions of B28 for four months markedly reduced both amyloid plaques and Aβ oligomers, attenuated plaque-associated astrocytosis and microgliosis, preserved neurons, and lessened memory decline. Biochemical and histological analyses showed that B28 engaged both plaque-bound and diffusible Aβ, forming immune complexes and depleting free Aβ. In in vitro immunoassays, B28 had significantly higher affinity and captured more Aβ from AD brain extracts than did lecanemab. B28 is a potent, aggregate-preferring Aβ antibody that clears plaques and decreases gliosis, supporting its clinical development.

INTRODUCTION

On July 6, 2023, four decades after the discovery of the amyloid β-protein,1 the US Food and Drug Administration2 granted the first full regulatory approval to a disease-modifying treatment for Alzheimer’s disease (AD),2 lecanemab (Leqembi).3 The next year, a second amyloid-clearing antibody, donanemab (Kisunla),4 was approved by the FDA. When administered intravenously over 12–18 months, each antibody robustly clears amyloid plaques from the brain, accompanied by variable slowing of the rate of decline in cognition and function (activities of daily living). The principal side effect of such agents is called amyloid-related imaging abnormality (ARIA),5,6 observed by brain magnetic resonance imaging as focal areas of edema (ARIA-E) and/or microhemorrhages (ARIA-H), usually without accompanying neurological symptoms. Passive immunotherapy with either antibody is increasingly being offered to patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to AD or mild AD dementia. Lecanemab is currently approved in over 48 countries, and donanemab in over 13 countries.

Like most new disease-modifying treatments for previously untreatable diseases, these initial agents are likely to be followed by additional drugs that may prove to be more efficacious and/or safe. It is important to identify and characterize new anti-Aβ monoclonal antibodies as well as other parenteral and oral treatments for AD. In a multi-year collaboration between our laboratory at Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Sanofi Pharmaceuticals, we created several fully human monoclonal antibodies by immunizing Trianni mice7–9 (which are engineered to express human instead of mouse immunoglobulin genes) with synthetic human Aβ1–42 aggregates and screening the resultant hybridoma clones for two therapeutically desirable properties: (1) ability to prevent the tau-positive neuritic dystrophy of cultured human neurons induced by natural Aβ oligomers (oAβ) isolated from homogenates of AD cerebral cortex; and (2) ability to dose-dependently protect wild-type mouse hippocampal slices from the inhibition of synaptic plasticity (long term potentiation) by the human oligomers.10,11 These screens are also fully described in our issued patent (Google Patents: https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2021011673A2/en). Using the first of these two bioassays, we calculated an IC50 (inhibitory concentration-50%) for each of 5 particularly potent oAβ-neutralizing antibodies in human neuronal cultures, and the values ranged from 1.531 to 4.077 μg/mL. We recently reported one of these 5 new monoclonals, B24, which has the unusual property of being calcium-dependent: it only binds oAβ efficiently in the presence of calcium, so that calcium chelators such as EGTA prevent its binding.12 We mentioned in that report a second of our new monoclonals, B28, which has the highest potency among our 5 best antibodies (1.531 μg/mL; ~10.2 nM) in preventing human neuritotoxicity of endogenous oligomers isolated from AD brains.

Here, we have taken the next key step in therapeutic antibody development, testing B28 in vivo for its ability to both decrease human oAβ and clear amyloid plaques in the brains of a compelling mouse model of AD: the NL-G-F humanized mutant APP knock-in (KI) mouse that expresses familial AD-causing missense mutations in the mouse APP locus, thus avoiding transgenic overexpression and achieving endogenous regulation of the knocked-in human APP gene.13–15 To gauge the activity of B28 against human oAβ in these amyloid-depositing and progressively behaviorally impaired mice, we compared a murinized version of B28 (called mB28 herein) to both a mouse IgG2a isotype control monoclonal and another oAβ-preferring mouse monoclonal antibody, 71A1, also of IgG2a isotype. The 71A1 antibody was generated in wild-type (wt) mice against a cyclized form of two Aβ9–18 synthetic peptides hypothesized to mimic a dimeric conformation of the N-terminal region.16–18 We have shown that 71A1 neutralizes synaptotoxic human oAβ in electrophysiological assays and detects diffusible oAβ in both mouse and human brain tissue and human cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).17,18 Passive immunization of the APPNL-G-F KI mice beginning at age 4 months (after they begin developing plaques) with each of these 3 antibodies was conducted blindly. After 4 months of weekly intraperitoneal infusions, we blindly measured for each of the three antibodies: (1) brain oAβ levels using 3 different oligomer-specific ELISAs, (2) plaque binding, (3) plaque clearance, (4) plaque-associated astrocytosis and microgliosis, (5) behavioral impairment, and (6) neuronal loss.

The rationale for testing the B28 vs. 71A1 oAβ antibodies derives from their differing binding properties and thus hypothetical therapeutic potential. B28 is a plaque-binding oAβ antibody that binds a spectrum of Aβ aggregates, including large oligomers/protofibrils and mature amyloid fibrils in AD amyloid plaques. 71A1 preferentially binds a small subset of Aβ aggregates, including dimers and multimers of dimers, that are diffusible in aqueous brain extracts and human CSF and plasma.17,18 Both antibodies had previously been shown to neutralize oAβ-induced synaptotoxicity (LTP inhibition) by extracts of AD cortex, suggesting their potential for in vivo efficacy.12,17 We thus sought to compare two distinct oAβ antibodies in a blinded fashion in a single, well-defined, randomized cohort of mutant hAPP KI mice beginning early in amyloid plaque deposition.

RESULTS

Identifying monoclonal antibodies that neutralize diffusible neurotoxic oligomers of endogenous Aβ from AD brain

To select novel antibodies that potently inhibit the neurotoxicity of human oAβ, we used an all-human bioassay that we previously described as advantageous for identifying next-generation antibodies for AD.10,11 We isolated Aβ aggregates from clinicopathologically confirmed sporadic AD brains by aqueous diffusion of minced neocortical tissue bits, avoiding homogenization that releases Aβ from insoluble amyloid plaques.19 These “soaking extracts” were pipetted onto cultures of glutamatergic neocortical neurons derived from iPSCs of normal humans. The degree of tau-positive neuritotoxic injury was quantified by live-cell imaging on an automated IncuCyte microscope in a tissue culture incubator. Hybridoma supernatants were obtained by immunizing Trianni mice with aqueously diffusible aggregates of synthetic human Aβ1–42 peptide. We previously used this paradigm to quantify the relative protective potencies of six aggregate-preferring antibodies, including four clinical agents previously assessed in patients with AD [bapineuzumab, aducanumab, gantenerumab, BAN2401 (lecanemab precursor)] and two potent preclinical antibodies (SAR228810; 1C22) that served as standard comparators.11 We reported a blinded ranking of the inhibition of neuritotoxicity (IC50) in which all six antibodies conferred significant protection, with our 1C22 antibody10,20,21 nominally conferring the strongest protection.

In the current study, we screened our new hybridoma sups in identical fashion.10,11 After testing 31 clones reactive with synthetic Aβ, we selected the 5 that had the highest protection against human brain oAβ-induced tau-positive neuritic dystrophy; their IC50s in the human neuronal cultures ranged from 1.531 to 4.077 μg/mL (means of 3–5 independent blinded assays run on separate days). The antibodies were counter-screened by surface plasmon resonance against the self-aggregating neurodegenerative proteins, α-synuclein and superoxide dismutase-1 (SOD1), yielding no reactivity. They were similarly unreactive with synthetic Aβ monomers. Among these 5 best antibodies, the most potent was clone B28 (Figure S1). For a representative run, B28 exhibited an IC50 of 1.499 μg/mL, and across three independent runs, the mean IC50 was 1.531 ± 0.211 μg/mL (mean ± SEM; ~10.2 nM) (Figure S1D). This was comparable to 1C22, which had a mean IC50 of 1.677 ± 0.549 μg/mL across eight runs (Figure S1D), which in turn was the most potent Aβ antibody in our earlier comparison to the clinical Aβ antibodies BAN2401 (lecanemab) and aducanumab.11 B28 was further characterized using Octet (BLI) kinetic analysis for binding to synthetic Aβ42 monomers, Aβ40 monomers, and Aβ protofibrils (PFs) (Figures S3D–S3G). These data indicate that B28 preferentially binds oligomeric/protofibrillar Aβ species, with minimal binding to monomeric Aβ40 or Aβ42, based on its oligomer selectivity and favorable Octet kinetic profile. In addition, B28 fully prevented the impairment of LTP in wt mouse hippocampal slices by AD brain extracts, representing an orthogonal bioassay we ran alongside the human neuritotoxicity bioassay.12 After large-scale production of the murinized recombinant B28 antibody, we reevaluated this mB28 and the 71A1 in this assay. Both antibodies fully prevented LTP impairment by AD extracts in wt mouse hippocampal slices at 3.01 and 2.12 μg/mL, respectively (Figure S2). These validated batches were subsequently used for the in vivo treatment of the APPNL-G-F mice (Figure 1).

Characterizing the oligomer-preferring antibodies used in the mouse immunotherapy trial

To assess conformational selectivity for oAβ, each of the two active antibodies (mB28 and 71A1) to be tested in our mouse trial was used as capture antibody in a sandwich ELISA paired with the widely characterized Aβ N-terminal antibody 3D6 as detector. We compared the capacities of these two antibodies to those of lecanemab as a benchmark oAβ capture antibody (also paired with 3D6). These 3 sandwich ELISAs were first applied to synthetic Aβ species: amyloid-derived diffusible ligands (ADDLs) (larger oligomeric aggregates of wt Aβ1–42)22; Aβ40 (S26C)2 (disulfide crosslinked dimers and small oligomers of a substituted serine26-to-cysteine Aβ1–40 peptide)17,18; and Aβ1–40 monomers. All 3 antibodies barely recognized the monomers (Figures S3A–S3C, black), supporting their designation as strongly oAβ preferring antibodies. All 3 antibodies recognized ADDLs (Figures S3A–S3C, green) in the rank order lecanemab ≅ 71A1 > mB28. In contrast, only 71A1 could recognize the Aβ40 (S26C)2 (Figures S3A–S3C, red), indicating its selective preference for dimers/small oligomers (Figure S3C).

However, when we assayed human oAβ isolated as above by aqueous diffusion from minced cortical bits (“soaking extracts”)19 of 5 sporadic AD brains, mB28 captured more of this endogenous diffusible oAβ from each brain than did lecanemab, while 71A1 captured very small amounts (Figure S4A). The greater amounts of oAβ captured by mB28 vs. lecanemab varied considerably among the 5 brains, suggesting variable degrees of immunoreactive oligomers across AD cases. Next, we fractionated the soaking extracts from 3 AD brains by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Sephacryl S-500 HR 16/60 column and quantified all SEC fractions with the 3 oAβ assays (Figures S4C–S4E). mB28 detected substantially more oAβ than lecanemab in the high- and mid-molecular weight range SEC fractions of all 3 AD brains, whereas 71A1 recovered much lower amounts. Quantitative integration of these SEC profiles revealed that mB28 captured 1.6-fold, 2.4-fold, and 2.2-fold more oAβ than lecanemab in these 3 AD cases, respectively. Collectively, the findings in Figures S3 and S4 demonstrate distinct selectivity profiles among the three oAβ antibodies as well as substantial differences in the measurable amounts of immunoreactive oAβ among AD brains.

Because these sandwich ELISAs only tested binding at a single capture antibody concentration, we compared B28 and lecanemab as to their equilibrium binding affinities and maximum total oAβ binding from AD brain homogenates using a titration IP-ELISA, as done previously.23 With this technique, the binding capacity of the 3 capture antibodies is measured independently of the solvent exposure of the 3D6 detector epitope. At saturating (maximum) antibody concentrations, B28 and lecanemab bound similar quantities of Aβ across six sporadic AD brains tested, suggesting the two antibodies have the capacity to bind similar amounts of Aβ aggregates at high concentrations. However, the equilibrium binding constant (KD) of B28 was 3.3-fold lower than lecanemab (5.50 nM vs. 18.3 nM), suggesting a 3.3-fold greater affinity for human brain Aβ (Figure S4B). We conclude that B28 appears to bind to a similar population of Aβ aggregates as lecanemab but does so with significantly greater affinity. These findings are supported by the observation that the antibodies capture a similar distribution of size profiles by SEC but with a substantially greater amount of human brain oAβ bound by B28 at a specific antibody concentration (Figures S4C–S4E).

Anti-Aβ passive immunization with mB28 reduces brain Aβ oligomer levels by >90% in APPNL-G-F knock-in mice

Ninety-six APPNL-G-F KI mice from a single cohort bred in-house were randomized into 3 equal groups and injected intraperitoneally weekly for 19 weeks beginning at age 4 mo with one of the 3 monoclonal antibodies: mB28, 71A1, or an isotype control mouse IgG2a (Figure 1). The APPNL-G-F KI line first develops amyloid plaques and bioactive oligomers of human Aβ1–42 around age 3–4 months.15,24–28 Experimenters were blinded throughout the study to antibody identities. Thus, antibody vials and the mouse recipients (via ear tags) were color coded by an investigator not involved in data acquisition or analysis: green for mB28, blue for 71A1, and red for mIgG2a (also used in all figures herein). Mice were randomly assigned one of the three ear-tag colors, and treatments were administered accordingly. Treatment groups were unblinded only after data collection. The trial design and immunization schedule are shown in Figure 1.

After 19 weeks of immunization, Aβ species in APPNL-G-F mouse brain homogenates prepared in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) were quantified on four ELISAs: three oligomer-selective assays [71A1/3D617,18; 1C22/3D620,29; and lecanemab/3D630] and an Aβx-42 monomer assay (post-GuHCl-denaturation of oligomers).31,32 Notably, free Aβx-42 monomers (measured without GuHCl denaturation of oligomers) were below the lower limit of detection of that assay, consistent with the very low soluble monomer levels reported in the APPNL-G-F mice due to the strong oligomer-promoting intraAβ mutations.25 Consistent with the notion that homogenization of brain tissue in aqueous buffer produces an extract that contains both small amounts of freely diffusible forms of Aβ and more abundant forms of insoluble Aβ derived from plaques,19,33 the lecanemab/3D6 assay, which best detects large assemblies (ADDLs) of synthetic Aβ (Figure S3B), detected the most oAβ in brain homogenates of control-treated mice (Figure 2C). Conversely, the 71A1/3D6 assay, which preferentially detects smaller synthetic Aβ aggregates (Figure S3C) detected the least oAβ in the homogenates of control-treated mice (Figure 2A).

mB28 treatment markedly reduced brain oAβ levels by all 3 oAβ ELISAs (Figures 2A–2C; Table S1A). In the 71A1/3D6 ELISA (Figure 2A), mB28 treatment lowered oligomer levels by ~70% relative to the mIgG group (p < 0.0001), whereas 71A1 treatment produced no significant change.

Using the 1C22/3D6 ELISA (Figure 2B), mB28 treatment reduced oAβ by a striking ~93% compared to the mIgG placebo (p < 0.0001), with no detectable reduction by 71A1 treatment. A similar ~93% reduction by mB28 vs. placebo was observed using the lecanemab/3D6 assay (Figure 2C; p < 0.0001), again with no effect of 71A1 treatment. These results underscore the robust ability of mB28 to bind and clear human oAβ in vivo.

Denaturing samples with GuHCl and then measuring Aβx-42 (Figure 2D; Table S1A) revealed that mB28 treatment reduced levels by 88% compared to mIgG treatment. As with oligomers measured using the 3 oAβ assays, denatured Aβx-42 levels in the 71A1 treatment group did not differ significantly from the mIgG group (Figure 2D), despite 71A1 binding to small and larger synthetic oligomers (Figure S3C) and the 71A1/3D6 assay readily detecting oligomers in the brains of APPNL-G-F mice (Figure 2A).

Notwithstanding the possibility that the 3 oAβ assays may capture somewhat different forms of oAβ species, when the levels of Aβx-42 (measured after denaturation with GuHCl) were plotted against the levels measured with each of the oAβ assays, there was a remarkable consistency (collinearity) of the effects of the antibodies used for immunotherapy (Figure 2E). Collectively, our findings demonstrate that prolonged mB28 immunotherapy markedly reduces human oligomeric Aβ (as measured using 4 distinct assays) in the APPNL-G-F mouse brain, whereas 71A1 showed no significant treatment effects in this study.

We also assessed sex effects. No sex differences were observed across the three cohorts, including behavioral analyses (data not shown). Consistent results were obtained in ELISA (Figure 2) and behavioral tests (Figure 7), which included all treated mice. Therefore, males and females were used in balanced numbers in subsequent experiments but not analyzed separately by sex.

Anti-Aβ passive immunization with mB28 markedly reduces Aβ plaque burden in APPNL-G-F mice

To determine whether the large reductions in brain oAβ levels were associated with decreased amyloid plaque pathology, we performed immunofluorescence histochemistry on the APPNL-G-F mouse brains after 19 weeks of antibody treatment. Coronal sections were co-labeled with a standard rabbit monoclonal antibody to the Aβ N-terminal region, D54D2 (Table S2),12,34,35 and DAPI. Quantitative image analysis was used to blindly assess plaque burden in hippocampal and neocortical regions.

mB28-treated mice exhibited a marked reduction in Aβ plaque pathology relative to both the mIgG and 71A1 treated groups (Figures 3A–3F). Blinded quantification revealed significantly lower plaque area fraction (~70% reduction by mB28 vs. placebo, Figure 3G) and overall fluorescence intensity (~62% reduction by mB28 vs. placebo, Figure 3H) in the mB28 group (p < 0.01 for all comparisons). No significant changes were observed in the 71A1-treated group vs. mIgG controls. Representative hemispheres from three additional mice per treatment group are shown in Figure S5.

Representative immunofluorescent images illustrate the widespread clearance effect of mB28, with visibly fewer and less intensely labeled plaques in both hippocampus (Figure S6G) and neocortex (Figure S6J). In contrast, mIgG and 71A1 treatment groups showed abundant, dense Aβ deposits with similar regional distribution. Further analysis revealed that although mB28 treatment substantially reduced plaque burden, the size distribution of remaining plaques was unchanged (Figures S6A–S6E), indicating that mB28 decreases all sizes of plaques.

Together with the oAβ ELISA results of aqueous homogenates of the same APPNL-G-F mice, it is apparent that mB28 immunotherapy markedly reduces both diffusible oligomeric Aβ and deposited amyloid plaques. In contrast, 71A1, despite binding small Aβ assemblies in vitro (Figure S3) and in aqueous extracts of APPNL-G-F brain (Figure 2A) and human CSF,18 failed to significantly alter plaque burden in vivo (Figures 3A–3G and 3H). These data highlight the therapeutic potential of B28 to robustly clear existing Aβ plaques and oAβ and limit further deposition in actively depositing mice during 4 months of treatment.

Anti-oAβ passive immunization with mB28 promotes antibody binding to plaques while reducing free Aβ in brain tissue

To assess antibody engagement in vivo, we examined antibody-Aβ interactions using both immunohistochemistry and ELISA. Dual-label immunofluorescence demonstrated strong co-localization of a fluorescently labeled anti-mouse IgG2a as a marker of antibody presence on Aβ plaques in mB28-treated brains, with yellow signals indicating overlapping antibody-antigen complexes (Figure 4A). As simultaneous negative controls for this mB28 co-labeling, both 71A1-and mIgG-treated mice exhibited minimal or no detectable co-localization of mIgG2a with Aβ deposits. Thorough PBS perfusion of all mice prior to tissue collection minimized the possibility that the complexes detected in situ were derived from circulating antibodies.

Quantification of free and bound Aβ pools was performed by separating protein G-captured immune complexes from unbound fractions in aqueous brain homogenates.21 mB28-treated mice showed a markedly (>15-fold) increase in levels of antibody-bound Aβx-42 (202.9 ± 166.6 pg/mg) compared with the mIgG-treated mice (13.1 ± 6.3 pg/mg) (p < 0.0001) (Figure 4B). In accord, free (unbound) Aβx-42 levels were markedly (>80%) reduced in the mB28-treated mice (2,119.9 ± 2,106.7 pg/mg) compared to treatment with mIgG (10,690.0 ± 7,515.4 pg/mg; p < 0.0001) (Figure 4C). In contrast, 71A1 treatment did not significantly alter either antibody-bound Aβ (23.1 ± 10.8 pg/mg) or the free pool of Aβ (9,790.9 ± 6,612.6 pg/mg) in brain homogenates compared to mIgG treatment (Figures 4B and 4C).

Together, these findings provide compelling evidence that mB28 directly engages cerebral Aβ in vivo, forming immune complexes that correlate with marked depletion of free Aβ, whereas 71A1, as a simultaneously tested anti-Aβ control, shows negligible engagement.

Anti-oAβ passive immunization with mB28 reduces astrocytic and microglial activation in APPNL-G-F mice

Given the robust reduction of cerebral Aβ by mB28, we next investigated whether prolonged antibody treatment modulated glial reactivity, as astrocytic and microglial activation are tightly linked to amyloid pathology and are established biomarkers of neuroinflammation in AD. Elevated immunoreactivity for GFAP (astrocytes) and Iba1 or CD68 (microglia) in human AD and in mouse models correlates with plaque pathology as well as CSF and plasma biomarkers of AD progression,36–39 making these important readouts of therapeutic efficacy.

mB28-treated APPNL-G-F mice exhibited substantial reductions in GFAP-reactive astrocytes compared with both the 71A1- and mIgG-treated groups (Figure 5A). Astrocytic burden, quantified by GFAP-positive area and signal intensity, was substantially reduced in mB28-treated brains, whereas 71A1 treatment produced no measurable improvement relative to the mIgG control (Figure 5B; Table S1B). Most residual GFAP labeling was associated with amyloid plaques, indicating that decreases in astrocytic burden primarily reflect reduced amyloid load. Consistent with its lack of effect on Aβ burden, 71A1 did not significantly alter this astrocytic measure compared to mIgG.

Microglial responses were evaluated using both Iba1 (Figure 5C) and CD68 (Figure 5E) immunostaining, the latter marking activated phagocytic microglia.40,41 mB28-treated mice exhibited markedly reduced microglial burden across all parameters. Both Iba1 (Figure 5D) and CD68 (Figure 5F) measurements demonstrated significantly lower microglial area and staining intensity following mB28 administration, with 71A1 again indistinguishable from mIgG across all parameters (Table S1B). As with astrocytes, residual activated microglia remained localized to plaques, consistent with the interpretation that reductions in gliosis were largely secondary to the decrease in plaque burden. Single-channel images for all gliosis markers are provided in Figures S7A–S7C for reference.

Together, these findings demonstrate that chronic mB28 treatment markedly decreases certain oAβ and plaque deposition and thereby lessens astrocytic and microglial reactivity, two hallmark neuroinflammatory responses in AD. The strong correlations reported between Aβ pathology and glial activation in both preclinical models and human biomarker studies suggest that the ability of mB28 to reduce gliosis may reflect its broader potential to modify AD-associated inflammatory cascades. In contrast, 71A1, despite binding certain synthetic and natural oAβ,18 failed to reduce glial activation in vivo.

Prolonged anti-Aβ passive immunization with mB28 is associated with increased neuronal integrity in APPNL-G-F mice compared with both mIgG and 71A1 treatment groups

Findings on neuronal loss in APPNL-G-F mice are mixed. Tang et al. reported that loss varies by age and region and correlates more with amyloid-β oligomers than with plaques.42 To assess whether our anti-Aβ antibody treatment influenced neuronal preservation, we examined NeuN immunoreactivity of neurons in cortical (Figure 6A) and hippocampal (Figure 6B) regions. After 4 months of mB28 treatment, APPNL-G-F mice retained twice as much NeuN-positive neuronal labeling across both regions than mice randomized to 71A1 or mIgG, with improvements observed on both raw area and total signal intensity measures (Figures 6C and 6D; Table S1B). These results suggest that prolonged mB28 treatment is associated with improved maintenance of NeuN immunoreactivity relative to mIgG, whereas 71A1 fails to prevent the neuronal loss that occurs in the placebo-treated APP KI mice. We included the lateral ventricle signal measurement in Figure S7D, which is similarly very low (negligible) for all three antibodies. All calculations of immunofluorescence were performed after subtracting the lateral ventricle background signal.

Brain homogenates of APPNL-G-F mice passively immunized with mB28 no longer impair synaptic plasticity

To assess the effects of antibody treatment on synaptic plasticity, we recorded LTP in the primary visual cortex. The visual cortex is more sensitive than the hippocampal CA1 region to soluble oAβ43 and provides a smaller, more homogeneous brain volume for reliable cortical recordings. Consistent with prior evidence that soluble Aβ species isolated from brains of patients with AD or APP model mice potently suppress cortical synaptic plasticity,43–45 the exposure of wt mouse visual cortex slices to brain homogenates from untreated 12 mo APPNL-G-F mice markedly impaired LTP after theta-burst stimulation (TBS) (111.9 ± 5.6%, n = 6, purple), whereas age-matched wt brain homogenates supported robust potentiation (151.7 ± 7.5%, n = 6, black) (Figure 7A). These results demonstrate that visual cortex LTP is sensitive to the low nanomolar concentrations of oAβ present in APPNL-G-F brain homogenates.

To determine whether passive immunization can alter this Aβ-dependent synaptic impairment, homogenates were prepared from mice treated with mB28, 71A1, or mIgG, and pooled homogenates of 3 brains from each treatment group were applied to wt mouse visual cortex slices, followed by TBS (Figure 7B). Homogenates from mB28-treated mice, shown above to have markedly reduced brain oAβ burden, did not suppress LTP and instead supported robust potentiation (138.8 ± 4.5% of baseline fEPSP amplitudes, n = 9; green). In contrast, homogenates from 71A1- or mIgG-treated mice permitted only 117.5 ± 5.1% (n = 9; red) and 117.6 ± 3.5% (n = 7; blue) potentiation, respectively. Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant treatment effect (p < 0.01), indicating that only mB28 rescued the oAβ-dependent LTP deficit.

Together, these findings suggest that mB28 treatment can allow LTP in the visual cortex by lowering bioactive diffusible Aβ species. Given the early vulnerability of visual association circuits in AD43,46,47 and the high sensitivity of glutamatergic synapses to diffusible oAβ,45,48 the prevention of impaired plasticity by mB28 provides mechanistic evidence linking oAβ reduction to preserved synaptic function.

Prolonged mB28 treatment lessens memory decline in APPNL-G-F mice

Given the beneficial effects of mB28 on amyloid burden, glial activation, neuron number, and synaptic plasticity, we asked whether these neuropathological benefits were associated with behavioral benefits in hippocampal-dependent learning and memory (Figure 7). Behavioral testing in the Morris water maze was conducted before antibody treatment (baseline), to ensure balanced randomization of the 96 mice, and again beginning at 16 weeks of chronic dosing. The second testing was conducted over 3 consecutive weeks, with each mouse receiving its weekly injection 24 h before behavioral assessment to maintain circulating antibody levels (Figure 1).

In the Morris water maze recall paradigm, mice were trained before and after antibody treatment (Figure 7C). At baseline, training lasted 6 days, whereas after 16-week treatment, the training period was shortened to 4 days to minimize potential confounds from long-term memory retention. Probe testing was performed on the last day of training with the platform removed. For each mouse, the change in total time(s) spent in the target quadrant (where the hidden platform had been located during training) from baseline to post-treatment (Δ time(s) = post - pre) was calculated as an index of spatial memory function, where a greater positive Δ time indicates improved recall of the platform location. mB28-treated mice exhibited a highly significant longer target-quadrant occupancy (13.5 ± 27.7 s) compared with mIgG controls (−17.3 ± 28.8 s; p < 0.0001), reflecting relative preservation in spatial memory. 71A1 (7.5 ± 32.1 s) also provided a small but significant benefit relative to mIgG (p < 0.01).

In the reversal water maze test (Figure 7B), the hidden platform was relocated to a new quadrant, followed by 6 days of training and a probe trial. Time spent in the new platform quadrant relative to baseline (Δ time) was again calculated. Only mB28-treated mice demonstrated a significant improvement (5.7 ± 34.3 s; p < 0.05) vs. mIgG control (−12.1 ± 23.6 s). While the 71A1 (3.3 ± 29.2 s) and mB28 (5.7 ± 34.3 s) groups performed similarly, the improvement seen in the 71A1 group was not significantly different from the mIgG control (−12.1 ± 23.6 s; p = 0.0808).

These data indicate that mB28 treatment preserved spatial learning flexibility and attenuated memory decline on both platform recall and platform reversal paradigms, whereas 71A1 treatment only showed a significant effect on the recall paradigm, with a limited, non-significant effect in the reversal measure.

DISCUSSION

Two monoclonal antibodies targeting distinct epitopes in human amyloid β-protein have achieved full regulatory approval in the US and many other countries and have thereby launched the therapeutic era of AD. Their pioneering advances to date open up opportunities for many additional treatments that could prove to be more efficacious. During the Phase 3 registrational studies of lecanemab and donanemab, we initiated a project to create new monoclonal Aβ antibodies designed to have several novel properties that could be therapeutically advantageous.

First, candidate antibodies were generated in Trianni mice, which produce fully human immunoglobulin variable domains that can be directly reformatted as human IgG1. B28 is the first amyloid-clearing monoclonal antibody derived from this platform. This strategy enables in vivo discovery of antibodies with fully human binding regions, eliminating the need for subsequent humanization of murine antibodies. In contrast, clinically approved antibodies such as lecanemab and donanemab are humanized IgG1 antibodies derived from murine precursors and retain murine CDR sequences. Although humanization lowers immunogenicity, in clinical studies, most patients treated with donanemab developed treatment-emergent anti-drug antibodies (ADAs), while lecanemab appears to show lower immunogenicity. By contrast, B28, as the first potential AD therapeutic antibody from Trianni mice, should have an advantage by providing fully human variable domains from the start. Although we used a re-engineered mouse version of B28 for our mouse trial as required, the human B28 therapeutic candidate itself carries human variable genes and can be produced as human IgG1, supporting its clinical applicability with far lower risk of developing ADAs.

Second, the therapeutic potential of B28 is rooted in a unique “all-human” selection paradigm that prioritizes AD-relevant cytopathology over simple recombinant protein binding. We established a first-of-its-kind screening pipeline utilizing a highly sensitive, quantitative bioassay of tau-positive neuritic dystrophy in human iPSC-derived neurons exposed to soluble oligomers isolated directly from the cerebral cortex of sporadic patients with AD.10,11 This human-centric approach ensured the identification of clones capable of neutralizing the most pathologically relevant, diffusible oligomeric species derived from patients’ brains, a critical distinction from traditional screening methods relying on synthetic peptide aggregates. The functional properties of B28 were further demonstrated through an orthogonal electrophysiological assay, where it successfully rescued impaired hippocampal synaptic plasticity (LTP) induced by AD brain-derived oligomers.

To enhance specificity, B28 was counter-screened against Aβ monomers and unrelated aggregated proteins α-synuclein and SOD1, supporting selective recognition of aggregated Aβ species. Quantitative comparison of bioactivity in the human tau neurite assay prioritized B28 for in vivo testing, and its selection was subsequently supported by robust efficacy in a human APP KI model of AD-type β-amyloidosis. Collectively, this integrated discovery framework, progressing from fully human antibody production to AD patient-derived pathogenic proteins (Aβ and tau) and functional validation, provides a translationally oriented strategy for identifying next-generation Aβ immunotherapies10,11 (Google Patents: https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2021011673A2/en).

Based on this selection process, we chose our most potent antibody (B28) for the critical next step: testing its efficacy in a compelling animal model of AD-type β-amyloidosis. The results of our randomized, controlled, blinded trial in mutant human APP KI mice show that compared to an oAβ antibody, which sensitively binds small, diffusible oligomers in human biological fluids (CSF and plasma) and to an inactive isotype-matched control, B28 markedly lowers oAβ in aqueous homogenates of brain by >90%. The reduction in oligomer levels by B28 was documented using four distinct immunoassays (Figures 2A–2D) and was accompanied by a robust reduction in amyloid plaque burden (Figure 3). Moreover, B28 reduced microgliosis and astrocytosis by removing plaques and preserved neuron number compared to the two controls. Additionally, brain homogenates of B28-treated mice did not impair cortical LTP whereas those of 71A1- and mIgG-treated mice did, suggesting the ability of B28 to preserve synaptic plasticity (Figures 7A and 7B). This is in accord with B28’s improvement in spatial memory in two forms of the Morris water maze test (Figures 7C and 7D). The cognitive benefits likely reflect in part the preservation of hippocampal synaptic plasticity through binding and clearance of oAβ both in their aqueously diffusible and plaque-bound states, as amyloid plaques have been shown to be composed of insolubilized oAβ.44

We used the APPNL-G-F KI mouse model14 to avoid the confounding effects of heterologous transgenic APP overexpression. This KI mouse line recapitulates progressive β-amyloid pathology and behavioral impairment while retaining the endogenous regulation of the APP gene and not altering APP expression level, making it a pathophysiologically relevant model of AD.15,49–52

While the highly significant outcomes of B28 treatment in our blinded trial occurred without obvious evidence of adverse clinical effects, including on body weight, food intake, and observed behavior, the risk of ARIA remains a critical concern for plaque-targeting immunotherapies. To explore this initially, we conducted a preliminary ARIA assessment in aged (18-month-old) mice, comparing a high dose of mB28 (50 mg/kg) to a lower dose (15 mg/kg) of 3D6, the murine precursor of the ARIA-inducing clinical antibody bapineuzumab.53–55 After 8 weeks of treatment, no microhemorrhage events were observed in the mB28 mice (0/3), whereas 2 of 3 mice treated with 3D6 exhibited pronounced microhemorrhages in the cerebellum and some in the cerebral leptomeninges (Figure S9). Although limited by the availability of aged APPNL-G-F KI mice, these pilot data provide initial evidence that B28 may have a low propensity to induce ARIA-H-like pathology relative to an existing benchmark (3D6), even at concentrations far above those required for robust therapeutic benefits. To settle this question, we are conducting large-scale studies in the 5xE4 line (5xFAD × ApoE4/4 mice) reported to develop ARIA-H,56,57 where we will perform a head-to-head comparison of mB28 with the murine version of lecanemab (mAb158). Importantly, the future clinical development of B28 in humans is likely to involve its conjugation with an endothelial-targeting moiety such as anti-transferrin receptor or other fragment, e.g., the “Brain shuttle” system currently being tested with gantenerumab (as trontinemab58) in Phase 3 clinical trials.

To examine the possibility that mB28’s properties may provide advantages over lecanemab, an Aβ antibody in clinical use, we quantified their respective binding affinities and capacities on oAβ from sporadic AD brains. B28 consistently showed higher oAβ binding capacity than lecanemab when tested side-by-side on aqueous soaking extracts of five neuropathologically confirmed sporadic AD brains (Figure S4A). Further, SEC fractionation of these extracts followed by pulldown at equal antibody concentrations demonstrated substantially greater capture of high- and mid-molecular weight oligomers by B28 (Figures S4C–S4E). Moreover, binding affinity estimates (KD) indicated a 3.3-fold greater affinity (lower KD) of B28 than lecanemab (Figure S4B), highlighting its stronger binding of patient-derived neurotoxic assemblies. In APPNL-G-F mouse brains, these biochemical advantages translated into robust reductions in both amyloid plaque burden and associated diffusible oligomers. While these biochemical differences might not turn out to be meaningful at high supra-therapeutic doses applied in clinical settings, the superior binding kinetics of B28 over lecanemab, if maintained without elevating the risk of ARIA, strongly support its planned clinical evaluation.

A finding of our study is the therapeutic divergence between mB28 and 71A1. Although 71A1 exhibits high in vitro affinity for small synthetic Aβ dimers/oligomers (Figure S3C) and effectively neutralizes some AD brain-derived diffusible oAβ17 (Figure S2B), these properties did not translate into therapeutic benefits in our APPNL-G-F mouse trial. To rule out poor bioavailability as a factor, we conducted a pilot pharmacokinetic study (Figure S10), which showed that both antibodies displayed typical mouse IgG kinetics, with plasma half-lives exceeding two weeks (Figures S10A and S10B), and comparable brain penetration (~0.1%) (Figures S10C and S10D). These data suggest that 71A1’s lack of in vivo efficacy is not attributable to rapid clearance or inadequate brain exposure. Several factors likely contribute to this discrepancy. B28 and 71A1 were both designed to target oligomeric Aβ species but differ in their selectivity. B28 displays broader binding to pathological Aβ assemblies, recognizing both soluble oligomers and insoluble deposited aggregates in unfixed AD brain sections (Figure S11), whereas 71A1 preferentially recognizes a small subset of diffusible oligomers in AD brain tissue (Figures S4C–S4E). Immunohistochemical analyses confirmed that B28 labeled parenchymal plaques, colocalizing extensively with lecanemab in unfixed tissue sections, consistent with the recognition of native pathological structures; its labeling was reduced by tissue fixation (Figure S11). ELISA data further supported that B28’s binding in human brain appears to be conformation-dependent and optimal for native diffusible aggregates rather than denatured epitopes (Figures S3 and S4). Together, these findings suggest that B28’s broader Aβ recognition may underlie its superior efficacy over 71A1 in reducing Aβ burden and mitigating amyloid-related cytopathology.

71A1’s limited efficacy in vivo may also reflect its peripheral and central sequestration by small diffusible oligomers in plasma, CSF, and brain interstitial fluid, thereby contributing to its demonstrated lack of plaque clearance. Supporting this hypothesis, 71A1 readily forms immune complexes with oAβ present in CSF and plasma.18,59 Our pulldown assays further demonstrated that 71A1 bound far fewer diffusible Aβ aggregates from brain parenchyma than did lecanemab and especially B28 (Figure S4), consistent with its lack of plaque targeting (Figure 4). An additional explanation could be that 71A1 recognizes conformational epitopes exposed only in small, diffusible oligomers but largely concealed within fibrillar plaques, limiting its engagement with the predominant pathological Aβ species in brain parenchyma. Accordingly, no co-localization with plaques was observed at endpoint (Figure 4A), nor was plaque engagement detected 72 h after an acute intraperitoneal administration (data not shown).

Mechanistically, B28’s therapeutic benefit appears to derive from its ability to facilitate Fc-mediated microglial clearance of plaques and also to engage diffusible oAβ that are in equilibrium with them. This promotes the removal of both aqueously diffusible and insoluble Aβ pools. Immunohistochemical analyses revealed a marked reduction in Iba1- and CD68-positive activated microglia and GFAP-positive astrocytes that were clustered around Aβ plaques in control-treated animals. The attenuation of glial activation parallels effects reported for Aβ aggregate-preferring antibodies such as lecanemab, donanemab, and SAR228810.30,60,61

Limitations of this study

Although direct in vitro quantification of B28 vs. lecanemab in the same assay revealed higher affinity to and more binding of human oAβ by B28 in AD aqueous extracts, this difference may not translate into better oligomer- and plaque-clearing efficacy in humans with AD. A new trial directly comparing the two antibodies in APPNL-G-F KI mice is currently underway. Our preliminary search for ARIA-H using B28 vs. 3D6 treatment in APPNL-G-F KI mice included only 3 mice per treatment arm (due to low mouse availability), with 3D6 inducing hemosiderin deposits (ARIA-H) in 2 of 3 mice, while none of the 3 mice given 3.3-fold higher doses of B28 had any hemosiderin. This result needs replication in more mice and an additional mouse model, a study now underway. While we plan to place B28 into a brain shuttle vector as an attractive way to move this antibody forward therapeutically, it is unknown until tested in mice and humans that this will have any advantages over trontinemab or other derivatized Aβ antibodies.

In conclusion, B28 represents a next-generation, oligomer-preferring, Aβ monoclonal antibody that robustly reduces both soluble and insoluble Aβ, mitigates gliosis, preserves neuronal number, and lessens cognitive decline in a human APP KI mouse model of AD pathobiology. Its avid in vitro binding to Aβ conformations in AD brain extracts appears to make it more efficient at interacting with brain oAβ than lecanemab. It also lacks the epitope restriction of donanemab, which targets the small minority (<3%) of post-translationally modified fibrillar amyloid in AD brain that begins with pyroglutamate-3.60 Together, these findings indicate the translational potential of B28 and support its further preclinical and clinical development, particularly if combined with endothelial targeting technologies designed to improve delivery to the capillary bed and lessen CAA-related ARIA.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Animals

APPNL-G-F knock-in mice on a C57BL/6J background were a generous gift of Dr. Takaomi Saido (RIKEN Center for Brain Science, Tokyo, Japan). Homozygous APPNL-G-F mice were monogamously mated to produce offspring until sufficient pups were bred (n = 96) and aged to 4 months. Then, these mice were randomly assigned to one of three groups with roughly equal numbers of each sex, in order to generate the 3 balanced cohorts used in the study. All mice were genotyped by Transnetyx Inc., using ear snips. All mouse procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at BWH (IACUC protocol # 2016N000342).

Human participates

Human brain samples were obtained with informed consent under protocols approved by the Institutional Review Board at Brigham and Women’s Hospital. Brain donor characteristics and pathologic information is described in Table S3. Data on gender, race, ancestry, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status were not available for the deidentified brain tissue samples. Low sample size (n = 11) precluded subgroup analysis by diagnosis.

Cell line

Induced human neurons (iNs) were produced from iPSCs (YZ1 line) via Neurogenin-2 (Ngn2) induction.64,65

METHOD DETAILS

Antibody administration

Mice were randomly assigned to one of three treatment groups: mB28, 71A1, or an isotype control (mouse IgG2a). All three antibodies are recombinant versions derived from their original hybridoma sequences. To ensure full blinding throughout, each antibody was color-coded (mB28 = green, 71A1 = blue, mIgG = red), and mouse ear tags matched these designations. All baseline testing, treatment, and data collection were performed under blinded conditions, with unblinding only after completion of all analyses. Antibodies were dissolved in sterile PBS at 300 μL of 1 mg/mL (pH 7.4, 0.15 M NaCl; Gibco), aliquoted, and stored at −80°C until use. Mice received weekly intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections (15 mg/kg) for 19 consecutive weeks. Behavioral testing began at 16 weeks of dosing and continued for three weeks (weeks 16–19), with the final injections administered 24 h before testing to maintain circulating antibody levels. Following the completion of behavioral testing, all mice were euthanized, i.e., less than 36 h after their last injection of antibody, and brains were collected for histological and biochemical analyses.

Harvest and preparation of mouse brain tissue

After 19 weeks of treatment, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused transcardially with 0.9% NaCl (0.15 M). Brains were then removed and bisected along the midline. One hemisphere was immersion-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) prepared in 0.15 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for histological analysis, while the other hemisphere was snap-frozen for subsequent biochemical assays.

Preparation of mouse brain homogenates

One hemisphere of each mouse brain was homogenized in TBS buffer (0.15 M NaCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl), pH 7.4 as described previously.20,31,44

Fixed tissue and cryosectioning

Fixed hemispheres were equilibrated in 15% and then 30% sucrose (in PBS) overnight, embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T., and frozen at −80°C. Frozen blocks were placed in the cryostat chamber set to −19°C for 2 h to soften tissue prior to sectioning. Each brain was coronally sectioned on a Leica cryostat cm1850 (Wetzlar, Germany) at 25 μm thickness at v19°C and stored in an ethylene glycol-based cryoprotectant at −20°C.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Free-floating sections were permeabilized and blocked in 5% normal serum (matching the host species of the secondary antibody) in PBS (Gibco pH 7.4, 0.15M NaCl) plus 0.25% Triton X-100 (PBS-X) for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies (Table S2). After washing (3× PBS-X), sections were incubated for 1 h with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (BioLegend; 1:2500 in PBS-X + 1% serum), washed again, and counterstained with DAPI (abcam 10mM - 1:5000) for 10 min. Sections were mounted on MAS-GP Adhesion Microscope Slides (Matsunami) and cover-slipped using ProLong Gold Antifade (Invitrogen) and stored at 4°C before imaging.

Fluorescence imaging and quantification

Fluorescence of stained brain slices was imaged on the Andor Dragonfly 600 Spinning Disk Confocal Microscope or tile-scanned with the Zeiss AxioScan7. Image processing, analysis, and signal quantification was conducted with ImageJ (FIJI). Fluorescence intensity, plaque area, and perimeter were quantified using standardized thresholds applied uniformly across all samples. Three-dimensional models of z-stacked images were constructed and rendered with the Imaris (Oxford Instruments) software. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 10.

MesoScale discovery (MSD) homebrew Aβx-42 ELISA

Total monomeric Aβx-42 was quantified per previous reports using a custom electrochemiluminescence assay on the MSD platform using the Aβ mid-region antibody 266 as capture and biotinylated 21F12 (anti-Aβ42) as detector.32

Oligomeric Aβ quantification (SMCxPRO single-Molecule immunoassay)

oAβ levels reactive with 71A1, 1C22, or lecanemab as capture and 3D6 as detector were each quantified using a homebrew assay on the SMCxPRO platform as described previously.18

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and immunodepletion (ID) of antibody-Aβ complexes

Dynabeads Protein G magnetic beads (Invitrogen, 10003D) were washed three times with TBS (25 mM Tris, 0.15 M NaCl) and resuspended for immunodepletion. Mouse brain homogenates were diluted 4X with TBS and incubated with the beads at a ratio of 1.5 mg beads per 250 μL of undiluted homogenate. The mixture was nutated overnight at 4°C to immunoprecipitate mIgG-Aβ complexes. Following incubation, the beads were magnetically separated, and the supernatant was collected and stored at −80°C. The beads were then washed 3 times with TBS-T (10 mM sodium phosphate, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 7.5) and eluted in 50 μL of 7 M guanidine hydrochloride (GuHCl) overnight at 4°C. The input, post-IP supernatant, and IP eluate were treated overnight with GuHCl as indicated to depolymerize oAβ into monomeric species. Aβx-42 levels were then measured using the homebrew MSD 266/21F12 monomer assay.

LTP induction in acute visual cortex slices

Acute coronal slices containing primary visual cortex were prepared from adult wild-type mice and maintained in continuously oxygenated aCSF following established intracortical LTP protocols.43,44 Slices recovered for ≥1 h before transfer to a submerged recording chamber perfused with aCSF at 30°C–32°C. Field EPSPs (fEPSPs) were recorded following stimulation of local intracortical afferents with a bipolar electrode. After establishing a stable ≥20 min baseline, LTP was induced using a theta-burst stimulation (TBS) protocol consisting of bursts of four pulses at 100 Hz delivered at 5 Hz, repeated for one 10-burst train. fEPSPs were recorded for ≥60 min after TBS. All recordings, stimulation procedures, and analyses were performed blind to experimental condition. Homogenate test samples were bath-applied for 20 min prior to TBS.

Electrophysiological recordings

Experiments were conducted as previously described.43,44 Adult mice (3–4 months old) were euthanized with isoflurane. Brains were rapidly removed and placed in ice-cold, oxygenated sucrose-based aCSF containing (in mM): 206 sucrose, 2 KCl, 2 MgSO4, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 1 CaCl2, 1 MgCl2, 26 NaHCO3, and 10 D-glucose (pH 7.4, 315 mOsm). Transverse hippocampal slices (350 μm) were prepared using a vibroslicer. Slices were transferred to standard aCSF containing (in mM): 124 NaCl, 2 KCl, 2 MgSO4, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 2.5 CaCl2, 26 NaHCO3, and 10 D-glucose (pH 7.4, 310 mOsm) and allowed to recover for at least 90 min. A single slice was then placed in the recording chamber, continuously perfused with aCSF equilibrated with 95% O2/5% CO2, and incubated for 20 min before stimulation at room temperature (~24°C).

For recordings in the primary visual cortex (V1), field potentials were recorded from layer 2/3, identified under differential interference contrast optics. Baseline responses (40–60% of maximal amplitude) were recorded every 20 s for at least 20 min. Recordings were excluded if baseline fEPSPs were unstable or if the maximal reproducible amplitude was <1 mV. LTP in V1 was induced using three theta-burst stimulation (TBS) episodes delivered at 10 s intervals, each consisting of four pulses at 100 Hz repeated 10 times at 5 Hz.62 fEPSP amplitudes were normalized to the mean baseline amplitude during the final 10 min before TBS. Unless otherwise noted, LTP values reflect responses measured 60 min after stimulation. Field potentials were amplified (100×) with an Axon Instruments 200B amplifier, digitized using a Digidata 1322A, sampled at 10 kHz, and filtered at 2 kHz. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using pClamp 9.2 and Clampfit 9.2. Data were analyzed in GraphPad Prism, with group differences assessed by two-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Morris water maze (MWM) behavioral experiments

Behavioral experiments were conducted at two time points: prior to the first antibody injection (age 4 months) and after the final administration of antibody (age 8 months). Paired analyses compared pre- and post-treatment performance within each subject, followed by between-group comparisons. Testing and data acquisition were performed using EthoVision XT 17 (Noldus, Wageningen, the Netherlands).

Spatial reference memory was assessed using a circular pool (150 cm diameter) filled with water maintained at 26°C–29°C. Non-toxic white paint was added to create opaque water that concealed the submerged escape platform. The pool was divided virtually into four quadrants (NW, NE, SW, SE), and a hidden circular platform (10 cm diameter) was positioned 1 cm below the surface in a fixed quadrant. Spatial cues around the testing room remained constant throughout.

Baseline MWM testing (~4 months)

Baseline MWM testing comprised three stages: [1] cued training (day 1), [2] acquisition training (days 2–6), and [3] a probe trial (day 7). Three such trials were conducted each day.

During cued training, the platform was marked with a visible flag and moved to a different quadrant for each of three 90 s trials. Mice were released into the tank from the quadrant opposite to the platform, guided to it if not found within 90 s, and required to stay there for ≥10 s. Animals were towel-dried between trials to prevent hypothermia, and the inter-trial interval was ~3 h.

For acquisition training, the flag was removed, and the platform remained in a fixed location. Each mouse completed two 90 s trials per day for five days under the same release conditions. On day 7, the probe trial was performed with the platform removed, allowing mice to swim freely for 90 s. Swim paths, velocity, and time in each quadrant were recorded using EthoVision XT 17 (Noldus, Wageningen, the Netherlands).

Post-treatment MWM testing: Recall and reversal (~8 months)

Approximately 24 h after the final antibody injection (at age ~8 mo), mice underwent two additional MWM paradigms to assess memory retention and cognitive flexibility.

The recall MWM was similar to baseline testing but condensed to four days: mice were re-trained with 3 cued trials on day 1, followed by a shorter acquisition period with 3 non-cued trials per day on days 2, 3, and 4. On the fifth day, 2 more non-cued trials are performed, followed by the probe trial at the end of the day. The purposely shortened training period post-treatment is meant to assess the recall abilities of the treated mice and how long- and short-term spatial memory is affected by treatment.

After the recall test is completed, the reversal test is performed by changing the platform to the quadrant opposite to the original position to examine the mice’s ability to acquire new spatial memories. Mice are trained with 3 cued tests on day 1, followed by an acquisition period of 3 non-cued trials per day on days 2, 3, 4, and 5. On the sixth day, 2 more non-cued trials are performed, followed by the probe trial after an interval of 3hrs. Mice are lowered into the arena opposite to the current position of the escape platform near the edge of the pool.

Preparation of AD brain extracts, iPSC-derived neurons, and live-cell neuritotoxicity assays used for oAβ antibody screening.

AD brain extracts

Aqueous extracts from AD) temporal neocortex were prepared as described.63 Briefly, 20 g of gray matter were Dounce-homogenized in 5 vol ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid base buffer (aCSF-B: 124 mM NaCl, 2.8 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 26 mM NaHCO3, pH 7.4) containing protease inhibitors (5 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, leupeptin 5 μg/mL, aprotinin 5 μg/mL, pepstatin A 2 μg/mL, pefabloc 120 μg/mL, NaF 5 mM). Homogenates were centrifuged at 200,000 × g for 110 min at 4°C (SW41 Ti rotor). The upper 80% of each supernatant was collected and dialyzed extensively against fresh aCSF-B for 72 h (three buffer changes). Dialyzed extracts were aliquoted and used for all neurotoxicity says.

Generation of iPSC-derived human neurons

Induced human neurons (iNs) were produced from iPSCs (YZ1 line) via Neurogenin-2 (Ngn2) induction.64,65 iPSCs were transduced with pTet-O-NGN2-puro, Tet-O-FUW-eGFP, and FudeltaGW-rtTA vectors, induced with doxycycline (2 μg/mL) on day 1, and selected with puromycin (10 μg/mL) on day 2. On day 4, cells were replated at 5,000 per well on Matrigel-coated 96-well plates and maintained in Neurobasal medium supplemented with B27, Glutamax, dextrose, MEM-NEAA, and BDNF/CNTF/GDNF (10 ng/mL each). Neurons reached maximal neurite complexity by day 14 and were stable until assayed beginning on day 21.

Live-cell neurite integrity assays

Day 21 iNs (see above) were imaged on an IncuCyte system to obtain 6 h of baseline neurite length and branchpoint measurements (images every 2 h). AD brain extracts (see above) were buffer exchanged into Neurobasal/B27/Glutamax and added at 50 μL per well together with 50 μL fresh medium. Recombinant B28 was co-applied to wells at concentrations of 0.75–6 μg/mL. Live-cell imaging continued every 2 h for ≥72 h. Neurite lengths and branch points were quantified using the IncuCyte Neural Track module (brightness segmentation; adjustment 1.2; cell-body filter ≥500 μm2; neurite width 2 μm; sensitivity 0.4) and normalized to each well’s baseline (time 0). Percent protection was calculated from final neurite length (mean of last three time points) relative to AD extract alone and immunodepleted AD extract as controls; EC50 values were determined when curve fitting permitted.

Hippocampal electrophysiological recordings

350 μm coronal hippocampal brain slices from wt C57BL/6J mice were prepared in ice-cold, oxygenated aCSF and allowed to recover for at least 1 h before recordings. Aqueously diffusible extracts of Aβ prepared from AD brain were floated over the samples with and without the addition of mB28 or 71A1 in a chamber submerged in aCSF. Extracellular field potentials were recorded in the CA1 region according to standard procedures and baseline responses were collected at 50 to 60% of the maximum amplitude for 10 min prior to the beginning of the experiment to ensure response stability. LTP was recorded for 50 min after induction by two consecutive trains (1 s) of stimuli at 100 Hz that are 20 s apart.

Soaking extract preparation and size exclusion chromatography (SEC)

Aqueous soaking extracts of human AD and control brain tissues (Table S3). were prepared as described.32 For SEC fractionation, a Sephacryl S-500 HR 16/60 column (Cytiva) was equilibrated with 120 mL of TBS (25 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.2) on an ÄKTA system. A total of 3.5 mL of soaking extract was loaded onto the column using a 5 mL loop and flushed with 20 mL TBS. Elution fractions were collected in 1 mL increments beginning after the first 20 mL of column volume. Collected fractions were immediately analyzed using the 71A1/3D6, lecanemab/3D6, and mB28/3D6 oAβ immunoassays to quantify oligomeric Aβ species.

Measurement of equilibrium binding affinity to AD brain extracts

Equilibrium binding affinity was evaluated similarly to our previous study (Butler et al. 2025 bioRxiv). Postmortem human brain tissue was thawed on ice. Parenchymal tissue was dissected, ensuring the inclusion of less than 10% white matter, then chopped into 0.05 mm increments using a McIlwain tissue chopper. Tissue was resuspended in 5 volumes of TBS (Bio-Rad, 1706435) containing Aprotinin, Leupeptin, and Pepstatin A sourced from Sigma Aldrich, Pefabloc (Sigma, 76307), and NaF (Sigma, S7920). Parenchymal tissue was mechanically homogenized in a 30 mL glass Dounce homogenizer using an IKA Microstar 7.5 Digital stirrer set to 800 rpm. Crude homogenates were centrifuged at 25,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C in a Type 70.1 Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter). The top 90% of the supernatant (TBS-soluble fraction) was collected and pooled into fresh 50 mL LoBind tubes, and subsequently aliquoted into 1.5mL LoBind tubes stored at −80C. Extracts were thawed on ice and diluted 5-fold in immunoprecipitation (IP) sample buffer containing 1X TBS, 0.05% Tween 20, and 1mM calcium chloride. Immunoprecipitation proceeded in 96-well plates in 150 μL volumes using lecanemab and B28 serially diluted from 100 μg/mL with 20 μL washed Protein G beads at 800 rpm at 4°C for approximately 18 h. Beads were washed three times in IP sample buffer then denatured with 50 μL 5 M guanidine hydrochloride at 800 rpm at 4°C overnight. The denatured eluates were diluted 20-fold for 266/21F12 x-42 MSD ELISA assay.

Hemosiderin staining

PFA-fixed cryostat sections were incubated in filtered 2% potassium hexacyano-ferrate (II) trihydrate (Thermo) in 2% HCl for 20 min and washed thrice in MilliQ water for 5 min each. Slides were mounted and cover slipped and hemosiderin deposits were observed with brightfield microscopy.

Tail nick blood collection and plasma PK

Whole blood was collected via tail bleeding by puncturing the lateral tail vein at the distal end of the tail using a 22G needle at 1, 3, 7, 10, 14 and 17 days after an acute dose of 71A1 or mB28 administered by i.p. injection (15 mg/kg). Bleeding is stopped by applying manual pressure at the site of collection followed by application of styptic powder. Serum is prepared by centrifuging coagulated whole blood immediately after collection at 2000 x g for 20 min 1 μL of plasma is loaded with 5% βMe and electrophoresed on a NuPAGE Bis-Tris 4–12% gel and probed with an anti-MIgG2a heavy-chain reactive antibody (IRDye 680LT #926-68051) and scanned on the Odyssey CLx Imager.

Alexa 647 mAb conjugate administration and detection

mB28 and 71A1 (mIgG2a) were conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647 according to protocol using Erenna Detection Antibody labeling Kit (EMD Millipore #03-0076-02). Conjugated antibodies were administered via i.p. injections at 15 mg/kg in sterile PBS. Animals were euthanized 72 h post-injection and transcardially perfused with saline. Brain tissue was harvested, and the hemispheres were split along the sagittal plane. One hemisphere is fixed by 4% PFA while the other is prepared into aqueous TBS homogenate. Serum was prepared from whole blood collected via cardiac puncture immediately before perfusion. Fluorescence response is measured directly from the serum and TBS brain homogenate using the SMCxPRo (EMD Millipore) plate reader and relative response units are corrected against a MIgG2a standard curve to calculate the protein concentration.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Between-group comparisons were evaluated using unpaired t-tests and nonparametric Mann-Whitney tests, and multiple-group comparisons were assessed using one-way or two-way ANOVA with Kruskal-Wallis tests, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), and p <0.0001 (****).
