Modification of reduced-sodium carp myofibrillar protein by a combined strategy with ultrasound and MgCl2 treatment: physicochemical, structural and functional properties
Authors: Shengming Zhao, Zimeng Wang, Ligong Zhai, Yanyan Zhao, Mingyuan Huang, Haibo Zheng, Guoyuan Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explored the modification by which high-intensity ultrasound (HIU, 20 kHz, 300 W) combined with magnesium chloride (MgCl2, 0–40 mM) influences the physicochemical, structural and functional attributes of reduced-sodium carp myofibrillar protein (MP). The zeta potential (11.39 mV), solubility (80.86 %), surface hydrophobicity (68.72 μg), emulsification activity (20.73 m2/g) and UV intensity significantly elevated after treatment with HIU and 40 mM MgCl2. Moreover, this combined treatment declined the particle size, total sulfhydryl content, and intrinsic fluorescence intensity of MP. FT-IR analysis revealed that the synergistic processing modified the secondary structure of MP, characterized by an increase in β-sheet and random coil content, while declining the α-helix and β-turn content. SDS-PAGE results revealed that myosin heavy- chain and actin-specific bands increased the band intensity. Additionally, the combined treatment resulted in a lower shear stress and G’ value, and the alteration of the particle size and secondary structure was evidenced by rheological variation. The results revealed that the combination of HIU and MgCl2 treatment is promising as an effective method for application in the reduced-sodium aquatic product processing industry.Keywords: Carp myofibrillar protein, High-intensity ultrasound, MgCl2, Physical properties, Structure

Introduction

Common carp (Cyprinus carpio) is extensively distributed in China and ranks among the six major freshwater fish species in the country [1]. Common carp surimi products such as fish cakes and sausages, are well-received by consumers due to their high contents of protein and 5′-ribonucleotides, tender texture and distinctive flavor [2,3]. Sodium chloride (NaCl, 0.47–0.86 M) is typically used in surimi products to increase ionic strength. The added salt promotes structural unfolding and solubility of myofibrillar protein (MP), thereby improving their techno-functional properties [4]. Nevertheless, meat products account for 20 %-30 % of salt intake in the human daily diet, and chronic consumption of a high-sodium diet (Na) can be detrimental to health, particularly by increasing the incidence of hypertension and cardiovascular diseases [5]. Hence, consumer preference for Na-mitigated, health-oriented meat products is increasing. However, lowering the salt content can decrease ionic strength, and an ionic strength of < 0.3 M can weaken the techno-functional attributes of proteins, resulting in quality attrition in processed meat products. Consequently, non-thermal physical techniques and fractional substitution of NaCl with non-Na salts (KCl, CaCl2, MgCl2) have been utilized to address this primary issue.

MgCl2, a magnesium supplement (Mg), is essential for the physiological functions of the brain, heart, and skeletal muscles in the human body [6]. MgCl2, a representative divalent salt with a salty flavor akin to NaCl, has been extensively used as a substitute for NaCl in recent years, significantly improving the techno-functional attributes of low-salt MP and the quality characteristics of processed muscle commodities [5,7,8]. However, protein type, Mg ion concentration, and pH conditions can influence net- charge of proteins, thereby modulating the functional attributes of MP. Moreover, the overuse of MgCl2 can cause MP aggregation due to the overbearing electrostatic effects of divalent ions, thereby deteriorating the functional properties of the proteins [9]. Accordingly, determining the appropriate MgCl2 level remains a challenge in meat product processing.

Ultrasonic technology, an innovative cold-processing and environmentally friendly strategy for contemporary food systems, is crucial for enhancing meat tenderization, meat product curing, food modification, microbial inactivation, eco-efficient extraction of bioactive compounds, and food thawing and freezing [10,11,12]. Low-frequency high-intensity ultrasound (HIU, intensity power 10–100 W/cm2, 20–100 kHz) has been employed to modify MP via micro-scale shock waves and microjets generated by transient cavitation implosions [13,14,15]. The cavitation effect induced by ultrasound can alter the structural properties of MP under certain conditions, primarily by disrupting the hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding of protein molecules, causing protein conformational unfolding. After conformational disassembly, the active sulfhydryl (SH) groups and sequestered hydrophobic domains are exposed to the exterior [16]. Consequently, various physicochemical characteristics of MP, including solubility, emulsifying ability, gelling properties, and water retention, have been significantly improved. Moreover, ultrasonic cavitation produces transient bubbles, and their collapse generates numerous reactive free radicals. Radicals induce protein oxidation, thereby modifying the functional properties of the proteins [17,18]. Ultrasonic processing is now widely used to study the conformational and techno-functional properties of MP [16,19,20]. However, no reports have been published on the variation in conformational and techno-functional attributes of reduced-sodium carp MP influenced by HIU combined with MgCl2. Accordingly, the primary focus of this study was to investigate how HIU (20 kHz, 300 W) and MgCl2 (0–40 mM) influence the physicochemical properties, protein tertiary structure, rheological, and emulsifying characteristics of reduced-sodium carp MP (0.3 M NaCl). This research provides a theoretical foundation for developing novel health-oriented and high-quality reduced-sodium surimi products.

Materials and methods

Materials

Live common carps (Cyprinus carpio) (approximately 1300 g ± 90 g and 40 cm length) were procured from a local aquaculture vendor (Xinxiang, China). MgCl2 was provided by Dean Chemical Co., Ltd (Tianjin, China).

Extraction of MP

MP was extracted from the dorsal muscle of common carp using the procedure described by Sun et al. [1]. The harvested MP suspensions were stored at 4 °C until further analysis (within 2 d). The MP suspensions were adjusted to 50 mg/mL with PIPES buffer (15 mM, pH 6.25, 0.3 M NaCl) containing various concentrations of MgCl2, (0, 10, 20, 30, and 40 mM).

HIU treatment

The MP suspensions containing MgCl2 (0, 10, 20, 30, and 40 mM) were subjected to HIU processing (20 kHz, 300 W) for 15 min employing a focused-probe ultrasound generator (JY 92-IIN, Scientz Co., Ltd., China).

Particle size distribution and zeta potential

The particle size distribution and zeta potential of MP suspensions (0.1 mg/mL) were analyzed by a nanoparticle size analyzer (Zeta-sizer Nano-ZS 90, Malvern, UK) at 25 °C [21].

Solubility

The solubility was quantified using a protocol of Zhao, Liu, et al. [4]. The MP samples (5 mg/mL) were centrifuged at 4 °C and 10,000×g for 30 min, and the biuret assay was used to quantify the protein concentration in the supernatant. The MP supernatant (1 mL) was added Biuret reagent (4.0 mL), and the mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 30 min. Intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence

The intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence spectrum of MP suspensions (0.1 mg/mL) was assessed employing a spectrofluorometer. The parameters were fixed at λ_ex 295 nm, λ_em scan 300–400 nm, 600 V voltage, and 5 nm slit width [13].

Ultraviolet (UV) spectroscopy

The UV spectra of MP suspensions (0.5 mg/mL) were evaluated using a UV–Vis analyzer (TU-1900, Persee, Beijing, China). The parameters were fixed at scan range of 200–400 nm, resolution of 1 nm, and slit width of 5 nm. Origin 9.0 was employed to calculate the second derivative (d2A/d2λ) of the UV spectra.

SDS-PAGE

Diluted MP suspensions (1 mL) were heated to 100 °C for 5 min. Afterward, 15 μL of diluted suspensions and the standard protein marker in equal amounts were loaded into the SDS-PAGE with a gradient of 4 %–20 %. SDS-PAGE was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 for 1 h., before destaining in fresh destaining solution for 12 h.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

The freeze-dried carp MP was finely pulverized with potassium bromide (KBr, 1:100; w/w) and compressed into translucent pellets for FT-IR analysis (TENSOR 27, Bruker, Bremen, Germany). Spectra were collected from 4000 to 400 cm−1 (64 scans, 4 cm−1 resolution) using a KBr background for single-channel correction. The secondary structure compositions of carp MP were quantified by OMNIC 8.0 and Peakfit 4.12 software [48].

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Freeze-dried carp MP (10 mg) was placed in an aluminum crucible, and its thermal characteristics were evaluated using a DSC apparatus (TA Q100, TA Instruments, New Castle, USA). The testing parameters were configured as follows: the initial temperature was set at 20°C, and then it was increased to 100°C at a rate of 5°C/min. The entire testing process was conducted under a nitrogen atmosphere for protection [23].

Rheological analysis

The rheological characteristics of MP suspensions were assessed using a dynamic rheometer (HAAKE MARS III, Germany) fitted with a P35 TiL probe, featuring a 1 mm plate gap. The shear stress of the MP suspensions (60 mg/mL) was recorded at shear rates of 10–1000 s at 25 °C. The storage modulus (G' value) of the dynamic frequency scanning sweep was determined under the following parameters: frequency scanning range, 0–10 Hz; strain, 1 %; temperature, 20 °C [24].

Emulsifying properties

The emulsification capacity of MP was evaluated by referring to the protocol of Lan et al. [16]. Briefly, 6 mL of MP (10 mg/mL) was blended with 1.5 mL of soybean oil and homogenized using a homogenizer (T25, IKA, Germany) at 10,000 × rpm for 2 min. After dispersing 50 µL of the samples in 5 mL of 1 mg/mL SDS solution, the absorbance was measured at 500 nm. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 23.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Inter-group significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA with Duncan’s post-hoc test (P < 0.05). Each variable was recorded in triplicate at a minimum, and each measurement included four levels.

Results and discussion

Particle size distribution and zeta potential

The functional attributes of proteins are governed by their particle size, which indicates the extent of protein aggregation and accompanying structural alterations. The influence of HIU and MgCl2 on the particle size distribution of the reduced-sodium carp MP is presented in Fig. 1A. As the amount of MgCl2 increased, the primary peak of the particle size distribution of reduced-sodium carp MP in the non-HIU treated groups shifted towards a smaller size region. This may be due to the high electronegativity of divalent Mg ions, which can exert electrostatic interactions with protein molecules and facilitate the dissolution and more uniform distribution of MP, decreasing the particle size by altering the surface charge density and inter-particle repulsion of MP [8]. On the other hand, the hydration repulsion effect between proteins could be due to the interaction of Mg2+ on the MP surface, preventing MP aggregation and declining particle size [13]. Moreover, the primary peak of the particle-size distribution of HIU-treated, reduced-sodium carp MP shifted left to a greater extent than that of the non-HIU group as more MgCl2 was added. This shift was presumably due to the disaggregation of MP aggregates by the shear forces generated during ultrasonic processing, which contributed to the reduced particle size of the low-sodium carp MP. Li, Dai, Chen, Hao, Wang, & Mao [25] reported analogous results, indicating that HIU treatment remarkably declined the average particle size of shrimp MP. Additionally, ultrasonic cavitation action did not affect the surface charge of proteins, however, it broke the MP aggregates, prompting the generation of new surfaces and smaller protein particles, which is a reason for the decreased particle size [26].

Effects of HIU combined with MgCl2 treatment on particle size (A), zeta potential (B), solubility (C), and surface hydrophobicity (D) of reduced-sodium carp MP. a-j indicates significant differences among the groups (P < 0.05).

The surface charge of MP can be characterized by zeta potential, which is a vital marker for stabilizing protein solutions. Fig. 1B depicts the effect of HIU and MgCl2 on the zeta potential of the reduced-sodium carp MP. Every instance of the MP sample exhibited a negative potential value, implying that the MP surface featured a preponderance of negatively charged amino acids over positively charged amino acids. The absolute zeta potential value of the reduced-sodium carp MP gradually increased with increasing amounts of MgCl2. No significant difference was obtained between 0 and 10 mM MgCl2 addition. Chloride ions can bind to lysine, histidine, and arginine residues on the protein surface, amplifying electrostatic repulsion among MPs [27]. Moreover, Mg2+ impacts the chemical potential of the non-polar portion exposed in macromolecular unwinding or depolymerization processes [28]. The HIU treated group showed a significantly higher potential absolute value of reduced-sodium carp MP than the non-HIU-treated group at similar MgCl2 concentrations, and a peak value (11.39 mV) was observed with the addition of 40 mM MgCl2. This is possibly because the HIU treatment disassembles protein aggregates, increasing the surface area, thereby increasing the surface negative charges. Accordingly, the repulsive electrostatic interactions between MP particles are amplified, further hindering aggregation and elevating dispersion stability [29]. Zhang et al. [22] found that the MP colloidal systems with high absolute potential were more stable, while those with low potential were prone to aggregation or flocculation. Accordingly, HIU combined with MgCl2 treatment improved the stability of reduced-sodium carp MP suspensions.

Solubility

The impact of ultrasound and MgCl2 on the solubility of reduced-sodium carp MP is depicted in Fig. 1C. The solubility of non-HIU treated groups increased (P < 0.05) with an increase in MgCl2, reaching a peak of 73.54 % at a MgCl2 concentration of 40 mM. This could be explained by the elevated divalent cations, which could interface with the negative charges of the uncoiling MP to generate salt bridges. The ion pair remains solvent-separated rather than forming an insoluble contact pair because the divalent charge of Mg2+ is delocalized over the hydration shell. The net result is a soluble “protein − Mg2+- water” cluster that is highly polar and remains dispersed. This salt formation prevents MP aggregation and increases the MP solubility [5]. Chang, Hultin, & Dagher [30] reported analogous results, demonstrating that MgCl2 induced a shift in the isoelectric point of myosin of chicken breast, augmenting electrostatic interactions between proteins and causing an increase in protein solubility. The solubility of HIU treated groups with a the similar MgCl2 addition was distinctly higher (P < 0.05) than that of non-HIU treated groups, implying that HIU treatment further promoted the solubility of reduced-sodium carp MP with MgCl2 addition. The probable explanation is that the “cavitation effect” arising from ultrasound, generating a series of physical actions, such as shear forces, turbulence, and high-speed fluid microjets, resulting in the fragmentation of protein molecules, the expansion of specific surface area, and the boosted interactions between protein and water molecules. Another possible reason for the increased solubility of reduced-sodium carp MP is that the reactive oxygen species induced by HIU rapidly oxidize solvent-exposed thiols on myosin, driving the formation of intermolecular disulfide bridges that yield soluble myosin oligomers. These oligomers sterically hinder the anisotropic assembly of filamentous myosin, thereby markedly increasing the aqueous solubility and colloidal stability of MP [11]. Ultrasonic treatment significantly improved the solubility of sea bass MP, the results were in line with those reported by Lan et al. [16].

Surface hydrophobicity

Hydrophobic groups characterize the tertiary structure of proteins. The binding of BPB to MP can be used to assess changes in surface hydrophobicity [31]. Fig. 1D depicts that with the increase in MgCl2 in non-HIU treated groups, the surface hydrophobicity of reduced-sodium carp MP rose significantly (P < 0.05), peaking at 63.51 μg when MgCl2 was added at 40 mM, indicating that MgCl2 addition promoted the emergence of hydrophobic amino acid residues in MP. The elevated surface hydrophobicity of MP due to MgCl2 addition may be because that Mg2+, a divalent bivalent cation, promotes the unfolding of protein molecules and can develop salt bridges with the side chain-COOH of proteins, exposing the hydrophobic groups and active SH groups embedded within the protein to the surface, thereby increasing surface hydrophobicity [32,33]. Moreover, Mg2+ lowers the free energy needed for the transition of nonpolar amino acids into aqueous environments, thereby diminishing intramolecular hydrophobic interactions and facilitating protein uncoiling. Shi et al. [31] found that the fractional substitution of NaCl with MgCl2 enhanced the surface hydrophobicity of low-sodium pork MP. The surface hydrophobicity of the HIU treated groups was notably (P < 0.05) higher than that of non-HIU treated groups with increasing MgCl2 addition. This was presumably due to the destruction of hydrophobic interactions within the MPs by shear force and high-speed fluid microjets caused by HIU, triggering the uncoiling of MPs. The hydrophobic amino acid residues hidden within the MP were further displayed to the exterior, allowing more BPB to bind with the previously concealed hydrophobic residues, thereby increasing surface hydrophobicity [16]. Mao et al. [28] reported similar findings, revealing that ultrasound and KCl treatment significantly induced alterations in protein structure and resulted in protein segregation, facilitating the exposure of more hydrophobic groups.

Total SH content

The SH is crucial for stabilizing and maintaining of protein conformation and is a key factor influencing the functional attributes of proteins [16]. Fig. 2A illustrates that the total SH content of non-HIU treated groups significantly declined as the addition of MgCl2 increased (P < 0.05). Compared to the control group (62.12 μmol/g), the total SH content (52.43 %) of the non-HIU treated group decreased by 15.60 %, possible because MgCl2 facilitates the unraveling of MP structure and the unveiling of reactive SH groups. These exposed groups undergo disulfide rearrangement reactions or oxidation, promoting the formation of disulfide bonds and leading to a decline in total SH content [31]. The partial substitution of NaCl with MgCl2 evidently decreased the SH content and elevated the disulfide bond content of pork MP by enhancing the accessibility of amino acids with hydrocarbon side chains buried inside the internal structure of MP[8]. Compared to non-HIU treated groups, the total SH content of HIU treated groups significantly decreased with increasing MgCl2 addition. A minimum value (48.55 μmol/g) was observed with the addition of 40 mM MgCl2. This suggests that the physical actions generated by ultrasonic treatment disrupted the protein molecular structure, exposing more hidden thiol groups to the exterior, intensifying hydrophobic interactions and shortening the distances between SH groups. When exposed to oxygen in protein solutions, more active thiol groups and reduced distances facilitate the generation of disulfide bonds, thereby reducing the total SH content [34]. Analogous results were observed by Wu et al. [35], where lysine-assisted ultrasonic treatment decreased the total SH groups of sea bass MP. Consequently, the total SH content of reduced-sodium carp MP in the HIU and MgCl2 combined treatments was lower.

Intrinsic tryptophan fluorescence

In the microenvironment of proteins, the intrinsic fluorescence spectra can characterize the polarity variations of residues such as Trp, which can further ascertain the variations in the tertiary structure of MP [36]. The intrinsic fluorescence intensity of reduced-sodium carp MP decreased with increasing MgCl2 addition, and a valley value was observed at 40 mM (Fig. 2B), indicating that MgCl2 affected the tertiary structure of MP. MgCl2 can elevate the ionic strength of the microenvironment, and alter the polar microenvironment of tryptophan, leading to variations in its the tertiary structure [13]. MgCl2 addition also increased the disulfide bond amount of MP and the emergence of a nearby disulfide bridge adjacent to Trp residues could quench the excited-state Trp residues [37]. After HIU treatment, the intrinsic fluorescence intensity of reduced-sodium MP was distinctly lower than that of the non-HIU treatment groups, suggesting that HIU treatment induced the exposure of Trp to protein surfaces (a hydrophilic environment) by promoting protein unfolding, leading to fluorescence quenching, thereby decreasing intrinsic fluorescence intensity [28,38]. Zou et al. [39] reported the similar results, where sodium bicarbonate and ultrasound treatment modified the polar microenvironment of proteins, prompting energy transfer and reducing in fluorescence intensity. Moreover, the maximum emission wavelength (λ max) was approximately 334 nm for all groups, indicating that Trp and other residues in the protein molecules were in a hydrophobic microenvironment. Meanwhile, HIU and MgCl2 treatment triggered a slight blue shift of the reduced-sodium MP compared to that of the untreated group, suggesting that Trp residues were in a more hydrophobic microenvironment [40], aligned with the surface hydrophobicity results (Fig. 1D). Therefore, HIU combined with MgCl2 can impact the tertiary structure of reduced-sodium carp MP.

UV spectroscopy

UV spectroscopy is a valuable technique to detect protein alterations, such as conformational shifts, hydration changes, dissociation, and denaturation, which can affect the microenvironment of aromatic residues, including phenylalanine, tryptophan, and tyrosine [41]. The UV intensity of reduced-sodium carp MP increased with the addition of MgCl2 (Fig. 2C), implying that the MgCl2 addition triggered the unwinding of the MP structure, exposing the chromophoric groups buried in the hydrophobic region of the protein core, thereby enhancing the UV absorption intensity. After HIU treatment, the UV absorption intensity of MP increased with the same MgCl2 incorporation, possibly due to a progressive unrolling of the MP structure triggered by ultrasonic cavitation, accelerating the exposure of chromophoric groups buried in the protein. Wang et al. [17] reported analogous findings, depicting that HIU treatment (20 kHz, 500 W) induced an increase in UV absorption intensity of white croaker MP.

However, the superimposition of signature peaks from aromatic amino acids often obscures the evaluation of the protein tertiary structure. Accordingly, the UV second derivative spectrum can improve the resolution and effectively analyze alterations in the microenvironment of characteristic amino acid residues. Fig. 2D depicts that all MP samples displayed two peaks (288 and 295 nm) and two valleys (284 and 292 nm) between 280–300 nm. The absorption peak detected at 288 nm was associated with the interaction of tryptophan and tyrosine residues, while the peak at 295 nm was attributed to the independent function of tryptophan residues [41]. The peaks at 288 and 295 nm exhibited a gradual blue shift (moving to shorter wavelengths) with increasing MgCl2 addition. Moreover, HIU treatment induced a blueshift in the peaks at 288 and 295 nm. This could be explained by the fact that HIU and MgCl2 treatments induced the emergence of tryptophan and tyrosine residues from hydrophobic areas to hydrophilic surroundings [37]. Wang et al. [42] presented analogous findings for white croaker MP, demonstrating that ultrasound treatment promoted the exposure of Trp and Tyr residues embedded deep within the protein interior. The amplitude ratio (the proportion between the distance of the positive and negative absorption peak troughs and peaks, r = a/b) can be used to characterize the variation in the microenvironment of Tyr residues. The r value for tyrosine is highly related to solvent polarity, whereas the r value for tryptophan (Trp) exhibits a minor association with solvent polarity [43]. The r value of the non-HIU treated group remarkably raised from 1.1460 to 1.5438 with the addition of MgCl2 addition (Fig. 2D). The r value of HIU treated groups significantly elevated from 1.2536 to 1.882 with increased MgCl2 addition, implying that the microenvironment of Tyr residues shifted to a more hydrophilic state, supporting the surface hydrophobicity results (Fig. 1D). This phenomenon is likely because HIU and MgCl2 triggered the extension of protein structure and the alteration in the three-dimensional locations, resulting in the migration of tyrosine residues from nonpolar surroundings to hydrophilic environments during the disassembly of the protein tertiary conformation [17].

SDS-PAGE

MPs mainly include myosin, actin, and tropomyosin, with molecular masses of 200, 43–48, and 33–36 kDa, respectively [44]. The effects of ultrasound and MgCl2 on the protein profiles of reduced-sodium carp MP are displayed in Fig. 3A and 3B. The reduced-sodium carp MP revealed a typical pattern, primarily consisting of myosin heavy chain (MHC, 200 kDa), actin (43 kDa), and tropomyosin subunits (35 kDa). With increasing addition of MgCl2, solubilization of MHC and actin in non-HIU treated groups increased (Fig. 3A). Moreover, the electrophoretic profiles of MP exhibited no evidence of protein degradation. Consequently, these findings indicate that the solubilizing action of MgCl2 on MP was not due to cleavage or reduction in the length of the polypeptides within MP. The MHC and action bands of HIU treated groups with similar MgCl2 addition exhibited no significant differences compared to those of non-HIU treated groups(Fig. 3B), implying that ultrasonic treatment did not influence the protein profiles and altered the protein distribution of reduced-sodium carp MP, similar to scallop proteins [45] and Clanis Bilineata Tingtauica Mell proteins [46]. Taha et al. [47] also observed that the molecular weight distribution remained unchanged, irrespective of the sonication conditions.

FT-IR

Infrared spectra were obtained in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1 and were attributed to energy absorption by chemical bonds, primarily involving stretching and bending actions, which are widely acknowledged as an effective method for the structural analysis of proteins [48]. Generally, the amide I band of the infrared spectrum (1700–1600 cm−1) is mainly due to C=O stretching vibrations as well as N-H bending and C-H stretching motions. Especially, C=O stretching vibrations in proteins are greatly influenced by their multiple secondary structures and intermolecular or intramolecular behaviors, such as molecular geometries (α-helices, β-turns, β-sheets, and random coils). Hence, the amide I band is the most susceptible infrared spectral area to analyze the secondary structural features of proteins. The amide II band region is 1600–1500 cm−1, which is primarily associated with the C-N bond stretching and N-H bond bending vibrations. The amide A band region at 3400–3200 cm−1, is primarily related to the stretching vibrations of the intermolecular hydrogen-bonded N-H and O-H groups. The amide B band region (3000–2800 cm−1) was linked to C-N stretching and asymmetric CH2 stretching vibrations. Fig. 3C presents the infrared spectra (4000–400 cm−1) of the reduced-sodium carp MP treated with HIU and MgCl2. The combination of HIU and MgCl2 did not significantly change the peak shapes of the entire infrared spectrum of reduced-sodium carp MP, indicating that the peptide backbone of the protein remained unchanged (Fig. 3C). In non-HIU treated groups, the amide A band presents no obvious changes with the increasing concentration of MgCl2. However, a slight blue shift was observed in the amide A band of the FT-IR spectrum after ultrasound treatment. This might be because the intense physical forces (cavitation and mechanical impact) produced by HIU processing destroy the non-covalent associations entailing N-H or O-H groups. Accordingly, N-H or O-H bonds are shortened, which elevates the electron density, ultimately resulting in a shift of the absorption peaks to higher wavenumbers in the infrared spectrum of the reduced-sodium carp MP.

The locations of 1692–1662, 1662–1645, and 1645–1638 cm−1 correspond to β-turns, α-helices, and random coils, respectively, while the regions of 1700–1692 cm−1 and 1638–1615 cm−1 correspond to β-sheets. Table 1 reveals that increasing the incorporation of MgCl2 significantly lowered the proportion of α-helices and β-turns and raised the proportion of β-sheets and random coils in the non-HIU treated groups. This is presumably because the increased MgCl2 addition modified the polarity state of the MP in the microenvironment, contributing to the disruption of intra-protein hydrogen bonds [49]. Previous studies have indicated that the surface hydrophobicity of MP is negatively associated with the α-helix content. Thus, the results of surface hydrophobicity (Fig. 1D) of reduced-sodium carp MP are in accordance with changes in α-helix content. HIU treated groups with similar MgCl2 addition significantly decreased the proportions of α-helices and β-turns, with a notable increase in the proportions of β-sheets and random coils compared to those of non-HIU treated groups (Table 1). The possible cause of this phenomenon is that the cavitation and microjet action generated by ultrasonic treatment promote the unwinding of MP and weaken some hydrogen bonds, collapsing of the regular structures of α-helices and β-turns, and generating β-sheets and random coils. Liu et al. [13] demonstrated that HIU treatment evidently declined the compositions of α-helices and β-turns, and increased the compositions of β-sheets and random coils of MP with low ionic levels. Similar results were reported for ultrasonically treated Coregonus peled MP [50] and Neosalanx taihuensis MP [51].

DSC

DSC is a powerful tool for evaluating the thermal stability of MP, and the transition temperature is associated with MP denaturation. The effects of HIU and MgCl2 on DSC curves of reduced-sodium carp MP are shown in Fig. 3D. The control samples exhibited two distinct, typical endothermic peaks at 52.1, and 80.6 °C, corresponding to the characteristic transition temperatures of MP. Previous studies have indicated that the first peak is predominantly associated with the myosin head (peak Ⅰ), and the second peak (peak II) corresponds to the actin [44]. The positions of two peaks of non-HIU treated groups shifted to lower temperatures compared to those of the control, as the MgCl2 addition increased, suggesting that the high concentration addition of MgCl2 triggered the disruption of conformational stability. This could because Mg2+ interacts with the negatively charged residues of MP, thereby reducing the electrostatic repulsion between proteins and decreasing the thermal stability of myosin and actin [41]. The denaturation temperature of HIU treated groups was significantly higher than that of non-HIU treated group at similar MgCl2 concentrations, indicating that HIU could counteract the destabilization of the MP caused by MgCl2 addition and facilitate the construction of structures with improved thermal stability. This phenomenon could be explained by the cavitation effect triggered by HIU treatment, destroying the hydrogen bond and hydrophobic interactions, inhibiting the MP aggregation and modifying the tertiary structure of MP [28]. Li et al. [52] presented consistent results, demonstrating that ultrasonic treatment increased the transition temperature of the golden threadfin bream and improved the thermal stability. As a result, DSC findings revealed that HIU and MgCl2 treatments could influence the thermal stability of the reduced-sodium carp MP.

Shear stress

Shear stress reflects the velocity and resistance of emulsions subjected to external forces and provides physical support for the stability of emulsified suspended particles. The shear stress of all MP samples increased nonlinearly with the acceleration of shear rate from 0.01 to 1000 s−1 (Fig. 4A), evidencing that the MP solution is a non-Newtonian fluid. The shear stress of non-HIU treated groups decreased significantly with the increased MgCl2 addition under a similar shear rate, probably attributed to the relatively large electronegativity of Mg2+, prompting the formation of polar bonds in MPs, thereby restraining the protein aggregation and causing the reduction of shear stress. The shear stress decreased in HIU treated groups compared to the non-HIU treated groups at the same shear rate and MgCl2 addition, which was analogous to the finding of Yu, Li, Sun, Yan, & Zou, [53], revealing that ultrasonic treatment decreased the shear stress of mussel MP at the same shear rate. Amiri et al. [54] concluded that the cavitation effect generated from ultrasonication induced the rapid molecular motion, which produces shear forces strong enough to destruct protein chains and the hydrophobic attractions between MPs, thereby contributing to the inhibition of protein aggregation [54]. Consequently, the results revealed that HIU and MgCl2 significantly inhibited MP aggregation. The variation in shear stress further demonstrates the potential influence of HIU combined with MgCl2 treatment on protein structure modification.

Dynamic rheology

The storage modulus (G') reflects the energy stored during the deformation of the elastic MP within a frequency period under different oscillation frequency conditions. It is commonly employed to evaluate the correlation between internal structure and macroscopic rheology. The greater the energy storage, the stronger the interaction forces within the emulsion system, enhancing the elastic stability. The effect of HIU and MgCl2 on the G' value of reduced-sodium carp MP under a dynamic frequency sweep is illustrated in Fig. 4B and C. The values of G′and G″ in all samples exhibited progressively increased moduli with increasing oscillation frequency, and the value of G′ was much higher than G″ for each sample, demonstrating that the intense interactions primarily responsible for the elastic modulus required a prolonged period to relax. This phenomenon suggested that the emulsion retained permanent interactions, and exhibited a typical weak gel structure at low frequencies [53]. The G' value of non-HIU treated groups initially increased, followed by a progressive decline trend with the increased addition of MgCl2, which could be explained by the fact that low concentrations of Mg2+ induce a moderate level of ion hydration that prevents phase separation caused by excessive aggregation, thereby raising the G' value. However, excessively high concentrations of Mg2+ induce the generation of numerous salt bridges, weakening the interactions between protein molecules thereby reducing the G' value. Compared to non-HIU treated groups, the G' value decreased of HIU treated groups at the similar oscillation frequency and MgCl2 addition, consistent with the finding of Xiong, Li, Miao, Zhang, Zheng, & Zhang, [55] and Yu et al. [53], who found that ultrasound treatment decreased the G' value of fish MP and mussel MP at oscillation sweep. This phenomenon was probably attributed to the variations in the decreased particle size (Fig. 1A) induced by cavitation from HIU treatment, influencing the rheological characteristics of reduced-sodium carp MP [55]. Moreover, HIU treatment facilitated the unwinding of the MP structure and decreased the β-sheet content (Table 1), weakening non-covalent interactions, thereby reducing the intermolecular interactions of MP molecules [56].

EAI and ESI

EAI of proteins indicates their capacity to adsorb at the water–oil droplet interface, preventing gravitational division, clustering, and coalescence during the construction of emulsions, while the ESI reflects the stability of proteins at the oil–water interface following storage [25]. The EAI and ESI of MP are important in the manufacturing of emulsified meat products, including Harbin and Frankfurt sausages. Fig. 5A and 5B depict the influence of HIU and MgCl2 on the EAI and ESI of reduced-sodium carp MP. The EAI and ESI of non-HIU treated groups increased with the addition of MgCl2, reaching peak values of 19.87 m2/g and 16.55 min, respectively, when MgCl2 concentration was 40 mM. This phenomenon could be assigned to the reduced particle size (Fig. 1A) and the increased solubility (Fig. 1C), facilitating the rapid movement and diffusion of proteins to the oil–water junction [57]. Compared to non-HIU treated groups, the EAI and ESI of HIU treated groups increased significantly with the increased MgCl2 addition, and the peak value (20.73 m2/g and 18.02 min) was observed, respectively, when MgCl2 was added at 40 mM. Parallel observations were documented by Deng et al. [51], where ultrasound treatments (200–600 W) increased the EAI and ESI of Neosalanx taihuensis MP. Li et al. [25] reported that the EAI and ESI of shrimp MP were significantly improved after HIU treatment (400 W). This phenomenon suggests that the homogenization effects induced by ultrasound declined the particle size of MP, lowered the interfacial tension, facilitated the attachment of more MP at the oil–water interface, stabilized the interfacial film and network structure, and aided in the enhancement of EAI. The enhanced emulsifying stability is possibly due to heightened exposure of hydrophobic residues in MP induced by cavitation of ultrasonication treatment, enhancing the surface hydrophobicity (Fig. 1D) of the reduced-sodium carp MP. This increased surface hydrophobicity promoted the adsorption of MP onto the surface of the soybean oil globules. Subsequently, the MP films surrounding the soybean oil globules interact via hydrophobic forces, repelling each other and inhibiting further aggregation [58].

Conclusions

MgCl2 addition combined with HIU treatment ameliorated the functional characteristics of reduced-sodium carp MP by altering the particle size, zeta potential, and secondary and tertiary structure. Specifically, adding 40 mM MgCl2 significantly improved zeta potential, solubility, surface hydrophobicity, UV intensity, and emulsification activity while reducing particle size, exposure of total SH groups, and intrinsic fluorescence intensity of MP reduced-sodium carp MP by inducing a conversion from the α-helix structure to form β-sheets and increasing the solubilization of MHC and actin. Moreover, MgCl2 addition decreased the thermal stability and rheological properties. The cavitation and homogenization effects of HIU treatment further amplified the positive impact on the physical, structural, and functional properties of reduced-sodium carp MP. Accordingly, combining MgCl2 and HIU treatment is a potential and efficient approach to improving the functional attributes of aquatic products by modulating changes in protein structure under reduced-sodium conditions.
