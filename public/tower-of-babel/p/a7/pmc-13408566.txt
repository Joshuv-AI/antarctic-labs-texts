Extroparvum occidentale gen. et sp. nov. and the Cretaceous diversification of Lauraceae
Authors: Keana K Tang, Kelly K S Matsunaga, Brian A Atkinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPremiseThe fossil record of Lauraceae extends back to the Early Cretaceous and comprises numerous flowers. However, phylogenetic relationships of many of these fossil flowers remain unresolved. To better understand the early evolutionary history of Lauraceae, we conducted phylogenetic analyses of living and extinct members of the family and described a new fossil flower from the Coniacian of British Columbia, Canada.MethodsSerial sections of the new fossil flower were prepared and used to create a three‐dimensional reconstruction. We performed a series of phylogenetic analyses, including total‐evidence analyses using a novel morphological matrix, to analyze the new fossil flower and 16 previously described fossil flowers. Phylogenetic uncertainty was explored for each taxon, and phylogenetic pseudofossilization analyses were conducted to test whether our floral data set can accurately recover systematic relationships within Lauraceae.ResultsThe Eden Main fossil flowers are assignable to Lauraceae and represent a new genus and species: Extroparvum occidentale. Phylogenetic analyses recovered E. occidentale and 12 fossil flowers among Lauraceae. Phylogenetic uncertainty within Lauraceae was high for many of the fossils, but we identified two taxa as crown‐group members that can be employed as new node calibrations for the family. Pseudofossilization analyses indicated that our floral data set can accurately recover taxa within crown‐group Lauraceae with high support.ConclusionsOur work adds to the known Cretaceous diversity of Lauraceae and provides the first phylogenetic evidence of crown‐group Lauraceae diversifying by the Late Cretaceous. Furthermore, our novel morphological data set lays an important foundation for directly integrating fossil flowers into macroevolutionary studies of the family.Keywords: fossil calibration, fossil flowers, Laurales, morphological dataset, total‐evidence analysis

With a cosmopolitan distribution in temperate and tropical regions, Lauraceae comprise up to 3300 species of trees, shrubs, and vines circumscribed within 50 genera making it conspicuously more species rich than all other families in Laurales (Rohwer, 1993; van der Werff and Richter, 1996; Renner, 2011; Li et al., 2025; WFO, 2026). Furthermore, Lauraceae are diverse and abundant in many rainforests in South America and East Asia (Gentry, 1988; Fang and Yoda, 1989; Tang, 2015). Despite its high species richness, wide distribution, and ecological importance, little is known about the evolutionary history of the family. The age of Lauraceae is uncertain, with recent molecular dating estimates ranging from 157.96 to 56.39 million years ago (Ma) for the origin of the crown group, due in part to uncertainty over the placement of calibration fossils (Michalak et al., 2010; Magallón et al., 2015; Li et al., 2020; Ramírez‐Barahona et al., 2020; Silvestro et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023; Zuntini et al., 2024). Integrating Lauraceae fossils into a phylogenetic framework is therefore necessary to refine age estimates for the family, as done in other plant groups (Manos et al., 2007; Larson‐Johnson, 2016; Atkinson, 2018, 2020; Matsunaga and Smith, 2021; May et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2022; Coiro et al., 2023; Nguyen and Atkinson, 2024).

The fossil record of Lauraceae reaches back to the Early Cretaceous (Aptian‐Albian) and is often represented by flowers (e.g., Drinnan et al., 1990; Herendeen et al., 1994; Eklund and Kvaček, 1998; Eklund, 2000; Takahashi et al., 2001, 2014; Kvaček and Eklund, 2003; Frumin et al., 2004; von Balthazar et al., 2007, 2011; Viehofen et al., 2008; Chambers et al., 2011, 2012; Atkinson et al., 2015; Poinar, 2017; Beurel et al., 2024). Rich in taxonomically informative features (Rohwer, 1993; van der Werff and Richter, 1996), flowers are the most reliable structure for identifying Cretaceous occurrences of the family. While 12 fossil flowers have been identified from Cretaceous deposits (see Table 1), it is uncertain whether they represent stem‐ or crown‐group members of Lauraceae.

To date, only two such lauraceous fossils have been analyzed within a phylogenetic framework: the Early Cretaceous Cohongarootonia hispida (von Balthazar et al., 2011) and the Late Cretaceous Mauldinia mirabilis (Drinnan et al., 1990). While the floral morphology of these genera is consistent with Lauraceae, phylogenetic analyses have placed Cohongarootonia and Mauldinia within Laurales, outside of the total group of Lauraceae (Doyle and Endress, 2010; von Balthazar et al., 2011; Schönenberger et al., 2020; López‐Martínez et al., 2023). Although these previous studies have clarified the relationships of Cohongarootonia and Mauldinia among angiosperms, the morphological matrices used to place them had broad taxon sampling across the angiosperm phylogeny with limited sampling below the family level (e.g., Doyle and Endress, 2010; Schönenberger et al., 2020), and thus the lower‐level relationships of these species are yet to be fully explored. Taxon sampling that more fully captures character distributions across the Lauraceae phylogeny has much potential for clarifying relationships of fossil flowers that have been assigned to the family.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the early evolution of Lauraceae by describing new fossil flowers and conducting phylogenetic analyses that extensively sampled living and extinct species of Lauraceae. We described a new genus and species, Extroparvum occidentale Tang, Matsunaga & Atkinson, based on five permineralized flowers from Coniacian (89.8–86.3 Ma) deposits of the Eden Main locality on Vancouver Island, British Columbia, Canada. To test whether the crown group of Lauraceae diversified during the Cretaceous, we conducted a series of phylogenetic analyses using a novel morphological data set that included 16 previously described fossils ranging in age from the Late Cretaceous to the Neogene and 102 extant species.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Five flowers permineralized within calcium carbonate concretions were recovered from the Eden Main locality on Vancouver Island, British Columbia, Canada. While exposures from the Eden Main locality were originally assigned to the Dunsmuir Member of the Comox Formation (Karafit and Stockey, 2008; Karafit, 2008; Klymiuk et al., 2015), Huang et al. (2022) assigned the Eden Main locality to an unnamed unit that belongs to the Turonian–Campanian strata of the Comox sub‐basin of the Nanaimo Group. In the recent past, the Comox Formation was understood to be highly diachronous in nature, encompassing Turonian to early Campanian sediments (Mustard, 1994; Johnstone et al., 2006; Karafit and Stockey, 2008; Karafit, 2008; Klymiuk et al., 2015). Johnstone et al. (2006) assigned the diachronous strata to the Comox Formation based on similar lithology and sedimentology. However, recent facies analyses and detrital zircon dating have been employed to challenge the grouping of the highly diachronous strata (Kent et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2019, 2022; Girotto et al., 2024). These recent studies indicate that the Dunsmuir member, not including the Eden Main exposures, is Santonian (85–84 Ma) in age, which contrasts from the Coniacian age (89.8–86.3 Ma) of the Eden Main strata as inferred from ammonite biostratigraphy (see Karafit and Stockey, 2008; Klymiuk et al., 2015; Huang et al., 2022). Fossils from the Eden Main locality are likely Coniacian in age and therefore do not belong to the Santonian Dunsmuir member. Following Johnstone et al. (2006), we consider exposures of the Eden Main locality as belonging to the Comox Formation. Sediments and marine fossils recovered in the locality indicate the concretions formed in a shallow marine environment (Mustard, 1994; Haggart et al., 2003; Johnstone et al., 2006). Fossil plants recovered from the Eden Main locality include bryophytes, lycophytes, ferns (Karafit and Stockey, 2008; Karafit, 2008), gymnosperm stems and cones (Jud et al., 2010; Klymiuk et al., 2015; Stockey et al., 2020), and angiosperm wood, infructescences, flowers, and fruits (Karafit, 2008; Mindell et al., 2014; Jud et al., 2017; Atkinson et al., 2018; Scharfstein et al., 2020).

Specimens were serial‐sectioned using the cellulose acetate peel technique (Joy et al., 1956), and peels were mounted onto microscope slides using Eukitt mounting medium (O. Kindler & ORSAtec GmbH, Bobingen, Germany). Images of the slides were obtained using a Nikon SMZ25 stereomicroscope and a Nikon Eclipse LV100ND microscope with a Nikon DS‐Fi3 camera attachment (Nikon Corp., Tokyo, Japan). A three‐dimensional reconstruction of specimen 114 F bot was created using Avizo 9 Lite 3D (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and specimen P14467 B top was reconstructed using the software Mimics 26.0 (Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). The image series and three‐dimensional reconstructions of the specimens are archived on Morphosource (Boyer et al., 2016) under the project title “Cretaceous Lauraceae flowers from western Canada” (ID: 655793). All specimen slides are housed in the University of Alberta Paleobotanical Collections (UAPC‐ALTA).

Phylogenetic analyses

Sixteen previously described lauraceous fossil flowers, ranging from Cretaceous to Miocene in age, were selected from the literature (see Table 1). Only fossils that were well enough preserved to be scored for phylogenetic analysis were selected. To assess the systematic affinities of the Eden Main fossil flower and previously described lauraceous fossil flowers, a series of phylogenetic analyses were conducted. All fossil flowers were first analyzed using the phyloscan method (Doyle et al., 2008; Schönenberger et al., 2020; Doyle and Endress 2010, 2014, 2024) to investigate possible affinities across the angiosperm phylogeny. We specifically used the data set of Schönenberger et al. (2020) to conduct our analyses. Fossils that had affinities with Lauraceae in the phyloscan analyses were then analyzed in a total‐evidence Bayesian framework using a novel data set of Lauraceae.

The phyloscan method uses a floral character matrix and molecular backbone tree of angiosperms to identify all most‐parsimonious positions (MPs) among extant angiosperm orders and families. This method allowed us to first confirm the assignment of fossil flowers to Lauraceae and eliminate potential affinities with distantly related groups. The previously described fossil flowers were scored based on their descriptions. Scoring justifications are detailed in Appendix S1. The character matrix is available online at MorphoBank under project 5451 (O'Leary and Kaufman, 2011), and all phyloscan outputs are compiled in Appendix S2. Some flowers did have additional MPs outside of Lauraceae. In these cases, detailed morphological comparisons were conducted to determine whether such affinities warranted investigation and to confirm assignment to Lauraceae (see Discussion and Appendix S3).

To analyze the systematic relationships among modern Lauraceae of the Eden Main flower and fossil flowers assigned to Lauraceae in the phyloscan analyses, we conducted more focused phylogenetic analyses using a combined morphological and molecular data set. The morphological data set was constructed in Mesquite (Maddison and Maddison, 2023) and consisted of 46 floral characters scored for 119 taxa (Appendix S4). The data set included 81 extant species of Lauraceae, representing all nine tribes (Li et al., 2025), and 20 species of Laurales plus Magnolia grandiflora as outgroups. Taxa were scored from the literature based on both species level and generic descriptions. The morphological data set is available online at MorphoBank under project 5451 (O'Leary and Kaufman, 2011). The molecular data set included sequences for five loci obtained from GenBank: ITS, rbcL, trnK‐matK, psbA‐trnH intergenic spacer, and trnL‐trnF intergenic spacer (Appendix S5). Sequences were aligned in Aliview (Larsson, 2014) using MAFFT (Katoh and Standley, 2013).

The total‐evidence phylogenetic analyses were conducted in MrBayes using the concatenated morphological and molecular data set. PartitionFinder2 (Lanfear et al., 2016), which uses the corrected Akaike information criterion (AICc) for model selection, was used to find the optimal partitioning scheme and evolutionary model for each partition. The best partitioning scheme assigned the GTR + G model to trnK‐matK, psbA‐trnH intergenic spacer, and trnL‐trnF intergenic spacer and GTR + G + I to ITS and rbcL. The Mkv model (Lewis, 2001) was used for the morphological data. The total‐evidence phylogenetic analyses were performed on the CIPRES Science Gateway (Miller et al., 2011) and the Community Cluster operated by the Center for Research Computing at the University of Kansas. Previous studies have shown that including additional fossils in phylogenetic analyses potentially yields more accurate phylogenetic relationships between extinct and extant taxa (Mongiardino Koch and Parry, 2020; Mongiardino Koch et al., 2021). Therefore, all fossil flowers were analyzed individually and then altogether in a single analysis. All analyses used Metropolis‐coupled Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMCMC) consisting of two independent runs with four chains (three hot and one cold) running for 10,000,000 generations and sampling every 1000 generations. The first 25% of posterior trees were discarded as burn‐in and the remaining trees summarized in a maximum compatible tree (see Appendix S6 for MrBayes infiles). Tracer 1.7 (Rambaut et al., 2018) was used to visually assess run convergence and confirm all parameters had effective sample size values over 200.

Analyzing phylogenetic uncertainty

To understand uncertainty in the phylogenetic placement of each fossil flower, we used iterative RoguePlots (Klopfstein and Spasojevic, 2019) in R 4.1.2 (R Core Team, 2021) to visualize alternative placements sampled in the posterior distribution of trees and identify rogue taxa. We generated RoguePlots for each of the 17 fossil flowers based on the analysis that included all fossils simultaneously (Appendix S7). Fossil flowers with numerous alternative phylogenetic positions outside of Lauraceae were identified as rogue taxa. Rogue taxa were then removed from the data set, and the phylogenetic analysis was repeated to understand the effect of rogue taxa on posterior probabilities, particularly along the backbone of the phylogeny. RoguePlots were then constructed for the remaining fossil flowers retained in the final analysis (Appendix S8).

Phylogenetic pseudofossilization analyses were conducted to test whether the morphological data set can accurately recover phylogenetic positions of extant taxa, enabling us to gauge confidence in the placement of fossil taxa in the total‐evidence analyses (Matsunaga and Smith, 2021; Nguyen and Atkinson, 2024). First, a phylogeny was inferred using molecular data from extant taxa only; this tree is referred to as the reference tree. Second, we selected 19 extant species (“pseudofossils”), representing each tribe within Lauraceae, and pruned each from the reference tree using the R package ape 5.7 (Paradis and Schliep, 2019), resulting in 19 trees with a single missing taxon. Each pruned tree was subsequently used as a constraint tree in a maximum likelihood analysis of the morphological data set only. A maximum likelihood analysis was performed for each of the 19 pseudofossils, resulting in 19 trees in which each pseudofossil was placed based on morphological characters only (“inferred trees”), much like the actual fossils in the total‐evidence analyses (Appendix S9). All analyses were conducted in the RAxML GUI (Edler et al., 2021) using the Mkv model and 100 bootstrap replicates. The shallowest well‐supported node (bootstrap support = 100) was identified for each pseudofossil.

Description

Extroparvum occidentale is based on five fossil flowers permineralized in calcium carbonate concretions from the Eden Main locality. The flowers are small, pedicellate, perfect, and actinomorphic. Flowers are trimerous and perigynous (Figure 1A–D; see Appendix S10). The Eden Main flowers measure 1.37–3.0 mm long and 1.79–2.2 mm wide. The pedicel is 0.6–1.3 mm long, and a shallow hypanthium up to 0.5 mm long is present.

Perianth

Two alternating trimerous whorls of tepals comprise the perianth (Figure 1A,C). The reconstruction of paratype 114 F bot shows the most completely preserved outer tepal (Figure 1C). In this specimen, two of the three tepals of the outer whorl were lost in the original saw cut, but the remaining tepal in this whorl is much narrower and shorter compared to the inner tepals. The tepal of the first whorl is lanceolate in shape with an acute apex (Figure 1C), measuring up to 1.3 mm long and 0.9 mm wide at its base. The larger inner tepals are ovate in shape with an acute apex and are 1.5–2 mm long and 2.3–2.6 mm wide. The tepals are glabrous with a uniseriate layer of small epidermal cells (Figure 2A,B).

Androecium

The androecium consists of nine fertile stamens in three alternating trimerous whorls and three staminodes in an innermost fourth whorl (Figure 2C). Stamens are 1–1.2 mm long with filaments that are 0.6–0.8 mm long and anthers that are 0.2–0.4 mm long. Stamen filaments are distinct from the anthers and are covered in unicellular trichomes (Figure 2D). Oil cells are present throughout the anthers and filaments (Figure 2D,F). Anthers are tetrasporangiate (Figure 2A,E) and dehisce by apically hinged valves (Figure 2D–F) that are composed of thick‐walled endothecial cells (Figure 2F). Staminodes are 0.8–1 mm long.

Among the fossil flowers that have dispersed their pollen, the outer whorls of stamens curve inward toward the inner whorls (Figure 2D). Anthers of the outer whorls of stamens are flattened and oblong in shape (Figure 2C–E). Anther locules are introrse and arranged in two superposed pairs (Figure 2E). Each stamen is supplied by a single vascular trace that diverges from the hypanthium (Figure 3A).

The inner whorl of stamens is erect and appressed to the ovary (Figure 3B). Anthers of the inner whorl are elongated with an acute apex pointing toward the outer whorls of the flower (Figures 2, 3). Anther locules are arranged in two superposed pairs and the locules are extrorse‐latrorse (Figures 2, 3). Paired staminal appendages are present at the base of the inner stamens (Figure 3B,C). The staminal appendages are attached by a short stalk and have a glandular head that is peltate in shape (Figure 3E). Five vascular traces enter each stamen in the inner whorl. Three traces enter the stamen, while a single trace enters each staminal appendage (Figure 3C,D).

The innermost whorl of the androecium is composed of staminodes appressed to the ovary (Figure 3A,B). Staminodes are stipitate and sagittate. Unicellular trichomes are present on the staminodes, and oil cells are present throughout the staminode tissue (Figure 3A). A single vascular trace enters each staminode.

Pollen

Three pollen grains were found in situ on the holotype specimen. One grain was preserved within an anther, and the other two grains were preserved near filaments. The grains are spheroidal and rugose, measuring 15–15.9 µm in diameter (Figure 3F).

Gynoecium

The gynoecium consists of a superior ovary with a single style (Figures 1, 4). The ovary is 0.8–0.9 mm long and 0.4–0.5 mm wide. The style is 0.3–0.5 mm long and plicate (Figure 4A) like styles of extant Lauraceae (Endress and Igersheim, 1997). Scattered oil cells are present throughout the ovary walls. The epidermis of the ovary consists of a single layer of smaller cells. A single locule is present in the ovary that holds an apically attached bitegmic ovule (Figure 4B,C).

Phyloscan results

Phylogenetic results for all fossil flowers are summarized in Appendix S11. For Extroparvum occidentale, the phyloscan analysis recovered seven MPs within Laurales, specifically among Siparunaceae, Hernandiaceae, Monimiaceae, and Lauraceae (Figure 5). Additionally, a single MP along the branch leading to Najas minor All. (Hydrocharitaceae) was recovered (see Discussion for comparisons between these taxa). Among the 16 previously described fossil flowers assigned to Lauraceae, MPs within Lauraceae were recovered for 13 fossils (see Appendices S2 and S11). The three fossil flowers, Cryptocarya sp. (Beurel et al., 2024), Neusenia tetrasporangiata (Eklund, 2000), and Tinaflora beardiae (Atkinson et al., 2015), were placed in Lauraceae with no additional MPs outside of the family. For the other 10 fossil flowers that were placed within Lauraceae, additional MPs outside of Lauraceae were recovered among other families in Laurales, Nymphaeales, Ceratophyllaceae, and several lineages within the monocots. The remaining three fossil taxa, Cascolaurus burmitis (Poinar, 2017), Potomacanthus lobatus (von Balthazar et al., 2007), and Treptostemon domingensis (Chambers et al., 2012) were not placed within Lauraceae by our phyloscan analyses. Cascolaurus burmitis was placed in Siparunaceae. Potomacanthus lobatus was placed on a branch leading to a clade comprising Hernandiaceae and Monimiaceae (Laurales). Multiple MPs were recovered for Treptostemon domingensis among Nymphaeales, Laurales, and several monocot lineages. On the basis of morphological comparisons of each fossil flower and their MPs (summarized in Appendix S3), we retained these fossils for further analysis because their morphology did not convincingly match the families to which they were allied in the phyloscan analysis and they fit within the morphological variation observed within Lauraceae. Therefore, Extroparvum occidentale and all 16 fossil flowers were included in our subsequent analyses.

Relationships among extant species

Our Bayesian phylogenetic analyses recovered a monophyletic Lauraceae. All genera are monophyletic with the exception of Ocotea and Persea. All nine previously recognized tribes within the family were recovered: Hypodaphnideae, Cryptocaryeae, Cassytheae, Neocinnamomeae, Caryodaphnopsideae, Mezilaureae, Laureae, Perseeae, and Cinnamomeae (Kostermans, 1957; van der Werff and Richter, 1996; Rohwer and Rudolph, 2005; Song et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025). The clade comprising Lauraeae, Perseeae, and Cinnamomeae are often referred to as “the Core Lauraceae” (Rohwer and Rudolph, 2005; Tian et al., 2021) and was identified in our analyses. These results conform to previous molecular analyses of Lauraceae (Rohwer, 2000; Chanderbali et al., 2001; Rohwer and Rudolph, 2005; Rohwer et al., 2009, 2014; van der Merwe et al., 2016; Li et al., 2004, 2011, 2016, 2020; Trofimov and Rohwer, 2020; Tian et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021, 2022; Trofimov et al., 2016, 2019, 2022; Xiao and Ge, 2022; Xiao et al., 2020, 2022; Song et al., 2020, 2023; Yang et al., 2022, 2023).

We first analyzed all 17 fossil flowers together in a total‐evidence Bayesian analysis and then performed a second analysis in which rogue taxa had been removed. In the initial multiple fossils analysis that included all fossil flowers, Extroparvum occidentale was recovered as sister to the clade comprising the extinct Persea avita (Chambers et al., 2011) and living Caryodaphnopsis (PP = 0.08; Figure 7), which differs from the phylogenetic position among Perseeae that was recovered in the single fossil analysis of Extroparvum occidentale (Figure 6). The remaining 16 lauraceous fossil flowers were placed within crown‐group Lauraceae with moderate support at the crown node (PP = 0.75; Figure 7) and low support for the node just above the crown node (PP = 0.28), suggesting frequent placement of at least some of these fossils outside of Lauraceae or on the Lauraceae stem in the posterior distribution.

DISCUSSION

An increase in the known diversity of Late Cretaceous flowers for Lauraceae

The Late Cretaceous flowers from Eden Main are assignable to the family Lauraceae based on the presence of trimerous whorls, two whorls of tepals, three whorls of fertile stamens, anthers that dehisce by apically hinged valves, paired glandular appendages on the third whorl of stamens, an inner whorl of staminodes, and a perigynous ovary that bears a single ovule (Rohwer, 1993). Due to high amounts of homoplasy in floral characters, it is difficult to assign the Eden Main fossils to an extant genus or tribe within the family. However, most extant genera can be differentiated by unique combinations of characters. The Eden Main fossils are characterized by the presence of an outer whorl of tepals smaller in size than the inner whorl, tetrasporangiate anthers, stamen filaments longer than the anthers, peltate staminal appendages present at the base of the third whorl of stamens, and a whorl of sagittate staminodes. Among extant genera, unequal sized tepals, tetrasporangiate anthers, long stamen filaments, and sagittate staminodes are present in Caryodaphnopsis and Persea Mill. (Kopp, 1966; Kostermans, 1974a; van der Werff, 1986; Rohwer, 1993; van der Werff, 2002). In the past, Caryodaphnopsis was synonymous with Persea due to their similar floral morphology (Kostermans, 1974a; van der Werff and Richter, 1985). They are now recognized as two distinct genera in separate tribes based on vegetative morphology (Kostermans, 1974a; van der Werff and Richter, 1985) and molecular data (Chanderbali et al., 2001; Rohwer and Rudolph, 2005; Li et al., 2016). However, without vegetative or molecular data, the Eden Main fossils cannot be confidently placed within Caryodaphnopsis or Persea.

The Eden Main flowers differ from Caryodaphnopsis and Persea based on the shape of paired staminal appendages and pollen morphology. Paired staminal appendages are typically sagittate in shape among the two extant genera, but the Eden Main flowers differ in having peltate staminal appendages. We are not aware of any Lauraceae with peltate staminal appendages. Pollen grains of Carydaphnopsis and Persea are spheroidal and have a spiny exine (Raj and van der Werff, 1988; van der Merwe et al., 1990; Shang and Tang, 1995), or they can be verrucate in Caryodaphnopsis (Shang and Tang, 1995). The pollen grains found in situ with the Eden Main flowers are rugose with small ridges, although the exine may not be completely preserved. The thin exine of Lauraceae pollen is delicate and will often detach or become partially removed from the intine resulting in what appears to be pollen lacking exine structuring (Rohwer, 2018). However, the spheroidal shape and size of the pollen grains associated with the Eden Main flowers are diagnostic of Lauraceae. Therefore, the fossil flowers are assigned to the family as the new genus and species, Extroparvum occidentale.

Our phylogenetic analyses support the placement of Extroparvum occidentale within the total group (encompassing the stem and crown group) of Lauraceae. The phyloscan method recovered multiple MPs among the order Laurales for E. occidentale, including crown‐group Lauraceae, and an MP outside of the order along the branch leading to Najas minor (Hydrocharitaceae). Najas minor significantly differs from E. occidentale in floral morphology and only shares the presence of a unicarpellate gynoecium with a single ovule in the phyloscan matrix. Flowers of Najas are structurally unisexual, one fertile stamen is present, and anthers dehisce by an apical slit (Haynes et al., 1998). The genus is now included in the family Hydrocharitaceae, which has trimerous flowers, but the family differs from E. occidentale in having up to six whorls present in the androecium, an inferior ovary, and more than one carpel present (Cook, 1998). Based on these floral differences, Najas and Hydrocharitaceae can be excluded from taxonomic consideration.

Within Laurales, the phyloscan recovered Extroparvum occidentale within the families Siparunaceae, Lauraceae, Hernandiaceae, and Monimiaceae. Flowers of Siparunaceae are structurally unisexual and are enclosed by a floral roof (Renner and Hausner, 2005) where numerous carpels or stamens protrude out of a small opening. Lacking a floral roof, the structurally perfect flowers of E. occidentale are clearly distinct from flowers of Siparunaceae. Extroparvum occidentale can be distinguished from both Hernandiaceae and Monimiaceae based on morphological differences in the androecium and gynoecium. Flowers of Hernandiaceae have one whorl of fertile stamens, bisporangiate anthers with latrorse dehiscence, and an inferior ovary (Shutts, 1959; Kubitzki, 1969, 1993). Flowers of Monimiaceae are often structurally imperfect and have numerous carpels or stamens present (Lorence, 1985; Philipson, 1984, 1993; Whiffin, 2007). These floral differences affirm the lauraceous affinities of E. occidentale. Additionally, our Bayesian phylogenetic analyses consistently recover E. occidentale within Lauraceae among the Perseeae or Caryodaphnopsideae tribes. Although support values for a phylogenetic position among these tribes were low, support values were high for the recovery of E. occidentale within the total group of Lauraceae.

Comparisons with extinct Lauraceae

Among fossil flowers previously assigned to Lauraceae, Cretaceous taxa Cascolaurus burmitis (Poinar, 2017), Microlaurus perigynus (Takahashi et al., 2014), and Perseanthus crossmanensis (Herendeen et al., 1994) and a Miocene taxon, Persea avita (Iturralde‐Vinent and MacPhee, 1996; Chambers et al., 2011; Iturralde‐Vinent and Macphee, 2019), are similar to Extroparvum occidentale in having tepal whorls with unequal sizes and/or tetrasporangiate anthers. Nonetheless, Extroparvum occidentale is distinct from each of these taxa based on its unique combination of characters.

Cascolaurus burmitis is described from a single staminate flower preserved in amber (Poinar, 2017). The flower is trimerous with unequal perianth whorls and three whorls of fertile stamens. Large staminal appendages are present in the third whorl of stamens that extend to the top of the flower and appear to be fused together. Fossil flowers of Extroparvum occidentale differ in having perfect flowers and smaller staminal appendages that are not fused together. Furthermore, staminodes are absent in Cascolaurus burmitis.

Fossil flowers of Microlaurus perigynus are perfect with three whorls of fertile stamens, an inner whorl of staminodes, and a perigynous ovary bearing a single ovule (Takahashi et al., 2014). The general floral morphology of Microlaurus perigynus is similar to that of Extroparvum occidentale. However, differences in the androecium distinguish the two taxa from one another. The outer whorls of stamens in Microlaurus perigynus have narrow filaments while the inner whorl of stamens have much wider filaments. Stamen morphology in Extroparvum occidentale is more consistent in having narrow filaments throughout the androecium. Additionally, it is uncertain whether the paired staminal appendages present in Microlaurus perigynus are associated with the first or third whorl of stamens. The appendages appear to be inserted on the hypanthium rather than the stamen filament bases which differs from Extroparvum occidentale. Extroparvum occidentale can be distinguished from Microlaurus perigynus based on differences in stamen morphology.

The charcoalified flower of Perseanthus crossmanensis is trimerous with three whorls of stamens, paired staminal appendages present on the third androecial whorl, an inner whorl of staminodes, and a superior ovary (Herendeen et al., 1994). While anthers were not preserved, Perseanthus crossmanensis differs from Extroparvum occidentale based on the shape of paired staminal appendages and staminodes as well as pollen morphology. Staminal appendages of Perseanthus crossmanensis are sagittate, and staminodes are club shaped, while staminal appendages of Extroparvum occidentale are peltate in shape, and staminodes are sagittate. Pollen grains found on the ovary and filament bases of Perseanthus crossmanensis are spheroidal with a diameter of 28–33 µm and covered in spinules. Pollen grains of Extroparvum occidentale are spheroidal, but are smaller and lack spinules. Differences in stamen and pollen morphology distinguish Extroparvum occidentale from Perseanthus crossmanensis.

Persea avita is described from a fossil flower preserved in Miocene Dominican amber (Iturralde‐Vinent and MacPhee, 1996; Chambers et al., 2011; Iturralde‐Vinent and Macphee, 2019). The perfect flower is trimerous with three whorls of fertile stamens, an inner whorl of staminodes, and a single superior ovary. Dense trichomes are present throughout the entire flower, which differs from Extroparvum occidentale. Trichomes are only present within the androecium and at the base of the receptacle of Extroparvum occidentale, which differentiates it from Persea avita. The morphology of Extroparvum occidentale does not fit within the diagnoses of previously described fossil flowers assigned to Lauraceae and is therefore likely a distinct taxon.

Pseudofossilization analyses

The phylogenetic pseudofossilization analyses demonstrate that our floral data set can recover most taxa within the correct tribe or respective larger clade, as reflected by the reference tree, but requires a strict node‐support criterion. While some taxa were placed in the correct tribes with bootstrap support as low as 67%, others were placed incorrectly with bootstrap support as high as 98%. For this reason, we recommend a conservative threshold of 100% bootstrap support for placing fossil flowers with this data set, particularly if morphological comparisons yield ambiguous results. Additionally, we observed that certain clades tended to incorrectly “attract” pseudofossils. Incorrectly placed taxa were frequently recovered within Perseeae or Cinnamomeae, indicating that fossils placed within these tribes will need to be treated with caution. In particular, two taxa (Litsea coreana and Neocinnamomum caudatum) were placed incorrectly in Cinnamomeae with 100% bootstrap support.

Most extant genera can be differentiated based on a unique combination of floral characters. However, some floral character combinations appear to have evolved independently across the Lauraceae phylogeny, which likely makes it difficult to place some taxa in the correct tribes as demonstrated in some of our pseudofossilization analyses. Notable examples include Caryodaphnopsis, Litsea, Mezilaurus, Neocinnamomum, and Hypodaphnis which are discussed below.

Caryodaphnopsis tonkinensis was incorrectly recovered within Perseeae, which parallels the historical treatment of Caryodaphnopsis. Previous classifications based on floral morphology placed Caryodaphnopsis in genus Persea (Kostermans, 1974a; van der Werff and Richter, 1985), but subsequent comparative and phylogenetic analyses using vegetative morphology (Kostermans, 1974a; Richter, 1981) and molecular data (Chanderbali et al., 2001; Li et al., 2016; Tian et al., 2021) have since revealed that these two genera are distinct from one another and belong to two relatively distantly related tribes within Lauraceae. The incorrectly recovered position of pseudofossil Caryodaphnopsis tonkinensis is likely due to both Caryodaphnopsis and Persea having unequal sized tepal whorls and tetrasporangiate anthers.

Similarly, Litsea coreana, Mezilaurus opaca, and Neocinnamomum caudatum were incorrectly placed within Cinnamomeae. These species were historically considered synonymous with or closely related to genera that are now assigned to Cinnamomeae (Meissner, 1864; Bentham and Hooker, 1880; Mez, 1889; Pax, 1889; Hutchinson, 1964; Kostermans, 1974b; van der Werff and Richter, 1996). Notably, Litsea was considered closely related to Sassafras (van der Werff and Richter, 1996), and Mezilaurus was thought to be closely related to Licaria (Meissner, 1864; Bentham and Hooker, 1880; Mez, 1889; Pax, 1889; Hutchinson, 1964). Unsurprisingly, in the pseudofossilization analyses, Litsea coreana was placed sister to Sassafras, and Mezilaurus opaca came out sister to Licaria, mirroring previous morphology‐based classifications (see Appendix S9). Additional data from vegetative morphology and molecular phylogenetics have since shown that Litsea, Mezilaurus, and Neocinnamomum are all distinct genera that belong to other tribes (Richter, 1981; van der Werff, 1987; Li et al., 2004; Rohwer and Rudolph, 2005; Alves and Souza, 2013; Xiao et al., 2020).

Hypodaphnis zenkeri was also incorrectly recovered within Cinnamomeae. Flowers of Hypodaphnis zenkeri follow the general floral structure of Lauraceae, with the exception of having an inferior ovary. Hypodaphnis zenkeri is monotypic and belongs to its own tribe within Lauraceae. Such taxa are usually challenging to place in pseudofossilization analyses, and its incorrect placement here is unsurprising (Guillerme and Cooper, 2016; Matsunaga and Smith, 2021; Nguyen and Atkinson, 2024). Removing monotypic taxa from the reference tree reduces the phylogenetic signal in the distribution of characters defining the genus and tribe, resulting in inaccurate placement of the pseudofossil. Its incorrect placement in Cinnamomeae, along with several other pseudofossils, indicate that any fossils placed in Cinnamomeae with this data set should be treated cautiously and ideally should have additional data supporting placement within the tribe. Cinnamomeae is the largest tribe within Lauraceae and is morphologically diverse (Rohwer, 2000; Chanderbali et al., 2001; Rohwer and Rudolph, 2005; Tian et al., 2021). Our results are similar to those of Matsunaga and Smith (2021), who found that the largest palm tribe Areceae also tended to erroneously attract taxa in pseudofossilization analyses.

Overall, although our floral data set was limited in its use for accurately place some taxa at the tribe level, particularly within the Perseae and Cinnamomeae, we note that all 19 pseudofossils were recovered within crown‐group Lauraceae with high support. Therefore, given our extensive outgroup sampling among Laurales, we are confident that we can use our morphological data set to identify crown‐group members of Lauraceae. Higher‐level relationships within Lauraceae may be accurately inferred using the data set, but such relationships should ideally have high node support and additional lines of supporting evidence.

Phylogenetic relationships of lauraceous fossil flowers and taxa ideal for node calibrations

While there is some uncertainty in the relationships of many of the fossil flowers included in our analyses, some of these fossils are ideal for use as node calibrations in divergence time analyses (Table 3). Four rogue taxa (Cohongarootonia hispida, Lauranthus futabensis, Potomacanthus lobatus, and Treptostemon domingensis) were identified via RoguePlots. Multiple placements in Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae were recovered for each of the four fossil flowers, which lowered posterior support for the node of the total group of Lauraceae. Comparisons to both Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae were previously discussed for the Early Cretaceous flowers of Cohongarootonia hispida and Potomacanthus lobatus (von Balthazar et al., 2007, 2011), and a previous phylogenetic analysis of Cohongarootonia hispida recovered it as sister to a clade comprising Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae (von Balthazar et al., 2011). The two fossil flowers share several characters with the two families, which likely resulted in the positions within Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae in our analyses. Moreover, the unique combination of floral characters present in the Early Cretaceous taxa are not seen in extant Lauraceae or Hernandiaceae, indicating that these fossil flowers may represent extinct lineages closely related to these families. Monimiaceae, Hernandiaceae, and Lauraceae comprise a clade that remains unresolved (Renner and Chanderbali, 2000; Renner, 1999, 2005; Doyle and Endress, 2010; Soltis et al., 2011; Zuntini et al., 2024; Helmstetter et al., 2025). Therefore, following recommendations of Massoni et al. (2015), it is conservative to use Cohongarootonia hispida and Potomacanthus lobatus (Early to Middle Albian, 113–107.7 Ma) as molecular divergence time calibrations for the stem node of the clade comprising Lauraceae, Hernandiaceae, and Monimiaceae.

Lauranthus futabensis from the Late Cretaceous of Japan (Takahashi et al., 2001) and Treptostemon domingensis from Dominican amber (Chambers et al., 2012) are known from staminate flowers. While the general floral organization and androecial morphology of these fossil flowers show a clear affinity with Lauraceae, additional placements in Hernandiaceae with low support (<10%) were shown in our RoguePlots. The two fossil flowers share the presence of equal sized tepal whorls and structurally imperfect flowers, a combination of character states also scored in our morphological dataset for sampled species of Hernandiaceae, that likely caused topological instability in our analyses. Because both of these fossils are relatively young, they are not suitable as calibration fossils for the Hernandiaceae‐Lauraceae‐Monimiaceae clade.

The remaining 13 fossil flowers can be assigned to the total group of Lauraceae with greater certainty. However, seven of the fossils (Extroparvum occidentale, Cascolaurus burmitis, Microlaurus perigynus, Neusenia tetrasporangiata, Persea avita, Perseanthus crossmanensis, Tinaflora beardiae) show moderate phylogenetic uncertainty within the total group of the family. Multiple phylogenetic positions across the phylogeny were recovered for these fossil flowers. This phylogenetic uncertainty is likely caused by high levels of random homoplasy in our data set (see Coiro, 2024). For instance, almost all of these fossils have unequally sized tepal whorls and sagittate staminodes, a combination present in many species scattered throughout the phylogeny of extant Lauraceae. While the seven aforementioned fossil flowers cannot be confidently assigned to the crown group, they do represent the divergence of the Lauraceae total group. Therefore, the oldest fossil flower of this group, Cascolaurus burmitis from Cenomanian deposits (98.79 ± 0.62 Ma; Shi et al., 2012; Poinar, 2017), could be conservatively employed as a minimum calibration for the stem node of Lauraceae.

Six fossil taxa analyzed in this work were recovered within the crown group of Lauraceae with low phylogenetic uncertainty: all four Mauldinia species, Pragocladus lauroides, and a fossil Cryptocarya species. The Miocene (14.8 ± 0.6 Ma to 14.7 ± 0.4 Ma) fossil flower Cryptocarya sp. (Beurel et al., 2024) was consistently recovered nested within the crown group of the Crypotocaryeae tribe, sister to extant Cryptocarya, with high posterior support in the single fossil analysis (see Appendix S6: Figure S37). Phylogenetic positions recovered for the fossil Cryptocarya were restricted to Cryptocaryeae according to the RoguePlots, which agrees with the previously hypothesized systematic affinity of the fossil flower (Beurel et al., 2024). Results from our pseudofossilization analyses additionally show that Cryptocaryeae is not a problematic group. Therefore, our analyses strongly support the assignment of the extinct Cryptocarya sp. to Cryptocaryeae. Based on our analyses, the fossil can be employed as a novel fossil calibration at the crown node of the tribe.

All four Mauldinia species and Pragocladus lauroides were recovered along the stem branch of Cassytha with high support and few alternative positions as indicated in the RoguePlots. These fossils are from Cenomanian–Turonian (Late Cretaceous; 100.5–89.8 Ma) deposits in North America, Europe, and central Asia (Drinnan et al., 1990; Eklund and Kvaček, 1998; Kvaček and Eklund, 2003; Frumin et al., 2004; Viehofen et al., 2008). It is worth noting that our pseudofossilization results do not suggest Cassytha as a problematic group. Furthermore, our single fossil Bayesian phylogenetic analyses recover these fossil flowers among Cassytheae, with the exception of Mauldinia mirabilis, which was recovered as sister to the clade comprising Neocinnamomeae and Caryodaphnopsidae. Within our morphological data set, stigma shape (character 42) was scored as wide for Mauldinia mirabilis, while the same character was scored as spheroidal for both extant Cassytha species. Stigma shape was scored as missing for the remaining Mauldinia species due to incomplete preservation. Within our sampled species of Neocinnamomeae and Caryodaphnopsidae, wide stigmas are present and a differentiated perianth is present in Caryodaphnopsis, which likely resulted in the placement of Mauldinia mirabilis as sister to the two tribes. Nonetheless, our analyses placed the five fossil flowers within crown‐group Lauraceae, which agrees with their previously hypothesized systematic affinities (Drinnan et al., 1990; Eklund and Kvaček, 1998; Kvaček and Eklund, 2003; Frumin et al., 2004; Viehofen et al., 2008). However, our results for the position of Mauldinia within Lauraceae conflict with two previous phylogenetic analyses (Doyle and Endress, 2010; Schönenberger et al., 2020).

Doyle and Endress (2010) conducted a series of careful phylogenetic analyses that assessed the positions of fossil angiosperms while using a matrix that sampled many angiosperm families and floral and vegetative characters. Families or subfamilies were used as operational taxonomic units and inferred plesiomorphic states were scored for characters that varied within the family (Doyle and Endress 2010). The analyses of Doyle and Endress (2010) recovered Mauldinia mirabilis as sister to a clade comprising Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae. Doyle and Endress (2010) discussed four characters (and their states) that likely resulted in this position: (1) paratracheal parenchyma (character 13), (2) floral base (character 48), (3) tepal differentiation (character 57), and (4) endosperm in the mature seed (character 138).

Doyle and Endress (2010) scored paratracheal parenchyma (character 13) in Mauldinia mirabilis as absent or scanty as seen in fossil wood associated with some of its inflorescence axes (Herendeen, 1991) while scoring the same character as well developed in Lauraceae and Hernandicaeae. However, paratracheal parenchyma is scant to absent in some genera (Actinodaphne, Caryodaphnopsis, Dahlghrenodendron, Licaria) of Lauraceae (van der Werff and Richter, 1985; Richter and Van Wyk, 1990; Richter, 1985, 1990) and Illigera of Hernandiaceae (Shutts, 1959). A floral base (character 48) with a hypanthium and superior ovary present was scored for Mauldinia mirabilis, while a floral base with a partially or completely inferior ovary was scored for Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae. A backbone tree that shows Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae as sister groups was used for the analyses of Doyle and Endress (2010). Therefore, a floral base with an inferior ovary was inferred as the plesiomorphic state of Lauraceae since Hypodaphnis is sister to all other Lauraceae and has an inferior ovary with epigynous flowers (Rohwer and Rudolph, 2005; Doyle and Endress, 2010), but almost all other extant species of the family have superior ovaries. Tepal differentiation (character 57) was scored as distinctly sepaloid and petaloid for Mauldinia mirabilis, while the character was scored as more or less sepaloid in Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae (Doyle and Endress, 2010). Flowers of Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae typically have undifferentiated perianth whorls, but the outer whorl of tepals can be significantly smaller than the inner whorl across different tribes of Lauraceae (Kubitzki, 1993; Rohwer, 1993). Furthermore, these authors scored endosperm in the mature seed (character 138) as present in Mauldinia mirabilis (see Drinnan et al., 1990) and absent in Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae. Endosperm is present in the ovules and immature seeds of Lauraceae and Hernandiaceae, but is absorbed once seeds and fruit reach maturity (Sastri, 1962; Heo and Tobe, 1995; Heo et al., 1998; Kimoto and Tobe, 2008). Moreover, as seen in the immature fruit of Mauldinia mirabilis, ovules and seeds of immature fruits of Cassytha have a prominent amount of endosperm present (Sastri, 1962). Overall, floral and wood morphology of Mauldinia fits within the range of variation seen in Lauraceae, which supports the recovered placement of this mid‐Cretaceous taxon in crown‐group Lauraceae.

Phyloscan analyses of Schönenberger et al. (2020) included Mauldinia mirabilis and recovered multiple most parsimonious positions within the Lauraceae, Hernandiaceae, and Monimiaceae clade for this fossil. The phyloscan floral data set is focused on floral organization, and many of the characters scored are shared among Mauldinia, Lauraceae, Hernandiaceae, and Monimiaceae. For example, extant species sampled in the phyloscan for each family have radially symmetric flowers with a whorled perianth, free stamens that dehisce by flaps, and free carpels bearing one ovule. However, the floral morphology of Mauldinia greatly differs from that of Hernandiaceae and Monimiaceae (see Appendix S3). Moreover, diagnostic characters such as the presence of paired staminal appendages were not sampled in the phyloscan data set. Paired staminal appendages are only present on the inner whorl of stamens in Mauldinia and essentially all extant Lauraceae, while paired staminal appendages are typically present throughout the entire androecium or completely absent in Hernandiaceae and Monimiaceae (Kubitzki, 1993; Philipson, 1993). Based on our character analysis and considering the distribution of characters in a matrix that samples significantly more Lauraceae taxa, the recovered position of Mauldinia within the crown group of Lauraceae is the one best supported by the data. We suggest that the oldest fossil flowers, Mauldinia mirabilis and M. angustiloba, from early to middle Cenomanian deposits (100.5–97.2 Ma; Drinnan et al., 1990; Viehofen et al., 2008) would make appropriate fossil calibrations at the crown node of Lauraceae.

Cretaceous diversification of crown‐group Lauraceae

Previous estimates of the age of the Lauraceae crown‐group range from 157.96 to 56.39 Ma (Michalak et al., 2010; Magallón et al., 2015; Li et al., 2020; Ramírez‐Barahona et al., 2020; Silvestro et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2023; Zuntini et al., 2024). Difficulty distinguishing between stem‐ and crown‐group fossils, as well as the use of a wide range of taxon sampling and fossil calibration schemes across the different studies, has likely caused much of this uncertainty. Before this present study, the fossil flower Potomacanthus lobatus, recovered from early to middle Albian (Early Cretaceous; 113–107 Ma) deposits, was accepted as the earliest record of Lauraceae (von Balthazar et al., 2007). However, results from our phylogenetic analyses indicate that Potomacanthus lobatus cannot be confidently placed within the crown group or total group of Lauraceae. Our phylogenetic analyses identified six fossil flowers as crown‐group members of the family. Based on our analyses, two Mauldinia species recovered from early to middle Cenomanian deposits (100.5–97.2 Ma; Drinnan et al., 1990; Viehofen et al., 2008) provide the oldest record of crown‐group Lauraceae. Thus, a minimal clade age of 97.2 Ma can be considered for the crown‐group diversification of Lauraceae. Although phylogenetic uncertainty prevents a confident assignment to the crown group, five additional fossil flowers from Late Cretaceous deposits, including the newly described Extroparvum occidentale, can be assigned to the total group of Lauraceae with certainty. Overall, our results highlight the fact that the Late Cretaceous was a critical time for the early evolution and diversification of Lauraceae.

CONCLUSIONS

Extroparvum occidentale represents a new genus and species of Lauraceae from Coniacian deposits of western North America, which adds to the Cretaceous diversity of the family. Our phylogenetic analyses recover Extroparvum occidentale and 12 previously described fossil flowers, spanning in age from the Late Cretaceous to the Miocene, within the total group of Lauraceae. Among the analyzed fossils, Mauldinia and the extinct Cryptocarya sp. were identified as reliable calibration points for the crown node of Lauraceae and crown node of Cryptocaryeae, respectively. While relationships of some fossil flowers remain uncertain, tip‐dating analyses that integrate stratigraphic data and incorporate fossils as tips on the phylogeny may allow us to better reconcile phylogenetic relationships (Lee and Yates, 2018). Nonetheless, our novel morphological data set provides an important foundation for elucidating phylogenetic relationships of lauraceous fossil flowers, which is critical for understanding the early evolutionary history of Lauraceae.
