AIoT-infused sustainable commerce architecture integrating adaptive green intelligence and autonomous audit pipelines for sustainable operations
Authors: Feisal Hadi Masmali, Ziad Hmwd A Almtiri, Abdulwahab Mujalli, Tahir Hakim

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid growth of digital commerce has created an even bigger appetite for operational intelligence that is sustainable, transparent, and carbon-efficient. This work proposes AGIA2 (AI-driven Green Intelligence and Audit), an AIoT-infused sustainable commerce architecture that integrates adaptive green intelligence with autonomous audit pipelines to offer real-time environmental optimizations and Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG)-compliant decisions. The method utilizes a mixed multi-modal dataset comprising e-commerce transaction logs, IoT supply chain sensor streams, carbon intensity time series, and audit/ESG compliance indicators. Such a dataset allows extensive modeling of the digital-physical-environmental interactions in modern commerce ecosystems. The proposed solution uses machine learning such as SVM, RF, and LR; deep learning like CNN and RNN/LSTM; and hybrid ensemble to derive sustainability scores, detect anomalies, quantify the operational emissions, and automate compliance. Adaptive Green Intelligence Module: This does carbon forecasting, energy optimization, and dynamic inference routing between edge-cloud layers. Autonomous Audit Pipeline will handle real-time audit checks, ESG rule validations, and explainable anomaly detection via SHAP/LIME. The proposed AGIA2 framework demonstrates superior performance compared to baseline models, including standalone Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression (LR), and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) models, across key evaluation metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score in experiments with an accuracy of 96.1%, an F1-score of 96.0%, and a ROC-AUC of 0.98. It greatly decreases the false negatives of sustainability violations and increases audit confidence ratings. In the correlation and confusion matrix studies, there are significant dependencies between IoT activity, carbon intensity, and sustainability categories. The AGIA2 framework advances sustainable commerce towards a unified, real-time, AIoT-enabled method to run businesses sustainably, be carbon emission conscious, and audit themselves. Unlike fragmented AI-only or IoT-only approaches, AGIA2 embeds sustainability scoring and ESG-aligned auditability as core system functions rather than post hoc reporting layers.Keywords: AIoT architecture, Adaptive green intelligence, Sustainability analytics, ESG compliance automation, Carbon-aware optimization, Ensemble learning, Explainable AI, Sustainable digital commerceSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences, Mathematics and computing

In this area, smart algorithms allow connected devices to make decisions based on real-time data. When it comes to sustainable commerce, adding AIoT creates a one-of-a-kind chance to connect large-scale digital deals with operations that are energy-efficient, supply lines that are carbon-aware, and self-enforcing rules. Literature review

In this section we will discuss and analyze the existing work in this field and what gaps are remained to address. With increased adoption of AI-enhanced e-commerce and Internet-connected supply lines, more study is being done on how to make digital operations more sustainable. More recently, studies have shown that environmental intelligence and openness need to be added to commerce environments in order to make transactions more efficient, transportation more efficient, and customer behavior analytics more important. Machine Learning and Deep Learning have become two of the main forces behind this change. They enable the use of multi-modal shopping and IoT sensor data to perform predictions that are less power-consuming, detect problems so that regulations may be followed, and perform optimizations that are environmentally friendly. Most ML-based efforts at sustainable trade involve forecasts, estimating carbon emissions, fraud detection, demand prediction, and optimal use of resources. These methods generally operate with disjointed datasets, making it difficult for them to comprehend the whole sustainable e-commerce world.

Deep Learning research has expanded into transportation, carbon-emission-aware processes, and AIoT-enabled solutions for sustainability. The various DL architectures such as CNNs, RNNs, LSTMs, and attention-based models have proven to be superior in time-series forecasting, supply chain anomaly detection, energy consumption forecasting, and fusion of heterogeneous data over the IoT networks. However, DL-based systems are usually difficult to interpret and consume a great quantity of computational resources, hence challenging in various IoT edge deployments3, while recent secure deep learning frameworks integrating cryptographic protection and AI-based anomaly detection have demonstrated improved robustness and data preservation capabilities4. Ensemble and Explainable AI methods are increasingly appealing to green trade and digital accounting for overcoming issues of scale and readability. Ensemble models can handle high-dimensional cross-modal datasets much better and provide more stable predictions5. XAI methods, including SHAP, LIME, and rule-based explainers, which are developed for clear decisions on sustainability, will enable the validation of AI-driven carbon-aware suggestions and outputs by businesses and regulators or accountants for outlier detection6. Despite all these enhancements, there has not yet been any research that integrates ML, DL, ensemble learning, and XAI into a single AIoT-driven sustainable commerce design with self-checking pipelines. Recent nature-inspired metaheuristic optimization algorithms further highlight the need for efficient and scalable optimization strategies in AI-driven systems7. Previous studies also utilized separated datasets instead of mixed multi-modal datasets that combine e-commerce logs, IoT streams, carbon intensity data, and ESG/audit records. Further the existing works evaluates sustainability or logistics performance without explicitly modeling audit confidence or compliance reliability as a measurable system output.

The gap, therefore, points to the serious relevance of a comprehensive multi-layer sustainable commerce design based on the AIoT systems, green intelligence modules, and self-checking audit processes underlying this study. Accordingly, this study targets a unified design that combines multimodal learning, carbon-aware intelligence, and autonomous auditing as co-equal components of sustainable commerce operations rather than independent modules.

A.Machine Learning (ML)-Based Approaches

Machine Learning (ML)-Based Approaches

Machine learning techniques have been very important in making digital trade more sustainable, enabling predictive analytics, optimization, finding outliers, and tracking the environment. A mix of regression-and classification-based machine learning methods has been applied to predict carbon emissions, find fake transactions, and make packing use more efficient1. This has helped make trade ecosystems more sustainable and trustworthy. Random Forests, Support Vector Machines, and Gradient Boosted Trees are in wide usage because they are straightforward to understand and can combine various types of data, including transportation information, energy consumption signs, and product lifecycle characteristics. With sensor-based tracking data, ML has been used in sustainable shipping to find the best routes and cut down on emissions. It is also very important to avoid overstocking and waste in the supply chain, and machine learning can help with this. ML models also help with compliance and auditing by finding strange things in transaction logs and creating reports on sustainability. However, most of these ML systems, which are already in existence, don’t work with IoT sensor networks, carbon intelligence modules, or real-time compliance engines because they were built on separate architectures. Because of this problem, people are working on mixed AIoT designs that blend machine learning with deep learning and self-auditing.

B.Deep Learning (DL)-Based Approaches

Deep Learning (DL)-Based Approaches

Deep Learning helps in sustainable commerce by modeling complex patterns within large amounts of trade data, sensor streams, records of carbon intensities, and transportation tracks. Many different people utilize the CNNs and RNNs to estimate demands, find outliers in their supply chains, and predict resultant environmental impacts for their businesses in an environmentally conscious manner5. Similarly, the LSTM and GRU design architectures are best suited for precise time series-based carbon emission prediction and IoT-driven energy usage. New AIoT frameworks combine deep learning with multiple sensors to monitor, in real-time, fleet pollution, energy usage in warehouses, and traces of the product lifecycle by2. Advanced transformer-based models combined with metaheuristic optimization have further improved forecasting accuracy and feature selection in energy-aware systems8. Model architectures based on transformers are also gaining interest in multi-model trade analytics and event forecasting driven by IoT. Even so, with such advantages, DL models have some critical defects: they demand high computational capacity; the decisions of DL models on the determination of sustainability are often not transparent; and they do not function effectively in edge–device environments. These defects evidence the importance of hybrid systems that integrate deep learning and lightweight machine learning models and ensemble and XAI techniques. Such hybrid systems should support energy-efficient, explainable AIoT processes for long-term business. Therefore, hybrid edge–cloud routing and explainable decision outputs become important for practical deployment in sustainable commerce auditing contexts.

C.Ensemble and Explainable AI (XAI)-Based Approaches

Ensemble and Explainable AI (XAI)-Based Approaches

Ensemble learning and XAI methods have become particularly significant to ensure the interpretability and trustworthiness of sustainable commerce systems. Finally, ensemble methods, such as Random Forests, Gradient Boosting, Stacking, and Hybrid Deep-ML combinations, stabilize predictions, reducing bias and generalizing results to a wider range of datasets6. When applied for emission prediction, detection of issues in the supply chain, or compliance error evaluation in sustainable logistics, an ensemble model is more reliable compared to a single model. They can also be reused in enhancing sustainable commerce in general since they are capable of fusing financial, sensor-based, and environmental features. Explainable AI adds a much-needed level of accountability since it tells auditors and other interested parties why AI makes choices regarding carbon-aware optimization, suggestions on sustainability, and alerts to strange behavior. In settings requiring auditing, such techniques as SHAP, LIME, attention maps, and rule-based explainers provide better clarity and reduce the risks of automated bias. While ensemble and XAI methods on their own improve speed and readability, the present study stands out as the first that combines these methods with AIoT pipelines, carbon intelligence, and autonomous accounting in one design9. While there have been substantial improvements in technologies relating to AIoT-driven solutions, sustainable e-commerce analytics, and explainable AI methods, several important areas still require investigation. Current studies mostly concentrate on specific issues such as building a model of predictions, using IoT data to monitor processes, or using an explanation of the results. Moreover, few researchers have considered integrating multimodal data, including transactional, environmental, and sensor-based sources. Current practices also lack effective methods of obtaining explainable results in terms of sustainability and calculating scores to assess the credibility and trustworthiness of audit findings. It means that existing tools cannot be used in practice because of their lack of evaluation criteria related to performance and transparency. To address these limitations, this research will present the AGIA2 model that incorporates AIoT data streams, hybrid ensemble learning algorithms, and AI explainability methods. In particular, the study will develop new components, such as the Green Score and Audit Trust Score, allowing for measuring performance and explainability simultaneously. To ensure that model predictions are interpretable and actionable, explainability techniques such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations) are integrated into the audit decision pipeline.

Specifically, SHAP values are used to quantify the contribution of individual features toward a given prediction, enabling the identification of key drivers behind sustainability violations or anomaly flags. LIME complements this by generating local approximations of model behavior for individual instances.

Research methodology

The study methodology employs a multi-faceted, data-centric experimental framework that incorporates AIoT-enabled sensors, machine learning analytics, deep learning architectures, ensemble learning, and autonomous auditing procedures. In the following sections, the whole methodological framework has been divided into four parts, which are interconnected: building a hybrid dataset, preprocessing and feature engineering, development and training of the model, and performance evaluation and comparative studies. The basic understanding of the research method and technique may also understand from the following Fig. 2. The methodology is designed to reflect realistic sustainable commerce operations by jointly modeling transactional, physical (IoT), and environmental (carbon-intensity) signals under a unified learning and audit pipeline.

AIoT-Infused Sustainable commerce System.

First, the e-commerce transaction logs are combined into one hybrid dataset: IoT supply-chain sensor streams and carbon intensity data, along with compliance indicators related to ESG and audits. Such a multimodal dataset helps build models that can represent how sustainable commerce works digitally, physically, and environmentally. In order to ensure that diverse data is coherent, cleaning, removal of anomalies, alignment of timestamps, and normalization are performed. Feature engineering involves extracting transactional features, event signatures derived from sensors, carbon intensity features, logistical circumstances, and audit policy tags. Accordingly, it establishes a baseline of several machine learning models such as Support Vector Machines, Random Forest, and Logistic Regression to provide starting points for comparisons. The work leverages models based on structured transactional data to produce concrete decision limits, especially for tasks related to anomalous elements’ categorization. To reduce evaluation bias, the same preprocessing, feature definitions, and data splits are consistently applied to all baseline models and the proposed ensemble pipeline.

Traditional machine learning and deep learning models such as SVM, Random Forest, Logistic Regression, and LSTM are employed as base learners within the proposed framework. Detailed algorithmic foundations are well established in the literature and are therefore not repeated here; instead, this work focuses on their integration within the AGIA2 architecture. Finally, the models are tested using a wide range of performance measures, such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, and specificity. Other ways to evaluate include confusion matrices, correlation matrices, and comparing the results to prior research. The experimental setting will take place in a multi-tier environment that simulates AIoT situations, with calculations spread out across edge and cloud levels. This approach ensures that sustainable sustainable commerce activities can be reiterated, are analytically sound, and are useable in real life. The evaluation focuses on reducing false negatives for sustainability and compliance violations, since undetected violations are high-impact in ESG contexts.

A.Practical Interpretability for Audit Transparency

Practical Interpretability for Audit Transparency

Although explainability techniques like SHAP and LIME are popular, their outputs may occasionally be difficult for non-technical users to comprehend. For this reason, AGIA2 includes an additional step where the model outputs are mapped into auditor-understandable audit statements. The method entails using a sorting function on the SHAP values and only focusing on the highest-ranking factors affecting predictions and filtering out irrelevant features on LIME output. Then, the outputs from both explainability techniques are converted to auditor-comprehensible statements.

For instance, when there is an identified sustainability anomaly, the system outputs the following explanation:

“Audit Flag Triggered: Sustainability anomaly in Warehouse A because the following factors contributed to the reduction of the sustainability score: (i) unusually high energy consumption (+0.42 SHAP contribution), (ii) high carbon intensity (+0.31) especially during peak time periods, and (iii) exceeded emission threshold (+0.18).” The system offers several visual representations to help the auditor understand the anomaly, such as bar charts indicating the important features or decision indicators like red flags for high-risk audits, yellow for moderate risk, and green flags for low-risk anomalies. In this way, explainability techniques not only make the models’ internal operations easy to understand but also provide auditor-friendly interpretations of their decision.

B.Algorithm of the Proposed Framework

Algorithm of the Proposed Framework

The proposed sustainable commerce architecture integrates classical machine learning, deep learning, and hybrid ensemble approaches into a multi-algorithmic pipeline to model transactional, sensor-driven, and carbon-intensity data. The pipeline commences with the use of Support Vector Machines and Logistic Regression as simple linear classifiers. They make it simpler to understand the limits of the decisions while performing tasks such as categorization for sustainability and anomaly detection. Going beyond the baseline, RF merges a number of decision trees to find nonlinear links in datasets that are not all the same. This works on high-dimensional IoT and carbon-aware data streams through CNNs, which help extract spatial and hierarchical patterns from product, logistics, and sensor properties. On the other hand, how things change over time can be represented by RNNs, comprising variations such as LSTM, considering changes in carbon intensity, energy signals from edge devices, and so forth, and events in the sequence of logistics. This is further combined through a hybrid ensemble fusion layer. These weighted outputs from the ML and DL models help in creating sustainability scores, anomaly identification, and carbon-aware recommendations. Multi-model architecture ensures strength, noise tolerance for multi-modal sustainable commerce data, and generalization. This layered strategy facilitates more open operations that permit independent audits and improve sustainable commerce in AIoT settings. To improve operational interpretability, the pipeline outputs are explicitly mapped to two system-level indicators: a Green Score (Gₛ) for sustainability and an Audit Trust Score (Ta) for compliance confidence, enabling traceable decision-making.

C.Problem Statement

Problem Statement

Now it is very important to discuss the problem statement as now we are in a state to set-up the experiment and all the technical and mathematical notation of algorithm by which we will do this. So, in this section we precisely discuss the problem statement in confined way. Fast growth in e-commerce has made digital supply chains more difficult to operate, consume more energy, emit more carbon, and become more prone to auditing. Thus far, AI, IoT, and data-driven optimization approaches have come a long way in their development but are not yet fully integrated within the sustainable commerce ecosystem. In most current systems, sustainability, carbon intelligence, and audit compliance are separate parts of a fragmented system instead of becoming parts of one real-time operating system. Often, Machine Learning and Deep Learning models are independently used for forecasting logistics or detecting fraud, respectively; however, they do not represent systemic integration with the IoT sensor networks, carbon-intensity indicators, and ESG compliance standards. Similarly, current IoT-based supply chain designs are more focused on monitoring rather than optimizing sustainability through algorithms or automated audits. Besides this, it is also the lack of hybrid multimodal datasets that would combine e-commerce transactions, IoT sensor streams, energy consumption data, carbon-intensity profiles, and audit/ESG indicators that makes it hard to test unified sustainable commerce systems in real life. Without this kind of connection, making carbon-aware decisions, reporting on sustainability in a clear manner, and tracking audits become quite difficult for a business. As a result of this, today’s digital commerce systems are not able to fully meet international standards of environmental responsibility, adhering to regulations, and operational efficiency. The main gap this research seeks to fill is in providing a unified AIoT-driven architecture for sustainable commerce by integrating green intelligence, autonomous audit pipelines, and multi-modal data streams to enable real-time optimization of sustainability and trustworthy operational transparency. The absence of explicit, system-level outputs that quantify sustainability and audit confidence makes it difficult to operationalize ESG governance beyond static KPI reporting.

Green Score (GS)

Green Score (GS)

Green Score is an indicator of environmental sustainability of each transaction based on an environmental score ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores denote environmentally sustainable practices in transaction processes. The formula for computing GS involves the normalization of several environmental indicators using min-max technique followed by a weighted aggregation of these variables. These indicators are:

Carbon intensity: kg CO₂/transaction

Energy usage: kWh

Distance traveled: km

Interpretation of Score:

Interpretation of Score:

GS ≥ 75: Environmentally efficient (green compliant)

50 ≤ GS < 75: Environmentally moderate (needs monitoring)

GS < 50: Environmentally inefficient (high-risk category)

Audit Trust Score (ATS)

Audit Trust Score (ATS)

The audit trust score is a measure of confidence in the environmental integrity and reliability of transactions ranging from 0 to 100. The score combines three main factors, namely model prediction, history of compliance, and deviation from established operations patterns.

Interpretation of Score:

Interpretation of Score:

ATS ≥ 80: High confidence (audit not needed)

60 ≤ ATS < 80: Medium confidence (flagged for review)

ATS < 60: Low confidence (audit needed)

Calibration and Threshold Selection:

The selection of thresholds for GS and ATS relies on validation set distributions and percentile calibration. This process entails finding the appropriate balance between false positive and false negative cases in order to have efficient anomaly detection without causing undue audit processes. ROC analysis is employed in determining the efficacy of the thresholds chosen.

G.Overfitting Mitigation and Concept Drift Handling

Overfitting Mitigation and Concept Drift Handling

Various precautions have been taken to ensure the robustness and generalization capability of the proposed AGIA2 method, especially during the model training process. This includes using stratified train/validation/test splits (70/15/15) to guarantee the balanced distribution of samples between normal and abnormal data. In addition, k-fold cross-validation (with k=5) has been adopted to check the consistency of model performance under different data splits. Regarding regularization, various regularizations have been used in each type of model separately to avoid overfitting. For example, L2 regularization was used in logistic regression and neural networks, while dropout was used for deep learning models. Decision trees and Random Forest models have also been limited in terms of tree depth and minimal sample sizes for each node to avoid overcomplexity.

Moreover, concept drift occurs in many real-world problems, especially when the problem environment is dynamic, like in an e-commerce and ESG case where carbon intensity patterns continuously change. The AGIA2 framework has therefore been designed to detect such concept drifts in its architecture by continuously monitoring statistical divergences like Kullback-Leibler divergence and population stability index.In the case of deviation being noticed, an adaptive training procedure is activated. In other words, the training parameters are readjusted, with the help of fresh data, thus keeping the system updated with changes in operating and environmental conditions. On the other hand, anomalies thresholds are set in accordance with fresh data trends, in order to keep false auditing minimum. Such approaches guarantee that accuracy of the results will not be related to overfitting but rather to generalization abilities of the model.

The transaction dataset comprises around 50,000 data points that include attributes such as order value, category of the product, delivery distance, and timestamps of fulfilling the order. The IoT-based sensor readings include around 120,000 observations that track energy usage, temperature, and warehouse activity levels in a periodic manner. The carbon intensity dataset consists of regional emission factors that are timestamped according to the timeline of transaction data.

Data Integration:

All three data types were integrated through the timestamping process, where IoT and carbon intensity were averaged out in fixed-hour windows and matched to corresponding transaction data. Missing data points were replaced using median substitution for static attributes and forward fill for time series data.

Definition of Labels:

The labels for sustainability and anomalies were determined using both rule-based thresholds and metrics obtained from data. For example, the transaction was defined as ESG anomaly (audit_flag = 1) if one of the following conditions held true:

Carbon emission associated with the transaction was greater than 500 kg CO₂ equivalent,

There were deviations in energy consumption exceeding 30% compared to historical baseline emissions,

Transaction took place under the circumstances associated with a high carbon intensity period (top 20% of emission levels). In all other scenarios, the label would be defined as ‘compliant’ (audit_flag = 0). The chosen thresholds represent sustainability metrics and were set according to industry standards and heuristics.

Distribution of Classes and Treatment of Imbalance:

As the ESG violations are rare phenomena, there is a class imbalance in the dataset. The distribution is 18% anomalies vs. 82% compliant transactions. The problem with the class imbalance was addressed by applying SMOTE oversampling technique during the training phase in order to increase the number of samples for the minority class. Besides that, class weights were taken into account during the loss function optimization. Test set evaluation was conducted for original data, not the one with oversampling.

Results and discussion

The experimental evaluation demonstrates that the AGIA2 Framework significantly enhances sustainability score, anomaly detection precision, and audit reliability within the multi-modal hybrid dataset. The best among the simple ML algorithms was the Random Forest (RF), which could deal with features quite different from each other and, as expected, was very accurate and correct for classification tasks. IoT and carbon intensity time-series data did really well on CNN and RNN models, respectively, which were able to extract geographical and temporal patterns relevant to transport issues, carbon spikes, and operational inefficiencies. The hybrid ensemble outperformed all the algorithms individually, with the best accuracy, F1-score, and ROC-AUC. This improvement indicates that no single learning paradigm is sufficient to capture the full diversity of transactional, sensor-based, and environmental signals present in sustainable commerce systems. To further validate the robustness of the proposed model, a separate evaluation was conducted using only the real-world portion of the dataset. The AGIA2 framework achieved an accuracy of 93.4% under these conditions, demonstrating that the high overall performance is not solely dependent on synthetic data augmentation but reflects the model’s ability to generalize to real operational environments.

The confusion matrix indicates that there are far fewer false negatives associated with sustainability breaches, while the correlation matrix demonstrates solid interrelations exist among warehouse energy consumption, carbon intensity, and sustainability labeling. The ensemble model performed well for all measures, thus suggesting it would generalize well on the test data. The findings prove that the AGIA2 architecture is appropriate for aggregating ML, DL, and IoT inputs toward decisions that ultimately benefit the environment while automating audits in an eco-friendly way. Experimental findings denote that putting together AIoT sensing, deep learning modeling, and ensemble fusion contributes a lot to increasing intelligence in sustainable commerce and operational transparency.

The reduction of false negatives is critical in ESG contexts, where undetected violations can lead to regulatory penalties, reputational damage, or unreported environmental impact. The model was evaluated on an imbalanced test set reflecting real-world conditions, ensuring that performance metrics are not biased by synthetic resampling techniques.

The high accuracy achieved by the AGIA2 framework is supported by rigorous validation strategies, including cross-validation and regularization techniques, as well as evaluation on unseen test data. Furthermore, the integration of concept drift detection and adaptive retraining mechanisms ensures that the model maintains its performance over time, even in the presence of dynamic ESG and operational conditions. The integration of simplified XAI outputs ensures that interpretability enhances, rather than hinders, audit transparency. All performance measures mentioned in this research paper are calculated using the test set, which has been kept aside for testing purposes and has not been used in any way for training or validating the models. In order to provide a measure of variability, standard deviations are also calculated along with the means of all performance measures. In addition to that, 95% confidence intervals are also provided to ensure the credibility of results. Further validation on the assertion that the traditional models are inefficient in handling time series data was conducted through an additional experiment with the AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model. The ARIMA model was chosen based on its nature as a conventional statistical method for time series analysis. The efficiency of the ARIMA model in terms of accuracy and ability to capture temporal dependencies, especially in dynamic situations, was measured by comparing it with the LSTM-based model proposed in this research framework. From the experiments, it was clear that ARIMA demonstrated low efficiency compared with deep learning-based approaches for modeling time-dependent sustainability metrics in the proposed system.

A.Discussion

Discussion

These experimental results emphasize the crucial role that is played by multi-modal data fusion and hybrid AI models in enabling sustainable commerce operations to become more sustainable and transparent. The superior performance of the integrated ensemble system shows that no single model can uncover the various patterns hidden in transaction logs, IoT sensor data, and carbon-intensity signals. Instead, such a combination of classical ML, deep learning, and audit-driven features provides results that are more consistent and easier to understand. The high level of accuracy and specificity of the AGIA2 system is very important in terms of sustainability audits, since false negatives may lead to lawbreaking or hidden carbon costs. Explainable AI further opens things up by allowing auditors and decision-makers to understand why the technology identifies emissions anomalies or ESG infractions. This interpretability is particularly important in regulatory and compliance-driven environments, where automated decisions must be supported by traceable evidence. The suggested AGIA2 model shows a considerable decrease in false negatives in comparison to existing baseline models. In particular, the hybrid ensemble approach outperformed the single Random Forest model in terms of false negatives by about 35–40%, while in relation to the SVM approach, the number of false negatives decreased by 30%. These results can be explained by the use of multimodal data and an effective process of ensembling, which improves the performance of the model in detecting minor and borderline cases of violations related to sustainability. False negatives pose a significant problem in ESG audits because they may indicate problems in compliance.

Reason of Low R2 and High Model Performance

Reason of Low R2 and High Model Performance

It can be concluded that relatively low R2 values (0.1–0.2) reported in Fig. 5 suggest weak linear association between specific pairs of features. Nevertheless, it should be highlighted that R2 reflects only linear dependency of variables and does not consider the possibility of more complicated associations in multidimensional space. As mentioned above, the AGIA2 model employs a hybrid approach combining machine and deep learning approaches. The models utilized in the project, such as trees and neural networks, are capable of capturing non-linear associations, feature interaction effects, and underlying patterns which are impossible to detect via pairwise correlation analysis.

Therefore, weak correlations obtained in this case demonstrate the limitations of linear statistics in capturing complicated multimodal relations existing between the variables of interest. The presented ensemble model uses different data sources and extracts meaningful insights by considering joint representation of various modalities, namely transactional, IoT, and carbon time-series data. Finally, feature engineering procedures performed prior to modeling, namely normalization, aggregation, and construction of sustainability indicators, improve the ratio between signals and noise, which leads to the model’s high accuracy despite weak linear associations between the features. Therefore, the apparent discrepancy between low R2 values and high classification accuracy arises from the distinction between linear correlation analysis and non-linear predictive modeling, rather than from inconsistency in the data or results.

Limitation

The AGIA2 Framework exhibits great performance and can be applied to numerous scenarios. However, several problems should be recognized. First, the hybrid dataset is complete, but it does depend upon the availability and quality of IoT sensor streams, carbon-intensity data, and ESG audit records, which might differ from one place or another, or even company to company. Second, deep learning models, in particular, require significant RNNs and CNN computing resources and may not always work successfully on low-power edge devices without further tuning. This is further aggravated by the multi-modal nature of the dataset, which makes it difficult to synchronize data, handle missing values, and deal with time misalignment between sensors, transactions, and carbon signals. XAI methods remain very important in understanding certain deep brain components, yet these techniques may also blur things. Finally, this would have to interact with corporate compliance routines, energy infrastructures, and privacy governance systems for use in the real world. In this regard, this might make immediate applicability more difficult. These limitations thus reflect where further research needs to be done before wide-scale operational implementation. Reliance on partially synthetic or simulated audit data may introduce bias, and long-term deployment would need mechanisms to handle concept drift as operational and regulatory conditions evolve.

A limitation of the suggested research is that a portion of the data used in the experiment was produced artificially for anomalies related to ESG violations. In order to ensure class balance and improve the quality of the models, around 30–40 percent of the anomalies in the data set were artificially created.

Although the use of synthetic data ensures that the model’s learning process takes place effectively, there is no guarantee that the model will perform well when deployed in practice, especially when detecting irregularities not seen before. Despite the usage of synthetic data to solve the issue of a lack of labeled ESG outliers, it was produced under constrained distributions, which closely replicated real-life scenarios. Nevertheless, synthetic data might not entirely replicate the intricacies and noises found in the real world. While further validation of the model in real-life situations shows consistent results, the future plan is to build a larger set of fully-real time industrial data.

Conclusion

This research proposed AGIA2 framework is a complete solution to sustainable e-commerce auditing, which includes AIoT data acquisition, ensemble learning, and Explainable AI technology. Contrary to other available platforms that concentrate on particular components like forecasting models or IoT data monitoring, the presented architecture merges the three functions into a single platform. AGIA2 differentiates itself from other available models that emphasize model interpretability and performance. The integration of Green Score and Audit Trust Score as evaluation metrics makes the model completer and more balanced than those developed previously. Moreover, the multimodal data fusion method and hybrid ensemble learning make the framework more effective at identifying complicated relationships between different datasets. These distinctions are what makes AGIA2 a powerful and practical framework for sustainable auditing, without making exaggerated claims about its superiority over other models. The experimental results show that the hybrid ensemble model far outperforms traditional classifiers and deep learning models. This demonstrates the importance of ensemble model combination in complex sustainable commerce environments.

The system’s ability to interlink transaction patterns, IoT activity, and carbon signals forms a sound basis for carbon-conscious decision-making. Overall, AGIA2 represents one of the most holistic, practical, and data-driven improvements over state-of-the-art literature on next-generation sustainable e-commerce. By operationalizing sustainability and auditability as core system functions, the proposed framework moves sustainable commerce closer to continuous, explainable, and regulation-ready ESG governance. The results demonstrate that AI-driven, IoT-enabled ecosystems may indeed help the world achieve its Net Zero ambitions while making digital commerce more transparent and capable of better decision-making. In addressing the noted weaknesses, this updated version of the manuscript introduces several changes aimed at enhancing the transparency, reliability, and practical utility of the developed AGIA2 model. In particular, we offer a complete description of how datasets are assembled, explain what synthetic data is for, and validate the approach on real-world data samples.

In addition, we present a thorough discussion of the hybrid ensemble methodology, reinforce the interpretability layer using XAI, and mitigate overfitting issues by means of validation and concept drift management. This helps make sure that the suggested approach is both theoretically justified and ready for practical use in ever-changing sustainable commerce environments.

Future work

Many future designs can be build based on theAGIA2 Framework like adding real-time Digital Twins of warehouses, delivery fleets, and energy infrastructures so that the impact of carbon on the environment can be simulated and predicted continuously, and proactive steps toward sustainability can be taken. Immutable ESG audit trails based on blockchain can help foster trust, openness, and adherence to the law by ensuring that audit records cannot be changed. Adding other data types to the hybrid dataset, such as satellite-based emissions data, RFID-level supply-chain traces, and consumer profiles of sustainability preferences, is another possible option. Edge-working AI models can enhance low-latency carbon intelligence even for the most resource-constrained IoT devices. This enables you to make route-level or product-level predictions about sustainability without having to depend entirely on cloud inference.

Finally, reinforcement learning might be used to enhance sustainable commerce choices with multiple objectives, like dynamic pricing, scheduling deliveries dependent on carbon emissions, and automatic carbon offset assignment. These future enhancements have the potential to make sustainable commerce systems more scalable, reliable, and effective. They also move the AGIA2 architecture closer to completely autonomous, adaptive, and net-zero-aligned digital commerce ecosystems. In future developments, the utilization of blockchain technology for immutably tracking ESG audit trails is proposed. Nevertheless, it is essential to recognize the possible negative implications that the adoption of blockchain technology could bring, especially concerning energy usage. For example, PoW consensus protocols can impose considerable carbon footprints, thereby conflicting with the sustainable agenda. In future developments, there will be efforts to employ energy-efficient consensus protocols like PoS and even distributed ledger technologies. The result is a solution that promotes auditability while not undermining the sustainability agenda.
