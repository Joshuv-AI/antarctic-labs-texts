To the Editor:

Determining the appropriate treatment strategy for nontuberculous mycobacterial pulmonary disease (NTM-PD) is challenging due to interpatient variability in clinical phenotypes. Recently, the BACES score has gained interest as a tool for predicting all-cause mortality in patients with NTM-PD [1, 2]. The model includes five easily obtainable components: body mass index <18.5 kg·m−2, age ≥65 years, cavitary disease, erythrocyte sedimentation rate ≥15 mm·h−1 in males and ≥20 mm·h−1 in females, and male sex, yielding a total score ranging from 0–5. Based on the total score, patients are classified into three categories: low risk (0–1), intermediate risk (2–3), or high risk (4–5).

Recent studies added that the model could be useful for predicting other clinical outcomes [3, 4]. Therefore, the aim of this study was to assess the predictive value of the BACES score for antimycobacterial treatment initiation, culture conversion and clinical improvement in a Dutch NTM-PD cohort.

This retrospective chart review was conducted at Radboudumc, the national reference clinic for NTM disease. We included the cohort from our BACES validation study (submitted), and merged it with patients enrolled in the prospective Mycobacterial Cohort Study. Adults diagnosed with Mycobacterium avium complex or Mycobacterium abscessus pulmonary disease who attended the clinic between January 2008 and October 2024 were enrolled. Verbal consent was obtained from patients in the BACES validation cohort who were alive and had up-to-date contact details. Patients from the Mycobacterial Cohort Study provided written informed consent. Both studies were approved by the institutional ethics committee (METC Oost-Nederland; 2024-17038; 2021-13231).

The studied outcomes were initiation of antimycobacterial treatment, culture conversion and clinical improvement. Due to the retrospective design, culture conversion was defined as ≥2 consecutive negative sputum cultures with a minimum 4-week interval or, if sputum could not be expectorated, a single negative culture from bronchoalveolar lavage or bronchial wash fluid. Clinical improvement was assessed based on clinical notes of the treating physician prior to treatment initiation and at end of treatment or the most recent outpatient visit, whichever was applicable. Both outcomes required a minimum of six months of antimycobacterial treatment. Time to initiation of antimycobacterial therapy was calculated from the date of NTM-PD diagnosis, defined as the first positive NTM culture in patients meeting all diagnostic criteria [5]. The BACES components were recorded at or within one year of NTM-PD diagnosis, but no longer than three months after initiation of antimycobacterial therapy.

Data analysis was carried out in SPSS version 29. Categorial data were reported as counts and proportions, while continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (25th–75th percentile). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. The BACES risk score was calculated for participants with all five components available and for those with four components adding up to an incomplete score of 0, 2, or 4 (as adding the fifth component would not change the risk-group assignment). Chi-square tests and the median test for k samples were used to compare baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes among BACES risk groups. Univariate logistic regression determined the predictive value of BACES risk scores for clinical outcomes.

195 patients were enrolled, with a median age of 65 years (IQR 57–72) and 121 (62.1%) females. Of these, 170 patients had all five BACES components available and 25 patients had 4 out of 5 components adding up to a total score of 0, 2 or 4. Among all, 53 (27.2%) were classified as low risk, 113 (57.9%) as intermediate risk, and 29 (14.9%) as high risk. Table 1 summarises all baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes across BACES categories. Overall, antimycobacterial treatment was initiated in 148 (76.3%) participants. Among those receiving six months or more of treatment, 98 of122 (80.3%) achieved culture conversion and 86 of 135 (63.7%) experienced clinical improvement.

Compared to low risk patients, those classified as intermediate risk (OR 2.59, CI 95% 1.25–5.35; p=0.01) and high risk (OR 3.91, 95% CI 1.18–12.89; p=0.03) were more likely to be initiated onto antimycobacterial therapy. No significant difference in treatment initiation was observed between the intermediate and high risk categories (OR 1.51, 95% CI 0.48–4.79; p=0.48). Compared to high risk patients, individuals in the intermediate risk (OR 3.58, 95% CI 1.25–10.23; p=0.02) and low risk categories (OR 9.0, 95% CI 1.68–48.37; p=0.01) had higher odds of achieving culture conversion. The odds of culture conversion were not different between the low and intermediate risk categories (OR 2.52, 95% CI 0.53–11.99; p=0.25). No different odds were observed across BACES risk categories for achieving clinical improvement (p>0.05). A sensitivity analysis including only individuals with all BACES components available did not affect the predictive value of BACES risk scores.

We evaluated whether the BACES score could predict the initiation of antimycobacterial therapy, culture conversion and clinical improvement in a cohort of patients from the Netherlands with NTM-PD. Our findings revealed that, compared to those classified as low-risk, patients in the intermediate- and high-risk categories were more likely to begin treatment, and the time from diagnosis to treatment initiation was shorter. Furthermore, compared to high-risk patients, those with intermediate-risk and low-risk had higher odds of achieving culture conversion. No significant differences across risk categories were found for the probability of achieving clinical improvement.

Our results are consistent with previous cohort studies from South Korea, which demonstrated that higher BACES severity was associated with a greater likelihood of antimycobacterial treatment initiation [3, 4], and that high risk patients were less likely to obtain culture conversion than low risk patients [4]. The lack of associations between individual BACES components and culture conversion (results not included) suggests that an interplay of components, or non-included factors, may contribute to predicting treatment response.

The proportion of participants with a positive acid-fast bacilli smear, active tobacco use, and a history of cardiovascular disease, alcohol abuse, or diabetes mellitus increased with higher BACES severity. These factors are associated with poor prognosis in the general population, indicating that the BACES score stratifies risk profiles. This may have influenced both the decision to initiate treatment and the chance of obtaining culture conversion.

In conclusion, the BACES score could serve as a valuable tool in guiding treatment decisions for patients in the Netherlands with NTM-PD. Patients classified as low- or intermediate-risk would benefit most from antimycobacterial therapy, while those at high risk may need a more tailored approach, which could include additional supportive care, host-directed therapy and adjuvant surgery.
