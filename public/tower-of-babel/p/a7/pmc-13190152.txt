Short-term gaseous and particulate air pollution exposure and risk of acute kidney injury: a 12-year case-crossover study [2013–2024] in Guangzhou, China
Authors: Yuqi Chen, Zhiman Liang, Keran Zhang, Wenhan Cao, Youpeng Chen, Chengtao Zhou, Guanghui Dong, Baoqing Sun, Zhangkai J. Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a major public health burden associated with high morbidity, significantly increasing long-term mortality, and escalating healthcare costs. While long-term air pollution exposure is a known risk factor for chronic kidney disease (CKD), evidence regarding the acute triggers of AKI remains limited, especially in Asian megacities with complex pollution profiles. We investigated the independent effects of major air pollutants on the risk of AKI using algorithmically identified clinical data.MethodsA time-stratified case-crossover study was conducted involving adults in Guangzhou, China [2013–2024]. We included patients with longitudinal serum creatinine records and strictly excluded those with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) or a history of kidney transplantation. AKI events were defined using the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) serum creatinine criteria. Daily meteorological and air pollutant data were collected from respective monitoring stations. Conditional logistic regression and generalized additive models were employed to estimate the associations between short-term exposure to fine particulate matter [particulate matter less than 2.5 μm (PM2.5)], sulfur dioxide (SO2), and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and the risk of AKI, adjusting for meteorological factors.ResultsA total of 10,857 AKI cases (60.0% male; 62.2% aged <65 years) were included. In multi-pollutant models, gaseous pollutants showed robust independent associations with AKI. A 10 µg/m3 increase in SO2 and NO2 was associated with a 4.6% [odds ratio (OR) =1.046, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.027–1.066] and 3.3% (OR =1.033, 95% CI: 1.028–1.039) increase in AKI odds, respectively. NO2 showed immediate effects (lag 0), while SO2 exhibited delayed effects (lag 1). Notably, associations for NO2 and SO2 were significantly enhanced during the cold season. After adjusting for gaseous pollutants, PM2.5 exhibited a negative association (OR =0.977), which likely reflects statistical artifact due to high correlation rather than a protective biological effect.ConclusionsShort-term exposure to ambient SO2 and NO2 are significant, independent risk factors for AKI in Guangzhou. These findings underscore the dominant nephrotoxic potential of gaseous pollutants. Clinicians should maintain heightened awareness of AKI risks during high-pollution episodes, particularly in the cold season, and multi-pollutant management strategies should be prioritized.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Short-term exposure to SO2 and NO2 is significantly associated with an increased risk of acute kidney injury (AKI), with odds ratios of 1.046 and 1.033 per 10 µg/m3 increase, respectively.

• Gaseous pollutants (SO2 and NO2) exhibit robust independent nephrotoxic effects, which are significantly enhanced during the cold season.

• NO2 shows immediate effects (lag 0), while SO2 exhibits slightly delayed effects (lag 1) on AKI onset.

What is known and what is new?

• While air pollution is a recognized risk factor for chronic kidney disease, evidence regarding its role as an acute trigger for AKI in Asian megacities has been limited.

• This 12-year study identifies gaseous pollutants, rather than particulate matter, as the dominant acute environmental triggers for AKI in an urban population.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Public health policies should prioritize the control of gaseous pollutants (SO2 and NO2) to mitigate their acute impact on kidney health.

• Environmental health guidelines should emphasize increased protection for urban populations against AKI during periods of high gaseous pollution and the cold season.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a significant global health problem associated with high morbidity, mortality, and progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD) (1,2). While traditional risk factors are well-established, ambient air pollution may serve as an important trigger for acute renal events (3). The pathogenesis of AKI is multifactorial, involving complex hemodynamic shifts, immune responses, and direct nephrotoxicity. Because the kidneys are highly vascularized organs receiving 20–25% of resting cardiac output, they are uniquely susceptible to systemic inflammatory mediators and inhaled oxidants (3,4). With increasing urbanization, understanding the role of specific air pollutants in precipitating AKI represents an important public health concern.

Most epidemiological research has focused on long-term fine particulate matter [particulate matter less than 2.5 µm (PM2.5)] effects on CKD (5,6), while evidence on acute AKI effects remains limited and inconsistent (7,8). Furthermore, the independent effects of gaseous pollutants in urban environments remain poorly understood (3). Gaseous pollutants such as nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and sulfur dioxide (SO2) act as potent reactive oxidants. Upon inhalation, they trigger pulmonary oxidative stress, which subsequently spills over into the systemic circulation (9). This systemic cascade leads to widespread endothelial dysfunction and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha)], ultimately precipitating ischemic and tubular renal injury (10,11).

Crucially, the evaluation of environmental triggers for AKI must also consider the profound contribution of meteorological factors, which are intrinsically linked to both pollutant dispersion and human physiology. Recent compelling evidence identifies ambient temperature, humidity, and heat-induced dehydration as primary, independent drivers of acute renal injury (12,13). Variations in these meteorological factors can independently or synergistically alter renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate (GFR). For instance, cold spells can induce peripheral vasoconstriction, activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and hypertensive responses (14), whereas extreme temperatures promote dehydration and hyperosmolarity, both of which severely stress renal hemodynamics. Without understanding how these factors influence renal function, interpreting the nephrotoxic potential of air pollution is challenging. Consequently, it is imperative to rigorously adjust for these meteorological variables as confounders in environmental epidemiological models.

Several critical gaps continue to limit our current understanding, leading to controversies and inconsistent evidence in previous literature. These discrepancies may be attributed to geographic differences in regional pollutant compositions (15), variations in AKI definitions across different healthcare systems (16), or the predominant reliance on single-pollutant models that fail to capture the complex real-world “nephrotoxic mixture” (17). First, many studies rely on administrative diagnostic codes for AKI identification, which lack clinical sensitivity and specificity (16). Second, most studies focus on Western populations, with limited data from Asian megacities (18). Third, few studies have robustly differentiated the independent effects of multiple pollutants or explored their complex, non-linear associations using a time-varying approach (19).

To address these gaps, we investigated short-term associations between major air pollutants and algorithmically-identified AKI based on clinical criteria in Guangzhou, China. Using a time-stratified case-crossover design with algorithmically-identified AKI events, we sought to: (I) quantify independent effects of NO2, SO2, and PM2.5 using multi-pollutant models; (II) characterize temporal dynamics and exposure-response relationships; and (III) identify effect modifiers and vulnerable populations. We hypothesized that gaseous pollutants would be independent risk factors for AKI, with effects varying across subgroups and conditions. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2767/rc).

Methods

Study design

We conducted a time-stratified case-crossover study to investigate the short-term associations between ambient air pollution and hospital admissions for AKI in Guangzhou, China, from 2013 to 2024 (Figure 1). In this design, each individual serves as their own control. Because the case and control days for each specific AKI event are tightly matched within the same calendar month, the comparison window for any given individual is extremely short (within 30 days). Consequently, this design inherently controls not only for strict time-invariant confounders (e.g., sex, genetics) but also for slowly time-varying individual-level factors, such as age-drift and the long-term progression of underlying comorbidities, which remain entirely stable within this brief matching period (19).

Flowchart of the study design. AKI, acute kidney injury; AQI, air quality index; CO2, carbon dioxide; NO2, nitrogen dioxide; O3, ozone (8-hour mean); PM10, particulate matter less than 10 μm; PM2.5, particulate matter less than 2.5 μm; SO2, sulfur dioxide.

To select control periods, we employed a time-stratified sampling approach. For each case day [defined as the date of peak serum creatinine (SCr) during the AKI episode], control days were selected from the same calendar month and year, matched on the same day of the week. This strategy yields approximately 3–4 control days per case and minimizes bias from seasonal patterns, long-term trends, and day-of-week effects.

Data collection

We retrospectively analyzed electronic medical records (EMRs) from The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University, spanning the period from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2024. To construct our study cohort, we initially identified all adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with a preliminary or discharge diagnosis related to renal disease documented in their EMRs. This case-enrichment strategy was employed to focus the analysis on patients with renal diseases. From this cohort, we excluded individuals with established end-stage renal disease (ESRD), those receiving maintenance dialysis, or with a history of kidney transplantation. Longitudinal SCr measurements were compiled for eligible subjects. All SCr measurements were performed at the central laboratory of the hospital. SCr concentrations were determined using a commercial enzymatic sarcosine oxidase assay (Maccura Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China) on a fully automated biochemical analyzer. The entire testing process, including daily calibration and quality control, was conducted in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (No. 2018-119) and informed consent was waived.

To identify incident AKI events, we first algorithmically segmented the longitudinal SCr records for each patient into discrete hospitalization episodes; a new episode was demarcated if the time gap between two consecutive SCr tests surpassed 30 days. Subsequently, each episode was systematically evaluated for AKI based on the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) creatinine-based criteria (20). An episode was classified as an AKI event if it met either of the following conditions: (I) an increase in SCr by ≥26.5 µmol/L (0.3 mg/dL) within 48 hours, or (II) an increase in SCr to ≥1.5 times the baseline value—defined as the first SCr measurement during that hospitalization—within 7 days.

For each identified AKI event, the date of the peak SCr concentration during the episode was defined as the case day. To ensure the independence of events for the same individual, we enforced a 30-day washout period, retaining only the first AKI event or any subsequent event that occurred more than 30 days after the preceding one. This approach identified AKI cases for the time-stratified case-crossover analysis.

Environmental data

We obtained daily air pollution data from the Guangdong Department of Ecology and Environment (http://gdee.gd.gov.cn/), including 24 h average concentrations of carbon monoxide (CO), NO2, PM2.5, particulate matter less than 10 µm (PM10), SO2, and 8 h mean O3 concentration in Guangzhou from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2024. Data from multiple monitoring stations were spatially averaged to represent city-wide exposure levels. Corresponding daily meteorological data, including mean temperature (℃), precipitation (mm), and wind speed (m/s), were obtained from the China Meteorological Administration for the same period. Each case and control period was matched with their respective environmental data.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic characteristics of the AKI cases and the distributions of environmental variables. We reported frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables. A Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the interrelationships between air pollutants and meteorological factors.

Our statistical approach involved a multi-stage process. The primary analysis was conducted using a multi-pollutant generalized additive model (GAM) with a logit link function. This approach was chosen to simultaneously assess the independent effects of pollutants of primary interest (NO2, SO2, and PM2.5) and to characterize potentially non-linear exposure-response relationships. Both the non-linear curves and the overall linear effect estimates [odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) per 10 µg/m3 increase] were derived from this primary model.

To supplement our primary findings, we conducted two secondary analyses using conditional logistic regression, which is intrinsically suited for the case-crossover design. First, to explore the temporal dynamics of associations, we developed separate single-pollutant models for each major pollutant across various single-day and moving-average lag structures. Second, as a sensitivity analysis to confirm the robustness of our primary findings, we fitted a multi-pollutant conditional logistic regression model. For this multi-pollutant sensitivity model, pollutants with statistically significant associations in the single-pollutant models (P<0.05) were included (Table S1), and PM10 was excluded to address multicollinearity with PM2.5 (r≈0.95). All conditional logistic regression models were adjusted for the non-linear effects of temperature, precipitation, and wind speed using natural splines (ns with 3 degrees of freedom). To assess potential multicollinearity in the multi-pollutant models, we calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each pollutant. A VIF value <10 was considered indicative of no severe multicollinearity.

Finally, to identify potentially susceptible populations, we conducted stratified analyses based on our primary multi-pollutant GAM to evaluate effect modification by age (<65 vs. ≥65 years), sex, day of the week (weekday vs. weekend), season (cold vs. warm), pandemic period (<2020 vs. ≥2020), pre-existing comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, CKD), and co-pollutant levels (high vs. low, categorized by the median). The statistical significance of the differences in effect estimates between subgroups was assessed by testing an interaction term between the pollutant and the stratification variable in the model.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3) with the “survival” and “mgcv” packages. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P value <0.05.

Multi-pollutant associations and exposure-response relationships

Our primary analysis utilized a multi-pollutant GAM to assess the independent associations of NO2, SO2, and PM2.5 with AKI risk, while simultaneously characterizing the shape of the exposure-response relationships.

The resulting curves are visualized in Figure 3. After adjusting for co-pollutants and meteorological factors, the association between SO2 and AKI risk displayed a generally linear, monotonically increasing trend (Figure 3C,3D). For NO2, the relationship exhibited a more complex, non-linear pattern, although the overall trend remained positive (Figure 3A,3B). The curve for PM2.5 was also non-linear, showing a slight increase in risk at lower concentrations before declining at levels above approximately 60 µg/m3 (Figure 3E,3F).

The overall linear effect estimates from this primary model are presented in Table 2. Quantitatively, a 10 µg/m3 increase in SO2 and NO2 concentrations was associated with a 4.6% (OR =1.046, 95% CI: 1.027–1.066) and a 3.3% (OR =1.033, 95% CI: 1.028–1.039) increase in the odds of AKI, respectively. Conversely, a 10 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5 was associated with a 2.3% reduction in the odds of AKI (OR =0.977, 95% CI: 0.972–0.983). All these associations were statistically significant (P<0.001).

Sensitivity analysis

As a sensitivity analysis to confirm the robustness of our findings, we also fitted a multi-pollutant conditional logistic regression model. This model yielded directionally consistent results. For a 10 µg/m3 increase, it showed statistically significant positive associations for SO2 (adjusted OR =1.041, 95% CI: 1.020–1.051) and NO2 (adjusted OR =1.030, 95% CI: 1.030–1.041), and a significant inverse association for PM2.5 (adjusted OR =0.961, 95% CI: 0.951–0.970). These results confirm the robustness of our primary findings (see Table S5 for full details). Furthermore, the VIF analysis for the multi-pollutant model indicated low multicollinearity among PM2.5, SO2, and NO2, with all VIF values remaining below 3 (Table S6).

Stratified analyses

To identify potential susceptible populations and explore effect modification, we conducted stratified analyses across various demographic, clinical, and environmental subgroups (Figure 5). Several significant interactions were observed.

For NO2 (Figure 5A), the association with AKI risk was significantly stronger on weekdays compared to weekends (P for interaction =0.004) and during the cold season compared to the warm season (P for interaction <0.001). Furthermore, the effect of NO2 was significantly modified by co-pollutant levels, with stronger associations observed at higher ambient concentrations of O3 (P=0.03), SO2 (P=0.004), PM2.5 (P<0.001), and PM10 (P<0.001).

The effects of SO2 were also modified by seasonality and day of the week, showing substantially stronger associations during the cold season (P<0.001) and on weekdays (P=0.007) (Figure 5B). A significant interaction was also observed for the pandemic period, with a stronger effect observed during the post-2020 period (P<0.001). Additionally, the association was significantly modified by ambient NO2 levels (P<0.001).

For PM2.5 (Figure 5C), a statistically significant modification of the risk estimate was observed by day of the week, with a stronger positive association on weekdays than on weekends (P for interaction =0.02). The association also differed significantly by season (P=0.04). Furthermore, we observed significant interactions with co-pollutants; the risk estimates were significantly higher at higher levels of O3 (P=0.004), NO2 (P=0.002), and PM10 (P=0.001). A significant modification by pre-existing CKD was also noted (P=0.03).

No statistically significant effect modification was observed for other subgroups such as age or other pre-existing comorbidities for any of the pollutants.

Discussion

In this large-scale, time-stratified case-crossover study in Guangzhou, China, we report evidence on the acute renal effects of ambient air pollution. Our primary discovery is the independent and deleterious effect of gaseous pollutants, with short-term exposure to SO2 and NO2 significantly increasing the risk of AKI. This association exhibited distinct temporal patterns, with an immediate impact for NO2 and a delayed effect for SO2. Furthermore, our multi-pollutant models highlighted the dominance of these gaseous pollutants; while single-pollutant models showed weak signals for PM2.5, its association was attenuated and inverted after adjustment for NO2 and SO2. These findings suggest that in this specific urban setting, gaseous emissions are the primary drivers of acute renal risk, especially during the cold season.

Our observation that NO2 and SO2 are independent risk factors for AKI provides evidence suggesting traffic-related and industrial emissions as direct contributors to acute renal pathology. The role of NO2, a primary constituent of traffic-related air pollution (TRAP), as a potent systemic oxidant is well-established (4). Its inhalation is known to trigger a cascade of events beginning with pulmonary oxidative stress, which rapidly spills over into the systemic circulation, leading to widespread endothelial dysfunction, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, and activation of inflammatory pathways—all of which can acutely impair renal microcirculation and precipitate ischemic acute tubular injury (21,22). Our finding of a highly acute effect at lag 0 is consistent with this proposed mechanism of a rapid-onset, hemodynamically-mediated renal insult (23). Similarly, the association with SO2, predominantly from industrial combustion and shipping, corroborates its known role as a systemic inflammatory agent (10). The delayed effect we observed for SO2 (significant from lag 1) may reflect a more complex, multi-step pathophysiology; this could involve an initial pulmonary inflammatory response that gradually releases cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) into the circulation, which then take 24–48 hours to induce a significant inflammatory response and leukocyte infiltration within the renal parenchyma (11). By disentangling these effects from the broader pollutant mixture in our multi-pollutant model, our study suggests that NO2 and SO2 may act as independent nephrotoxicants rather than merely markers of pollution in the context of short-term exposure.

A notable observation in our multi-pollutant analysis was the inverse association between PM2.5 and AKI risk. While a protective biological effect of particulate matter is implausible, this “sign reversal” is a recognized phenomenon in environmental epidemiology when modeling highly correlated pollutants (17). This likely reflects the dominant role of gaseous pollutants as proximal surrogates for the nephrotoxic mixture in Guangzhou. NO2, a primary marker of fresh traffic exhaust, and SO2, indicative of industrial emissions, demonstrated robust signals that may have overshadowed the effect of PM2.5 mass concentration. Statistically, although our VIF analysis (VIF <3) ruled out severe multicollinearity that would technically invalidate the model, the strong correlation coefficients among these co-pollutants likely resulted in the stronger predictors (NO2 and SO2) overshadowing the effect of PM2.5, leading to an unstable negative coefficient. Therefore, our results suggest that in this specific study period and location, gaseous pollutants were more precise indicators of the acute environmental triggers for AKI than total PM2.5 mass.

While a statistical artifact remains the most compelling and parsimonious explanation, a secondary, more speculative hypothesis involves PM2.5 chemical composition (15). The acute toxicity of PM2.5 is dictated not by its total mass, but by its specific components (3,24). It is conceivable that in Guangzhou, where PM2.5 can be rich in secondary aerosols, the particle mixture remaining after accounting for the effects of primary gaseous pollutants possesses a lower acute toxic potential compared to that in other regions (25,26). However, this hypothesis is secondary to the issue of collinearity and would require speciated PM2.5 data to be properly tested.

Our stratified analyses identified potential windows of vulnerability. The enhanced adverse effects of both NO2 and SO2 during the cold season are particularly noteworthy. This is likely attributable to a “double-hit” phenomenon. Winter meteorology not only increases pollutant concentrations due to lower boundary layer heights and thermal inversions (27) but may also alter particle chemistry. Concurrently, physiological responses to cold, including increased blood pressure, systemic vascular resistance, and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, create a pro-hypertensive and pro-inflammatory internal milieu that primes the kidneys, rendering them hyper-susceptible to the additional oxidative and inflammatory insult from inhaled pollutants (14). Furthermore, our observation of significant synergistic interactions, where the effect of one pollutant is amplified by another (e.g., the NO2 effect was stronger at high PM2.5 levels), points towards complex biological interplay (28). This is not merely an additive effect but true synergism. Mechanistically, this could occur at multiple levels: NO2-induced airway inflammation might compromise the alveolar-capillary barrier, facilitating the translocation of ultrafine particles and their toxic components into the bloodstream (29). Alternatively, co-exposure could overwhelm antioxidant defense systems more effectively than exposure to a single pollutant (30). These findings suggest that current approaches may not fully account for the complexity of pollutant interactions in risk assessment models that treat pollutants as independent stressors and support the need for air quality management strategies that are based on the health risks of real-world, complex pollutant mixtures.

This study is strengthened by its large sample size of over 10,000 events—which post-hoc calculations indicate provides robust statistical power (>90%) to detect the observed subtle risk increases—and the use of a time-stratified case-crossover design. Most importantly, this design inherently controls for time-invariant and slowly changing individual-level confounders. Factors such as sex, genetics, baseline body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and chronic pre-existing medications [e.g., angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors] remain stable within the short 1-month matching strata, thereby eliminating the need to adjust for them in our models. Furthermore, our KDIGO-based algorithmic approach to AKI case identification from longitudinal creatinine data improves diagnostic accuracy over standard diagnostic codes.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, our reliance on fixed-site monitors for exposure assessment introduces potential measurement error and does not account for personal mobility. Second, defining baseline SCr as the first measurement during hospitalization is a pragmatic but recognized limitation in retrospective EMR-based studies when pre-admission outpatient data are unavailable (31). We acknowledge that this surrogate baseline may misclassify some cases of community-acquired AKI (where the admission value is already the peak) as non-AKI or obscure the true magnitude of injury. Third, our study population was enriched for patients with clinically indicated creatinine measurements, and relying on a single-center hospital database (despite being a major regional hub) potentially limits the generalizability of our findings to the broader, healthy population. Finally, while our multi-pollutant models are a significant advance, residual confounding from unmeasured pollutants cannot be entirely ruled out.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that short-term exposures to ambient SO2 and NO2 are independent risk factors for AKI, with effects particularly pronounced during the cold season and in the presence of other co-pollutants. Rather than a protective effect of particulates, the inverse association with PM2.5 observed in multi-pollutant models highlights the dominant role of gaseous emissions in driving acute renal risk in this region. From a public health perspective, these results suggest the need for more stringent and targeted regulations on traffic and industrial emissions to protect renal health. Clinicians should be aware of air pollution as a potential trigger for AKI, especially for at-risk patients during winter pollution episodes. Future research should consider multi-city studies with detailed PM2.5 speciation data to unravel the complex role of particulate matter and to better understand the mechanisms of pollutant synergy.
