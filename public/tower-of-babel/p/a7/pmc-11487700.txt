Assessing well-being of university students and evaluation of its determinants
Authors: Orsolya Szepe, Agnes Meszaros
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOur study aimed to examine the well-being of students studying at the three (non-medical) faculties of Semmelweis University Budapest, to analyse the relationships between well-being, stress, emotional support, sleep patterns, sport, and further variables like smoking, consumption of sleeping pills, sedatives, psychoactive agents, energy drinks, and exam anxiety.MethodsIn our cross-sectional study, we asked 561 students of the Semmelweis University of Budapest; two internationally recognised instruments were used: the WHO-5 well-being index and the Perceived Stress Scale.ResultsThe mean WHO-5 well-being index was 42.62 ± 20.51 on a scale ranging from 0 to 100. We saw an inverse correlation between student well-being and years of study. The average anxiety level regarding oral exams and assessments was 7.63 ± SD = 2.04, while the mean level was lower concerning written exams and assessments 5.74 ± SD = 2.09. The result of a linear regression model indicates that the best level of explaining power by the regression model was with following variables combination: grade average + anxiety before written exam + academic year + stress.ConclusionOur data indicate that some students have poor or very poor well-being, which fluctuates over the study period. The worst state was found in the first and final academic year. The students’ well-being level is positively correlated with emotional support, physical activity, and quality of sleep and correlates negatively with anxiety before exams. Our results suggest that well-being could be improved when universities play an active role, supporting influential activities and offering help regarding coping with stress, like stress management, as well as by providing information on learning methods and techniques.

Background

Good health, quality of life, and well-being currently are at the heart of our society, also of public health and personalised health and social care. The developed world takes these parameters for granted in the lives of young individuals like university students. However, the fact that someone has entered a university and has the opportunity to obtain a degree and a profession does not automatically mean that everything is perfect at this phase of a student’s life. We could assume that at this life stage students are highly motivated, ready to face different challenges, and make special efforts to reach their aims.

Well-being as well as stress and anxiety gets special attention today in case of university students; the beginning of university life is an important transition period from adolescence to young adulthood, which brings many challenges and difficulties for the student due to the interaction between individual psychological characteristics and general stressors and stress factors. Such stressors include new academic demands, lifestyle changes, separation from home and family, possible inadequate living conditions, physical and emotional problems, and potential financial worries. The stress levels of university students may also be related to the pressure of exams, the expectations of success, the need to prove good performance, and an uncertain future, as well as stress can interact with student performance and well-being. To intervene in time, if necessary, therefore, testing indicators of well-being is essential. Concerns are increasing regarding the prevalence of mental health issues within higher education. Numerous surveys are prompting academic institutions to explore intervention strategies. A report from 2015, indicated that graduate students in life sciences were experiencing depression between 43% and 46% [1]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of longitudinal studies by A.T. du Toit et al. identified several antecedents of student well-being that could be targeted for interventions [2]. These included self-relationship, emotion regulation, and interventions to decrease mental illness. Universities could facilitate this by establishing and maintaining groups such as study cohorts, and interest groups. Another systematic review of 31 studies examined different factors from 2010 to 2020 that are associated with mental health among students and identified childhood trauma, and autism spectrum conditions as key factors for poor mental health. However strong social support networks and the ability to adapt to the challenges of higher education contributed to well-being too. It has also been observed that involvement in academic and leisure activities, and the level of mental health literacy are essential to maintaining optimal mental health. It was also found that family and social support as well as belonging to the university community positively influenced the mental health of students [3]. A thorough investigation of the health status of both domestic and international students at an Australian university revealed that mental health issues, including depression and anxiety, are prevalent and negatively impacting academic performance; the study also examined general lifestyle factors (such as diet, exercise, and sleep duration), risk-taking behaviors (including the use of alcohol, tobacco, and other drugs), and psychosocial stressors. Findings indicated that 30.7% of local students exhibited moderate to severe depressive symptoms. Additionally, approximately 20% of the students had an eating disorder, and nearly 5% reported engaging in self-harm or attempting suicide within the past year [4]. An online survey of 574 UK university students in 2018 aimed to identify the most important factors influencing student well-being. The majority of participants reported feeling extremely stressed or overwhelmed at university, while residential students expressing higher levels of loneliness and perceived stress compared to commuters [5].

A recent meta-analysis by Li and colleagues (2022) looked at the global prevalence of depression and anxiety symptoms among university students. Analysing data from 64 studies involving more than 100,000 participants in total, their study found alarmingly high rates of mental health problems. The overall prevalence of depressive symptoms was 33.6%, while anxiety symptoms reached 39.0%. These figures highlight the significant burden of students mental health problems worldwide. The study also showed that there are differences in prevalence based on certain factors; like being African, living in lower middle-income countries and studding at medical programmes had corresponding higher rate of depression. Similarly the prevalence of anxiety was highest in North America, in lower middle-income countries, medical students; depression status was tested with the Beck Anxiety Inventory. These findings highlight the urgent necessity for effective prevention and intervention strategies to tackle the mental health issues faced by college students globally [6].

Our study aimed to examine the well-being of students studying at the three (non-medical) faculties of Semmelweis University Budapest, Hungary, and to analyse the relationships between well-being, stress, emotional support, sleep patterns, sport, and further variables like smoking, consumption of sleeping pills, sedatives, psychoactive agents, energy drinks, and exam anxiety.

Methods

Our survey was a non-interventional, cross-sectional study conducted between 1st. February and 30th April 2023. Hungarian students were recruited at the Faculty of Pharmacy, Faculty of Health Sciences, and Andras Petho Faculty (offering a course on conductive education) of the Semmelweis University, Budapest (Hungary). Participation was voluntary and anonymous (no personal data was recorded while filling out the questionnaire) and it was open to all students studying at these faculties. To take part in our online questionnaire survey, students were invited by an email sent by the dean’s office to every student who was registered at the university in that spring term. Responses were registered in a coded form for data analysis. Our survey was authorised by the Ethics Committee of Semmelweis University (SE-RKEB 173/2022).

In our questionnaire, we asked demographic questions (age, gender, place of birth), further questions were focusing on academic performance in the form of an average grade of the previous term, physical activity/ sports, sleep patterns, medication use (sleeping pills, sedatives), alcohol and energy drink consumption habits; as well as about the level of students anxiety, they experience during either oral or written exams (e.g. such as reports, presentations, exams, etc.) At Semmelweis University students do have quite some oral exams (approximately 40% of all exams) mainly as the closing of a particular subject, as well as other forms of oral assessments (e.g. oral presentations). To assess anxiety regarding exams we have used a visual analogue rating scale. The level of exam anxiety (panic) could be rated from 1 to 10, a point scoring scale, that represents a continuum between “1 = no anxiety” and “10 = maximum anxiety.”

Two, most widely used internationally recognised self-administered instruments were applied to assess well-being and stress.

The WHO-5 well-being index: The World Health Organization’s General well-being scale (WHO-5) is one of the most widely used measure of subjective well-being. The questionnaire is scored by summing the responses of five dimensions and multiplying the numbers by 4, providing a score with a range of 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate better well-being. A score over 51 indicates good well-being, and a score below 50 indicates poor well-being [7–9].The Perceived Stress Scale 14 (PSS14): one of the most commonly used measures of stress levels is the Perceived Stress Questionnaire (PSS14), which asks about an individual’s subjective feelings and coping with stress in a given month. PSS14 measures feelings and thoughts experienced in the past month, assessing the extent to which a person perceives his or her life to be overwhelming, unpredictable, and out of his or her control [10, 11]. Individual scores on the PSS14 range from 0 to 56, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. Scores between 0 and 19 indicate low levels of stress. Scores between 20 and 37 indicate moderate levels of stress, while scores between 38 and 56 indicate high levels of perceived stress.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed by using Software Package for Statistical Analysis 28.0.1. In addition to descriptive statistical analysis, we used ANOVA to determine differences between more than two groups. To examine the relationship between well-being (dependent variable) and potential influential independent variables, we performed multivariate analyses by linear regression models. The odds ratio (OR) and the 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated to determine the strength of the association between nominal values. We used a p-value of 0.05 or less as the cut-off for statistical significance.

To estimate the explanation power of various parameters of well-being a regression model was developed. In this procedure, it was first assessed whether a linear regression model fits for our purpose. Then diagnostics were done regarding the independent variables to assess the applicability of the model.

Results and discussion

Sociodemographic and academic characteristics of the sample

After cleaning the sample, a total of 561 university students’ responses were analysed. The average age of the participants was 23.88 ± 6.29 years, and 87.7% of them were female; the gender distribution among the three faculties of Semmelweis University closely mirrors that of the respondents, with students comprising roughly 83% female and 17% male [12].

Nearly 85% of the respondents grew up in the capital (Budapest) or a city, while 15% grew up in a town or a village. Around half of the respondents live with their parents or relatives, around 24% with their partner or spouse, almost 10% live alone, and the rest live in a shared flat or residential community. The majority of participants were studying at the Faculty of Health Sciences followed by the Faculty of Pharmacy and Faculty of Andras Petho. The fractions of participants represent well the size of the faculties. The Faculty of Pharmacy offers undergraduate courses for students who wish to become a pharmacist; the Faculty of Andras Petho offers training on conductive education, whose graduates will work as conductors with disabled children. The Faculty of Health Sciences covers quite a wide range of courses such as dietician, physiotherapy, nursing, health tourism management, and midwifery.

Results of the WHO-5 well-being index

In our study, the mean WHO-5 well-being index was 42.62 ± 20.51 on a scale ranging from 0 to 100, where a score of 51 and over indicates good well-being, a score between 29 and 50 indicates poor well-being, a score below 28 indicates potential depression and suggests further medical examination to identify depression.

The prevalence of poor well-being was 63.6% among the participants, out of which 30.1% had a lower WHO-5 score ≤ 28 indicating depression. Results are presented in Table 2.

Our results agree with the published data. The WHO World Mental Health Surveys International College Student Project highlighted the increased risk of mental disorders among students in a study conducted at 19 universities in eight countries, underlying the urgency of improving prevention and intervention strategies [13]. A Romanian cross-sectional study among dental students found that nearly 60% of students had a low well-being index which led to recommendations for depression screening [14].

Results of potential influential factors of well-being

We could not find a significant correlation between students’ age, gender, and students’ well-being similar to other studies [15–17].

When examining the relationship between study time and well-being, we saw an inverse correlation between student well-being and years of study. The further students were in their studies the lower the WHO-5 well-being score they had (F = 3.08, p = 0.016). Participants studying in years 2 and 3 had the best well-being scores, while those studying in years 4 and 5 had significantly lower scores (only pharmacy training lasts for 5 years, whereas studies at Peto Faculty and Health Sciences cover 4 years). This observed relationship indicates that students are insecure at the beginning and at the end. First, because everything is new and later possibly because of uncertainty about the future. All this points to the fact that students at the beginning of their studies have a high degree of uncertainty and quite some novelties to face in the first year indicating poor well-being. These stressor factors may decrease as students progress in their studies, they adapt to university life. In the final year, however, as students got used to the university, uncertainty re-emerged in connection with their future life after graduation, since it is time to make decisions about future careers.

Several longitudinal studies showed similar data to our results, that well-being and anxiety fluctuate over the study years, Bewick et al. evaluated changes in psychological well-being over the university years and found that the first year had the lowest levels of well-being and the highest levels of anxiety. Thereafter, levels of anxiety decrease, and well-being increases [18]. Bayram et al. while analysing the depression prevalence, anxiety, and stress, found as well that first and second-year students had faced the highest levels of depression, anxiety, and stress [15].

Emotional support seemed to be a significant parameter to assess, so we have asked whether students receive emotional support in challenging life situations. 79.3% of the participants said they do. Most of the students said that they receive emotional support primarily from their partners and family, most commonly in the form of active listening and counselling. A significant relationship was found between the WHO-5 well-being index and the presence of emotional support (Cramer V = 0.186, F = 3.174, p < 0.001). In total 89.2% of students with a WHO-5 score of 50 and below reported receiving emotional support. In contrast, emotional support was received by 73.7% of students with poor well-being (WHO-5 score of 50 and below). Among students who reported not receiving any emotional support, 54% had a well-being score of 28 and below, indicating depression. Supporting our findings published studies, have reported that supportive peer relationships have a positive impact on well-being [19]. Academic support from the university and peer relationships both enhance coping with stress and improve resilience [5].

Living alone compared to living with someone: whether a student lives alone or with a family/partner or in a shared flat/ residential community did not affect well-being index, as the WHO-5 scores were 43.93 and 42.63 respectively (p > 0.05).

Anxiety regarding exams and academic performance can also be a major stressor in students’ lives, which can have a significant impact on their well-being. The average anxiety level in relation to oral exams and assessments was 7.63 ± SD = 2.04, while the mean level was lower concerning written exams and assessments 5.74 ± SD = 2.09. The WHO-5 well-being index showed a moderate negative correlation with oral exam anxiety (r=-0.296, p < 0.001) and a weak negative correlation with written exam anxiety (r=-0.168, p < 0.001), however, we have assumed a much stronger relationship A significant but very weak positive relationship was found between the average academic grade and student well-being. Like in our survey, published studies in the literature investigating the relationship between well-being, exam anxiety, and academic performance, clear positive correlation was demonstrated [3, 20–22]. A paper by du Toi T.A. et al. found a strong relationship between the mental health of university students and their academic performance [2]. A further study by Sparfeldt et al. reports that on average, examinees experienced higher levels of cognitive and emotional distress when having oral exams than in case of written exams [23]. However, we have to note that the level of anxiety may of course vary from individual to individual and may also depend on the level of preparation and confidence of the examinee.

The next set of questions have focused on lifestyle parameters, about the use of alcohol, energy drinks, sleeping pills, sedatives, and other psychoactive agents, as well as coffee consumption and smoking. Since the respondents were exclusively students visiting faculties on health/medical disciplines, we assumed that their knowledge of these issues may exceed that of their schoolmates at universities of other disciplines. As a result, participants demonstrated a greater awareness than their peers regarding the effects of smoking and the use of various psychoactive substances on health. We did not specify psychoactive substances in the questionnaire to avoid limiting the responses, as it was assumed that students were generally knowledgeable about them and their health implications.

Regarding smoking and well-being, we identified a student population of 69.3% who responded never to smoke, 11.1% of the respondents had quit smoking and 19.6% were current smokers.

The proportion of regular smokers is relatively low when compared to the national figures, where nearly 32% of the adult population aged between 18 and 34 years in Hungary are smokers [24]. In the European Union statistics from 2019 show that 15% of the adolescents and young adults aged 15–24 smoked regularly [25].

Our findings show that good well-being has a weak but positive impact on increasing the time when the first wake-up cigarette is smoked. Similar smoking rates were found in Italy where medical students had lower smoking prevalence rates compared to students studying at non-medical universities [26]. A study from Spain investigating smoking habits of medical students found that 27% of respondents were regular smokers and that most students started smoking after entering medical school [27]; this situation is worse than in our case.

Data on alcohol, energy drink, and coffee consumption and their relationship to well-being is quite limited in the literature, therefore, in our survey, we looked at consumption habits. Participating students’ answers are presented in Tables 4 and 77.2% of the respondents drank alcohol only occasionally, and 11.6% drank alcohol 2–4 times a week. The proportion of respondents who never drank energy drinks was 66.6%. A similar pattern was observed with sleeping pills and sedatives or other psychoactive agents. We anticipated stronger correlations between well-being and the parameters we examined. However, the correlation rates we observed indicate a weak relationship. We assumed that students with low well-being and high-stress levels tend to consume more alcohol and energy drinks, as well as smoke more frequently. Nevertheless, an American survey showed that nearly 70% of the students consumed energy drinks almost daily. The study also showed a positive relationship between energy drink consumption and perceived stress and that energy drink consumption had a negative influence on academic performance [32]. These facts would indicate the need for further studies.

Sleep hygiene, the regularity and quality of sleep does certainly influence general well-being, consequently, in our study, we assessed students’ sleeping habits. When asking questions of its regularity and quality as well as, how do exams affect the sleep/ falling asleep, almost half of the students (42.2%) answered that they usually wake up tired in the morning and 7.9% reported that they experienced a bad mood the next day. As for the quality of the sleep, 40.5% considered it to be generally good and acceptable, while 46.2% found it rather poor and 13.4% of students considered it to be particularly unsatisfactory. Of the participants, 77.3% had no or only minor problems falling asleep, while 22.7% said that it took them a long time or that they could not fall asleep at all before the night of an exam. 85.2% of the students reported that the quality of sleep deteriorated that night. Our results confirmed a moderate or medium strong relationship between well-being. The results of the correlation analysis are presented in Table 6.

Comparing our finding to a Brazilian cross-sectional study investigating the prevalence of insomnia found that 22.6% of university students suffered from sleeping problems, which is better than what we have found [33].

Another cross-sectional study in Saudi Arabia examined the relationship between perceived stress, energy drink consumption, and academic performance among 237 pharmacy students. The study found that shorter sleep duration before exams and the use of medications for insomnia were significantly associated with poorer academic outcomes. Furthermore, less sleep duration was significantly correlated with fatigue, daytime sleepiness, and sleepiness during classes [34].

It is broadly discussed that regular physical activity is essential for both physical and mental well-being. Our results about the frequency of sport show that 11.1% of the study population did not exercise at all, 24.1% exercised occasionally, the majority 53.1% exercised regularly about 1 to 3 times a week. Assessing the frequency of sports, and exercise and their impact on well-being indicates an association between the frequency of sports activities and perceived well-being (F = 5.783, p < 0.001). To be able to improve the well-being of the students, we aimed to identify influential variables. To assess their impact, we developed a linear regression model. Potential impacting factors were selected based on correlations with well-being and on our assumption of their potential importance. Sports and wake-up cigarettes can have a significant impact, but these factors were excluded since they were reported in only about 20% of the sample. Regression model selection procedure

Model B with its variable combination had the highest explanatory power. When any of the independent variables was excluded the fit of the model decreased. The linearity fit diagnostic of the best-fit model is shown in Fig. 3.

Fig. 3Linearity fit of the “B regression model”

Linearity fit of the “B regression model”

The result indicates that the best level of explaining power by the regression model was when the following set of variables were combined: grade average + anxiety before written exam + academic year + stress. The inclusion or exclusion of the variable grade average did not really change the fit of the regression model.

It was interesting to see this combination, as we did assume that other factors would have played a more important role, such as anxiety before the oral exam, however the nature of an oral exam could be supportive in a way, by the fact that the examiner does ask questions with which the student could get some help, or to focus on crucial knowledge with their answers. The fact that academic performance was among the variables, was also underpinned by other studies [38].

At Semmelweis University, student counselling services are available in Hungarian, English, and German to help students cope with the challenges of studying. The Institute of Behavioural Sciences provides counselling for issues such as study-related anxieties, stress, relationship difficulties, loneliness, and low self-esteem.

A systematic review, focused on the effectiveness of mindfulness-based interventions of mental health status of university students and dropout rates. Results showed that mindfulness-based interventions did improved mental health compared to controls [39]. At the Semmelweis University the Department of Clinical Psychology is involved similarly in mindfulness by offering support to students; however, formal training is not available yet in the curriculum. To further support students, the university could consider: increasing awareness of the available counselling services through more active promotion and advertising to the student body. Expanding the availability of counsellors to meet the potential increase in demand for support, emphasizing the importance and benefits of seeking help when facing difficulties during the university experience.

The best-fit model also indicates that universities should focus their activities on improving well-being by concentrating on providing help regarding stress management and coping strategies of stress, as well as offering courses and other forms of help regarding learning methods and techniques. It seems that the first and final-year students need special attention. At the beginning of the studies, students face a high degree of uncertainty and anxiety regarding their wish to comply with expectations, whereas final-year students experience uncertainty and anxiety concerning how they can face future challenges in life after graduation. By implementing these measures, universities could ensure that students would get essential support.

Limitations of the study

The fact that our questionnaire was self-completed and self-declared, could have impacted the answers by causing recall bias; however, we acknowledge that this would have been difficult to be assessed.

Certainly, a follow-up study or a focus-group setting could have provided a deeper understanding of influential factors of well-being; thus, a focus-group study is planned to be done in the future.

Several publications point out that the COVID era had an impact on social relations and the process of new societal norm development. Therefore, it should be considered that our study was conducted in the post-COVID era; since our data mainly was compared to studies that were conducted before COVID, there might be some degree of heterogeneity within those data, that was not possible to take into account.

On the other hand, since in the literature, majority of studies were conducted in relation to medical students, and so there is limited information on students studying other disciplines in relation to health, such as pharmacy and health science students, as well as students studying conductive therapy; consequently, our overview highlights the special need of students studying at these various faculties, disciplines.

Conclusion

Our study aimed to assess the well-being of university students and to evaluate influencing factors, at faculties in relation to health and medicine, but who are not medical students. It is to our knowledge the first survey providing a comprehensive picture of the well-being of students studying “health science” in Hungary.

Our data reveals that a significant percentage of students experience poor or very poor well-being, with fluctuations observed throughout the study period. The lowest levels of well-being were recorded during the first and final academic years. Additionally, students’ well-being is positively associated with emotional support, physical activity, and sleep quality, while it shows a negative correlation with exam-related anxiety.

Smoking, consumption of sleeping pills and sedatives, as well as energy drinks could be considered indicators of poor well-being, and so students should get information from the university about this existing relationship and should be offered support to fight against their use.

Our findings suggest that universities can take an active role in enhancing student well-being in several ways:

supporting stress management activities and offering help for coping with stress,providing information on effective learning methods and techniques,educating students on how to face challenges in their chosen profession after graduation.
