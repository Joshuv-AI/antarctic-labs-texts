The detour technique: transcirculation stent coiling for complex cerebellar artery aneurysms. Illustrative cases
Authors: Ahmed Altaf, Nathan Yu, Om H. Gandhi, Sami Almasri, Luis O. Tierradentro- Garcia, Linda Bagley, Omar A. Choudhri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDCerebellar artery aneurysms are exceedingly rare, accounting for only 0.1%–0.3% of all intracranial aneurysms. They often present with wide necks and are usually treated with endovascular means through the vertebral arteries. Transcirculation approaches can offer ergonomic advantages when conventional anterograde endovascular approaches are technically challenging, particularly with incorporation of cerebellar artery origin from the aneurysm and less optimal stent placement angles.OBSERVATIONSThe authors present the transcirculation cases of 2 patients, a 41-year-old female with a 5- to 6-mm right superior cerebellar artery (SCA) aneurysm treated via right posterior cerebral artery P1-to-SCA stent-assisted coil embolization, and a 68-year-old female with a 3 × 3–mm right anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) aneurysm treated using a basilar-to-AICA stent coiling approach. Complete aneurysm occlusion was achieved in both cases without procedural complications. Postprocedural imaging confirmed excellent technical results with preserved parent vessel patency.LESSONSThe transcirculation technique offers a reliable alternative for posterior circulation aneurysms when conventional anterograde access is suboptimal. This study demonstrates its application in cerebellar artery aneurysms, where it provides more favorable stent working angles and supports durable occlusion. This technique is particularly advantageous when the parent cerebellar artery originates from the aneurysm sac, as it facilitates optimal stent placement and enables superior device delivery compared to conventional approaches. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25958

Cerebellar artery aneurysms are rare lesions that make up a small fraction of intracranial aneurysms but carry high mortality and rebleeding rates.1 Among these, posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) aneurysms are the most common, with reported incidence rates up to 3%.1 Superior cerebellar artery (SCA) and anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) aneurysms are even less frequent, with incidences up to 1.7% and 1.5%, respectively.2,3 Although endovascular treatment can be used to treat these aneurysms, it remains challenging due to their anatomical characteristics, small vessel caliber, and thin vessel wall, which put the vessel at risk of rebleeding; the complexity of surrounding structures; and the acute angulation of the parent vessels from the basilar artery.1,4–6

The endovascular management of wide-neck aneurysms in the posterior circulation often uses techniques such as balloon-assisted coiling, stent-assisted coiling (SAC), intrasaccular devices, and, less often, flow diversion. The acute angle between the SCA and basilar artery in most cases makes anterograde stent delivery difficult.7 Similarly, AICA aneurysms present challenges due to the vessel’s small caliber, tortuous course, and critical relationship to eloquent areas.8 Furthermore, there is a significantly increased risk for possible thromboembolic complications secondary to deployment of the stents in vessels with small diameters.9

Transcirculation techniques, first described by Moret et al., involve navigating from one circulation to another through communicating arteries.10 This approach has shown promise for treating complex aneurysms where conventional access routes are suboptimal.11 Recent multicenter studies have demonstrated the safety and efficacy of transcirculation approaches for SAC of intracranial aneurysms.12,13

Although transcirculation approaches have been described for intracranial aneurysms, their application to cerebellar arteries is exceptionally rare. We report 2 cases of these rare posterior circulation aneurysms successfully treated with transcirculation SAC, demonstrating the technical advantages and clinical utility of this “detour technique” in the light of prior literature.

Illustrative Cases

Patients with unruptured cerebellar artery aneurysms were selected for transcirculation approaches after multidisciplinary review when anterograde stent placement was limited by 1)wide-neck aneurysm morphology involving or arising adjacent to the cerebellar artery origin, 2) an acute takeoff angle precluding safe stent deployment from a direct approach, and 3) an adequate posterior communicating artery (PComA) caliber (≥ 0.8 mm) to accommodate a 0.017-inch microcatheter system. These patients had undergone cerebral angiography to allow detailed evaluation with 3D reconstructions to assess aneurysm angio-anatomy and to plan treatment strategy.

All patients received dual antiplatelet therapy (either aspirin 325 mg and Plavix 75 mg, or aspirin 81 mg and Brilinta 90 mg BID) 5–7 days before the procedure. Platelet function testing was performed to confirm optimal platelet inhibition (P2Y12 reaction units [PRUs] < 180).

Under general anesthesia, bilateral upper extremity radial or ulnar access was obtained to allow simultaneous catheterization of the anterior and posterior circulations, enabling ergonomic microcatheter positioning and improved device delivery angles. A triaxial catheter construct was established in each circulation to provide stability for stent and coil delivery systems. Three-dimensional rotational angiography was performed from both the anterior and posterior circulations, and a 3D-3D fusion dataset was generated to identify optimal working projections for aneurysm access and device deployment.

An LVIS EVO braided stent (MicroVention) compatible with a 0.017-inch microcatheter was navigated through the transcirculation route and deployed across the aneurysm neck. A separate microcatheter, introduced via vertebral artery (VA) access, was advanced into the cerebellar artery aneurysm sac. Sequential coil embolization was performed through the jailed microcatheter until complete aneurysm occlusion was achieved. Patients were monitored overnight in the neurocritical care unit and discharged the following morning. Dual antiplatelet therapy was continued for 3–6 months, followed by aspirin monotherapy for life. Six-month vascular imaging confirmed occlusion and parent vessel patency.

Case 1

A 41-year-old woman presented with persistent migraines. Her head CT showed no evidence of hemorrhage, but an occipital arteriovenous malformation (AVM) with an associated prenidal posterior circulation aneurysm was noted on CTA. A cerebral angiogram identified a Spetzler-Martin grade II right occipital AVM and an associated 3.9 × 4.2 × 5–mm dysplastic, unruptured saccular aneurysm of the right SCA located at the basilar apex (Figs. S1 and S2).

A staged endovascular approach was planned with AVM treatment prioritized due to higher hemorrhage risk. The SCA aneurysm, being dysplastic but unruptured with stable morphology, was managed expectantly during AVM treatment. Following successful staged AVM treatment involving embolization and resection over a 4-month period, definitive management of the SCA aneurysm was undertaken 6 months from the initial presentation.

The technical rationale for using the transcirculation approach was based on the aneurysm’s challenging location and acute angulation from the basilar artery, combined with altered vascular anatomy from prior AVM treatment and the right SCA’s origin from the aneurysm base. The more favorable SCA–right posterior cerebral artery (PCA) P1 angle provided optimal geometry for device delivery via transcirculation compared to the prohibitively acute SCA-basilar angle (Fig. S3).

Preprocedural optimization included dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin 81 mg and ticagrelor 90 mg twice daily, owing to the patient’s nonresponsiveness to Plavix. The patient’s baseline neurological examination was intact with a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 0.

The intervention was performed under general anesthesia. Bilateral radial artery access was established using 6-Fr guiding catheters positioned in the vertebrobasilar system. Procedural anticoagulation included 5000 units of intravenous heparin with therapeutic activated clotting time (ACT) > 250 during the procedure. The transcirculation approach utilized a Headway 17 microcatheter (MicroVention) for stent delivery and a Headway Duo 156 microcatheter (MicroVention) for coiling. The transcirculation navigation was accomplished by advancing the Headway 17 microcatheter using a Synchro2 microwire (Stryker Neurovascular) through the right internal carotid artery (ICA) and right PComA to the target right SCA. Device deployment commenced with the placement of an LVIS EVO 4 × 21–mm stent from the right P1 segment across the aneurysm neck into the right SCA. Coil embolization proceeded with sequential deployment of five coils through the jailed microcatheter. The framing coil was a 4-mm × 4-cm OptiMAX coil (Balt), followed by a 3mm × 3–cm OptiMAX secondary coil. Two HydroSoft 3D 2 × 2–cm coils (MicroVention) provided volume filling, and an OptiMAX 1.5-mm × 2-cm coil was used as the finishing coil. Five coils achieved approximately 40% packing density and Raymond-Roy class 1 occlusion without complications.

The patient exhibited no new deficits and was discharged on postoperative day 1 with an mRS score of 0. Postprocedural antiplatelet management included aspirin 81 mg and ticagrelor 90 mg twice daily. Three-month angiography confirmed occlusion, prompting ticagrelor discontinuation. Lifelong aspirin 81 mg was continued in accordance with institutional protocol. At 6 months, neurological evaluation and surveillance MR angiography confirmed persistent occlusion.

Case 2

A 68-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with a transient episode of speech disturbance. Digital subtraction angiography revealed an unruptured broad-based right AICA aneurysm measuring 3.1 × 2.6 × 3 mm. Her past medical history was significant for hypertension, mitral valve prolapse, and migraine disorder.

Preprocedural digital subtraction angiography with 3D image reconstruction provided detailed anatomical characterization of the lesion. The aneurysm demonstrated broad-based saccular morphology incorporating the parent AICA vessel (Fig. S4).

The technical rationale for a transcirculation approach was based on the unfavorable anatomy for direct AICA catheterization. The acute AICA takeoff angle combined with the small vessel caliber made conventional anterograde access technically challenging and potentially hazardous. The transcirculation approach via the left PComA provided optimal working angles for device delivery (Fig. 3 and Fig. S5). A preprocedural antiplatelet regimen consisting of aspirin 81 mg daily and ticagrelor 90 mg twice daily 7 days prior to the procedure was used. Appropriate platelet inhibition was confirmed with PRUs of 4 and 5, indicating optimal antiplatelet activity. Procedural anticoagulation included 5000 units of intravenous heparin with a therapeutic ACT > 250 during the procedure.

Under general anesthesia, bilateral arterial access was established with a 6-Fr Zebra guide catheter (Q’apel Medical) via the right radial artery, which was parked in the left ICA, and a 7-Fr RIST guide catheter (Medtronic Neurovascular) via the left ulnar artery, which was parked in the left VA. Radial artery access included administration of a cocktail containing 5 mg of verapamil, 200 μg of nitroglycerin, and 2000 units of heparin.

The transcirculation approach required navigation from the left ICA to the left PComA. Using roadmap guidance from fusion 3D rotational angiography via left ICA and left VA injections, the Vecta 46 intermediate catheter (Stryker Neurovascular) was used over the Headway 17 microcatheter and Synchro-2 microwire to select the left PComA and navigate into the left P1 segment, basilar artery, and right AICA segment past the aneurysm. The right AICA aneurysm dome was then catheterized with a Headway Duo 167 microcatheter (MicroVention) and a 0.014-inch Synchro2 microwire. Navigation proceeded smoothly with excellent visualization using simultaneous bilateral contrast injections.

Device deployment utilized a staged approach for optimal aneurysm treatment. Initial partial coil placement was performed to establish aneurysm access and geometry (Fig. 4). An LVIS EVO 4 × 13–mm stent was deployed through the positioned Headway 17 microcatheter in the AICA. The stent was deployed in the AICA distally and basilar artery proximally. Following stent deployment, coil embolization was performed via the jailed Headway Duo microcatheter. The pathway shows navigation from the left ICA through the left PComA to the basilar artery, and subsequently accessing the right AICA. This represents the first reported contralateral transcirculation approach for AICA aneurysm treatment, overcoming the technical challenges of direct AICA catheterization due to acute vessel takeoff angles and small caliber. The route provides superior working geometry and smoother navigation compared to conventional anterograde approaches, enabling successful stent-assisted coil embolization.

The pathway shows navigation from the left ICA through the left PComA to the basilar artery, and subsequently accessing the right AICA. Coil embolization achieved complete aneurysm occlusion using three sequentially deployed OptiMAX coils. The coiling strategy included an OptiMAX 2.5 × 5–cm framing coil, followed by an OptiMAX 1.5 × 2 cm packing coil, and concluded with an OptiMAX 1 × 1–cm finishing coil. Three coils achieved approximately 54% packing density and Raymond-Roy class 1 occlusion; there were no procedural complications.

Parent vessel assessment confirmed preserved AICA and basilar artery patency with no compromise to flow. The patient tolerated the intervention without complication and exhibited no new neurological deficits. The patient was discharged in the morning after overnight observation. Dual antiplatelet therapy was continued for 6 months. Six-month follow-up cerebral angiography confirmed continued occlusion, at which point the patient was transitioned to aspirin 81 mg daily. The patient remained neurologically intact on long-term follow-up with no active symptoms.

Discussion

Observations

These cases contribute to the limited literature on transcirculation approaches for cerebellar artery aneurysms (Table 1). Our comprehensive literature review identified only 3 prior cases of SCA aneurysm treatment via transcirculation techniques, with no reported cases of AICA aneurysm treatment using this approach. While there are prior accounts of transcirculation PICA aneurysm treatments, they were completed from the posterior circulation going across the vertebrobasilar junction. Our cases demonstrate two distinct transcirculation strategies that expand the therapeutic armamentarium for these challenging lesions. Case 1 utilized an ipsilateral P1-to-SCA approach, consistent with the limited prior literature. Sakurada et al. reported 2 similar cases using identical transcirculation routes with Neuroform Atlas stents, achieving comparable technical success and clinical outcomes.7 However, our case involved complex post-AVM anatomy, demonstrating the technique’s versatility even in altered vascular landscapes. The evolution from Neuroform Atlas to LVIS EVO stents may offer improved deliverability and wall apposition in small-caliber vessels, although long-term comparative data remain limited.14–17

The second case is the first reported instance of use of the transcirculation approach for AICA aneurysm treatment. This strategy overcomes challenges with AICA catheterization by utilizing the transcirculation pathway. The PComA-basilar-AICA route provides a less tortuous microcatheter path compared to the acute angle encountered with a conventional anterograde approach.

Successful transcirculation treatment requires careful patient selection based on specific anatomical criteria. Critical factors include adequate communicating artery caliber (minimum 0.8 mm in our experience), absence of significant atherosclerotic disease, and favorable vessel geometry for device navigation.11,18 A staged approach with preprocedural 3D angiography with quantitative vessel measurements is essential for route planning and device selection.

Importantly, the transcirculation approach places both the anterior and posterior circulations at risk during the procedure, necessitating precise patient selection and technical execution. In our technique, simultaneous VA access for coil delivery is necessary to avoid placing two microcatheters through the narrow PComA, which would risk vasospasm or vessel injury. Additionally, simultaneous VA access is needed for contrast opacification of the posterior circulation and to visualize the target aneurysm well. This is a key aspect of making the procedure safe by mapping the anterior and posterior circulation fusion anatomy during the case, especially since slight displacement of vessels can happen with introduction of catheters. An alternative single-catheter approach involves crossing the stent with the same microcatheter used for stent deployment, which offers the advantage of accessing only one circulation and may reduce overall procedure time. However, this technique has important limitations including difficulty navigating through stent struts at an unfavorable angle, potential for catheter tip herniation into the aneurysm sac during crossing attempts, and constraints on stent selection due to the need for larger cell designs to facilitate crossing. The dual-access strategy used in our cases, while requiring coordination across two circulations, provides more reliable catheter positioning and broader stent options.

The acute SCA-basilar angulation (typically < 30°) versus the more obtuse SCA-P1 angle (typically > 140°) provides quantitative justification for transcirculation in SCA aneurysms.Similarly, AICA aneurysms with acute takeoff angles and small vessel calibers benefit from alternative access routes that provide improved working geometry. Optimal antiplatelet response, confirmed by platelet function testing, is critical for stent-based procedures, with PRUs < 180 indicating adequate inhibition.19

Device selection remains crucial for technical success. Low-profile 0.017-inch compatible stents enable navigation through small communicating arteries while maintaining deliverability.20 The LVIS EVO platform’s enhanced radiopacity and flexibility facilitate precise deployment in challenging anatomy, although comparative studies with other modern stent platforms are needed.20–22 Deployment of these stents in submillimeter vessels is off-label since LVIS EVO is indicated for vessels 2 mm or larger, but is well tolerated based on prior accounts.23,24 The cerebellar vessels are usually 0.5–2.5 mm; therefore, by default our technique requires deployment of these stents in small vessels. We have found that the high radiopacity and resheathability of the LVIS EVO stent make it a reliable tool for assessing the stent opening in smaller vessels before definitive deployment. There are prior reports of successful deployment of stents in cerebellar vessels of smaller caliber.25,26 It is critical to size the stent to the larger basilar artery to allow successful stent apposition and appropriate shouldering, and to prevent flattening and malposition.

While transcirculation approaches are safe, the complexity of these cases should not be trivialized. This detour technique should only be performed with extensive planning, adequate heparinization, and when less complex anterograde approaches are not available. By default, the transcirculation approach places two circulations at risk with the catheter manipulation involved.

While balloon-assisted coiling and flow diversion represent alternative endovascular approaches, transcirculation SAC offers specific advantages for cerebellar artery aneurysms. Unlike balloon remodeling, which requires multiple inflations and carries rupture risk, stent scaffolding provides permanent neck remodeling with potentially superior long-term occlusion.27–29 Flow diversion, although increasingly utilized for challenging aneurysms, may pose risks in small-caliber cerebellar arteries due to perforator coverage concerns and the need for extended dual antiplatelet therapy.30,31 Indirect basilar flow diversion remains a bailout. With its variable occlusion rates, it is a potential option through placement of the diverter in the basilar artery across the origin of the involved cerebellar artery. In addition to a high risk of thromboembolic events from jailing the basilar perforators, the PICA literature demonstrates an occlusion rate of 81% from flow diversion.32,33 Furthermore, next-generation flow diverters have been successfully placed in cerebellar vessels in some series with durable occlusion.34 In addition, intrasaccular devices can be used in some of these cases in an off-label fashion.35 The parent vessel angle, size, and aneurysm morphology in our cases made intrasaccular therapy a less attractive option.

Microsurgical clipping, while definitive, requires complex operative approaches with associated morbidity. The ventral location, particularly with proximal SCA and AICA aneurysms, makes microsurgical approaches challenging, especially in obtaining proximal control. The SCA’s relationship to cranial nerves III, IV, and V, and the AICA’s proximity to brainstem structures, create substantial surgical risks that endovascular approaches can circumvent.36,37 However, surgical approaches should always be considered for cases in which endovascular treatment fails or is not amenable, especially in younger patients.38,39

These cases demonstrate that a detour technique can provide better working angles for stent placement, allowing excellent direct endovascular treatment and obviating the need for more invasive microsurgical treatments. Long-term angiographic follow-up is essential to assess the durability of transcirculation-assisted treatments, particularly regarding in-stent stenosis rates in small-caliber vessels.

This 2-patient, single-center series has short-term follow-up and selection bias toward specific anatomies suitable for transcirculation. Hence, generalizability is limited and may not reflect outcomes achievable across different institutions or operator experience levels. Longer multicenter studies are needed to further clarify indications, risks, and durability.

Lessons

Transcirculation approaches expand endovascular options for cerebellar artery aneurysms when anterograde working angles are suboptimal. The detour technique enables SAC of challenging proximal cerebellar aneurysms by providing a less angulated trajectory for stent deployment and more reliable neck coverage. Although it provides an elegant construct, it should be reserved for cases in which simpler anterograde endovascular approaches, including intrasaccular devices, are not feasible. It is important to complete planning angiography to assess the entire circle of Willis and to have an alternate plan available. Use of a triaxial catheter construct is imperative for precise control. Additionally, antiplatelet response should be documented, and complete heparinization maintained while undertaking this technique.
