Molecular endotypes in musculoskeletal disorders: a systematic review
Authors: Ming Liu, Jingyi Huang, Ali Mobasheri, Proton Rahman, Guangju Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMusculoskeletal (MSK) disorders are highly heterogeneous and have diverse cellular and molecular pathobiological mechanisms, termed “endotypes”. This systematic review aimed to summarize current knowledge of molecular endotypes in MSK disorders.MethodsThis review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). Five databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Ovid Medline, and Embase were searched for publications on molecular endotypes of MSK disorders classified by the International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (ICD-11) that were published prior to September 1st, 2024. Articles were included if they were peer-reviewed original research articles on adult-onset human MSK disorder molecular endotypes/subtypes that used directly collected human specimen, in English language, and had full text available. Citations of the included studies were also screened manually using the same inclusion criteria.ResultsOur search identified 44,660 unique records and 34 eligible studies were included. The majority of these studies focused on osteoarthritis (OA, n = 14) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA, n = 12), with a small number of studies focused on other MSK conditions. In OA, high- and low-inflammation clusters were consistently identified using different types of samples, and high- and low-pain clusters were observed in several other studies. In RA, endotypes with different levels of inflammation markers or treatment responses were reported in several studies, and one study reported that RA patients displayed two different dysbiotic microbiota patterns which corresponded to different responses to treatments.ConclusionsMSK endotype research is still in its early stage. More endotype studies on MSK disorders are needed to assist in designing clinical trials and developing personalized therapy strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10145-z.Keywords: Musculoskeletal disorders, Molecular endotypes, Osteoarthritis, Rheumatoid arthritis

Background

Musculoskeletal (MSK) disorders encompass a broad range of conditions that affect muscles, bones, joints, and adjacent connective tissues. They are the leading contributor to disability and need for rehabilitation worldwide, responsible for 162 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2021 [1] and projected 281 million by 2050 [2]. MSK disorders greatly lower patients’ quality of life and impose significant socioeconomic burden on both personal and societal levels [3]. Considerable efforts have been made to develop effective treatments for MSK disorders. However, the patient response to these treatments vary widely [4], indicating the existence of patient subgroups corresponding to differential therapeutic responses. This highlights the importance of disease subtype identification for patient stratification in clinical trials to develop tailored treatments.

In 2011, Lötvall et al. proposed the term “endotype” which refers to a subtype of a condition defined by a distinct pathophysiological mechanism [5]. Compared to disease subtypes classified by clinical measurements and features (phenotypes), endotypes can link pathobiological mechanisms with visible properties via specific biochemical markers, which are quantifiable characteristics of biological and pathological processes, and can be further translated into pathway-specific diagnostic tests, which opens the gate to early diagnosis, accurate disease classification, and individualized treatments [6]. It has been recognized that instead of being individual diseases, some MSK disorders are spectrums of diseases with diverse cellular and molecular pathophysiological mechanisms [7]. The MSK research community has invested increased efforts on MSK endotypes to better understand these disorders and guide targeted drug development. This review aimed to summarize current knowledge of molecular endotypes in MSK disorders through a thorough literature review.

Methods

This review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [8] (Additional file 1).

Search strategy

MSK disorders included in the search were in accordance with Chapter 15 of the International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (ICD-11) [9], “Diseases of the musculoskeletal system or connective tissue”, with the exclusion of the following conditions: non-adult-onset diseases, secondary conditions, monogenic diseases, infection related conditions, mineral deposit diseases, and non-pathological fracture.

Five databases including PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Ovid Medline, and Embase were searched for publications on endotypes of included MSK disorders that were published before September 1 st, 2024. In accordance with the endotype definition by Lötvall et al. [5], we defined “endotype” as a subtype of a condition defined by profiles of biochemical markers (DNA, RNA, protein, metabolites/compound) or microbiota which closely interact with host, and used this definition in the literature search. The key phrases used for the search were: (musculoskeletal system diseases/connective tissue diseases/specific disease name) + endotype/subtype/subgroup/cluster + gene/genom(ic/e)/methylat(ed/ion)/histone modifi(ed/cation)/epigeno(me/mic)/RNA/transcriptom(ic/e)/protein/proteom(ic/e)/metabol(omic/ome/ite/ism)/biochemical/microbio(ta/tic/me). The search queries and filters are presented in Additional file 2. Controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH/Emtree) were not used due to the exclusion of specific MSK disorders mentioned above. All five databases were searched on September 1 st, 2024.

Study selection

Two reviewers independently conducted abstract and full-text screening according to the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. Citations of the included studies were also screened manually to identify records that met the inclusion criterion but were not identified by the database search.

Inclusion criteria: original peer-reviewed research articles on adult-onset human MSK disorder endotypes/subtypes/subgroups based on genomic/epigenomic/transcriptomic/proteomic/metabolic/microbiotic data using directly collected human specimen.

Inclusion criteria: original peer-reviewed research articles on adult-onset human MSK disorder endotypes/subtypes/subgroups based on genomic/epigenomic/transcriptomic/proteomic/metabolic/microbiotic data using directly collected human specimen.

Exclusion criteria: no full text; not in English; not population-based study; pure computational study/review article/meta-analysis/clinical trial/case report/guideline; classification was purely based on patient characteristics/imaging markers/clinical presentations/cell profiles; sample size < 100 before clustering and no replication.

Exclusion criteria: no full text; not in English; not population-based study; pure computational study/review article/meta-analysis/clinical trial/case report/guideline; classification was purely based on patient characteristics/imaging markers/clinical presentations/cell profiles; sample size < 100 before clustering and no replication.

Data extraction

The following information was extracted from all eligible articles: author(s), disease(s) studied, disease joint(s), population(s) for endotype identification—discovery and replication cohorts (if applicable), sample size(s), sample type(s), biomarker type(s), number of markers/factors used for cluster analysis, key biomarkers/factors for cluster analysis, number(s) of clusters, sample size in each cluster, and main findings. Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers. The extracted information was summarized using a tabulated format. In case of unclear or insufficient information, attempts were made to contact the first and/or corresponding authors.

Study risk of bias assessment

Studies were assessed using a narrative analysis on the following aspects [10]—patient recruitment/selection processes, study populations, methods of sample collection, methods of biomolecule measurement, validity of outcome measurement.

Results

A total of 164 MSK disorder categories summarized using 122 key words/phrases were used in the search (Additional file 3), which identified a total of 71,573 records, out of which 18,808 were from PubMed, 11,738 from Web of Science, 19,459 from Scopus, 2,758 from Ovid Medline, and 18,810 from Embase. After duplicate removal and abstract screening, full text of the remaining 67 studies were accessed, and 33 studies were further excluded based on the exclusion criteria. Thirty-four (34) studies were included in this review and no records were identified during the citation search. The detailed screening process is presented in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

Characteristics of each study including article first author, disease(s) studied, study hypothesis, disease joint(s), population(s) for endotype identification—discovery and replication cohorts (if applicable), ethnicity, sample size(s), and sample type(s) were extracted and summarized during full text review and are presented in Table 1, Table 2, and Fig. 2 (panel A and B). Among the 34 included studies, 14 focused on osteoarthritis (OA) [11–24] (n = 8, 2, 2, 1, and 1 for knee, hip, knee and hip, spine, and mixed joints, respectively), 12 on rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [25–36], two on osteoporosis (OP) [37, 38], and two on mixed rheumatic diseases [39, 40]. The other four studies focused on anti-nuclear antibody (ANA)-associated arthritis [41], ankylosing spondylitis (AS) [42], axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) [43], and psoriatic arthritis (PsA) [44], respectively. The most used sample type was blood or blood component(s) (n = 30), followed by urine (n = 4), cartilage (n = 3), synovial fluid/membrane (n = 3), fecal samples (n = 3), subchondral bone (n = 1), and subgingival plaque (n = 1). A total of thirteen (13) studies used multiple types of samples.

Risk of bias in studies

Study settings were clearly defined in all 34 studies and patient ethnicities were reported in 11. Six out of the 11 studies were conducted (predominantly) in White, two in Black and White, one in Danish White, and two in indigenous populations (Fig. 2C). Valid methods for sample collection and storage were used in 26 studies, while 31 studies employed valid methods for measuring biomolecule levels. Standard criteria were used for outcome measurement in all 34 studies. The detailed quality assessment results are presented in Additional file 4.

MSK disorder endotypes

The majority of the studies identified disease endotypes/subgroups based on protein/peptide/metabolite concentrations and autoantibody levels/reactivity (n = 20), while the applicability of gene expression levels (n = 3), microbiota abundances (n = 2), metal concentrations (n = 1), and the integrative multi-omics profiles (n = 1) was also explored in some studies. Patient characteristics and clinical measurements and features were used in combination with biochemical markers in seven studies. Several clustering methods including hierarchical cluster analysis (n = 15), K-means (n = 10), random forest (n = 2), Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP, n = 1), Dirichlet multinomial mixtures (DMM, n = 1), hard clustering method (n = 1), Gaussian mixture modelling (GMM, n = 2), co-prevalence analysis (n = 1), single-cell consensus clustering (SC3, n = 1), deep latent space fusion (DLSF, n = 1), and t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE, n = 1) were utilized. Principal component analysis (PCA) was also used in combination with other clustering methods (n = 3). The number of clusters reported ranged from two to four in most of the studies. Results for individual studies are present in Table 3, Table 4, and Fig. 2 (panel D and E).

Discussion

OA endotypes

In OA, inflammation and pain were the primary characteristics that distinguish between subgroups or representing certain subgroups. Additionally, disease progression has been evaluated in several studies (Fig. 2F).

In knee OA (KOA), patients were classified into high and low inflammation subgroups using expression levels of 21 and eight genes, respectively, measured in peripheral blood leukocytes (PBLs) [12]. Classification using mRNA levels of the key gene, interleukin (IL)−1β, in two independent validation cohorts showed that the resulted subclasses shared the same characteristics as those identified in the discovery cohort with the high inflammation group having significantly higher pain scores. More joint space narrowing (JSN) was also observed in this group in one of the validation cohorts, characterizing this group as aggressive OA with inflammatory phenotype. In addition to being the main feature distinguishing between subgroups, inflammation was also used to characterize at least one of the clusters in a number of studies. Angelini et al. [11] identified three OA endotypes based on the levels of 16 baseline serum and urine biochemical markers: low tissue turnover, structural damage, and systemic inflammation. The authors also validated the clustering in another cohort. However, the cluster separation in the discovery cohort was not clear, and all markers showed differences in median cluster values between the discovery and validation cohorts. Using a transcriptomics approach, Yuan et al. [24] grouped KOA patients into four clusters using expression levels of 4,000 genes in cartilage. The main features of clusters C1 to C4 were glycosaminoglycan metabolic disorder, collagen metabolic disorder with severe osteophytes, activated sensory neuron with early joint replacement, and inflammation with narrow joint space, respectively. Analysis on crosstalk between cartilage, synovium, and subchondral bone highlighted vasculature development in C2 leading to osteophyte formation, and ossification, osteoblast differentiation regulation, and vasculature development in C4 leading to narrow joint space. Validation in two public datasets showed that C1 and C2 were consistently identified while C3 was absent, which was proposed to be due to the small sample size or older age in these cohorts. According to the differentially expressed genes and proteins and pathways involved, the authors suggested specific potential treatments for each OA subgroup. This was a relatively thorough OA endotype study, but the sample size was small. Pain was the other feature distinguishing between OA subgroups. Giordano et al. [15] classified KOA patients into two subtypes according to serum levels of 48 inflammatory proteins. C1 had lower pain and better function, and C2 had higher pain and worse function before and after knee replacement. Interestingly, the significant inflammatory proteins separating the subtypes were all downregulated in C2, which appears to contradict the conclusion that C2 was a high-pain group. The authors did not provide any plausible explanation for this observation. In a study conducted in OA patients undergoing knee replacement using a metabolomics approach, two clusters were identified based on plasma concentrations of 151 metabolites, and genes linked to the 24 key metabolites were enriched into tRNA acylation and B-vitamin metabolism pathways [20]. This study was unable to examine the differences in OA clinical phenotypes or trajectories because all study participants were at the end stage of the disease.

Three studies used combinations of biochemical markers and clinical features/assessment/patient characteristics for endotype identification. Calvet et al. reported two high- and two low-inflammation phenotypes in female KOA patients [13]. The identified endotype profiles were only partially replicated in the replication male cohort (n = 23), which could be due to the small sample size and/or sex differences. In another study conducted by Nelson et al. [18], four KOA subgroups were identified: female progressor, male progressor, largely female non-progressor, and largely male non-progressor. The difference between progressors and non-progressors within the same sex was much less than the difference between men and women regardless of progression status. These two studies highlighted the importance of sex-specific considerations in OA study. Egsgaard et al. [14] identified four KOA clusters characterized as having low sensitivity to pain, being in a transitional stage from mild to moderate/severe pain, typical knee pain patients, and highly sensitive knee pain patients, respectively. Although all studies used circulating (plasma/serum) protein levels in combination with other patient features, the findings did not correlate with each other likely due to the differences in variables and clinical outcomes examined and the clustering methods used. A common weakness of these studies was that the contributions of biochemical markers were either small or unclear, limiting their ability in explaining the underlying molecular mechanisms driving these endotypes or guiding the development of personalized management strategy. The majority of KOA endotype studies had small sample sizes and some clusters had fewer than 20 patients; thus validation in larger cohorts is necessary.

In hip OA (HOA), inflammation, pain, and progression were also the key elements in characterizing patient subgroups. Huang et al. identified two HOA endotypes using plasma metabolomic profiles[17]. Multi-omics data showed that the four metabolites upregulated in one cluster, γ-aminobutyric acid, spermine, aconitic acid, and succinic acid, were positively correlated with pro-inflammatory cytokines and associated with genes related to energy demand and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, characterizing this cluster as having severe inflammation and alteration in energy metabolism, and the other cluster as a group with moderate inflammation. This was the only study that highlighted the role of energy metabolism in OA endotypes, adding an interesting piece to the OA endotype puzzle. Patients in this study were all end-stage OA patients, so whether the findings are also applicable to patients in early stage of the disease needs further investigation. Using a combination of three baseline clinical variables and one biomarker score (serum CTX-I + urine CTX-II), Sapundzhiev et al. identified three subgroups of patients representing low (C1), medium (C2), and high (C3) pain and function limitations, respectively, closely associated with hypertrophic, intermediate, and atrophic X-ray patterns observed eight years prior[21]. A secondary classification using a nonbiochemical marker, bone mineral density (BMD) score change from baseline to 36-month showed that C2 was split into two clusters associated with female rapid progressors and male slow progressors, respectively, indicating the sex differences in OA progression. Patients from different subgroups had different therapeutic responses to treatments with corticosteroids being the most effective in C1, hyaluronic acid in C2, and zoledronic acid in C3 and female rapid progressors.

In mixed knee and hip OA patients, two molecular clusters were identified using RNA sequencing data in synovium and low-grade (less degenerated) cartilage, respectively [22]. Cartilage clustering was independent of the synovium clusters, however, the differentially expressed genes between clusters in both tissue types were related to inflammation, extracellular matrix-related, and cell adhesion pathways, indicating the molecular profiles of involved joint tissues might experience synchronized alterations in early stage of the disease. A seven-gene classifier was validated in an independent KOA cohort. No discrete subgrouping was observed in high-grade (more degenerated) OA cartilage, indicating less clear variation in molecular profiles in cartilage with advanced degeneration. Another study classified knee and hip OA patients into three clusters using plasma metabolomic profiles [23]. The key metabolites separating these clusters were butyrylcarnitine, arginine, phosphatidylcholine, and lysophosphatidylcholine, linking these clusters to muscle weakness, arginine deficiency, and low inflammatory OA, respectively. This study was conducted using a relatively large cohort, however, similar to one of the studies in HOA, the findings might be limited to end-stage OA patients due to the study population.

In spinal OA, three patient subgroups were identified based on profiles of 10 serum and urine protein/metabolite biomarkers associated with lumbar spine structural changes, inflammation, and pain with C1 being a reference group with lower levels of pain and structural degenerative changes, C2 representing structural degenerative changes, associated with disc space narrowing and facet joint OA, and C3 representing structural degenerative changes and inflammation with pain, associated with lower back pain [16]. These findings could help differentiate particular subgroups of patients who may have a nociception generating structure resulting in pain to determine targeted interventions.

Another OA subtype analysis was conducted in a large cohort of OA patients of various joint categories (knee, hip, arm, spine, and foot) and non-OA participants [19]. Fourteen subgroups were identified using clinical measurements, patient characteristics, and clinical features at baseline, with three subgroups being strongly predictive, six modestly predictive, and five least predictive for OA diagnosis within five years. The full variable list was not provided but the only biochemical marker in the top six features in the model was vitamin D level. The authors then defined 14 sets of rules for the 14 subgroups mostly using patient characteristics, socioeconomic status, and clinical features with insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol measurement being the only biochemical markers in one rule set each, and then applied these rules to a validation cohort, in which the proportion of individuals in each cluster and percentage of cases between clusters correlated with those in the discovery cohort. Metabolomics, proteomics, and genomics data were also available for the discovery cohort; however, they were only analyzed for OA risk prediction, not endotype identification or characterization, which was a missed opportunity for deeper understanding underlying mechanisms of OA subtypes [17].

When performing cross-study comparisons, we observed that all studies using plasma metabolomics approaches [17, 20, 23] identified different endotypes. These discrepancies may be attributed to the differences in the joints studied (hip, knee, knee and hip, respectively), profiling platforms used, and clustering analytic methods applied (UMAP, K-mean, hierarchical clustering, respectively). Additionally, metabolome is influenced not only by genetic makeup but also environmental factors, making it a more sensitive and potentially more variable measure than other omics approaches. Despite these inconsistencies, these studies all explored endotype-associated pathways, which may still provide valuable insights for personalized OA management.

Similarly, differences in joint sites, profiling platforms, and clustering methodologies were observed in two transcriptomic studies using synovium or cartilage samples [22, 24]. Nevertheless, both studies identified an endotype characterized by high degree of inflammation. This inflammatory endotype was also reported in a study using targeted gene expression levels in PBLs [12], although it was not consistently observed in validation cohorts. Notably, the findings from metabolomics and transcriptomics studies did not align, which is not surprising as systemic alterations do not always reflect local tissue status in OA [45]. Instead, these approaches may provide complementary perspectives.

In contrast, studies using circulating protein levels for clustering [11, 15, 16] showed greater consistency. One KOA and one spine OA study both identified three endotypes with similar clinical features: high and low levels of structural change/progression, and structural change with pain and inflammation. These protein-based endotypes appear to transcend joint-specific differences, supporting the view that OA might be a systemic syndrome rather than solely a local disease [46], a perspective that is gaining increasing acceptance in OA research community.

Among the six studies including validation cohorts, three fully replicated their discovery findings, while the other three showed partial replication. Notably, two of the partially replicated studies used combined proteins and clinical/patient characteristics for clustering, suggesting that this integrative approach may be less stable and underscoring the importance of robust validation.

Overall, the clinical characteristics associated with identified endotypes were mostly related to pain, inflammation, and progression. One study also reported differences in pain and function before and after knee replacement across endotypes. However, the limited number of available studies constrains firm conclusions. More studies are needed to evaluate the consistency across studies in terms of biomarker modality, tissue source, clustering methodology, and clinical relevance.

RA endotypes

The majority of RA endotype studies used anticitrullinated protein antibodies (ACPAs) for endotype identification and/or characterization as they are closely associated with the development and severity of RA [47]. Using four and three ACPAs, respectively, Manca et al. [29] identified 2 clusters of RA patients with one cluster having higher levels of ACPCs, frequency of rheumatoid factor (RF) positivity, and lung involvement compared to the other. A hierarchical cluster analysis using 19 ACPAs resulted in three clusters: negative for all serologies, negative for non-canonical (independently expressed antibody) Fib β 62–81 cit 72 serology, and positive and enriched for Fib β 62–81 cit 72 and Fib α 36–50 cit 3 [34]. The implications of these clusters in RA development or severity were not explored as RA endotype was not the focus of this study. A study investigating clinical relevance of ACPA profiles in early RA patients identified twelve distinct clusters using reactivities with 20 citrullinated peptides, highlighting the heterogeneity of ACPA response in RA patients, although these ACPA profiles were not correlated with clinical features or progression [35]. A similar study in a relatively large cohort also concluded that the epitope recognition profiles of RA patients were highly heterogeneous, and patients cannot be characterized by unique and specific recognition patterns [36]. In a mixed group of patients with RA, AS, and PsA and healthy individuals, RA subgroups with distinct patterns of autoantibody specificities were observed. However, no actual clusters were identified and the characteristics of these subgroups were not investigated as the aim of this study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of a multiplex assay in differentiating RA from other types of arthritis [26]. The concordance between RA classifications using this assay and RF, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (CCP) antibodies, and shared-epitope (SE) alleles was also observed, although the necessity of classification using this complicated assay is debatable when much simpler tests are sufficient. Another study used a broader range of 181 autoantibodies and identified four clusters of RA patients: ACPA negative (C1), complement C4-B reactivity (C2), high ACPA reactivity (C3), and moderate ACPA reactivity with cross-reactivity with C1 and C3 (C4). These clusters only consisted of 61% of the patients included in the study and all other patients were not characterized. C1 and C2 showed worsened while C3 and C4 had improved Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS28) after adalimumab or methotrexate treatment, but the trend was non-significant [28].

In addition to autoantibodies, circulating proteins related to inflammation, tissue turnover, and cardiovascular diseases were also used for the classification of RA patients. Two studies reported that RA patients could be classified into two clusters with one having higher levels of cardiovascular and inflammation-related proteins, regardless of anti-CCP positivity. One of these studies found no differences in demographic or clinical characteristics between clusters[30], while the other reported higher burden of comorbidities and worse clinical conditions in the cluster with higher protein levels [27]. In the latter study, proteins tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-related apoptosis-inducing ligand receptor 2 (TRAILR2) and TNF receptor superfamily member 11a (TNFRSF11A) were found to be the key proteins separating the two clusters, pointing out the central role of TNF superfamily pathways in these RA clusters. A study in the first-degree relatives of RA patients identified six clusters using a panel of cytokines/chemokines/growth factors with two clusters consisting mostly of ACPA-negative and ACPA-positive samples, respectively, with the latter having more female and higher portion of individuals that progressed into inflammatory arthritis [32]. Again, no further investigation was performed on these RA endotypes as it was not the focus of this study. Another RA study performed cluster analysis in a mixed group of RA and OA patients using seven proteins reflecting tissue inflammation or turnover, and found two high inflammatory RA clusters distinguished by macrophage activity, two clusters of mixed RA and OA patients with one uncharacterized and the other having low inflammation and high bone activity, and one cluster consisting mostly of OA patients with low inflammation and low bone and cartilage activity. Higher change of erosion was observed in the RA group with high inflammation and macrophage activity, and DAS28 was different between clusters [25].

The application of microbiota composition in RA endotype discovery was also explored. A cluster analysis using gut microbiome data identified a Prevotella abundant (C1) and a Bacteroides abundant endotype (C2) in RA patients, with microbial gene functions related to glycan biosynthesis and metabolism enriched in C1 and vitamin and peptidoglycan biosynthesis enriched in C2. However, the top two PCs only explained less than 23% of the variance observed, the clusters were poorly separated, and no differences were found in clinical features/indicators, inflammatory biomarkers, or auto-antibody levels between clusters. The authors also validated the clustering in another two cohorts and observed a higher percentage of responders to methotrexate therapy in C1 than in C2 in one of these cohorts, but the sample size of this cohort was quite small (n = 27) [33]. Subgingival microbiome data clustered a mixed group of RA and OA patients into two major subgroups. Similarly, no associations between the cluster membership and RF or anti-CCP concentrations were observed, and the subgingival microbial fingerprint cannot discriminate RA patients from those with OA, either [31]. Whether microbiota composition could be applied as biomarkers for RA endotype identification requires further investigation.

All endotype studies in RA used blood (components) except for the two microbiomics studies. Compared to ACPAs, circulating protein levels showed better performance as target markers for endotype identification; however, the results remained highly heterogeneous. The findings from proteomics studies using multiplex antigen arrays or OLINK protein panels were also highly inconsistent. Most of these studies failed to identify either endotypes or their associations with clinical outcomes, and a small number of studies linked inflammation, DAS28 (after treatment) and cardiovascular risk with the identified endotypes. Validation cohorts were only included in one microbiomics study, highlighting a general lack of validation across the field.

Evidence in OA and RA, the most studied diseases in MSK endotype research, showed that joint tissue transcriptomics and plasma metabolomics were used in OA studies while serum/plasma proteomics and microbiomics were often used in RA studies. This could be due to the previous recognition that OA is a disease of joints, or a metabolic disease, while RA is a systemic autoimmune disease. However, these concepts are being updated with increasing evidence showing that OA might be a whole-body syndrome. OA microbiomics studies are also emerging but the focus has not been laid on endotype identification yet. In OA, a minimally inflammatory metabolic disease, pain, inflammation, and collagen/bone turnover markers, and metabolic factors were used for endotype identification; while in immune mediated diseases such as RA, endotype biomarkers mainly focused on antibodies, cytokines, and bone turnover and cardiovascular disease markers (Fig. 3).

OP endotypes

Using integrative multi-omics data, Yuan et al. identified two subtypes of OP in a Chinese population [37]. C1 was a cluster with lower hip BMD and lower fracture rate at 4-year follow-up and was sensitive to calcium supplementation; C2 had lower lumbar spine BMD and was less sensitive to calcium supplementation; hyper-methylation, expression down-regulation, or pathway inhibition related to calcium were observed in C2. These findings could explain the heterogeneity in efficacy of calcium supplementation and better guide osteoporosis medication; however, such studies would require huge human omics data, potentially requiring remarkable human and material resources. Zhang et al. identified three OP clusters using concentrations of six metals zinc, iron, selenium, lead, cadmium, and arsenic and investigated the microbiota profiles of these clusters [38]. C1 was a low-exposure group for all six metals; pathways related to carbohydrate metabolism, calcium channel, biological oxidation, genetic information transmission, and biological transshipment were more abundant. C2 was a high-exposure group for selenium and arsenic, while C3 was a high-exposure group for zinc, iron, lead, and cadmium which interacted with Bacteroidaceae and Bacteroides, resulting in reduction in LS1-4 BMD. Iron receptor protein was functionally enhanced in both C1 and C3. The cluster separation in this study was vague, and important confounding factors for microbiota profiles such as antibiotic use and dietary factors were not adjusted in the analysis.

Endotypes of other MSK disorders

In axSpA, high inflammation, low inflammation, and high cartilage turnover clusters were identified using 14 extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, though the overlap between the latter two clusters was quite visible. Compared to the low inflammation group, the other two clusters had better clinical responses to TNF-inhibitor adalimumab [43]. In AS, a high matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) and a low MMP cluster were identified using levels of five MMPs. The high MMP cluster had significantly higher disease activity and function limitation, and patients in this group were more likely to be current smokers [42]. In PsA, seven subgroups were identified using a variety of clinical features and several laboratory measurements, with two being RA-like groups, three polyarticular groups, one oligoarticular group, and one pustular group. Differences were observed for arthritis patterns, radiographic changes, and laboratory parameters between clusters [44]. All these studies had very small sample size with five subgroups consisting of ≤ 8 patients each in the PsA study, thus the findings need further validation.

Cluster analysis was also performed in mixed groups of patients with different rheumatic diseases and/or systemic autoimmune diseases. In a group of patients with RA, axSpA, or PsA, two subgroups characterized by high and low cardiometabolic risk and disease activity, respectively, were identified using levels of complement component 3 and homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), with the former group consisting mainly of PsA patients and the latter having the majority of axSpA patients, suggesting the possible association between progression of these rheumatic diseases and inflammation and abnormal glucose metabolism mediated by a defective cardiometabolic system [39]. Another research group studied Native American (NA) patients with RA, systemic autoimmune disease (systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), Sjögren’s syndrome (SS), systemic sclerosis (SSc)), polyarthritis/undifferentiated connective tissue disease (UCTD), or arthralgia/OA and controls. Five clusters with different immune profiles were identified using a panel of cytokines and chemokines, with one cluster consisting mostly of RA patients and one controls. These clusters did not correlate with clinical diagnoses, suggesting diagnoses were not exact correlates of underlying immune dysfunction [40]. However, the sample size for each disease group was very small, and the study participants derived from different tribes and nations, which makes it difficult to replicate or generalize these findings to other NA populations. Another study on ANA-associated arthritis performed cluster analysis using a combination of proteins, clinical features, and socioeconomic factors in a group of patients with various systemic autoimmune diseases as well as arthritis and identified two subgroups characterized by high and low MSK activity, respectively, with the former containing more patients with inflammatory features and the latter more patients with non-inflammatory causes of joint pain and significant symptom burden, but no evidence of active inflammation [41]. A difficult question to answer in these studies is whether the identified endotypes genuinely reflect the differences in underlying molecular mechanisms of the investigated rheumatic disease(s) given that these patients with established diseases were most likely taking treatments specific to their rheumatic or systemic autoimmune disease(s), which could greatly affect the levels of biochemical markers examined for endotype identification.

This systematic review had several limitations. First, due to challenges in article translation and interpretation, and to ensure accessibility for the global research communities, only publications in English language were included which may have potentially introduced language bias. Second, studies without replication cohorts were excluded if their sample size was < 100. Although no formal guidelines exist for minimum sample size in cluster analysis, it has recently been suggested that a sample size equal to 70 times number of variables and number of clusters may be sufficient [48]. We applied a more relaxed threshold to include all studies with sample size ≥ 100 (no limitation for studies with replication cohort). This might have led to the exclusion of some smaller scale endotype studies such as single cell transcriptomics studies which could also add value to this field. Finally, this review was not registered in the PROSPERO database as the data extraction had already commenced prior to the registration.

Conclusions

In the current literature, endotype studies in MSK disorders mainly focus on OA and RA. For OA, inflammation, pain, and structural change/progression were the main features distinguishing between or characterizing the clusters regardless of the joint affected or type of biomarkers used in cluster analysis. High- and low-inflammation clusters were consistently identified using different types of samples, and high- and low-pain clusters were observed in several other studies. A few studies pointed out the importance of sex-specific considerations in OA endotype study. In RA, although there is ample evidence that ACPAs play significant roles in the development of RA, efforts to classify RA patients using ACPAs have not been very successful. Several studies have reported endotypes with different levels of inflammation markers or responses to treatment. The associations between identified endotypes and clinical features were often lacking. Additionally, several RA studies simply conducted a cluster analysis but did not specifically focus on endotypes. Studies on other MSK disorders were very limited and sample size was often small. More investigations in larger cohorts are much needed before any conclusions could be drawn for these disorders.

In the field of MSK endotype research, microbiota profiles are being recognized as new biomarkers for endotype identification and characterization. Multi-omics approaches and tissue crosstalk analysis are showing promise in deciphering molecular mechanisms underlying OA endotypes. Additionally, clustering panels with smaller numbers of biomarkers might have higher applicability in clinical settings. The stability of MSK disorder endotypes should also be examined as the progression of diseases is associated with the changes of biochemical marker profiles and involvement/contributions of different underlying pathways; longitudinal endotype research could provide much more valuable information than the cross-sectional study strategy applied by current studies. In addition, the biomarkers and statistical methods for endotype identification need to be carefully considered, and it is important to assess their power to ensure the findings are biologically meaningful and reliable. Several studies had a priori hypotheses which could have potentially affected the biomarkers selected for endotype identification as well as the assessed endotype characteristics in some cases. However, the same bias also exists for studies without hypotheses as most studies preselected biomarkers for clustering based on their previous associations with the disorder studied or were post hoc studies that used available data in the studied cohorts. Attention should be paid to the study limitations when reading this review. Molecular endotype research in MSK diseases will be greatly boosted by the development of more advanced omics platforms, especially spatial omics and more sensitive panels of biomarkers that can distinguish mechano-inflammatory, fibrotic, and microthrombotic processes. It is also important to note that this field is fraught with inconsistencies. The terms “subtype” and “endotype” were sometimes misused. Some studies performed stratification solely based on clinical features, thus the identified subgroups were subtypes instead of true endotypes. Researchers need to have a clear understanding of the endotype definition and carefully consider the types of markers used for clustering to avoid misleading readers/researchers. In addition, only one third of the studies clearly stated the ethnicity of study populations, with the majority being conducted in White participants. Future endotype studies should consider including populations of other ethnic origins to expand the applicability and generalizability of the findings. Further studies on MSK disorder endotypes could pave the way for deeper understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of these conditions and assist in the design of clinical trials and the development of personalized therapy strategies.
