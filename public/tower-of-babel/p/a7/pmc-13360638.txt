IFNα and bleomycin-induced DNA damage activate distinct stress responses in human islets and induced pluripotent stem cell-derived islet cells
Authors: Roozbeh Akbari Motlagh, Nayara Rampazzo Morelli, Nicole Alcasid, Peter J Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPancreatic β-cells undergo different stress responses during the development of type 1 diabetes (T1D), such as the type I interferon (IFN) response and senescence. Although the IFN response leads to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and has been linked to senescence in other cell types, the extent to which senescence and the type I IFN response are related pathways in human β-cells are unclear. To address this question, we compared the transcriptional and protein secretory responses of human donor islets and induced pluripotent stem cell-derived islet cells (SC-islet cells) to IFNα and chemically-induced DNA damage using bleomycin. While IFNα induced a classical IFN response involving HLA Class I genes, ER stress genes, and reversible chemokine secretion, it did not lead to stable induction of senescence-related genes or a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) in human islets. Similarly, in SC-islet cell models derived from two donors with different HLA haplotypes, IFNα treatment led to the upregulation of IFN genes and HLA Class I genes without an effect on senescence genes or SASP. Conversely, bleomycin-induced DNA damage in SC-islet cells led to signatures of senescence without an effect on IFN genes, ER stress genes, or HLA Class I. Taken together, these data suggest that IFNα exposure and DNA damage elicit distinct stress responses in human donor islets and SC-islet cells. These findings have implications for our understanding of heterogeneity in β-cell stress in T1D.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) results from a chronic autoimmune disease leading to selective T-cell-mediated destruction of pancreatic β-cells and insulin deficiency. 1 While the role of immune system dysfunction is widely accepted, the role of islet stress and dysfunction in the disease process is comparatively less understood. 2 β-cells undergo a variety of stress programs early during the development and onset of T1D, such as the IFN response, the unfolded protein response (UPR), the integrated stress response (ISR), changes in polyamine biosynthesis, dysfunctional autophagy and cellular senescence. 3 , 4 Major efforts over the past several years have aimed not only to characterize the molecular underpinnings of these responses but also to move therapies that can mitigate β-cell stress towards the clinic. The fruit of these efforts is evident in that drugs targeting the IFN response and UPR have reached clinical trials with early successful outcomes in new-onset T1D patients. 5–8 However, owing to the growing appreciation of heterogeneity in β-cell phenotypes and stress, 3 , 9 the extent to which these responses are inter-related or distinct remains unclear. 10

Cellular senescence is a developmentally and physiologically essential stress program 11 involving several hallmark phenotypic changes. These include a stable cell cycle arrest enforced by cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors such as p21 and/or p16INK4A, altered chromatin structure, resistance to apoptosis, lysosomal compartment changes (senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity) and the secretion of a variety of bioactive and immunogenic molecules known as senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). 12 , 13 A subpopulation of β-cells undergoes a form of senescence as a result of metabolic or immune inflammatory stressors in T2D 14 and T1D, 15 , 16 respectively, and senescent β-cells also accumulate in monogenic diabetes due to Mafa mutation in a sex-dependent manner. 17 Senescence accelerates diabetes progression because small molecule or genetic approaches ablating senescent cells slowed T1D development in nonobsese diabetic (NOD) mice 15 , 18 and improved metabolic dysfunction in high-fat diet (HFD) mice. 14 , 19 Importantly, heterogeneity has emerged in the senescence phenotype of β-cells, 20 as senescence resulting from stress and involving DNA damage, p21 expression, and SASP is distinct from the gradual age-related process of cell cycle exit in β-cells due to de-repression of p16INK4A. 21 Notably, some of the senescence phenotypes observed in vivo in T1D can be modeled in human donor islets and the non-proliferative EndoC-βH5 human β-cell line 22 following exposure to sub-lethal chemically induced DNA strand break damage. These phenotypes include stable upregulation of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor gene CDKN1A (encoding p21), activation of SASP involving flagship factor growth and differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), and elevation of the prosurvival gene B-cell lymphoma 2-like 1 (BCL2L1). 20 However, the extent to which senescence is distinct from or connected to IFN response and/or UPR remains unclear.

Type I and type II IFN responses have been well studied in T1D and shown to contribute to overall disease progression. IFN responses are activated upon binding of IFNα/β (type I IFNs) or IFNγ (type II IFN) to their cognate cell surface IFN receptors, leading to the activation of the downstream Janus Kinase/Signal Transducer Activator of Transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway, and converging on interferon-stimulated gene expression and upregulation of Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) Class I and/or II. 23–25 A body of evidence points to type I and II IFN signatures in the pancreas of T1D donors, 26–29 which can be modeled by exposure of human donor islets to IFNα or IFNγ ex vivo, leading to mild ER stress and robust HLA Class I hyperexpression and remodeling of the antigen presentation landscape. 30 , 31 HLA Class I and II alleles are central to immune recognition of β-cells in T1D and Class II alleles such as the HLA-DR3-DR4 are among the strongest genetic risk alleles for T1D, found in 90% of individuals with the disease of European ancestry. 32 On the other hand, the type II IFN response involving IFNγ is thought to be a later signature linked to β-cell death. 33 Both type I and II IFN signaling can also activate adaptive survival mechanisms such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) upregulation on β-cells and their extracellular vesicles, which may protect them from CD8 T cell-mediated killing. 34 , 35 The type I IFN response has received considerable attention in preclinical studies due to the number of T1D genomewide association studies (GWAS) that have identified risk polymorphisms in key pathway components, including interferon induced with helicase C domain 1 (IFIH1), tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2), and protein tyrosine phosphatase non-receptor type 2 (PTPN2). 36 Moreover, links between type I IFN response, UPR, and senescence have been suggested from studies in other contexts and cell types. For instance, induction of senescence with DNA damage in cultured human fibroblasts leads to activation of a type I IFN response several weeks later. 37 Also, both the type I IFN response and senescence have been linked to the UPR, since exposure to type I IFN leads to ER stress, 26 while deletion of UPR mediators Atf6α or Ire1α in β-cells of NOD mice leads to an alternative senescence program that is adaptive and protects against T1D. 38 Aged β-cells from human islets, or EndoC-βH3 cells 39 rendered senescent via excision of human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) and simian virus 40 (SV40) large T antigen transgenes, show elevated signatures and responsiveness to IFNα exposure. 40 We also recently demonstrated that exposure of human islets and EndoC-βH5 cells to sub-lethal T1D-related cytokines IFNγ, TNFα, and IL-1β leads to the development of stable senescence phenotypes, with some similarity to DNA damage, 16 suggesting that certain cytokines elicit senescence phenotypes in human islets. Interestingly, both IFNα and IFNγ exposure to human islets elicits similar transcriptional signatures to those found in β-cells of T1D donors. 27 , 33 However, no studies have yet investigated the relationship between the type I IFN response and DNA damage-induced senescence in human islets and β-cells.

There are well-established culture models to study the type I IFN response in human islets 26 , 41 , 42 and the DNA damage response leading to senescence phenotypes in human islets. 43 Therefore, in this study, we sought to compare the transcriptional and protein secretory responses to IFNα exposure with those of bleomycin-induced DNA damage to determine the extent to which these responses are related or distinct. We extended our observations in human islets to two induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived islet cells (SC-islet cells) models. Overall, our results support the notion that IFNα and bleomycin-induced DNA damage lead to different phenotypic outcomes at the transcriptional and protein secretory levels in human islets and SC-islet cells, suggesting that they are indeed distinct stress programs in human β-cells.

Materials and methods

Human cell line culture

Transformed human embryonic kidney 293 (293T) cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA, Cat# CRL-3216) and cultured in DMEM high glucose (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat# 11965092) containing 2 mM L-glutamine, 1X-antibiotic-antimycotic (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 15240096), and 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher, Cat# A2720801) at 37°C and 5% CO2. The cells were seeded at ~1 × 106 cells per T75 flask and subcultured every 3‒4 d. The cells were treated with 200 or 1000 IU/mL (1 or 5 ng/mL, respectively) recombinant human IFNα2A (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, Canada, Cat# 78076) or DMSO (vehicle control) for 2 h or 24 h. After 24 hof treatment, we collected the cells for RNA extraction and qRT-PCR. For Western blot analysis, the cells were harvested after 2 hof treatment.

Human donor islet culture

Human islets from deceased adult male and female organ donors were obtained from the Alberta Diabetes Institute IsletCore at the University of Alberta in Edmonton (www.bcell.org/adi-isletcore) with the support of the Human Organ Procurement and Exchange (HOPE) program, Trillium Gift of Life Network (TGLN). The Human Research Ethics Board at the University of Alberta approved islet isolation (Pro00013094). Informed consent was obtained from all the donors' families for the use of pancreatic tissue for research purposes. Human donor islets were also obtained from the Integrated Islet Distribution Program (IIDP) at the City of Hope, supported by the NIDDK through NIH grant #U24DK098085 and the Breakthrough T1D-funded IIDP Islet Award Initiative. Human donor information is found in Supplementary Table 1. Human islets were rested in culture at 37°C with 5% CO₂ for 24 hours after arrival. They were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium without glucose (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 11879020) containing 2 mM L-glutamine, 1× antibiotic–antimycotic, 10% fetal bovine serum, and supplemented with 5.5 mM D-glucose (Thermo Fisher, Cat# J60067.EQE). Islets were hand-picked into non-treated multi-well plates and cultured in islet media containing 200 IU/mL or 1000 IU/mL of recombinant human IFNα2A (StemCell Technologies, Cat# 78076) or DMSO (vehicle control) (Thermo Fisher, Cat# J66650.AD) for 24 h. The conditioned media were collected for ELISA, while the islets were harvested for RNA extraction and qRT-PCR.

ELISA

CXCL10 or GDF15 levels were measured in the conditioned media from human islets using commercial ELISA kits (Invitrogen, Cat# KAC2361 and Novus Biologicals, Cat# DGD150) according to the supplier's instructions. The absorbance readings were performed on a microplate reader Epoch (BioTek) at 450 nm wavelength. The data are expressed as pg/mL.

Human iPSC-derived islet cell culture

Human iPSC islet cells (Cellartis® hiPS Beta Cells ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22) were purchased from TakaraBio (San Jose, CA, USA, Cat# Y10100, Y10106) and cultured as previously described. 44–46 Cells were confirmed to be mycoplasma free, and cell viability was checked using trypan blue staining upon thawing, and only vials with a viability ≥ 75% were used for the experiments. These cells typically have 50%–55% INS+ cells, with ~12% INS+/NK6.1+ cell population (ChIPSC12) and ~30% INS+/NKX6.1+ cell population according to the supplier; hence, we use the term SC-islet cells rather than SC-β-like cells, as they are mixed islet-like cell populations in both donors. The cells were seeded into tissue culture-treated 24-well plates (Fisherbrand, Cat# FB012929) coated with Cellartis Beta Cell Coating (Cat# Y10103) and maintained at 37°C and 5% CO2 to differentiate into an islet-like cell population. This process was carried out over a period of 14 d using a two-step proprietary medium developed by the supplier, which consists of Cellartis Beta Cell Basal Medium (days 0–7, Cat#Y10104) and Cellartis Beta Cell Medium 2 (days 7–14, Cat#10105) containing Cellartis Beta Cell Supplement (Cat#Y10102). The media was replaced daily or every other day. On day 12, differentiated cultures were exposed either to 1000 IU/mL of recombinant human IFNα2A (StemCell Technologies, Cat# 78076) or DMSO (vehicle control) for 2 or 24 h, to bleomycin sulfate (Millipore-Sigma, Cat. #971) at 35 or 50 µM, to etoposide (MilliporeSigma, Cat# 341205-25MG) at 50 or 100 µM, or to DMSO (vehicle control) for 48 h. Following bleomycin treatment, medium was replaced with fresh drug-free medium 2, and the cells were cultured for 3 or 4 more days. Twenty-four hours prior to harvest, the media was replaced, and the conditioned media was collected for Luminex assays, while the cells were harvested for RNA extraction and qRT-PCR. Pilot studies were initially conducted with 35 and 50 µM bleomycin or 50 and 100 µM etoposide compared to DMSO (vehicle control) to induce DNA damage in these cells (Supplementary Figure 3). In the pilot studies, cell viability was assessed using trypan blue staining on an automated cell counter (Bio-Rad TC-10). The cells were then harvested for RNA extraction (qRT-PCR) and western blot analysis, while the conditioned media were collected for Luminex assays.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcriptase (qRT-PCR)

RNA isolation from SC-islet cells was carried out using the commercial column-based purification with an RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany, Cat# 74034). RNA isolation from human islets was performed using the TRIzol-based kit Direct-Zol RNA MicroPrep (Zymo Research, Cat# R2062), as previously described. 43 RNA quantification was carried out on a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). LunaScript® RT SuperMix (New England Biolabs Cat# M3010) was used for cDNA synthesis from approximately 100–200 ng of total RNA using the same amount of total RNA per sample used for each experiment. qPCR was performed in a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad) using the LUNA Universal qPCR Master Mix (New England BioLabs, Cat#M3003). The primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Luminex assay

Luminex Discovery Assays (R&D Systems) were performed as described using a 5 or 7-factor custom kits (GDF15, CXCL1, CXCL8, IGFBP4, TNFRSF10C, or a 7-plex kit that also included CXCL5 and CCL20). The measurements were conducted using a Bio-Plex 200 Luminex (Bio-Rad, Hercules CA, USA) platform. The data are expressed as pg/µg of total islet RNA content for human islet experiments or pg secreted per 100,000 viable cells for SC-islet cell experiments.

Western blot analysis

Ice-cold radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer, containing 1× protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific), was used to lyse 293T cells and SC-islet cells. BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for protein quantification. Approximately 8–12 μg of total protein (equal amounts per sample for each experiment) was electrophoresed on precast 4%–12% gradient gels (Bolt Bis-Tris Plus, Thermo Fisher) in 1× MES SDS‒PAGE running buffer and electrotransferred to nitrocellulose membranes using a Bolt-iBlot2 apparatus (Thermo Fisher). The membranes were blocked in 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at RT. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 3) in TBST, followed by detection using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies after a 1-hour incubation at room temperature in 5% skim milk in TBST. The membranes were incubated using SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher) and subsequently developed following exposure to X-ray film (Fujifilm Medical). Multiple exposures were taken to ensure that the signals were in the linear range.

Public RNA-seq dataset analysis

We examined publicly available RNA-seq datasets on human donor islets exposed to 1000 IU/mL IFNα for 18 h (GSE148058) 47 and SC-islet cells exposed to 2000 U/mL IFNα for 24 h (GSE203430). 48 RNA-seq data from GSE148058 (n = 6 donor islet preparations) were run on the internal NCBI GEO2R platform to calculate differentially expressed genes with a Log2FC cut-off of ≥0.58 or ≤−0.58 (approximately 1.5-fold up- or downregulated, respectively) in IFNα-treated samples compared to controls. A volcano plot was exported from GEO2R showing the differentially expressed genes. The full list of differentially expressed genes determined by this analysis is found in Supplementary Table 4. We also plotted selected genes from this analysis and indicated those that were significantly different between groups at padj < 0.05. For GSE203430 (n = 5 SC-islet preparations per group), we downloaded the processed gene table of normalized read counts from the NCBI GEO entry for these data and plotted these data for selected senescence genes in IFNα-exposed cells versus vehicle controls. None of these genes was found to be significantly differentially expressed between groups.

Statistical analysis

Sample sizes (n) and definitions of the measured groups are provided in the figure legends. Statistical testing was only performed on islet data where n = 3 or more islet donor preparations were examined and are shown as the mean ± s.d. where each data point is from a different donor islet experiment. For SC-islet cells, the data points shown are mean ± s.d. of independently treated wells from the same batch/vial of cells for each experiment shown and thus represented only 1 experiment per vial of cells. Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism (version 11.1; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05, based on two-way ANOVA with multiple-testing correction by the Sidak‒Holm method.

Results

IFNα activates the type I IFN response in human islets without an effect on senescence-responsive gene CDKN1A

To investigate the transcriptional responses of type I IFN in human islets, IFNα was first validated in the transformed human embryonic kidney line 293T. Treatment of these cells with 1000 IU/mL of human IFNα led to classical signatures of the type I IFN response, including activation of JAK-STAT pathway (Supplementary Figure 1a) and upregulation of Class I HLA genes HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C (Supplementary Figure 1b) as well as IFN genes IRF7 and IRF9 (Supplementary Figure 1c). We next treated human donor islets with IFNα for 24 h to evoke the type I IFN transcriptional response, as has been done in previous studies 26 , 31 , 47 (Figure 1a). Exposure of human islets from donors without diabetes to 200 IU/mL (n = 3 donors) or 1000 IU/mL (n = 3 donors) IFNα for 24 h led to increases in HLA-B and HLA-C expression, as expected, 26 , 31 without affecting the HLA Class II gene HLA-DRB1 (Figure 1B and C). Examination of ER stress-related and IFN genes revealed induction of the IFN master regulatory transcription factor interferon regulator factor 9 (IRF9), 49 confirming IFN pathway activity, without a significant effect on ER stress/UPR genes ATF4, DDIT3, sXBP1, or the ER degradation-enhancing α-mannosidase-like protein 1 EDEM1 (Figure 1B and C), which is a component of ER-associated decay (ERAD), often activated by sXBP1 that regulates INS mRNA levels in β-cells. 50 The cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor gene CDKN1A, a key early marker of senescence in β-cells, 43 , 51 was not affected under these conditions (Figure 1B and C). These data suggest that the typical 24 h exposure to IFNα at a dose that activates the IFN response does not similarly activate a senescence transcriptional response involving CDKN1A.

IFNα activates the IFN response but not the senescence gene CDKN1A in T1D donor islets

We next tested whether IFNα exposure of T1D donor islets had similar effects on the type I IFN response as compared to islets from donors without diabetes. Although isolated T1D donor islets are mainly comprised of α-cells due to the profound loss of β-cells in the disease, 52 we used islets from donor R539 obtained from the Alberta Diabetes Institute, a 57-y-old male T1D donor (Supplementary Table 1) that had residual β-cells according to the pancreas insulin content from Humanislets.com 53 and our previous flow cytometry analysis of insulin content in live islet cells from this donor. 54 Islet secretory responses are largely restored in cultured T1D donor islets containing residual β-cells because the islets recover from inflammatory stress in vivo 55 ; hence, we used this rare opportunity to examine phenotypes of the IFN response. Exposure of T1D donor islets to IFNα for 24 h, followed by gene expression analysis (Figure 2A), showed that IFN-treated islets upregulated HLA-A, HLA-B, and HLA-C but not HLA-DRB1 (Figure 2B). Notably, in addition to IRF9, DDIT3 and sXBP1 were also upregulated in this donor (Figure 2B), which was consistent with an intact IFN response and elevated ER stress. However, again, CDKN1A expression was not affected (Figure 2B). Although these data were only from one T1D donor and thus should be considered exploratory and qualitative, they suggest that even in isolated islets from a T1D donor, which mainly consists of α-cells, activating the type I IFN response does not concomitantly trigger a senescence transcriptional response.

IFNα exposure and washout lead to stable upregulation of HLA Class I genes, but not senescence genes

We next explored whether IFNα could lead to longer-term stable effects on senescence genes that would not be detected immediately following 24 h of exposure. Following 24 h of treatment with IFNα and extended culture in media lacking the cytokine, upregulation of HLA Class I gene expression persists for 7 d after the initial exposure, whereas other features, such as secretion of the chemokine CXCL10 and ER stress, are reversible and resolve. 41 To explore whether senescence-related changes occur later following IFNα exposure, we treated human islets (n = 3 donors) with IFNα for 24 h, followed by longer-term culture in media lacking the cytokine (termed “washout”) and measured gene expression changes that persisted or developed 4 d after washout (Figure 3A). HLA-B remained upregulated at this later timepoint in the absence of exposure to IFNα (Figure 3B), which is consistent with prior studies. 41 IRF9 also remained stably upregulated (Figure 3B), suggesting a sustained IFN transcriptional response, whereas no ER stress genes were upregulated, as expected. 23 , 41 However, evaluation of the senescence gene CDKN1A and SASP gene GDF15 revealed that neither was upregulated in these experiments (Figure 3B). Accordingly, the secretion of the IFN-responsive chemokine CXCL10 was elevated in the short-term after 24 h of exposure to IFNα, but returned to control levels at the 4-d washout timepoint (Figure 3C); however, GDF15 secretion was not affected by IFNα exposure either initially after 24 h or after the washout period 4 d later (Figure 3D). These data indicate that IFNα does not elicit senescence-related changes as a late phenotype after initial exposure.

IFNα and bleomycin-induced DNA damage lead to different stress responses in SC-islet cells

Individuals with genetic susceptibility to T1D typically have one of a few specific risk profiles of HLA Class II alleles. 56 To examine the impacts of IFNα signaling in relation to T1D genetic susceptibility in human islet cells, we turned to SC-islet cell models using the commercial cell lines ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22. Publicly available validation data from the supplier indicate that these SC-islet cell models were differentiated from human iPSCs into endocrine progenitors and into a final population of islet-like cells with a mixed population of approximately 12% (ChiPSC12) or 30% (ChiPSC22) of the cells positive for both INS and NKX6.1, and these cells are capable of responding to glucose with stimulated C-peptide secretion (https://www.takarabio.com/learning-centers/stem-cell-research/technical-notes/beta-cells/beta-cells-for-disease-modeling). ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 SC-islet cells have been characterized and used for diabetes modeling in previous studies. 44–46 For our purposes, we took advantage of the different HLA haplotypes of the donors used to generate the iPSC lines for these cells. ChiPSC12 is from a 24-y-old male with a T1D susceptible HLA Class II profile, whereas ChiPSC22 is from a 32-y-old male with a T1D non-susceptible HLA Class II profile (Table 1). Specifically, ChiPSC12 cells contain the well-known DR3 risk haplotype with HLA-DRB1*03:01 and HLA-DQB1*02:01 alleles, 56 which are not found in ChiPSC22 cells (Table 1).

To induce an IFN response, ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 cells were treated with 1000 IU/mL IFNα for 24 h, followed by gene expression profiling by qRT-PCR or Luminex assays to examine secreted factors (Figure 4A). Similar to results with 293T cells, JAK/STAT pathway activation occurred in both ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 cells as early as 2 h of IFNα exposure as evidenced by phosphorylation of STAT1 (Supplementary Figure 2). qPCR analysis revealed dramatic upregulation of HLA-B and HLA-C, but not HLA-A or HLA-DRB1, in the IFNα-treated ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 cells relative to vehicle controls (Figure 4B and C), similar to our observations from human islets (Figure 1B) and consistent with IFNα studies of others on human islets and ECN90 β-cells, 31 iPSC-derived β-like cells exposed to IFNα, 57 and stem cell β-like clusters (sBCs) exposed to proinflammatory cytokines. 58 IRF9 was upregulated ~5-fold in both ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 cells (Figure 4B and C), while DDIT3 was upregulated ~3-fold in IFNα-treated ChiPSC22 cells (Figure 4C). When we examined early senescence and SASP genes, GDF15 was upregulated ~3-fold in IFNα-treated ChiPSC12 cells relative to vehicle controls but not in ChiPSC22 cells (Figure 4B and C). To examine SASP factor secretion, we used our 5-factor Luminex panel, which included GDF15, IGFBP4, CXCL1, TNFRSF10C, and CXCL8. 43 Notably, only GDF15 and IGFBP4 were detected in both lines, and both factors were similar between IFNα treatment and vehicle control (Figure 4D), suggesting that the observed transcriptional increase in GDF15 in IFNα-exposed ChiPSC12 cells (Figure 4C) was not reflected in the level of protein secretion. Thus, 24 h IFNα exposure did not lead to consistent induction of senescence transcriptional or SASP secretory responses in ChiPSC12 or ChiPSC22 cells.

Given the lack of a consistent effect of IFNα on senescence and SASP gene expression and protein secretion, we next investigated whether these cells were competent to mount a canonical senescence-like response to DNA damage, similar to human islet β-cells. We previously established a double-strand break DNA damage model using sub-lethal bleomycin treatment of human islets, which elicits upregulation of CDKN1A at mRNA and protein levels in β-cells, SASP gene expression and protein secretion, and prosurvival gene expression at 4–6 d post-drug washout. 43 , 51 Treatment of EndoC-βH5 human β-cells with bleomycin leads to similar hallmark phenotypes as human islets, 43 indicating that this response to DNA damage can occur in a human β-cell line.

To determine whether ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 SC-islet cells mount a stress response to DNA damage involving senescence phenotypes, in pilot studies, we treated these cells with either bleomycin or etoposide, another agent inducing DNA double-strand breaks, for 48 h, followed by a washout and extended culture for another 4 d in drug-free media, similar to our previous studies. 43 Both ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 cells retained similar viability to controls following treatment with bleomycin (35 or 50 µM) or etoposide (50 or 100 µM) (Supplementary Figure 3a) and qPCR analysis indicated upregulation of senescence genes CDKN1A and to a lesser extent CDKN2A (encoding p16INK4A), along with SASP genes GDF15, CXCL1, TNFRSF10C and prosurvival gene BCL2L1 in both lines (Supplementary Figure 3b and c). Notably, INS expression remained similar in bleomycin- or etoposide-treated ChIPSC22 cells as compared to controls (Supplementary Figure 3d), suggesting that there were no major changes in differentiation state. Western blot analysis confirmed the stabilization of p53, the master regulator of the DNA damage response, 59 along with the upregulation of p21 (encoded by CDKN1A) in bleomycin- and etoposide-treated ChiPSC22 cells (Supplementary Figure 3e). Luminex assays revealed elevated secretion of GDF15 and IGFBP4 from both bleomycin- or etoposide-exposed ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 cells (Supplementary Figure 3f and g), which was consistent with a SASP response in both lines. Although qualitative and preliminary in nature owing to the inability to perform these studies on independent batches/vials of cells, our pilot results suggested that ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 cells mount a similar senescence-like stress response to sub-lethal DNA damage that is stable following removal of the damaging agent several days later. We therefore used 35 µM bleomycin to induce DNA damage in ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 cells to study senescence in subsequent experiments.

We next characterized the type I IFN response and senescence-like phenotypes in the SC-islet cell models after 48 h of bleomycin treatment and a 3-d drug washout period (Figure 5A). Bleomycin treatment did not affect HLA Class I, IFN, or ER stress genes but led to the upregulation of both CDKN1A and GDF15 in both ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 cells (Figure 5B and C). This effect was accompanied by increased concentration of the SASP factor GDF15 in the media following bleomycin treatment, whereas another detected SASP factor, CXCL1, was slightly reduced in the bleomycin-treated cells (Figure 5D). Together, these exploratory results suggest that, regardless of their T1D susceptibility to HLA Class II alleles, ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 cells mount a senescence-like response to DNA damage that does not involve features of the type I IFN response, including elevated HLA Class I genes and IRF9 expression.

Public RNA-seq data show that IFNα exposure activates a type I IFN transcriptional response but not senescence genes in human islets and SC-β-like cells

Given the exploratory nature of our experiments on ChiPSC SC-islet cells and our limited number of human islet donor experiments, we sought additional validation of our studies through analysis of public RNA-seq datasets. To establish whether the activation of a type I IFN response led to concurrent activation of a senescence transcriptional program, we examined publicly available RNA-seq datasets on human donor islets exposed to IFNα 47 and iPSC-derived β-like cells treated with IFNα 48 (Figure 6A). For the human islet RNA-seq data, we used the NCBI GEO2R tool to re-analyze the datasets of control and IFNα-treated islets (n = 6 donors) and performed differential expression analysis with adjusted p < 0.05, and identified fold changes of ≥1.5-fold up- or downregulated between groups (Figure 6B). This identified 1072 upregulated genes and 349 genes downregulated in IFNα-exposed human islet cells (Figure 6B, Supplementary Table 4). Among the upregulated genes were many well-known interferon-stimulated genes, such as IFIH1, IRF9, STAT1, CXCL10, CXCL11, MX2, and OAS2 (Figure 6C, Supplementary Table 4). Interestingly, while DDIT3 was significantly upregulated, no other ER stress genes were upregulated (ATF4, XBP1, ATF6, and EDEM1) (Figure 6C, Supplementary Table 4). Finally, we found no differences in a variety of senescence, SASP or prosurvival genes in IFNα-exposed human islets in this dataset, including CDKN1A, CDKN2A, GDF15, CXCL1, IGFBP4, MMP2, MMP3, BCL2, BCL2L1, and BCL2L2 (Figure 6C, Supplementary Table 4). To extend these observations to SC-islet cells, we also examined a published bulk RNA-seq dataset from iPSC-islet cell preparations (n = 5) exposed to 2000 U/mL IFNα for 24 h 48 . This study also demonstrated the upregulation of HLA Class I genes, ER stress genes, and IFN genes in IFNα-exposed SC-islet cells. 48 However, examination of the same set of senescence genes revealed no significant changes in their expression in IFNα treatment compared with controls (Figure 6D), which is consistent with what we observed with CDKN1A and GDF15 expression in IFNα-exposed ChiPSC12 and ChiPSC22 cells (Figure 4B and C). Together, evidence from these publicly available RNA-seq datasets confirmed our studies on human islets and ChiPSC cells and indicated that IFNα exposure to human islets and SC-islet cells activates a type I IFN response but not senescence-related genes.

Discussion

The type I IFN response, UPR, and senescence occur in subpopulations of stressed β-cells during the development and onset of T1D. 10 However, the inter-relationships between these stress responses in β-cells are unclear. Mechanistic links have been established between UPR and senescence in the NOD mouse model and the human T1D donor pancreas, as genetic deficiency of UPR in β-cells leads to the activation of an adaptive senescence program in NOD mice, and the signatures of this response are detected in residual β-cells of T1D donor pancreas. 38 Similarly, the type I IFN response occurs as a very late phenotype of senescence in human fibroblasts. 37 However, it remains to be determined whether stressors that trigger senescence can lead to the activation of type I IFN signaling or, reciprocally, whether the type I IFN response can lead to senescence in β-cells. A recent study showed that aged senescent p16INK4a-expressing human islet β-cells or transgene-excised senescent EndoC-βH3 cells are primed for type I IFN signaling and exhibit exaggerated responses to IFNα exposure compared to non-senescent cells, 40 suggesting heterogeneity in the islet response to type I IFN depending on the senescent cell burden. However, whether there are any clear interconnections between senescence triggered by DNA damage and type I IFN response in β-cells has not been explored.

To address this question, we compared the transcriptional and protein secretory responses of human donor islets ex vivo and SC-islet cell models to IFNα and DNA damage with bleomycin. We validated the potency of our IFNα treatments on 293T cells, and studies on human islets revealed the same hallmark features of IFN response that other studies have reported, including the upregulation of HLA Class I and IFN signaling genes, as well as acute IFN-responsive chemokine secretion. 26 , 31 , 41 Our results suggest that IFNα and chemical DNA damage trigger largely distinct phenotypes in human islets and SC-islet cells, supporting a distinction between these stress programs. Specifically, while we confirmed that acute IFNα exposure leads to a transient JAK/STAT pathway activation and chemokine secretion that is reversible, 23 it did not lead to robust upregulation of the DNA damage-inducible senescence regulator and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor CDKN1A or the core human islet SASP factor GDF15 20 in human islets from donors without diabetes and islets from a donor with T1D. Using iPSC-derived islet-like cells from donors with T1D-susceptible or non-susceptible HLA Class II polymorphisms, we found that while IFNα treatment recapitulated the same hallmark features of IFN response as shown by other studies using islets and SC-β-like cells, 31 , 47 , 48 , 57 it did not lead to a consistent senescence response. Importantly, although our studies on human donor islets were relatively limited in the number of different donors and our ChiPSC cell studies were largely exploratory due to insufficient independent batches of cells, we confirmed our findings using publicly available RNA-seq datasets. 47 , 48 In contrast, sub-lethal DNA damage with bleomycin or etoposide to SC-islet cells showed that these cells are competent to activate a senescence-like stress response resembling what occurs in DNA-damaged human islet β-cells. 43 , 51 , 54 , 60 This response involved features such as elevated senescence and SASP gene expression, p53/p21 pathway activation at the protein level, and increased secretion of some SASP factors that were stable several days after exposure to the damage. Notably, the DNA damage-induced senescence response in SC-islet cells did not involve transcriptional signatures of the type I IFN pathway under the conditions and timepoints we studied. This latter finding is consistent with our previous observation that bleomycin-induced senescence also does not activate the transcription of type I IFN response or ER stress genes in human islets. 43 Thus, unlike the exposure of human islets, the proinflammatory T1D-related cytokines IFNγ, TNFα, and IL-1β, which lead to senescence phenotypes, 16 IFNα alone does not have a similar effect.

Our observation that SC-islet cells are capable of a senescence-like response to DNA damage was intriguing. A recent study showed that bleomycin treatment improved SC-β-like cell differentiation by removing proliferating cells, but did not go on to explore senescence-like phenotypes after DNA damage exposure. 61 The relationship of β-cell differentiation status and maturation stage to the capacity for activating a DNA damage response has not been studied. Human β-cell senescence driven by DNA damage and p53 activation and the upregulation of p21 and SASP have hitherto been studied only in adult islet β-cells and the EndoC-βH5 β-cell line. 43 Our findings now suggest that DNA damage-mediated senescence may be a stress program of interest in the context of SC-β-like cell biology. Indeed, the factors that hamper efficient in vitro differentiation, glucose-responsive function, engraftment, immunogenicity, and long-term survival of SC-β-like cells remain obstacles for the field to effectively surmount in order to maximize the beneficial impacts of this cell replacement therapy for T1D patients. 62 Our finding that SC-islet cells respond to DNA damage by activating senescence phenotypes warrants further investigation of this stress response in the context of improving SC-β-like cell differentiation and transplantation.

Limitations of study

While our study provides insights into the key differences at the mRNA and protein levels between type I IFN response and DNA damage response in human islets and SC-islet cells, there are also limitations. First, it should be noted that our study was only an initial survey of the differences between IFNα- and bleomycin-induced DNA damage in human islets and SC-islet cells, rather than a comprehensive characterization of all phenotypic outcomes from these agents. It is entirely possible that longer-term treatments or exposures to either agent could lead to greater similarities in phenotypes than we measured here. We also recognize that our study used islets from a limited number of donors without diabetes and only 1 T1D donor, and owing to the variability between human donor islet preparations and the inability to examine sex as a variable, these studies should be expanded with larger numbers of male and female donor islets to reproduce the findings. Similarly, our studies with ChiPSC cells were limited to a few independent batches of cells from only two different male donors, and we did not have sufficient cells to perform all the assays on multiple independently differentiated batches of cells from each donor. We also note the lack of female SC-islet cells for comparison with our results on male SC-islet cells. Heterogeneity among islet β-cells, including the presence of p16+ aged senescent β-cells in the middle-aged donor islets we studied, could also impact the connections between IFN response and senescence. Future studies employing islets from younger donors would be of great interest to interrogate these relationships and would be more relevant to pathology in pediatric and young adult individuals who develop T1D. Finally, it is important to acknowledge that our chemically-induced DNA damage senescence model is not entirely physiological, and while it is a helpful tool to study aspects of β-cell senescence, it likely does not reflect the full range or extent of senescence phenotypes in vivo.

In conclusion, we provide evidence to support that the type I IFN response and chemically triggered DNA damage lead to distinct phenotypic outcomes at the gene and protein levels in human islets and SC-islet cells. The observation that SC-islet cell models respond similarly to human islets and EndoC-βH5 cells to bleomycin-induced DNA damage opens avenues for exploring and modeling the impact of this stress response on SC-islet cell differentiation, engraftment, and function in vivo.
