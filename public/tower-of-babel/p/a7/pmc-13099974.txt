Identifying intact and fibrotic parenchyma in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas using compression optical coherence elastography
Authors: E Gubarkova, A Potapov, E Vasilchikova, D Kuchin, P Ermakova, D Voronina, A Anina, M Karabut, L Lugovaya, M Sirotkina, V Zaitsev, S Gamayunov, N Gladkova, A Kashina, V Zagainov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntraoperative assessment of the condition of pancreatic tissue, including identification of “soft” pancreatic parenchyma and areas of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) are important for the prevention of postoperative pancreatic fistulas (POPFs), a predictor of the likely success of pancreatic islet isolation for transplantation, and for determining accurate resection margins in case of PDAC. The aim of this study was to analyze pancreatic stiffness using compression optical coherence elastography (C-OCE) to differentiate intact from fibrotic parenchyma and to determine the resection margins of PDAC. A total of 35 freshly excised human pancreatic specimens containing PDACs together with some of the surrounding non-tumorous pancreatic tissue derived from pancreatectomies were studied. In our investigation, calculation of the stiffness (Young’s modulus, kPa) and its color-coded 2D distribution were performed for different pancreatic tissues using C-OCE. The accuracy of the resulting data was confirmed by histological studies. The palpation stiffness of the pancreatic parenchyma was also qualitatively evaluated intraoperatively by the surgeon. Stiffness values were compared between POPF positive and negative groups. It has been shown that C-OCE can be used to differentiate various tissue types in pancreatic specimens (such as adipose tissue, acinar tissue, connective tissue, pancreatic cancer, and islets of Langerhans) and to objectively distinguish intact pancreatic parenchyma from fibrotic tissue based on quantitative stiffness assessment (p < 0.0001). A high negative correlation was observed between the percentage of acinar tissue present, determined using histological examination, and the average stiffness of the specimens, based on C-OCE data (r = -0.86; p < 0.0001). It was found that, when using a C-OCE value of < 84 kPa as the cutoff for pancreatic parenchymal stiffness to predict POPF, the sensitivity and specificity were 85% and 95%, respectively. Furthermore, our study is the first time that C-OCE technology has been demonstrated accurately to visualize the transition zone between PDAC and the surrounding non-tumorous tissues both after neoadjuvant chemotherapy and without. Therefore, the present study provides a new approach to the further rapid and objective in-traoperative assessment of the status of pancreatic parenchyma in patients, based on calculation of its stiffness using real time C-OCE data enabling improved differentiation of ‘soft’ pancreatic parenchyma from ‘hard’, as well as areas of PDAC.Keywords: Compression optical coherence elastography (C-OCE); Stiffness; Pancreatic parenchyma; Postoperative pancreatic fistulas (POPF); Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), islets of Langerhans; Resection margins; PancreatectomySubject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Gastroenterology, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Intraoperative assessment of the condition of pancreatic tissue, including identifying any fibrotic pancreatic parenchyma and areas of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), are important prognostic factors for making a variety of surgical and postoperative management decisions1,2,3. Thus, it is known that the state of the resection margin is an important indicator of survival after PDAC resection4. However, the currently recommended method to achieve tumor-free resection margins using intraoperative frozen section analysis of the margins, has limitations due to small tissue sample sizes and the processing of artifacts5–7. Furthermore, margin assessment is complicated by the inflammation, atrophy, and fibrosis associated with pancreatic cancer, particularly in cases with prior pancreatitis or preoperative polychemotherapy. This can sometimes lead to unjustified arterial resections, which in turn worsen both the immediate and long-term outcomes8. The prevention of postoperative pancreatic fistulas (POPFs) also represents an unresolved and very pressing problem for surgical pancreatology. This is the most formidable and potentially fatal complication of pancreatic surgery. In some cases, involving both a “soft” pancreas and a main pancreatic duct of small diameter, the question of total pancreatectomy arises as a method for preventing POPFs9. In relation to this, the typical method currently used by surgeons for assessing, the stiffness of the pancreas during surgery is by palpation, but this approach is subjective in nature. Additionally, in some instances, intraportal islet autotransplantation may be performed after total pancreatectomy to prevent postoperative diabetes and its consequences. This intervention can both reduce dependence on chronic insulin therapy and enhance blood glucose control after total pancreatectomy10,11. However, the presence of variably severe fibrosis, fibrocystic alterations, necrosis and inflammatory processes, or tumor invasion in pancreatic cancer patients may compromise the effectiveness of isolation of viable, functional islets of Langerhans12. Thus, novel intraoperative imaging tools are needed to improve assessment of the state of the pancreatic tissue and to guide subsequent resection.

It is known that the biomechanical properties of tissues, i.e., their stiffness can be an important indicator of various diseases, including chronic pancreatitis or pancreatic cancer13. The increased tissue stiffness, which stems from the rich fibrotic stroma in the tumor microenvironment, is central to disease development, physiology, and drug resistance 14-16. There are special medical imaging techniques, such as elastography, that can assess tissue stiffness, with pancreatic stiffness usually being assessed using ultrasound elastography (US-elastography)17–19 or MR-elastography20,21. These studies suggested that, pre-operatively, MR-elastography and US-elastography could effectively evaluate the degree of pancreatic fibrosis by quantifying its stiffness to, differentiate between benign and malignant pancreatic masses. However, these techniques do not have sufficient resolution to detect individual tumor cells or pancreatic islets, while their usefulness is also limited as the degree of stiffness of benign and malignant lesions may overlap. In addition, there are difficulties with the intraoperative application of these diagnostic methods, given that they depend on the use of specific markers or contrast agents.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is particularly promising for intraoperative tissue assessment as it offers non-invasive, label-free structural and functional 3D imaging technology to the study of biological tissues, together with high spatial resolution (1–20 μm) at depths of 1–3 mm22. Recently, extensions to OCT have been widely used in both animal research and clinical settings These include calculation of the attenuation coefficient of the OCT signal, polarization-sensitive OCT, OCT-angiography and OCT-elastography (OCE) for imaging of the collagenous microstructures, the microvasculature and for assessing the elastic properties of tissues23–26. Previously, OCT has been successfully applied to image fixed human pancreatic tissue27 and the main pancreatic duct28 as well as for distinguishing, ex vivo, between benign and malignant pancreatic cysts29. Recently, novel technology has been developed that is capable of detecting early-stage pancreatic cancers using high-resolution three-dimensional endoscopic OCT, and for treating them using high dose-rate brachytherapy under endoscopic OCT image guidance30. It has been shown that extended-focus optical coherence microscopy31,32 can image in vivo and ex vivo islets of Langerhans in murine pancreas without labeling, at a spatial resolution close to cellular dimensions33. Despite technological developments over the past decade, there are still several challenges in performing high-level pancreatic tissue characterization using OCT data. As the contrast in the OCT images is produced largely by optical scattering, this can sometimes be insufficient for tissue differentiation. For example, fibrous connective tissues within the pancreas can appear similar to a dense tumor. Therefore, there is a need for new approaches that allow quantitative analysis of OCT data and provide an additional contrast mechanism that will help OCT more accurately differentiate between pancreatic tissue types.

Compression OCE (C-OCE) based on OCT is a relatively new technique that is used to assess tissue stiffness at micron level, using assessment of tissue deformation under mechanical stress34. The advantage of C-OCE is its ability, quantitatively, to estimate stiffness values (Young’s modulus, kPa) distribution with a spatial resolution at the level of groups of cells (~ 40–50 μm) up to 1–1.5 mm deep within the object under study. The С-OCE method is particularly promising for intraoperative examination of various tumors, allowing the high stiffness of such tissues to be detected with greater contrast than in structural OCT images35. It has previously been shown that the quantitative compression С-OCE method can be used to determine the boundaries for breast tumor resection36–38, to guide biopsies for colorectal cancer39, and to assess the effectiveness of therapeutic treatment in these pathologies40,41. The use of C-OCE to assess the absolute stiffness values of the pancreatic parenchyma may have similarly useful potential due to its sensitivity to objective elasticity changes of the pancreatic tissue in adenocarcinoma and peritumoral parenchymal lesions.

Here we present for the first time the use of C-OCE for the robust intraoperative stiffness assessment of freshly excised human pancreatic specimens in partial pancreatectomy for PDAC. In the first part of the study, we demonstrate the promising potential of C-OCE for detecting qualitative and quantitative differences between intact and fibrous pancreatic parenchyma, which is important for predicting the likelihood of pancreatic fistula formation and the effectiveness of pancreatic islet isolation after pancreatectomy. In the second part of the study, we demonstrate the feasibility of using C-OCE to determine accurate resection margins in cases of PDAC. This integrated assessment may be valuable for both scientific research and clinical practice, particularly in the context of surgery, transplantation and diabetes management.

Materials and methods

Human Pancreatic Specimens after Pancreatectomy

The study was carried out on 35 freshly excised human pancreatic specimens obtained from 27 patients (ages 38–83) with stage I or II (T1–2 N0–1 M0) pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas (PDACs) after pancreatectomy (Fig. 1(A)). Five patients had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy with FOLFIRINOX prior to resection42. In all patients, non-tumor tissue was obtained at a distance of more than 10 mm from the visible tumor border to assess the condition of the pancreatic parenchyma. In eight patients, transition zones between PDAC and surrounding tissue were obtained to assess the feasibility of using C-OCE for identifying positive resection margins. The specimen sizes ranged from 0.5 × 1.0 × 0.5 cm³ to 1.0 × 2.0 × 0.5 cm³ (Fig. 1(A)). Subsequently, all postoperative pancreatic specimens were classified into groups based on histological examination (see section “Histological Examination”).

Overview of the experimental scheme. (A) Isolation of the pancreatic specimens for subsequent study of (i) pancreatic parenchyma and (ii) resection margins. (B) Palpation assessment of parenchyma stiffness (“soft” or “hard”). (C) C-OCE imaging using a custom-made 20 kHz spectral-domain OCT system. The tissue specimen is compressed by the OCT probe through an intermediate layer of reference silicone. (D) C-OCE image reconstruction and evaluation of the absolute stiffness (Young’s modulus, kPa) values of the pancreatic tissues. (E) Imaging was followed by histological staining and data analysis.

This study was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of the Privolzhsky Research Medical University (Protocol #3 from March 29, 2024). Written informed consent was obtained from all participating subjects or from their legally authorized representatives when participants lacked decision-making capacity. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Intra-operative palpation of pancreatic stiffness

During surgery, a surgeon palpated each patient’s pancreas distant from the primary tumor to qualitatively assess the stiffness of the pancreatic parenchyma (Fig. 1(B)). The textures of the pancreas were allocated as “soft” or “hard”. This procedure was performed by a surgeon with at least twenty years’ experience in pancreatic surgery.

Diagnosis of post-operative pancreatic fistula (POPF)

To identify postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF), patients were observed for three weeks. All diagnosed cases of POPF were classified according to the criteria of the International Study Group on Pancreatic Fistula (ISGPF)43. In this study, seven patients were diagnosed with grade B POPF. The diagnostic criteria included elevated amylase levels in the drainage fluid above the threshold, along with signs of infection like fever (> 38 °C), leukocytosis, localized erythema, induration, or purulent discharge.

Multimodal OCT Device and data acquisition

A spectral domain multimodal OCT setup (Institute of Applied Physics of the Russian Academy of Sciences, Nizhny Novgorod, Russia) was used in the study for scattering and elastic properties visualization of the pancreatic specimens (Fig. 1(C))44,45. The multimodal OCT setup has a common-path interferometric layout that operates at a 1.3 μm central wavelength with an axial resolution of ~ 10 μm, a lateral resolution of ~ 15 μm and a scanning depth of 2 mm in air. Two types of images — structural OCT and С-OCE—can be acquired simultaneously in real time from a single tissue area. For realization of C-OCE, a sequence of B-scans for the same lateral position is acquired. B-scans with sizes are 256 × 256 pixels covering laterally 4 mm were acquired at 20 Hz rate.

OCT and C-OCE images of the fresh pancreatic specimens were acquired 30–60 min after resection (Fig. 1(D)). Each specimen was used to acquire 10–14 OCT and C-OCE images (in 1–2 rows) depending on the size of the specimen and these were taken with image overlap in order to obtain data from the entire surface being studied. The OCT probe was attached to an automated positioning stage (Purelogic R&D PLRA4, Voronezh, Russia) that enabled both slow vertical motions to produce the tissue compression for C-OCE and the possibility of stepwise shifts to another position of B-scans, which was used for optical sectioning of the pancreatic specimens, as well as for B-scan shifting and then stitching of several C-OCE scans acquired in the same lateral direction to cover up to 2 cm.

OCE data processing

The elastic properties of the pancreatic tissues were studied in ex vivo specimens using phase-sensitive compression OCE (C-OCE) as described in34,46. The axial inter-frame strain was estimated by finding the axial gradients of the inter-frame phase variations. To estimate the phase gradients we used the “vector” method proposed in47. In this method, for finding strains, the axial gradients of the inter-frame phase variations are found by operating with the complex-valued OCT signals as vectors in the complex plane. In the resultant 2D OCE image, the resolution was about 4 times lower than that in the initial OCT images, i.e., ~ 40–50 μm in both directions. The resultant OCE images were color-coded: the stiffer areas (lower strain) appear blue, while softer areas (higher deformation) appear red (Fig. 1(D)).

To quantify the elastic properties of the tissue specimens, we used a reference layer of translucent silicone with pre-calibrated stiffness. The reference silicone layer used in this study had a Young’s modulus of 100 kPa, this value being found the most suitable for the study of pancreatic tissue stiffness (Young’s modulus) that could range from 10 kPa to 500 kPa.This reference silicone layer was placed between the output window of the OCT probe and the surface of each tissue specimen being studied so that it could then be slightly compressed by the OCT probe (Fig. 1(C)). The approach to OCT-image processing, developed in48,49, made it possible to perform mapping of the compression-induced strains and therefore quantitatively estimate the tangent Young’s modulus (in kPa) for a preselected level of stress applied to the tissue. Details of this approach can be found in previous publications49–51. The tangent elastic Young’s modulus of the tissue is defined as the ratio of the incremental strain in the silicone multiplied by the silicone stiffness relative to the incremental tissue strain.

It should be noted that tumor tissue stiffness properties are usually pronouncedly nonlinear, so that their Young’s modulus depends on the current stress52,53. Therefore, for the quantitative estimates of Young’s modulus presented below, in all measurements, we used a “standardized” level of applied stress in the range from 1 kPa to 3 kPa centered at 2 kPa.

Histological examination

After OCT and С-OCE imaging of the pancreatic specimens the scanning area was marked with histological India ink for correlation with subsequent histology examination (Fig. 1(E)). Histological sections were prepared through the marked areas, so that their planes corresponded to the structural OCT and С-OCE images. Slides of these sections were prepared according to the standard formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded procedure and stained according to standard protocols with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to identify histological structures and tissue types in the pancreatic specimens, while Van Gieson’s picrofuchsin (VG) was used for a more detailed examination of the connective tissue (VG stains collagen bundles bright pink). The histological sections were scanned using the EVOS M7000 All-in-one Imaging System (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA USA) in transmitted light. Whole-section histological images were subsequently analyzed and processed in open-source software for digital pathology—QuPath 0.5.154. For each pancreatic specimen the following were calculated from the histological sections: the proportion of acinar tissue as a percentage, the number and density of islets per mm2.

All postoperative pancreatic specimens were classified into three groups based on histological examination: (1) intact pancreatic specimens without signs of chronic pancreatitis or PDAC invasion (n = 11); (2) fibrotic pancreatic parenchyma with varying degrees of acinar tissue reduction as a result of chronic pancreatitis (n = 16) and (3) PDAC without or after neoadjuvant chemotherapy with varying degrees of invasion and fibrosis in adjacent parenchyma (n = 8).

Correlation of the С-OCE data with histology, and region of interest selection

After saving the central B-scan of the structural and elastography OCT images acquired as the 3D dataset we selected one of the histological sections prepared from the same specimens that best matched the OCT/C-OCE images. In this study, to provide more accurate identification of certain pancreatic tissue structures in the OCT and C-OCE images, we scanned a large specimen field and then analyzed the area consisting of 4–5 stitched structural OCT images and the corresponding C-OCE images. On such stitched OCT and C-OCE images, we analyzed the topological similarity of the mutual positions and geometrical dimensions of the structural components of the tumorous and non-tumorous pancreatic tissues and how they appeared in the corresponding histological sections.

For a comparative quantitative analysis of data in each C-OCE image, corresponding histological regions of interest (ROIs) were selected for calculating the local stiffness. In doing so, we first selected ROIs based on the structural OCT images, and then assessed these in the C-OCE images. At the first stage of numerical C-OCE data analysis the different pancreatic tissues types in each image were selected for quantitative statistical analysis and were categorized into five types: adipose tissue (n = 20), non-tumorous fibrous connective tissue (n = 31), acinar tissue (n = 33), adenocarcinoma (n = 20) and islets of Langerhans (n = 21) (n—number of ROIs). The typical size of each ROI, over which the mean values of stiffness were calculated, was 200 × 200 μm, and these are shown in the C-OCE images by the colored squares (Fig. 5). Given the fairly limited number of pancreatic tissue image ROIs (n = 125), the ROI-windows on the C-OCE images were selected manually by exact matching with the histology.

Palpatory stiffness classification of specimens based on surgeon-defined “soft” and “hard” pancreas (A), identified misclassified cases, Fisher’s exact test demonstrates a lack of correlation between the histological and palpatory assessments (n is the number of examined patients). Comparison of palpatory stiffness with the average stiffness values based C-OCE data (B), a low non-significant positive correlation was observed.

At the second stage of the study, a comparative quantitative analysis of the average stiffness values was performed in relation to the two identified main conditions of the pancreas based on histological examination: intact (non-fibrous) and fibrotic pancreatic parenchyma. In this case, the new ROIs were selected on the basis of the full scan of each C-OCE image. These ROI windows on the C-OCE images included different types of pancreatic tissue for further comparison with the data from manual palpation (“soft” or “hard” tissue) and from the histology (percentage of acinar tissue).

Statistical analysis

The variables for statistical inter-group comparison were the stiffness (Young’s modulus, kPa) calculated from the C-OCE images and the percentage of acinar tissue area calculated from the histological images. To evaluate the results of the quantitative image processing, we used the mean values ± standard deviation.

The Mann-Whitney U-test with Bonferroni correction was used to evaluate the statistical significance of the numerical data between the independent groups. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the categorical variables of the manual palpation stiffness index. In all cases, the differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.

Spearman’s correlation coefficient (r) was calculated to determine the correlation between the mean stiffness in the pancreatic specimens determined by C-OCE, the percentage of acinar tissue found in the histological examination, and the manual palpation stiffness index.

Box plots were used for graphical presentation of the data. In these box plots the center lines represent the medians, “+”—the mean values; the box limits are the 25th and 75th percentiles; and the whiskers—the 5–95 percentiles.

Receiver-operator characteristic analysis (ROC) was used to define the cut-off C-OCE value for the prediction of POPF and evaluate the diagnostic efficacy. The cut-off value was calculated taking into account the least cost (minimum number of false positive and false negative cases).

To assess the prognostic significance of palpation and С-OCE in predicting POPF, confusion matrix was constructed with calculation of test accuracy parameters.

For statistical data processing and graphical presentation of data, the programs Statistical Package for Social Sciences 16.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and Prism 8.0.2 statistical software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California) were used.

Results

Qualitative and quantitative assessment of the pancreatic parenchyma using OCT and C-OCE imaging in comparison with histological data

Since both the probability of pancreatic fistula formation after partial pancreatectomy, and the success of islet isolation from the pancreas for subsequent transplantation depend on the state of the pancreas, the stiffness of intact and fibrous pancreatic parenchyma was assessed using qualitative palpatory and quantitative C-OCE studies. At the first stage of the study, we performed a targeted comparison of the C-OCE data with the histological analysis data to quantitatively determine the stiffness of various types of pancreatic tissue and to differentiate intact tissue from fibrous parenchyma. At the second stage of the study, we analyzed the reliability of the results of manual palpation for assessing postoperative pancreatic samples as “soft” or “hard” in comparison with the results of the C-OCE studies used to differentiate intact tissue from fibrous parenchyma that were verified against the histological observations.

Figure 2 (A) shows the results of the study of a postoperative pancreatic specimen with intact parenchyma. Morphologically, this specimen is represented by acinar tissue divided into lobules, between which there are thin septa of connective tissue and layers of adipose tissue. In this case, the acinar tissue makes up 72% of the specimen area and contains a large number of islets of Langerhans (44 pcs; 1.64 pcs per mm2). The structural OCT image showed a homogeneous high level of scattering and rapidly decreasing OCT signal intensity, with greater penetration depth in the area of acinar tissue and a low OCT honeycomb-like signal level in the area of adipose tissue. However, structural OCT did not allow visualization of the presence of the islets in the acinar tissue. The corresponding C-OCE image showed a heterogeneous distribution of high and low stiffness values. In this case, the lowest stiffness values (< 100 kPa) were predominant in the area of adipose and acinar tissue, in addition to there being individual round or oval inclusions of high stiffness (˃300 kPa), which morphologically corresponded to the islet areas (black arrows in Fig. 2(A)). The high stiffness of the islets can be explained by the presence of closed peri-islet capsules of collagen bundles (Fig. 2(A), dark blue square). In addition, small inclusions of medium stiffness (200–300 kPa) were observed on the C-OCE images that also morphologically corresponded to islets, but, in particular, to those with incompletely closed capsules, which presumably led to their decreased stiffness.

In the overview histological image; the blue square contains an enlarged pancreatic islet; the green square indicates enlarged acinar tissue; while the orange square highlights enlarged connective tissue.

Figure 2 (B) shows the results of the study of postoperative pancreatic specimens with fibrotic parenchyma as a consequence of chronic pancreatitis. According to the histological examination, numerous large islets (16 pcs represented in the figure) were enclosed in fibrous tissue and were located with a high density of 4 pcs per mm2, this being associated with a reduction in the total amount of acinar tissue and a convergence of the residual islets. The structural OCT image showed a high level of the OCT signal, slowly attenuating with depth, in the area of the fibrous connective tissue. The corresponding C-OCE images were characterized by a heterogeneous distribution of stiffness values, with the lowest (less than 100 kPa) being in the area of residual adipose tissue and surrounding loose connective tissue bundles, while there were significantly greater stiffness values (100–200 kPa) in regions of densely packed connective tissue bundles, with even higher stiffness values (more than 300 kPa) in the areas with large islets. It is worth noting that the level of stiffness differs depending on the thickness of the collagen bundles and the density of their arrangement. The stiffness values are lower with a regular and loose arrangement of thin collagen bundles, but higher with a dense arrangement of thickened bundles.

Figure 3 shows the general trend of stiffness change based on quantitative assessment of the C-OCE images for four main types of pancreatic tissue. It was found that adipose tissue was characterized by the lowest stiffness values (33 ± 10 kPa). Acinar tissue, by low stiffness values in the range of 74 ± 20 kPa. Fibrous connective tissue had greater stiffness values (136 ± 30 kPa) compared to the acinar tissue, while pancreatic islets had the highest stiffness values (332 ± 80 kPa). As a result, a statistically significant difference in stiffness values was found between acinar tissue and fibrous connective tissue (p = 0.015). A highly statistically significant difference was also found between the lower stiffness values for acinar tissue compared to higher stiffness values for the islets (p < 0.0001), as well as between the stiffness values for fibrous connective tissue and islets (p < 0.0001). There was a lower statistically significant difference between acinar tissue and adipose tissue (p = 0.024).

Distribution diagram of stiffness values for the main morphological types of pancreatic tissue. Segments indicate a statistically significant difference between the study groups, where p is the magnitude of the statistical significance of the differences, and n is the number of examined areas for each group.

Using C-OCE data, the mean stiffness values of postoperative pancreatic samples with intact parenchyma and fibrous parenchyma resulting from chronic pancreatitis were determined (Fig. 4(A)). As a result, a high statistically significant increase in stiffness was found in pancreatic samples with fibrosis compared to intact pancreatic parenchyma represented by acinar tissue (182.2 ± 46.6 kPa vs. 86.1 ± 19.1 kPa; p < 0.0001). A morphometric study of pancreatic samples was performed to confirm the results of the C-OCE study and to determine the level of their correlation. In the morphometric study, the parameter of the proportion (%) of acinar tissue in relation to the entire studied sample, calculated from the histological sections, was chosen as the parameter for assessing the state of intact parenchyma and fibrous parenchyma. A higher proportion of acinar tissue was shown to be present in pancreatic samples having intact parenchyma compared to those with fibrous parenchyma (80.2 ± 9.7 vs. 13.2 ± 22%) (Fig. 4(B)).

(C) Correlation analysis of the histological and OCE assessments. A large, significant negative correlation was found for comparison of the percentage of acinar tissue with the average stiffness values.

Correlation analysis between the percentages of acinar tissue determined by histological examination and the average stiffness according to the C-OCE data demonstrated a high negative (r = -0.86, p < 0.0001) correlation (Fig. 4(C), which confirms the representativeness of the C-OCE data. Thus, in pancreatic samples with a proportion of acinar tissue of less than 40%, the tissue stiffness values were more than 100 kPa, and in samples with a proportion of acinar tissue more than 70%, the tissue stiffness values were less than 100 kPa.

As a result, the possibility of using C-OCE for quantitative assessment of stiffness (Young’s modulus, kPa) of the pancreas, as well as for objective differentiation of intact from fibrous parenchyma, was demonstrated. C-OCE allows for determination of the presence of “soft” intact parenchyma by its low values of Young’s modulus (86.1 ± 19.1 kPa), and this can serve as an objective criterion for the likelihood of development of pancreatic fistula. It was found that C-OCE also enabled the presence of pancreatic islets to be identified by finding individual inclusions having high stiffness (332 ± 80 kPa) with in the surrounding softer acinar tissue (74 ± 20 kPa), as well as the estimation of their likely numbers. The latter can serve as a prognostic factor for the successful isolation of islets for transplantation.

Comparative assessment of tissue stiffness determined by intraoperative palpation and C-OCE data

In the next step of the study, we compared tissue stiffness determined by intraoperative palpation and from C-OCE data. During surgery, 11 pancreatic specimens (37%) were classified by the surgeon as “soft”, while the remaining 17 (63%) were defined as “hard” (Fig. 5(A)). However, when comparing this palpation data with the histology, it was found that 25% of cases had been erroneously identified as “soft” tissue parenchyma, while 45% of cases were erroneously identified as “hard”. Thus, while the proportion of “soft” pancreas determined by palpation did not differ significantly between intact and fibrous parenchyma (p = 0.223), according to the C-OCE data, the mean stiffness values of the intact and fibrous parenchyma, classified by the surgeon as “soft” and “hard”, were 130.6 ± 77.5 kPa (range 54–305 kPa, median 99 kPa) and 151.7 ± 50.9 kPa (range 73–227 kPa, median 159 kPa), respectively.

Figure 5(B) shows the absence of statistically significant differences between the samples assessed as “soft” and “hard” in the mean stiffness value according to C-OCE (p = 0.226), indicating the low reliability of manual palpation. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was calculated to analyze the relationship between the mean stiffness according to the C-OCE data and the palpatory assessment. A weak positive, non-significant correlation was found between pancreatic stiffness according to C-OCE and manual palpation (r = 0.18; p = 0.376).

During the three-week follow-up period after pancreatectomy, POPF grade B was detected and clinically confirmed in 7 of 27 patients (35%). These patients exhibited signs of infection, including drainage fluid amylase levels three times the serum threshold. There were no cases of sepsis or deaths, and none of the patient’s required reoperation.

To assess the diagnostic effectiveness of predicting POPF after pancreatectomy based on pancreatic parenchymal stiffness, an ROC curve analysis was performed. Stiffness values were compared between groups with positive and negative POPF results. The stiffness measured by C-OCE of the pancreatic parenchyma in the POPF group was significantly lower than that in the non-POPF group (P = 0.0002) (Fig. 6(A)). ROC analysis demonstrated a high level of accuracy of C-OCE as a prognostic test, with the area under the curve (AUC) of 0.94. Taking C-OCE value < 84 kPa as a cut-off value of stiffness of pancreatic parenchyma for predicting POPF, sensitivity and specificity was 85% and 95% respectively, positive predictive value and negative predictive value was 86% and 95% respectively (Fig. 6(B), (C)). As a result, palpation produced false-negative results in 5 patients (initially, the pancreas was described as “hard” on palpation in cases that later developed POPF), while only two cases yielded true-positive findings. In comparison, the C-OCE method demonstrated significantly higher accuracy, detecting POPF in 6 out of 7 patients; only one result was false-negative (Fig. 6(C)).

Thus, the comparison of pancreatic tissue stiffness data obtained using quantitative C-OCE and qualitative palpation indicates the low reliability of the palpatory approach for assessing tissue stiffness. C-OCE could serve as a potential non-invasive intraoperative imaging method for evaluating tissue stiffness to predict POPF.

Application of C-OCE to identify pancreatic resection margins with PDAC invasion

To determine the effectiveness of the C-OCE method as an alternative to intraoperative express biopsy for assessing the presence of invasion of resection margins with PDAC, the transition zones of tumor and non-tumorous pancreatic tissue were examined. Pancreatic samples were examined with C-OCE and with subsequent histological verification, both in untreated patients and in patients following preoperative chemotherapy treatment.

Figure 7(A) shows the results of OCT and C-OCE examination of a pancreatic sample with PDAC without preoperative treatment. Histological examination showed this sample to contain a PDAC area and acinar tissue with a small number of islets (4 pcs; 0.1 pcs per mm2). Structural OCT and C-OCE images revealed differences in the level of backscattered light and of the elastic properties between the surrounding non-tumorous pancreatic tissue and regions of PDAC. While the structural OCT observation did not allow us to accurately determine the border of the tumor transition into the surrounding intact acinar tissue. The corresponding C-OCE image thus visualized a significantly more contrasting border with low stiffness values (less than 100 kPa) in the acinar tissue area and high stiffness values (more than 300 kPa) in the PDAC area. In this case, in the area of tumor tissue, a non-uniform distribution of the highest (more than 400 kPa) and lower (200–300 kPa) stiffness values could be observed. The highest stiffness values morphologically corresponded to tumor tissue with pronounced desmoplasia, i.e., tumor stroma proliferation with single or grouped tumor cells in the ducts (blue square in Fig. 7(A)). Lower stiffness values morphologically corresponded to areas of dense inflammatory lymphohistiocytic infiltrate in the tumor area (orange square in Fig. 7(A)).

Figure 7(B) shows a pancreatic specimen excised from a patient following neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Histological examination of this specimen showed residual tumor with therapeutic pathomorphism and an area of parenchymal fibrosis with a total reduction of acinar tissue but with residual islets. In this case, the structural OCT image shows heterogeneous OCT signal levels: a high and deeply penetrating OCT signal in the region of parenchymal fibrosis, and a low OCT signal, rapidly attenuating with depth, in the tumor area with necrosis. The corresponding C-OCE image showed a heterogeneous distribution of high, medium and low stiffness values. When comparing the C-OCE and histological data, it was found that the С-OCE image in the central region visualized a heterogeneous area with high (more than 400 kPa) and low (less than 200 kPa) stiffness values, which histologically corresponded to residual tumor tissue and to the necrosis, respectively. The С-OCE image also visualized heterogeneous areas with medium (100–200 kPa) and high (more than 300 kPa) stiffness values around the tumor tissue, which morphologically corresponded to connective tissue with residual islets (black arrows in Fig. 7(B)).

Figure 8 shows the results of quantitative stiffness assessment of malignant and non-malignant pancreatic tissues. The results of the quantitative stiffness assessment showed a highly statistically significant difference between high stiffness values for adenocarcinoma areas compared to the lower stiffness values of acinar tissue (497 ± 115 kPa vs. 74 ± 20 kPa; p < 0.0001) and fibrous connective tissue (497 ± 115 kPa vs. 136 ± 30 kPa; p < 0.0047).

Thus, C-OCE technology was shown to accurately visualize the transition zone between PDAC and surrounding non-tumorous tissue in patients both without and after neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Intraoperative C-OCE assessment of the status of the pancreatic margin during partial resection of PDAC may therefore improve the likelihood of obtaining a margin-negative resection. Furthermore, in some cases, the use of C-OCE could reduce the number of unjustified arterial resections that worsen the prospects of both short and long-term recovery8.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated for the first time the possibility of differentiating between intact and fibrotic pancreatic parenchyma, as well as determining the resection margins in PDAC, based on qualitative and quantitative assessment of tissue stiffness values (Young’s modulus) using the C-OCE method. We found that C-OCE allows accurate determination of the “soft” pancreas (i.e. without signs of parenchymal fibrosis) in patients, which is important for predicting the likelihood of pancreatic fistula after pancreatectomy and for assessing the effectiveness of islet isolation for possible transplantation. In addition, we demonstrated the significant potential of using C-OCE for obtaining greater contrast in images of the transition zone of malignant tissue into the surrounding non-tumor tissue, to enable more accurate determination of the limits of the zone and therefore a greater likelihood of obtaining a resection with a negative margin or, conversely, reducing the number of unjustified resections.

The richness of the extracted information from C-OCE examination may be comparable with the use of histological examination, although the resolution of C-OCE-images at ~ 30–40 μm is lower than in microscopic histological studies. On targeted correlation with histopathological findings, it was established that the C-OCE method, based on the level and pattern of distribution of stiffness values, allows for reliable differentiation between the various tissue types of pancreatic parenchyma (adipose tissue, acinar tissue, connective tissue, adenocarcinoma and islets). C-OCE was found to detect islets as isolated inclusions of high stiffness (332 ± 80 kPa) within the surrounding ‘soft’ acinar tissue (74 ± 20 kPa) or fibrotic connective tissue (136 ± 30 kPa). The greater stiffness of the pancreatic islets compared to acinar tissue can be explained by their histological structure. Pancreatic islets are spherical clusters of endocrine cells that are connected by strong intercellular junctions (desmosomes) and enclosed within a three-layer capsule55,56. Inside the islets, there is a network of capillaries and thin layers of connective tissue. It is important to note that the stiff islets, as measured by quantitative C-OCE, are those with an intact structure; fragmented islets and islets with inflammatory infiltrate lose their elastic properties and therefore much of their stiffness. Furthermore, samples with pancreatic pathology contained larger islets than normal pancreatic parenchyma. The presence of these larger islets is associated with islet cell hyperplasia and indicates pathological changes in the endocrine part of the pancreas57,58. The capability of C-OCE to identify acinar tissue containing islets has significant potential for predicting the likely yield of islets of Langerhans and their suitability for subsequent transplantation. Despite OCT’s superior resolution compared to C-OCE, the former’s endogenous contrast fails to visualize islets of Langerhans within acinar tissue. C-OCE overcomes this limitation by leveraging tissue stiffness measurements to enhance contrast for morphological structures in pancreatic specimens. In our recent study, we demonstrated for the first time, a relationship between stiffness values and the number of isolated pancreatic islets59. The number of isolated islets significantly decreased (≤ 110 pcs/g) in samples exhibiting stiffness values above 150 kPa and below 75 kPa. The optimal stiffness range for the efficient isolation of islets (≥ 120 pcs/g) from pancreatic tissue was identified as 75–150 kPa.

Currently, in order to avoid risk of the development of POPF and to select the optimal volume of resection, the stiffness of the pancreas is typically evaluated using intraoperative manual palpation by the surgeon60. Numerous studies have demonstrated that a soft pancreatic texture is a significant risk factor for developing POPF9,60,61. However, it has been noted that a reliable and standardized definition of what constitutes a soft or hard pancreas is still lacking61,62. This is confirmed by our study, which showed that manual palpation of the pancreas does not always enable accurate distinction between intact and fibrotic pancreatic parenchyma. When compared with histological data, we found that in 25% of cases, the surgeon incorrectly identified parenchyma with fibrosis as “soft” tissue, and in 45% of cases, parenchyma without pathology was classified as “hard” tissue (Fig. 5). This study demonstrated that palpation produced false-negative results in five patients who later developed POPF, while only two cases yielded true-positive findings. This further highlights the unreliability of palpatory measurements and the need for additional quantitative, high-resolution, and intraoperative methods to measure pancreatic stiffness. It was shown that the shear wave velocity values in US elastography of pancreatic parenchyma in POPF positive group was significantly lower than that in POPF negative group (P = 0.001)63. At the same time as another study has shown that pancreatic elastography is not useful to predict the risk of pancreatic fistulas after pancreatic resection64. Preoperative MR elastography has been used to quantify pancreatic stiffness and assess its association with the development of POPF65. However, MR imaging is labor-intensive, expensive, and always requires a contrast agent.

In our study, for the first time, it has been shown that C-OCE can be used to differentiate “soft” pancreatic parenchyma from “hard” regions of fibrotic pancreatic parenchyma developed as secondary results of chronic pancreatitis and acinar tissue reduction (p < 0.0001). Correlation analysis between mean stiffness values measured by C-OCE and the proportion of acinar tissue determined by histological examination in both intact and fibrotic pancreatic parenchyma revealed a robust negative correlation (r = -0.86, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4), confirming that C-OCE data accurately represents the histological features. To our knowledge this is the first study to evaluate the correlation between histological slides and С-OCE in detecting intact and fibrotic parenchyma in fresh human pancreatic resection specimens. In our study, the values of palpatory stiffness have been compared with the stiffness values measured using C-OCE. We found a low, non-significant positive correlation between pancreatic stiffness calculated from C-OCE and by manual palpation (r = 0.18; p = 0.376), which indicates the low reliability of the qualitative palpatory data.

What is most important is that we found that the stiffness of the parenchyma, measured in kPa of Young’s modulus, is also a predictor of POPF. In our study, we examined the potential utility of the C-OCE method for predicting POPF. A total of 27 patients were included, of whom 7 developed POPF following pancreaticoduodenectomy. The stiffness value based on C-OCE data for the pancreatic parenchyma was significantly lower in the POPF group compared to the non-POPF group (P = 0.0002) (Fig. 6). ROC analysis demonstrated a high level of accuracy of C-OCE as a prognostic test, with the area under the curve (AUC) of 0.94. In this study, a cutoff value of < 84 kPa for stiffness was identified, which was considered indicative of a relatively high likelihood of developing POPF after pancreaticoduodenectomy (sensitivity and specificity were 85% and 95%, respectively). Thus, we hypothesize that, compared with intraoperative palpation, C-OСE may be a more reliable quantitative intraoperative method for assessing pancreatic stiffness. Moreover, it could serve as a potential imaging technique for predicting POPF after pancreatectomy in future clinical practice.

In addition, this study has shown that the use of C-OCE may have great potential for accurately visualizing the transition zone between PDAC and the surrounding non-tumorous tissues in patients both without and after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (Fig. 7). The previous applications of the C-OCE method by our, and other, research groups have mainly focused on the area of resection margin assessment, such as for breast cancer36–38 and colon cancer39. Previously, in relation to pancreatic tissues, the possibility of using the ultra-high-resolution OCE method (axial optical resolution—1.5 μm, and lateral—1.6 μm) through a microendoscope for the visualization of pancreatic cancer has only been demonstrated for a mouse model66. In the present study, the use of C-OCE for the study of pancreatic tissue from human patients has been demonstrated for the first time. Here, traditional backscatter-based OCT can differentiate intact pancreatic parenchyma from PDAC, although the boundary definition may be imprecise, and differentiation between fibrotic parenchyma and PDAC remains challenging. These findings align with prior research using full-field OCT to distinguish malignant from benign tissue in resected pancreatic cancer specimens67. Our results highlight C-OCE’s significant potential for discriminating PDAC regions from both acinar tissue and chemotherapy-induced fibrotic tissue. Adenocarcinoma exhibits substantially higher stiffness values (> 400 kPa) compared to acinar (74 ± 20 kPa) and connective tissues (136 ± 30 kPa), with extensive areas of maximum stiffness (Fig. 8). This elevated PDAC stiffness correlates histologically with desmoplastic reaction—proliferative connective tissue growth surrounding tumor cells and ductal structures. It is worth noting that OCE data from postoperative pancreatic specimens aligns with endoscopic and intraoperative US-elastography findings. These modalities similarly demonstrate lower stiffness values for pancreatic parenchyma and significantly increased stiffness in fibrosis or adenocarcinoma68,69. However, considering that a diseased pancreas could have a heterogenous texture, the US-elastography measure may not be able, intraoperatively, to precisely reflect the stiffness of the pancreatic parenchyma while also having insufficient resolution to indicate the presence of residual tumor at the margins of the removed tissue. We have not imaged the resection margins of the surgical specimens as that would have affected the clinical workflow too much, however, we did include fresh pancreatic tissue for imaging to mimic the future application of intraoperative use. Moreover, we selected interesting and clinically relevant locations on the H&E and the corresponding C-OCE images; for example, transition zones of infiltrating tumor, glands and surrounding tissue (Fig. 6). Moreover, even if promising, US-elastography still provides little information for the evaluation of benign conditions. Notably, C-OCE, with 30–50 μm resolution—comparable to histology—better captures the morphological features of pancreatic parenchyma than does US-elastography. This enables intraoperative detection of intact pancreatic parenchyma, fibrotic regions, and tumor foci, serving as a critical predictive parameter for assessing pancreatic tissue quality.

In the future, real-time intraoperative imaging modalities, such as provided by C-OCE, may aid surgeons in obtaining tumor-free resection margins70,71. The application of stiffness parameters instead of using intraoperative frozen section analysis may reduce waiting times in the operating room. In addition, according to a number of authors, the current accuracy of intraoperative frozen section biopsy of resection margins for pancreatic cancer, particularly following neoadjuvant polychemotherapy, is suboptimal, with reported rates of up to only 80%72. The application of our suggested novel intraoperative imaging modalities aims to further improve radical resection results: either by enhancing detection of resection boundaries, or conversely, by reducing unwarranted venous and arterial resections. Such unnecessary resections worsen both immediate and long-term outcomes8. Using such a technique, resection margins could be examined in vivo—before resection, during resection in the resection bed, and after resection on the resected specimen. This approach aligns with Brendan F. Kennedy et al.73, who employed a portable OCE system to visualize the resection bed following wide local resection for breast cancer.

Along with the promising results, it is important to highlight the following limitations of our study. On the one hand, we need to mention the limitations of the OCT method, such as the low penetration depth (2 mm in air) of the probing light inside the tissue. This aspect is also impacted by the small size (4 мм) of the selected C-OCE images and, consequently, the small volume of tissue scanned. In addition, despite efforts to select the area of comparison as accurately as possible, complete correspondence between the OCT and C-OCE images on the one hand and the corresponding histological images on the other hand may be difficult in some cases. We have identified several reasons for this. First, the shape of the specimen could be distorted or deformed during the histological preparation procedures due to the fixation in formalin, dehydration, paraffining, etc.74. Second, a slight difference in the histological slice positions (even ~tens of microns) also affects the geometry of the pancreatic tissue’s structural components and complicates the comparison with the OCT-based images. Third, although OCT images have micrometer-scale resolution (typically 10–15 μm) approaching that of histology75, they do not typically reach the cellular-level of histology, therefore slight discrepancies in the size of the individual structural components of the tissue are possible. Given the above, only larger (~ tens of microns or more) regions or components can be matched at this scale, while smaller structures (a few microns in size) may appear much more distorted and displaced. Moreover, in this work we were only able to detect the presence and distribution of the islets. Due to the lack of cellular resolution, we could not determine the exact size of the islets and quantify their total number in the pancreatic tissue. We acknowledge that our study was preliminary, and these findings should be validated through further research. Although C-OCE shows promising potential for risk stratification of pancreatic fistula development, manual palpation assessment performed by an experienced pancreatic surgeon remains the primary method for evaluating pancreatic stiffness, as it is an independent predictor of pancreatic fistula.

Nevertheless, the C-OCE data presented above can already be used to formulate reliable criteria to detect areas of pancreatic lesions and to select intact pancreatic parenchyma. It must be emphasized that the C-OCE estimates were obtained for a standardized stress (2 ± 1 kPa in this study). This is also an important factor as human pancreas is a nonlinear viscoelastic soft tissue76. In the future, it may be necessary to develop technologies with increased speed and scanning fields. It may also be possible to obtain images in the en face plane, which would allow us to analyze the condition of tissues with a larger field of view and thus more accurately compare them with the corresponding histological sections. This in vivo technology will be applicable for open surgery only. Therefore, issues such as safety, sterilizability, space limitations, and short data acquisition cycle times will need to be addressed.

To summarize, the C-OCE method with quantitative measurement of tissue stiffness is a promising tool for intraoperative human pancreatic tissue differentiation during the surgical resection of pancreatic cancer and for assessing the state of the parenchyma. We believe that the results reported here represent a baseline in the use of this technique and are a first step towards establishing its use in a clinical setting. In the future, the use of C-OCE to measure the elastic properties (stiffness) of the pancreas during surgery may be the next step forward for quantitative assessment of pancreatic quality, rather than taking specimens and risking the formation of fistulas or the possibility of positive resection margins.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have demonstrated both qualitatively and quantitatively that OCE imaging shows great promise for the objective classification of different tissue types in human pancreatic parenchyma (adipose tissue, non-tumorous fibrotic connective tissue, acinar tissue, adenocarcinoma and islets of Langerhans). C-OCE has demonstrated the ability to identify lesions, and to enable the selection of intact “soft” parenchyma. In operative specimens with fibrosis or PDAC invasion, a significant increase in tissue stiffness was observed compared to intact tissue. A high correlation was found between pancreatic parenchyma estimates using C-OCE and microscopic histology. Compared to intraoperative palpation, C-OCE may serve as a more reliable and quantitative method for assessing pancreatic stiffness during surgery. We believe that this study is a step towards intraoperative evaluation for accurately aligned surgical resection of pancreatic parenchyma, using C-OCE volumetric data-based stiffness for real-time lesion identification and tissue quality assessment. The use of new real-time intraoperative imaging techniques may aid surgeons not only to achieve tumor-free resection margins, but also to identify areas of “soft” pancreatic parenchyma as a prediction of the likelihood of pancreatic fistula formation after pancreatectomy and to predict the efficiency of islet yield and their potential autotransplantation.
