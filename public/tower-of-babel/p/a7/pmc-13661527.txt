HMGB3 facilitates malignant phenotype of esophageal carcinoma via histone lactylation- and Wnt/β-catenin-dependent transcription activation of c-myc
Authors: Ting Qian, Jin Liu, Guochun Cao, Hui Jia, Meng Song, Min Wang, Luni Guo, Qian Zhang, Delin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-mobility group box 3 (HMGB3) mediates hypoxia-induced glycolysis in various cancers and acts as an oncogene in esophageal carcinoma (EC/ESCA). However, its roles in regulating glycolysis and histone lactylation during EC progression remain unclear. In this study, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and western blotting were conducted to measure HMGB3 levels in EC samples and cells. 5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) assays, colony formation assays, and flow cytometry analyses were conducted to measure cell proliferation and apoptosis. Xenograft mouse models were established to explore tumorigenesis in vivo. Lactate production and glucose uptake were measured using commercial kits. Immunofluorescence staining was used to evaluate the co-localization of c-myc with lysine lactylation (Kla) and Ki67 expression in tumor tissue. A TOP/FOP assay was employed to evaluate Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity. Results revealed upregulated HMGB3 and elevated histone lactylation in EC. HMGB3 knockdown inhibited EC glycolysis, c-myc lactylation, and cell proliferation, yet promoted apoptosis, mimicking the effect of the glycolysis inhibitor 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG). HMGB3 depletion also inactivated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling and impaired xenograft tumor growth, glycolysis, and lactylation. In conclusion, HMGB3 promotes EC malignant progression and tumor growth via histone lactylation and Wnt/β-catenin-mediated c-myc transcriptional activation.

Introduction

EC is the 7th most prevalent malignancy worldwide, with over 470,000 new cases diagnosed annually1,2. The two primary histological subtypes are esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and esophageal adenocarcinoma3. The progression of EC involves multiple genetic alterations that accelerate the malignant transformation of esophageal epithelial cells4. These genetic changes enhance the invasive and metastatic capabilities of cancer cells, leading to a poor prognosis4. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying malignant cellular behavior and tumorigenesis in EC is crucial for advancing our understanding and improving clinical outcomes.

Cancer cells undergo metabolic reprogramming to meet the heightened demands for energy and biosynthesis required for proliferation, invasion, and migration5,6. A hall marker of this reprogramming is the dependence on glycolysis for energy generation, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect, which represents a primary mode of metabolic adaptation in tumors7. As the preferred energy metabolic pathway in cancer cells, glycolysis is characterized by the conversion of glucose to pyruvate and subsequently to lactate, thereby enabling rapid energy production8. Increased glycolytic activity, along with the resultant lactic acidosis, reshapes the tumor stroma into a microenvironment that promotes tumorigenesis9. Leveraging the distinct metabolic differences between cancer and normal cells, targeting glycolytic enzymes may offer a therapeutic opportunity to modulate cancer metabolism and inhibit disease progression.

HMGB3, a member of the HMG family, plays a role in cancer development by regulating cancer cell proliferation and apoptosis; its dysregulation can disrupt cellular homeostasis10. Bioinformatics analyses indicate that HMGB3 is highly expressed in EC tissues compared with non-tumorous tissue, and elevated HMGB3 expression is related to poor survival in EC patients. Moreover, these bioinformatic findings have been validated in a previous study by Gao et al.11. A recent study further proposed that hypermethylation of miR-216a leads to HMGB3 upregulation, thereby enhancing malignant proliferation and migration of EC cells10. Although HMGB3 has been recognized as an oncogene in EC, its role in regulating glycolysis and lactylation—two key features of cancer metabolic reprogramming—has not been explored in this disease context. To date, while the functions of HMGB3 in glycolysis and lactylation during EC progression remain unclear, HMGB3 has emerged as a novel regulator of glycolytic activity in other cancer types, including lung cancer, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, colorectal carcinoma, and bladder cancer12–16. For example, HMGB3 overexpression promotes glycolysis in lung cancer by increasing glucose uptake and lactate production14. Another study reports that HMGB3 facilitates glycolysis by activating the β-catenin pathway in nasopharyngeal cancer cells13. Importantly, these findings from other cancer types cannot be directly extrapolated to EC due to the distinct metabolic and genetic landscape of EC. Therefore, investigating the HMGB3-glycolysis axis specifically in EC is required.

Beyond glycolysis itself, its end product lactate has emerged as a signaling molecule that drives post-translational lactylation of proteins17. Histone lactylation has been shown to regulate gene expression in various cancers18. However, whether HMGB3 regulates lactylation of non‑histone proteins, particularly the transcription factor c‑myc, in EC remains completely unknown. Given that c‑myc is a master regulator of glycolytic genes19,20 and is frequently hyperactivated in EC21,22, exploring its lactylation status may reveal a novel feedback mechanism linking metabolism to oncogenic transcription.

Therefore, the study aimed to investigate whether HMGB3 can promote glycolysis to facilitate EC cell malignancy and tumorigenesis. Moreover, the regulatory role of HMGB3 in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway was also explored. This pathway can enhance glycolysis by increasing the expression of downstream glycolytic enzymes, including lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1), and hexokinase-2 (HK2), as previously reported23. Specifically, we tested the hypothesis that HMGB3 activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling to upregulate c‑myc transcription, which in turn undergoes lactylation to enhance its transcriptional activity. This study suggests that targeting HMGB3 might represent a promising strategy to regulate cancer metabolism and inhibit EC progression.

Materials and methods

Tissue sampling

EC samples (n = 43) and paired non-carcinoma samples (n = 43) were collected from Jiangsu Cancer Hospital & Jiangsu Institute of Cancer Research, & The Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. All samples were promptly submerged in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80 °C for later analysis of lactate levels and lactylation. All participants in this study provided written consent after being fully informed. All human studies included in this research were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Jiangsu Cancer Hospital & Jiangsu Institute of Cancer Research & the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (approval number: KY-2025-124) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Bioinformatics analysis

Two tools, GEPIA and Kaplan Meier plotter, were used for bioinformatics analysis24,25. GEPIA was used to analyze HMGB3 level in EC samples (n = 182) and normal samples (n = 286), HMGB3 expression in four stages of EC, and the correlation between 10-year survival rate of EC patients and HMGB3 expression. In addition, expression correlations between HMGB3 and pyruvate kinase muscle isozyme M (PKM)/LDHA/HK2/c-myc in EC tissue are also analyzed by GEPIA. The relationship between HMGB3 level and 5-year survival rate in EC patients was explored using Kaplan Meier plotter. All abbreviations used in the study are available in supplementary table 1.

Cell lines and cell culture

Four EC cell lines (TE-1, ECA109, KYSE30, and KYSE450) and normal esophageal epithelial cell line (Het-1A) were used for cellular experiments. TE-1, KYSE30, and Het-1A cell lines were obtained from National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (#TCHu89, #TCHu266, and #GNHu51; Shanghai, China). ECA109 and KYSE450 cell lines were purchased from Fenghui Biology (#CL0092 and # CL0606; Hunan, China). All cell lines were authenticated by their respective suppliers using STR profiling, verified by morphological and growth characteristic examination, and confirmed to be mycoplasma-free.

The cell lines were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (RPMI-1640; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) added with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (Sigma Aldrich) at a constant 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Cell transfection and treatment

To silence HMGB3 expression, short hairpin RNA targeting HMGB3 (sh-HMGB3-1/2) was synthetized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). For HMGB3 overexpression, pcDNA3.1 vectors containing full sequence of HMGB3 were synthesized. The negative controls for sh-HMGB3 and pcDNA3.1-HMGB3 were sh-NC and empty pcDNA3.1 vectors. Transfection into TE-1 or ECA109 cells was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) for 48 h following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Western blot analysis or reverse transcription qPCR was performed to evaluate the efficiency of cell transfection.

To activate the Wnt/β-catenin signaling, TE-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well) overnight, cultured to 70–80% confluence, then treated with 20 μM SKL2001 (Medchem Express, USA) for 48 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2. SKL2001 was dissolved in DMSO (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) to 20 mmol/L stock solution and stored at −20°C; the working concentration was freshly diluted in culture medium (final DMSO concentration ≤0.1%). Control cells received an equal volume of DMSO. Furthermore, TE-1 cells were treated with a glycolysis inhibitor, 2-DG (8 mM; GlpBio, Montclair, CA, USA), for 48 h. The doses of 2-DG and SKL2001 were identified according to previous studies26,27.

Xenograft mouse model

A total of 10 BALB/c nude mice (aged 4–5 weeks, weighing 18–20 g) were obtained from Vital River Animal Technology (Beijing, China). All rodents were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12/12 h of light/dark cycle. Experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University (No. IACUC-2406053). The mice were randomly allocated to two groups: sh-NC and sh-HMGB3-1 (n = 5/group).

To establish the xenograft mouse model, a total of 5 × 106 TE-1 cells, transfected with either sh-HMGB3-1 or sh-NC, were injected subcutaneously into the right upper back of each mouse. Once the tumor volume in the sh-NC group reached around 70 mm³, tumor volume was assessed every other day over a period of 26 days. Tumor volume (mm3) was calculated utilizing the formula: (length × width2)/2. Finally, the euthanasia of mice via cervical dislocation, as well as tumor imaging and weighing, was performed. Lactate content in the tumors was analyzed, and histone H3 lysine 18 lactylation (H3K18la) level was assessed using western blot. The proliferation in mouse tumor tissue was evaluated through immunofluorescence staining for Ki-67. Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining was conducted to measure cell apoptosis in mouse tumor tissue. Immunofluorescence staining was also conducted to assess Kla and c-myc expression in mouse tumors.

Mouse tissue immunofluorescence staining

The mouse tumor tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin for 2 h at 37 °C, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Ki67 (ab15580, Abcam), c-myc (ab32072, Abcam), and Kla (PTM-1401RM, PTM Biolabs) overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies were then further incubated with the sections for 1 h. A fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) was used for observation and imaging. Five individual xenograft tumor samples (n = 5 mice) were used for ex vivo analysis.

TUNEL assay

A TUNEL detection kit (Roche, Switerland) was utilized to measure cell apoptosis in mouse tumor tissue. The sections underwent deparffinization through the use of xylene and rehydrated using a graded alcohol series. Following permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100, the sections were incubated with TUNEL reaction solution (50 μL) for 1 h. After washing with phosphate buffered saline, 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining was performed. The stained cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope, and the number of TUNEL-positive cells was analyzed by ImageJ software. Three independent biological replicates were performed for in vitro experiments, and five individual xenograft tumor samples (n = 5 mice) were used for ex vivo analysis.

Western blotting

EC cells or tissue were lysed with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The total protein concentration was determined via the bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Denatured proteins were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Following a 1-h blocking step at 25 °C with 5% bovine serum albumin (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (listed in Supplementary table 2). This procedure was followed by three washing steps and a 1-h incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies at 26 °C. Protein bands were detected using Typhoon FLA 9500 (GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, UK). The intensity of blots was determined using ImageJ v1.42q software, and relative protein expression was normalized to GAPDH. Three independent biological replicates were performed for in vitro experiments, and five individual xenograft tumor samples (n = 5 mice) were used for ex vivo analysis.

Reverse transcription qPCR

RNA extraction from EC cells and tissue was performed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Nanodrop ND-1000 was used to determine RNA concentration and purity. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was obtained using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan) and utilized for qPCR using SYBR Green PCR master mix (Life Technologies) on a CFX96 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sequences of primers are provided in Supplementary Table 3. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Measurement of lactate content

After 10 min of centrifugation at 12,000 g, the supernatants from homogenized tumor tissue or EC cells were collected. Lactate production was assessed using a lactic acid assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as instructed. Absorbance readings were taken at 570 nm with a spectrophotometer. The lactate levels were then normalized relative to the number of cells. Three independent biological replicates were performed for in vitro experiments, and five individual xenograft tumor samples (n = 5 mice) were used for ex vivo analysis.

Detection of pyruvate kinase activity, NAD+/NADH ratio, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) level

The activity of pyruvate kinase was assessed using a pyruvate kinase assay kit (Abnova, China) following a previously described protocol, with fluorometric detection at excitation/emission wavelengths of 540/590 nm. The NAD+/NADH ratio was measured with a colorimetric detection kit (Abcam, UK) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Mitochondrial ATP levels were determined using an ATP assay kit (Abcam) as per the manufacturer’s instructions, and the ATP concentration was normalized to total protein content.

Detection of cell viability

A total of 5 × 103 ECA109 and TE-1 cells per well were plated in 96-well plates and subjected to indicated treatment. Subsequently, each well was treated with 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Medchem Express, USA). Following a 2 h of incubation, the optical density was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer (BioTek, USA). Each experiment was repeated three times.

Measurement of cell proliferation

EdU and colony formation assays were conducted to measure EC cell proliferation. Three independent replicates were performed for each assay. For EdU assays, 1 × 10⁵ TE-1 and ECA109 cells per well were plated into 24-well plates following transfection. Cells were then subjected to 2 h of incubation with 50 μM EdU (RiboBio, China). Subsequently, the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, China), permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, followed by DAPI staining. Cells positive for EdU staining were finally visualized using a microscope (Olympus, Japan).

For colony formation assays, TE-1 and ECA109 cells (1000 cells per well) with indicated transfection were plated into 6-well plates. After a 10-day incubation at 37 °C, cell fixing with methanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and subsequent staining with crystal violet (Medchem Express, USA) were carried out. Colony formation was then evaluated using microscopic analysis.

Flow cytometry analysis

After resuspending in 1× binding buffer, EC cells were incubated with FITC Annexin V (5 μL) for 15 min and then with propidium iodide (PI; 5 μL; Dojindo, Japan) for 10 min at 26 °C. The analysis of apoptotic cells was performed using flow cytometry (Cytek Biosciences, Shanghai, China). Gating strategy for flow cytometry apoptosis quantification is shown in Supplementary Fig 1. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Measurement of extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR)

Glycolytic activity and mitochondrial respiration were assessed by measuring ECAR and OCR using an XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse, Billerica, USA). TE-1 and ECA109 cells were seeded at 1.5 × 104 cells/well in XF96 cell culture plates and cultured for 24 h. Prior to detection, the culture medium was replaced with glucose-free XF base medium (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) containing 2 mM L-glutamine (pH 7.4), followed by incubation in a non‑CO₂ incubator at 37 °C for 1 h. For ECAR analysis, a glycolysis stress test was performed via sequential injection of 10 mM glucose, 1 μM oligomycin, and 50 mM 2-DG. For OCR analysis, a mitochondrial stress test was conducted with sequential injection of 1 μM oligomycin, 1 μM carbonyl cyanide-4-phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and 1 μM rotenone/antimycin A. After the assay, cells were lysed, and total protein content per well was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid kit (Beyotime). ECAR and OCR values were normalized to corresponding protein concentration. All experiments were performed in three independent replicates.

Detection of glucose uptake

Glucose uptake in EC cells was assessed utilizing glucose uptake colorimetric assay kit (Biovision, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Immunoprecipitation

EC cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against c-myc (ab32072, Abcam) and pan-Kla (PTM-1401, PTM Biolabs). Protein A/G beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) were then added and incubated for an additional 2 h. The mixture was subjected to 2 min of centrifugation at 2000 × g, and the resulting pellets were resuspended in 1× loading buffer. Following a 5-min boiling, the samples were prepared for western blotting. The assay was performed three times.

Cell immunofluorescence staining

TE-1 and ECA109 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at 4 °C. Cell permeabilization using 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocking using 5% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were then performed. Primary antibodies against Ki67 (ab15580, Abcam), c-myc (ab32072, Abcam), and Kla (PTM-1401RM, PTM Biolabs) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, cell incubation with secondary antibodies was conducted for 1 h, followed by DAPI staining of nuclei. Each assay was performed in triplicate.

TOP/FOP assay

A TOP/FOP assay was employed to evaluate Wnt/β-catenin transcriptional activity. EC cells were seeded in 24-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) 24 h prior to transfection. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) with 100 ng of TOP-Flash or FOP-Flash reporter, 10 ng of pRL-SV40 vector (Renilla luciferase, internal control), and 50 ng of sh-NC or sh-HMGB3-1/2 plasmids per well. For the HMGB3 knockdown + SKL2001 group, cells were additionally treated with 20 μM SKL2001 (or equal volume DMSO as control) for 48 h. Cells were then lysed in 100 μL Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega), and luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) on a GloMax 20/20 luminometer. Wnt/β-catenin activity was expressed as the ratio of TOP-Flash to FOP-Flash luciferase activity after normalization to Renilla signal. Each condition was tested in triplicate, and three independent experiments were performed.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed by GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad software, USA). Statistical tests were chosen based on study design and data characteristics. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons, assuming independent samples, normality, and equal variances. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test was applied for multi-group comparisons to evaluate main effects and interactions. All data distribution assumptions (normality and homogeneity of variances) were verified prior to analysis using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. The value of p < 0.05 was the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

HMGB3 expression is upregulated in EC and predicts poor prognosis

Bioinformatics analysis using GEPIA database shows that HMGB3 is highly expressed in ESCA samples (n = 182) compared with that in normal samples (n = 286) (Fig. 1A, p < 0.05). Results from RT-qPCR confirmed the upregulation of HMGB3 levels in collected EC tissue (n = 43) compared to its expression in corresponding normal tissue (n = 43) (Fig. 1B, p < 0.05). In addition, HMGB3 expression was consistently elevated across all clinical stages (Ⅰ-Ⅳ) of EC in Fig. 1C (violin plot from GEPIA). The median relative HMGB3 level in each stage exceeded 5, notably higher than the baseline in normal endometrial tissue. Statistical analysis (F = 3.35, p = 0.0204) in Fig. 1C confirms high HMGB3 expression across stages and significant differences among them. Bioinformatics analysis using the Kaplan-Meier plotter illustrates that high expression of HMGB3 correlates to a low 5-year survival rate in patients with EC (Fig. 1D, p = 0.012). Consistently, GEPIA analysis shows that high HMGB3 expression is associated with reduced 10-year survival rate in patients with EC (Fig. 1E, p = 0.027). As shown by RT-qPCR and western blot analyses, the significant high HMGB3 expression levels were found in four EC cell lines (TE-1, ECA109, KYSE30, and KYSE450) compared with its expression in Het-1A cells (Fig. 1F, G, p < 0.001). Consistently, lactate production in four EC cell lines is higher than that in Het-1A cells (Fig. 2B, p < 0.001). Given that large amounts of lactate are produced and serve as a substrate for histone lactylation, we then assessed the levels of protein lactylation in EC tissue and cells. Histone modifications are crucial for numerous biological processes, and studies have indicated that H3K18la regulates various biological functions, including oncogenesis28,29. HMGB3 knockdown inhibits EC cell proliferation while promoting cell apoptosis

Two EC cell lines (TE-1 and ECA109) were randomly selected for subsequent cellular experiments. The plasmid sh-HMGB3-1/2 was used to silence HMGB3 expression in the two EC cell lines. As shown by Fig. 3A, HMGB3 expression was effectively reduced in sh-HMGB3 groups compared to that in the sh-NC group (p < 0.001). Additionally, the protein level of HMGB3 was also significantly diminished by sh-HMGB3 plasmids (Fig. 3B, p < 0.001). Results of CCK-8 assays showed that the optical density values were markedly decreased in the sh-HMGB3 groups compared to that in control group (Fig. 3C, p < 0.001), indicating the repressive impact of HMGB3 knockdown on EC cell viability. The proportion of EdU-positive cells was diminished in the context of HMGB3 depletion (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig 2A, p < 0.001). Similarly, HMGB3 knockdown led to a decrease in the proportion of EC cell colonies (Fig. 3E, F). The results from EdU staining and colony formation assays indicate the repressive impact of HMGB3 deficiency on EC cell proliferation. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the apoptotic rate of TE-1 and ECA109 cells was markedly elevated in the context of HMGB3 depletion compared to that under sh-NC condition (Fig. 3G, H). Protein levels of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP were prominently elevated in sh-HMGB3 groups compared with their levels in sh-NC group (Supplementary Fig 2B, C, p < 0.001). The upregulation of apoptotic markers indicated the promoting role of HMGB3 knockdown in EC cell apoptosis. Furthermore, gain-of-function experiments were conducted following transfection of pcDNA3.1/HMGB3 vectors and empty vectors (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). Notably, HMGB3 overexpression significantly increased EC cell viability and the number of EdU positive cells (Supplementary Fig. 3C–E). HMGB3 knockdown represses EC cell glycolysis

As shown by Fig. 4A,B, HMGB3 depletion led to a decrease in ECAR and an increase in OCR in TE-1 and ECA109 cells. In addition, the glucose uptake was reduced in response to HMGB3 deficiency (Fig. 4C). The protein expression of glycolytic markers (PKM2, HK2, and LDHA) was reduced in TE-1 and ECA109 cells upon HMGB3 knockdown (Fig. 4D). Moreover, bioinformatics analysis using GEPIA reveals that HMGB3 expression is significantly correlated to PKM (p = 0.0016), LDHA (p = 0.025), or HK2 (p = 1.6e-11) expression in EC tissue (Fig. 4E, F). Furthermore, pyruvate kinase activity and ATP level were markedly reduced by HMGB3 depletion, while NAD+/NADH ratio was significantly increased by sh-HMGB3 (Fig. 4H–J). Collectively, the knockdown of HMGB3 inhibits EC cell glycolysis. In contrast, HMGB3 overexpression promotes the glycolysis of EC cells. Knockdown of HMGB3 suppresses lactylation of c-myc

The lactate production in ECA109 and TE-1 cells was significantly attenuated by HMGB3 depletion (Fig. 5A). A previous study shows that intracellular lactate can drive c-myc expression in the progression of cancer30. c-myc is an extensively studied oncogene, plays a crucial role in regulating tumor cell migration, invasion, proliferation, and angiogenesis across various cancers, including esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC)31. In this study, as shown by results of immunoprecipitation, HMGB3 depletion inhibits c-myc lactylation in EC cells (Fig. 5B). Protein levels of c-myc and H3K18la were all diminished in the context of HMGB3 silencing (Fig. 5C). According to immunofluorescence staining, the nuclear co-localization of c-myc and Kla in ECA109 and TE-1 cells was significantly restrained by HMGB3 deficiency (Fig. 5D). Moreover, there exists significant expression correlation between HMGB3 and c-myc (Fig. 5E, p = 6e-15). HMGB3 knockdown exerts a similar inhibitory effect on glycolysis and c-myc lactylation in EC cells as treatment with 2-DG

2-DG was shown to reduce lactate production (Supplementary Fig. 5A), ameliorate c-myc lactylation (Supplementary Fig. 5B), reduce H3K18la and c-myc protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 5C), and inhibit the co-localization of c-myc and Kla in TE-1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Importantly, HMGB3 depletion exerted a similar suppressive effect on c-myc lactylation as 2-DG did (Supplementary Fig. 5A–D). Moreover, 2-DG diminished ECAR while increasing OCR in EC cells (Supplementary Fig. 5E, F), inhibited glucose uptake (Supplementary Fig. 5G), and downregulated glycolytic markers in TE-1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5H). The repressive impact of glycolysis inhibitor (2-DG) on EC cell glycolysis was consistent with the role of sh-HMGB3 (Supplementary Fig. 5E–H).

HMGB3 knockdown and 2-DG treatment exert a comparable inhibitory effect on EC cell proliferation and induce similar levels of apoptotic rate

It was found that the glycolysis inhibitor (2-DG) and sh-HMGB3 exert a similar inhibitory effect on TE-1 cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 6A) and proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 6B, C) compared with sh-NC transfection (p < 0.001). Conversely, the apoptotic rate of TE-1 cells was increased by sh-HMGB3 or 2-DG (9.97 ± 0.51%, 14.6 ± 0.84% vs sh-NC: 6.53 ± 0.38%) (Supplementary Fig 6D). The cleaved PARP and cleaved caspase-3 levels were upregulated by 2-DG treatment, showing a trend similar to that observed with sh-HMGB3-1 (Supplementary Fig. 6E, p < 0.001). The results suggest that HMGB3 knockdown and 2-DG treatment have comparable effects on EC cell proliferation and apoptosis.

Discussion

A significant portion of global EC cases are diagnosed in China, where nearly half of all new annual cases occur, representing a serious threat to human health35,36. Improving treatment strategies therefore requires a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying EC. In this study, we observed upregulation of HMGB3 in EC tissues, cell lines, and xenograft mouse models, consistent with previous reports10,11. We further demonstrated that HMGB3 positively regulates c-myc in EC cells. The c-myc oncogene is aberrantly overexpressed in various malignancies, including EC, and is essential for tumor initiation and progression31,37; its overexpression correlates with shorter survival in EC patients21. Inhibition of PKM2/STAT3/c-myc signaling suppresses glycolysis in ESCC38. Moreover, deacetylation of β-catenin promotes its nuclear translocation, directly enhancing transcription of the downstream c-myc oncogene39,40, which is consistent with our identification of an HMGB3/β-catenin/c-myc regulatory axis in EC cells.

Although our data indicate that HMGB3 knockdown reduces c-myc lactylation, the molecular connection between HMGB3 and the lactylation machinery remains unclear. As a chromatin-binding protein primarily involved in transcriptional regulation, HMGB3 may indirectly influence lactylation by modulating the expression of glycolytic enzymes or lactylation erasers. Alternatively, HMGB3 might physically interact with these enzymes, as previously reported for HMGB1 interacting with the glycolytic enzyme PFKFB3 in cervical cancer41. Future co-immunoprecipitation studies should explore whether HMGB3 associates with LDHA, p300 (a proposed lactyltransferase), or HDACs (potential delactylases). Moreover, although HMGB3 knockdown reduced c-myc lactylation, we cannot distinguish direct lactylation of c-myc from indirect effects due to reduced lactate availability. Rescue experiments using cell-permeable lactate or lactylation-mimetic c-myc mutants would help establish causality, representing important directions for future research.

Our finding that HMGB3 promotes Wnt/β-catenin signaling is consistent with a previous study10. We further confirmed that HMGB3 positively regulates additional downstream targets of this signaling, including Cyclin D1 and MMP7. As a key cell cycle regulator, Cyclin D1 drives unchecked cell proliferation and contributes significantly to cancer pathogenesis42, which may explain the pro-proliferative effect of HMGB3 in EC cells. MMP7, a member of the matrix metalloproteinase family, degrades the extracellular matrix during normal processes such as tissue remodeling, reproduction, and embryonic development43,44.

While our data confirm that HMGB3 positively regulates β-catenin signaling and its downstream effectors, the detailed molecular mechanism remains unresolved. In response to the reviewer’s concern regarding the partial rescue by 20 mM SKL2001 in HMGB3-knockdown cells, this incomplete recovery may reflect HMGB3 acting through β-catenin-independent pathways. Four possible mechanisms underlying the HMGB3–β-catenin regulatory axis are proposed: direct physical interaction between HMGB3 and β-catenin; regulation of β-catenin stability and degradation; modulation of E-cadherin/β-catenin complex dissociation from the membrane; and alteration of the APC/Axin/GSK-3 destruction complex. Further validation using SKL2001 concentration gradients, degradation-resistant β-catenin mutants, dominant-negative TCF/LEF reporters, and β-catenin siRNA to clarify whether HMGB3 affects β-catenin stability, subcellular localization, or transcriptional activity. Until such experiments are completed, the conclusion that HMGB3 activates Wnt/β-catenin signaling should be considered a functional upstream relationship requiring further mechanistic evidence.

Another HMGB family member, HMGB2, regulates glycolysis in gastric, breast, and pancreatic cancers45–47. HMGB2 silencing suppresses hypoxia inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α)-mediated glycolysis in pancreatic cancer and positively regulates glycolytic genes such as HK2, LDHA, and glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1)47. This pro-glycolytic effect of HMGB2 aligns with the role of HMGB3 in EC revealed in this study. HMGB1 has been shown to promote antitumor immune responses in digestive cancer48. HMGB3 has been analyzed as an immune-related gene in EC49, with a positive correlation with follicular helper T cells and potential as an independent prognostic factor50. Therefore, future studies should examine the relationship between HMGB3 and immune responses in EC.

Beyond its metabolic and Wnt-regulatory functions, HMGB3 may also have immunological implications in EC. As a potential damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMP), HMGB3 could influence the tumor immune microenvironment through receptors such as TLR4/RAGE, as demonstrated for HMGB151. Given that HMGB3-driven glycolysis increases lactate production, HMGB3 may promote immunosuppressive cells, including M2 macrophages and Tregs52. HMGB3 also correlates with immune cell infiltration in pancreatic cancer53. Future studies in immunocompetent models should examine whether HMGB3 inhibition reshapes the tumor immune microenvironment and synergizes with immune checkpoint blockade, potentially offering a dual therapeutic strategy for EC patients with high HMGB3 expression.

The emerging role of HMGB proteins (particularly HMGB1 and HMGB2) as metabolic regulators in multiple cancers stems from their dual functionality as nuclear architectural factors and extracellular DAMPs54–56. Intranuclearly, HMGB proteins modulate chromatin structure and facilitate transcriptional activation of key metabolic enzymes involved in glycolysis (e.g., HK2, PKM2, LDHA)47,57, glutaminolysis57, and lipid biosynthesis58 through interactions with transcription factors such as HIF-1α, c-Myc, and p5359–61. Extracellularly, secreted HMGB proteins bind to receptors like TLR4 and RAGE51, activating downstream NF-κB, MAPK, and PI3K/AKT cascades that promote Warburg-like metabolic reprogramming62. Post-translational modifications (acetylation, phosphorylation, oxidation) regulate their subcellular localization and metabolic target selectivity. Given that metabolic heterogeneity across cancer types converges on enhanced anabolism and redox balance, HMGB proteins serve as central hubs integrating genomic stability, stress signaling, and metabolic adaptation, with feedback loops involving ROS and lactate sustaining HMGB expression and release63,64. Targeting HMGB-mediated metabolic pathways may therefore represent a broad-spectrum therapeutic strategy.

An important question is whether HMGB3 inhibition synergizes with metabolic therapies (e.g., 2-DG, PFKFB3/PFK1 inhibitors) or Wnt pathway inhibitors (e.g., IWR-1). Since HMGB3 promotes both glycolysis and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, dual targeting may outperform monotherapy. Preclinical evidence shows that HMGB1 interacts with PFKFB3 and regulates its protein stability41. In EC, HMGB3 knockdown phenocopied 2-DG effects, suggesting that combining HMGB3 inhibition with low-dose 2-DG or PFK inhibitors could enhance metabolic stress and reduce toxicity. Moreover, because SKL2001 only partially rescued HMGB3 knockdown phenotypes, concurrent HMGB3 and Wnt inhibition (e.g., with IWR-1) may more effectively suppress c-myc, Cyclin D1, and MMP7. Future studies should assess synergy via isobologram analysis, colony formation, and xenograft models, and explore optimal sequencing to overcome metabolic adaptation. Such combinations may benefit EC patients with high HMGB3 expression and poor therapy response.

This study has several limitations. First, detailed clinicopathological data (e.g., stage, grade, survival) were unavailable for the 43 EC patient samples, precluding correlation analysis between HMGB3 expression and clinical outcomes in our cohort; we therefore used public databases for survival analysis. Second, HMGB3 protein expression in patient tissues was not examined by immunohistochemistry or western blotting, leaving its clinical relevance unaddressed. Third, our xenograft study used immunodeficient mice, limiting assessment of HMGB3’s role in the tumor immune microenvironment. Addressing these gaps in future studies will further solidify the translational potential of targeting HMGB3 in EC. Fourth, although HMGB3 knockdown phenocopied 2‑DG treatment, we did not perform rescue experiments to restore lactylation (e.g., exogenous lactate or lactylation‑mimetic c‑myc mutants), so causality for lactylation‑dependent oncogenic effects remains unproven. Additionally, xenograft experiments used only TE-1 cells, limiting generalizability given EC heterogeneity, and key findings were not validated in patient-derived xenografts or immunocompetent models. Future studies using additional EC cell lines (e.g., ECA109) and patient-derived xenografts models are needed to confirm the translational potential of targeting HMGB3.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that HMGB3 promotes malignant behavior of EC cells and facilitates xenograft tumor growth in mice by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling and promoting glycolysis and c-myc lactylation. This is the first report of HMGB3 modulation in EC, highlighting the potential of targeting HMGB3 as an effective strategy to regulate cancer metabolism and inhibit EC progression.
