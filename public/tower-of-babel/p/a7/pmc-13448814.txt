Combination therapy with HSP90 inhibitors and NanoPulse stimulation synergistically impedes hepatocellular carcinoma and breast cancer growth in mice
Authors: Christiana Dimitropoulou, Gagan Thangjam, Brittany P. Lassiter, Teresa Hooker, Ruben M. L. Colunga Biancatelli, John D. Catravas, Stephen J. Beebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanopulse Stimulation (NPS) represents a promising and innovative therapeutic approach to cancer. Heat shock protein 90 Inhibitors (HI), on the other hand, are currently under investigation in multi-drug chemotherapy trials for various types of malignancies. Here we present results of a combination therapy employing two HSP90 inhibitors (AUY-922 or 17-AAG) and NPS to ablate cancer growth. We firstly tested the inhibitors alone or in combination with NPS in vitro in N1S1 cells, and then into two models of orthotopic Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) and 4T1-Luc breast cancer. NPS + HI reduced tumor growth more than the sum of HI and NPS administered alone, promoting cell death through DNA-damage, PARP activation and caspase-3-mediated apoptosis. Furthermore, HI and NPS, when combined, were used in lower doses than those required to achieve similar results, thus reducing the risk of potential side effects. By acting synergistically, combination therapy with HSP90 Inhibitors and NanoPulse Stimulation acts through distinct mechanisms, demonstrating a potent treatment that results in synergistic tumor mass reduction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55633-3.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) exemplify solid malignancies for which current therapeutic options remain inadequate. HCC is the sixth most common cancer worldwide and the third leading cause of cancer-related death; fewer than 20% of patients are candidates for curative resection or transplantation at diagnosis, and systemic therapies — including multi-kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors — provide modest survival benefit in advanced disease1,2. TNBC, which lacks targetable hormone receptors and HER2 amplification, carries the worst prognosis among breast cancer subtypes despite recent advances in immunotherapy and antibody-drug conjugates3. In both diseases, locoregional therapies (radiofrequency ablation, transarterial chemoembolization, cryotherapy) can achieve local tumor control but are limited by incomplete ablation margins, recurrence, and the inability to address systemic micrometastatic disease4,5. There is therefore a strong rationale for combining locoregional tumor destruction with systemic agents that exploit the unique biological vulnerabilities created by focal therapy. NanoPulse Stimulation (NPS; also referred to as nanosecond pulsed electric fields, nsPEFs, or nanosecond electric pulses, nsEPs) is a biophysical technique in which ultrashort, high-intensity electric fields that last on the order of billionths of a second (1/1 × 109 s), are applied to cells and tissues6,7. The translation of pulsed-power physics into biological systems provides a stimulus that does not naturally exist in living organisms; as a consequence, NPS elicits unique bioelectric and biochemical responses that have not been shaped by evolutionary adaptation. Unlike conventional electroporation, the electric fields generated by NPS penetrate through cells rather than traveling around them, producing transient, nanometer-scale channel-like pores that allow ionic redistribution and disrupt membrane homeostasis8.

If NPS conditions are not lethal, different cell response mechanisms have been observed ranging from transient ion passage9,10 and cell oxidation11 to changes in cell signaling12, effects on the mitochondrial membrane potential13,14, modulation of cytoplasmic proteins, activation of transcriptional factors15 and cell re-programming16. Using higher field intensities, pulse rate, number, waveforms5, frequencies, NPS showed to be a valuable approach for inducing cell death17,18. These multiscale perturbations position NPS as a novel modality capable of simultaneously modulating electrophysiological, metabolic, and stress-response networks within tumor cells19–21. Besides advances in preclinical investigation, multiple groups are generating new electrodes that can be applied to larger or deep-seated tumors7,22, while providing safe stimulation protocols with limited side effects23–25.

In contrast, Heat Shock Proteins (HSPs) constitute a highly-evolutionary conserved family of molecular chaperones that support protein folding, stabilization, and function during environmental stress, including heat, hypoxia, and oxidative injury26. Although predominantly cytoplasmic, HSPs can translocate to the nucleus to modulate transcriptional programs27, localize to the plasma membrane to promote immune cell interactions28, or be released extracellularly to signal tissue damage and activate innate immune responses29. HSP90, the most abundant protein of this family, is overexpressed in different types of cancer30 as it is responsible for the folding of several oncoproteins31. HSP90 Inhibitors (HI) are currently under investigation for cancer32,33, neurodegenerative disorders34, and fibrosis35–39. Indeed, the inhibition of HSP90 has been shown to sensitize cancer cells to drugs-40 and radiation-based therapy41. HI block the ATP-binding site of HSP90, impeding its chaperone function, and interfering with the signaling of cell survival pathways, and thus, increase the efficacy of standard monotherapies42,43. Thus, targeting HSP90 represents a rational strategy to disable pro-oncogenic networks while simultaneously amplifying cellular vulnerability to external stressors.

The combination of NPS and HSP90 inhibition holds particular therapeutic promise. By simultaneously destabilizing membrane integrity, calcium homeostasis, and mitochondrial function (via NPS), while suppressing pro-survival signaling and apoptotic escape mechanisms (via HI), the two modalities may converge on complementary cell-death pathways. Importantly, combining NPS with HI could reduce the voltage requirements of NPS and lower the dosing burden of HI, thereby minimizing toxicity of both treatments.

In this study, we present the results of combined NPS and HI therapy in vitro and in vivo using models of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and breast cancer. We describe the intracellular mechanisms influenced by each treatment, quantify their synergistic effects on regulated cell death, and compare the outcomes of each modality alone versus in combination. Together, our findings support the emerging paradigm that integrating bioelectric and molecular-chaperone-targeted therapies may represent a next-generation approach to solid tumor ablation.

Results

N1-S1 HCC cell viability

We first evaluated the effects of the HSP90 inhibitors AUY-922 and 17-AAG, as well as NPS exposure, on the viability of rat N1-S1 hepatocellular carcinoma cells. AUY-922 decreased cell viability by approximately 35% to 0.3 µM and produced an IC50 of 0.10 ± 0.07 µM (Fig. 1A, D). Similarly, 17-AAG reduced cell viability at 0.3 µM, although higher concentrations produced greater variability, with a final IC50 of 0.11 ± 0.09 µM (Fig. 1B, E). Cells were next exposed to increasing electric field strengths (60ns, 1 Hz, 10 pulses, 0–50 kV/cm) to generate initial NPS field–response curves (Fig. 1C, F). We subsequently employed a single 600 ns pulse to determine the effect of NPS alone or in combination with AUY-922 at 0.3 µM. NPS at 50 kV/cm reduced viability by approximately 25%, while concurrent incubation with AUY-922 produced a much larger reduction of 60 to 80% (Fig. 1G). The additional decrease in viability produced by combining NPS with AUY-922, calculated as a delta (Δ) relative to NPS alone, showed a strong correlation with electric field intensity (R² = 0.98, p = 0.0001) and reached approximately 46.5% at 50 kV/cm (Fig. 1H). When all treatments were compared directly, the combined HI + NPS condition produced a markedly synergistic reduction in viability (Fig. 1J). These preliminary observations indicate that NPS and HSP90 inhibition engage complementary mechanisms that enhance cellular susceptibility to regulated cell death when applied simultaneously.

Caspase 3 activation

HSP90 inhibition disrupts numerous chaperone functions that support cell survival following injury. To determine whether HSP90 inhibition induces regulated cell death through a caspase-dependent mechanism, we performed a dose-response for caspase-3 cleavage after treating N1-S1 cells with AUY-922 at 1 µM or 10 µM for 24 h. Western blot quantification, normalized to beta-actin, showed a two–fold increase in cleaved caspase-3 at 1 µM and a seven–fold increase at 10 µM (Fig. 2A). We next assessed the effects of NPS alone or in combination with AUY-922. NPS exposure (600 ns, 1 Hz, 20 kV/cm) produced a modest increase in caspase-3 activity, similar to the low-dose AUY-922 response. However, combining NPS with AUY-922 resulted in a robust seven–fold induction of cleaved caspase-3 relative to control (Fig. 2B). These findings suggest that NPS triggers regulated cell death primarily through a caspase-independent pathway, whereas HSP90 inhibition activates caspase-dependent apoptosis. The combined treatment shifts the death response toward strong caspase-3 activation, which aligns with the synergistic loss of viability observed in Fig. 1.

Effects on N1S1- HCC tumor growth in Sprague Dawley rats

To validate the in vitro observations, we established an orthotopic N1-S1 tumor model in Sprague Dawley rats. Tumors were measured two weeks after initiation of therapy. The combined HI + NPS treatment produced the most pronounced inhibition of tumor growth, with an approximate reduction of 87%. This effect was substantially greater than either treatment alone, since NPS reduced tumor size by approximately 46% and HI by 22% (Fig. 3A). Representative images were also collected via IVIS, indicating a reduction in tumor size during combined treatment (Fig. 3B).

Caspase 3, 8 and 9 activation in orthotopic N1-S1 HCC in Sprague Dawley rats

Activated caspases are central mediators of apoptotic regulated cell death. We quantified cleaved caspase-3 (17 and 19 kDa), cleaved caspase-8 (18 kDa), and cleaved and total caspase-9 (38 and 17 kDa) in tumor tissue from treated and control rats. NPS (100 pulses of 200 ns, 2 Hz, 30 kV/cm) and AUY-922 (1 mg/kg, three times per week) each produced mild activation of caspase-3. In contrast, combined treatment resulted in a significantly stronger increase in both caspase-3 isoforms (Fig. 4A, B). Significant increases in cleaved caspase 8 and 9 were only observed in the NPS + HI group (Fig. 4C, D).

DNA damage and PARP activation in orthotopic N1-S1 HCC tumors

To assess DNA damage, we quantified phosphorylated histone H2AX, a marker of DNA double-strand breaks and radiosensitivity44,45. Phospho-H2AX levels were highest in tumors treated with combined HI + NPS, exceeding values from HI alone, NPS alone, or control (Fig. 5A). Activation of PARP, a key enzyme in DNA repair and regulated cell death46, was also greatest in the combination treatment group (Fig. 5B).

NPS and HI synergistically decrease orthotopic 4T1-luc mammary tumors in Balb/c mice

To examine whether synergy extends to other tumor types, we evaluated the combined therapy in a 4T1-Luc orthotopic mammary tumor model. Treatment with either NPS (20 to 50 pulses, 200 ns, 2 Hz, 30 kV/cm) or AUY-922 alone reduced tumor growth. However, tumor reduction was greatest in the combined HI + NPS group, with an approximate 80% decrease in tumor volume (Fig. 6).

Caspase and RIPK1 activation in mouse orthotopic mammary tumors (4T1-Luc cells) treated with either HI, NPS or NPS + HI

To further investigate the mechanisms of inhibition of cancer growth induced by NPS and HI therapy, we analyzed caspases 3, 8, 9, 12 and RIPK1 activities in mouse orthotopic mammary tumors. Caspase activity was measured in control mice and mice treated with HI (AUY-922 1 mg/Kg 2x/week i.p.) or NPS + HI (AUY-922 1 mg/kg + 20/50 pulses of 200 ns, 2 Hz, 20 kV/cm). Caspase 3 activity was strongly enhanced by HI, NPS and HI + NPS treatment (20 or 50 pulses) (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7A). Similarly, cleaved caspase 8 was increased in both HI and HI + NPS (50 pulses) (p < 0.05; p < 0.001), with the combined therapy displaying the highest increment (Fig. 7B). No differences, however, were observed in total caspases 9 and 12 levels for all groups (Fig. 7C, D).Receptor interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) is an enzyme involved in cellular pathways related to both cell survival and death47, specifically in apoptosis and necroptosis-mediated cell death pathways48. In order to investigate if the inhibition of tumor growth was caused by necroptosis, we analyzed RIPK1. Mice treated with NPS (20/50 pulses of 200 ns, 2 Hz, 30 kV/cm) or HI (1 mg/Kg AUY-922 2x/week i.p.) alone exhibited unaltered values of RIPK1. However, mice treated with both NPS and HI (NPS + HI), showed decreased RIPK1 levels (Fig. 8).

NPS and HI synergistically increase immune Cytotoxic T and natural killer cell infiltration

Cytotoxic cells of the immune system, such as CD8 + lymphocytes and Natural Killer (NK) cells are involved in the immune response against the orthotopic 4T1-luc tumor49. NPS stimulation has been reported to increase the levels of immune cells infiltrating the malignancy50. We measured Granzyme B serine protease activity to investigate potential synergy between NSP and HI. Whereas Granzyme B activity remained unchanged with NSP or HI alone, the combination of HI (AUY-922 1 mg/Kg, 2 times/week i.p.) and NPS (20 pulses of 200 ns, 2 Hz, 30 kV/cm), exhibited a synergistic increase in Granzyme B levels (Fig. 10).

Discussion

Cancer is the cause of 1/3 of all human deaths (CDC). Malignant cells accumulate multiple genomic errors that results in dysfunction of crucial regulatory pathways, exhibit aberrant interactions with somatic tissues, modify normal metabolic pathways, dysregulate cell cycle progression and promote survival over apoptosis. This process is known as “immortalization” and causes the abnormal growth of pathologic tissue. Anti-cancer treatments are often delivered within multidrug approaches that target different pathways, providing a stronger effect than the sum of individual compounds when administered alone. NanoPulse Stimulation (NPS) disrupts cancer cell homeostasis51, damages DNA52, dissipates the mitochondria membrane potential13,14, provokes regulated cell death (RCD)14,53, and thus provides the potential use in chemoresistant malignancies52,54. Although NPS induces vaccine effects in both the mouse breast50,55 and rat liver56 cancers, it is reasonable, in order to reduce the amplitude of the used electrical field, to test and study possible compounds whose co-administration enhance the effects of NPS. 17-allylamino-17-demethoxygeldanamycin (17-AAG) is the first HSP90 inhibitor that entered clinical trials57. In the recent years, more than 14 new inhibitors have been developed, with improved pharmacodynamic profiles, reduced toxicity and increased efficacy58. AUY-922 is a second-generation resorcinylic isoxazole-based HSP90 inhibitor, with improved tolerability59. In this article, we have investigated both 17-AAG and AUY-922 and their synergistic mechanism of action when associated with NPS, suggesting their use as a combined anti-cancer therapy.

In our in vitro experiments, NPS deployed in single 600 ns pulse with electric fields up to 60 kV/cm to rat N1-S1 HCC cells, reduced viability by only 25% (Fig. 1A). However, when HI and NPS were administered together and additional 47% reduction was observed leading to ~ 20% survival (Fig. 1B, C). HI treatment activated in a dose- and time-dependent manner the cleavage of caspase 3 (Fig. 2A). HSP90 negatively regulates two distinct apoptotic pathways: it inactivates apoptotic protease activating factor-1 (APAF-1), which is necessary for caspase 9 cleavage, and supports AKT activation -resulting in Bcl-2 Associated Death (BAD) phosphorylation- which, after binding 14-3-3 is not capable of inducing the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria and achieve apoptosis60. Thus, by inhibiting these anti-apoptotic mechanisms, HSP90 inhibition promotes caspase activation and provides a new signaling landscape to synergize with NPS, which works through a caspase independent mechanism.

Combination treatment, in fact, cleaved caspase 3 (Fig. 2B) and resulted in a synergistic reduction of orthotopic N1-S1 HCC tumor growth in (Fig. 3).

Similarly, 4T1-Luc cells syngeneic mammary tumor in Balb/c mice showed a significant increase of caspase 3 and caspase 8 cleavage when treated with NPS and HI combined (Fig. 7a, b), which resulted in a significant reduction of tumor growth size (Fig. 6). This data suggests that while HI induce RCD primarily via caspase dependent pathways, NPS provokes regulated cell death by caspase-independent mechanisms. The activation of one or the other pathways could be related to an intrinsic cell-type sensitivity or voltage dependent activation as HCC cells didn’t show any NPS-mediated caspase activation while breast cancer cells did. However, it is possible, that NPS could activate caspases in a mitochondrial-independent manner61. Additionally, it is important to note that at least part of the increased levels of caspases observed in vivo in this study, could derive from apoptotic immunosuppressor cells like T Regulatory cells, that are recruited by the release of cytokines, infiltrate the tumor and participate in mechanisms of immune surveillance62.

Furthermore, the reduction in cell viability and tumor size of the malignant cells were not caused by a necroptosis-mediated cell death pathway, as indicated by the lower activation of RIPK1 observed in NPS + HI group (Fig. 8). In fact, the increased phosphorylation of H2AX histone indicates cell death through DNA-damage mediated apoptotic pathways induced by a synergistic action of HI + NPS treatment (Fig. 9). Inhibition of HSP90 sensitizes tumor cells to NPS, as previously shown for radiation63, thus disrupting DNA repair mechanisms and cell cycle checkpoint activation.

Our results suggest that HI reduces cell viability when administered alone and in combination, probably by blocking the HSP90 inhibitory action on APAF-1 and the subsequent activation of RCD pathway. Also, since AKT induces proliferation by promoting the progression through the cell cycle acting on G1/S and G2/M transitions, HI treatment could also act by stopping malignant cells cycle by blocking HSP90-AKT interaction. Our data suggest that HI induces cell RCD by mitochondria-mediated mechanisms that lead to caspase activation while NPS could stimulate RCD through different pathways, which however, when employed in combination with HI, are directed to DNA damage, PARP activation and the caspase-dependent apoptotic pathway.

NPS treatment can influence the level of immune system involvement providing, in some cases, immunity and memory cells against treated cancer cells64. Here, NPS + HI was associated with cytotoxic and NK cell infiltration, as reflected in higher levels of Granzyme B serine protease activity, than those in NPS or HI groups (Fig. 10), which potentially can induce PARP cleavage and caspase dependent apoptotic pathways. We acknowledge several limitations of the current study. First, the sample sizes (n = 3–5 per group) are modest, though sufficient to detect the large effect sizes observed. Second, while our data are consistent with apoptotic cell death, formal exclusion of necroptosis would require additional assays such as MLKL phosphorylation and pharmacological rescue with necrostatin-1. Similarly, the proposed caspase-independent contribution of NPS to cell death should be validated with pan-caspase inhibitors (e.g., Z-VAD-FMK) in future studies. Third, Granzyme B activity was assessed only in the 20-pulse condition; dose-dependency of immune responses should be characterized. Fourth, normal tissue toxicity was not formally assessed, although no significant weight loss was observed in any treatment group, and the doses of both AUY-922 and NPS used here are well below thresholds associated with toxicity in published studies. Finally, the differential caspase activation patterns between the HCC and breast cancer models likely reflect intrinsic differences in apoptotic pathway dependencies and highlight the need for model-specific mechanistic studies.

Conclusions

NanoPulse Stimulation (NPS) and HSP90 Inhibitors (HI) therapy synergistically impeded tumor growth in both an orthotopic HCC cancer model and an orthotopic 4T1-Luc mammary tumor model, better than when HI or NPS are administered alone. This combined treatment alters malignant cells homeostasis at different levels, inducing a strong DNA-damage, a caspase-3 dependent and a caspase-independent mediated pro-apoptotic effect. The combination of HSP90 Inhibitors and NanoPulse Stimulation is a new therapeutic approach for cancer characterized by a synergistic effect obtained using lower doses of either HI or NPS.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Mouse 4T1-luc mammary cells were obtained from Dr. Gary Sahagian, Tufts University); rat N1-S1 liver hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (CRL-1604) 4T1-luc cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM ((American Type Culture Laboratory (ATCC®) 30–2002™ Rockville, MD) -supplemented 10% FBS, NEAA and 100 units/ml penicillin, and 100 µg/ml streptomycin. Cells with passage number 9–30 were used. The N1-S1 HCC cell line (ATCC) was cultured in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Media (IMDM) Modified Media containing 4 mM Glutamine, [4(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid] (HEPES) and 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS).

Animals tumor models

All animals’ studies were approved by the Old Dominion University Institute for Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). All animals’ studies and methodologies were in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Orthotopic rat N1-S1 HCCs were grown in the livers of male Sprague Dawley rats (250 g; Harlan (Frederick, MD or Dublin, VA) and orthotopic 4T1-luc mammary cancer cells were grown in the mammary fat pads of female Balb/c mice (8–10 weeks of age; (Jackson Laboratory or Harlan Laboratories). Animals were housed and maintained in a pathogen-free ODU AAALAC approved animal facility. Animals received care according to the Guide for the Care and Uses of Laboratory Animals. All animal studies were approved by the Old Dominion University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. For tumor initiation in the orthotopic liver model, the median liver lobe was exposed through an incision in the abdomen, and 1 × 106 N1-S1 HCC cells in 50 µL saline were injected under the liver capsule as previously described56. For tumor initiation in the orthotopic breast cancer model, 1 × 106 4T1-luc cells in 50 µL Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline with calcium and magnesium (DPBS) were injected into the posterior part of the mammary fat pad as previously described55. The size of primary tumor was assessed by calipers and the Xenogen IVIS Spectrum system (Caliper Life Sciences). Mice were weighed before and after treatment and twice weekly. At the end of experimental procedures mice were euthanized according to the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA), via lethal injection of Pentobarbital (200 mg/kg. i.p.) followed by cervical dislocation.

Tumor measurement

Tumor sizes were measure with the caliper technique for both orthotopic models of rat HCC and mouse mammary cancers. During these studies rat HCC tumors appeared as single nodules that grew as solid structures along the liver surface as previously observed by ultrasound65 without observable intrahepatic or peritoneal wall metastasis. Mouse mammary tumor grew in mouse fat pads. For both HCC and mammary tumors volumes were determined by the formula volume = (shortest diameter)2 × (longest diameter) × 0.5.

In vitro and in vivo NPS delivery

In vitro studies the NPS device provided either 600 ns or 60 ns pulse durations with fast (15 and 5 ns, respectively) rise fall times14,18. About 100 µl of cells (1 × 106 / ml) were added to cover the electrodes in 0.1 mm cuvettes and cells were exposed to NPS with different electric fields and/or pulse numbers.

In vivo studies: before animals were treated with NPS, hair was thoroughly removed by shaving with electric clippers and followed by using brief Nair treatment. Electrodes were thoroughly covered with K-Y jelly to prevent air introduction of gel between electrodes, which would cause electrical breakdown and absence of delivery of electric fields to tumor and the possibility of burning tissues. For N1-S1 tumor treatment, seven days after tumor initiation, the tumor-bearing liver lobe was exposed again, and tumors were either sham-treated (5 needle array electrodes inserted, not pulsed) or treated with NPS. The NPS parameters included 100 ns pulse duration with fast rise-fall times (5–10 ns), 50 kV/cm electric fields and a pulse number varying from 50 to 200 pulses depending on experimental designs.

For 4T1-luc NPS treatment was initiated 9–11 days after tumor inoculation when average tumor size was 60–100 mm3. NPS delivery ensured that only tumor masses were treated. NPS was delivered to the tumor tissue using a two-plate pinch electrode with a 6- or 8-mm diameter. The NPS parameters were 100 ns pulse duration, fast 5 ns rise-fall, 50 kV/cm electric fields (with a range of 46–54 kV/cm) and pulse number from 50 to 200 (2 Hz pulse frequency) depending on experimental designs.

HSP90 inhibitors and NPS tumor treatment

Tumors were initiated by injection of cells as described above and 7 to 11 days later animals were treated. For groups with combined NPS and HI treatment, NPS was delivered first at 7–10 days post-tumor implantation, followed by HI treatment (1 mg/kg i.p., 2×/week) for 2 weeks. Heat Shock Protein Inhibitors (HI) AUY-922 (NVP-AUY922, 99.1% purity) was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA) and 17-AAG was obtained from the National Cancer Institute (Bethesda, MD, USA). HI were resuspended in 10% DMSO saline solution and injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) at 1 mg/kg, 2 times per week. For groups with combined HI and NPS treatments, mice received NPS treatment at 7–10 days of post-tumor implantation, followed by HI treatment for 2 weeks at 1 mg/kg i.p., 2X/week. At the end of treatments, mice were euthanized and tumor sizes determined.

In vitro cell viability

Cell viability in vitro was determined 24 h after treatment with NPS, HI, and NPS + HI according to the manufacturer’s protocol in the CellTiter-Glo luminescent assay. The luminescent signals for ATP levels were directly proportional to viable cell numbers. Luminescence was analyzed in luminometer (Gemini XPS, Molecular Devices, CA).

Western blot analysis

Protein homogenates were extracted from either cultured cells or cancer tissue by sonication (50% amplitude, 3 times for 10 s) in ice-cold RIPA buffer with added protease inhibitor cocktail (100:1). The protein lysates were gently mixed under agitation for 3 h at 4 ◦C, and then centrifuged twice at 14,000xG g for 10 min. The supernatants were gathered, and total protein concentration was determined using the micro BCA assay. Equal amounts of proteins from all lysates were used for Western blot analysis. The samples were first mixed with Tricine Sample Buffer 1:1, boiled for 5 min and then separated on a 10–12% polyacrylamide SDS gel by electrophoresis. Separated proteins were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, incubated with the appropriate primary antibody, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody and detected by digital fluorescence imaging (LI-COR Odyssey CLx, Dallas, TX, USA). Beta-actin was used as loading control. ImageJ software v.1.8.0 was used to perform densitometric quantification of the bands from the Western blot membranes (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/; accessed on 27 May 2021; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) as published66–69. Cropped western blot images are provided in the supplementary materials for all in vitro and in vivo experiments across both animal models. Unfortunately, full-length blots were not saved at the time of the original experiments and therefore cannot be provided.
