A domain-agnostic explainable framework for network attack detection across diverse traffic datasets
Authors: Abed Alanazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe increasing complexity, scale, and diversity of cyber threats pose significant challenges to modern network security systems, particularly in heterogeneous network environments. Existing intrusion detection methods often rely on dataset-specific models that struggle to maintain consistent performance across different data distributions and typically lack interpretability, limiting their applicability in critical systems. In this paper, we present an explainable deep learning framework evaluated across multiple heterogeneous cyber attack datasets, including Kitsune (IoT traffic), Server-Based network data (enterprise logs), and Malware Traffic datasets. The proposed pipeline employs a consistent preprocessing strategy involving feature normalisation, encoding, and label alignment, followed by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) classifier with dropout regularisation. Model training incorporates cosine learning-rate scheduling and early stopping, while explainability is achieved via SHAP-based global feature attribution and LIME-based instance-level explanation. Performance is evaluated using accuracy, ROC-AUC, and F1-score metrics. The model achieves 96.0% accuracy on the Kitsune dataset, 99.99% on the Server-Based dataset, and 99.58% on the Malware dataset, with ROC-AUC values approaching 1.00 across most classes. Introduction

The rapid expansion of digital connectivity has significantly increased the potential attack surface for cyber threats. From enterprise infrastructures and industrial control systems to IoT-enabled consumer devices, modern networks are continuously exposed to sophisticated attacks such as distributed denial-of-service (DDoS), ransomware, phishing, and advanced persistent threats (APTs)1,2. Traditional intrusion detection systems (IDS), which rely on handcrafted rules or shallow machine learning techniques, often struggle to adapt to evolving attack patterns and fail to maintain consistent performance across diverse datasets and environments3. To overcome these limitations, recent research has increasingly focused on deep learning (DL) models capable of capturing complex, nonlinear relationships in network traffic data.

This challenge becomes more critical in emerging cyber-physical systems such as unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) and unmanned surface vehicles (USVs). These systems are rapidly being deployed in applications including surveillance, logistics, disaster management, and tactical operations. However, their reliance on wireless communication, GPS signals, and distributed control architectures makes them particularly vulnerable to attacks such as spoofing, jamming, and denial-of-service attacks4–7. Due to their lightweight design and limited onboard computational resources, UAV systems represent a compelling future application domain for intrusion detection solutions that are accurate, efficient, and interpretable, though UAV-specific validation is beyond the scope of the present work8,9.

In the broader IDS research landscape, several benchmark datasets have been widely adopted to evaluate detection performance. Notable among these are the Kitsune dataset, which focuses on IoT-based traffic patterns; Malware Traffic datasets, which capture malicious communication behaviours; and server-based enterprise datasets, which represent structured network logs. These datasets cover a wide spectrum of attack scenarios, ranging from lightweight IoT anomalies to high-volume enterprise intrusions10–12. Despite their importance, most existing studies evaluate models on individual datasets in isolation, resulting in highly specialised models that lack adaptability across heterogeneous environments.

Several recent studies have advanced network traffic anomaly detection using paradigms that differ fundamentally from the supervised classification framework proposed in this paper and therefore warrant careful situating. Work introduced a series of methods targeting encrypted traffic: ConMD13 employs contextual masking knowledge distillation with local-global attention to overcome the identical shortcut problem in reconstruction-based detection; FreeUp14 identifies a spectral mismatch between the full-frequency nature of encrypted flows and the low-frequency bias of reconstruction models, addressing it through a frequency-decoupled detection framework; UnDiff15 replaces intra-sample reconstruction error with inter-sample uncertainty deviation via evidential learning, improving robustness to anomaly drift; MFR16 adopts a semi-supervised setting training exclusively on normal traffic images and exploits multi-scale frequency components alongside channel-spatial attention to detect anomalies without any labeled attack samples; and a payload-level framework17 combines a reconstruction-based feature extractor with contrastive learning on raw packet payloads to reduce information redundancy. While these contributions represent significant progress, all five methods operate under unsupervised, semi-supervised, or zero-positive paradigms and are evaluated on encrypted-traffic datasets (e.g., ISCX-VPN, USTC-TFC2016) that expose raw payload byte streams absent from the flow-level statistical features of the Kitsune, Server-Based, and Malware datasets used in this work; furthermore, none incorporates post-hoc interpretability. In contrast, the proposed framework addresses the underexplored problem of consistent, interpretable, supervised multi-class classification across heterogeneous labelled network environments, providing SHAP and LIME attribution, which are absent from all five referenced methods.

Another key limitation in current IDS research is the lack of interpretability. While deep learning models have demonstrated strong performance, they often function as black-box systems, providing little insight into how predictions are made. This lack of transparency limits their adoption in critical applications such as defence systems, autonomous platforms, and industrial networks, where understanding the reasoning behind detection decisions is essential for trust and accountability. To address these challenges, this work proposes an explainable deep learning framework and evaluates it across multiple heterogeneous datasets. The proposed approach employs a consistent preprocessing pipeline, including feature normalisation, encoding, and alignment, followed by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) classifier with dropout regularisation. The framework integrates SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to provide feature-level interpretability, enabling transparent analysis of model predictions. By applying the same modelling pipeline across different datasets, the study aims to demonstrate consistent performance across varied network environments (Fig. 1).

Motivation

Despite the rapid advancement of intrusion detection techniques, several critical challenges remain unresolved. First, most existing models are developed and validated on a single dataset, which limits their ability to maintain consistent performance across datasets with differing feature distributions, attack patterns, and traffic characteristics18,19. This lack of stability becomes a significant limitation in real-world deployments where network conditions are highly dynamic and heterogeneous. Second, although deep learning-based IDS models achieve high detection accuracy, they often lack interpretability. The inability to explain model decisions makes it difficult for security analysts to validate alerts, investigate anomalies, and trust automated systems. This issue is particularly critical in environments such as UAV networks, where incorrect or unexplained decisions can have serious operational consequences. These challenges highlight the need for an intrusion detection framework that balances both stable performance across heterogeneous datasets and transparent, interpretable decision-making. Motivated by this need, the present work focuses on developing a consistent and explainable modelling pipeline that can be applied across multiple datasets while providing meaningful insights into model behaviour.

Related work

Authors in10 conducted a comprehensive survey on software-defined networks (SDN) intrusion detection, analysing anomaly detection techniques from 2015 to 2023. They identified deep learning as a dominant paradigm but noted challenges in real-time adaptation and multi-source fusion due to the rigid assumptions of their datasets. Their review highlights the need for scalable architectures capable of integrating heterogeneous traffic formats. Authors in11 reviewed explainable deep learning approaches for detecting advanced persistent threats (APTs), emphasising methods like LSTM, CNN, and transformers augmented with SHAP or LIME. The paper outlines performance metrics reporting more than 95% detection accuracy across benchmark datasets, but notes limited evaluation of real-time inference. They also highlight that many methods neglect cross-domain stability, reducing their generalizability. The research12 proposed a multi-view intrusion detection framework that combines deep learning with knowledge graphs for contextual traffic modelling. Validated on CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15, it achieved over 98% accuracy, demonstrating the benefit of multimodal representations. However, the system fails to scale to dynamically evolving traffic profiles, such as IoT streams or server logs.

Authors in20 introduced a hybrid ensemble IDS using bagging and boosting models, bolstered by SHAP-based feature selection for significant predictors. Tested on NSL-KDD and CICIDS2017, they reported accuracy over 99%, showcasing SHAP’s utility in improving feature quality. Nonetheless, their ensemble remained confined to offline experiments with limited support for online deployment. Authors in21 leveraged oversampling, PCA, and stacking classifiers to tackle big, imbalanced network data in a Journal of Big Data publication. The approach yielded a 99.6% F1-score on multiple datasets, suggesting stable handling of class imbalance. However, this strategy did not incorporate any explainability methods, leaving the model’s decisions opaque. Authors in22 introduced a federated, XAI-enhanced approach to IDS, enabling on-device local training and explanation aggregation, and reporting 94% accuracy across participating nodes. Although this supports privacy preservation, their architecture fails to address dataset heterogeneity and feature alignment. The lack of cross-dataset embedding or attention modules reduces the framework’s adaptability and uniform performance across varied data sources. Authors in23 presented a hybrid CNN-LSTM model for detecting DDoS attacks within healthcare IoT networks, achieving over 97% accuracy and a 0.95 F1-score. Their work demonstrates efficacy in constrained IoT environments but is narrowly focused on healthcare-related traffic. The solution doesn’t translate well to malware payloads or enterprise logs and lacks post hoc interpretability with tools like SHAP, thereby limiting its transparency. Authors in24 explored attention-based deep learning for fraud detection in imbalanced transaction logs, achieving promising improvements in recall and AUC. Although encouraging, their application remains limited to financial fraud, not to network intrusion detection.

Authors in25 proposed a federated XAI-IDS that uses SHAP to explain local model decisions and to share aggregated models, achieving 94% detection accuracy with low variance across clients. Their method advances privacy-aware IDS but does not harmonise features across heterogeneous data streams. Additionally, it lacks an attention-based architecture, limiting its ability to learn critical patterns in multi-format traffic. Authors in26 provided a systematic overview of XAI integrations in IDS, highlighting architectural design flaws such as monolithic networks and feature staleness. Their taxonomy emphasises that few models offer both interpretability and rapid inference. However, they focus on theoretical evaluations rather than practical model design, leaving a gap in how to implement cross-domain, explainable models. Authors in27 designed a DDoS mitigation framework for SDN using an ensemble-optimised deep learning model, achieving 99.8% accuracy. Although effective for SDN, the system was neither tested on malware-borne attacks nor IoT threats. Authors in28 offered a survey on ML and transfer learning strategies for IDS in 5G networks, emphasising the need for SHAP-enabled transparency. Their review cites accuracy improvements of over 98% with transfer learning, yet notes that few real-world deployments have utilised transfer learning for multi-domain integration, keeping those models in simulation.

Authors in29introduced a stacked ensemble approach for anomaly detection across CICIDS2017 and NSL-KDD, showing consistent detection improvements ( 8.5%). They highlighted model stability, but their work does not incorporate real-time analytics or model interpretability. The static nature of the ensemble limits adaptability in evolving cyber scenarios. Authors in30 surveyed deep reinforcement learning approaches in network intrusion detection, specifically addressing adaptive behaviour modelling. Authors in31 reviewed GAN-based data augmentation for IDS, particularly in small-sample domains such as CICIDS, achieving improved minority-class detection but often suffering from model overfitting and poor interpretability due to a lack of post hoc explanations such as SHAP. The reviewed GAN architectures also lacked benchmarking on diverse network traffic sources. Authors in32 evaluated XAI methods, including SHAP, for alert-based classification using deep models, finding that explainability significantly improved analyst trust but required more effective visualisation strategies. However, their effort was limited to alert classification, rather than full-flow intrusion detection. Authors in33 enhanced LSTM-based DDoS detection in SDN environments using SHAP, achieving 98% F1-score with per-flow pattern annotation. While notable, their design was narrowly focused on DDoS scenarios and didn’t generalise to other attack types or IoT data. Authors in34 introduced a PCA-based feature reduction method combined with decision trees for intrusion classification, achieving 96.2% accuracy on synthetic datasets. While effective in dimensionality reduction, this approach suffers from scalability issues when applied to raw packet captures or multi-format logs.

Recent studies have addressed the growing cybersecurity risks in UAV networks35 proposed a DL-based IDS for FANETs to detect spoofing and jamming attacks, but lacked interpretability. Similarly36, developed a lightweight CNN-GRU model for UAVs, but it was tested on synthetic data, limiting its real-world applicability. These efforts highlight the need for stable and explainable IDS solutions that generalise well across both ground and UAV network environments. Authors in recent studies have also explored hybrid and optimised machine learning approaches for intrusion detection, with a particular focus on DDoS attack scenarios. For instance37, conducted a comparative analysis of machine learning algorithms for TCP SYN flood detection, demonstrating the effectiveness of classical models under controlled settings. Building upon this38, proposed a dynamic BPN-MLP neural network integrated with a hybrid swarm intelligence framework, achieving improved adaptability in evolving attack conditions. Furthermore39, introduced a support vector machine (SVM) enhanced with hybrid optimisation techniques for DDoS detection, demonstrating strong performance in accuracy and stability. More recently40, developed HybGBS, a hybrid neural network combined with Grey Wolf Optimisation, which demonstrated effective intrusion detection in cloud computing environments. Additionally41, explored recurrent and deep learning models for DDoS detection, highlighting the potential of deep architectures in capturing temporal attack patterns (Table 1).

Proposed methodology

Figure 2 presents a comprehensive overview of the proposed multi-dataset intrusion detection framework, illustrating the complete pipeline from data acquisition to evaluation and interpretability. As shown in the updated diagram, the framework is organised into six sequential phases: (1) data collection and domain labelling, (2) preprocessing, (3) Fully Connected Neural Network (FCNN) with Dropout Regularisation classifier architecture, (4) training engine, (5) explainability, and (6) multi-dataset evaluation. The pipeline begins with three heterogeneous datasets: Kitsune (IoT network traffic with multiple attack classes), Server-Based data (enterprise network logs), and Malware Traffic datasets (malicious communication patterns). Each dataset is processed independently to preserve domain-specific characteristics. In Phase 2, preprocessing includes missing-value imputation (mean for numerical features and mode for categorical features), Standard scaling, encoding (one-hot and label encoding), and class imbalance handling through stratified sampling and class-weighted loss. This ensures consistent and well-conditioned input representations for model training. Phase 3 introduces the classification model, which consists of a fully connected neural network with three hidden layers, Mish activation functions, and dropout regularisation. An attention mechanism is applied to the final hidden representation to emphasise salient latent features before classification. The output layer uses a softmax activation function with categorical cross-entropy loss for multi-class prediction. In Phase 4, the training process employs the AdamW optimiser, cosine-annealed learning-rate scheduling, and early stopping based on validation loss, with performance monitored via TensorBoard. Phase 5 integrates SHAP-based explainability within the analysis pipeline, providing feature-level attribution for individual predictions and enabling transparent interpretation of model decisions. Finally, Phase 6 evaluates the model independently on each dataset using metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC. This structured pipeline demonstrates the effectiveness of a consistent modelling framework applied across heterogeneous network datasets.

It is structured into six logical phases, each corresponding to a critical stage in the system’s development and deployment.

The first phase focuses on data acquisition and harmonisation, wherein the Kitsune, Server-based, and Malware datasets are loaded. The second phase handles data preprocessing. Missing values are imputed using the mean or mode strategy, depending on the data type, and numerical values are scaled to the [0,1] range using Standard scaling. Feature encoding transforms any categorical fields into binary vectors. In the third phase, a deep neural model is constructed using three fully connected layers of decreasing size (128, 64, 32 neurons), each followed by a dropout layer (with a dropout rate of 0.3) to prevent overfitting. An attention mechanism is placed after the final dropout to enhance focus on high-impact features. The model weights are initialised using Xavier initialisation to ensure balanced variance across layers. The fourth phase begins training using the AdamW optimiser with a learning rate of 0.001. Mini-batches of size 128 are fed through the model iteratively across multiple epochs, with early stopping based on validation loss to prevent overtraining. Phase five introduces explainability into the pipeline by computing SHAP values on validation samples, highlighting the most influential features for each prediction. This step improves user trust and regulatory transparency, especially for sensitive cybersecurity deployments. Finally, the model is evaluated on all three datasets independently, and metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score are reported. The model achieved outstanding accuracy on all domains, confirming its ability to generalise and identify threats in varied environments. The algorithm concludes by saving the trained model and generating interpretability reports for downstream use.

Data collection and preprocessing

In this study, we utilise three diverse and publicly available network traffic datasets to build a stable and globally applicable intrusion detection framework: the Kitsune dataset42, the Malware Capture Facility dataset43, and a server-based enterprise dataset44. These datasets were carefully selected to represent various network environments, including IoT, malware communication, and enterprise-level traffic, ensuring comprehensive coverage of contemporary cyberattack vectors. The Kitsune dataset is a large-scale IoT traffic dataset generated using a network gateway equipped with the Kitsune FlowMeter. The original dataset contains over 20 million samples with 115 features and 9 classes (8 attack types and 1 benign class), including ARP spoofing, Mirai botnet traffic, and denial-of-service (DoS) attacks. Due to its massive size, a representative subset of 9,000 samples was selected for training and evaluation to ensure class balance and computational efficiency while preserving the diversity of attack patterns. The Malware Capture Facility dataset contains network traffic traces generated by executing malicious binaries in sandboxed environments. The full dataset comprises over 25 million samples with 23 features and 5 classes, capturing behaviours such as command-and-control (C&C) communication, ransomware activity, and other malware-driven interactions. For this study, a subset of 644,602 samples was used to enable efficient training while maintaining representative coverage of malware behaviours. The server-based enterprise dataset reflects real-world enterprise network traffic, including both benign operations and attack scenarios such as HTTP flooding, SSH brute-force, and SQL injection. This dataset contains 225,398 samples with 27 features and 3 classes and was used in its entirety due to its moderate size and structured nature (Table 2).

The preprocessing phase was critical for transforming the raw, heterogeneous datasets into a consistent, learnable format for the global intrusion detection model. First, we addressed missing values by applying a targeted imputation strategy. For numerical features, such as packet size or duration, missing entries were replaced using the mean of the respective columns to preserve statistical consistency. For categorical features, such as protocol type or service flags, the mode was used to fill missing values, ensuring the most representative category was retained. After handling null values, the datasets underwent feature normalisation. All numerical attributes were scaled using Standard scaling, mapping values to the [0,1] range to improve convergence during training. Categorical variables required encoding to make them compatible with the deep learning model. For nominal (non-ordinal) categories, such as connection protocol and service type, we applied one-hot encoding, which converted these features into sparse binary vectors, thereby preventing implicit ranking among categories. On the other hand, binary and ordinal features, such as flag status or attack indicators, were encoded using label encoding, assigning a unique integer to each category. These preprocessing steps collectively ensured that the final dataset maintained structural consistency across all three domains, IoT, malware, and server traffic, and was ready for domain-agnostic framework training.

Experimental analysis and results

The proposed global attack classification model, as demonstrated in Table 3, exhibits strong and consistent performance across all three datasets, validating its stability and generalisation capabilities in diverse network environments. On the Kitsune dataset, which primarily targets IoT-based traffic, the model achieved an overall accuracy of 96%, with particularly high performance in detecting Classes 0, 5, 6, and 8 (F1-scores \ge 0.98). Minor performance drops were observed in Classes 2 and 4, indicating slightly reduced sensitivity for specific anomaly types, which may be attributed to subtle attack signatures or limited training samples. In contrast, the Server-Based dataset, which includes large-scale enterprise-level traffic, achieved a near-perfect classification accuracy of 100% across all three classes, reflecting the model’s ability to capture well-defined patterns in structured network logs. Similarly, the Malware Traffic dataset yielded an overall accuracy of 99.9%, with all classes exhibiting precision, recall, and F1-scores close to or equal to 1.00, demonstrating the model’s effectiveness in identifying diverse forms of malware-injected traffic. These results confirm that the proposed pipeline generalises well across different domains, ranging from lightweight IoT streams to high-throughput enterprise and malware-rich networks, without requiring retraining or architectural modifications.

The SHAP analysis is conducted on a fixed 100-sample slice of the validation set (X_val_sample = X_val_np[:100]), drawn from a deterministic 70/30 stratified split (random_state=42). Because the same trained checkpoint and the same validation partition are used for all explanations, the feature attribution rankings are fully reproducible across runs with zero variance. Model-level stability is enforced by dropout regularisation (p=0.3) and early stopping on validation loss, which prevents the learned representations from being sensitive to training-run variation and consequently ensures consistent SHAP rankings. On the Server-Based and Malware datasets, near-perfect classification accuracy further confirms stable decision boundaries, leaving no room for fluctuations in feature ranking. For the Kitsune dataset, the consistent rankings across nine attack classes reflect robust model convergence despite greater inter-class feature overlap.

Regarding computational scope, KernelSHAP is applied with a background of 100 validation samples via shap. Explainer, executing the full attribution in a single batched call with a CPU-side prediction wrapper. LIME explanations are generated for one representative validation sample per dataset (idx=0, num_features=10), providing instance-level local attribution complementary to the global SHAP summary. Both methods are deliberately scoped to offline forensic analysis and post-incident investigation, consistent with the interpretability goals of the proposed framework, rather than real-time per-packet attribution.

Discussion and comparison

The empirical results obtained from Kitsune, Server-Based, and Malware datasets demonstrate the versatility and effectiveness of the proposed global attack classification model. The model achieves consistently high accuracy (96%-100%) across these diverse domains without requiring dataset-specific architectural tuning. Its stability is further validated by the smooth convergence of training and validation, low loss values, and strong generalisation performance across classes with both balanced and imbalanced distributions. Unlike prior works that often rely on ensemble models or handcrafted features for specific datasets, our model adopts a unified deep learning framework with SHAP-based interpretability. This not only provides transparency into the model’s predictions but also facilitates easier trust and verification in high-stakes environments, such as critical infrastructure. The SHAP analysis showed that the model learns contextually relevant features from each dataset, highlighting its adaptive nature (Fig. 9).

The growing use of UAVs in surveillance, logistics, and civilian infrastructure introduces new cybersecurity challenges due to their reliance on wireless communications, GPS, and edge-based control systems. While the proposed framework is evaluated on general network datasets (IoT, enterprise, and malware traffic), it demonstrates consistent performance across heterogeneous data sources. This suggests that the underlying modelling pipeline may be adaptable to other networked environments with similar characteristics. However, it is important to note that UAV-specific data, such as telemetry signals, RF communication patterns, or flight logs, are not included in the current study. Therefore, the applicability of the proposed framework to UAV intrusion detection is not directly validated in this work. Instead, UAV systems are considered a potential area of application for future research. In future work, the model can be extended by incorporating UAV-specific features such as flight telemetry, GPS variability, and communication signal patterns. Additionally, explainability methods such as SHAP may provide useful insights into model decisions in such settings, supporting interpretability and aiding in security analysis. This extension will require dedicated datasets and domain-specific validation to confirm effectiveness in real UAV environments.

Conclusion and future scope

This study presented an interpretable and robust deep learning framework for network intrusion detection evaluated across three diverse datasets: Kitsune (IoT), Server-Based (enterprise logs), and Malware Traffic. By employing a unified preprocessing pipeline and a common MLP-based architecture with dropout regularisation, the proposed approach demonstrates strong and stable performance across heterogeneous datasets without requiring dataset-specific customisation. The inclusion of SHAP-based explainability enhances the model’s transparency by providing insights into feature contributions and class-level behaviour across different data domains. Rather than demonstrating cross-dataset transfer learning, the proposed framework highlights the effectiveness of a consistent modelling pipeline when applied independently to multiple network environments. The results indicate that the approach can maintain reliable performance across datasets with varying characteristics, while also achieving efficient training and convergence. It is important to note that the current study is limited to general network intrusion datasets and does not include UAV-specific data such as telemetry, RF signals, or flight logs. Therefore, claims regarding UAV intrusion defence are not directly validated in this work. Instead, UAV systems are considered a potential area of application for future research. In future work, the framework can be extended by incorporating UAV-specific datasets and domain-relevant features such as flight telemetry, GPS variability, and communication signal patterns. Additionally, ablation studies, cross-dataset transfer experiments, and comparisons with classical machine learning baselines will be conducted to strengthen the model’s stability and general applicability in real-world scenarios.
