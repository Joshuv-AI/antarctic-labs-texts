Aztreonam intervention recalibrates microbial interactions between Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Streptococcus salivarius
Authors: Shiyi Gou, Mengna Feng, Yi Wu, Yang Yuan, Xin Hu, Xiting Yang, Yiwen Chu, Ting Huang, Kelei Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolymicrobial infections are recognized as a direct cause of exacerbation in chronic respiratory infections. In patients with reduced lung function, the transfer of oral microbiota to the lungs is often higher than in healthy individuals. However, the mechanisms underlying the interaction between dominant pathogens and oral microbiota, as well as their relationship with antibiotics, remain poorly understood. Using Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Streptococcus salivarius, co-isolated from the bronchial fluid of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patients, as a model, we investigated their interspecies invasion dynamics under antibiotic intervention. This study employed a combination of phenotypic and transcriptomic analyses to elucidate interaction dynamics under antibiotic intervention, providing insight into the gene expression and virulence impacts of antibiotics during co-infection.ResultsOur findings revealed that P. aeruginosa significantly inhibited the growth of S. salivarius on blank agar plates. However, in the presence of the β-lactam antibiotic aztreonam, at a sub-inhibitory concentration, the quorum sensing (QS) system of P. aeruginosa was suppressed, and genes related to growth and metabolism were downregulated. Conversely, the intervention of aztreonam activated S. salivarius in co-culture, enabling its successful invasion of P. aeruginosa. Although S. salivarius exhibited inhibitory activity against P. aeruginosa, antibiotic intervention played a critical role in reversing their inherent competitive relationship.ConclusionThis study suggests that the repeated use of antibiotics may contribute to the persistent coexistence of polymicrobial communities within the host, leading to complex microbial dynamics that impact disease progression. It underscores the critical need to explore bacterial interactions in detail, as understanding these relationships could revolutionize our approach to chronic diseases caused by polymicrobial infections, offering new therapeutic strategies that go beyond traditional pathogen-targeted treatments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04303-x.Keywords: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Streptococcus salivarius, Aztreonam, Interspecific interaction

Background

Microbial interactions form the foundation for the flow of energy and matter in all ecosystems [1]. Most microbial infections arise from the interactions of polymicrobial or mixed-species communities, including those observed in chronic wound infections, otitis media, dental caries, and cystic fibrosis (CF). Polymicrobial infections can lead to increased virulence and antibiotic resistance, with virulence primarily manifesting as enhanced pathogen persistence at the site of infection and increased disease severity [2, 3]. In chronic respiratory infections that are refractory to treatment, established communities of multiple microbes are closely related to host immunity, antibiotic susceptibility, and bacterial interactions [4]. Given that bacteria with lower abundance at the site of infection can significantly influence the physiology and behavior of dominant pathogens [2, 5], understanding the interactions of co-infecting pathogens is crucial for antibiotic-based treatment of chronic infections.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa, an opportunistic pathogen, colonizes chronic pulmonary lesions and undergoes a series of adaptive mutations [6–8]. Some patients may be chronically colonized by more than one strain of P. aeruginosa [8]. Notably, loss-of-function mutations in the quorum-sensing (QS) regulator lasR are frequently observed in isolates from chronic pulmonary infections and CF patients [7], where these strains coexist stably. In late-stage P. aeruginosa infections, the prolonged infection and repeated cycles of antibiotic treatment led to the emergence of multidrug-resistant strains, complicating treatment further. Numerous studies have explored the mechanisms underlying dual and polymicrobial infections in chronic pulmonary pathogens [9–12], with most focusing on the interactions between dominant pathogens such as P. aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus aureus. Recent findings highlight the integral role of oral microbiota as core residents of the lung microbiome in patients with reduced lung function [13], The oral cavity, directly connected to the upper respiratory tract, contributes significantly to the lung microbiome, and the proportion of oral microbiota in the lungs is significantly negatively correlated with inflammatory markers and lung function [14]. In addition to dominant pathogens, oral microbiota such as Streptococcus, Prevotella, and Staphylococcus species, are frequently co-isolated from bronchial and sputum samples of patients with reduced lung function [15]. Studies suggest that Streptococcus may play a crucial role in increasing the diversity of the CF lung environment and promoting patient stability [16].

Although P. aeruginosa and Streptococcus coexist in chronic respiratory infection lesions, the dynamics of their interactions remain understudied. Furthermore, as a common approach to treating infectious diseases, antibiotic intervention frequently impacts bacterial phenotypes, intracellular transcription, and extracellular signal-regulated transcriptional processes [17–19]. We hypothesize that antibiotics are critical determinants in shaping interbacterial relationships. This study aims to investigate the potential interaction dynamics of P. aeruginosa and Streptococcus salivarius, isolated from the same COPD patient, within the context of antibiotic treatment. Two pairs of P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius isolates collected at different time points were selected: one pair consisting of the lasR-intact strain P. aeruginosa (PAcx1) and its corresponding S. salivarius strain (SScx1), and the other pair consisting of the lasR-mutant P. aeruginosa strain (PAcx2) and its corresponding S. salivarius strain (SScx2). Combining the phenotypic interactions of the two species with transcriptomic changes under antibiotic treatment, we determined that the intrinsic suppression of S. salivarius by P. aeruginosa could be reversed by the presence of aztreonam at a sub-inhibitory concentration to P. aeruginosa. We demonstrated that S. salivarius extracellular products also inhibit the virulence factors of P. aeruginosa, although this effect is insufficient to support S. salivarius as the dominant pathogen. However, in the presence of aztreonam, the QS system and growth-related genes of P. aeruginosa were more strongly suppressed, while S. salivarius was activated, successfully invading P. aeruginosa.

Methods

Strains and culture media

The clinical P. aeruginosa strains and S. salivarius strains, isolated from the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of COPD patients, were provided by the Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu University. The clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius were identified through molecular characterization. Genomic DNA was extracted from pure cultures using the Bacterial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN, DP302). Universal primers 27 F and 1492R (sequences provided in Table. S1) were used for PCR amplification of the 16 S rRNA gene. Amplicons were sequenced and analyzed against the NCBI database using BLASTn with ≥ 99% sequence identity threshold for species confirmation. The P. aeruginosa reference strain PAO1 and the S. salivarius reference strain (S. salivarius ATCC 27945) were stored in the laboratory. Individual colonies were selected after streaking and subsequently cultured overnight at 37 ℃ to maintain the strains. Given the unique growth requirements of S. salivarius, Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth was employed for its cultivation.

Determination of MIC of antibiotics

Following the guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI M100-S34), gradient drug treatments were conducted in 96-well plates for both species. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) testing was performed using P. aeruginosa PAO1 (ATCC 15692) and S. salivarius ATCC 27,945 as reference control strains, in accordance with CLSI M100-S34 (2024) guidelines. Aztreonam susceptibility was interpreted using CLSI breakpoints: ≤ 8 µg/mL (susceptible), 16 µg/mL (intermediate), and ≥ 32 µg/mL (resistant). After overnight incubation at 37 ℃, the OD600 values were measured. MIC was defined as the drug concentration at which the OD600 value matched that of the blank Mueller-Hinton (MH) broth control. The susceptibility of P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius to commonly used laboratory antibiotics was assessed (Table. S2). Given the specific growth requirements of S. salivarius, MH broth supplemented with 5% defibrinated sheep blood was consistently used in the MIC experiments.

Bacterial interaction assays on agar plates

In prior research conducted in our laboratory, models for testing the effects of the lawn agar inhibition zone, colony pair distance, and bacterial extracellular product distribution were established [10]. The concentration of aztreonam was selected based on sub-MIC results, with 8 µg/mL chosen as the treatment concentration. PAO1 and S. salivarius ATCC 27,945 were used as standard controls. Overnight-cultured bacterial cells were harvested, centrifuged, and the pellets were collected. Based on colony-forming units (CFU), the cell concentration was adjusted to 1.0 × 109 CFU mL-1, using saline as the diluent, for subsequent experiments. In the lawn inhibition zone assay, 100 µL of P. aeruginosa or S. salivarius suspension was spread evenly onto blank BHI agar plates. Subsequently, 5 µL of the other bacterial suspension was inoculated onto the lawn. To investigate the role of S. salivarius in an antibiotic-stressed P. aeruginosa environment, 50 µL of S. salivarius suspension was spread onto one half of an agar plate, leaving the other half untreated. Finally, 5 µL of P. aeruginosa suspension was inoculated onto agar plates containing aztreonam. For the colony pair distance experiment, as previously described, 5 µL of both P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius were serially inoculated on blank and antibiotic-treated (8 µg/mL aztreonam) agar plates at increasing distances, and this was repeated six times. All plates for the lawn agar inhibition zone and colony pair distance experiments were incubated overnight at 37 ℃ for 16–18 h. The extracellular product distribution experiment followed the same colony arrangement as the colony pair distance experiment, but the incubation method and time differed significantly. First, one bacterial strain was continuously inoculated on one side of the agar plate and incubated at 37 ℃ for 24 h. Afterward, the bacterial culture was removed with a sterile blade, leaving only the extracellular products on the plate. The second bacterial strain was then inoculated on the opposite side and incubated overnight at 37 ℃ to assess the effect of the removed bacterial strain’s extracellular products. Finally, colonies from the proximal and distal sides were aseptically collected, washed, and dispersed in 1 mL of sterile saline, serially diluted to an appropriate concentration, and plated on blank BHI plates for CFU enumeration.

Competition experiments with mixed ratios of two bacteria

In prior research conducted in our laboratory, a model for testing the dynamic interaction of bacterial species was established [10, 12]. 5 µL bacterial mixture containing P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius (each at 1 × 109 CFU/mL, OD600 = 1) at different ratios (1:9, 1:1, 9:1) was added to sterile plastic tubes (1 cm diameter × 1 cm height) placed on BHI agar plates under different conditions (control/drug-treated). The sterile plastic tubes were used to concentrate the interaction of the two species and to prevent the sustained expansion of colony. Every 2 h, the bacterial colonies inside the plastic tubes were removed, washed with 1 mL of sterile saline, diluted, and plated onto blank BHI agar plates. Due to the distinct appearance of the two bacterial species, CFUs for each species were directly counted. Six independent replicates were performed at each time point. All plates were incubated at 37 ℃ for varying durations depending on the experimental requirements.

Pyocyanin production assay

SScx1 and SScx2 were cultured in 30 mL of BHI medium under shaking conditions (220 rpm) for 24 h at 37 ℃. The cultures were centrifuged, and the supernatants were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove bacteria. Equal amounts of PAcx1 and PAcx2 were inoculated into sterile 12-well plates containing varying concentrations of the SScx1 and SScx2 supernatants, followed by static incubation at 37 ℃ for 24 h. The bacterial cells of P. aeruginosa were then collected by centrifugation and resuspended in 1 mL of physiological saline, with the cell density measured at OD600. Pyocyanin was extracted from the supernatants using chloroform and 0.2 N HCl, following the method described by Essar et al. [20], and absorbance was measured at OD520. Finally, the OD520 values were normalized by dividing them by the corresponding OD600 values of P. aeruginosa and then compared with the control group.

Mixed culture and transcriptome sequencing

Preliminary co-culture experiments demonstrated that direct contact between the two bacterial species leads to the complete elimination of one species. To prevent this situation from obstructing RNA extraction, a 12-well plate with a microporous filter membrane (0.22 μm) was used to co-culture P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius, while single-species cultures were carried out using standard 12-well plates. During co-culture, different bacterial species were placed inside and outside the membrane, with 3 mL of BHI medium added. The cultures were either untreated or treated with aztreonam at a concentration of 8 µg/mL, and incubated overnight for 18 h. In monoculture, all conditions were the same as in co-culture, except for the absence of the filter membrane. The bacterial cells from the different culture conditions of the two species were collected and total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Takara), with three independent replicates for each group. The RNA was purified and selected on the AMPure XP beads platform at Smartgenomics Technology Institute (Tianjin, China) to generate a cDNA library. After the library passed quality control, different libraries were pooled according to the required concentration and target sequencing depth, and then subjected to Illumina sequencing, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. HTSeq v0.6.1 software was used to calculate the read count mapped to each gene, and the FPKM of each gene was computed based on gene length, followed by counting the reads mapped to each gene. Differential expression analysis between two conditions/groups was performed using DESeq2 (R version 1.20.0). The p-values obtained were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. Genes with adjusted p-values < 0.05 identified by DESeq2 were considered differentially expressed. GO enrichment analysis for differentially expressed genes was performed using the GOseq R package, and KEGG pathway enrichment of differentially expressed genes was analyzed using KOBAS software. GSEA analysis was performed on the GO and KEGG datasets of the species using the local version of the GSEA tool (http://www.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp). Venn diagrams for the differentially expressed QS genes of P. aeruginosa under different conditions were constructed using the online software Venny 2.1 (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/), with the QS list of P. aeruginosa revealed in 2003 [21]. All data from the Venn diagrams can be seen in the Additional files 1–6.

Quantitative PCR

PAcx1 and PAcx2 were cultured under three conditions: untreated, in the presence of 50% S. salivarius supernatant, and in the presence of 50% S. salivarius supernatant with 8 µg/mL of aztreonam. After 24 h of cultivation in BHI, bacterial cells were harvested using Trizol reagent for total RNA extraction. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed with 16 S rRNA as the internal reference gene, and gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method [22]. The specific primers for QS-related genes in P. aeruginosa are summarized in Table S1.

Caenorhabditis elegans assay

Following a previously established method for evaluating P. aeruginosa lethality toward C. elegans [23], We implemented minor modifications. Fresh suspensions of P. aeruginosa strains PAcx1 and PAcx2 (OD600 = 1) were spread on 3.5 cm peptone-glucose-sorbitol (PGS) agar plates. Control groups consisted of worms fed uracil-deficient Escherichia coli OP50 (additive-free), while experimental groups included: (1) Untreated group; (2) Treated with 8 µg/mL aztreonam; (3) Treated with 50% supernatant from a 24-hour culture of S. salivarius; (4) Treated with 8 µg/mL aztreonam and 50% S. salivarius 24-hour culture supernatant. Both control and experimental plates were incubated at 37 ℃ for 24 h. After this, the plates were further incubated at 25 ℃ for 24 h before introducing 10 newly cultivated L4-stage adult worms to each plate. Worm survival was monitored throughout the experiment under continued incubation at 25 ℃. The growth of nematodes on plates supplemented with or without 20% of the 24-hour supernatant from SScx1 or SScx2, and fed with uracil-auxotrophic E. coli OP50, was set as controls.

Statistical analyses

Data analysis and statistical tests were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons of mean standard deviations were performed using two-tailed paired or unpaired t-tests or one-way ANOVA. The survival curves of nematodes were compared using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.

Results

Treatment with aztreonam affects the interspecific interaction of bacteria

The interaction between P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius was first revealed on BHI agar plates. Regardless of whether examining PAcx1, PAcx2, or the model strain PAO1, all strains grew normally on S. salivarius lawns and produced varying degrees of inhibition zones (Fig. 1A, Fig. S1A). In contrast, S. salivarius was unable to grow on P. aeruginosa (Fig. S2). In previous experiments, we conducted MIC testing using commonly used antibiotics on two pairs of strains (Table. S2). To simulate the differential effects of antibiotics on the complex pulmonary microbiota, we selected aztreonam, a β-lactam antibiotic with significant resistance differences between P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius, as the background antibiotic for further research. At 8 µg/mL aztreonam (sub-MIC for P. aeruginosa), the growth of S. salivarius was unaffected, whereas the initial growth of P. aeruginosa was delayed. However, within 24 h, P. aeruginosa reached a bacterial density nearly equivalent to that observed in the blank control (Fig. S3). Next, we tested the effect of different gradient concentrations of aztreonam on the growth of P. aeruginosa, with and without S. salivarius lawns, near the MIC of P. aeruginosa (16 µg/mL) (Fig. S4). Interestingly, by contrast, P. aeruginosa was almost unable to grow on S. salivarius with high antibiotic resistance in the presence of sub-inhibitory concentrations of aztreonam (8 µg/mL). Compared to its growth on no lawn, P. aeruginosa grew poorly on the S. salivarius lawn and failed to produce an inhibition zone (Fig. 1B). Under the same drug-treated conditions, the growth of clinical P. aeruginosa strains on S. salivarius ATCC 27,945 remained suppressed (Fig. S5). However, this inhibitory effect was significantly weaker than that observed with clinically isolated S. salivarius strains (Fig. S4). The stronger inhibition by clinical S. salivarius strains likely reflects their evolutionary adaptation in COPD airways.

To further investigate, a pairwise proximity assay was designed to test the mutual effects of the extracellular products of the two species. The two species were grown on BHI plates at increasing distances from proximal to distal regions, and CFU quantification was performed on the proximal and distal colonies of P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius. Under antibiotic-free conditions on BHI agar plates, S. salivarius exhibited no significant inhibitory or stimulatory effects on either proximal or distal P. aeruginosa colony formation (Fig. 1A), but with the addition of 8 µg/mL aztreonam, S. salivarius inhibited the growth of the proximal P. aeruginosa colonies (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, we further confirmed that aztreonam significantly influences the interspecies interaction by growing the two species on each other’s extracellular products. P. aeruginosa, which typically produces varying levels of anti-Streptococcus molecules on blank BHI plates, showed almost no effect on S. salivarius when cultured on aztreonam-containing plates. In contrast, S. salivarius, which initially had no effect on P. aeruginosa, exhibited antimicrobial inhibition of proximal P. aeruginosa in the presence of aztreonam (Fig. S6). It was suggested by these findings that the growth of both species could be inhibited by each other, but the competitive advantage was dependent on the presence of aztreonam.

Aztreonam reverses the dynamic competition between P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius

After exploring the static interaction between P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius on plates, we began monitoring their dynamic interaction. Using mixed bacterial cultures with different initial ratios under various growth conditions, we monitored the final P. aeruginosa to S. salivarius ratio at different time points to assess changes in the competitive dynamics under varying conditions. The laboratory has developed a relatively mature method for dynamic bacterial game analysis [10]. In brief, the ratio of the two bacterial species in sterile plastic tubes is measured every 2 h during the 24-hour antibiotic efficacy period.

On blank plates, regardless of the initial ratio of the mixed bacterial cultures, both PAcx1 and PAcx2 invaded SScx1 and SScx2 within a short period (8 h) with overwhelming competitive advantage (Fig. 2A-C, G-I), consistent with the activation time of P. aeruginosa’s QS system [24]. Interestingly, at sub-inhibitory concentrations of aztreonam (which had minimal effect on P. aeruginosa’s growth density) (Fig. S3), regardless of the initial ratio, SScx1 and SScx2 gained the upper hand and successfully invaded PAcx1 and PAcx2 (Fig. 2D-F, J-L).

Extracellular products of S. salivarius inhibit QS virulence gene expression in P. aeruginosa

P. aeruginosa secretes various virulence factors (such as elastase, exotoxins, rhamnolipids, pyocyanin, and lectin A) to adapt to the host’s adverse environment [25, 26]. All of these virulence factors are regulated by cell density and controlled by three key QS systems (las, rhl, and pqs) [27]. Among these virulence factors, pyocyanin is one of the key contributors to disease severity, tissue damage, and organ dysfunction [28]. Notably, the production of pyocyanin is regulated by multiple QS systems and is directly controlled by mvfR (pqsR) [29, 30]. To evaluate the impact of S. salivarius extracellular products on P. aeruginosa virulence, different gradients of cell-free S. salivarius culture supernatant were added to P. aeruginosa cultures. The results showed that while increasing the S. salivarius supernatant concentration negligible impact on P. aeruginosa growth density, it significantly inhibited pyocyanin production by P. aeruginosa (Fig. 3), particularly in the lasR-mutant strain PAcx2 (Fig. 3B and D). RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) results identified significantly differentially expressed genes from P. aeruginosa treated with S. salivarius supernatant and mapped them to the list of QS activation genes reported for P. aeruginosa by Schuster et al. [21]. The results showed that after treatment with 50% S. salivarius supernatant, PAcx1 had a reduction of 120 QS-related genes, while PAcx2 showed a decrease of 70 QS-related genes compared to the untreated group (Fig. 4A and C). Both P. aeruginosa strains showed a reduction in central regulatory genes (las, rhl) and key genes involved in pyocyanin biosynthesis (phzA1). Additionally, PAcx2 exhibited inhibition of mvfR, phzS, and other key genes controlling pyocyanin synthesis. Furthermore, downregulated genes were enriched in GO pathways, with PAcx1 primarily downregulated in pathways related to cell communication, intracellular signaling, propionate metabolism, and amino acid degradation (Fig. 4B). Due to the poor oxidative stress tolerance of lasR-mutant strain PAcx2, downregulated genes were primarily enriched in oxidative phosphorylation, cell motility (cilia and flagella), and nucleotide metabolism and synthesis (Fig. 4D). Therefore, the transcriptomic data suggest that S. salivarius extracellular products inhibit the virulence regulation of P. aeruginosa and reduce its metabolic capacity.

Aztreonam activated S. salivarius and inhibited P. aeruginosa during cross-feeding

To investigate the transcriptomic and metabolic differences between P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius under aztreonam intervention, we conducted a cross-feeding experiment [31]. The cells were cultured in BHI medium and physically separated using a 0.22 μm filter that did not allow cellular penetration. After normalizing the cell densities, both bacterial species were inoculated on the inner and outer sides of the filter membrane and cultured in either untreated or aztreonam environments. RNA sequencing results showed that after antibiotic addition, the transcriptomes of both species in the cross-feeding setup were differentially affected compared to when cultured individually.

Two sets of RNA sequencing comparisons were performed for SScx1 and SScx2: (1) with and without aztreonam intervention in individual S. salivarius cultures; (2) with and without aztreonam intervention in co-cultures of S. salivarius and P. aeruginosa. In the individual S. salivarius culture, the addition of aztreonam had minimal effect on its genome (Fig. S7A and S7B). Consistent with the previous phenotypic observations, the co-culture of P. aeruginosa under blank conditions resulted in the differential downregulation of hundreds of S. salivarius genes (Fig. S7C and S7D). Interestingly, under cross-feeding co-culture conditions, aztreonam intervention, in contrast to the drug-free condition, activated the expression of S. salivarius genes. SScx1 exhibited an upregulation of 676 genes, while SScx2 demonstrated an upregulation of 711 genes, with the degree of differential expression being significantly higher than that of the downregulated genes (Fig. S7E and S7F). Additionally, both SScx1 and SScx2 exhibited differential upregulation of growth and biosynthetic metabolic pathways, including those related to translation, transcription, protein synthesis, ribosome activity, and macromolecule production (Fig. S8A and S8B).

Two sets of RNA sequencing comparisons were performed for PAcx1 and PAcx2: (1) with and without aztreonam intervention in individual P. aeruginosa cultures; (2) with and without S. salivarius intervention in P. aeruginosa cultures under aztreonam treatment. In individual P. aeruginosa cultures, aztreonam treatment led to differential downregulation of 220 QS-related genes in PAcx1 and 135 in PAcx2, including the central regulatory genes lasR, rhlR, mvfR, and other associated genes (Fig. 5A and C). Additionally, it is worth noting that under antibiotic-treated conditions, the introduction of S. salivarius had a differential impact on P. aeruginosa compared to its monoculture. In PAcx1, the addition of SScx1 resulted in downregulation of 32 QS-related genes, including pqsH, which promotes the functioning of the PQS system, and phzA1, which is associated with pyocyanin production (Fig. S9A). For the lasR-mutant strain PAcx2, the lasI gene, which synthesizes the autoinducer C12 molecule (3-oxo-C12-HSL), and key genes upstream of the PQS system (pqsABCDE) were suppressed by the intervention of SScx2 (Fig. S9C). The downregulated pathways were enriched in KEGG pathways, with PAcx1 primarily showing downregulation in glyoxylate metabolism, carbon metabolism, and QS (Fig. S9B). In contrast, the lasR-mutant strain PAcx2 exhibited downregulation not only in carbon metabolism but also in several other metabolic pathways, including fatty acid biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. S9D).

In summary, transcriptomic data indicate that S. salivarius can be activated by aztreonam during co-culture. For P. aeruginosa, however, regardless of whether it is cultured alone or in co-culture, and irrespective of aztreonam addition, its QS system and metabolic activity are inhibited to a certain extent.

Extracellular products of S. salivarius Reduce virulence and mortality of P. aeruginosa under aztreonam treatment

To validate the downregulation of P. aeruginosa QS genes by extracellular products of S. salivarius and under aztreonam treatment, qPCR was conducted with three control groups targeting P. aeruginosa core QS genes. Stimulation by S. salivarius supernatant almost completely inhibited the expression of key QS system-regulated genes in P. aeruginosa (Fig. 6A and C). Furthermore, in the presence of aztreonam, the S. salivarius supernatant exhibited even greater toxicity toward P. aeruginosa, significantly further reducing QS gene expression. Notably, in the qPCR results of PAcx2, while the SScx2 supernatant alone showed no significant effect on certain QS genes, aztreonam intervention led to a moderate suppression of genes such as lasB and rhlA (Fig. 6C). This observation aligns with the previous transcriptomic data. Overall, the qPCR results further confirmed that the downregulation of P. aeruginosa QS-related genes is associated with S. salivarius involvement and aztreonam treatment.

Finally, we utilized a C. elegans model to test the impact of S. salivarius extracellular products in the presence of aztreonam on the lethality of P. aeruginosa. Untreated PAcx1 and PAcx2 exhibited markedly higher toxicity toward C. elegans, with nearly complete mortality recorded within 72 h (Fig. 6B and D). In contrast, treatment groups exposed to either aztreonam or S. salivarius supernatant showed no significant differences but exhibited consistently lower mortality rates compared to the untreated group, with survival rates exceeding 0% at 72 h. Interestingly, in the presence of aztreonam, the addition of S. salivarius supernatant markedly improved nematode survival, substantially reducing the lethality of P. aeruginosa. These findings indicate that both S. salivarius and aztreonam can suppress the virulence of P. aeruginosa. Notably, in the presence of aztreonam, S. salivarius exhibits enhanced inhibitory effects on P. aeruginosa, leading to a significant reduction in its lethality toward nematodes.

Discussion

Polymicrobial infections are common in human diseases, including chronic respiratory infections, gastrointestinal diseases, urinary tract infections, and wound infections, among others [2]. These infections often lead to increased antimicrobial resistance and heightened disease severity [29, 32]. As research into human diseases progresses, the complexity and significance of microbial interactions of microbial interactions are increasingly recognized. These interactions can be mutually beneficial, such as in mutualism and symbiosis [33]; unidirectional, as seen in commensal relationships [13]; or competitive or antagonistic, as in parasitism and harmful symbiosis [34].

It is well known that P. aeruginosa, equipped with a robust metagenome and QS system, often outcompetes other microorganisms in polymicrobial enviroments [28]. However, the physical characteristics and physiological states of microbial interactions between pathogens and surrounding communities are frequently neglected when considering antibiotic efficacy and resistance evolution. Repeated use of antibiotics may disrupt microbial balance, leading to the structural reorganization and selective enrichment of bacterial communities [35]. In this study, two pairs of P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius were isolated from the bronchial fluids of advanced COPD patients, with P. aeruginosa displaying an inherent competitive advantage (Fig. 1A, Fig. S1), even in the presence of the lasR-mutant strain PAcx2. We hypothesize that, during COPD treatment, the introduction of exogenous factors, such as antibiotic use, facilitates the survival and coexistence of S. salivarius with P. aeruginosa. A series of competition experiments on agar plates, along with dynamic monitoring of bacterial mixing ratios, demonstrated that antibiotic application recalibrates the competitive relationship between P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius (Figs. 1 and 2, Fig. S5, Fig. S6).

Several independent microbiome studies utilizing sputum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid have confirmed the presence of S. salivarius in the airways of patients with chronic lung disease, suggesting that its presence is not due to saliva contamination [16, 36, 37]. Notably, S. salivarius is increasingly recognized as a core resident of the microbiome in individuals with impaired lung function [13]. Filkins et al. conducted bacterial community profiling of sputum genomic DNA samples from patients with acute exacerbations of CF. Their findings suggested that Streptococcus species, including S. salivarius, may play a significant role in enhancing the diversity of the CF lung microbiome and contributing to patient stability [16]. In addition to considering the antibiotic evasion capabilities of S. salivarius, we also identified that its extracellular products play a role in the process of establishing competitive advantage among species (Fig. 3). In this study, in addition to considering the high resistance of S. salivarius itself, we also identified that its extracellular products played a role in establishing competitive advantages among species.

RNA sequencing results confirmed that the QS system and metabolic processes of P. aeruginosa can be inhibited by S. salivarius (Fig. 4), although this inhibition was insufficient to reverse the competitive advantage of P. aeruginosa. A study has shown that S. salivarius supernatants inhibit the growth of P. aeruginosa and K. pneumoniae and enhance their sensitivity to antibiotic treatment, which is attributed to bacteriocins and bacteriocin-like inhibitory substances (BLIS) produced by S. salivarius [38, 39]. S. salivarius is one of the earliest colonizers of the human oral and gastrointestinal tract [40], and its biosynthesized RiPPs (salivary toxins) exhibit immune activity and targeted antimicrobial properties against known oral pathogens and multi-species biofilms [41]. This antimicrobial effect was further validated by RNA sequencing, where we found that S. salivarius extracellular products significantly downregulated many QS-controlled genes in P. aeruginosa (315 QS-induced genes, with 120 downregulated in PAcx1 and 70 in PAcx2), including key QS regulatory genes (lasR, rhlR, pqsR, etc.), biofilm-related genes (mucA), antibiotic resistance genes (mexR), and various regulatory systems (rpoN, gacA) (Fig. 4).

We next evaluated the RNA sequencing results of the co-culture under aztreonam intervention. Aztreonam did not significantly affect the growth or transcription of high-resistance S. salivarius in monoculture, however, in the co-culture, the presence of aztreonam became a critical factor for activating S. salivarius (Fig. S7). In the presence of aztreonam, metabolic pathways related to both intracellular and extracellular growth were differentially upregulated in SScx1 and SScx2, while these same pathways were suppressed in P. aeruginosa (PAcx1 and PAcx2) (Fig. S8). This suggests that the presence of P. aeruginosa is a necessary condition for aztreonam to successfully activate S. salivarius. Although aztreonam at sub-inhibitory concentrations had no significant impact on the growth density of P. aeruginosa (Fig. S3), it is noteworthy that multiple QS-related genes in PAcx1 and PAcx2 were downregulated, including central QS regulatory genes such as lasR, rhlR, and mvfR. Furthermore, aztreonam also inhibited processes like QS, transmembrane transport and redox reactions (Fig. 5). Our findings provide compelling evidence for how aztreonam intervention enables S. salivarius to counteract P. aeruginosa dominance during co-infection.

It is worth noting that due to the complex interaction pathways and the unpredictable dynamics of extracellular products during the interplay between the two species, it remains challenging to determine the relative contributions of antibiotic toxicity and extracellular products in the interaction between P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius. However, integrating the results of qPCR and C. elegans infection experiments confirms that extracellular products of S. salivarius inhibit the virulence of P. aeruginosa (Fig. 6). Nevertheless, the reversal of competitive dominance still depends on the presence of aztreonam. This supports the earlier predictions from transcriptomic data, suggesting that aztreonam and S. salivarius may independently and additively inhibit P. aeruginosa during co-infection.

Conclusions

In summary, our study elucidates the interaction dynamics between P. aeruginosa and S. salivarius and their responses to antibiotic intervention. P. aeruginosa exploits its QS system to gain a competitive advantage within microbial communities. However, this advantage can be disrupted, or even completely eliminated by exogenous factors such as antibiotics. In the context of bacterial co-culture, antibiotics preferentially select S. salivarius, thereby enhancing its competitive ability and adaptability, while continuously suppressing the QS system of P. aeruginosa. The bacterial survival advantage, initially held by P. aeruginosa, is reversed to S. salivarius in the presence of aztreonam. This study highlights the significant impact of antibiotics on bacterial interactions, suggesting that reciprocal inhibition and competitive strategies form the scientific basis for the sustained coexistence of these two species within host tissues. However, two limitations should be noted: (1) the specific molecular targets underlying S. salivarius-mediated inhibition of P. aeruginosa virulence remain unidentified, and (2) the mechanism by which aztreonam activates S. salivarius growth-related genes requires further investigation. These findings underscore the importance of broadening the scope of research on multi-microbial chronic respiratory infections. Rather than focusing solely on primary pathogens, a more comprehensive approach is needed—one that accounts for the complex dynamics of the entire microbial community. The selection pressures exerted by antibiotics are influenced not only by the primary pathogens but also by the intricate, interdependent interactions within the microbiota as a whole.
