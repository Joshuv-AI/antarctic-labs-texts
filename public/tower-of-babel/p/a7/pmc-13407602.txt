Comparison of Mechanical and Surface Properties of Glass Ionomer, Resin-modified Glass Ionomer, and Alkasite Restorative Materials

Introduction

Maintaining the integrity of primary teeth until their natural exfoliation is fundamental to pediatric oral health. These teeth preserve arch space for permanent successors and contribute significantly to speech development, mastication, and the child's psychological well-being. In pediatric dentistry, restorative procedures must be adapted to unique clinical challenges such as reduced attention span, limited chairside cooperation, and an increased susceptibility to dental caries. Consequently, selecting an ideal restorative material in pediatric practice depends on factors including ease of application, minimal technique sensitivity, biocompatibility, fluoride release, and adequate mechanical strength.1

“Glass ionomer cements” (GIC) have long been used in pediatric restorative dentistry due to their chemical adhesion to tooth structure, fluoride release, and excellent biocompatibility.2,3 However, the relatively poor mechanical properties of conventional GIC, such as low fracture toughness, limited compressive strength (CS), and poor wear resistance, restrict their application in stress-bearing areas such as posterior occlusal restorations.4 To overcome these limitations, “resin-modified glass ionomer cements” (RMGIC) were developed by the incorporation of water-soluble resins. RMGIC exhibited improved physical properties, faster setting time, and retained fluoride release with chemical adhesion.5 Despite these improvements, their durability and strength still fall short compared to resin-based restorative materials such as composites.

Alkasites are resin-based bioactive materials designed to bridge the gap between the mechanical performance of resin composites and the therapeutic benefits of GIC. They contain alkaline glass fillers which release acid-neutralizing ions, such as hydroxide, calcium, and fluoride—into the surrounding environment, helping to maintain a favorable pH and support remineralization, thereby exhibiting a bioactive nature.6

Two commercially available alkasite materials are “Cention N” and “Cention Forte,” with Cention N being the first introduced to the market. Both are supplied as dual-cure, self-curing materials with optional light-curing capability, allowing bulk placement due to their dual-curing property. According to the manufacturer, alkasites can be considered superior restorative materials due to their bioactivity, enhanced fluoride release, and excellent mechanical properties. Studies on Cention N have reported strength and longevity comparable to dental amalgam and esthetics similar to composite resins.7 Comparative research has also shown the mechanical properties of Cention N to be on par with composite resins and superior to GICs.8 While multiple studies have evaluated Cention N,9 there is a paucity of literature on the mechanical properties of the newly introduced Cention Forte in comparison to other restorative materials used in pediatric dentistry. Given the increasing interest in bioactive, high-strength restorative options for children, it is important to compare the mechanical properties of these newer materials with established alternatives. Such evidence-based comparisons will aid clinicians in making informed decisions for optimal pediatric restorative care.

Given this background, we aimed to compare the mechanical [CS and surface hardness (SH)] and surface properties [surface roughness (SR)] of a newly introduced alkasite material, Cention Forte, with GIC and RMGIC. The null hypothesis was that there would be no difference among these restorative materials.

Materials and Methods

This in vitro study compared the CS, SH, and roughness of GIC, RMGIC, and alkasite restorative materials. This in vitro study did not involve human participants, human tissue, or biological samples and was exempted by ethical committee. No artificial intelligence is used.

Sample Size Estimation

Using “G*Power 3.1 software (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany),” the sample size was estimated to be 36 (12 for each group) with 80% power and alpha of 5%.

The following cements were used in this study:

GIC: GC Fuji 2 Gold Label Universal Restorative, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan.

GIC: GC Fuji 2 Gold Label Universal Restorative, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan.

RMGIC: GC Fuji II LC, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan.

RMGIC: GC Fuji II LC, GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan.

Alkasite materials: Cention Forte, Ivoclar Vivadent, AG, Schaan, Liechtenstein.

Alkasite materials: Cention Forte, Ivoclar Vivadent, AG, Schaan, Liechtenstein.

Sample Preparation for CS Measurement

A total of 36 cylindrical-shaped samples (6 mm high × 4 mm diameter; n = 12 per group) were made using a split mold. The samples of GIC were made with a recommended powder and liquid weight ratio (2.7:1) mixed for 25 seconds on a mixing pad. The material was packed into the mold, covered with a cellulose acetate strip, and pressed with a glass slab to minimize porosity. The samples were left undisturbed for 15 minutes with weights, following which the samples were retrieved. Samples showing any signs of porosity were discarded. All the samples were stored in distilled water for 1 day. RMGIC samples were prepared with a powder and liquid weight ratio of 3.2:1 and mixed for 20 seconds. The procedure was similar to the manipulation of GIC with an additional light-curing for 20 seconds. Defective samples with any porosity were discarded. Defect-free samples were immersed in distilled water for 1 day before testing. Preparation of alkasite material involved the activation of the capsule by pressing the plunger on a flat surface, then mixing at 4000–4800 rpm for 15 seconds in a capsule mixer. The mixed material was extruded into the mold, ensuring the tip was immersed to prevent air entrapment, then covered with a cellulose acetate strip and compacted. After a 15-minute static period under weights, the samples were retrieved from the mold, inspected for defects, and those without porosity were stored in distilled water for 1 day before testing.

Sample Preparation for Surface Hardness and Roughness

A total of 72 disk-shaped samples of dimensions 10 mm × 2 mm were prepared using a silicon mold (n = 24 per group). The material was placed in the mold, covered with a cellulose acetate strip, and pressed with a glass slab. The samples were left undisturbed under the weight for 15 minutes, after which the sample was retrieved and inspected. All defect-free samples were stored in distilled water for 1 day before measuring SH. Twelve samples per group were used for SH, and 12 samples were used for SR assessment.

Measurement of Compressive Strength

Compressive strength was measured using a Universal Testing Machine (Instron 3366, UK). Samples were centered between flat platens and loaded at 0.5 mm/min until fracture. The maximum load at failure (P, in newtons) was recorded and expressed in MPa.

Assessment of Surface Hardness

The SH was measured with a digital Vickers microhardness tester with a diamond indenter (MMT-X7A700, Matsuzawa Co. Ltd., Japan). The indenter was placed on the sample surface with a 300 gf load and a dwell time of 15 seconds, and five indentations were made. The “Vickers hardness number” (VHN) was calculated by averaging the indentation measurements across all points.

Measurement of Surface Roughness

A profilometer equipped with diamond stylus (tip radius of 2 µm) (Form Taly Surf 50, Taylor Hobson, United Kingdom) was used for SR measurement. The stylus traversed 1 mm at a speed of 0.25 mm/s across each sample's surface. Measurements were performed in three directions at three different locations per sample, and the average roughness (Ra) was expressed in micrometer.

Statistical Analysis

Data for CS, SH, and SR were summarized as mean ± standard deviation. Normality and equality of variances were confirmed using the “Shapiro–Wilk” and “Levene's tests,” respectively. Given that assumptions were satisfied, “analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test” was used to compare mean CS, SH, and SR among the three materials. Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 20.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, United States). A p-value of ≤0.05 was considered significant.

Results

The CS, SH, and SR of the three materials are shown in Table 1. A significant difference in the mean CS was seen among the three restorative materials (F = 114.09, p < 0.001). Post hoc test showed that Cention Forte exhibited the highest CS (184.05 ± 13.44 MPa), followed by RMGIC (136.01 ± 11.07 MPa), and GIC (111.01 ± 11.47 MPa) (Fig. 1). Similarly, a significant difference in the mean SH was seen among the three groups (F = 85.69, p < 0.001). However, post hoc test showed that RMGIC (65.48 ± 6.86 VHN) and Cention Forte (69.80 ± 6.34 VHN) had significantly higher SH than GIC (38.78 ± 4.64 VHN) (Fig. 2). The mean SR showed a significant difference among the three materials (F = 35.35, p < 0.001). Post hoc test showed that RMGIC (0.088 ± 0.033 µm) and Cention Forte (0.067 ± 0.025 µm) exhibited significantly lower SR than Conventional GIC (0.16 ± 0.033 µm) (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study compared the CS, SH, and SR of three commonly used restorative materials in pediatric dentistry. CS reflects how well a restorative material resists the forces of biting and chewing, including normal and parafunctional loading such as clenching or grinding.10 In the present study, Cention Forte (alkasite) demonstrated the highest CS, followed by RMGIC and GIC. Similar findings have been reported by several researchers, who observed that the strength of alkasite materials was higher than that of various types of conventional and modified GIC.11–13

Glass ionomer cements are acid–base reaction products formed between “fluoroaluminosilicate glass” and polyalkenoic acid, characterized by relatively low filler loading and weaker ionic crosslinking, which limits their strength under high masticatory stress. RMGIC incorporates resin monomers [e.g., 2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (HEMA), bisphenol A-glycidyl methacrylate (Bis-GMA)] that polymerize alongside the acid–base reaction, improving cohesive strength and matrix rigidity.14 The alkasite, Cention Forte, is a resin-based material with a high filler load (≥75 wt%) including barium glass, ytterbium trifluoride, and calcium fluorosilicate, combined with a urethane dimethacrylate matrix and efficient silane coupling, yielding superior stress transfer, crack resistance, and polymer crosslinking.7 These differences echo previous findings that increasing filler content, silane coupling efficiency, and resin crosslink density markedly enhance CS and hardness in restorative materials.15 Such properties are critical for stress-bearing posterior restorations.

Regarding SH, Cention Forte and RMGIC performed similarly, outperforming conventional GIC. Similar observations were reported by Mazumdar et al.16 and Adsul et al.,12 where the alkasite material possessed significantly higher SH than GIC.12,16 This is likely due to the resin phase in RMGIC and alkasite materials providing higher elastic modulus and wear resistance than the more brittle GIC matrix. Higher hardness suggests better resistance to occlusal wear and surface deformation, supporting longer service life in functional areas.

Both inherent material characteristics and external influences shape the SR of restorative materials. Roughened surfaces tend to promote greater plaque accumulation and premature material degradation. These elevated surface irregularities act as conducive sites for bacterial attachment and biofilm formation, thereby increasing the risk of future gingival inflammation and periodontal complications.17 The Ra is the most widely used metric for SR. In this study, the highest Ra value was exhibited by conventional GIC, indicating greater roughness of the surfaces. Meanwhile, RMGIC and Cention Forte exhibited lower Ra values, with no significant difference, suggesting smoother surfaces. Clinically, lower Ra values are important for minimizing plaque retention and staining; the commonly referenced threshold of 0.2 µm Ra marks the point beyond which biofilm accumulation increases substantially.18 In the present study, all materials were below this threshold, but GIC approached it most closely, suggesting greater susceptibility to plaque retention. The particle size is a pivotal factor in determining SR of restorative materials.19 The improved smoothness in RMGIC and alkasite materials can be attributed to smaller, more uniform filler particles and the resin matrix that allows for a smooth surface.

From a clinical standpoint, Cention Forte offers the best combination of strength, hardness, and smoothness, making it ideal for stress-bearing posterior restorations where both durability and aesthetics are desired. RMGIC is a strong intermediate option, with improved mechanical and surface properties over GIC and the benefit of fluoride release and easier handling. Conventional GIC offers fluoride release and good biocompatibility and is better suited to nonload-bearing restorations, atraumatic restorative treatment (ART) procedures, or high-caries-risk patients where chemical adhesion and fluoride release are more important than maximum strength.

Conclusion

Within the study's limitations, the alkasite restorative material (Cention Forte) offered superior mechanical and surface properties that are desirable for successful restorations. It can be a suitable alternative for stress-bearing posterior restorations where both durability and aesthetics are desired.

Limitations and Future Research

Owing to the in vitro study design, this study could not completely replicate the complex oral environment. Factors such as thermal and pH variations, enzymatic degradation, and mechanical fatigue can alter mechanical and surface properties in the oral environment. Future work should include long-term clinical trials and artificial aging protocols to validate whether the observed superiority persists over time. Comparative wear resistance, marginal adaptation, and ion release under clinical conditions merit further investigation.
