SCAI Position Statement on Intracardiac Echocardiography to Guide Structural Heart Disease Interventions
Authors: Mackram F. Eleid, Christine J. Chung, Matthew J. Daniels, V. Vivian Dimas, Andrew M. Goldsweig, Harsh Golwala, Eric M. Horlick, Matthew J. Price, Carlos E. Sanchez, John T. Saxon, Markus D. Scherer, Gilbert H.L. Tang, Dee Dee Wang, Omar K. Khalique
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As structural heart disease interventions rapidly evolve, demand for minimally invasive intraprocedural imaging techniques has increased. Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) has emerged as a valuable imaging modality, offering real-time, high-resolution visualization of cardiac structures without the need for general anesthesia. This position statement from the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions outlines the clinical applications, advantages, limitations, and implementation strategies for ICE in guiding transcatheter structural heart disease procedures. The document details standardized ICE imaging protocols and views for interventions involving the mitral, tricuspid, and pulmonary valves, as well as left atrial appendage occlusion atrial septal defect, and patent foramen ovale closure. It emphasizes the importance of operator training, physician workflows, and institutional readiness for successful ICE integration. The statement also advocates for updated reimbursement models that reflect the complexity and value of ICE-guided procedures and calls for further research comparing ICE and transesophageal echocardiography outcomes.

Introduction

The field of structural heart disease (SHD) interventions continues to expand rapidly, driven by innovations in device technology, advancements in imaging techniques, and the growing expertise of specialized structural heart teams. In parallel, the demand for minimally invasive procedural imaging continues to increase with the introduction of newer transcatheter therapies for tricuspid regurgitation and the accelerating adoption of left atrial appendage occlusion (LAAO) for stroke prevention. Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) technology has been employed by physicians for decades to guide catheter-based procedures, but until recently, its use was limited for complex valvular interventions in large part due to hardware and software limitations. Although transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) remains a mainstay to guide transcatheter SHD interventions, several factors prompt a larger role for physician-led intraprocedural ICE imaging. Distorted cardiac anatomy, intracardiac shadowing, and the location of the esophagus relative to cardiac structures can limit TEE imaging windows, thereby requiring the adjunctive use of ICE to optimize visualization. Additionally, in an era of minimally invasive transcatheter procedures, medical teams and patients are gravitating away from the use of general anesthesia and intubation, which are typically required to perform TEE. This position statement will discuss the emerging role of ICE for various SHD interventions and propose clinical workflow pathways for the integration of novel ICE technologies into health systems.

Comparison with TEE: Advantages and disadvantages of ICE

Three-dimensional (3D) TEE led by an interventional imaging physician is the gold standard for preprocedural and intraprocedural imaging of structural heart interventions. The 3D TEE offers the most extensive set of high-resolution ultrasound arrays and unparalleled imaging quality over alternative ultrasound imaging modalities. However, general anesthesia is usually required for airway protection during procedures, and esophageal injury from TEE probe manipulation during structural heart procedures is a recognized risk.2 There are certain anatomical challenges restricting the ability to safely utilize intraprocedural TEE, including but not limited to prior history of esophagectomy, esophageal stricture, or a hiatal hernia. The ICE is not affected by such challenges and allows for the use of conscious sedation, promoting rapid patient recovery, early discharge, and avoidance of the risk of esophageal injury (Table 1). However, there are several limitations to ICE use in structural heart procedures. Currently, ICE catheters incorporate fewer ultrasound crystal arrays than TEE probes, which lowers imaging resolution compared with TEE. The ICE catheter may also require a second operator to handle the device to maintain an appropriate position for optimal image quality. There are also financial limitations due to the cost of ICE catheters and limitations on the scalability of ICE technology, depending on the physician operator training and experience. There may be a tangible increase in procedural risks associated with ICE, including a potential risk of cardiac perforation and potential complications inherent with the need for additional transvenous access.3 Furthermore, as most physician operators will not have extensive prior experience working in ICE views, misidentification of anatomic landmarks can result in devices implanted in incorrect structures and technically unsuccessful procedures.

For LAAO procedures, ICE guidance can support cases with conscious sedation. There is a learning curve to puncture the atrial septum safely at the proper location, cross the septum, and enter the left atrium (LA) with the ICE catheter to achieve appropriate views of the left atrial appendage (LAA) and implanted device. Obtaining 2-dimensional (2D) views of the LAA from different perspectives (mid LA, supramitral, left upper pulmonary vein) or using 3D ICE from a single position are also key components to assure good device seating with no leaks in a fashion that compares favorably to multiplanar TEE.4, 5, 6

In the case of tricuspid valve (TV) intervention, ICE can avoid acoustic shadowing from adjacent cardiac structures (eg, mitral or aortic valve prostheses, cardiac leads, lipomatous septum) given the ability to position the probe much closer to the valve compared with TEE. In cases where neither 3D TEE nor ICE alone is sufficient, the attributes of both may be leveraged to offer high quality images of the TV leaflets from the RV perspective (transgastric TEE) and from the right atrium (RA) perspective, enabling proper visualization of all relevant anatomy to guide tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (T-TEER) or transcatheter TV replacement (TTVR) procedures. For mitral interventions, TEE remains the gold standard due to its reproducibility, high quality images, and established success in procedural guidance; however, there are instances where TEE may be contraindicated or lack image clarity as previously discussed. In such cases, ICE may serve as an alternative to guide mitral interventions that might otherwise be unfeasible. Similar to LAAO procedures, proficiency in performing transseptal puncture (TSP), advancing the ICE catheter across the atrial septum into the LA, and positioning the ICE catheter to achieve optimal mitral valve (MV) visualization is crucial for procedural success. Data are available comparing 2D ICE and TEE in LAAO; however, there are limited comparative outcomes data on 3D ICE. For valvular interventions, there are no comparative data at the time of this writing.

Establishing standard ICE views

Standardized imaging acquisition angles are critical for interventional imaging operator training, development of catheter manipulation muscle memory, pattern recognition, and comparative evaluation between imaging modalities. Standardized imaging acquisition protocols obtained in a consistent sequence help ensure a protocol is built for quality assurance. Standardized protocols expedite training and adoption of new technologies, thereby enabling procedural teams to be confident that important anatomical findings are not overlooked among different physician operators. Standardized nomenclature of imaging “knobology” and imaging acquisition windows enables effective communication that facilitates patient care and research.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and TEE employ standardized views named for both the transducer location and orientation with respect to the cardiac structure being interrogated. For example, most complete TTE studies begin with parasternal long-axis views. Imaging then progresses in a standardized sequence, typically with the apical and subcostal views. Traditionally, ICE technology has been employed to guide TSP.7 With the advent of larger fields of view and improved hardware and software capabilities, ICE devices have evolved to help interventional imaging physicians guide procedures. This shift in utilization and clinical application of ICE technology shifts it from the realm of diagnostic imaging to the field of interventional imaging. Similar to the creation of standardized protocols for intraprocedural TEE imaging, the development of standardized intraprocedural imaging protocols using ICE is necessary.

We propose the following imaging protocol views and nomenclature, illustrated with reference images in Figure 1A and B. Unlike TTE or TEE, periprocedural ICE imaging is not intended to be a comprehensive diagnostic examination and should be tailored to guide the specific intervention being performed. Unlike traditional standard of care TEE protocols, ICE imaging windows are not obtained at precise imaging angles due to variation in patient anatomy and ICE catheter positioning within various intracardiac chambers. Standard ICE views

Standard views, beginning with the “home view,” are obtained by physician operators advancing the ICE probe from a femoral vein to the RA, where the structures visualized include the TV (Supplemental Video 1), aortic valve, and right ventricular inflow and outflow (Figure 1A). Using standard settings, objects in the right-most aspect of the ICE image represent the area in front of the tip of the probe. Therefore, to advance into the right ventricular (RV), pulmonary artery (PA), or LA, the appropriate passage should typically be visible on the right-most aspect of the image. From the home view, clockwise rotation of the catheter will reveal the MV, after which further clockwise rotation with posterior flexion will demonstrate the interatrial septum (IAS).

To advance into the RV, the TV is visualized from the home view, and the catheter is anteflexed and advanced across the TV. To advance into the PA from the RV, the catheter is rotated clockwise until the pulmonic valve is visualized. The catheter is withdrawn until the pulmonic valve appears at the right-most aspect of the image, and then the catheter is anteflexed across the pulmonic valve.

After a TSP has been performed, the catheter can be advanced into the LA. The transseptal wire should be placed into the left superior pulmonary vein, and the puncture site dilated with a sheath or balloon larger than the ICE catheter. From the RA septal view, the transseptal wire is visualized, and the catheter is anteflexed to be parallel with the wire trajectory in the left anterior oblique projection at approximately 20° on fluoroscopy. If, while advancing, the catheter veers away from the wire, fluoroscopy is repositioned to the orthogonal right anterior oblique view at approximately 20°, and right/left steering is adjusted, or the ICE catheter is rotated clockwise/counterclockwise to be parallel with the wire trajectory again. The operator should avoid adjusting the catheter position relative to the wire in more than 1 dimension in each fluoroscopic projection.

Intracardiac echocardiography for LAAO typically follows a standard imaging protocol8, 9, 10 of views as detailed in Figure 1C. From the femoral vein, the probe is advanced to the RA home view. Assessments for pericardial effusion are made from the home view and left ventricular from RV views. An assessment for LAA thrombus may be performed from the LAA in RA, RV, or PA view. The TSP is performed in the RA septal view, the septum is dilated, and the ICE probe is advanced along the transseptal wire across the septum into the LA. Four LAA views are used to measure the LAA, visualize LAAO device deployment, and assess the LAAO device for stability and complete closure: the LA LAA mitral, aortic, pulmonic, and supramitral views. All 4 quadrants of the LAA can often be identified in a single position using 3D ICE if the imaging transducer is coaxial to the face of the LAA and LAA occlusion device. Finally, the LA 4-chamber view is captured to assess pericardial effusion before removal of the ICE probe.

ICE for TSP and septal interventions

Intracardiac echocardiography from the RA may provide superior images of the atrial septum and RA structures compared with TEE, which can be limited by the distance from the esophagus to the lower RA. The 3D ICE with the use of multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) and digital steering allows for precise localization of TSP needed based on the type of structural procedure being performed. The significant cost associated with disposable ICE probes, coupled with the requirement for venous access and lack of physician ICE operator reimbursement, often limits their utilization in standalone diagnostic procedures. The ICE and TEE are endorsed for intraprocedural imaging during atrial septal interventions.11,12 Guidelines and clinical equipoise reflect the relative merits of each imaging modality and the lack of evidence for superiority of one over the other.

From a training perspective, familiarity with ICE begins with low-complexity and more common procedures like closure of patent foramen ovale (PFO) or simple secundum atrial septal defect (ASD). Complex secundum ASD, although more challenging, can be effectively assessed and closed under ICE guidance with practice.13 ICE-guided transeptal puncture can more easily be adopted by the operator once imaging of the atrial septum is mastered during PFO and ASD closure.

Intraprocedural imaging enhances safety and effectiveness, reduces radiation exposure, and delivers cost efficiency by minimizing the number of devices used, deployments attempted, and cardiac catheterization laboratory time.14 Importantly, 3D ICE imaging of the atrial septum can provide the same views traditionally obtained with TEE by using digital steering and biplane. Although 3D ICE probes offer advanced imaging, their higher cost can limit their routine use to more complex structural interventions unless combined with cost-saving practices, including avoidance of general anesthesia and same-day dismissal.

PFO assessment and closure

Protocols to screen for PFO vary. Some institutions add bubble testing to TEE during cryptogenic stroke workup, whereas others prefer transcranial Doppler or transthoracic bubble studies for screening, deferring ICE or TEE for detailed assessment during closure. A complete PFO assessment includes identifying anatomical variants that increase the potential for right-to-left shunting, such as a prominent Eustachian ridge or septal aneurysm (Figure 2A-G).15 The atrial septum is examined with color Doppler for PFO and incidental ASD. Intraprocedural bubble injections from the superior and inferior vena cava can confirm shunt presence and magnitude (Figure 3A, B).16 It is increasingly important to document shunt magnitude.17 Closure involves crossing the PFO, sizing it (per operator preference), and deploying a device under ICE guidance to ensure proper configuration (Figure 4). ASD assessment and closure

For secundum ASD, key considerations are defect size, location relative to cardiac structures, the potential presence of concomitant structural abnormalities, and features of pulmonary hypertension (eg, right-to-left shunting). Preprocedural imaging with 3D TEE can provide a comprehensive evaluation prior to intraprocedural ICE guidance. Defining the supporting rims of the defect via ICE is crucial (Figure 5), especially in complex defects where 3D capabilities might be beneficial, as seen during the rollout of 3D TEE.18 Proper probe rotation and angulation are necessary for comprehensive rim assessment.19 The ICE helps orient the device during deployment and confirms appropriate positioning and interaction with the atrial septum or other structures. Intracardiac echocardiography facilitates effective closure of PFO and secundum ASD, reducing procedural risks and enhancing outcomes. Balancing the cost and benefit is an important objective. Therefore, 3D ICE might be reserved for more complex cases, as additional expertise at the imaging console is needed to derive the most from the more expensive volumetric probes.

TSP

The main goal of TSP is for either: (1) diagnostic purposes when direct LA pressure measurement is necessary, and/or (2) interventional purposes when a therapeutic atrial septostomy is required, or most commonly to safely and efficiently advance delivery sheaths into the LA for left-sided structural procedures. As summarized above, ICE is particularly useful for visualizing inferior puncture sites.

TSP set-up

The sensitivity to detect LAA thrombus is better with TEE compared with ICE when the ICE catheter is positioned in the RA. Preprocedural imaging (TEE or cardiac computed tomography [CT]) should be considered for patients with atrial fibrillation at risk for LAA thrombus. Without preprocedural imaging, exclude LAA thrombus by imaging through the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) or RA (Figure 6), and avoid wire advancement into the LAA if the LAA is difficult to visualize (Figure 7). If a large-bore sheath will be introduced into the same femoral vein as the ICE catheter or if the femoral vein is small (eg, patients <40 kg), contralateral venous access for ICE is helpful. TSP imaging views

Operators should evaluate for the presence or absence of baseline pericardial effusion prior to dynamic intraprocedural ICE imaging. This survey for preexisting pericardial fluid can be performed once the ICE catheter is in the RA. For optimal views, use left flexion at the RA/superior vena cava junction for a 4-chamber view or advance into the RV and rotate to view the free wall of the LV (Figure 8A, B). Adjust the ICE catheter rotation and flexion to visualize septal tenting. Fluoroscopy can help ensure the ICE imaging plane is directed toward the transseptal sheath tip. Use the long-axis view to determine the inferior/superior position and the aortic valve short-axis view for the anterior/posterior position. Postprocedure ICE should confirm the absence of significant right-to-left flow from the iatrogenic ASD, and pericardial effusion in the 4-chamber or RV-to-LV view (Figure 8A, B).

ICE for LAAO

Intracardiac echocardiography has rapidly grown as an imaging modality to guide LAAO. The ICE is minimally invasive, allowing operators to perform LAAO under conscious sedation, which facilitates same-day discharge and improves efficiency and patient satisfaction. With the avoidance of general anesthesia and intubation necessary for TEE, in the hands of high-volume, experienced ICE operators, ICE-guided LAAO procedures can reduce catheterization laboratory procedure room utilization time.20 ICE-guided LAAO may obviate the need for general anesthesia, reducing resource utilization and simplifying scheduling.

With advancements in ICE catheter technology and reimbursement strategies, ICE could become the default intraprocedural imaging technology when used in conjunction with fluoroscopy to guide LAAO (Table 3). This approach may be particularly suitable for patients with uninterrupted anticoagulation, appropriate LAA anatomy on preprocedure CT screening imaging, preserved renal function, and tolerance for moderate sedation.21 The 3D ICE is preferred over 2D ICE when available due to its superior imaging quality and because it allows digital rotation of the imaging plane, enabling acquisition of multiple views with less catheter manipulation (Figure 10). Intracardiac echocardiography technology, similar to interventional imaging physician-led TEE procedures, has an operator learning curve for catheter manipulation and image acquisition. ICE for tricuspid valvular interventions

Transesophageal echocardiography imaging for T-TEER and TTVR poses substantial challenges due to the anterior position of the TV.8 The quality of TEE imaging can also be limited by several other factors, including the presence of hiatal hernia, the relative location between the esophagus and the right heart, right heart orientation, and the presence of structures that shadow the TV, such as cardiac implantable electronic device lead(s), aortic and mitral prostheses, tricuspid annuloplasty devices, and LAA and interatrial septal devices.

Three-dimensional ICE has evolved to provide substantial additive value for guiding transcatheter tricuspid interventions (Supplemental Videos 2-7).9 ICE technology has advanced from traditional small sector 2D imaging to the incorporation of live MPR. Acoustic shadowing with 2D and 3D ICE is rare due to the closer proximity of imaging elements to the TV compared with TEE. The 2D ICE can provide superior imaging resolution of the TV leaflets to complement TEE in confirming leaflet grasping during T-TEER23 or leaflet capture and engagement during TTVR or annuloplasty (Figure 11). The 3D TEE is currently considered the most accurate tool for device steering and trajectory assessment in tricuspid interventions; however, 3D ICE may be able to provide similar guidance (Supplemental Videos 8-13). Although the 3D ICE workflow for tricuspid interventions is not yet standardized, Table 4 lists key imaging views that have a complementary role in achieving procedural success. Greater roles for ICE in transcatheter tricuspid interventions are anticipated as advancements in device technology occur, including larger full-volume sector size and improved 3D image quality. ICE for mitral valvular interventions

ICE for mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair

Although ICE guidance for mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) has been shown to be safe and effective in select patient populations across multiple case series, it is not commonly utilized in the United States, accounting for <2% of cases.10,24 This subgroup generally includes patients with intolerance to TEE due to esophageal contraindications, cervical injuries, or poor imaging windows, among other issues.10 In such cases, as discussed earlier, transeptal access can be obtained at the mid and posterior fossa under 3D ICE guidance using MPR, specifically to determine the distance from the puncture site to the MV (Supplemental Video 14). Once an adequate TSP is achieved, the puncture site can be predilated with a balloon (typically 8.0 mm) or dilated with the M-TEER delivery sheath, and a stiff wire placed in the pulmonary vein or the LA. Using the wire for guidance, the ICE can be advanced into the LA in either a left anterior oblique or right anterior oblique view under fluoroscopy guidance (Supplemental Video 15). Once in the LA, the appropriate positioning of the ICE catheter is parallel to the MV, usually with anterior flexion and right-left catheter steering as needed. Evaluation of the mitral regurgitation location and severity, leaflet characteristics, opening area, gradient severity, and 3D MPR assessment can be used for subsequent steps of the M-TEER procedure (Figure 12, Supplemental Videos 16-23). Interaction between the ICE catheter and the M-TEER system can occasionally lead to difficulty manipulating the delivery system, which can be addressed by using a different transeptal site for ICE or maneuvering the ICE with a deflectable sheath (eg, Oscor Inc). Additionally, shadowing from the M-TEER device itself can be overcome using minor adjustments with the flex knob and/or right-left tilt. Subsequently, as discussed above, MPR can be used for orientation of the TEER delivery system and subsequent grasping, release, and assessment of final gradients and residual MR (Figures 13 and 14, Table 5). The 3D imaging quality of TEE remains superior to currently available ICE technologies, and this is an important tool to ensure proper device arm orientation and positioning on the MV for repair. For this reason, TEE currently remains a superior tool and the standard of care for guidance of M-TEER procedures. ICE for other mitral interventions

The utility of ICE for mitral valve-in-valve or valve-in-ring interventions has been demonstrated in several case reports and series.25, 26, 27 The fluoroscopic marker of the valve and the ring is the most helpful in positioning the transcatheter valve and is the primary imaging tool used to guide the final deployment of the balloon-expandable valve. Like above, in these cases, inferior and posterior transeptal access is obtained, and the transeptal distance to the failed surgical mitral bioprosthetic valve is measured with MPR assistance. Once the transeptal puncture is obtained, predilation of the transseptal site can be done as previously discussed. The ICE probe can then be advanced, and evaluation of baseline mitral regurgitation and MV gradient performed (Supplemental Video 24, Figure 14). Assessment of paravalvular leak can be challenging with ICE, owing to the need to systematically interrogate the entire prosthetic valve circumference, but can be done with increased experience. The 3D ICE offers the ability to digitally steer around the MV, which may allow for better comprehensive interrogation. Once the baseline assessments are complete, the ICE probe can be left in the LA while the device delivery system is advanced, straddling the bioprosthetic valve. Alternatively, the ICE probe can be retracted back into the RA, with the device delivery system advanced across the transeptal puncture and bioprosthetic valve, and deployment can be accomplished using fluoroscopic markers for guidance. Following deployment, the ICE probe can then be readvanced into the LA, and final assessments, including paravalvular leak and gradients, can be obtained (Supplemental Video 25, Figure 15).Figure 15Mitral valve-in-valve case 3-D intracardiac echocardiography post paravalvular leak and gradient check. ICE for pulmonary valve interventions

Transcatheter pulmonary valve replacement (TPVR) is increasing in frequency, with expanding percutaneous options for failing native outflow tracts. Evaluating the RVOT prior to and following TPVR is challenging with TTE due to difficulties in visualizing the RVOT secondary to poor acoustic windows, particularly in older patients with larger body habitus.28 This group of adults represents the majority of patients undergoing TPVR. The ICE provides a better assessment of the RVOT and pulmonary valve by imaging the RVOT from within the RV.29 Understanding the etiology of the valve dysfunction prior to TPVR is helpful to rule out paravalvular leaks or the presence of vegetations suspicious for endocarditis. These can be evaluated immediately prior to implantation with ICE (Figure 16). Following TPVR, assessment of valve function as well as the presence of any potential complications can be evaluated in real-time. TV injury, paravalvular leak, acute leaflet dysfunction, or pericardial effusion can all be detected with ICE. Reimbursement for ICE

At present, professional reimbursement for ICE uses only one add-on Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code, +93662. This code is valued at 2.80 relative value units. As an add-on code, +93662 may only be billed by the operator who also bills for the primary interventional procedure. Most common structural heart interventional procedures are eligible for the +93662 add-on including LAA occlusion (33340), PFO or ASD closure (93580), M-TEER (33418), mitral balloon valvuloplasty (92987), transcatheter aortic valve replacement (33361), and paravalvular leak closure (mitral 93590, aortic 93591).

The present professional reimbursement paradigm dates from an era when 2D ICE was principally used to provide static imaging of the IAS for TSP and PFO closure; however, current state-of-the-art ICE technology provides dynamic image-guided functionality to 3D TEE imaging. Advancements in multiplanar reformat integration, 3D sector width captures, and enhanced frame rates have enabled use of ICE technology to guide complex transcatheter interventions, including LAAO and mitral and TV interventions.

Reimbursement is a significant obstacle to increased ICE adoption: 3D ICE guidance for complex transcatheter interventions requires a nonsterile operator to obtain and process images. ICE reimbursement does not currently include the value of this separate interventional imaging physician operator in addition to the interventional implanting operator. Currently, reimbursement for ICE is a fixed amount regardless of the type of procedure performed, and there is no separate reimbursement for the interventional imaging physician who operates ICE.

Appropriate reimbursement paradigms must be developed to address the technical complexity and expertise necessary for structural heart interventional ICE guidance. In the long term, novel CPT codes to compensate structural heart interventional imaging physicians must be developed by the American Medical Association CPT Editorial Panel and adopted by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services and other payers. In the short term, hospitals must implement revenue-sharing programs to compensate structural heart interventional imaging physicians for ICE guidance in the absence of a billing code for interventional imaging physicians standing alongside their cooperator colleagues with associated relative value unit generation.

Intracardiac echocardiography catheters are complex medical devices with the ability to dynamically measure, visualize structures, and provide color Doppler flow for transcatheter interventions under expert operation by an interventional imaging physician or interventional cardiologist. Branded under the postmarket approval of a catheter pathway, ICE technology has more offerings than a traditional medical device. The cost of ICE catheters highlights the complexity of this medical device’s technology. The expertise required to fully utilize and apply the additive value of ICE imaging expertise requires designation of usage to experienced physician operators trained in 3D TEE imaging and transcatheter imaging expertise. Similar to TEER imaging expertise National Coverage Determination recommendations, when it comes to the newest generation 3D ICE catheter utilization, dual-operators are recommended such that dynamic imaging and intraprocedural guidance is not sacrificed. The interventionalists must primarily focus on operation of the non-ICE interventional device, particularly in the event of procedural clinical instability requiring catheter manipulation and intervention of the non-ICE access sheath. Implementing ICE at your institution

Initiating a new ICE program within an SHD program requires an understanding of the following: (1) the additive value, benefits, and advantages of utilizing ICE guidance for transcatheter heart procedures, (2) the impact of incorporation of ICE technology use on the institutional workflow and finances, and (3) the multiple factors and training required to successfully utilize ICE in the cardiac catheterization laboratory.

Administrative

Data regarding the value of ICE, including its utility in procedural guidance and beneficial effect on procedural duration and avoidance of general anesthesia, should be shared with hospital leadership to provide a perspective into the practical advantages of implementing ICE. ICE score cards with procedural times, anesthesia utilization times, procedural volume pre- and post-ICE guidance should be tracked. These score cards should be implemented at the beginning of the ICE program, with metrics review after the first 25, then 50 ICE-guided procedures to evaluate the halo effect of ICE imaging to health system procedural growth.

Cost and value

Deciding on the type of ICE catheters to purchase and use is an important consideration that should take into account the breadth and complexity of procedures performed at an institution. Although standard 2D ICE with a single physician operator is an effective model for more simple procedures such as PFO closure, 3D ICE and a 2-physician operator model (an interventionalist operating the ICE probe and echocardiographer operating the imaging console) is increasingly being used to guide more complex structural procedures including T-TEER and transcatheter mitral valve replacement. The additional cost of the ICE catheter and an interventional imaging physician cooperator should be balanced with the benefits gained from shortened procedural duration, potential avoidance of general anesthesia, and attendant costs. As ICE is offered by several major vendors, compatibility with existing vendor services present at the site may be synergistic. More efficiencies will be developed as vendors develop the same console ICE and TEE imaging connections. An additional benefit is for cases where ICE technology is incorporated as either the primary imaging catheter of choice or supplementary to the TEE probe, where the procedure could not be accomplished without the use of ICE. For example, T-TEER with extreme anatomical variation or right-sided acoustic shadowing precluding visualization of relevant anatomical areas by TEE probe positioning.

Workflow and training

Incorporation of ICE into clinical workflows can be subdivided into stepwise training levels of expertise and procedural goals. Each institution may have a unique set of procedural expertise goals, and the different levels correspond to the diversity of health care system expertise provided across the nation. Proposed ICE expertise training levels are shown in Table 6. Level I training includes a basic understanding of 2D ICE technology with the ability to identify the fossa and distinguish the atrial septum from the surrounding structures to facilitate TSP, PFO closure, and simple ASD closure. Level II training is characterized by the ability to integrate CT imaging for procedural planning and utilize 2D and/or 3D ICE for more precise transseptal crossing location as is needed for LAAO procedures. Level III training applies to comprehensive utilization of 3D ICE technology to guide mitral and tricuspid interventions where a steep learning curve exists both for the interventionalists and echocardiographer. Ideally, interventional operators using ICE to guide structural interventions will have participated in formal ICE training during advanced structural interventional fellowship training. Similarly, echocardiographers ideally will undergo advanced imaging fellowships including experience with ICE imaging guidance for transcatheter interventions. Depending on staffing and expertise, local workflows should be developed. Examples include subdivision of cases into those that need an anesthesiologist and/or an interventional imaging physician and those that only include the interventional cardiologist. This allows coverage from anesthesiologists and imaging physicians to be arranged for appropriate cases in an efficient manner.

Future directions

Although periprocedural ICE is not intended to be a comprehensive diagnostic exam, significant advancements in ICE technology have made it possible for guidance of complex structural interventions using invaluable tools for navigation and confirmation of device engagement such as biplane 2D imaging, live 3D imaging, and 3D MPR imaging. Despite these advances, several areas for further improvement exist. These include more stable ICE probe positioning with greater steerability, as well as improved 3D imaging quality and sector width to the level and quality of currently available TEE probes. Development of a reimbursement system that recognizes the important role of the interventional imaging physician in addition to the implanting physician for optimal use and operation of the ICE system is essential to ensure the feasibility of ICE implementation across institutions. More evidence regarding clinical outcomes of the use of ICE guidance compared with TEE guidance for mitral and tricuspid interventions, preferably with improved ICE technologies, will be needed to better understand the role of this evolving and versatile technology.
