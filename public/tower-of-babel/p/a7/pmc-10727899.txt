An Excimer Laser-Induced Eruptive Sebaceous Hyperplasia
Authors: Gyoo Huh, Ga-Young Lee, Seung-Wan Chae, Young-Jun Choi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractSebaceous hyperplasia (SH) is a benign proliferation of the sebaceous glands. Ultraviolet radiation is known to be the cofactor for the pathogenesis of SH. Frequently reported adverse events of 308-nm excimer laser are erythema, burning or pain, and itching sensation. However, the role of excimer laser as an aetiological factor of SH is rarely reported in the literature. A 59-year-old female presented with several elevated, umbilicated papules on the face. Papular eruption appeared abruptly after 11 weeks of treatment for vitiligo which includes 308-nm excimer laser therapy. The distribution of lesion was associated with the treated area. Total cumulative dose was 3,300 mJ/cm2 over 16 sessions. Histopathologic findings showed enlarged sebaceous glands composed of numerous lobules around a centrally located, widened sebaceous duct. Chronic sun exposure clinically causes SH was proved in the previous animal studies. Thus, we introduce a rare case of eruptive SH in the patient treated with 308-nm excimer laser.Keywords: Excimer laser, Immunosuppressive agents, Sebaceous gland hyperplasia, Ultraviolet therapy, Vitiligo

INTRODUCTION

Sebaceous hyperplasia (SH) is a benign proliferation of sebaceous glands that most commonly presents as asymptomatic yellowish papules with central umbilication on the face. Although SH is idiopathic in most cases, many etiological factors are reportedly associated with this condition, such as immunosuppressive medication (e.g., cyclosporine), reduction in androgen levels associated with old age, insulin, thyroid stimulating hormone, and hydrocortisone1. Ultraviolet radiation (UVR) is also known to be a cofactor for the pathogenesis of SH.

The 308-nm excimer laser has been used to administer targeted therapy to patients with psoriasis, vitiligo, and cutaneous T-cell lymphoma. The most frequently reported adverse events of narrow band ultraviolet B irradiation are erythema, mild burning or pain, and an itching sensation, all of which are well-tolerated by most patients and generally appear transiently after treatment. However, the role of excimer laser as an etiological factor of SH has been rarely reported. We present a patient with the infrequently reported condition of excimer laser-induced eruptive SH.

CASE REPORT

A 59-year-old Korean female visited our clinic complaining of asymptomatic multiple, non-scaly, whitish macules and patches with distinct margins on the both cheeks, forehead, and neck (Fig. 1A). The skin depigmentation appeared after a change in cosmetics, and skin irritation had occurred in the same area 2 months ago (Fig. 1B). She was in good general health and not on any regular medication. Laboratory examination revealed an anti-thyroid peroxidase antibody titre of 887.89 U/ml. Her laboratory findings were otherwise unremarkable including thyroid function tests. The lesion fluoresced when illuminated on Wood lamp examination. Histopathology revealed a loss of basal melanin pigments with thin epidermis and no inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 2A). Based on clinical manifestations and histopathologic findings, the lesion was diagnosed as vitiligo. The patient was treated with a short course of oral corticosteroids, a topical corticosteroid, and 308-nm excimer phototherapy.

(A) Before the treatment, skin irritation was followed by depigmented lesion. (B) Depigmented lesions on the both cheeks, forehead, and neck fluoresced bright white under Wood’s lamp. (C) Multiple discrete yellow, umbilicated, flesh-colored papules erupted over the forehead and cheeks after 16 sessions of excimer phototherapy. Arrow indicates newly developed lesions. We received the patient’s consent form about publishing all photographic materials.

(A) Loss of basal melanin pigments with thin epidermis was observed in the depigmented lesion of the neck (H&E, ×100). (B) Histopathology of the papular lesion on the right temple showed enlarged sebaceous glands composed of numerous lobules around a centrally located wide sebaceous duct (H&E, ×40).

Eleven weeks after initiation of excimer phototherapy, sudden papular eruptions of the facial skin were observed. There was no other adverse event during the treatment course. The dosage of oral corticosteroid, which started at prednisolone 15 mg daily, had been tapered to 5 mg every other day. The initial dose for phototherapy was 150 mJ/cm2 and had gradually increased to 330 mJ/cm2. The total cumulative dose was 3,300 mJ/cm2 over 16 sessions. Physical examination revealed several discrete, yellow, slightly umbilicated papules on her forehead and both cheeks (Fig. 1C). Histopathology showed an enlarged sebaceous gland composed of numerous lobules around a centrally located, widened sebaceous duct, and the eruptions were diagnosed as eruptive SH induced by excimer laser therapy (Fig. 2B). Considering that the vitiliginous lesions would probably worsen subsequent to treatment of the SH, we decided to manage the lesion of the SH after the improvement of vitiligo.

DISCUSSION

Little is known about the pathogenesis of SH or its genetic profile, but association with several factors has been suggested. Medications, hormone levels, and UVR have been proposed as etiological factors of SH1. While SH occurs in only 1% of the healthy population, it occurs in 16% of immunosuppressed organ transplant recipients2. Cyclosporine-induced SH is well-documented and occurs in up to 30% of renal transplant patients treated with cyclosporine3. Cases related to immunosuppressant drugs other than cyclosporine have been reported, including azathioprine, tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and predisone1,4.

Cases of eruptive SH following immunosuppression with predisone have been well reported1. However, this case differed from those reports in the degree of immunosuppression. The patient took low dose of prednisolone for a short period as opposed to the high dose of the previous report. During the treatment course, the dosage of corticosteroids was tapered off while total cumulative dose for phototherapy gradually increased. The area of skin eruption approximated excimer-laser irradiated site as well. Therefore, association with factors other than medication, such as an excimer laser, should be considered. But we cannot exclude the possibility that systemic corticosteroid have contributed the development of SH to a certain extent.

UVR could induce hyperplasia of sebocytes and sebaceous glands. This was demonstrated through in vitro study using sebocytes of hamster and in hairless murine model5,6. Sebaceous gland of hamster and those of human are similar in terms of their size, response to androgens, turnover time, proliferation and lipid synthesis7,8. Therefore, sebocytes of hamster have been widely used as a substitute for those of human in the research for sebaceous gland8,9. The glands are situated superficially in a thin-skinned, hairless mouse. In such circumstances, UVR could more easily reach the gland and cause the enlargement5,10. Likewise, the patient had a history of irritant contact dermatitis which caused the impairment of the barrier functions. This could give rise to deeper penetration of 308-nm excimer laser than normal condition, leading to the development of SH. Although epidermis strongly attenuates transmission of UVR, a certain portion reaches the gland. High cumulative exposure with repeated exposure to UVR is thought to be implicated in development of SH5. These findings support a diagnosis of eruptive SH most likely secondary to 308-nm excimer laser therapy. It is unfeasible to study the effect of high-dose UVR on the human subject. Thus, this case has its significance in suggesting the possibility that UVR could induce SH in human subject as well as in animal model.

We could not find the distinguishing clinical features between 308-nm excimer laser induced SH and SH in general. Also, there was not any unusual histopathological findings in our case deviated from the classic findings of SH.

Treatment for SH includes photodynamic therapy, laser ablation, cryotherapy, cauterization, topical therapy with trichloroacetic acid, or surgical excision. However, these options often pose a risk of skin discoloration and scar formation1.

To our knowledge, this was a rare case of eruptive SH after 308-nm excimer laser treatment. Considering the disfiguring nature and limited treatment options of SH, clinicians should be aware of this rare but potential adverse effect of excimer laser treatment.
