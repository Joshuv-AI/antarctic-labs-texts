Limited Ventilatory Reserve During Exercise in Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction
Authors: Gizem Cifci, Daniel J. Brown, Barry A. Borlaug, Joshua R. Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) exhibit a tachypneic breathing strategy during exercise. Patients with HFpEF have pulmonary system alterations that may contribute to the breathing strategy in HFpEF.Research QuestionDo patients with HFpEF have limited ventilatory reserve compared with control patients during exercise? Furthermore, does greater upper body adiposity contribute to the lower ventilatory reserves in HFpEF?Study Design and MethodsPatients with HFpEF (n = 16; mean age ± SD, 70 ± 8 years) and control patients (n = 19; mean age ± SD, 69 ± 8 years) performed incremental cycling to volitional exhaustion. Trunk percent fat was quantified with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. Inspiratory capacity, inspiratory and expiratory reserve volumes, and ventilatory variables were compared between groups at rest and during exercise at 40 W and peak oxygen uptake.ResultsPatients with HFpEF had lower total lung capacity (5.4 ± 1.1 vs 6.4 ± 1.2 L), FVC (3.1 ± 0.7 vs 3.8 ± 0.6 L), and FEV1 than control patients (all P < .04), respectively. During exercise at 40 W, patients with HFpEF had higher breathing frequency and smaller inspiratory reserve volume (1.2 ± 0.4 vs 1.5 ± 0.3 L) and expiratory reserve volume (0.9 ± 0.4 vs 1.1 ± 0.5 L) than control patients (both P < .05), respectively. At peak oxygen uptake, inspiratory and expiratory reserve volumes were not different between groups (both P > .21), but patients with HFpEF had lower end-inspiratory lung volume (P = .02). Patients with HFpEF had higher trunk percent fat than control patients (49% ± 7% vs 40% ± 8%, respectively; P < .01). Percent trunk fat was related to % predicted FVC (r = –0.57) and tidal volume to FVC ratio during exercise at 40 W (r = 0.53) for patients with HFpEF (both P < .04) but not control patients (both P > .72).InterpretationThese findings demonstrate that patients with HFpEF have limited ventilatory reserve during exercise, in part due to their smaller lung capacity. Furthermore, our findings suggest that the greater distribution of upper body adiposity in HFpEF is an important contributor to the smaller lung capacity for these patients.

Take-Home Points

Study Question: Do patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) have limited ventilatory reserve compared with control patients during exercise?

Results: Patients with HFpEF had lower ventilatory reserves during submaximal exercise compared with control patients and a significant relationship between upper body adiposity and lung capacity.

Interpretation: Our results show that patients with HFpEF have reduced lung capacity, in part due to upper body adiposity, resulting in limited ventilatory reserve during exercise.

The pathophysiology of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) syndrome is complex and involves multiple physiological systems.1,2 Exertional dyspnea and exercise intolerance are defining characteristics of HFpEF symptomology. It is well established that patients with HFpEF exhibit a tachypneic breathing strategy (shallow, rapid breathing pattern) during exercise3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and this breathing strategy is linked with exertional symptomology.3 High left ventricular filling pressures during exercise in HFpEF are considered the primary determinant of the tachypneic breathing strategy along with other ventilatory abnormalities during exercise exhibited by patients with HFpEF (eg, pulmonary congestion, reduced lung compliance, impaired lung diffusion, elevated physiological dead space).3, 4, 5,8 Pulmonary system alterations are highly prevalent in HFpEF and include restrictive and/or obstructive lung patterns, lower lung diffusion capacity, and reduced inspiratory muscle strength and endurance.4,5,9, 10, 11, 12, 13 The smaller lung capacity reported in HFpEF is likely also an important contributor to the breathing strategy for these patients.4,14 Specifically, the smaller lung capacity may reduce the ventilatory reserves (ie, inspiratory and expiratory reserve volumes) used to increase tidal volume (VT) with exercise necessitating a higher breathing frequency (fB) to meet the ventilatory demands in HFpEF.

Overweight and obesity are highly prevalent in patients with HFpEF.15 Obesity represents a distinct phenotype of HFpEF.16 Furthermore, HFpEF syndrome is associated with higher abdominal adiposity.17, 18, 19 Greater upper body adiposity directly impacts pulmonary function via its effect on diaphragm movement, chest wall properties, and lung inflation.20 In this context, it is unclear if greater upper body adiposity is a contributing factor to the smaller lung capacity in patients with HFpEF.

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to investigate the impact of HFpEF syndrome on ventilatory reserves during exercise. We hypothesized that patients with HFpEF would have limited inspiratory and expiratory reserve volumes compared with control patients during exercise and these limited ventilatory reserves would contribute to the tachypneic breathing strategy in patients with HFpEF. Furthermore, we hypothesized that greater upper body adiposity would be associated with lower ventilatory reserves in patients with HFpEF.

Study Design and Methods

Participants and Experimental Design

Sixteen patients with HFpEF and 19 control participants were recruited to participate in this study. HFpEF was defined by left ventricular ejection fraction ≥ 50%, clinical symptoms (eg, exertional dyspnea), and elevated left-sided heart filling pressures at rest and/or with exercise in accordance with established guidelines.21 Exclusion criteria for the participants included COPD, BMI > 40 kg/m2, and limited ambulatory capabilities. A subset of the participants were included in a previous study comparing work of breathing and operating lung volumes at matched absolute and relative ventilations during exercise.4 All aspects of the study were approved by the Mayo Clinic institutional review board (17-007785) and conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki, except for registration in a database. All participants were informed about the experimental procedures and potential risks involved before providing written and verbal informed consent.

In this cross-sectional investigation, all measurements were conducted during a single study session. First, participants were familiarized with all experimental measures and protocols followed by standard pulmonary function testing. Next, participants performed an incremental cycling test until task failure. During the incremental cycling test, participants performed inspiratory capacity (IC) maneuvers to determine end-expiratory lung volume (EELV) and end-inspiratory lung volume (EILV). In this study, ventilatory variables were compared between groups at rest, 40 W, and peak exercise.

Body Composition, Pulmonary Function, and Exercise Testing

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scans (Lunar iDXA Series X; GE Healthcare) were used to quantify trunk tissue composition. Trunk was defined via standard definition with this system and includes neck, chest, and abdominal areas.22 Trunk % fat mass is reported. Before each scan, the scanner was calibrated against a standardized calibration phantom to control for possible baseline drift according to manufacturer recommendations.

Participants performed pulmonary function tests according to the American Thoracic Society guidelines.23 Total lung capacity (TLC), residual volume, FVC, FEV1, and FEV1/FVC were measured and reported along with % predicted values.24,25

Participants performed an incremental cycling test with work rate increases of 20 W for patients with HFpEF and 40 W for control participants every 3 minutes until task failure (Lode Corival). Ventilatory and gas exchange variables were continuously collected during the incremental cycling test (MedGraphics CPX/D) and were averaged over 60 seconds at rest and 30 seconds for each incremental stage.

IC Maneuvers and Operating Lung Volumes

Multiple maximum flow volume loops were performed from TLC to residual volume before and immediately after exercise. The largest maximum flow volume loop (after exercise) was used for analysis. Before exercise, participants performed multiple IC maneuvers until these maneuvers were deemed valid and consistent. Near the end of each work rate, participants performed an IC maneuver from EELV to determine the placement of VT. Ventilatory and gas exchange variables are reported from the breaths preceding the IC maneuver at rest and at each exercise stage. A breath was considered typical if it exhibited similar volume and flow characteristics as the preceding breaths before the IC maneuver. Physiological drift during exercise was corrected using a computer program based on the IC ratio method.26 It was assumed that TLC did not substantially change during exercise.27 Inspiratory reserve volume was calculated by subtracting VT from IC. Expiratory reserve volume was calculated by subtracting IC from FVC. EELV was calculated by subtracting IC from TLC. EILV was determined by adding EELV and VT. EELV and EILV were also expressed as percentages of TLC and FVC. In a subset of control participants (n = 2) and patients with HFpEF (n = 2), TLC was not measured.

Statistical Analyses

Data are reported as mean ± SD. We conducted statistical analyses using SigmaStat version 2.0 software (Jandel Scientific). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk tests, and nonparametric tests were used when appropriate. Participant characteristics were compared using unpaired t tests or Fisher exact tests. The ventilatory data were compared within (rest, 40 W, and at peak oxygen uptake [Vo2peak]) and between groups (patients with HFpEF vs control patients) via 2-way mixed factorial measures analysis of variance. A Tukey post hoc analysis was performed to determine where significance existed. Relationships were determined via linear regression. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 for all analyses.

Discussion

It is well established that patients with HFpEF have a tachypneic breathing strategy during exercise as shown herein.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 High cardiac filling pressures during exercise are associated with this altered breathing strategy in HFpEF.3 The purpose of this study was to determine if pulmonary system abnormalities contribute to this tachypneic breathing strategy. Our primary novel finding is that patients with HFpEF have limited ventilatory reserve during exercise contributing to their tachypneic breathing strategy. A main contributor to the limited ventilatory reserve in HFpEF is the smaller FVC in these patients. Furthermore, both smaller FVC and higher fB during exercise were related to lower exercise capacity in patients with HFpEF, but not control participants. Our secondary purpose was to determine the potential influence of upper body adiposity on ventilatory reserve in patients with HFpEF. Our novel findings are that upper body adiposity was related to FVC and VT/FVC during submaximal exercise in patients with HFpEF, but not control participants. Taken together, our findings suggest that HFpEF-mediated reductions in lung capacity, in part due to upper body adiposity, result in limited ventilatory reserve during exercise directly contributing to the tachypneic breathing strategy in patients with HFpEF.

Our findings support that the smaller FVC in HFpEF had a significant impact on the breathing strategy during exercise. Specifically, patients with HFpEF had lower inspiratory and expiratory reserve volumes at rest and during exercise at 40 W compared with control participants. As a result, patients with HFpEF had less ventilatory reserve to increase VT and relied on a higher fB during submaximal exercise. At Vo2peak, the ventilatory reserve was not different between groups, with inspiratory reserve volumes reaching approximately 0.7 L for both groups. However, the smaller FVC in HFpEF directly contributed to the lower EILV compared with control participants at Vo2peak. The lower EILV in HFpEF coupled with the similar EELV elicited a smaller VT at Vo2peak in HFpEF compared with control participants. The operating lung volumes relative to lung size were not different in HFpEF than control participants at rest or during exercise. Patients with HFpEF have lower relative EILV (expressed as % of TLC) during submaximal exercise when HFpEF and control participants are compared at relative exercise intensities.4 Our findings are supported by a study reporting relationships between lung size and breathing pattern during exercise in patients with HFpEF and control participants.4 Villarraga et al4 reported lung size (expressed as % predicted TLC) was positively related with VT and negatively related with fB during submaximal and peak exercise in patients with HFpEF, but not control participants. We have also extended these findings by showing a greater tachypneic breathing strategy (as indicated by a higher fB/V˙co2) during exercise, in part due to the lower FVC, was associated with a lower Vo2peak in patients with HFpEF. Future studies with larger cohorts are necessary to determine the role of lung capacity and tachypneic breathing strategy on exercise capacity in HFpEF. High left-sided heart filling pressures during exercise also contribute to the altered breathing strategy in HFpEF (ie, attenuated increase in VT) likely via lower lung compliance, left atrial distention, impaired lung diffusion capacity, and/or worsening ventilation-perfusion mismatch.3, 4, 5,28,29

A secondary purpose of this study was to determine the potential influence of upper body adiposity on ventilatory reserve in patients with HFpEF. Overweight and obesity are highly prevalent (approximately 60%-80%) in patients with HFpEF.15 Furthermore, HFpEF is associated with altered regional adipose tissue distribution. In this context, patients with HFpEF exhibit greater abdominal (including both visceral and subcutaneous) and thigh adiposity than control participants.17, 18, 19 Consistent with this, trunk adiposity was significantly greater in patients with HFpEF than control participants in the present study. Overweight and obesity (assessed via BMI) are associated with reductions in functional residual capacity and expiratory reserve volume in an exponential fashion.20,30 In contrast, the impact of overweight and obesity on TLC and FVC are modest and more apparent with high degrees of obesity.20,30 The primary mechanism likely contributing to these pulmonary system alterations is the direct effect of trunk adiposity, particularly abdominal and rib cage adiposity, on diaphragm movement, chest wall properties, and lung inflation.20 In this study, trunk adiposity was not statistically related to FVC (% predicted or z score) or VT/FVC during submaximal exercise in control participants. In patients with HFpEF, trunk adiposity was negatively related to FVC (both % predicted and z score) and positively related to VT/FVC during submaximal exercise. Taken together, these findings suggest that the altered regional adipose tissue distribution and greater abdominal adiposity in HFpEF hastens the reduction in lung capacity for these patients. Future studies incorporating interventions to reduce abdominal adiposity (eg, dapagliflozin,31 incretins,32,33 caloric restriction34) are necessary to elucidate its impact on pulmonary function and breathing strategy in HFpEF.

Some methodologic considerations should be considered when interpretating data from this study. We acknowledge that N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide measurements were not included and the relatively small sample size of the control and HFpEF groups. The goal of this study was to compare the operating lung volumes at an absolute workload that simulates activities of daily living.1 However, the 40-W work rate represents an exercise intensity that is likely above the lactate threshold for a higher proportion of patients with HFpEF than control participants, subsequently resulting in an additional stimulus for ventilation. Future studies using a workload that is below (or above) the lactate threshold for all patients with HFpEF (and control participants) may provide additional insight into the contributing mechanisms to the tachypneic breathing strategy during exercise in HFpEF. Finally, the BMI of the patients with HFpEF included in this study is not representative of the range of BMI and obesity in the HFpEF population, which is typically higher in the United States. Future studies incorporating a larger cohort of patients with HFpEF and control participants with greater heterogeneity in BMI and trunk adiposity is necessary to confirm the findings of this study.

Interpretation

Patients with HFpEF were shown to have limited ventilatory reserve to increase VT during exercise, in part due to their smaller lung capacity, contributing to the tachypneic breathing strategy. Furthermore, our findings suggest that the greater distribution of upper body adiposity in HFpEF is an important contributor to the smaller lung capacity for these patients. Future studies are necessary to mechanistically investigate the impact of upper body adiposity on ventilatory limitations during exercise in patients with HFpEF.
