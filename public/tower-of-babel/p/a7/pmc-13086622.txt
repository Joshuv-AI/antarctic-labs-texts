Nomogram for Predicting the Risk of Post‐Craniotomy Intracranial Infection in Patients With Brain Tumors
Authors: Haohui Yu, Min Liu, Ni Yan, Wei Yang, Jun Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis study aims to develop a nomogram to predict the risk of post‐craniotomy intracranial infection (PCII) in patients with brain tumors.MethodsData from patients with brain tumors hospitalized between January 2021 and December 2023, encompassing personal attributes and clinical assessments, were compiled from the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University. Multifactorial analysis was employed to identify potential PCII risk factors. A nomogram model was crafted based on these risk factors. The model's predictive efficacy was evaluated using the concordance index (C‐index), area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and calibration curve. Additionally, PCII risk was forecasted at −3, −7, and −14 days post‐surgery.ResultsAmong 968 patients with brain tumors, 120 developed PCII post‐surgery, resulting in a 12.40% incidence rate. Via multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis and Akaike information criterion, nine PCII risk factors were identified, including BMI, surgical duration, preoperative albumin levels, hypertension, diabetes, indwelling drainage tube usage, subtentorial location, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, and intraoperative blood loss ≥ 400 mL. A nomogram model incorporating these predictors yielded a C‐index of 0.876. The AUC values for PCII at −3, −7, and −14 days were 0.787, 0.842, and 0.873, respectively. Notably, the calibration curve illustrated close alignment between predicted and actual infection rates in patients with brain tumors at −3, −7, and −14 days.ConclusionsWe developed a nomogram model to predict the occurrence of PCII in patients with brain tumors, facilitating the assessment of PCII risk in this patient population.Keywords: brain tumor, nomogram, post craniotomy intracranial infection, risk factors

Background

Hospital‐acquired infection (HI) refers to infections contracted while patients are hospitalized [1]. According to WHO reports, there were approximately 14 million people worldwide suffer from HI annually, with the highest rates in regions such as the Eastern Mediterranean (11.8%), Southeast Asia (10.0%), and the Western Pacific (9.0%) [2].

Post‐craniotomy intracranial infection (PCII) is a common acute central nervous system infection primarily caused by bacterial pathogens [3]. Studies have reported PCII incidence rates in neurosurgery ranging from 4.6% to 25.0% [3, 4]. PCII occurrence can lead to prolonged hospital stays and increased medical expenses, with untreated cases often resulting in serious complications or even fatalities [5, 6]. Research by the International Nosocomial Infection Control Consortium (INICC) revealed that intracranial infections contribute to a 30.0% increase in excess deaths among hospitalized patients globally and extend the average length of stay by 6.5 days [7]. In European countries alone, over 100,000 PCII cases occur annually, with each patient causing economic losses ranging from US$788 to US$18,747 [8, 9, 10].

PCII is multifactorial, with common risk factors including posterior fossa (subtentorial) surgery, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, prolonged surgical duration, and diabetes [3, 11]. Studies have shown that subtentorial surgery carries a higher PCII incidence and mortality rate compared to supratentorial surgery [3]. Notably, patients undergoing posterior fossa surgery for tumors such as gliomas, meningiomas, medulloblastomas, hemangioblastomas, and metastases are at heightened risk of infection, with mortality rates ranging from 3% to 33% [12, 13, 14, 15]. Given the severity of PCII and its multifactorial nature, early identification of high‐risk patients and implementation of effective prevention measures are crucial for reducing infection rates and patient mortality.

Currently, limited attention has been given to risk factors and predictive modeling for PCII in patients with brain tumors. Nomograms, which integrate multiple risk factors, have emerged as valuable prediction tools in medical research. This study investigates independent risk factors for PCII in patients with brain tumors and develops a nomogram model based on these factors, offering an effective tool for early identification and risk prediction of PCII in this patient population.

Method

Study Population and Inclusion Criteria

This study enrolled patients admitted to the Neurosurgery Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University from January 1, 2021 to December 31, 2023. Ethics approval was obtained from the hospital's Ethics Committee, and informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. Inclusion criteria comprised patients: (a) diagnosed with primary brain tumors; (b) undergoing craniotomy; (c) age over 18 years; (d) surviving postoperatively for more than 1 week; and (e) free from intracranial or brain infection upon admission. Exclusion criteria for patients: (a) patients with non brain tumors or with metastatic brain tumors; (b) no craniotomy surgery was performed; (c) age under 18 years; (d) death within one week after operation; (e) at admission (including within 48 h), the patient already had intracranial infection; (f) the patient undergoes non craniotomy surgery, such as spinal surgery or endoscopic intervention surgery. Out of 4495 admissions during this period, 968 patients met the eligibility criteria and were randomly allocated into training (677 patients, 70%) and validation (291 patients, 30%) sets at a 7:3 ratio. The patient selection process is illustrated in Figure S1. Intracranial infection was defined as infection occurring 48 h or later after admission. Observation endpoints included: (a) Date of confirmed intracranial infection during hospitalization; (b) Date of follow‐up completion without intracranial infection during hospitalization or within 30 days after surgery.

Definition of Intracranial Infection

The diagnosis of intracranial infection followed the diagnostic criteria established by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in the United States. Patients were diagnosed with intracranial infection if any of the following criteria were met: (a) isolation of pathogenic microorganisms from brain tissue or dura; (b) detection of abscess or intracranial infection evidence during surgical operation or histopathologic examination; (c) presentation of at least 2 of the following signs or symptoms without other identifiable causes: headache, dizziness, fever (> 38°C), focal neurological signs, altered level of consciousness, or confusion; coupled with any of the following conditions: (i) pathogen identification from brain or abscess tissue; (ii) positive antigen test in blood or urine; (iii) radiographic evidence of infection; (iv) diagnostic single antibody titer (IgM) or a four‐fold increase in paired sera (IgG) for the pathogen [16, 17].

Data Collection

Patient information, encompassing personal characteristics and clinical test data, was gathered from the hospital information system (HIS), electronic medical record (EMR), and laboratory information management system (LIS). Personal characteristics comprised the patient's hospitalization number, name, gender, age at diagnosis, height, and weight. Clinical test data included admission diagnosis, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, preoperative albumin level (ALB), surgical site, surgical duration, length of hospital stay, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, intraoperative blood loss, presence of indwelling drainage tube, occurrence of lumbar puncture, hypertension, diabetes, and blood lipid levels.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses in this study were conducted using R software (version 4.3.2). Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and group differences were assessed using t‐test or analysis of variance (ANOVA). Qualitative data were expressed as medians (or percentiles), with differences between groups compared using chi‐square tests or Fisher's exact tests. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was utilized to identify risk factors influencing the occurrence of PCII in patients with brain tumors, with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) calculated for each factor. Final predictor variables for the model were determined using the Akaike information criterion (AIC). Patients with brain tumors were randomly allocated into training and validation sets at a 7:3 ratio, where the training set was used for model development and the validation set for model validation. A nomogram was constructed based on the selected predictor variables, predicting PCII incidence at −3, 7, and −14 days post‐surgery. The concordance index (C‐index) was calculated using 1000 bootstrap resampling iterations. Model performance was assessed using calibration curves and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). All tests were two‐sided, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

The Characteristics of Patients

A total of 968 patients diagnosed with brain tumors who underwent craniotomy were enrolled in the study, among whom 120 developed intracranial infection, resulting in a PCII incidence of 12.40%. Among PCII infected patients, there were 17 cases infected within 3 days; 56 patients were infected within 4 to 7 days; 40 patients were infected within 7 to 14 days; 7 patients have been infected for more than 14 days. Of these patients, 544 were male, comprising 56.20% of the total cohort. The average age of the patients was 50.51 ± 15.02 years. Hypertension was present in 470 patients, accounting for 48.55% of the cohort, while 233 patients had diabetes, representing 24.07% of the total. Utilizing a 7:3 ratio, patients were divided into a training set (677 patients, 70%) and a validation set (291 patients, 30%). Screening of Potential Predictors

The results of both univariate analysis and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis are presented in Table 2. Nine potential risk factors influencing the occurrence of PCII in patients with brain tumors were identified, including BMI (HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.06–1.20), surgical duration (HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04), preoperative albumin levels (HR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.91–0.99), hypertension (HR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.06–2.77), diabetes (HR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.06–2.48), indwelling drainage tube (HR = 2.25, 95% CI: 1.01–5.04), surgical site (HR = 2.94, 95% CI: 1.82–4.75), cerebrospinal fluid leakage (HR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.02–2.71), and intraoperative blood loss (HR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.37–3.31). Factors such as age at diagnosis, sex, ASA score, NNIS score, preoperative white blood cell count, preoperative red blood cell count, preoperative hemoglobin levels, and preoperative blood lipid content were not found to be significantly associated with PCII occurrence (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Brain tumors are neoplasms that originate within the skull, encompassing types such as gliomas, meningiomas, and acoustic neuromas [18]. The incidence rate of brain tumors stands at approximately 4.5 per 100,000 person‐years, constituting around 1.6% of all tumor types [18, 19]. According to a report by Hyuna Sung et al. the global projection for new cases of brain tumor patients in 2020 is approximately 310,000, with an estimated 250,000 deaths [20]. Surgery represents a primary treatment modality for brain tumor patients, significantly extending their lifespan [21]. However, given the intricacies of brain structure and the complexity of nervous system functions, craniotomy, a common surgical intervention, is often associated with significant complexity, extensive trauma, and prolonged duration. Consequently, patients undergoing craniotomy frequently experience a myriad of complications, including intracranial infection, which may pose life‐threatening risks [20, 21, 22].

Due to the protective nature of the blood–brain barrier, meninges, skull, scalp, and surrounding tissues, the likelihood of intracranial infection is relatively low. However, when these protective barriers are compromised, such as in cases of craniotomy or trauma, the risk of intracranial infection significantly escalates. The incidence of post‐craniotomy intracranial infection (PCII) among patients with brain tumors ranges from approximately 5.2% to 28.1% [3, 23], with an average incidence rate of 12.4% in this study. Various studies have identified common high‐risk factors associated with PCII occurrence, including prolonged surgical duration, intraoperative blood loss, hypoalbuminemia, posterior fossa surgery, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, indwelling drainage tubes, and diabetes. Our research findings corroborate these findings [24, 25, 26].

Brain tumor resection surgery is often highly complex due to the tumor's location, size, invasiveness, and its anatomical relationship with surrounding neurovascular structures. This is particularly true for deep‐seated gliomas, large meningiomas, or tumors adjacent to critical functional areas, where surgical procedures often require several hours or more to complete [27]. Such prolonged surgical interventions first lead to prolonged exposure of brain tissue and the surgical field to airborne opportunistic pathogens, thereby significantly increasing the risk of pathogen colonization and intracranial invasion [20]. The complexity of brain tumor resection often induces ischemia and hypoxia in surrounding tissue due to prolonged surgery, triggering inflammatory responses [28]. Furthermore, surgery disrupts the integrity of brain tissue and the blood–brain barrier, with prolonged procedures exacerbating tissue damage and augmenting the likelihood of intracranial infection. Prolonged surgeries also escalate the risk of intraoperative blood loss, a confirmed risk factor for postoperative intracranial infection. Significant blood loss (exceeding 400 mL) during surgery may precipitate hypotension, leading to cerebral ischemia and hypoxia, thereby amplifying the risk of intracranial infection [25].

Albumin, the principal protein in human plasma, plays a crucial role in maintaining nutritional balance, osmotic pressure, and metabolite transport within the body. Decreased plasma albumin levels often indicate malnutrition and other underlying health issues [29]. Studies have demonstrated that malnutrition can heighten the risk of postoperative complications. Lu et al.'s research identified preoperative hypoalbumine‐mia as an independent risk factor for PCII occurrence [30], a finding consistent with our study results. Diminished plasma albumin levels decrease osmotic pressure and increase interstitial fluid, potentially leading to brain tissue edema and heightened intracranial infection risk [31]. Hence, enhancing patients' nutritional status before surgery through albumin infusions or high‐protein supplements may mitigate the occurrence of complications such as intracranial infection following surgery.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage refers to the escape of CSF from the cranial cavity to the exterior of the skull, commonly observed in cases of trauma and surgical procedures. It is widely acknowledged that CSF leakage constitutes a significant risk factor for intracranial infection [25, 32]. When a patient experiences CSF leakage without timely intervention, external pathogenic bacteria can easily breach the brain via the leak. CSF serves as a nutrient‐rich medium conducive to the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria. Once these bacteria infiltrate the brain, the risk of intracranial infection escalates, potentially leading to severe complications and even mortality [33]. Consequently, prompt and appropriate management of CSF leakage is paramount for reducing the incidence of intracranial infection and ensuring patient safety. Similarly, the presence of an indwelling drainage tube post‐surgery serves as a conduit between the brain and the external environment, thereby heightening the risk of external pathogen infiltration and subsequent PCII. Studies have indicated that prolonged retention of the drainage tube beyond 48 h increases the likelihood of retrograde infection by pathogenic bacteria [34, 35]. Therefore, expedited removal of the drainage tube minimizes the risk of intracranial infection.

When compared to supratentorial surgery, subtentorial (posterior cranial fossa) surgery poses a heightened risk of intracranial infection [36]. Our study corroborates this finding, identifying subtentorial surgery as an independent risk factor for PCII occurrence. Given that the posterior cranial fossa houses vital structures such as the cerebellum and brainstem, surgeries in this region are typically intricate and protracted, necessitating extended operation times. The thicker layers of skull, muscle, and adipose tissue in the posterior cranial fossa can result in various intraoperative challenges, including significant tissue trauma, surgical suturing difficulties, and fat liquefaction [36, 37]. Furthermore, patients undergoing posterior fossa surgery often assume a supine position postoperatively, leading to compression of the incision site tissue and heightened difficulty in wound care [38]. Infratentorial surgery increases the risk of PCII through mechanisms such as elevating the risk of cerebrospinal fluid leakage and accumulating local exudate [39]. These factors collectively render patients undergoing posterior fossa surgery more vulnerable to PCII.

Furthermore, our study identified hypertension and diabetes as additional risk factors for PCII in patients with brain tumors. As individuals age, the functionality of various organs gradually diminishes, accompanied by a decline in immune response and infection resistance. Compared to younger counterparts, older patients exhibit a heightened susceptibility to postoperative infections [25]. Underlying conditions such as hypertension and diabetes can induce alterations in the patient's vascular system and blood supply capacity [40, 41]. Diabetes, in particular, can impact peripheral vascular structure and microcirculation, thereby influencing the efficacy of antibacterial treatments [41]. Elevated blood sugar levels can also suppress the activity of neutrophils, T cells, and other immune cells, thereby compromising the patient's immune response and increasing infection risk [42]. Therefore, maintaining blood pressure and blood sugar levels within optimal ranges prior to surgery is imperative for mitigating the risk of postoperative infections.

Our research still has several limitations. First, tracking of intracranial infections was limited to the follow‐up endpoint of no infection during hospitalization or within 30 days postoperatively, which may miss infection events occurring more than 30 days after surgery and introduce information bias due to incomplete data coverage. Second, as a retrospective cohort study, its inherent limitations are unavoidable: on the one hand, there are unmeasured or missing confounding factors, such as the failure to systematically include analysis of steroid use, which may overlook its potential impact on PCII risk; on the other hand, selection bias and information bias may also affect the robustness of the results. Furthermore, this study adopted a single‐center design, with relatively homogeneous patient sources and limited distribution of postoperative PCII risk probabilities. Although the sample size is large, the calibration curve may still be biased due to excessive homogeneity of the population. In addition, single‐center data lack external validation support from multi‐center studies, making it difficult to fully verify the generalizability of the model. The study also lacks infection‐related microbiological data, with no clarification of specific pathogen types causing PCII, which to some extent limits in‐depth analysis of infection mechanisms. Based on these limitations, future studies should expand to include multi‐center patient cohorts from different countries or regions, improve prospective data collection to reduce missed confounding factors, and supplement microbiological testing data to clarify pathogen characteristics, thereby enhancing the predictive accuracy and clinical applicability of the nomogram for PCII in brain tumor patients.

Conclusion

This study has identified 9 independent risk factors influencing the occurrence of PCII in patients with brain tumors, encompassing BMI, hypertension, diabetes, preoperative albumin levels, subtentorial surgery, surgical duration, intraoperative blood loss, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, and indwelling drainage tubes. Based on these predictors, we developed a nomogram model for predicting PCII in patients. This model exhibits favorable predictive performance (high calibration, stable AUC), has initially demonstrated potential in aiding clinical risk assessment, and serves as a valuable tool for predicting PCII risk after craniotomy in brain tumor patients. It can be used as a risk stratification tool for intracranial infection after craniotomy to guide monitoring and prevention decisions for high‐risk patients.
