Fertility knowledge and employment status among Japanese pregnant women: J-PEARL study
Authors: Kyoko Namimatsu, Jun Takeda, Motoki Endo, Yuito Ueda, Shintaro Makino, Takeshi Tanigawa, Atsuo Itakura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDespite recent improvements, fertility knowledge among Japanese individuals remains substantially lower than in other countries. This study aimed to evaluate fertility knowledge among women with pregnancy and childbirth experience and assess differences based on employment status, given the increasing intersection of professional careers and reproductive decision making.MethodsA cross-sectional, multicenter questionnaire survey was administered to postpartum women at seven hospitals. The questionnaire assessed clinical and socioeconomic characteristics and incorporated the Cardiff fertility knowledge scale-Japanese version (CFKS-J). Participants were categorized as employed or non-employed and CFKS-J scores were calculated for both groups. Factors associated with greater fertility knowledge in each group were analyzed.ResultsOf the 963 postpartum women who participated in this study, 81% were employed during pregnancy. Employed participants had significantly higher fertility knowledge scores (60.0±21.5) than non-employed participants (55.8±22.8; P=0.03). Healthcare workers had the highest knowledge about fertility. Common misconceptions included the effects of mumps, obesity, and sexually transmitted diseases on fertility.ConclusionEmployed pregnant women had greater fertility knowledge than non-employed women. Factors linked to knowledge acquisition included education and the workplace environment. Improving access to fertility education among socially isolated groups, including the unemployed, remains essential.

Introduction

Fertility knowledge plays a crucial role in reproductive health worldwide, influencing decisions related to family planning, prevention of unintended pregnancies, and infertility management [1,2]. However, studies have reported considerable gaps in fertility knowledge across different populations. Notably, fertility knowledge is substantially lower in Japan than in other countries [3]. Bunting et al. [3] conducted an international survey using the Cardiff fertility knowledge scale (CFKS) and reported that Japan had the lowest average fertility knowledge score (below 40%) among countries with a “very high human development index (average score: 64.3%)” and even below the average for countries with a “not very high human development index”. Subsequent Japanese surveys indicated that CFKS scores improved slightly from below 40% to 44.4% among the general Japanese population; however, fertility knowledge remained comparatively low [4]. The CFKS-Japanese version (CFKS-J) identified factors associated with greater fertility knowledge, including being female, younger age, having a college education, attempting pregnancy, being a nonsmoker, having a higher family income, and possessing high health literacy [4]. Low fertility knowledge increases the risk of sexually transmitted infection [5], unintended teenage pregnancies [6], and infertility [7,8]. Increasing female workforce participation is correlated with delayed marriage, advanced maternal age [9–11] both of which are associated with fertility knowledge. Sustaining updated fertility knowledge in adulthood facilitates informed reproductive decision making amid expanding workforce engagement [12–14]. The relationships among employment status, workforce participation, preconception anxiety, and limited fertility knowledge remain poorly characterized.

This study hypothesized that working pregnant women, particularly those in regular employment, would demonstrate greater fertility knowledge than non-working pregnant women. This study aimed to evaluate fertility knowledge among women with pregnancy and childbirth experiences and to examine differences in fertility knowledge based on employment status. Greater fertility knowledge among working pregnant women could inform targeted sexual education programs for children and young adults.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

Participants were required to provide informed consent for participation in the questionnaire. This study was approved by the University Ethics Committee to which the authors belong (E21-0246).

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study.

Setting

This study was conducted as part of the Japan-pregnant women, employment, and actual radiant life (J-PEARL) study [15]. A cross-sectional, multicenter questionnaire survey was administered across four tertiary hospitals, one secondary hospital, and two primary hospitals in Japan. Two tertiary hospitals (which handle approximately 700 and 1,300 deliveries annually, respectively) and one primary hospital (which handle 300 deliveries annually) are located in Tokyo. The remaining tertiary hospitals (600 and 700 annual deliveries, respectively), secondary hospitals (200 deliveries annually), and other primary hospitals (600 deliveries annually) are located in the suburban areas of Tokyo. Tertiary hospitals are designated perinatal care centers, whereas other hospitals are community-based institutions. The survey was conducted sequentially between March and December 2022, and questionnaires were distributed over 6 months at each facility. Participants returned the completed questionnaires anonymously to their respective hospitals by mail. To ensure anonymity, including facility and regional identifiers, all questionnaires were collectively processed after collection.

Participants

The study population comprised postpartum mothers who delivered at the designated facility and received the study questionnaire. Participants who indicated their employment status were included and categorized based on their employment status. Patients with incomplete responses or missing CFKS-J data were excluded.

Variables

The content of the questionnaire was described in our earlier publication and included questions on the following: 1) demographic information, 2) obstetric history, 3) employment details, 4) CFKS-J [3,4] (Supplementary Material 1), 5) communicative and critical health literacy scale [16], and 6) Kessler 6-item psychological distress scale [17]. Educational level was classified as “high” for university and graduate school graduates and “low” for all other participants. Family income was dichotomized based on the average income of Japanese families with children [18].

Data sources/measurement

Most of the original questionnaire content was written in Japanese. Fertility knowledge was measured using the CFKS-J, which was translated into Japanese by Maeda et al. [4]. The scale consists of 13 questions, each of which has a possible response of “yes”, “no”, or “don’t know”. One point is given for each correct answer to a question; no points are awarded for an incorrect answer or for a response of “don’t know”. The total score was calculated by summing the scores for each item, dividing them by 13, and converting the result into a percentage that was used for the analysis. The internal consistency coefficient alpha (Cronbach’s α) for the original CFKS is 0.79 [3]. The internal consistency coefficient alpha for the CFKS-J is 0.72 [4], which indicates an acceptable level of internal consistency for research use.

Statistical methods

The mean±standard deviation (SD) or percentages were calculated to describe the characteristics of the full cohort and the employed and non-employed groups. Mean±SD CFKS-J scores were calculated for each variable category for the employed and non-employed groups. Normality was assessed using the t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney U-test for non-normally distributed data. Multivariate linear regression analysis (forced-entry method) was performed to identify the factors associated with CFKS-J scores in the employed and non-employed groups. Variables included in the model were those previously associated with the CFKS-J scores or identified as significant in the univariate analysis. Multicollinearity was evaluated using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values, with tolerance <0.1 or VIF >10 generally considered indicative of problematic multicollinearity. Furthermore, to assess whether the associations between the predictors and CFKS-J scores differed according to employment status, we constructed a general linear model that included interaction terms between the group (employed vs. non-employed) and each predictor. The correct response rates for the CFKS-J items were analyzed separately for each group. The missing values for each variable were less than 2% and were therefore ignored. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS for Windows version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R Studio (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Two-tailed P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1,810 postpartum women participated in the J-PEARL study, and 975 (54%) responded to the questionnaire (Fig. 1). Data were obtained from 963 respondents. Of these, 43 were excluded because they did not complete all CFKS-J items or failed to report their employment status, leaving 920 participants for the final analysis. Among them, 745 (81.0%) were employed at the time of pregnancy, while 175 (19.0%) were not employed. We excluded 43 women who did not answer all the CFKS-J questions or reported their employment status.

Table 1 describes the characteristics of the participants for the overall population and the employed and non-employed groups. A higher proportion of participants were younger than 35 years; however, the average family income exceeded the national average for the child-rearing generation in Japan, and most participants lived in Tokyo. The abortion rate was slightly higher among non-employed participants. Approximately 35% of the participants in each group underwent infertility treatment. The overall level of education was high; however, a greater proportion of employed participants had a higher educational level. A total of 80% of participants were employed and 82% of them had regular employment. Among the employed participants, 19.1% were healthcare workers, and over 80% were employed regularly.

The average CFKS-J score for the full population was 58.9±21.8. The average scores for employed participants and non-employed participants were 60.0±21.5 and 55.8±22.8, respectively, and these scores were significantly different (P=0.03) (Fig. 2). Among employed participants, regular employees scored significantly higher than non-regular employees (60.6±21.3, 55.5±22.1; P=0.012), and healthcare workers scored significantly higher than those in other occupations (68.3±19.5, 57.7±21.4; Fig. 3). Among healthcare workers, regular employees scored 66.9±19.7 on the CFKS-J and non-regular employees scored 70.2±21.0; there was no significant differences between-groups.

Table 2 lists the CFKS-J scores according to variable categories. For the total population, scores were higher among participants who were nulliparous, had not experienced abortion, had received infertility treatment, were married, had a high level of education, had higher family income (≥8.13 million yen), lived in Tokyo, scored highly on Kessler 6 Mental Health Scale (K6) score (≥9), and had higher health literacy (communicative and critical health literacy scale score ≥4). Among employed participants, the pattern of higher scores for participants with specific characteristics was almost the same as that for the total population, and almost the same for participants in regular employment. In contrast, among non-employed participants, higher scores were shown by participants who were nulliparous, had received infertility treatment, had higher education, and who scored highly on the K6 score.

The results of the multivariate linear regression analysis of the CFKS-J scores according to employment status are shown in Table 3. In the employed group, the CFKS-J score was significantly associated with experience of infertility treatment, high education level, high K6 score, and high health literacy. In contrast, the CFKS-J score was significantly associated with nulliparous status and high education level in the non-employed group. In addition, the interaction analysis revealed that the associations with CFKS-J scores differed significantly by employment status for parity (P<0.05), infertility treatment (P<0.001), education (P<0.001), K6 score (P<0.005), and health literacy (P<0.005). No significant interactions were observed for age category, abortion history, or residence (Table 4). In this study, all variables had tolerance values greater than 0.80 and VIF values below 1.18, suggesting no concerns regarding multicollinearity.

There was a similar trend between the employed and non-employed groups in terms of the correct response rate for each CFKS-J question (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). In both groups, the three questions with low correct response rates asked about indicators of reduced fertility: “No. 9 if a man has had mumps after puberty, he is more likely to later have a fertility problem”, “No. 11 if a woman is overweight by more than 13 kg (2 stone or 28 pounds), then she may not be able to get pregnant”, and “No. 13 people who have had a sexually transmitted disease are likely to have reduced fertility”. Healthcare professionals had corrected response rates for questions about diseases (No. 9, No. 13), but not for No. 10 (amenorrhea and fertility knowledge), for which the overall group had correct response rates.

Discussion

In this study, we found that employed women demonstrated greater fertility knowledge than non-employed women. Among the employed women, those in regular employment had greater fertility knowledge than those in non-regular employment. Furthermore, healthcare workers had greater fertility knowledge than non-healthcare workers. However, among healthcare workers, fertility knowledge levels were similar, regardless of employment status.

The CFKS-J scores of the employed and non-employed groups differed according to parity and abortion history. Educational level emerged as a significant factor for both employed and non-employed participants. Interaction analysis demonstrated that the strength of this association varied according to employment status. Sex education remains inadequate in Japan’s compulsory education system. The Hadome Kitei (“Stopping Rule”) [19], which is unique to Japanese education, restricts instruction about conception-related processes, thus preventing comprehensive sex education. This study showed that higher education increases fertility knowledge and supplements the limited sex education provided in Japan. However, despite this supplementation by higher education, the fertility knowledge gap between Japan and other countries persists.

In the employed group, higher fertility knowledge was associated with fewer abortions; however, this association was not observed in the non-employed group. However, the interaction analysis revealed no significant group differences, suggesting that the impact of fertility knowledge on abortion history does not differ substantially by employment status. Fertility knowledge may help reduce engagement in risky sexual behaviors, thereby lowering abortion rates [6,20–22]. Nonetheless, the lack of a significant interaction implies that abortion history is likely to be influenced by factors beyond employment-related constraints. In the multivariate analysis, most associations between fertility knowledge and other factors among employees were consistent with those previously identified [3,4,23,24]. A novel finding was the significant association between high fertility knowledge and anxiety. Contrary to previous research linking low health literacy to strong anxiety due to misinformation [25], greater knowledge about pregnancy and childbirth may be associated with increased anxiety. This anxiety may stem from the dilemma experienced by working women in choosing between career advancement and optimal timing of pregnancy and childbirth [26,27]. Despite the benefits of early conception, increased awareness of the career limitations associated with pregnancy and childbirth may contribute to greater anxiety about balancing work and family planning. However, this association may have been limited in this study, as the estimated difference in the association between the CFKS-J and K6 scores was 4.6 points, which is less than 7.7 points representing one question.

Among employed pregnant women, those with regular employment had significantly greater fertility knowledge than those with non-regular employment. Regular employees are more likely to have higher education levels, greater family income, and reside in urban areas, factors associated with fertility knowledge in previous studies [3,4,23,24]. Additionally, attempting to maintain their careers during pregnancy may motivate employees to actively seek fertility-related information. Workplace environments may also facilitate access to in-house fertility training [28,29]. By contrast, previous reports have indicated that non-regular employees are more likely to resign during pregnancy [15]. When facing pregnancy- and childbirth-related challenges, non-regular employees are more inclined to resign rather than seek further fertility-related knowledge.

The high levels of fertility knowledge among the employed participants could be attributed to the presence of healthcare workers in this group. Analysis of occupational differences indicated that healthcare workers had greater fertility knowledge than non-healthcare workers. Non-healthcare workers demonstrated lower accuracy in questions related to fertility decline, particularly regarding the effects of pubertal mumps on male infertility, excess weight (>13 kg, 28 pounds, or 2 stones) on female fertility, and the association between sexually transmitted infections and reduced fertility. Similar response patterns were observed in a Japanese survey conducted a decade ago [4] and in more recent surveys from other countries, suggesting that these items require specialized medical knowledge for accurate responses [30–32]. Additionally, because the survey targeted individuals who had already become pregnant, the awareness of fertility decline may not have been prioritized. Limited fertility education during compulsory schooling and restricted opportunities for subsequent learning may contribute to delayed conception times [33]. However, the present study also identified a bias in fertility knowledge unique to healthcare workers. Although healthcare workers showed adequate knowledge of disease-related fertility factors (Q9 and Q13), they showed lower accuracy for questions addressing the relationship between amenorrhea and fertility (Q10). Despite their extensive medical knowledge, healthcare workers may overestimate medical interventions while addressing their limitations [34].

This study had several limitations. First, the sample sizes of employed and non-employed women differed, which may have affected the identification of factors associated with significant differences within each group. This study did not directly compare the two groups but analyzed the relevant factors independently. However, given the increasing number of working women in Japan, this disparity in sample size may reflect current demographic trends and, therefore, may be within acceptable limits. Second, the use of a questionnaire survey may have introduced a recall bias. We aimed to administer the survey immediately after delivery to minimize bias. The CFKS-J reflects participants’ knowledge at the time of delivery and may also capture the knowledge obtained during pregnancy. Nevertheless, low scores suggest that minimal or no additional fertility knowledge was obtained during pregnancy.

In conclusion, employed pregnant women demonstrated greater fertility knowledge than their non-employed counterparts, with knowledge levels approaching the global standards reported a decade ago. This study identified several factors associated with fertility knowledge acquisition, particularly those linked to societal engagement, such as educational settings and workplace environment. Expanding educational opportunities that promote fertility knowledge, ranging from compulsory to higher education and postgraduate societal interactions, is essential.
