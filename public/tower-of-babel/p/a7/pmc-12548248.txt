Unequal budgeting and limited WHO AWaRe Access antibiotics in Punjab: procurement policy gaps in Pakistan’s National Action Plan against antimicrobial resistance
Authors: Shairyar Afzal, Mishal Bajwa, Mian Shahzeb Haroon, Huda Arooj, Rana Muhammad Zahid Mushtaq, Zikria Saleem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProcurement-side stewardship — unaddressed by Pakistan’s National Action Plan (NAP) against antimicrobial resistance (AMR) — is critical in ensuring access to needed antibiotics to curb AMR. However, a fragmented procurement model, disconnected from updated clinical evidence and inconsiderate towards budgetary allocation, is being implemented in Pakistan, affecting access to antibiotics. This study seeks to understand the implications of the current procurement policy on access to antibiotics.MethodThis study offers a descriptive content analysis of the Standard Medicine List (SML), a procurement policy currently implemented in Punjab’s primary and secondary healthcare facilities (PSHFs). Using procurement data from one of the DHA’s of Rawalpindi division and budget appropriation documents from the fiscal year (FY) 2023–2024, we evaluated the total cost, defined daily dose (DDDs), and cost per DDD of procured antibiotics in Punjab. We also measured the percentage of spending on antibiotics for the secondary care sector.ResultsOur analysis includes 2970 PSHFs of Punjab. Our findings suggest that SML does not ensure consistent access to antibiotics because budgetary allocations do not correspond with SML-advised quantities. If the SML-advised quantities were procured, 85% of the PSHFs’ medicine budget would be consumed; however, for primary care solely, 122.5% of the medicine budget is required to procure antibiotics as per SML-indicated quantities. The total Access category DDDs procured in Punjab as per SML-advised quantities is only 44%. Furthermore, key Access category antibiotics, such as Amoxicillin, are subject to conditional procurement or are missing entirely. Overall, SML promoted the WHO AWaRe Watch category antibiotic use, with ceftriaxone being the most procured antibiotic, consuming 20.7% (1.41 billion PKR) of the total budget spending as per SML-advised quantity.ConclusionSML is misaligned with the core concept of the WHO AWaRe system and the recent UNGA target of 70% Access category antibiotic consumption. The study elucidates that PSHFs lack sufficient funds to procure SML-advised quantities, highlighting its incompatibility with the budgetary grant, which affects consistent access to antibiotics. There is a need to recalibrate the procurement policy and leverage Pakistan’s NAP to propagate a national antibiotic procurement framework to restrain AMR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01720-2.

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has threatened global health security, contributing to 4.71 million deaths in 2021 [1]. Inappropriate use and sub-optimal access to safe, effective, quality, and affordable antibiotics are major propellants of AMR, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Pakistan, due to weakened health infrastructure, regulatory inadequacies, socio-economic disparity, lack of antibiotic use surveillance, lack of evidence-informed policies around antibiotic use and procurement resulting in stockouts and government’s affordability [2–11]. Consistent access to antibiotics hinges on an efficient procurement module and a fertile supply chain mechanism leveraged by stewardship protocols to combat AMR [12–16]. The principles of good antibiotic procurement involve an evidence-based antibiotic formulary, considering the local susceptibility profile and updated antibiotic use guidelines to ensure appropriate use [2, 17].

Recently, in the United Nations General Assembly (UNGA) high-level meeting on AMR, global leaders made commitments to adopt the World Health Organization (WHO) Access-Watch-Reserve (AWaRe) system as a stewardship guardrail to ensure the appropriate use and surveillance of antibiotics to curb AMR. The WHO AWaRe system classifies antibiotics into three categories: Access (low resistance potential, affordable, expected to be widely available to all, typical first- or second-line therapy), Watch (high resistance potential, broad-spectrum), and Reserve (last resort choice for multidrug-resistant infections) [18–21]. In 2022, WHO launched the AWaRe book, which offers treatment guidelines for 34 infections and outlines evidence-informed first and second-line treatment choices focusing on Access antibiotics [20, 22]. Furthermore, the UNGA high-level meeting on AMR recommended that 70% of Access antibiotics be used globally by 2030 [21]. Despite this, there is an escalating trend in the consumption of Watch category antibiotics in LMICs [23], including Pakistan.

Pakistan has been a subject of AMR crisis, with greater than 20,000 deaths attributable to AMR annually, with antibiotic consumption soaring from 24.8 defined daily dose/1000 inhabitants per day (DIDs) in 2019 to 44.1 DIDs in 2021, as reported by a recent study [24, 25]. Pakistan struggles to curtail inappropriate antibiotic prescribing, with trends disproportionately shifted towards Watch category antibiotics, resulting in falling behind the committed target of 60% Access category antibiotics use against total consumption, nationally (previous target) [24, 26–29]. Viewing antibiotic procurement as a linear budget-supply or demand-supply mismatch lessens its importance. Rather, antibiotic procurement and supply chain management are salient features of the WHO’s resilient healthcare system [30, 31], and must be integrated with WHO’s health systems building blocks [32]. However, at present, these also remain unaddressed subjects in Pakistan’s National Action Plan (NAP) against AMR (2017) [33]. This gap is also identified as a significant barrier to access to antibiotics and the fight against AMR. Our recent study showed that a fragmented procurement model, disconnected from updated clinical evidence, is being implemented in Pakistan, and is limiting access to antibiotics [2]. This study offers a descriptive account of the currently implemented procurement policy in Punjab regarding antibiotics, its implications for consistent access to antibiotics, and the allocated budget. This study also explores the conformity of procurement policy with the WHO AWaRe system and its ability to ensure promised Access category antibiotic use.

Methods

Study context and setting

This study is a follow-up to our qualitative study [2]. The qualitative report has already discussed in-depth details of our study setting and procurement process. A brief context is given here. Punjab is the largest province of Pakistan by population, accounting for more than 50% of Pakistan’s population, and leads the rest of the provinces concerning governance and policy initiatives. The health sector of Punjab province is bifurcated into two health ministries: the Primary and Secondary Healthcare Department (PSHD) and the Specialized Healthcare and Medical Education Department. Figure S1 represents an organogram of the PSHD and the number of hospitals under each domain. The PSHD follows a semi-centralized procurement model. At the provincial level, the procurement cell of the Directorate General Health Services (DGHS) performs prequalifications and propagates procurement policies. Procurement is done in accordance with the Punjab Procurement Rules (PPR, 2014) [34]. At the district level, the Chief Executive Officer (CEO) steers the District Health Authority (DHA), which comprises primary and secondary healthcare facilities (PSHFs) and other related health subsidiaries. In Punjab, two medicine procurement modes are followed: bulk purchase at the DGHS and DHA levels and Local Purchase (LP) at the hospital level.

Standard medicine list (SML)

Bulk purchase follows the Standard Medicine List (SML), a procurement policy set forth by the DGHS to carry out bulk procurement throughout the province (Additional file 1) [35]. The SML is a policy document that provides a list of medicines to be procured at primary and secondary level hospitals and mandates to be followed. The SML also identifies indicative quantities for each medicinal item and the level of care. Facilities can only deviate ± 30% from the SML-dictated quantities as per the policy. The SML segregates primary care and secondary care levels into three and two broad categories, respectively. Under the secondary care level, two broader categories (District Headquarter Hospital [DHQ] and Tehsil Headquarter Hospital [THQ]) are further categorized according to bed strength of the health facilities into four sub-categories: A, B, C, and D. Furthermore, the quantities of antibiotics for each sub-category differ from each other. Primary care level is subdivided into Rural Health Center (RHC), Basic Health Unit (BHU) 24/7, and Basic Health Unit (BHU). In a very recent development, the PSHD was merged with the Population Welfare Department and renamed to the Health and Population Department. However, there has been no change in administrative work relevant to the scope of this study. As the study was completed prior to this merger, we proceeded with the former name.

Study design and analysis

This study undertook a descriptive content analysis of SML about systemic antibiotics and their indicative quantities. Firstly, we extracted and classified each antibiotic in the SML according to the WHO AWaRe classification [38]. We also identified the distribution of antibiotics by level of care (primary and secondary level) and formulation (Intravenous [IV] and Oral) and calculated the Access-to-Watch Index (AWI) for each level of care and for different categories of health facilities in the secondary level tier by utilizing the SML indicative quantities (volume-based) and rate per unit (value-based) of antibiotics up for procurement and subsequent use. AWI was calculated by dividing the total quantity of Access antibiotics in the numerator by the total quantity of Watch antibiotics in the denominator. Similarly, for the value-based AWI calculation, we used the sum of the rate per unit for all Access antibiotics divided by the sum of the rate per unit for all Watch antibiotics.

We further collected procurement data from one DHA in the Rawalpindi division for the fiscal year (FY) 23–24, out of the total 36 DHAs in the Punjab, as a representative of the province. The rationale for choosing this division was due to the highest number of antibiotics procured as per the SML list. Moreover, the purpose of the study was not the comparative analysis of the procurement status as per the SML in different DHAs across Punjab; rather, the focus was to highlight whether the SML-indicative quantities of antibiotics for different levels of care are budget-adjusted or not. The rate/unit of each antibiotic was determined from the rate award contract of the tender-winning bids. We analyzed the total antibiotic cost for each SML class and each level of care, and then for Punjab as a whole. Initially, we compared the total antibiotic cost according to the SML for both primary and secondary care with the total budget allocated to both sectors. Then, in the ensuing step, we determined the percentage of the budget (with ± 30% range) that would be spent if SML-advised quantities of antibiotics were procured for the secondary care facility by utilizing the budgetary allocation for each facility.

We also classified all SML antibiotics according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification (ATC)/DDD methodology [39]. We estimated the Defined Daily Dose (DDD), which is a unit used to evaluate antibiotic consumption by using the ATC/DDD index 2025 for all SML antibiotics, and reported the total DDDs of antibiotics procured as per SML for Punjab (with ± 30% range) and DDD-Cost for antibiotics procured in FY 23–24. We also reported the DDDs according to the WHO AWaRe classification.

Descriptive analysis was done utilizing Microsoft Excel® 2021, and graphs were prepared using R Studio (R version 4.4.1).

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Human Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Pharmacy, Department of Pharmacy Practice, Bahauddin Zikariya University, Multan (BZU-FOPDPP-2467).

Our analysis compares the SML with the WHO AWaRe classification and enumerates antibiotics according to different categories. Of the 24 formulation-wise antibiotics listed in the SML, 14 are active ingredients. Oral antibiotics predominate with 14 entries, of which nine antibiotics are from the WHO Access group, and the Watch group accounted for five antibiotics. On the other hand, 10 antibiotics belong to the IV class, among which the Watch group predominate with six antibiotics compared to three from the Access group. Furthermore, there is only one antibiotic from the WHO AWaRe Not-Recommended group, and the SML lacks antibiotics from the Reserve group. Discussion

This study is the first empirical quantification of procurement-induced antibiotic access gaps under AWaRe guidance in Pakistan. The study findings indicate that a substantial portion of the budget (85%) in PSHFs would be consumed in purchasing antibiotics as per the SML. Primary care would exceed its allocated budget (122.56%), along with 30% of secondary care facilities, if rigid compliance with the SML regarding antibiotic quantities is ensured. Moreover, SML favours Watch antibiotic use, particularly among IV dosage forms in PSHFs, with IV ceftriaxone dominating other antibiotics in the indicative quantity listed in the SML and, as a result, consumes a considerable budgetary proportion (20.75%). SML is also devoid of key Access antibiotics, which are indicated as a first-line agent for many commonly reported infections, and thus shifts the burden to broad-spectrum, expensive, and non-prudent treatment options.

Antibiotic procurement – a missing piece in NAP Pakistan

Our study signifies repercussions of a critical gap in Pakistan’s NAP regarding sustainable procurement and supply chain management systems to ensure consistent access to antibiotics, unlike Nigeria’s and India’s [40] NAPs. The health sector lacks an efficient procurement mechanism to look forward to, which results in adopting substandard procurement policies such as the SML, ultimately compromising consistent access to antibiotics. Moreover, SML appears to be at odds with the WHO AWaRe classification, despite its adoption in the National Essential Medicine List (EML) 2023 [41] and provincial EML 2022 [42], and fails to reflect the national and provincial policy decisions. This points towards the weak implementation of NAP Pakistan at the provincial level and its incomplete nature in viewing antibiotic procurement as an unimportant component in combating AMR [5, 43]. The NAP of Pakistan requires comprehensive revisions which can address Pakistan’s circumstantial nuances.

AWaRe implementation gaps

In our qualitative report [2], we discussed that there are no currently implemented antibiotic use policies or standard treatment guidelines. In this scenario, the WHO AWaRe book can be embraced as empirical evidence to guide prescribing against common infectious ailments that appear at PSHFs [22, 44]. Therefore, we evaluated the concordance regarding the treatment options provided by the SML compared to the WHO AWaRe book. We compared SML antibiotics with first-and second-line therapies for infectious diseases covered by the WHO AWaRe book and extracted the missing Access antibiotics. Interestingly, SML prohibits and/or offers conditional procurement of amoxicillin adult formulations (capsules and tablets) for the secondary care sector. Amoxicillin is the first-line treatment for respiratory tract infections (RTIs), as guided by the WHO [45]. Dropping this critical antibiotic has financial implications for secondary care facilities, as it requires the acquisition of an expensive broad-spectrum oral variant, amoxicillin + clavulanic acid (costing 0.53 billion PKR). Moreover, this approach will ultimately hamper the trajectory to achieve United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) target 3.8, access to ‘affordable’ essential medicines for all [45, 46]. While discussing economic implications, we would also highlight another key Access antibiotic, cefazolin, which is missing in the SML. Cefazolin is the first choice for surgical prophylaxis owing to its optimum efficacy, proven safety, and low cost [22, 47]. The lack of cefazolin compels physicians to opt for expensive broad-spectrum antibiotics for surgical prophylaxis, particularly ceftriaxone, which increases procurement costs and the AMR burden [48].

Budgetary misalignment

Antibiotics exhaust a hefty budgetary portion of PSHFs in Punjab, similar to a study from Vietnam [49]. We analyzed facility-wise percentage antibiotic spending against the bed strength category for the secondary care sector. The rationale for doing so for only secondary care was based on the availability of facility-wise budget data compared to primary care, where the budget amount for the district’s primary care health facilities is a cumulative figure released and placed under the disposal of the office of the CEO of each district. Budget allocation to hospitals under PSHD follows a historical pattern, ignoring population growth, disease burden of the region. Our analysis suggests that the budget allocated to facilities is not proportionate to the quantities indicated in the SML for each health facility under each bed strength category. 30% of facilities (smaller bed-strength facilities) need more than 100% of the budget to procure SML-advised antibiotic quantities. Moreover, SML requires more than 50% of facilities to spend 51–99% of their budgets on 21/24 SML-notified antibiotics. In comparison, larger facilities may have more budgetary space, where less than 20% of those spend 26–50% of their budgets on antibiotics. Additionally, within the identical bed-strength category, there are stark variabilities in budgetary allocation among facilities. This highlights the lack of consideration of the economic ramifications of implementing SML-notified quantities. Access to antibiotics is closely tied to budgetary grants [50]. There is a discernible incongruence between budgetary allocations across facilities and the SML, representing a fragmented approach of the PSHD in providing optimum health to the province. A study from Ethiopia [51] corroborated similar findings and termed the public sector procurement policy “an incongruous government-enforced procurement policy”. The study discussed that budgetary limitations and stringent procurement policies affect access to essential medicines and supplies, leading to poor health outcomes. In the past, a Pakistani study [52] has also highlighted budgetary constraints in public health institutions and held suboptimal procurement procedures responsible for frequent stockouts, which ultimately restrict access to essential medicines. The SML propagates supply-driven procurement, which conflicts with a data-driven approach to procurement and ultimately ignores the stewardship principle of tailored antibiotic use as per susceptibility pattern and patient need. Our qualitative study also discussed that in hospitals, available antibiotics drive prescribing [2].

Overuse of Watch antibiotics – implications for AMR

Overall, our findings are consistent with earlier studies from Pakistan [48, 53–55] and worldwide studies [29, 56, 57], which include Pakistan in their analysis, that Watch category antibiotic consumption is high in Pakistan. SML supports the procurement of antibiotics in the Watch category, including oral and IV formulations. Ciprofloxacin and ceftriaxone (Watch antibiotics) are among the top-most procured agents. Moreover, SML exhibits a markedly high Watch category antibiotic consumption trend in IV formulation with AWI of 0.3 and 0.7 in primary and secondary care, respectively. However, overall, SML shows an AWI of 1.0, which is consistent with a study from China (0.91 AWI) [58]. Our recent scoping review [23] also concluded high Watch category utilization, particularly among inpatients in public sector hospitals of LMICs, with ciprofloxacin and ceftriaxone being the most consumed antibiotics, analogous to this study. The review strongly advocates the integration of the WHO AWaRe system into the formularies, which should be the key consideration of policy-makers in Punjab at this stage concerning the SML is discouraging Access category antibiotic use, 44% of total DDDs procured in Punjab, further strengthens our argument against the current policy decision of strict adherence to the SML and also exhibit contrast with the commitments made in the recent UNGA high-level meeting on AMR to promote Access category antibiotic use to about 70% at the global level [21]. Song et al. [58] reported comparable Access antibiotic consumption of 46.8% for the Sichuan province of Western China; the study also aids us in better contextualizing our results.

Of note, ceftriaxone is the most consumed antibiotic, and its procurement costs 1.41 billion PKR, which is 20.75% of the total budget spent on antibiotics as per SML-advised quantity across the PSHFs of Punjab, which is also comparable with other studies [54, 57, 59, 60]. Our analysis highlights the disparity in the preference for broad-spectrum Watch category antibiotics and divergence from international commitments by comparing injectable amoxicillin + clavulanic acid and ceftriaxone units indicated in the SML. Interestingly, ceftriaxone is the only IV injectable option available at the BHU level.

As discussed in our previous report [2], the selection of antibiotics in SML is not based on representative local AMR data. Numerous studies have reported resistance in Salmonella Typhi, Salmonella Paratyphi, Klebsiella Pneumoniae, and Escherichia Coli against ceftriaxone and ciprofloxacin in Pakistan [61–65]. Sabahat et al. reported an astounding 82.8% resistance rate of ceftriaxone against E. Coli from the urine and pus samples collected from hospital settings [66]. We correlate these findings with the extensive use of and quantity indicated for procurement in the SML for PSHFs in Punjab. With high consumption rates of ceftriaxone (11.62 million units) and ciprofloxacin (63 million units, all formulations) indicated as per SML across the PSHFs of Punjab, such rampant and erratic use will likely have ramifications for increased selection pressure on pathogens to develop AMR [67–69].

SML mimics hospital formulary in ensuring transparency and uniformity in the procurement process across the province, meanwhile restricting wishlist or influenced prescribing in an attempt to rationalize medication use [70], but without considering the current susceptibility profile of pathogens, resistance prevalence in different localities and hospital settings, expert consensus, particularly pharmacists on antibiotic selection and evidence-based antibiotic use policy, making it less of a formulary and more of an administrative policy without a concrete scientific foundation [2, 71]. However, assigning indicative quantities to each antibiotic for each type of health facility and enforcing them to remain in the stipulated range (± 30%) in SML signifies an over-generalization of the antibiotic demand setting process, disease epidemiology, and, more importantly, inconsideration of economic implications, which is also in contrast to the core concept of formulary formulation [71].

SML’s disconnect from the evidence base

Access to antibiotics is a major challenge in PSHFs, driven primarily by a flawed procurement mechanism based on SML policy, which we had established in our preceding qualitative report [2]. There is a critical need for a systematic appraisal of the SML as a formulary and demand-setting tool. Evidence-based healthcare policy-making materializes under the ambit of health technology assessment (HTA), which determines the safety, effectiveness, clinical need, and pharmacoeconomic evaluation of a healthcare policy or technology [72, 73]. Our analysis suggests that SML-advised antibiotic quantities would consume 85% (60% − 111%) of the allocated medicine budget for PSHFs combined; however, alone, primary care facilities would spend almost 122.56% (86% − 159%), which is impractical considering that there are other pharmaceutical needs as well. SML-suggested indicative quantities do not reflect the actual demand of the facilities [2], and with finite resources, as this study suggests, medicine provision and access seem to be compromised, which points towards a broader lapse in governance. Therefore, we recommend that the SML should be appropriately evaluated on HTA principles for it to be an effective policy that could deliver adequate access to antibiotics to Punjab’s healthcare facilities. Evidence suggests that HTA is effectively being utilized in formulary decision-making in Malaysia [73, 74] and Singapore [75]. Although HTA requires financial and technical human resources and, importantly, local representative data (AMR, disease burden, and economic evaluation data) to make informed recommendations, which is currently a challenge in evidence generation; however, its benefits outweigh its drawbacks [72, 76]. Moreover, adopting a mini HTA modality like Malaysia or commissioning a formulary management program like Singapore at the provincial level [73–75] or any methodology involving a provincial pharmacy and therapeutics committee, or any relevant department for that matter, responsive to optimum public health objectives would be an effective alternative initially. There is a need to institutionalize HTA in Punjab’s healthcare system by the provincial government (Health ministries of Punjab) and formulate an HTA advisory committee as per the National Health Vision 2016–2025, and build capacity in terms of health information systems, technical and organizational resources over time to make evidence-informed and data-driven decisions [77].

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, our estimated figures of antibiotic procurement, use, and expenditure are representative of PSHFs, strictly based on the SML-indicated quantities, and do not reflect the actual status of the whole province. Secondly, SML is employed as a procurement tool only in health facilities under PSHD administration; this administrative policy does not bind tertiary care hospitals and other government entities. Therefore, our results do not represent antibiotic use in these facilities. Thirdly, the total cost estimated in our analysis is based on antibiotics procured (21/24 antibiotics) through bulk procurement mode at the rate/unit awarded in the Rawalpindi division for FY 23–24. Therefore, variability in the rate/unit and number of total antibiotics awarded rate contracts is likely a possibility between different divisions in Punjab, which limits the scope of our analysis. Fourthly, our analysis does not consider antibiotics procured through the local purchase (LP) route, contributing an additional 25% of the bulk medicine budget, which is a missing part of the puzzle. Lastly, this study acknowledges that budgetary data for approximately 19% of the secondary health facilities could not be obtained, which could induce a bias in our analysis.

Despite multiple limitations, we believe that our analysis presents a thorough picture of SML policy implications on antibiotic procurement in around 2825 primary care setups and 145 secondary care facilities, which is a reasonable representation of the primary and secondary healthcare sectors. Our study robustly establishes that SML-advised quantities are not adjusted according to the budgets of health facilities. Therefore, budgetary constraints hinder consistent access to antibiotics and subsequently present a challenge in implementing an optimal stewardship program [2]. Our study offers sound evidence and rings alarm bells at the decision-making table to recalibrate the antibiotic selection process because SML promotes Watch category antibiotic usage, which is concerning for meeting UNGA’s recommended Access category antibiotic use target of 70%.

Practical implications

Our studies could serve as torchbearers in providing robust evidence for evaluating SML and reshaping its formulation to ensure consistent access to antibiotics for optimal treatment outcomes. Empirical evidence also favors an independent antibiotic formulary to ensure compliance with stewardship activities [78]. We propose a careful selection of antibiotics based on the local susceptibility profile and updated clinical and pharmacoeconomic evidence for a dedicated antibiotic formulary [79], assessed on HTA principles, enforcing a bespoke antibiotic use policy integrated with the WHO AWaRe system to ensure continuous antibiotic use surveillance and stewardship activities. The formulary should be inclusive and flexible enough to adapt to the local context and budgetary subvention of health facilities to achieve the best possible treatment goals. The onus to reformulate the SML is upon the notifying authority, DGHS, to constitute a committee of experts mentioned in its paragraph 2 (additional file 1), HTA advisory committee alongwith academicians’ expert in antibiotic use and AMR research and to reach to an expert concensus regarding the updation of the SML under guidance provided by WHO in the form of health system building blocks (Fig. 3 and additional file 4) and also in light of the above recommendations.

Our study effectively identified intra-bed-strength-category budget disparities and underscored the need to address these disparities to optimize the standard of care within identical-level facilities.

Clinical implications

Our study emphasizes the selection of antibiotics through rigorous HTA analysis coupled with context-specific antibiotic policy. This will ultimately improve clinician adherence to the overall formulary package and will be beneficial in curbing inappropriate antibiotic use, associated costs, and the AMR burden.

Our study highlights the need to include more cost-effective Access category antibiotics and balance the Watch antibiotic ratio with first-line treatment agents recommended in the WHO AWaRe book to optimize treatment protocols based on the latest evidence. We recommend the WHO AWaRe classification integration into the bidding documents for pharmaceutical procurement and the alignment of the latter with UNGA Access antibiotic consumption goals, with clear capping criteria for Watch antibiotics procurement based on local susceptibility data for procuring agencies (DHAs) across Punjab.

There are no WHO AWaRe Reserve category antibiotics in the current SML; ignoring their bulk procurement is analogous to refusing optimal treatment for resistant infections. Contrastingly, our qualitative study notes the use of Reserve category antibiotics in the health facilities, possibly procured through alternate procurement routes [2]. We recommend the inclusion of Reserve antibiotics in bulk procurement to achieve cost-effectiveness and simultaneously regulate their use through restrictive formulary management using a prior authorization mechanism and auditing their use [70, 78].

Conclusion

Consistent access to antibiotics is an outcome of an efficient procurement model based on stewardship principles and adequate budgetary grants. The study identified inconsistencies in budgetary allocations and SML-dictated quantities for PSHFs, leaving a margin for shortages and stockouts, and affecting consistent access to antibiotics. The currently implemented procurement policy is misaligned with the WHO AWaRe system and promotes the use of broad-spectrum Watch category antibiotics, compromising ASP and making it difficult to combat AMR. Moreover, it drops key Access category antibiotics, such as amoxicillin, entailing a critical appraisal of its composition. Until NAP Pakistan features antibiotic procurement as its own domain and stimulates the formulation of a national procurement policy, mal-procurement practices will dispense sub-optimal access to antibiotics in health facilities. Therefore, a thorough update of the NAP is imperative.
