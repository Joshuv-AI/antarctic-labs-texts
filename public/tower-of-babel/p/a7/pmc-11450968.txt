Functional Identification of miR2119 Targeting ADHs in Modulating Soybean Resistance to Heterodera glycines
Authors: Xiaoyu Zhang, Xiaofeng Zhu, Lijie Chen, Haiyan Fan, Xiaoyu Liu, Ning Yang, Yuanyuan Wang, Yuxi Duan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoybean cyst nematode (SCN, Heterodera glycines) is a sedentary endoparasite nematode that results in severe economic losses in soybean crops. miRNAs play crucial roles in plant responses to nematode. However, the role of miR2119 responding to SCN stress in soybean. Here, we demonstrated that the transcript levels of polycistronic precursors containing miR2119 and miR398a were significantly reduced in soybean upon nematode infection. Promoter of the miR2119-398a precursor analysis was conducted containing a GUS reporter gene. GUS activity assays demonstrated a decrease in miR2119-398a promoter during SCN infection. Overexpression of polycistronic precursor miR2119-398a (OE-premiR2119-398a) and miR2119 precursor (OE-premiR2119) rendered soybean more susceptible to SCN. Conversely, silencing miR2119 (STTM2119) increased soybean resistance against SCN. Furthermore, RNA-seq analysis revealed that miR2119 is involved in many defense signaling pathways. GUS reporter gene assays demonstrated that miR2119 targets GmADH1.1a and GmADH1.1b. Functional analysis indicated that ADHs act as a major role in responding to H. glycines by modulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. Together, the findings reveal a novel mechanism by which the polycistronic precursor miR2119–398a coordinately regulates in response to H. glycines. Additionally, miR2119 becomes an essential element contributing to H. glycines by modulating ADH activity and ROS homeostasis in soybean.Keywords: soybean, Heterodera glycines, polycistronic precursor, miR2119, alcohol dehydrogenase

Introduction

Soybean cyst nematode (SCN, Heterodera glycines) is a sedentary endoparasite nematode that damages the soybean (Glycine max) root system, posing a significant challenge to soybean production and resulting in extensive damage.1 It is estimated that annual losses amount to billions of dollars worldwide, among which more than US $120 million in China.2−4 The parasitic second-stage juveniles (J2s) penetrate soybean roots and migrate intracellularly to the vascular cylinder.5 Immediately, SCN initiates the formation of a multinucleated feeding site in roots, known as the syncytium, which recruits adjacent cells and supplies the nutrients for SCN growth and development. This structure is metabolically active and crucial for completing the life cycle of cyst nematode.6,7 Consequently, plants have evolved multiple defense strategies to cope with SCN stress and undergo coordinated changes at the transcriptional level. The most effective strategies are utilizing resistant cultivates to enhance soybean resistance against H. glycines.8,9

miRNAs, typically small noncoding RNAs (20–24 nt), recognize their target mRNAs through partial sequence complementarity and repress gene expression across both the transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels.10,11 Evidence supports that plant-clustered miRNAs can be transcribed either independently as precursors or cotranscribed into a polycistronic transcripts.12−14 Several miRNA clusters encoding the same miRNA precursor members were found in various plant species, such as miR166, miR169, and miR395.14−17 These clusters frequently summarized as homologous clusters and corresponded to conserved miRNAs.14 In rice, a polycistronic miRNA, miR166k-166h, acts as a potential regulator of rice immunity, showing enhanced resistance to rice blast and bakanae disease.18 Additionally, several clusters consisting of miRNAs with unrelated sequences are referred to as nonhomologous clusters. These consecutive MIRNA loci may coregulate different miRNAs that target similar transcripts involved in functionally related biological pathways.14 A similar situation occurs in the case of rice, where polycistronic miR1423-1868 and miR1876-1862d are coexpressed in rice.15 In common bean, a polycistronic transcript encoding miR398a and miR2119 was identified, highlighted the coordinated regulation of the targeted transcripts encoding Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase (CSD1) and alcohol dehydrogenase1 (ADH1) in response to water deficit.12

miR2119 is a legume-specific miRNA that has been functioned in response to various abiotic and biotic stresses, including drought, aluminum stress, nodules development, and nematodes.19−23 miR2119 targets a gene encoding alcohol dehydrogenase. Alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), a zinc-binding enzyme that functions as a dimer, utilizes the NAD(P) cofactor for the interconversion of ethanol to short straight chain aldehyde.24,25 In this system, ADH enzyme plays a crucial role in energy balance by regulating the consumption of pyruvate in the TCA cycle under hypoxic conditions.26 Furthermore, ADH is a member of the medium-length dehydrogenase/reductase (MDR) protein superfamily.27 The first report on ADH activity was in the grains and anthers of maize.28 Plant ADH genes are ubiquitous in plants and are crucial for their growth and development under various stress conditions.29 The induction of several genes encoding alcohol dehydrogenases has been shown to involve various defense responses in plant. For instance, ADH1, a target of miR2119, functions as various environmental stresses, including flooding and drought.12,30,31 In Arabidopsis, overexpression of AtADH1 not only confers resistance to abiotic stresses, including salt, drought, and cold, but also bolsters resistance to pathogen invasion.32 Researchers have demonstrated that the ADH proteins altered the wild peanut resistance to the peanut root-knot nematode (RKN) infection.33 Induction of barley ADH1 expression to defense the parasitic fungus Blumeria graminis has been reported.34 Nevertheless, the activities and functions of miR2119 and ADHs in soybean resistance to nematodes remain largely unknown.

Previous studies have confirmed that miR2119 and ADH genes participated in defense signaling during the invasion of plants by cyst nematodes.21,35 In this study, the objectives were to characterize the putative function of miR2119 in soybeans during simulated SCN invasion. Noteworthy, we found that miR2119 was cotranscribed as a polycistronic transcript encoding a conserved miR398a, which is known to target CSD gene.36 Additionally, the accumulation of polycistronic miR2119-398a decreased upon nematode infection, which was consistent with the transcript levels of miR2119 and miR398. Furthermore, ADHs have been described as targets of miR2119. The results indicate that ADH activity depends on miR2119-mediated regulation, suggesting that the regulatory pathway involving between miR2119-398a and ADHs plays pivotal roles in soybean resistance to nematode infection.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Nematode Inoculum Preparation

In this study, we utilized the susceptible soybean cultivar Williams 8237 and the resistant cultivar Huipizhi38 to H. glycines as the experimental materials. The seeds germinated in vermiculite under controlled environmental conditions: a 14 h light/10 h dark cycle at 25 °C, with relative humidity maintained within the range of 40–60% in a phytotron room. After 10 days of germination, the seedlings were transplanted into polyvinyl chloride (PVC) tubes containing sterilized sand for subsequent experiments. The cysts of SCN race 3 were isolated from diseased soil by sucrose centrifugation. For inoculation, eggs were incubated with a 3 mM ZnSO4 solution at 25 °C until the hatching of J2s. The hatched juveniles were then collected and used as the inoculum source.39

Plasmid Construction and Agrobacterium rhizogenes Mediated Soybean Hairy Root Transformation

To analyze the GUS activity of miR2119-398a promoter, the approximately 2 kb promoter region of miR2119-398a was amplified from the Williams 82 template. Subsequently, the sequence was inserted in the recombinant vector pCAMBIA3301 with a GUS reporter gene. To characterize the functions of miR2119, miR2119-398a, and miR2119 target genes GmADHs, the soybean miR2119 precursor, miR2119-398a precursor, GmADH1.1a, and GmADH1.1b coding DNA sequences were amplified from the DNA template of Williams 82 and subsequently inserted into the pNINC2RNAi empty vector. The insertion was controlled by the CaMV 35S promoter, accompanied by an enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) selectable marker.40 Silencing miR2119 (STTM2119) was generated utilizing the short tandem target mimic (STTM) method based on Yan et al. (2012).41 The STTM2119 sequence contained two copies of miR2119 complementary sequences that were linked by a 48 nt short spacer. The sequence was synthesized by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China) and subsequently inserted into the binary vector mentioned above. The recombinant plasmid vectors were introduced into Agrobacterium rhizogenes K599 by thermal shock. Subsequently, the bacteria were injected into the hypocotyls of 4 day-old Williams 82 soybean seedlings, which were maintained in high humidity to facilitate the transformation of hairy roots. Hairy roots gradually developed from the infection site within 8–9 days. Positive transgenic hairy roots were screened with a hand-held lamp (Luyor, China) to visualize EGFP expression. The composite plants were then transplanted into new pots filled with vermiculite and continued to grow for about 15 days.42

Nematode Inoculation Assay

To analyze the relative expression and abundance of miR2119 precursor (premiR2119), mature miR2119, and its target genes, soybean nontransgenic seedlings were inoculated with 2000 J2s. Seedling roots were harvested at 1, 5, 10, and 15 dpi (days postinoculation). For visualization of GUS activity, soybean hairy roots were infected with 1000 J2s. The infected roots were stained at 5, 10, and 15 dpi using the GUS Stain Kit (Solarbio Co.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.43 The experiment was performed 3 times. Photographs were taken by an Olympus DP80 (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). To assess the susceptibility of soybean hairy roots, plants were inoculated with 800 J2s. At 15 dpi, the total number of nematode juveniles in the hairy roots was determined using acid fuchsin staining.44

RNA Extraction and RT-qPCR Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from harvested soybean roots using the TRNzol Universal Reagent (TIANGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, stem-loop RT-qPCR and RT-qPCR assays were conducted to validate the expression levels of differentially expressed miRNAs and mRNAs, respectively. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the miRNA first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) by stem-loop with a specific stem-loop primer and the HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR Kit (+gDNA wiper; Vazyme, Nanjing, China). U6 and Actin were evaluated by GeNorm, NormFinder, and BestKeeper (https://blooge.cn/RefFinder/) as internal control genes to normalize the expression levels of miRNAs and mRNAs. RT-qPCR was performed using a SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio) on a CFX96 real-time PCR instrument (Bio-Rad). The results were collected from three independent biological replicates and three technical replicates. The expression levels of genes were analyzed using the 2–ΔΔCt method as described by Livak and Schmittgen.45 All qPCR primers are listed in Tables S1 and S2.

Synthetic miR2119 Mimics or Inhibitor and Treatment

The nucleotide sequence of the 5′ to 3′ terminals of miR2119 was downloaded from the miRBase database (www.mirbase.org). The mature sequence of soybean miR2119 is 5′-TCAAAGGGAGTTGTAGGGGAA-3′. miR2119 mimics and inhibitor were chemically synthesized based on miR2119 mature sequence by Sangon Biotech. The synthesized product was dissolved in sterile water to achieve a final concentration of 30 ng/μL. Subsequently, 10 day-old seedlings were treated with the synthesized product by foliar spray once a week.

4-Methylpyrazole Treatment

4-Methylpyrazole (4-MP) (CAS: 7554-65-6; Rhawn; Shanghai, China) is a competitive inhibitor of ADH in plants. The product was dissolved in sterile water to achieve final concentrations of 1, 5, and 10 mM, respectively. To evaluate the function of ADH activity in soybean in response to SCN infection, 8 day-old soybean seedlings were subjected to foliar application with 4-MP at different concentrations. Then, the soybean seedling roots were infected with cyst nematodes and harvested after inoculation for further analysis.

RNA-Seq Library Preparation and Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the soybean seedling roots treated with exogenous miR2119 mimics (Mimics), inhibitor (Inhibitor), and compared to control (CK) plants. The detailed procedure is described above. At least, three biological replicates were collected for each experimental group. RNA concentration, purity, and quality were assessed using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and the Agient2100/LabChip GX platform. RNA-seq libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform by Biomarker Technologies (Beijing, China). Transcript assembly and normalization were performed based on fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped (FPKM). The screening standard of transcripts included fold change (FC) ≥ 2 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses were conducted using the OmicShare toolset (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/Home/Soft/getsoft). The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in soybean roots treated with miR2119 mimics and inhibitor were subsequently validated using RT-qPCR. The transcriptomic sequence data reported in this manuscript was submitted to the NCBI database under the accession number PRJNA1105202.

Verification of miR2119 Targeted GmADHs by the GUS Assay

For the GUS reporter gene assay, the miR2119 precursor was inserted into the pBI121-GUS vector, which lacks the GUS reporter gene. Subsequently, GmADH1.1a and GmADH1.1b were integrated into the pBI121-GUS vector, which contains the GUS reporter gene. Following this, all recombinant vectors were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Nicotiana benthamiana leaves were infiltrated with four mixture groups a suspension (OD600 = 0.8) containing 35S:premiR2119, 35S:GmADH1.1a, 35S:GmADH1.1b, and the 1:1 ratio mixture (OD600 = 1.0) of 35S:premiR2119 + 35S:GmADH1.1a-GUS or 35S:premiR2119 + 35S:GmADH1.1b-GUS. The 35S:GUS (empty vector) was the control. The N. benthamiana plants were cultured in the dark for 24 h, followed by transfer to a phytotron room with a photoperiod of 14 h light/10 h dark for 2 days. Finally, N. benthamiana leaves were stained using the GUS Stain Kit (Solarbio Co.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.43 The GUS expression levels of the interaction between GmADH1.1a or GmADH1.1b with premiR2119 were analyzed by ImageJ software. The experiments were repeated 3 times with consistent results.

ADH Activity Assay

ADH activity was measured using an alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) activity assay kit (Solarbio). ADH catalyzes the reduction of acetaldehyde by NADH to produce ethanol and NAD+. NADH exhibits an absorption peak at 340 nm, while NAD+ does not. The rate of decrease in the 340 nm absorbance was measured to calculate ADH activity. Roots of soybean sample were harvested, then immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground to a fine powder using a mortar. The sample was placed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 1 mL of extraction buffer. After vortexing, ADH activity was determined by centrifuging at 16 000g for 20 min at 4 °C. The measurement procedure was manipulated according to the instructions. The absorbance at 340 nm was monitored using a spectrophotometer. ADH activity was calculated by corresponding formulas according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The experiments were repeated 3 times.

Phylogenetic Analysis

The coding sequences of GmADH genes were retrieved from SoyBase (https://www.soybase.org/sgn/). The sequences were aligned, and a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was built using the MEGA11 software, with 1000 bootstrap replications after alignment by CLUSTALW.

Measurement of Reactive Oxygen Species Contents

The reactive oxygen species (ROS) contents were monitored with DAB (Coolaber) staining and 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe labeling (Solarbio) according to the instructions. The roots from 10 day-old seedlings treated with ADH inhibitor 4-MP for 4 h were harvested. Briefly, to DAB staining, the soybean roots were soaked in DAB working solution, incubated at room temperature in the dark for 3 min, and washed with ddH2O. To DCFH-DA probe staining, the soybean roots were submerged in 10 μM DCFH-DA probe solution, incubated at 37 °C for 15 min, and washed with ddH2O. The experiment was replicated 3 times. Photographs were taken using an Olympus DP80 instrument equipped with a GFP visualizer module (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis of significant differences among three or more groups was performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Significant differences between groups were assessed using two-tailed Student’s t-test. Data analysis was conducted using either GraphPad Prism 9 software or IBM SPSS Statistics v.26. Error bars represent the mean ± SE, and asterisks indicate statistically significant differences among various treatments: *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; and ****, P < 0.0001.

Results

Polycistronic miR2119-398a in Soybean Is Repressed in Response to Infection by SCN

Previous studies have identified miR2119 as a legume-specific miRNA, which is involved in plant growth, development, and responding to various stress.46,47 Furthermore, the downstream of miR2119 precursor consists of a second miRNA precursor, that is miR398a.12 The distance between miR2119 and miR398a in the G. max genome is 135 nt, suggesting that both precursors are encoded in a dicistronic transcript in G. max (Figure 1A). To further characterize it, we conducted expression analysis of miR2119 precursor and polycistronic miR2119-398a in both soybean-susceptible (Willams 82) and resistant (Huipizhi) cultivates under SCN conditions. Ten day-old plants were subjected to SCN infection, which revealed that miR2119 precursor was downregulated in soybean resistance cultivate Huipizhi upon exposure to SCN at 1, 5, 10, and 15 dpi (Figure 1B). In the soybean-susceptible cultivate Willams 82, the expression level of the miR2119 precursor also decreased at 1, 5, and 10 dpi (Figure 1B). However, there was no significant change in the susceptible cultivar at 15 days postinfection (dpi) (Figure 1B). Consistent with the decreased abundance of the miR2119 precursor upon SCN stress, we observed a decrease in the accumulation levels of the precursor transcript premiR2119-398a after H. glycines inoculation (Figure 1C). The results indicated that premiR2119-398a expression patterns remarkably decreased in response to H. glycines at 1, 5, and 10 dpi in both soybean-susceptible and resistance cultivates (Figure 1C). At 15 days of pdi, the accumulation level of miR2119-398a exhibited a significant decrease in the resistant cultivar Huipizhi, while there was no change in susceptible cultivate Willams 82 (Figure 1C). Moreover, we observed that the reduction of miR2119 and miR2119-398a expression levels in the resistant cultivar was significantly lower than that in susceptible cultivar.

To determine whether miR2119-398a is downregulated in soybean in response to SCN infection, we monitored the promoter activity of miR2119-398a precursor using the pmiR2119-398a: GUS construct, which drives GUS gene expression in soybean hairy roots. Compared to 35S:GUS, the GUS activity of pmiR2119-398a was reduced in infected roots at 5 and 10 dpi (Figure 1D). At a late stage, the GUS activity of pmiR2119-398a was a slight increase, compared with the activity at an early stage (Figure 1D). These findings prompted us to experimentally confirm that miR2119-398a is downregulated during SCN infection, consistent with the results of RT-qPCR.

Coordinated Regulation of miR2119 and miR398a during SCN Infection

In our previous study, we have demonstrated that miR398 is involved in improving soybean immunity response to SCN via targeting CSDs.48 To further elucidate the coordinated regulation of miR2119 and miR398a, we cloned the precursor of miR2119-398a and transformed it into transgenic soybean hairy roots. RT-qPCR results revealed that the expression levels of miR2119-398a, miR2119, and miR398a precursors were upregulated in the roots of OE-premiR2119-398a plants compared to control plants (EV, empty vector) (Figure 2A). Moreover, the expression trend of the miR2119 precursor also showed an increase in the OE-premiR2119 plant roots (Figure 2B). We evaluated the infection rates of H. glycines in hairy roots and found that the total number of nematodes in both OE-premiR2119 and OE-premiR2119-398a plants were significantly higher than that in the empty vector control at 15 dpi (Figures 2C and S1A). Among them, OE-premiR2119-398a showed more susceptibility than OE-miR2119 to SCN. Additionally, the average percentage of J2s was lower in OE-premiR2119 and OE-premiR2119-398a plants compared to control plants, while the average percentage of fourth-stage juveniles (J4s) showed an opposite trend (Figure 2D,E). These data demonstrate that miR2119 and miR398a function together in soybeans in response to SCN.

Silencing miR2119 Elevates Soybean Resistance under SCN Infection

We further investigated the role of miR2119 in the soybean response to SCN by employing STTM technology to silence miR2119. RT-qPCR analysis of miRNA demonstrated a significant reduction in the accumulation of miR2119 in STTM2119 plants compared to empty vector-carrying plants, indicating successful silencing of miR2119 in soybean hairy roots (Figure 3A). Subsequently, STTM2119 plants and EV plants were inoculated with H. glycines J2s, and the susceptibility of transgenic hairy roots was quantified by counting nematode masses and the rate of J2s or J4s to the total number of nematodes in the roots. STTM2119 plants exhibited a substantial and significant decrease compared to EV plants (Figures 3B and S1B), with an average of 36 nematodes per gram compared to 49 nematodes per gram in EV plants (Figure 3B). Moreover, the average percentage of J2s and J4s in STTM2119 showed significant differences compared to EV plants, with a higher proportion of J2s and a lower rate of J4s observed in STTM2119 plants (Figure 3C,D). These infection assays suggest a negative regulatory role for miR2119 in the soybean response to SCN infection.

Transcriptome Changes Revealed the Molecular Mechanism of miR2119 in Soybean

To explore the molecular mechanism of miR2119 in soybean based on miR2119 mimics and inhibitor, the miR2119 expression level was significantly higher in soybean treated with miR2119 mimics than in the control (Figure S2A). miR2119 inhibitors are chemically modified oligonucleotides that strongly compete with mature miRNAs to prevent the complementary pairing of miRNAs and their target genes, thereby inhibiting the function of miRNAs. Accordingly, the abundance of miR2119 targets, GmADHs, was significantly higher in soybean treated with miR2119 inhibitor (Figure S2B). A total of 9 cDNA libraries, comprising those from the CK, Mimics, and Inhibitor groups, were sequenced in triplicate. On average, 93.80% of clean reads from each sample were successfully mapped to the reference genome. Sequencing data statistics, including clean reads and bases, along with comparison data of the clean reads, are listed in Tables S3 and S4. In total, 1181 and 2138 DEGs were identified in CK vs mimics, and CK vs inhibitor, respectively, with FC ≥ 2 and FDR <0.05 (Figure S3). Among these, 562 upregulated and 619 downregulated DEGs were detected in CK versus mimics, while 1125 upregulated and 1013 downregulated DEGs were identified in CK versus inhibitor (Figure S3).

KEGG analysis revealed that metabolic pathways, such as MAPK signaling pathway-plant (ko04016), isoflavonoid biosynthesis (ko00943), starch and sucrose metabolism (ko00500), glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (ko00010), and plant hormone signal transduction (ko04075), were highly enriched, with most genes in these pathways in CK vs mimics and CK vs inhibitor (Figure 4A,B).

To explore the functions of these DEGs in CK versus mimics and CK versus inhibitor, GO enrichment analysis was conducted. The top 25 GO terms with the lowest q-value for each group were significantly enriched. Notably, several enriched GO terms related to molecular function and biological processes were identified in CK versus mimics, such as DNA-binding transcription factor activity (GO:0003700), response to chitin (GO:0010200), and oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016709). Most genes in these pathways showed downregulated expression levels (Figure 5A). Several enriched GO terms were identified in CK versus inhibitor, including DNA-binding transcription factor activity (GO:0003700), oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016702), regulation of jasmonic acid-mediated signaling pathway (GO:2000022), and defense response (GO:0006952) (Figure 5B). The expressions of DEGs associated with the defense signaling pathway were validated using RT-qPCR, providing robust evidence supporting the RNA-seq data (Figure S4). In addition, to validate the expression levels of RT-qPCR correlated with FPKM values from RNA-Seq, the Pearson correlation coefficient alongside the p-value is presented in Tables S8 and S9. All genes except Glyma.11G014800, Glyma.19G256800, and Glyma.13G25200 showed obvious correlation (p-value ≤ 0.05), showing that the DEGs exhibited a similar trend in RNA-Seq and RT-qPCR.

Verification of the Interaction between miR2119 and GmADHs by GUS Staining

Based on degradome database, a plant miRNA–target interaction network showed that soybean miR2119 targeted 5 GmADHs by degradation sequencing.49 Certainly, our transcriptional sequence analysis demonstrated that several soybean alcohol dehydrogenase-related genes are enriched in soybean plants treated with miR2119 mimics and inhibitors (Figure S5). The expression levels of the GmADH genes were substantially increased in STTM2119 plants (Figure S6). The in vivo regulatory roles of GmADHs by gma-miR2119 were further verified by utilizing GUS histochemical staining in N. benthamiana. The different recombinant vectors were transformed into strains, including 35S:GUS, 35S:premiR2119, 35S:GmADH1.1a-GUS, and 35S:GmADH1.b-GUS were constructed. The N. benthamiana leaves infiltered with empty vector 35S:GUS, 35S:GmADH1.1a-GUS, and 35S:GmADH1.1b-GUS showed an obvious blue color [Figure 6A(a,b),C(e,f)]. The leaves injected with 35S:premiR2119 without a GUS gene did not exhibit a blue color [Figure 6A(c),C(g)]. In contrast, the leaves injected with 35S:premiR2119 and 35S:GmADH1.1a-GUS or 35SGmADH1.1b-GUS mixed in a 1:1 ratio showed a lighter blue color compared to that injected with 35S: GmADH1.1a-GUS or 35S: GmADH1.1b-GUS alone [Figure 6A(d),C(h)]. The grayscale value of GUS staining revealed that the GUS activity fused with GmADHs was decreased by miR2119 as shown in Figures 6B,D, indicating that miR2119 cleaves the target genes GmADH1.1a and GmADH1.1b.

ADH Activity Is Regulated by miR2119 in Soybean during SCN Infection

The ADH activity was investigated in soybean treated with exogenous miR2119 mimics and inhibitor compared to untreated (control) plants under SCN infection. The ADH activity in plants upon nematode infection was significantly higher than in uninfected plants (Figure 7). Under both SCN-uninfected and SCN-infected conditions, compared with control plants, the level of ADH activity in plants treated with miR2119 mimics was slightly lower than that in the controls. In contrast, the ADH activity in plants treated with miR2119 inhibitor increased compared with that of the control plants (Figure 7). Overall, these results indicated that soybean miR2119 is involved in plant responses to SCN by regulating ADH activity.

GmADHs Play Positive Roles via Regulating ROS Accumulation in Soybean in Response to SCN

In the G. max genome, there are 28 GmADH genes. To compare the genetic relationships among the GmADH genes, the phylogenetic tree of GmADH genes was constructed based on coding sequences. According to the phylogenetic tree, a close genetic relationship was revealed between GmADH1.1a (Glyma.04G240800) and GmADH1.1b (Glyma.06G122600) (Figure S7). To address the tolerance of GmADHs to SCN stress, overexpression constructs containing GmADH1.1a and GmADH1.1b were designed and utilized in A. rhizogenes-mediated hairy root transformation. Significant changes were observed in the accumulation levels of GmADH1.1a and GmADH1.1b in the roots of overexpressing plants, showing an increase of 6.67-fold and 3.75-fold compared with control plants, respectively (Figure 8A,B). The functional roles of GmADH1.1a and GmADH1.1b were further investigated in overexpressed plants OE-GmADH1.1a and OE-ADH1.1b under SCN exposure, respectively. The results demonstrated that the total number of SCN in overexpressing (OE-ADH1.1a and OE-ADH1.1b) plants were dramatically lower relative to EV plants (Figures 8C and S1C). Moreover, a decrease was observed in the J4s ratio in OE-ADH1.1a and OE-ADH1.1b plant roots (Figure 8D).

Discussion

A growing body of evidence has revealed that miRNAs play critical roles in contributing to various biotic and abiotic stresses in plants.52,53 miR2119 as a legume-specific miRNA has been previously reported in various legume species, including G. max,54 common bean,10,55Vigna mungo,56 and Medicago truncatula.57 However, miR2119 has been reported to be involved in many biological processes and stress, such as water deficit,12,54 drought,19 plant development,47 aluminum stress,20 soybean nodulation,22 cyst nematodes,21 and flower development in soybean cytoplasmic male sterility line.58 However, there are no reports investigating the underlying mechanisms of miR2119 in the soybean–Heterodera glycine interaction. In this study, we employed overexpression of polycistronic precursor miR2119-398a and found that reduced soybean tolerance was greater than that with miR2119 overexpression upon SCN stress. Conversely, silencing miR2119 improved soybean resistance against SCN. Further, alteration of miR2119 expression by mimics and inhibitor showed that miR2119 was involved in defense pathways such as the MAPK signaling pathway, plant hormone signal transduction, and so on. Then, ADH1.1a and ADH1.1b as miR2119 target genes increased soybean resistance by regulating ROS accumulation. Our results demonstrate that miR2119 not only targeting ADHs to affect ROS accumulation but also involved in some metabolic pathways to regulate soybean tolerance (Figure 11).

Hypothetical model of miR2119 in mediating soybean resistance Heterodera glycines. The schematic diagram was created in BioRender (https://www.biorender.com/).

In this study, we demonstrated a more pronounced reduction in the expression of miR2119 and miR2119-398a in resistant soybean cultivars compared to susceptible cultivars upon infection with H. glycine (Figure 1). The finding is consistent with a previous study, which reported that miR2119 was downregulated in both soybean-resistant cultivar KS4313N and susceptible cultivar KS4607 upon SCN stress.21 Furthermore, we discovered that miR2119 and miR398a are encoded in a dicistronic transcript in G. max, with a distance of only 135 nt from G. max genome, consistent with previous observation in common bean.12 Moreover, we observed a decrease in the expression level of miR2119-398a in soybean upon nematode infection through monitoring the promoter activity of its precursor, which is consistent with the result of RT-qPCR (Figure 1). Evidence is presented on the occurrence of polycistronic precursors consisting of conserved families in the plant genome, such as miR859-774 in Agrobacterium thaliana and miR395 in rice.14,59 Some miRNA precursor functioned as polycistronic miRNAs to coordinately regulate overlapping or complementary sets of targets with related functions.60 A study showed that polycistronic miR166k-166h positively enhances the immunity of rice to Magnaporthe oryzae via controlling ethylene-insensitive 2 expression.18 Upon water deficit, the transcript containing miR398 and miR2119 in Phaseolus leads to the coordinated regulation of different mRNAs for a plant adjustment.12 In this study, we employed overexpression to investigate the function of miR2119-398a (Figure 2). The expression levels of miR2119-398a, miR2119, and miR398a precursors were upregulated in OE-premiR2119-398a plants. The overexpression of miR2119-398a resulted in increased soybean sensitivity to SCN, indicating a negative regulatory role of miR2119-398a in soybean resistance to SCN (Figure 2). In a previous study, we elucidated that miR398 is also involved in improving soybean immunity response to SCN via targeting CSDs.48 In fact, an increasing number of studies elaborated that miR398 exhibited a significant role in soybean response to SCN; miR398a and miR398b were repressed in Kansas soybean-susceptible cultivars KS4607 response to SCN.21,61 A Similar phenomenon has also been observed: miR398b was significantly downregulated during Heterodera schachtii infection in Arabidopsis and showed a negative correlation with its targets CSD1 and CSD2.62

To delve into the individual molecular mechanism of miR2119 in response to SCN, both miR2119 overexpression and silencing plants were generated. Notably, overexpression of miR2119 was found to negatively impact soybean resistance to SCN, whereas STTM-mediated miR2119 silencing led to enhanced soybean tolerance to SCN (Figures 2 and 3). These findings strongly imply the negative regulatory role of miR2119 in soybeans in response to SCN stress. Furthermore, it was observed that soybean miR2119 serves as a negative factor of plant male fertility under high temperature stress.47 Conversely, in Caragana korshinskii, an important species for vegetation restoration, miR2119 has been reported to play a positive factor under drought stress.19 Hence, it is evident that miR2119 plays diverse roles across various stress conditions, suggesting that its underlying regulatory mechanisms are likely to differ significantly among plant species. Intriguingly, whether miR2119 and miR398 were overexpressed independently or simultaneously in soybean, a negative effect was consistently observed, highlighting the likelihood that coregulation is the primary advantageous characteristic of the dicistronic transcript. Functional analysis revealed that two human miRNA clusters, encoding distinct miRNAs, cooperatively regulate cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors, thereby facilitating cell cycle progression.63

In pursuit of unraveling the intricate molecular mechanism underlying miR2119’s involvement in plant defense response, we conducted transcriptome analyses comparing miR2119 mimic (CK vs mimics) and miR2119 inhibitor (CK vs inhibitor) roots (Figure 4). Remarkably, among the DEGs in two comparison groups, a substantial proportion was found to be enriched in the key metabolic pathways including the MAPK signaling pathway plant (ko04016), glycolysis/gluconeogenesis (ko00010), isoflavonoid biosynthesis (ko00943), and plant hormone signal transduction (ko04075) (Figure 4). GO enrichment analysis further elucidated that these DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways associated with DNA-binding transcription factor activity (GO:0003700), oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016709), regulation of jasmonic acid mediated signaling pathway (GO:2000022), and defense response (GO:0006952) (Figure 5). These findings imply that miR2119 triggers various defense gene expression in soybean, thereby shedding light on its enduring role in response to environmental stress in soybean.

Intriguingly, our transcriptional sequence analysis revealed a significant enrichment of several ADH genes in the pathway “glycolysis/gluconeogenesis” in soybean roots treated with miR2119 mimics and inhibitor. Among these, GmADH1.1a and GmADH1.1b are confirmed as target genes of miR2119 in the study through GUS activity (Figure 6). Prior reports presented that GmADHs function as conserved targets of miR2119 in soybean by degradome analysis.22,58,64 What is more, GmADH1.3b is the target of gma-miR2119 in soybean based on 5′ RLM-RACE assay.47 These findings suggest that there may exist a negative regulatory relationship between gma-miR2119 and GmADHs in soybean. Previous studies have demonstrated the targeting of ADHs by miR2119 control plant tolerance in Phaseolus vulgaris and male fertility in Arabidopsis, respectively.12,47ADH, a pivotal enzyme ubiquitous across multiple plant species, together with aldehyde dehydrogenase, constitutes the important aldehyde/alcohol conversion pathways.27 Plant ADH genes have been found generally throughout plants, emphasizing their significance in plant growth and development under various conditions.27,29 In Arabidopsis, ADH2/GSNOR1 has demonstrated enduring efficacy in counteracting genotoxic damage from formaldehyde and UV-B.29 Besides the well-established roles in flooding stress and seed development, ADHs have been implicated in pivotal plant–pathogen interactions.12,65−67 For instance, overexpression of AtADH1 in Arabidopsis not only conferred resistance to abiotic stresses like salt, drought, and cold but also enhanced resistance to pathogen invasion.32 In peanut, the upregulation of a novel zinc-binding ADH2 upon late leaf spot pathogen infection triggers a cascade of events, including hypersensitive response (HR), antioxidative enzymes, and pathogenesis-related (PR) proteins in tobacco.68 Conversely, a research of loss-of-function mutations in ADH genes of Arabidopsis have revealed an increase in overall necrosis, suggesting a role in resistance against rhizoctonia foliar blight.69 Previous reports have also documented significant upregulation of ADH during plant parasitic cyst nematode infection in soybean and tomato.35,70 The findings were consistent with those in this study that the increase ADH activities observed in soybean under SCN infection imply a crucial role for ADH genes in soybean–nematode interactions (Figure 7). In this study, overexpression of ADHs led to increased soybean immunity to SCN, resulting in a significantly lower J4s ratio (Figure 8). Conversely, exogenous application of the ADH inhibitor 4-MP drastically decreased cyst nematode resistance in soybean (Figure 9). However, the function of ADH1 in the connection between barley and the parasitic fungus B. graminis was addressed, revealing that ADH1 overexpression enhances susceptibility to the parasitic fungus, and knock-down of ADH1 leads to reduced fungal success.67,71 Such differential mechanisms in plant ADHs are extensively discussed, indicating that ADHs play a dual role in the interactions of plant and biotic stress.

Excessive ROS caused oxidative stress, exerting a detrimental effect on plants by compromising cell integrity.72 H2O2 serves as a pivotal molecular signal modulating ADH transcript levels in plants under diverse stress conditions.73 The generation of H2O2, originating from both AtrbohD and AtrbohF, orchestrates ADH expression under oxygen-deficient condition.74 Under salt stress, ADH was assumed a crucial role in cellular reprogramming by modulating ROS production in mitochondria in rice.75 Overexpression of HvADH4 in barley under waterlogging stress bolsters ROS scavenging enzyme activity, thereby improving stress tolerance.76 Similarly, overexpression of ScADH3 in sugarcane under cold stress enhances tolerance and triggers the accumulation of ROS scavenging-related genes.50 These results are consistent with our report that inhibiting ADH activity markedly elevates ROS levels, indicating the intimate relationship between ADH and ROS in plants (Figure 10).

In summary, overexpression of miR2119-398a and miR2119 increased soybean sensitivity to SCN, whereas silencing miR2119 increased soybean resistance to SCN, suggesting the negative regulatory role of miR2119 in the soybean response to SCN. RNA-seq analysis revealed that a total of 1181 and 2138 DEGs were modulated by overexpression and silencing of miR2119. Functional analysis demonstrated that ADHs play positive roles in modulating ROS accumulation in soybean in response to SCN. These findings elucidate a new regulatory role for miR2119 and ADHs upon SCN stress and provide a molecular foundation of disease resistance in soybean.
