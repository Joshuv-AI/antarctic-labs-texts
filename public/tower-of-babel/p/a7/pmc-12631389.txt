Synergistic Noncovalent Interaction Drives High-Performance Fluorescence Detection of Drug Analogs
Authors: Jing Yu, Chao Gu, Pei-Xuan Wu, Meng-Yao Luo, Na-Xuan Chen, Xiao-Qing Yu, Guan-E Wang, Gang Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate and sensitive detection of illicit drugs, including methamphetamine (MA), is essential for maintaining public health and security. Stringent regulatory controls on MA have driven the adoption of N-methylphenethylamine (MPEA) as a prevalent experimental surrogate in scientific research. While fluorescence-based sensing demonstrates efficacy in detecting MPEA, its practical application remains constrained by selectivity and sensitivity. To overcome these limitations, we engineered a fluorescence sensing strategy based on synergistic noncovalent interaction and employed AgPDT (PDT = 4-pyridinethiol) as a recognition motif enabling both exceptional selectivity (F 0/F shows a 450% enhancement versus structural analogs) and remarkable sensitivity (LOD = 1 nM) for MPEA detection. The excellent detection performance originates from optimized charge transfer and hydrogen bonding interactions between AgPDT and MPEA, synergistically enhancing the molecular recognition specificity. This ultrasensitive fluorescence-based detection system demonstrates significant potential for trace illicit drug analysis.

Introduction

Coordination polymers (CPs), with their structurally tunable frameworks and abundant functional sites, offer versatile platforms for developing CP-based fluorescent sensors that demonstrate exceptional promise for drug detection. − Methamphetamine (MA) is a quintessential example of an illicit substance. The strict regulatory oversight of MA has led to the widespread use of N-methylphenethylamine (MPEA) as a primary research analogous. − Despite stringent regulatory controls, illicit trafficking and misuse of this drug remain pervasive. Thus, the development of efficient and portable detection technologies is critically important for enhancing drug control efforts. Fluorescence sensing stands out as a particularly promising approach due to its high sensitivity, rapid response, and operational simplicity. − However, although CP-based fluorescence sensing demonstrates detection capability for MPEA identification, existing materials exhibit constrained performance in both discrimination accuracy and detectable thresholds.

The fluorescence detection mechanism for organic amines typically involves photoinduced electron transfer (PET), which relies on the electron-donating properties of organic amines. − Energy level matching is one of the critical factors influencing the PET mechanism. However, the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) in many fluorescent probes is higher than that of MPEA, which hinders electron transfer. Additionally, the HOMO of MPEA is lower than that of most other organic amines, making these fluorescent probes prone to interference from other amines during detection. Consequently, fluorescent probes for MPEA are limited by insufficient sensitivity and selectivity.

Energy level matching ensures the thermodynamic feasibility of the PET process, while the interaction between the fluorescent probes and the analyte substantially influences detection performance. , High-performance fluorescence detection necessitates that the distance between the probes and the target molecules be sufficiently close to facilitate efficient electron transfer, which requires strong interactions such as hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, etc. (Scheme ). − However, there are currently few reports on adjusting the energy levels of probe molecules to facilitate electron transfer with MPEA while also establishing effective charge transfer pathways between them, which may be one of the reasons for the relatively low selectivity and sensitivity in current MPEA fluorescence detection. It is important to develop effective and general design methods for the fluorescence detection of MPEA.

Schematic Design of Charge Transfer and Synergistic Noncovalent Interaction for the Fluorescence Detection of Target Amine

In this study, we proposed a synergistic noncovalent interaction for the fluorescence detection of MPEA and successfully constructed a CP-based fluorescence sensing material, AgPDT (PDT = 4-pyridinethiol). The N-substituted pyridine moiety acts as an electron acceptor, modulating the HOMO level of the probes. ,, Meanwhile, Ag+ forms a unique “X”-shaped tetrahedral coordination configuration that exposes the N atom from the pyridine moieties, facilitating efficient electron transfer between the probes and MPEA. − The exposed “X”-shaped distribution of S atoms in the [AgS] chains can form hydrogen bonds with the methyl groups (CH3) in MPEA, establishing efficient charge transfer pathways. The experimental results demonstrate that the F 0/F value of AgPDT for MPEA at 900 mM was 450% higher than that of structurally similar amines. Furthermore, the orange fluorescence of the AgPDT solution was quenched in the presence of MPEA at various concentrations, with an experimental limit of detection (LOD) of 1 nM. This study provides new insights into the design of efficient and selective MPEA sensing materials.

The single crystal of AgPDT was successfully synthesized via a solvothermal method (Scheme S1). Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis reveals that AgPDT crystallizes in orthorhombic space group Ibam (Table S1). As depicted in Figure a, each Ag+ ion coordinates with four S atoms from different PDT ligands to form Ag2S2 rhombic units, which are further connected by vertex-sharing to form continuous [AgS] chains along the c-axis (Figure b). Ag+ adopts a unique “X”-shaped tetrahedral coordination configuration that exposes N-substituted pyridine moieties and S atoms. The material is further stabilized into a 3D framework via hydrogen bonding interactions (d C–H···S = 3.02 Å) between neighboring units (Figure c). , These hydrogen bonds effectively facilitate a staggered arrangement of the structural units, contributing to the stability of the framework. Figure d shows that the centroid distance spacing of adjacent parallel sulfhydryl groups is 3.54 Å. The absence of the characteristic S–H stretching vibration at 2560 cm–1 in the FT-IR spectra further confirms successful coordination of sulfhydryl groups with Ag+ ions (Figure e). The purity of as-synthesized AgPDT was confirmed through powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns, which aligned well with the simulated pattern (Figure f). The energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) mapping confirmed the uniform distribution of Ag, C, N, and S elements, and the purity of as-synthesized AgPDT was also verified by elemental analysis (EA) and inductively coupled plasma emission spectroscopy (ICP) (Figures S1 and S2). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) shows that AgPDT is thermally stable up to at least 228 °C (Figure g). Additionally, AgPDT retains its chemical stability after being immersed in various solvents for 24 h (Figure S3). It can also maintain structural integrity at room temperature and under various pH conditions (Figures S4 and S5).

After grinding the rod-shaped crystals of AgPDT (QY = 2.00%), the powder was arranged into the letters “E” and “F”, appearing light yellow under daylight and exhibiting distinct orange fluorescence under UV light (Figure a,b). The powder sample shows a clear emission centered at 622 nm with an optimal excitation at 397 nm (Figure c). The CIE chromaticity coordinates further verify the orange emission (Figure S6). Furthermore, the absence of fluorescence from the PDT ligand confirms that the luminescence of the material does not originate from the ligand (Figure S7). Given the practical importance of detecting liquid-phase drugs, the fluorescence sensing of AgPDT was further evaluated in solution. AgPDT powder was ground and dispersed in n-hexane to investigate the effect of different MPEA concentrations on its fluorescence intensity. SEM (3–6 μm) and DLS (∼2.7 μm) analyses reveal a relatively uniform particle size and indicate good dispersibility of the suspension. Additionally, the emission spectra show only minor variations over time, supporting the stability of the suspension (Figure S8). Figure d reveals that the fluorescence intensity of AgPDT was efficiently quenched from 36.4 to 80.6% as MPEA concentration increased from 1 nM to 900 mM. Upon addition of 900 mM MPEA, the AgPDT solution under UV light exhibited a rapid visual response with fluorescence fading within 5 s (Figure S9). Furthermore, XRD analysis was conducted on the samples after fluorescence detection, revealing that their crystallinity remained unchanged (Figure S10). As shown in Figure e,f, a linear relationship between F 0/F and MPEA concentration is observed from 1 nM to 1 μM and from 200 to 900 mM, enabling fluorescence-based quantification across both trace and moderate concentration ranges. Figure e also shows the experimental LOD of AgPDT for MPEA reaching 1 nM, which is significantly lower than the cutoff values established by the European Workplace Drug Testing Society (EWDTS) for MA. This ultralow LOD enhances detection reliability, ensuring more accurate and comprehensive drug screening in practical applications. The Stern–Volmer plot deviates from linearity and increases exponentially at higher concentrations (Figure S11). Similar phenomena have been reported in previous studies and are often attributed to self-absorption or energy transfer mechanisms. , AgPDT scaffolds exhibited good reproducibility, as evidenced by the consistent fluorescence intensities and the F 0/F values of three independently prepared batches across different MPEA concentrations (Figures S12–S14). AgPDT also exhibited good long-term stability, as shown by the similar fluorescence intensities and F 0/F values of the as-synthesized samples and those stored for 5 months across various MPEA concentrations (Figure S15). These results collectively demonstrate that AgPDT is a promising fluorescent material for MPEA detection.

To further assess the selectivity of AgPDT, a series of structurally analogous amines was investigated. Under daylight, the pure AgPDT solution appears nearly colorless (Figure a). Under UV light, it exhibits a distinct orange fluorescence, which is differentially quenched upon the addition of various amines at 900 mM. Among them, the quenching effect is most obvious when MPEA is added. The fluorescence intensity changes of the AgPDT solution in response to various amines were systematically evaluated at four concentration levels (300 nM, 300 μM, 300 mM, and 900 mM) (Figures b and S16–S18). At each concentration, the addition of MPEA leads to a more pronounced fluorescence quenching compared to that of other amines. Similarly, the F 0/F values for MPEA are consistently higher than those of interfering amines at each concentration level and increase markedly with rising concentration (Figure c). In contrast, the F 0/F values of the other amines exhibit minimal variation across the concentration range. Notably, at 900 mM, the F 0/F value for MPEA is 450% higher than that of the interfering amines (Figure d). These results establish AgPDT as a highly selective fluorescent probe for MPEA detection.

To further explore the fluorescence detection mechanism, theoretical calculations and X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) were carried out. The alignment of the HOMO energy levels of AgPDT (−5.33 eV) and MPEA (−5.08 eV) supports electron transfer from the HOMO of MPEA to the HOMO of AgPDT, leading to fluorescence quenching (Figure a). Additionally, XPS analysis of the N 1s regions (shifting from 400.0 to 399.3 eV and 398.6 to 398.1 eV, respectively) exhibits shifts to lower binding energies upon interaction with MPEA, further validating the electron transfer (Figure b). − In order to probe the specific process of electron transfer, the molecular interactions during quenching were further simulated by using molecular dynamics. MPEA molecules preferentially approach the AgPDT framework, with the N atoms gradually moving closer to the N atom from the pyridine moieties of AgPDT (Figure c). Meanwhile, the hydrogen bond forms between the H atom from the CH3 group in MPEA and the S atom in AgPDT (d C–H⋯S = 2.65 Å). UV–vis measurements revealed time-dependent absorbance changes at 293 nm, and the adsorption kinetics can be well described by a pseudo-second-order kinetic model (Figures S19 and S20). FT-IR results show intensity changes and broadening of the methyl C–H stretching bands, together with fingerprint-region perturbations, evidencing C–H⋯S hydrogen-bond formation between AgPDT and MPEA (Figure S21). , XPS analysis of the S 2p regions exhibits shifts to lower binding energies (shifting from 162.7 to 162.2 eV and from 161.7 to 161.2 eV, respectively), further corroborating that the hydrogen bonds facilitate charge transfer (Figure d). These findings highlight the effectiveness of synergistic noncovalent interaction-driven sensing, supporting the strategy as a viable model for MPEA and related analyte detection.

In conclusion, we have synthesized a CP-based fluorescence sensing material, AgPDT, that enables highly efficient and selective detection of MPEA through synergistic noncovalent interaction. The unique structural design of AgPDT exposes abundant N and S active sites, enabling charge transfer between the N atom from pyridine moieties in AgPDT and the N atoms in MPEA, while the S atoms form hydrogen bonds (2.65 Å) with the CH3 groups of MPEA. These interactions facilitate efficient charge transfer and specific recognition of MPEA. AgPDT exhibits remarkable selectivity for MPEA, with an F 0/F value that is 450% higher than that of structurally similar amines at 900 mM. Moreover, AgPDT achieves an ultralow LOD below 1 nM, suggesting a potential application for drug detection. This work not only establishes a highly effective platform for the sensitive and selective detection of illicit drugs but also presents a universal strategy for the development of next-generation fluorescent sensors.

Experimental Methods

The AgPDT single crystal was synthesized by first dissolving AgNO3 (0.1 mmol, 17.0 mg) in 3 mL of DMF, followed by mixing with an aqueous solution of PDT (0.2 mmol, 22.0 mg in 3 mL of deionized water) in a sealed glass vessel. The mixture was then heated at 45 °C for 24 h under static conditions to facilitate crystal growth.

The synthesized AgPDT crystals were first ground into a fine powder. A total of 10 mg was weighed and dispersed in 100 mL of n-hexane. The mixture was sonicated at room temperature for 10 min to obtain a homogeneous AgPDT suspension with a final concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. The AgPDT solutions used in the experiments were all 0.1 mg/mL. A series of MPEA solutions with varying concentrations were prepared by directly mixing commercially available pure MPEA with n-hexane in different proportions.

The first-principles calculations were conducted using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). A tilted bilayer structure was employed to simulate the electronic properties of the crystal, ensuring a sufficient system thickness while fully exposing the pyridine group on the surface. In the adsorption simulations, the MPEA molecule was initially positioned away from the surface to allow natural structural relaxation with the computational unit cell containing 16 silver ions. All structural optimizations and self-consistent calculations utilized the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional under the generalized gradient approximation (GGA), with a 2 × 3 × 1 k-point grid. The force convergence criterion for structural optimization was set to 0.01 eV/Å, and the energy convergence threshold for self-consistency was 1 × 10–6 eV. To ensure accurate vacuum level referencing, a vacuum layer exceeding 20 Å was applied to both the crystal and the MPEA molecule. Given the extremely weak band dispersion of the crystal, its conduction band minimum (CBM) and valence band maximum (VBM) were safely approximated as the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), respectively.
