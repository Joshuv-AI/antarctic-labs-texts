Evaluation of an Esophageal‑Protective Lithium Coin‑Cell Battery Within Current Management Paradigms
Authors: Kris R. Jatana, Keith Rhoades, Toby Litovitz, Jackson Moore, Aaron A. Cadotte, Jamie Mueller, Mariya Dytso, Ian N. Jacobs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the first lithium coin‐cell battery (CCB) technology on the consumer market with ingestion burn protection claims in context of medical management practices.Study DesignCadaveric porcine esophageal model.SettingTertiary care children's hospital research institute and third‐party laboratory.MethodsAnterior‐posterior X‐ray imaging was performed. Using in vitro porcine tissue segments, traditional CCBs and protective CCBs were assessed for visible appearance and surface pH over 60 minutes; another group was assessed for 24 hours. A separate experiment evaluated existing protocols to administer honey/sucralfate and perform 0.25% acetic acid irrigation.ResultsAll protective CCBs demonstrated diagnostic double‐ring/halo sign on X‐ray. Traditional CCBs demonstrated diffusely highly alkaline pH of 13 and significant tissue necrosis. For protective CCBs, regions of tissue pH increased to 12‐13; however, substantially reduced tissue injury without obvious necrosis was noted. At 24 hours, protective CCBs compared to traditional CCBs maintained reduced visible tissue injury at both positive and negative poles. Honey, sucralfate or 0.25% acetic acid irrigations effectively neutralized pH.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this is the first study in the medical literature to assess recently marketed protective CCBs. X‐ray diagnosis, preremoval honey/sucralfate use, emergent removal for esophageal lodgment, postremoval 0.25% acetic acid esophageal irrigation practices should not change. While protective CCBs showed substantial reduction of injury severity, the highly alkaline pH at negative pole still warrants neutralization treatments and further study. Widespread adoption of advanced technologies for child safety across the battery, electronics, and consumer goods industries could prevent severe injuries or deaths.

Lithium coin‐cell batteries (CCBs) are commonly found in household electronics, and when ingested and lodge in the esophagus can lead to significant morbidity and mortality. 1 , 2 , 3 As designed, lithium CCBs contain 3 V of electric potential and are commonly used in everyday toys, tools, and general electronics. Analysis of the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System data from 1990 to 2009 estimated that every 3 hours a child presented with a battery‐related complaint to an emergency department in the United States 4 ; this more than doubled over the next decade to every 75 minutes. 5 Serious injury can occur within 2 hours of a coin‐cell or button battery lodging in the esophagus. Medical professionals should be familiar with X‐ray findings of round metallic objects in the esophagus, and zooming in can help differentiate a monetary coin from a CCB (Figure 1).

On radiographs, (A) a lithium coin‐cell battery/button battery has a double‐ring or halo sign in appearance; (B) coins have a homogenous appearance. Reproduced with permission from KR Jatana.

Unfortunately, traditional lithium CCBs in the esophagus are associated with significant morbidity and mortality. It is alarming that 12.6% of children under age 6 who ingested 20 mm diameter lithium CCB developed a major complication such as perforation, tracheo‐esophageal fistula, vascular fistula, esophageal stricture, vocal cord paresis/paralysis, or spondylodiscitis. 2 Unfortunately, many patients require gastrostomy tubes for feeding and/or tracheostomy tubes for airway management, as well as multiple major reconstructive surgical procedures. Systematic reviews of button or coin‐cell battery cases have described numerous airway 6 and vascular complications. 7 The National Capital Poison Center has documented 280 severe esophageal or airway injuries 8 and 71 fatalities related to button or coin cell battery ingestion. 9

In 2012, the National Button Battery Task Force (BBTF) was initially established through the American Academy of Pediatrics and American Broncho‐Esophagological Association to bring a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach to reducing battery‐related injuries. 3 The following mission statement was initially created: A collaborative effort of representatives from relevant organizations in industry, medicine, public health and government to develop, coordinate and implement strategies to reduce the incidence of button battery injuries in children.

More recently, the National BBTF has transitioned into the Global BBTF as an independent open forum for productive conversations between all stakeholders. As previously reported, several safety interventions have been employed over the years, 10 but as injuries have continued to occur, the innovative approach to hazard reduction of the battery itself was clearly necessary.

In October 2023, the American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery published a Position Statement that clearly defined what constitutes “safe” button and coin cell battery technology—technology that does not cause serious or life‑threatening injury if lodged in the body. 11 This statement has been endorsed or supported by many organizations, encouraging the battery industry to make a public commitment to bringing such technology to the consumer market. Now in 2026, Energizer becomes the first battery manufacturer to reach this critically important safety milestone of a commercially available CCB with the claim it “prevents ingestion burn” on its packaging. On the positive pole, these CCBs have a bitter coating to potentially deter ingestion and a blue dye to potentially aid in early detection of ingestion. Unfortunately, the use of bitterants has not been able to prevent accidental ingestion of other items. 12 , 13 Prior in vitro porcine esophageal studies by members of our team have shown that commercially available bitter‐coated only or blue dye/bitter‐coated CCBs have similar X‐ray findings, and there is no esophageal‐protective effect of these coatings compared to standard uncoated CCBs; in the presence of bitter or blue dye coatings, honey or sucralfate irrigations were still effective at preremoval injury mitigation. 14 The purpose of this current study is to assess the recently released protective titanium‐based CCB known as the Ultimate Child ShieldTM, within the context of current medical diagnosis and management practices. Specifically, to answer these questions: (1)Is the radiographic appearance of traditional and protective CCBs the same?(2)Does visual assessment within a porcine esophageal tissue model indicate any injury severity difference between the use of protective CCBs and traditional control CCBs?(3)Do ingested protective CCBs still produce hydroxide that needs neutralization?(4)Does the protective effect of these new CCBs still work with currently recommended pre‐removal honey or sucralfate esophageal injury mitigation strategies?(5)If a known protective CCB was ingested, is emergent esophageal removal still recommended?(6)Is irrigation with 0.25% sterile acetic acid in the operating room still recommended after protective CCB removal from the esophagus?

Methods

Studies were performed in a laboratory at the Research Institute at Nationwide Children's Hospital and third‐party laboratory with in vitro juvenile cadaveric porcine esophageal models. Lithium CCBs were identified by their imprint codes: CR designates the chemistry (lithium), the first 2 numbers designate the diameter (20 mm), and the last 2 numbers designate the thickness (5.0/3.2/2.5/1.6 mm). 1 , 2 , 3 All batteries were purchased from retail stores. Esophageal tissue was divided into 7 to 8 cm segments; tissue surface pH was assessed with litmus paper. Use of 0.9% saline was used to simulate saliva present in the esophagus. Voltage was measured with a multimeter. All photographic images show batteries flipped over and adjacent to the area where they had been applied; the negative pole was face down (lower portion) and the positive pole face up (upper portion) with the tissue folded as shown in Figure 2 during the experiment.

Example of porcine esophageal tissue folded over CR2032 CCBs to simulate esophageal lodgment; negative pole facing down and positive facing pole up in contact with tissue. CCBs, coin‐cell batteries.

Radiographic Imaging: Standard anterior‐posterior X‐rays were performed for the protective lithium coin‐cell batteries CR2032, CR2025, and CR2016.

Esophageal Tissue Experiment 1: Basic Mechanism

The following CCBs were collected: traditional CR2032 (x2) and protective CR2032 (x24). Initial tissue temperature and CCB voltage were recorded. The positive pole of protective CCBs were precisely cleaned using a water‐soaked wipe to remove the combined blue dye/bitterant to not interfere with visual assessment and detection of surface pH. Each CCB was placed into a separate esophageal segment. Every five minutes, temperature, voltage, and pH was recorded for 60 minutes duration. 12 protective CR2032s had administered ~0.25 mL of 0.9% normal saline (S) every 5 min, the other 12 were kept on tissue with no additional saline, will be noted as saline‐free (SF).

Esophageal Tissue Experiment 2: Mechanism and Mitigation Strategies

The following CCBs were used: traditional CR2032 (x2) and protective CR2032 (x6), all trials were performed in duplicate for each irrigation solution. Each battery was placed on a separate esophageal segment. Blue dye and bitterant on positive pole were removed similar to Experiment #1. Every 10 minutes the traditional CR2032 was irrigated with 10 mL 0.9% normal saline and esophageal tissue surface pH was assessed with litmus paper for 60 minutes. For each of the protective CCBs, 10 mL of either 0.9% normal saline, honey, or sucralfate was irrigated, and pH was assessed every 10 minutes. Visual inspection for tissue injury, pH, and voltage was also assessed at 24 hours for the normal saline groups (traditional and protective CR2032); at 24 hours, these were irrigated with 150 mL of 0.25% sterile acetic acid to assess efficacy of hydroxide neuralization.

Results

Radiographic Imaging: Anterior‐posterior X‐ray findings of each of the protective CCBs (CR2032, CR2025, and CR2016) demonstrated that a double‐ring or halo sign was consistently present, shown in Figure 3.

Radiographic Imaging. Standard anterior‐posterior X‐ray findings of protective CCBs (CR2032, CR2025, and CR2016); all demonstrated a double‐ring or halo sign.

Esophageal Tissue Experiment 1: Control CCBs were observed at the 60‐minute time point and performed as expected with tissue necrosis, bubbling, and discoloration of tissue (Figure 4). Protective CCBs had no measurable esophageal tissue temperature change throughout the 60‐minute study; bubbles along the perimeter of the anode formed within 15 minutes consistent with hydrolysis of water into hydrogen gas and hydroxide, pH increased to 9. Localized tissue pH increased from 7.5 to 12 in 35 minutes for both S and SF samples, peaking at an alkalinity of 13 in each S and SF study (Figures 5 and 6). For protective CCBs, the first visible signs of tissue injury were noted at 40 minutes for S samples and 50 minutes for SF samples; visible tissue injury was substantially reduced with no obvious tissue necrosis or battery casing corrosion seen, in contrast to traditional CCBs. Protective CCB peak voltage drop for S group was 0.32 V whereas peak voltage drop for SF group was 0.24 V over the 60‐minute period.

Esophageal Tissue Experiment 2: The traditional CCBs showed an esophageal tissue surface pH elevation to a diffusely high pH of 13 between the 30 to 60‐minute duration of the experiment and significant battery corrosion and extensive tissue necrosis was demonstrated which was even more significant at 24 hours (Figure 7). For protective CCBs, the esophageal tissue surface pH increased to a level of 10 at 10 minutes and 12 at 30 minutes of exposure time. Initial signs of visible tissue injury were identified at the negative pole at 40 minutes of exposure time, and Figure 8 shows the sequential findings through 24 hours when 0.25% acetic acid irrigation adequately neutralized any hydroxide present. The honey and sucralfate irrigation groups effectively neutralized any hydroxide formation produced within 10 minutes of exposure, as the surface pH remained at 6 between 10 to 60 minutes (Figures 9A and B, and 10). Slightly worse focal tissue injury was observed at junction of negative‐positive pole step‐off in trial 2 of the honey group over sucralfate group. Over 24‐hour timeframe, CCB voltage drop was more significant for the traditional CCBs (48.3%) compared to the protective CCB (8.9%) as shown in Figure 11 which implies less hydrolysis took place. Of note, there was no further change in voltage of the protective CCB in the 23 hours beyond the initial 1 hour of exposure time; however, over the next 23 hours, the traditional CCBs continued to drop voltage.

Discussion

For traditional lithium CCBs, the primary mechanism caustic damage occurs through electrolysis and rapid production of hydroxide ions. This alkaline caustic injury, powered by the voltage of the battery, induces a liquefactive necrosis leading to deeper tissue injury over time. In prior studies, it has been demonstrated that in as little as 15 minutes of impaction time visible tissue injury begins to occur 15 ; in the current study this was seen within 10 minutes. This highly alkaline caustic injury can be slowed by combined coating (viscous) and neutralization strategies using either ingested honey or sucralfate (both weakly acidic), and current guidelines recommend 10 mL of every 10 minutes until the battery can be removed. 16 , 17 Due to this highly alkaline injury, after removal 50 to 150 mL 0.25% sterile acetic acid irrigation has been recommended. 15 , 17 , 18 , 19

While acute life‐threatening or disabling injuries from CCBs lodged in the esophagus are the major concern, a link between other alkaline caustic injuries of the esophagus and development of esophageal carcinoma several decades later has been previously reported. 20 , 21 , 22 Patients with significant esophageal alkaline caustic injury from a button or coin‐cell battery may require surveillance endoscopy on a case‐by‐case basis. This potential long‐term risk underscores the need to prevent alkaline caustic esophageal injuries by continued innovative efforts to minimize hydroxide production as much as possible. In addition, a better understanding of the positive pole where acidic substrate accumulates and extent of coagulative necrosis is needed. Future histological studies in a live animal model with longer exposure times (>24 hours) should be performed to further assess the clinical significance of any tissue changes for protective CCBs and the ability for this tissue to appropriately heal over time.

Standard medical diagnosis on anterior‐posterior X‐ray looking for a double‐ring or halo sign for both traditional and protective CCBs should continue. Our study supports that for protective CCBs even within 60 minutes, regions of eosphageal tissue surface pH increased to 12‐13 which would typically be considered caustic range, whereas traditional CCBs more quickely featured a diffuse tissue surface pH up to 13 over the entirity of the CCB contact area. This implies that the overall quantity of hydroxide formation is less with the protective CCB. Since hydroxide formation at esophageal tissue surface was still detected, this justifies continued use of pre‐removal honey or sucralfate mitigation, 23 emergent CCB removal in operating room, and postremoval esophageal irrigation with 0.25% sterile acetic acid 18 per current practice guidelines. The National Capital Poison Center provides a clinical guideline for battery ingestion which can be found here. 17 Hospitals should continue to employ quality improvement efforts to expedite care. 24 , 25 , 26 A summary of responses to relevant clinical questions are shown in Table 1.

Clinical Questions Raised by Medical Professionals and Current Recommendations Based Upon the Results of This Study

The focus of this current study has been to assess the new protective lithium CCBs, however, it is important to point out that non‐lithium button batteries can still also cause severe injuries; these include alkaline, silver oxide, and zinc‐air chemistries. Alkaline and silver oxide button batteries can also cause severe esophageal injury when lodged in the esophagus. 15 While the zinc‐air is not an esophageal injury risk, it can still cause intranasal or ear canal injury (dry environment). 27 In the future, similar protective innovations should be pursued by battery manufacturers for nonlithium button batteries.

The prospective collection of human clinical outcome data is critically important to better understand whether ongoing liquefactive tissue necrosis occurs from presence of hydroxide produced and the adequacy of current neutralization practices, even with protective CCBs. Continued data collection directly from medical professionals is critical to assess clinical outcomes of all foreign body aspiration or ingestion events to drive future injury prevention efforts. It is important that these incidents including a clinical history with high‐resolution photographs of the object (ie, protective vs traditional coin‐cell battery, food, small part hazards, etc.), imaging and/or injury images are reported to a data source; the Global Injury Research Collaborative (GIRC), a nonprofit organization established a global injury database for this purpose. 28 , 29 The “GIRC App” can be downloaded for no charge by medical professionals from the iOS/App store and relevant de‐identified data including photos can be submitted in as little as two minutes.

Study limitations: While previously showing concordance with human clinical findings, the in vitro porcine esophageal model and use of intermittent saline irrigation may not exactly model human experience. Further, to visualize tissue injury and monitor surface pH, we removed dye and bitterant applied to the battery surface and assumed that this removal would not impact the efficacy of the titanium‐based protective CCB mechanism. Our previous work showed that the bitter/blue dye coatings did not have any protective effects for traditional CCBs and honey and sucralfate were still effective to help mitigate injury. 14 Finally, our study extended up to 24 hours, batteries may be lodged in the human esophagus for longer durations of time.

This study demonstrated that medical professionals should not change current practices after the release of a protective CCB technology on the consumer market. A double‐ring or halo sign was consistently present for protective CCBs, allowing an accurate radiographic diagnosis of CCB ingestion, but does not allow radiologic differentiation of traditional versus protective CCBs. As a result, the clinician is unlikely to know or be able to confirm whether the swallowed battery incorporated protective technology, thus must proceed with immediate removal of all CCBs. Even with the knowledge that an ingested battery was a protective CCB, the evidence of that hydroxide is still generated, and the absence of data beyond 24 hours of esophageal tissue contact, leaves us with no safe clinical choice other than prompt removal. As hydroxide formation still occurs at tissue surface, standard mitigation strategies before and after removal should still be employed. Our study confirmed that use of preremoval honey or sucralfate per current clinical guidelines did not adversely affect the protective mechanism of these new titanium‐based lithium CCBs. Lastly, postremoval 0.25% sterile acetic acid irrigation in the operating room should still be performed to try to neutralize any hydroxide present. Based on current evidence, this protective, titanium‐based CCB technology is a significant advancement in child safety. However, continued efforts to minimize hydroxide formation should be explored, the observed effects on tissue and clinical significance should be confirmed with future in vivo animal studies including histologic analysis, and clinical data showing the course and outcome of pediatric ingestions of protective CCBs should be assiduously collected and analyzed. We urge other battery manufacturers to adopt a titanium architecture or other technology that reduces severe esophageal injuries. Ideally, protective CCBs will eliminate the potential for injury beyond that caused by the foreign body obstruction itself. The universal adoption of the most advanced protective technologies by all battery manufacturers and by all electronics manufacturers for use in their products is essential to help prevent severe injury or death from CCB ingestion.
