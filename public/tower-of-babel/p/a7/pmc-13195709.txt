Interprofessional collaboration in primary care in Germany – collective irresponsibility, one-way-street or colourful bouquet of services? A qualitative study with stakeholders from various professions
Authors: Johanna Mink, Attila Altiner, Simon Schwill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterprofessional collaboration (IPC) in outpatient care can improve patient outcomes and satisfaction, health prevention and economics. German primary care (PC) is challenged by an ongoing demand for health care professionals to extend services. PC in Germany is regularly provided by general practitioners and medical assistants. Models on how to establish interprofessional primary care (IPPC) are scarce.ObjectivesAim of the study was to explore stakeholders′ suggestions on IPC in German PC to support development of an IPPC model.MethodsProfessionals from various nation-wide institutions and associations with professional backgrounds in general practice, medical assistance, nursing, physician assistance, social work and specialist medicine as well as from health insurance and law, were recruited. Semi-structured interviews were performed. Deductively identified main categories included strategies for implementing IPC on system-level, organisational-level, team-level and individual-level and were coded in sub-categories inductively.ResultsOn system-level professionals predominantly named accountability, funding and remuneration. On organisational level documentation systems, the necessity of coordination and the availability of qualified personnel were discussed. On team-level interviewees focussed on the necessity of a mutual understanding of roles and responsibilities, clear and collaborative distribution of tasks and team leadership as well as patient integration and health literacy support. On individual-level the acceptance of new roles and professions was pointed out.ConclusionTo establish IPPC in Germany, remuneration and accountability need to be addressed. Models of IPPC need to integrate suggestions explored, a step-by-step approach for general practitioners to enable change-management and longitudinal support to promote adapted leadership and sustainable implementation.Keywords: Primary care, interprofessional collaboration, change management, qualitative research

Introduction

In 2010 the World Health Organisation stated the importance of interprofessional collaboration (IPC) and published a respective framework for action on interprofessional education (IPE) and collaboration [1]. It has been shown that IPC in hospitals can have positive effects on health care professionals, patients and the organisation [2]. Similarly, IPC in outpatient care can improve patient outcomes, health prevention, patient satisfaction and economics [3]. Internationally, approaches to improve primary care (PC) by redesigning collaboration have shown promising results. In Europe, the importance to investigate innovative PC models with focus on patient-centred IPC has been pointed out and there is a call for research on individual-, team and system-level [4]. More recent research has shown that IPC can be influenced by various factors on four levels [2,5]:

With focus on allied health professions Seaton et al. [6] summarised the importance of concrete aspects that represent all four levels described above, like shared philosophy (individual and team level), communication and interaction, power and hierarchy (team level), physical environment (organisation level), and financial considerations (system level) which all impact IPC.

In Germany, PC is generally provided as outpatient medical treatment by general practitioners (GPs) with the support of medical assistants (MA). Around 2010, ideas to change German PC evolved [7]. In consequence, further training for MAs was established in order to enable delegation of medical tasks and thus relieve the GPs. Currently, academisation of health care professionals is being promoted in Germany and thus new professions like physician assistants (PAs), primary care manager (PCMs) and advanced practice nurses (APNs) are emerging. Furthermore, PC centres in Germany like the patient-oriented centres for primary and long-term care named PORT are being developed [8]. Fundamental domains of PC centres are described as needs-oriented GP services, mediating, health-promoting (nursing) specialists, team-concept of equal IPC, and cooperation agreement with the respective municipality or administration [9]. To foster the realisation and implementation of IPC, the Family Physician Association Baden-Wuerttemberg planned to develop an adapted interprofessional primary care centre, with similar preconditions, which should be feasible to be implemented step by step into the current system.

Based on the MRC framework for complex interventions [10] multi-professional perspectives should be integrated right from the beginning. Hence in this study, a potential design of interprofessional primary care (IPPC) centres was developed by the GP Association Baden-Wuerttemberg based on international examples and adapted to the German context. In order to revise the design and to consider relevant determinants on IPPC, aim of this study was to collect perspectives on IPC of relevant stakeholders representing various health care professions, legislation and health care insurance companies.

Methods

Design

An exploratory qualitative study based on semi-structured interviews was conducted with various stakeholders in the wider field of primary care in order to gather suggestions on IPC in German PC, that should be considered in a new, realistic model of German IPPC. The study is reported with the help of a COREQ checklist (Supplementary Appendix 1).

Recruitment

Within purposive nation-wide sampling nine professionals from different health professional backgrounds having a stakeholders-position within their field were identified and invited for participation via e-mail by the two researchers, who also conducted the interviews, both working as scientific researchers. One male researcher (SSC, Dr. med.) with a professional background in general practice, medical and interprofessional education and experiences in qualitative research was known to some of the participants due to collaboration in former projects. The other female researcher (JM, Dr. sc. hum.) with professional background in health services research, gerontology, general and interprofessional education and experiences in qualitative research, was unfamiliar with all participants. For the interview both interviewers introduced their role as researchers. Interview participation was voluntary and without financial compensation. Interviewees received information on the study and data collection. All interviewees were relevant stakeholders in outpatient care who represent the professions that could be part of an IPPC centre as well as experts in health economics and law. Expertise included a good overview on their profession, the German health care system and organisation of outpatient care. The interviewees represented the seven perspectives needed to gain first insight into possible interprofessional primary care solutions (medical assistance, nursing, physician assistance, social work, specialist and general practice, health insurance and advocacy) and with nine interviews code saturation was reached [11].

Data collection

In order to gather stakeholder’s opinions in general and in greater detail a semi-structured interview guide was developed by the two authors JM and SSC based on the theoretical approach of the MRC framework [10]. This strategy for intervention adaptation based on the MRC-framework’s suggestion to identify and address (key) uncertainties, engage stakeholders and consider the context factors, mechanisms of change and outcomes in order to adapt the intervention and plan a feasibility study in a next step. Knowledge about IPC in PC that was deepened by both authors in advance, was realised within questions that developed from general to detailed and were pretested within a cognitive interview with the other author (AA) [12]. Main aspects of this guideline were questioned with general questions first on experiences with IPC, potential benefits and challenges of an interprofessional health-care team including strategies to deal with these determinants, to more detailed questions on possible distribution of tasks within the professions, organisation and necessary structures for collaboration including digitalisation, involvement of patients, external cooperation, advantages compared to regular primary care and multidisciplinary care centres. After a written informed consent was received from the interviewees, online interviews (Webex by cisco, San José, CA, USA) were conducted by two researchers (JM & SSC) in a tandem, video-recorded, and transcribed verbatim (pseudonyms #1 to #9).

Data analysis

Qualitative content analysis [13] was conducted deductively based on relevant levels influencing implementation of IPC (System, organisational, team, individual) and complemented by further inductive coding. In order to provide intersubjective reliability, the authors (JM & SSC) analysed the interviews independently and discussed the process and coding system on a regular basis and adapted the categories until agreement was reached. AA was integrated if discrepancies occurred. Quotes to exemplify the categories were identified and translated into English.

Results

Nine interviews were conducted within three weeks from 01.03.2023 − 23.03.2023 (mean duration 49 mins) online. Out of the first nine contacted persons one refused participation because of scheduling difficulties, so another person with a similar professional background was contacted and consented to participate. Interviewed professionals were leading stakeholders from the fields of GP, health insurance, MA, medical law, nursing, PA, social work and case management. System level

The interviewees described possible challenges due to unresolved assumption of liability, activity and delegation situation and a distribution of tasks that is not based on competencies.

“Yes, of course, there is still a bit of an unresolved liability risk. The professional roles and liability issues are not yet fully defined in the area of physician assistants, nor for community health nurses.” (#7)

Informal consultations were described as current strategies to deal with legal restrictions. Interviewees mentioned the need for official changes in tasks and a consistent legal regulation.

“So, we’re training people who are supposed to be qualified in doing a lot of things but aren’t allowed to, and that’s nonsense. On the one hand we have the reality of care, where we need people to provide support, and on the other hand we have those we train but can’t use, which is somehow a pretty glaring contradiction.” (#8)

Financial requirements and resources were discussed as challenges. The lack of clarity regarding remuneration regulations and the funding institution, as well as a possible balancing act between flat payment (“Pauschalisierung”) and pay-for-performance (“Einzelleistung”) were described. It was criticised that reimbursements of procedures differ whether performed by physicians or non-physicians (e.g. home-visits).

Organisational level

The interviewees mentioned availability of qualified professionals as challenging.

Documentation was described as potential weakness due to interface problems, resulting in loss of information and hence leading to longer waiting times for patients and unnecessary repetitions of examinations.

Interviewees suggested standardised multi-professional meetings on a regular basis in terms of case-conferences, short daily meetings for organisational exchange and regular longer meetings in greater timely distance to address IPC. Quality management was described as important to scrutinise workflows in practices and to distribute tasks among the professions.

… targeted training in the area of interprofessional collaboration […]. Because you have to actually work on a common understanding. And this is not yet anchored in any of our job profiles.” (#5)

Team level

On team level it was stated that all team members need to know and understand their own and the others’ roles and responsibilities. Not knowing the roles, unstructured collaboration and poor agreements could result in “collective irresponsibility” (#1) and increased focus on one’s own professional perspective could hinder patient-centred care.

“ […] So, the idea that, when lots of professionals come together, that there’s still serious talk about patients, I think that’s very theoretical. I actually rarely experience that.” (#9)

Suggested solutions were the communication of responsibilities and roles within the team, role reversal or mutual work shadowing.

For coordination of care the individual stratification of patients to team members based on reasons for counselling, chronic conditions or the necessity to see a physician or not was suggested.

The development of team spirit and the sense of shared responsibility were said to be important for the IPPC team and external collaboration with physicians of other disciplines. Potential blurred boundaries and overlaps between professions like medical assistants and nurses were seen as reason for a moderated collaborative planning of teamwork.

“Well, it needs some kind of coordination function, if everyone starts at the same time and somehow, it’s difficult. So, the more collaboration, the more coordination is ultimately needed” (#2)

Also, a raised awareness for possible role conflicts and strategies for conflict resolution would have to be developed together.

Interviewees suggested that there could be a fixed contact person for patient integration and that patients need to know, that a physician is always available in the background. It was stated that giving all relevant information to and communicating openly with patients and including them in decision-making processes was helpful for building trust, making sure that patients are part of the team and strengthening patients’ health literacy.

“I believe that you always have to agree on the goal. […] And if I have a goal, then I can also derive measures from it. And who is then responsible for the various measures?” (#1)

Leadership

The necessity of leadership training was mentioned. Levels of hierarchy, heterachy and leadership were discussed differently.

A suggestion was to have more than one leader, namely one per profession and one for administration. Administrative leadership was said to be possibly fulfilled by any profession.

“That the various heads simply meet regularly. For example, from the therapy professions, from nursing, from the doctors, from the MAs, that they simply exchange information regularly.” (#4)

An interviewee voted for one final decision maker and added that the organisation itself should be functional, not hierarchical, but still with a leader who decides, which tasks are addressed by whom. A concern was mentioned that a pure chain of delegation would be initiated by the GPs without the possibility of mutual exchange and joint agreement.

Another suggestion was a shared leadership.

“… so, to even think that a single person can organize such a high level of complexity and ensure that it works for an individuum […] I think that’s nonsense.” (#9)

Within a self-organized team without leader or manager, supervisions on a regular basis and training for conflict resolution would be needed. Critique was that this form of organisation would be time consuming in implementation.

Individual level

It was considered challenging for patients if there is no longer standard contact with a physician and for physicians to accept the new professional groups. On the other hand, the necessity “to get away from this sacred doctoral treatment and restructure the care system” (#8) was stated.

“Now all these community health nurses are being discussed, and they are not only to be deployed in delegation, but also very generously in the context of substitution, which is why we are already seeing the panic that is going through the German Medical Association. […] Because you don’t want to give up any competencies, that’s on the one hand” (#8)

Possibly giving up services to other professions that are paid for was also mentioned as reason for medical reluctance.

Generally, the interviewed professionals had positive attitudes towards IPC but not all of them had the same understanding.

“… basically, there’s a colourful bouquet. I think it starts with time, listening and caressing […] And then recognizing deficits in order to be able to react to them. And if you can then also manage to actively offer various further-reaching either information or anything else […] then I think you’ve already gained a lot.” (#2)

An interviewee assumed that acceptance will come with positive experiences.

Discussion

In this study, we gained various perspectives on determinants and opportunities concerning IPC in German PC, which address different levels where changes can be influenced. Main aspects include funding, liability, documentation and communication, roles and responsibilities, coordination and leadership as well as attitudes and acceptance. Results of this study will be integrated in the development of a pilot study on the feasibility of an accordingly refined IPPC concept for German primary care practices including a programme theory that can be refined within the pilot study to inform implementation.

System level

Remuneration affects integration of IPC into PC. Financial investments aiming at the improvement of IPC within integrated care initiatives can result in better patient care [14]. Still, fee-for-services could be an economic constraint for IPC, since payment depends on services that are conducted or delegated by physicians, which perpetuates hierarchical structures [15]. Approaches are needed that remunerate IPC explicitly [16] and thus facilitate the organisation of regular team meetings as it is suggested by the professionals interviewed in this study to collaboratively plan, coordinate and reflect work processes. A compulsory primary care based health care in Germany [17] could induce IPC as a key-component of future PC.

In order to use existing resources efficiently, interviewees postulated that the professions represented in a primary care centre should be chosen according to local preconditions and not predefined. If not available, creating networks with other health care practitioners outside the practice can also be an efficient way of interprofessional work, which is not based on shared team identity or even colocation but functioning communication [18].

Organisational level

Communication between different healthcare professions is prone to errors due to differences in technical language or standards, which can lead to delays in care or treatment errors [19]. On the other hand, improved collaboration between healthcare professionals through IPE can prevent errors in patient care and improve patient outcomes [20]. Integrating new workflows, tasks and responsibilities in the quality management of the practice can ensure quality of care and patient safety. The lack of efficient information systems and compatible documentation was criticised as possible threat for efficient information exchange. In Germany, the introduction of electronic health files is in progress and obstacles comprise prevalence of paper-based documentation, existence of a plurality of documentation software impeding interoperability and lacking political regulations and incentives [21]. Still, the access to the complete patient’s history for all professionals has been described as facilitator for IPC in primary care centres [22].

Team and individual level

The variation in understanding IPC and in defining teams could indicate that the underlying concept of IPC lacks clarity. It was stated as important that all team members have the same understanding of IPC, their team and the respective roles and responsibilities. The relevance of role clarification within a PC team has been stated for Europe a decade ago, especially regarding newly developing professions [4]. A lack of role understanding by co-workers and patients can be an impeding factor for a positive work environment [and the knowledge about the others’ competencies, roles and responsibilities, a shared understanding of health care and a clear distribution of tasks and responsibilities and a sufficient exchange have also been described in terms of “bridging gaps”, “negotiating overlaps” and “creating spaces” [23] as tasks that need to be fulfilled by the professionals themselves. The challenge of insufficient meetings due to isolated focus on the own perspective of each team member hindering true collaboration can be interpreted as „… a type of protectionism where it mattered more to foreground one’s own professional identity than to find common ground.” [24]. Hence, in our study the role of a moderator in case discussions was mentioned as beneficial to keep track on how the care team can meet the patients’ needs. Mentioned reluctance of professional groups to engage in IPC has been described as professional closure strategy hindering IPC [24]. Still, a recent survey among German GPs showed that the majority of participants had positives attitudes and beliefs on reorganisation and shifting of tasks to other healthcare professions, especially younger GPs in the patient-centred care programs [25].

A strategy to foster IPC in outpatient care is the integration of IPE and “health care in outpatient care” into the curricula of health professions. In order to empower professionals in IPC, the National Interprofessional Competency Framework that focusses on domains that have also been mentioned by the interviewed professionals can be used as basis for educational strategies in PC teams [26].

The sense of belonging to a team, that has been addressed in our study in terms of shared philosophy, can be developed in informal interprofessional socialisation processes, that aim to “…break down misperceptions, prejudices, and stereotypes while maintaining professional uniqueness in their roles and scopes of practice” [27]. A shared team identity, sense of belonging to the team and mutual dependence can enable teamwork in the closest sense [28]. Furthermore, implementation of real IPC depends on the readiness of all team members to take on responsibility. Hierarchies in health care can be functional in complex, unpredictable settings but can also result in demotivation, poor communication, conflicts and hence strengthen professional silos and weaken efficient interprofessional collaboration [29]. In primary care practices in Germany the practice owner mostly is a GP, who employs other health care professionals, which hinders collaboration on eye-level. In order to enable true participation, hierarchical structures need to be diminished for the sake of shared responsibility, trust and efficient communication [30]. Patients’ acceptance of newly developing academized professions can have an influence on hierarchies– conceptualising them as replacement for the physician can restrain their actual professional role [31]. Accordingly, offering the patients more time and/or more services could be a strategy to strengthen the respective professional scope of practice. Furthermore, MAs with more responsibility might have a broader knowledge and understanding of the patients’ situation [32] and patients can discuss further aspects that cannot be addressed within the physicians’ consultations [33].

Strengths and limitations

The interviewee’s suggestions were collected in a structured approach which supports successful development of an appropriate intervention. This is a qualitative study with all limitations accompanying this design. One limitation could have been a possible interviewer or response bias since one of the two researchers (SSC) was known to the interviewees. In order to reduce this bias, interviews were conducted in a tandem. Data analysis could have been influenced by a confirmation bias, which was tried to be reduced by coding of all interviews by two researchers and regular exchange as well as in case of disagreement between the two the third author, who only read the transcripts without knowing the interviewed person was included. Nine interviews were conducted on total, as code saturation was reached.

Recommendations to practice, policy, and research

In terms of mitigating changes the practice change model for primary care describes “motivation of key-stakeholders”, “instrumental, personal and interactive resources for change”, “motivators outside the practice” and “opportunities for change” [34] as necessary influencing factors. From this study, it can be concluded that determinants of the intervention include 1. funding for team development and shifted tasks, 2. clarification of roles and responsibilities on system (legally) and team (tasks) level, 3.individual tailoring according to practice setting and available resources, 4. understanding and acceptance of IPC and task shifts by all practice members, 5. flat hierarchies and shared leadership, 6. understanding and acceptance by patients, 7. beneficial effects on quality and safety of patient care and 8. support of information exchange, interactions and team development. This resembles the domains of the Integrated Checklist of Determinants of Practice (TICD) [35], which can be helpful for structured piloting and implementation. In summary, recommendations to practice, policy and research could be the following (programme theory):

To improve primary care efficiency and quality, primary care practices should be supported to integrate at least one non-physician academised health care professional. They need to reorganise tasks and workflows including a shift from hierarchical leadership to heterarchical collaboration by optimising information exchange, interaction, documentation and integration of patients in decision-making processes on eye-level. Strategies include guaranteeing space and time for (moderated) team meetings, equal remuneration of services delivered by professionals of the PC team and abolishing single leadership styles. Considering the various preconditions in and around primary care practices the IPPC concept needs to be adaptable to available resources. Combination of external support and individually tailored intervention strategies can lead to practice specific change processes that are accepted and valued by employees resulting in a sustaining implementation of the innovation.

Conclusion

Fostering IPPC in Germany is appreciated by various stakeholders. Professional’s suggestions in this study served to develop a feasible model of IPPC as a first step as well as to focus and accompany necessary political measures for remuneration, legal frameworks and training of new health professions. GPs will need support for the conversion of practices and the new IP teams will need support for adapted leadership and the change-management process while new ways on working together in PC get implemented step by step.
