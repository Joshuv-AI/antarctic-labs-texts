Real-World Diagnostic Performance for Heart Failure Events of TriageHF® Algorithm
Authors: Mariana Pereira Santos, Sérgio Campos, Catarina Gomes, Maria Trepa, Bruno Bragança, Maria Isilda Oliveira, Mariana Rajão, Bruno Delgado, Sara Fernandes, Sónia Magalhães, Maria João Sousa, António Pinheiro Vieira, Hipólito Reis, André Luz, Mário Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesHeart failure (HF) remote monitoring programs collect HF-related parameters from cardiac implantable electronic devices, enabling early interventions and reducing healthcare burdens. This study aimed to characterize HF patients, alert burden, and the association between TriageHF® alerts and HF decompensation.MethodsThis retrospective observational study, 134 patients enrolled in an outpatient remote monitoring HF program with the TriageHF® algorithm between February 2022 and July 2023. Data on alert frequency, data driving the alerts, and alert duration were collected. Clinical outcomes included unplanned emergency room visits, HF hospitalizations, all-cause death, and a composite of these events.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 1.7 years, 77 individual high-risk alerts (0.44 alerts per patient-year) occurred. HF patients who eventually had an alert were similar to those without any alerts, except for the increased prevalence of chronic kidney disease, stroke history and use of furosemide. The event rate was 1.1% per month, and the alert state was 3.2% of the total follow-up duration. Six events occurred during high-risk days (event rate of 7.9% per month vs. 0.8% per month during standard-risk days). TriageHF® alerts demonstrated a sensitivity of 35%, a specificity of 71%, a positive predictive value of 25%, and a negative predictive value of 80% for predicting a composite of HF hospitalization, unplanned ER visits, and death.ConclusionsThe TriageHF® algorithm identified periods with a higher incidence of HF-related adverse events, supporting efficient resource allocation. The low alert burden also supports feasibility in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Heart failure, Telemetry, Defibrillators, Cardiac resynchronization therapy devices, Health resources

INTRODUCTION

Heart failure (HF) is a leading cause of morbimortality and is associated with a substantial economic burden to the healthcare system.1) Despite the modern advances in treatments, the rate of hospitalization for HF decompensation remains unacceptably high, with each patient being hospitalized, on average, once every year after the initial diagnosis.2) Hospitalizations reduce patients’ quality of life and longevity and represent the main driver of economic costs related to this disease.3)

Remote patient monitoring seeks to optimally manage health conditions outside healthcare facilities. The use of remote data continuously collected by cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIED) has gained increased attention as these devices provide insight into several physiological variables primarily implicated in the pathophysiology of HF decompensation and that precede symptoms and weight gain due to congestion.4) Devices like implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICD) and cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) are established recommended treatment options5) to a significant proportion of HF patients, independent of their potential use in telemonitoring. The concept underlying all the remote monitoring of these devices in HF is the early recognition by the physician of these actionable data that will allow a timely therapeutic intensification to avoid further cardiac decompensation and hospitalization.4)

The TriageHF® algorithm early validation studies established that patients in the “high-risk” category were 10 times more likely to experience an HF hospitalization within the next 30 days compared to those in the low-risk group.6) Subsequent analysis of the RAFT trial confirmed a 10.7-fold increase in hospitalization risk for high-risk patients,7) while the MORE-CARE study demonstrated that the algorithm not only identifies HF events but also stratifies risk for all-cause cardiovascular hospitalizations, with a negative predictive value (NPV) exceeding 98%.8) Further research has expanded the algorithm’s validation to diverse populations and clinical settings: the SCAN-HF study demonstrated its effectiveness in a Japanese population, confirming a 5.8-fold higher relative risk for high-risk patients,9) and large-scale real-world data from the Optum database, including over 22,000 patients, confirmed that the algorithm’s accuracy remains robust in the presence of various comorbidities.10) Beyond predicting HF events, the algorithm also seems to serve as a significant indicator of all-cause mortality, with evidence showing that over a 4-year period, mortality rates reached 38% for high-risk patients versus 14% for low-risk patients.10)

The reduced uptake of these remote monitoring risk stratification strategies might be explained by the fear of data overload, a common problem in modern healthcare systems.11) To address clinical implementation, the “TriageHF® Plus” pathway integrates automated alerts with standardized telephone triage, achieving 98.6% sensitivity for detecting clinical worsening while ensuring the alert burden remains manageable.12) In this regard, real-world data on remote HF monitoring are critical to properly inform healthcare professionals on the expected diagnostic performance of these algorithms in an unselected and diverse HF population and to adequately estimate the resources needed to implement these promising modern strategies.

Our aim was (1) to characterize the HF patients currently being followed at the outpatient invasive remote monitoring HF clinic of a tertiary care center and assess their clinical and risk profile (mortality and HF hospitalizations); (2) to characterize and compare the type of the alert and its overall burden and (3) to evaluate the association of TriageHF® alerts with HF-related clinical outcomes in this clinical setting.

METHODS

Study design and population

We performed an observational retrospective study involving patients followed at the outpatient invasive remote monitoring HF clinic of Centro Hospitalar Universitário de Santo António between February 2022 and July 2023 with the TriageHF® algorithm. By chart review, we collected data from each patient to characterize the baseline clinical profile and HF-related risk. Our variables of interest included demographic (age, sex) and clinical-related (New York Heart Association [NYHA] functional class, HF etiology, guideline-directed medical therapy [GDMT], left ventricle ejection fraction, plasma concentration of N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide [NT-proBNP]) and cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular comorbidities. The distance between each patient’s residence and the clinic was estimated using Google Maps (Google LLC, Mountain View, CA, USA). Both the road distance (km) and estimated travel time (minutes) based on the fastest driving route were recorded as measures of geographic accessibility. Distance was categorized as <10 km, 10–30 km, and ≥30 km, and travel time as <30 minutes, 30–60 minutes, or >60 minutes. All variables were registered at the time of study inclusion.

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the principles of Good Clinical Practice. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Centro Hospitalar Universitário de Santo António (2023-276 [237-DEFI/228-CE]).

TriageHF® algorithm

The TriageHF® algorithm is a validated algorithm that enables ICD and CRT devices to collect and integrate sensor data to risk stratify patients for HF hospitalization in the next 30 days.6) It continuously collects and computes these data using a Bayesian belief network and when the hospitalization risk crosses a predefined threshold an alert is sent to the HF clinical team. The monitored parameters and their clinical significance are as follows7):

• Thoracic impedance (OptiVol™): abnormal when the cumulative fluid index reaches ≥30 Ω·days (Level 2); higher levels of clinical concern are categorized as Level 3 (60–100 Ω·days) and Level 4 (>100 Ω·days);

• Thoracic impedance (OptiVol™): abnormal when the cumulative fluid index reaches ≥30 Ω·days (Level 2); higher levels of clinical concern are categorized as Level 3 (60–100 Ω·days) and Level 4 (>100 Ω·days);

• Patient activity: abnormal if it falls to ≤60 minutes per day or exhibits a significant decreasing trend relative to the patient's long-term average;

• Patient activity: abnormal if it falls to ≤60 minutes per day or exhibits a significant decreasing trend relative to the patient's long-term average;

• Presence of atrial fibrillation: flagged if the patient experiences ≥1 hour of rapid atrial rates within a 24-hour period;

• Presence of atrial fibrillation: flagged if the patient experiences ≥1 hour of rapid atrial rates within a 24-hour period;

• Ventricular pacing: in patients with CRT devices, abnormal pacing is defined as a reduction in biventricular/ventricular pacing to ≤90% of the daily duration;

• Ventricular pacing: in patients with CRT devices, abnormal pacing is defined as a reduction in biventricular/ventricular pacing to ≤90% of the daily duration;

• Treatment for ventricular tachycardia (VT)/ventricular fibrillation (VF): when the device detects ≥5 VT episodes treated with antitachycardia pacing or delivers at least one defibrillation shock within a 24-hour period;

• Treatment for ventricular tachycardia (VT)/ventricular fibrillation (VF): when the device detects ≥5 VT episodes treated with antitachycardia pacing or delivers at least one defibrillation shock within a 24-hour period;

• Night ventricular rate: abnormal if the average heart rate between midnight and 4 a.m. is ≥85 bpm, ≤55 bpm, or demonstrates a sustained increasing trend;

• Night ventricular rate: abnormal if the average heart rate between midnight and 4 a.m. is ≥85 bpm, ≤55 bpm, or demonstrates a sustained increasing trend;

• Heart rate variability: measured as the standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals over 24 hours, heart rate variability is abnormal at ≤60 ms or if a significant decreasing trend is detected.

• Heart rate variability: measured as the standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals over 24 hours, heart rate variability is abnormal at ≤60 ms or if a significant decreasing trend is detected.

We collected data on the number of alerts (high-risk HF risk status) and the type of sensor data that was driving the alert. We also evaluated the duration of continuous days under high-risk alert.

Clinical events

The clinical outcomes considered were: unplanned emergency room (ER) visits with the need for intravenous diuretic treatment, HF hospitalizations, and all-cause death. We also evaluated clinical outcomes as a composite of all these events. All the clinical events were analyzed through a review of the electronic health records. These clinical outcomes were not influenced by the TriageHF® alerts, as the remote monitoring clinic had not yet been organized to actively monitor these alerts or respond to them systematically during this period.

Data analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range) for nonnormally distributed data. Categorical variables are expressed as number of subjects and proportion [number (%)]. Comparisons between groups were performed using 2-sided unpaired or paired t-tests or Wilcoxon rank sums test for normally and non-normally distributed data, respectively. A 2-sided p value <0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analyses were done with Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS® version 27; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Sensitivity was defined as the number of patients with high-risk alerts and adverse events divided by the total number of patients with adverse events. Specificity was defined as the number of patients without high-risk alerts and no adverse events divided by the total number of patients without adverse events. Positive predictive value (PPV) was defined as the number of patients with high-risk alerts and adverse events divided by the total number of patients with high-risk alerts. NPV was defined as the number of patients without high-risk alerts and no adverse events divided by the total number of patients without high-risk alerts.

RESULTS

Studied population

We studied 134 patients with HF with reduced ejection fraction with an implanted cardiac device (CRT-D/P 82%, ICD 18%) enabled to run the TriageHF® algorithm. Their mean age was 73±11 years and 72% were male. Forty-seven percent had an ischemic etiology and most of them were on functional class NYHA II (60%). Their median NT-proBNP plasma concentration was 847 pg/mL (Supplementary Table 1). Regarding comorbidities, 40% were diabetic, 31% had a previous history of acute myocardial infarction, and 19% had permanent atrial fibrillation. Most of them were on GDMT (91% on beta-blockers; 65% on angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or angiotensin II receptor blocker; 21% on Sacubitril/Valsartan; 44% on sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i); 56% on mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist [MRA]). Only 19.4% were followed at a dedicated HF clinic, and the remaining were followed in general cardiology or cardiac device outpatient consult (Supplementary Table 2).

Travel time to the clinic differed significantly between groups (p=0.002). Overall, patients in the alert group tended to have longer travel times compared with the no-alert group, whereas road distance (km) did not differ between groups (p=0.278).

Alert characterization

There was a total of 77 individual risk alerts during the median follow-up of 1.7 years of the 134 patients, which represents 0.44 alerts per patient-year. The proportion of patients with at least one alert was 30% (n=40). The proportion of patients with at least 2 alerts was 18% (n=24) and 8% (n=10) had 3 or more alerts. The maximal number of alerts that any patient had was 5 (1 patient). Among patients who experienced alerts, the mean duration of the alert status was 60±93 days. The most common abnormal parameters driving the risk score were lung impedance measured by Optivol® (n=70), patient activity (n=57) and night ventricular rate (n=49). The data about TriageHF® alerts are displayed in Table 2. The subset of HF patients who eventually had a TriageHF® alert were remarkably similar to those without any alert, except for the increased prevalence of chronic kidney disease, stroke history and use of furosemide (Table 1).

Clinical outcomes

There was a total of 40 events (15 deaths, 10 HF hospitalizations, and 15 unplanned ER visits for HF decompensation) occurring in 29 patients. The death rate was 6.6 deaths/100 person-years (11.2% of all patients). The overall composite event rate was 17.6 events/100 person-years. When using the median of NT-proBNP levels, patients below the median had a composite event rate of 2.2 events/100 person-years compared to 41.3 events/100 person-years in those with NT-proBNP above the median.

TriageHF®

Out of the 134 patients, a cumulative total of 2,476 months of follow-up data were collected, during which 25 hospitalizations or ER visits due to HF were recorded, translating to an event rate of 1.1% per month. Utilizing the TriageHF® algorithm, a collective sum of 76 months was identified as high-risk, constituting 3.2% of the follow-up duration. There were 19 events during standard-risk days, with an event rate of 0.8% per month, whereas 6 events occurred during high-risk days, representing an event rate of 7.9% per month (Table 3). This denotes an incidence nearly ten times higher in the occurrence of adverse events during high-risk days.

DISCUSSION

Our study has yielded several noteworthy findings warranting further discussion. First, the alert burden was low and consistent with previous reports, which supports the feasibility of implementing these remote programs even in low-human resource settings. Second, the occurrence of an HF-related event was nearly ten times higher among patients in a TriageHF® alert state, which supports the clinical utility of these alerts to identify patients who need enhanced monitoring or treatment strategies. Likewise, the high negative predicted value could be used to optimize the allocation of resources away from those out of alert.

Initial reports have described monthly HF-related event rates ranging from 0.68% to 1.5%.6,7,8,9,10) This aligns with the reported rate of 1.1% per month for hospitalizations or ER visits due to HF in our cohort. These studies also consistently demonstrated a substantial increase in the incidence and relative risk of HF events in the 30 days following a high-risk alert, given that the algorithm assessed 10% to 13% of the total months as high-risk.6,7,8) In our cohort, distinct from previous investigations which considered the maximum monthly score, we evaluated the cumulative duration of continuous days under high-risk alert. This approach offers a more precise representation of the time-varying nature of patient risk by focusing on the actual period of physiological instability rather than a single peak occurrence. This also probably explains the comparatively lower percentage of time spent in the high-risk category (only 3% of the time). Nevertheless, our findings corroborate a nearly ten-fold increase in the incidence of HF decompensations during the alert period itself.

Patients who developed a TriageHF® alert had higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease, stroke history, and furosemide use, reflecting more advanced disease and greater risk of HF decompensation. These findings emphasize that HF risk varies over time and is best captured through longitudinal monitoring rather than cross-sectional evaluation.

The TriageHF® algorithm showed a high specificity and a high NPV (>70%), suggesting a potential role in identifying individuals that may not require immediate intervention or closer monitoring, which helps to optimize limited resources allocation8,9,12) allowing them to be redirected from patients in their non-high-risk periods to more vulnerable patients in their high-risk periods. However, the relatively low sensitivity and PPV indicate that the algorithm’s capacity to predict HF decompensation during high-risk alerts remains limited, and these alerts should therefore be interpreted within the broader clinical context.

Similarly, when stratifying patients according to NT-proBNP levels, we observed consistent diagnostic performance of the TriageHF® algorithm. The tendency toward higher sensitivity and PPV and slightly lower specificity and NPV likely reflects the higher baseline event rate in this subgroup.

One of the main barriers to implementing these remote monitoring programs, in such a prevalent disease as HF, is the fear of data overload by the clinical services. Using this alert-triggered evaluation approach, our data underline a low alert burden at 0.44 alerts per patient-year. This suggests that implementation in real-world settings is feasible. Although not directly evaluated in our series, we hypothesized that integrating a telephonic call into a standardized protocol for alert management could potentially enhance the diagnostic performance and cost-effectiveness of remote monitoring without substantially increasing workload, as previously described.12)

In addition, we observed that patients in the alert group overall tended to have longer travel times to the clinic compared with the no-alert group. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as it may reflect that patients living farther away have reduced access to in-person care or represent a more complex referral population, which could increase the likelihood of alerts. Previous studies have demonstrated that geographic barriers to care persist for many patients and that telemedicine and remote monitoring strategies may help mitigate these challenges by facilitating access to specialized care without requiring frequent in-person visits.13,14)

Overall, our findings are in close agreement with the recent real-world study by Tinoco et al.15) which reported an overall event rate of 1.2% per month and found that patients in the high-risk category had a 29-fold greater risk of experiencing an acute clinical decompensation event within the following 30 days compared with low-risk periods. The algorithm demonstrated a low PPV (9.6%) and a high NPV (97.8%) for predicting heart-failure events, closely aligning with our results. Also, other contemporary studies have also confirmed the strong diagnostic performance of the TriageHF® algorithm across different real-world settings,16,17) not only for heart-failure events but also for all-cause and non-cardiovascular hospitalizations, underscoring its broader role as an indicator of overall clinical instability.

Risk stratification of HF using invasive remote monitoring may also have a promising role in optimizing GDMT.18) In our series, baseline MRAs and SGLT2i were prescribed in only about half of the cohort. As this study was purely observational, we did not document therapy changes over time, and there was no formal protocol to act on TriageHF® alerts. Nevertheless, these alerts may potentially serve as a reminder to optimize GDMT.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective nature does not allow us to collect more detailed clinical data during alert periods. Second, being a single-center study, the generalizability of these results to different healthcare settings is limited. Third, the relatively low number of patients and event rate in our cohort may have impacted the estimates of the diagnostic performance of the algorithm. Lastly, the absence of data on interventions or treatment modifications during the follow-up precludes the assessment of their potential impact on clinical outcomes.

In this real-world study of a population of HF patients with CIED, the TriageHF® algorithm generated a low burden of alerts manageable in HF clinics with limited staff. The alerts identified patients with nearly ten-fold higher incidence of HF-related events and presented a very high negative predicted value in those out of alert, which supports this algorithm as a useful risk-stratifying tool in HF.
