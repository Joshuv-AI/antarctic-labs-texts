Coordination competition and hydrogen-bond reconstruction enable reversible I+/I2/I− conversion for large-capacity Zn‖I2 batteries over −50 °C to 70 °C
Authors: Bin Gui, Shilin Zhang, Shilong Li, Longhai Zhang, Rui Wang, Jianfeng Mao, Tengfei Zhou, Yun Gao, Yangyang Liu, Chaofeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Four-electron aqueous zinc–iodine batteries (4eZIBs) offer high energy density but suffer from irreversible I+ hydrolysis, polyiodide shuttling, poor zinc anode stability, and a limited operating temperature range. Here, we propose a coordination competition and hydrogen-bond reconstruction strategy using a hybrid electrolyte of Zn(ClO4)2·6H2O, ZnCl2, InCl3, and polyethylene glycol 400 (PEG400), which enables reversible I+/I2/I− conversion for large-capacity Zn‖I2 batteries over a wide temperature range. Specifically, In3+ ions suppress polyiodide formation through preferential coordination with I− and electrostatic shielding that blocks charge exchange between I− and I2, and they are also preferentially reduced to metallic indium on the zinc anode surface, forming a protective indium layer that inhibits hydrogen evolution and corrosion. Meanwhile, PEG400 reduces the activity of free water by reconstructing the hydrogen-bond network of water molecules, and its polar segments can confine the ICl intermediate to suppress I+ hydrolysis. This synergistic chemistry enables an ultra-long Zn anode lifespan (over 4000 h), excellent Zn‖I2 full cell stability (over 10 000 cycles), record wide-temperature operation (−50 °C to 70 °C), and scalable Ah-level pouch-cell stability. This work provides a robust strategy for simultaneously managing complex interhalogen chemistries and stabilizing the zinc anode, paving the way toward practical aqueous energy storage.

Introduction

Aqueous zinc-ion batteries (ZIBs) are regarded as ideal candidates for next-generation large-scale energy storage owing to their combined advantages of high safety, low cost, and environmental friendliness.1–4 Among them, aqueous zinc–iodine batteries (AZIBs) have attracted extensive attention by virtue of the abundant iodine resources, excellent reversibility, and the capability to support multi-electron transfer.5,6 The four-electron aqueous zinc–iodine battery (4eZIB) operates via a stepwise four-electron conversion of the iodine cathode (I+/I2/I−); this four-electron system delivers a theoretical capacity of 422 mAh g−1 and a high discharge voltage plateau of approximately 1.8 V (vs. Zn2+/Zn),7,8 significantly boosting the energy density and demonstrating considerable development potential. Nevertheless, achieving reversible four-electron conversion of iodine remains a central scientific challenge. The key issue lies in the strong electrophilicity of I+ species, which makes them highly susceptible to nucleophilic attack by water molecules in the aqueous electrolyte, leading to irreversible loss of active species.9–11 Meanwhile, the stepwise conversion process inevitably generates polyiodide intermediates such as I3−, and the resulting shuttle effect causes severe capacity fading and coulombic efficiency (CE) decay.12,13 On the zinc anode side, repeated Zn plating/stripping with parasitic side reactions can induce non-uniform deposition and dendrite growth, which may penetrate the separator and cause internal short circuits.14–17

To address the above challenges, researchers have reported numerous strategies, such as electrolyte engineering,7,18–21 I2 host materials,22–24 and cathode binder.25,26 Among these, electrolyte engineering is a relatively simple and efficient strategy because it can simultaneously regulate the iodine conversion reaction and zinc deposition behavior. For example, adding organic cations to the electrolyte allows the cations to preferentially adsorb onto the Zn electrode surface, regulating zinc deposition, while forming solid chelates with polyiodides and halogen intermediates through electrostatic interactions, thereby suppressing their shuttling and hydrolysis.27–29 However, this solid-phase conversion reaction compromises the kinetic advantages of the iodine conversion reaction. Moreover, using organic solvents as co-solvents to reduce water activity also plays a positive role in suppressing polyiodide shuttling and halogen intermediate hydrolysis. Nevertheless, polyiodides are also highly soluble in most organic solvents, making it difficult to fundamentally inhibit their shuttling.10,30 Furthermore, at elevated temperatures, side reactions on both the zinc electrode and the iodine electrode are exacerbated, severely limiting the application scenarios of aqueous zinc–iodine batteries.9,31 Therefore, it is imperative to develop alternative electrolyte design strategies that can simultaneously manage interhalogen reactions and Zn deposition behavior over a wide temperature range (Fig. 1a).

Schematic diagrams of batteries based on different electrolytes. (a) Traditional electrolyte. (b) Hybrid electrolyte with coordination competition and hydrogen-bond reconstruction.

In this study, we report a novel hybrid electrolyte for wide-temperature and highly stable 4eZIBs, which consists of polyethylene glycol 400 (PEG400) as a co-solvent, a dual-zinc-salt system of Zn(ClO4)2·6H2O and ZnCl2, and InCl3 as a functional additive, with the optimal formulation denoted as 50% P4–In. In this system, In3+ ions act as a bifunctional additive: they first suppress polyiodide formation by preferentially coordinating with I− and generating electrostatic shielding that blocks charge exchange between I− and I2 during the charge–discharge process. Meanwhile, they are preferentially reduced to metallic indium on the zinc anode surface, forming an indium-rich organic–inorganic hybrid solid electrolyte interphase (SEI). This interphase effectively mitigates side reactions induced by active water molecules and polyiodide species at the Zn anode and directs uniform zinc deposition along the Zn (002) crystallographic plane. Moreover, the strong hydrogen bonding between water molecules and PEG400 reduces the activity of free water, while the polar segments of PEG400 restrict the ICl interhalogen species, effectively suppressing its hydrolysis (Fig. 1b). Benefiting from this coordination competition and hydrogen-bond reconstruction strategy, the 50% P4–In electrolyte enables a Zn‖Zn symmetric cell to achieve a cycling lifetime exceeding 4000 hours at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2. Moreover, the assembled Zn‖I2 full cell exhibits excellent cycling stability, retaining 73.47% of its initial capacity after 10 000 cycles at a current density of 10C. Remarkably, this hybrid electrolyte achieves a record operating window from −50 °C to 70 °C and demonstrates scalability to Ah-level Zn‖I2 pouch cells.

Results and discussion

Modulatory effects of metal ions in iodine conversion

In aqueous zinc–iodine batteries, the discharge product I− of the iodine cathode exhibits nucleophilicity and readily attacks the electrophilic I2 generated during the charging process, forming I3− and higher-order polyiodides (e.g., I5−). The shuttle effect of these polyiodides causes continuous self-discharge of the battery even under static conditions, leading to a significant reduction in coulombic efficiency (CE).30 This study introduces metal cations to compete with iodine species, thereby inhibiting the formation of polyiodides. To screen suitable metal cations, deionized water was used as the solvent and Zn(ClO4)2·6H2O as the main salt. Six metal salts, namely AlCl3, InCl3, ZnCl2, MgCl2, LiCl, and NaCl, were separately added as functional additives to prepare different electrolytes, designated as ZI–X (X = Al, In, Zn, Mg, Li, Na). On this basis, Zn‖I2 full cells were assembled using each electrolyte, and their CE values were evaluated at room temperature at a current density of 1C. Fig. 2a shows the galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) curves of different Zn‖I2 batteries, among which the cell based on the ZI–In electrolyte exhibited the highest CE, reaching 99.7% (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, an equal amount of elemental iodine was added to each electrolyte, and seven iodine-containing electrolyte systems were obtained after standing for 24 hours (Fig. S1). Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectroscopy revealed the inhibitory effects of different metal cations on polyiodide formation. As shown in Fig. 2c, after adding iodine, the ZI–In electrolyte displayed the weakest I3− absorption signal (350 nm),10 indicating that In3+ can effectively suppress the generation of polyiodide ions. This, in turn, inhibits the shuttle effect of polyiodides, reduces the irreversible loss of active materials, and significantly improves the CE and cycling stability of Zn‖I2 cells.

These results raise the question of why In3+ exhibits the best performance in suppressing polyiodide formation. According to classical Lewis's acid–base theory, I− is a soft Lewis base and I2 is a soft Lewis acid, and the soft–soft interaction is the most stable, which intrinsically favors the formation of I3− and higher-order polyiodides in aqueous solutions. To inhibit this process, an ideal strategy is to introduce metal cations that can compete with I2 for I− by forming stronger interactions with I−.32 However, based on the hard–soft acid–base (HSAB) theory (Fig. S2), In3+ is classified as a hard acid, Zn2+ as a borderline acid, and I− as a typical soft base.33 From a thermodynamic perspective, the complex between a borderline acid (Zn2+) and a soft base (I−) would be expected to be more stable than that between a hard acid (In3+) and a soft base (I−). Thus, Zn2+ would intuitively be predicted to bind I− more effectively than In3+. Nevertheless, the experimental results contradict this expectation—In3+ shows a superior inhibitory ability.

To rationalize this counterintuitive phenomenon, we further investigated the binding processes between hydrated metal ions and I− using potential energy surface (PES) scans and density functional theory (DFT) calculations. As shown in Fig. S3a and b, the system energy decreases monotonically with decreasing distance between I− and [In(H2O)6]3+ or [Zn(H2O)6]2+, without an obvious reaction barrier. This indicates that the binding processes of both systems are thermodynamically and kinetically spontaneous and require no activation energy to overcome. Meanwhile, the potential well depth for the binding of I− to [In(H2O)6]3+ is significantly larger than that of the Zn2+–I− system, which quantitatively confirms that the electrostatic interaction strength between In3+ and I− is much higher than that between Zn2+ and I−.

Furthermore, DFT calculations were conducted to elucidate the energy differences in the binding of I− to [In(H2O)6]3+ and [Zn(H2O)6]2+, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2d, when free I− interacts with hydrated metal ions, the energy released during the formation of the outer-sphere ion pair [In(H2O)6]3+·I− by In3+ and I− is significantly higher than that in the Zn2+ system, reflecting the stronger electrostatic attraction of In3+ due to its higher positive charge. Notably, during the process where I− penetrates the solvation shell, enters the first coordination sphere of the metal ion, and replaces one coordinated water molecule, the Zn2+ system exhibits a significantly thermodynamically non-spontaneous process (ΔG2 = 18.07 kcal mol−1), whereas the Gibbs free energy change for the In3+ system is only 0.37 kcal mol−1. This indicates that I− is more prone to coordinate with In3+.

Fig. 2e further illustrates the structural evolution rules during the binding processes of the two systems; the hydrated In3+ ion has a flexible coordination shell and can rapidly increase its coordination number to seven through a “coordinate-then-dehydrate” mechanism, making its binding with I− more energetically favorable. Specifically, when I− encounters [In(H2O)6]3+, it first enters the inner coordination sphere and bonds with In3+, raising the coordination number to seven, and subsequently releases one water molecule.34 This process only needs to overcome an energy barrier of 0.37 kcal mol−1. In sharp contrast, the entry of I− into the hydrated Zn2+ ion follows a “dehydration-before-coordination” mechanism and must overcome a substantial energy barrier of 18.07 kcal mol−1. Moreover, due to the weak charge attraction of Zn2+ to I−, it cannot effectively break the solvation shell of I− and can only form unstable outer-sphere ion pairs, making it difficult to effectively sequester free I−. Thus, despite the hard–soft acid–base mismatch between In3+ and I−, its kinetically favored hydration process leads to the preferential formation of the [In(H2O)5I]2+ inner-sphere complex. Then, the high charge density of In3+ in [In(H2O)5I]2+ effectively screens the charge of I−, interrupting the charge transfer between I− and the soft acid I2, thereby suppressing the formation of I3− (Fig. 2f).

In addition to effectively suppressing the polyiodide shuttle effect on the cathode side, the In3+ additive can also simultaneously significantly improve the deposition stability of the zinc anode. As shown in Fig. 2g, the standard reduction potentials indicate that the reduction potential of In3+ (−0.34 V vs. SHE) is significantly more positive than that of Zn2+ (−0.76 V vs. SHE).35 Therefore, during the battery charging process, In3+ undergoes a reduction reaction on the zinc anode surface prior to Zn2+, forming a uniform and dense in situ metallic indium inducing layer.3 This inducing layer possesses both good electronic conductivity and excellent zinc affinity, which can provide a large number of uniformly distributed nucleation sites for zinc deposition and significantly reduce the zinc nucleation overpotential, thereby effectively avoiding local current concentration, guiding the flat and uniform deposition of zinc ions across the entire electrode surface, and fundamentally inhibiting the growth and propagation of zinc dendrites.30,36

These aforementioned calculation and analysis results collectively demonstrate that In3+, as a bifunctional electrolyte additive, can simultaneously regulate the iodine conversion reaction pathway and the zinc deposition behavior in aqueous zinc–iodine batteries.

Design and characterization of the hybrid electrolytes

In this study, we further optimized the electrolyte design and developed a composite electrolyte for highly stable aqueous four-electron zinc–iodine batteries (4eZIBs). The formulated system employs polyethylene glycol (PEG) as a co-solvent, Zn(ClO4)2·6H2O as the main salt, ZnCl2 as the auxiliary salt, and InCl3 as a functional additive. On the anode side, InCl3 promotes uniform zinc deposition.36 In addition, it effectively suppresses side reactions involving iodide ions. Through strong interactions with water, PEG restructures the hydrogen-bond network, reducing water activity and thereby inhibiting the hydrogen release reaction.37 Meanwhile, its polar segments restrict iodine monochloride (ICl), effectively suppressing its hydrolysis.38 First, UV-vis absorption spectroscopy was used to evaluate the influence of PEG with different molecular weights (PEG200, PEG300, PEG400, and PEG600) on the stability of ICl (Fig. S4a), and the ionic conductivities of the corresponding solvents were measured (Fig. S4b and c). PEG400 exhibited superior performance in anchoring ICl while maintaining a moderate ionic conductivity; therefore, PEG400 was selected as the co-solvent for further optimization. Subsequently, a dual-salt electrolyte consisting of 3 m Zn(ClO4)2 and 0.5 m ZnCl2 (1 m = 1 mol kg−1) was prepared, with a co-solvent system of PEG400 and H2O at various mass fractions. These electrolytes were labeled as X-P4, where X denotes the mass fraction of PEG400 (30%, 40%, 50%, and 60%). As shown in Fig. S5a–c, systems with higher water content (30% P4 and 40% P4) exhibited relatively high ionic conductivity. However, the higher water content also exacerbated the hydrolysis and shuttling of iodine species on the cathode side, as well as detrimental side reactions and dendrite growth on the zinc anode, leading to low CE. In contrast, in the system with an excessively high PEG400 fraction (60% P4), although the anchoring of iodine species was enhanced, the significantly reduced ionic conductivity and greatly increased viscosity severely limited the kinetics of iodine conversion and the uniform deposition of Zn2+, causing non-uniform reactions and severe polarization, which also prevented the full-cell CE from reaching an optimal level.38 Consequently, 50% P4 was identified as the optimal co-solvent composition.

To investigate the optimal concentration of In3+, a series of novel hybrid electrolytes were prepared by introducing different amounts of InCl3 (0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 m) into a 50% P4 solvent containing 3 m Zn(ClO4)2·6H2O. Since the variation in InCl3 content simultaneously changes the Cl− concentration in the electrolyte, the auxiliary salt ZnCl2 was introduced to eliminate the discrepancies caused by the different Cl− levels. Specifically, appropriate amounts of ZnCl2 were added to ensure a constant total Cl− concentration across all electrolytes with different InCl3 loadings, thereby removing the Cl− concentration difference that would otherwise arise solely from variations in the InCl3 concentration. These electrolytes were designated as 50% P4-X In-Y Zn, where X and Y are the molal concentrations of InCl3 and ZnCl2, respectively (X = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3; corresponding Y = 0.5, 0.35, 0.2). To further elucidate the actual role of different In3+ concentrations in 4eAZIBs, reversible Zn plating/stripping experiments were carried out and the cycling life of symmetric cells was systematically studied. It was observed that Zn‖Zn symmetric cells using electrolytes containing 0.1 m, 0.2 m, and 0.3 m InCl3 delivered cycling lifetimes of 512, 954, and 760 h, respectively. Notably, when the InCl3 concentration was increased to 0.3 m, the polarization voltage of the Zn‖Zn symmetric cell increased significantly and the cycling life dropped abruptly (Fig. S6), because excessive In3+ deteriorates the reaction kinetics. Taking all factors into consideration, the electrolyte composed of 50% PEG400, 3 m Zn(ClO4)2·6H2O, 0.2 m InCl3, and 0.35 m ZnCl2 is likely the optimized formulation. This electrolyte is labeled as 50% P4–In. For comparison, a reference electrolyte consisting of 3 m Zn(ClO4)2·6H2O and 0.35 m ZnCl2 in H2O was prepared and labeled as baseline electrolyte (BE).

The Zn2+ solvation structure in the 50% P4–In electrolyte was investigated by molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (Fig. 3a). As illustrated by the 3D snapshot from the MD trajectory (Fig. 3b), the first solvation shell of Zn2+ is not exclusively occupied by water molecules; instead, an anion-dominated contact ion pair structure is formed, thereby substantially reconstructing the coordination environment. Radial distribution function (RDF) and coordination number analyses (Fig. 3c) resolve three distinct coordination peaks at ∼2 Å, assignable to Zn2+–Cl, Zn2+–O(ClO4−), and Zn2+–O(H2O) interactions, with average coordination numbers of 0.40, 2.910, and 2.930, respectively. These values indicate the emergence of a new average solvation complex, Zn(H2O)3(ClO4)3Cl0.4. Against this coordination background, no distinct signal peaks are found in the g(r) either in the inner coordination region (2.0–3.0 Å) or in the vicinity of the second shell, demonstrating that PEG400 molecules are excluded from the Zn2+ solvation shells owing to their large steric hindrance. Rather than participating in direct coordination, PEG400 resides beyond the second solvation shell and in the bulk electrolyte, where it disrupts the hydrogen-bond network of water and thereby suppresses interfacial water decomposition.39 Simultaneously, a small fraction of In3+ enters the system and is likely to form a dynamic protective layer on the electrode surface, functioning synergistically with PEG400.

A series of composite electrolytes with different PEG400 mass fractions, each containing 3 m Zn(ClO4)2·6H2O, 0.2 m InCl3, and 0.35 m ZnCl2, were subsequently prepared and denoted as X P4–In (X = 30%, 40%, 50%, and 60%). Their structural evolution and hydrogen-bond interactions were systematically characterized by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, and Raman spectroscopy. First, high-sensitivity 1H NMR spectroscopy was employed (Fig. 3d and S7). The 1H NMR spectra exhibit two sharp signals, which were attributed to the H atoms of H2O and PEG400, respectively. Specifically, the 1H signal of pure H2O appears at 4.7 ppm,40 whereas the CH2 group of pure PEG is observed at 3.5 ppm.41 In composite electrolytes, as the PEG400 content increases, the 1H resonance of water shifts to a lower chemical shift, while the 1H signal of the PEG moiety shifts to a higher chemical shift, further confirming the formation of hydrogen-bond interactions between PEG400 and water molecules.38,40

FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. S8) further reveals the evolution of the hydrogen-bond network within the electrolytes. As shown in Fig. 3e and f, with increasing PEG400 content, both the O–H bending vibration (∼1627 cm−1) and the O–H stretching vibration (3200–3400 cm−1) of water progressively shift to higher wavenumbers. The blue shifts of the O–H vibrational bands indicate that PEG–water interactions disrupt the intrinsic hydrogen-bond network among water molecules, thereby reducing water activity.10,42,43 Complementary Raman spectroscopy shows a pronounced blue shift accompanied by narrowing of the O–H stretching band of water, further corroborating the altered hydrogen-bond environment in the composite electrolytes (Fig. S9). The intramolecular O–H stretching vibrations of water can be deconvoluted into three components: strong hydrogen bonds, weak hydrogen bonds, and non-hydrogen bonds, with peak positions at approximately 3240, 3440, and 3580 cm−1, respectively (Fig. 3g and h).40 Based on the fitted peak areas, the proportions of different hydrogen-bond states were calculated and summarized for the various composite electrolytes (Fig. 3i). Evidently, the fraction of strongly hydrogen-bonded water in the X P4–In electrolytes is lower than that in BE. With increasing PEG400 content in X P4–In, the proportion of strong hydrogen bonds decreases, whereas the proportions of weak and non-hydrogen bonds increase. The progressive incorporation of PEG400 disrupts the inherent hydrogen-bond network of water, resulting in reduced water activity and a lowered freezing point of the electrolyte.31

The low-temperature phase transition behavior of the 50% P4–In composite electrolyte was characterized by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (Fig. S10a). A distinct glass transition (tg) was observed at −77.23 °C, and no crystallization or melting peaks of water were detected within the temperature range of −90 to 0 °C. This indicates that the introduction of PEG400 effectively disrupts the hydrogen-bond network among water molecules, suppresses ice crystal formation, and enables the electrolyte to remain as a stable supercooled liquid at low temperatures.44 The temperature dependence of the ionic conductivity was also measured: the electrolyte exhibited an ionic conductivity of 2.38 mS cm−1 at −30 °C and 0.706 mS cm−1 at −50 °C (Fig. S10b). Such excellent anti-freezing properties ensure reliable battery operation even under harsh environmental conditions.

Electrochemical and physical characterization of zinc plating/stripping behavior

To elucidate the mechanism by which the 50% P4–In electrolyte enables an ultra-stable zinc anode, this study systematically investigates the electrochemical behavior, interfacial chemistry, and structural dynamics of this electrolyte system. First, to verify the enhancement of Zn2+ plating/stripping kinetics by the 50% P4–In electrolyte, the desolvation activation energy (Ea) and Zn2+ transference number (tZn2+) were determined. Electrochemical impedance spectra were collected over a temperature range of 20–60 °C and fitted to the Arrhenius equation to obtain the Zn2+ desolvation activation energy (Fig. S11). As shown in Fig. 4a, the 50% P4–In electrolyte yields an Ea of 18.29 kJ mol−1, significantly lower than the 26.71 kJ mol−1 obtained for BE, indicating faster desolvation kinetics and thus facilitating Zn2+ transport. Meanwhile, the Zn2+ transference number in BE is only 0.44, whereas it reaches as high as 0.79 in the 50% P4–In electrolyte (Fig. S12), further confirming that this electrolyte promotes rapid Zn2+ migration.

Having established the kinetic advantages, the nucleation and growth behavior of zinc deposition in different electrolytes was further investigated using chronoamperometry (CA). As shown in Fig. 4b, in the BE, the current continuously and significantly decays over time and fails to reach a steady state even after 200 s, indicating that zinc deposition follows a two-dimensional diffusion-controlled growth mode (2D diffusion), with Zn2+ preferentially depositing on existing protrusions, which readily induces dendrite formation. In contrast, in the 50% P4–In electrolyte, the current rapidly decreases and stabilizes within approximately 30 s, directly entering a plateau region. This behavior signifies a prompt transition to a three-dimensional diffusion-controlled growth mode (3D diffusion), demonstrating that the 50% P4–In electrolyte homogenizes the interfacial Zn2+ flux and consequently suppresses dendrite nucleation and growth.45,46

Furthermore, the positive effect of the 50% P4–In electrolyte in suppressing corrosion, the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), and parasitic side reactions was evaluated. First, this electrolyte exhibits excellent anti-corrosion properties. Tafel polarization curves (Fig. S13) reveal that the Zn anode in the 50% P4–In electrolyte displays a lower corrosion current density (0.74 mA cm−2) and a more positive corrosion potential (−0.029 V), indicating superior corrosion resistance compared with the BE system. Moreover, the electrochemical stability window was further assessed using linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) (Fig. S14a and b). In the BE, the onset potential of the HER is −0.069 V (vs. Zn2+/Zn), whereas it shifts negatively to −0.26 V (vs. Zn2+/Zn) in the 50% P4–In electrolyte. Meanwhile, the critical potential for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) shifts positively from 2.54 V to 2.7 V (vs. Zn2+/Zn). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the 50% P4–In system exhibits a markedly enhanced ability to suppress water-splitting side reactions, thereby mitigating hydrogen and oxygen evolution at the Zn anode interface and improving CE and cycling stability.

A stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) effectively suppresses parasitic reactions between the electrolyte and the anode, playing a crucial role in enhancing CE and extending cycle life.30 To elucidate the chemical nature and structural characteristics of the interphase formed in the 50% P4–In electrolyte, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) depth profiling was performed on a zinc anode after 50 plating/stripping cycles, combined with argon-ion sputtering to acquire chemical information at different depths (Fig. S15 and 4c and d).47,48 Prior to sputtering, the C 1s spectrum exhibits a rich variety of chemical states: signals assigned to organic species (C–C, C–O/C–Cl, and C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> O) and an inorganic O–CO signal, all originating from the decomposition of PEG400 and Zn(ClO4)2·6H2O.49–51 After 15 s and 30 s of sputtering, both the organic and inorganic signals are markedly attenuated. The O 1s spectrum confirms the presence of ZnCO3 on the surface (Fig. S16).49 The Cl 2p spectrum reveals the chemical states of chlorine, where organic C–Cl and inorganic Cl–Zn bonds can be clearly distinguished, and their intensities decrease simultaneously with increasing sputtering depth.52,53 Notably, the In 3d spectrum displays a strong metallic indium signal before sputtering, which weakens significantly afterward, indicating that metallic indium is predominantly enriched in the outer region of the interphase.54 Collectively, these XPS results demonstrate that an In-rich organic–inorganic hybrid SEI has been successfully constructed on the cycled zinc anode. This interphase combines the flexibility of the organic constituents with the high mechanical strength and high ionic conductivity of the inorganic constituents, along with embedded metallic indium, thereby enabling complete coverage of the electrode surface and effectively blocking direct electron transfer and parasitic reactions between the electrolyte and the zinc anode.55,56

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was subsequently employed to analyze the phase composition and crystallographic orientation of zinc anodes after 50 plating/stripping cycles in both the BE and the 50% P4–In electrolyte (Fig. 4e). In the BE, characteristic diffraction peaks of basic zinc perchlorate hydroxide (Zn4ClO4(OH)7) are observed, and the intensity ratio of the Zn (002) to (101) peaks, I002/I101, is merely 0.82. In contrast, in the 50% P4–In electrolyte, no diffraction signal from this corrosion product is detected, while characteristic peaks of metallic indium appear and the I002/I101 ratio rises to 1.21. The markedly elevated I002/I101 ratio implies that a greater fraction of zinc crystallites grow with their (002) planes oriented parallel to the electrode surface, a texture that promotes a smooth and compact deposition morphology.3

Informed by the above characterization, the practical potential of this electrolyte was further evaluated through battery performance tests. Zn‖Zn symmetric and Zn‖Cu asymmetric cells were first used to assess the stability and reversibility of Zn2+ plating/stripping in different electrolytes, with the CE of the Zn‖Cu cells serving as a key indicator of reversibility (Fig. 4f). In the 50% P4–In electrolyte, the Zn‖Cu asymmetric cell achieves 3000 stable cycles at 5 mA cm−2, 1 mAh cm−2, and a cutoff voltage of 0.8 V. It delivers an initial CE of 99.21% and retains an exceptionally high CE of 99.94% after 3000 cycles, accompanied by highly reproducible voltage profiles. In stark contrast, the cell with the BE electrolyte exhibits an initial CE of only 93.54% and undergoes severe CE fluctuations after merely 139 cycles, owing to hydrogen evolution and parasitic side reactions (Fig. S17a and b). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 4g) directly corroborate these findings: the zinc foil cycled in the BE is heavily covered with abundant, randomly oriented flake-like corrosion products, whereas the zinc anode cycled in the 50% P4–In electrolyte exhibits a uniform and dense deposition morphology.

Furthermore, the rate performance of Zn‖Zn symmetric cells was evaluated to assess interfacial stability across a wide range of current densities (Fig. S18). During a stepwise increase in current density from 1 to 10 mA cm−2, the voltage polarization of the 50% P4–In electrolyte rose linearly and reversibly with increasing current. Upon returning the current to 1 mA cm−2, the polarization recovered without noticeable hysteresis or degradation, indicating robust interfacial stability and excellent rate reversibility even under high-rate cycling. This favorable rate response demonstrates that the electrode/electrolyte interface remains mechanically and electrochemically stable under dynamic current loads, providing a solid foundation for prolonged cycling. As shown in Fig. 4h, at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2, the symmetric cell with this electrolyte achieved a cycle life exceeding 4000 h, maintaining a flat voltage profile and stable voltage hysteresis throughout, far outperforming the BE electrolyte, which failed after only 180 h with drastic voltage fluctuations. Moreover, at a high current density of 10 mA cm−2 and a high areal capacity of 10 mAh cm−2, the 50% P4–In electrolyte still delivered over 400 h of stable cycling, whereas the BE electrolyte exhibited severe polarization and rapid failure from the beginning (Fig. S19). Cycling tests under extreme temperatures further highlighted the remarkable environmental adaptability of this electrolyte. At an ultralow temperature of −50 °C, the cell cycled stably for over 3000 h at 0.2 mA cm−2 and 0.2 mAh cm−2; at a high temperature of 70 °C, it sustained more than 150 h of stable operation at 5 mA cm−2 and 2.5 mAh cm−2, demonstrating outstanding temperature tolerance (Fig. 4i and S20). Compared with recent related work reported in the literature, this study achieves a significant improvement in cycle life, demonstrating better long-term stability and practical potential (Fig. 4j).9,36,57–63

Full-cell performance across a wide temperature range

The electrochemical performance of the 50% P4–In electrolyte was systematically evaluated in a Zn‖I2 full cell. Rate performance was first examined by increasing the current density from 1C to 15C (Fig. 5a). The cell employing this hybrid electrolyte markedly outperformed the BE counterpart, delivering reversible capacities of 567.97, 493.84, 453.05, 399.66, 343.78, 311.81, 287.22, and 250.21 mAh g−1 at 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, and 15C, respectively, with negligible capacity fade upon returning to 1C. Moreover, the 50% P4–In system maintained higher CE across all tested current densities. The GCD profiles (Fig. 5b and S21) further revealed that this electrolyte provides more stable voltage plateaus, indicating that it effectively reduces the polarization overpotential of the iodine redox reactions and suppresses polyiodide shuttling, thereby enabling high CE, high reversible capacity, and stable voltage output.

The long-term cycling stability of the full cells with different electrolyte systems was further investigated. As shown in Fig. 5c, at a current density of 2C, the battery with the 50% P4–In electrolyte demonstrated far superior cycling stability to the BE system, maintaining a capacity retention as high as 88.48% after 1000 cycles and an average CE of 95.94%. This is attributed to the efficient synergistic suppression of polyiodide shuttling and parasitic reactions at the zinc anode by the P4–In electrolyte, which preserves the high reversibility of the iodine cathode and the stability of the zinc anode interface during prolonged cycling. When the current density was increased to 5C, the BE system cell suffered rapid capacity decay and failure in the early cycles, whereas the 50% P4–In system cell retained an exceptionally high capacity retention of 88.45% after 10 000 cycles (Fig. 5d). It is notable that, after 10 000 cycles at a high current density of 10C, the Zn‖I2 full cell still achieved a capacity retention of 73.47% (Fig. S22), further demonstrating that this electrolyte significantly enhances the cycling stability of Zn‖I2 cells. To evaluate the temperature tolerance of the electrolyte, cycling performance was tested over a wide temperature range. At a low temperature of −50 °C and a current density of 1C, the Zn‖I2 cell delivered 1000 stable cycles with a capacity retention of 96.81% (Fig. 5e). At a high temperature of 70 °C and a current density of 5C, the capacity retention remained at 71.26% after 200 cycles (Fig. 5f). These results collectively confirm that the 50% P4–In electrolyte possesses excellent tolerance across a wide temperature range, highlighting its great potential for practical applications. Compared with the recently reported wide-temperature-range 4eZIBs, this study achieves a superior temperature range, enabling stable operation over a broader temperature interval and demonstrating enhanced environmental adaptability as well as practical application potential (Fig. 5g).8–10,30,38 To further evaluate the practical application potential of the 50% P4–In hybrid electrolyte, we assembled a high-capacity Zn‖I2 pouch cell (Fig. S23). At room temperature and a current density of 1C, this pouch cell delivered a high capacity of 397.57 mAh, with a capacity retention of 83.14% after 200 cycles (Fig. 5h and i). Building on these results, we further assembled an Ah-level pouch cell, which achieved an initial capacity of 1.07 Ah and could be stably cycled for 20 cycles, fully demonstrating the excellent application potential of this electrolyte (Fig. S24).

Four-electron I+/I2/I− redox mechanism

The interconversion mechanism among I−, I2, and I+ was first investigated by XPS. High-resolution I 3d spectra acquired in the 618–623 eV range at different electrochemical potentials, together with their deconvoluted components, are shown in Fig. 6a. During the charging process, a monotonic shift of the entire spectral envelope toward higher binding energy is evident, reflecting a gradual increase in the iodine oxidation state. At a potential of 1.45 V, two intense peaks located at 619.1 and 620.3 eV are attributed to I− and I2, respectively, while a weak feature emerging at 621.8 eV is ascribed to I+.9 As the potential is increased to 1.82 V, the I+ signal intensifies substantially, confirming the formation of I+ cations, whereas the I− contribution is strongly attenuated and replaced by the characteristic signals of I2 and I+. Upon subsequent discharge back to 1.45 V, the I− peak progressively recovers, and the I+ peak nearly disappears, thereby verifying the reversibility of these redox transformations. Cl 2p spectra (Fig. 6b) provide corroborating evidence. At 1.45 V, two well-resolved peaks at 199.3 and 200.8 eV are assigned to the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 spin–orbit components of Cl−, and two additional weaker peaks at 200.2 and 201.7 eV correspond to the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 levels of ICl. Charging the cell to 1.82 V results in a marked intensity enhancement of the ICl-related peaks.64 This finding further confirms that the observed spectral changes originate from the coordinative interaction between I+ and Cl−. Importantly, the oxidation state of Cl− remains constant throughout the entire process, demonstrating that chloride ions function exclusively as stabilizing species and do not directly engage in the redox reaction.

The interaction between PEGs of different molecular weights and ICl was further evaluated by DFT calculations. As shown in Fig. 6c, the binding energies of PEG200–ICl, PEG300–ICl, PEG400–ICl, and PEG600–ICl are −0.352, −0.509, −0.586, and −0.842 eV, respectively, indicating that PEG molecules can effectively anchor ICl. These strong interactions significantly influence the chemical stability of iodine species. To evaluate the hydrolysis behavior of ICl in different solutions, ICl was added to H2O, 50% PEG400 (50% P4), 50% PEG400–ZnCl2 (P4–Zn), and 50% PEG400–InCl3 (P4–In) to obtain mixed solutions (Fig. S25), which were then characterized by UV-vis absorption spectroscopy. In Fig. 6d, the absorption peaks of ICl and I2 are observed at 338 nm and 440 nm, respectively.65 In pure water, I2 exhibits a strong absorption peak. Upon the addition of 50% PEG400, the I2 peak intensity decreases markedly, suggesting that PEG400 suppresses the hydrolysis of ICl. When the same concentrations of ZnCl2 and InCl3 are added separately to a 50% PEG400 solution, the I2 signal is significantly reduced in the presence of In3+ (P4–In), demonstrating that PEG400 and In3+ synergistically inhibit ICl hydrolysis. Fig. 6e presents the absorption spectra of I3−, with characteristic peaks at 288 nm and 350 nm. Among the tested systems, the P4–In electrolyte shows the weakest I3− absorption, indicating the most effective suppression of I3− formation.4

To further evaluate the dissolution or shuttling effect of polyiodides in the electrolyte, in situ UV-vis spectroscopy was performed during battery operation (Fig. 6f and g). In the Zn‖I2 cell assembled with the BE, the characteristic I3− peaks at 288 nm and 350 nm continuously intensify during charging.66 In contrast, the Zn‖I2 cell using the 50% P4–In electrolyte exhibits substantially lower I3− peak intensities, which remain stable throughout the charging process. These results demonstrate that the 50% P4–In electrolyte effectively suppresses the generation and dissolution of polyiodides (particularly I3−) during charging, thereby significantly mitigating their shuttling effect between the cathode and anode. Unlike the continuous accumulation of I3− signals observed in the BE system, the 50% P4–In electrolyte maintains a consistently low and stable I3− concentration, indicating that this electrolyte helps preserve the local chemical environment of iodine species and reduces active material loss. Consequently, it is expected to enhance both the CE and cycling stability of Zn‖I2 cells.

Conclusion

In summary, this study developed a novel coordination competition and hydrogen-bond reconstruction strategy and then designed a hybrid electrolyte for large-capacity Zn‖I2 batteries with I+/I2/I− conversion and wide temperature tolerance. Theoretical calculations and spectroscopic analyses reveal that In3+ exerts a dual-function effect on both electrodes: at the cathode side, hydrated In3+ suppresses the formation of polyiodides; at the anode side, In3+ is preferentially reduced to metallic In, which together with PEG400 and the decomposition products of zinc salts constructs an indium-rich organic–inorganic hybrid SEI. This SEI induces uniform zinc deposition along the (002) plane and effectively inhibits side reactions such as dendrite growth and hydrogen evolution corrosion. Meanwhile, PEG400 anchors the ICl intermediate, suppressing its hydrolysis and thereby enhancing the stability and reversibility of 4eZIBs. Furthermore, the strong interaction between PEG400 and water molecules reconstructs the hydrogen-bond network, significantly reducing water activity and simultaneously lowering the freezing point of the electrolyte. As a result, a Zn‖I2 full cell assembled with the 50% P4–In electrolyte achieves stable cycling for 10 000 cycles and exhibits excellent cycling stability over a wide temperature range from −50 to 70 °C. Moreover, the assembled Ah-level pouch cell demonstrates great promise for practical applications. This study provides new insights into the electrolyte design for high-stability and wide-temperature-range aqueous zinc–iodine batteries.
