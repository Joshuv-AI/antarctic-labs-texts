Physiological role of dietary energy in the sexual maturity: clues of body size, gonad development, and serum biochemical parameters of Chinese indigenous chicken
Authors: Lingqian Yin, Qian Chen, Qinke Huang, Xinyu Wang, Donghao Zhang, Zhongzhen Lin, Yan Wang, Yiping Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Sexual maturity is a crucial factor in the formation and development of poultry reproductive capacity. The nutritional status has been confirmed to play an important role in the regulation of sexual maturity. To investigate the effect of dietary energy levels on sexual maturity in chicken, diets with 3 energy levels (group L: 2,573 kcal/kg, group C: 2,836 kcal/kg, group H: 3,122 kcal/kg) were implemented to feed Guangyuan Gray chickens. During this trial, body weight, body size, organ development, sexual maturity, reproductive performance and blood biochemical parameters were monitored. The earlier sexual maturity was observed in group H, as well as a heavier first egg weight, larger interpubic distance and higher total cholesterol (T-CHO) content at sexual maturity. The dietary energy levels had no significant effect on body weight at first egg and egg production at 300 d of age. Although dietary energy levels had a significant effect on body weight, comb length, tibia length and girth, abdominal fat weight, oviduct weight and length, T-CHO, triglyceride (TG) content and estradiol (E2) level during the rearing period. No significant difference of gonadotropin releasing hormone (GnRH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) level among 3 groups was observed during the trial. The dietary energy levels had effects on mRNA expression of GnRH, estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), estrogen receptor 2 (ESR2) in hypothalamus, gonadotropin inhibitory hormone receptor (GnIHR) in pituitary and luteinizing hormone receptor (LHR), ESR2 in ovary. The GnIHR/GnRHR ratio in pituitary was higher before sexual maturity and decreased at sexual maturity. The results of correlations analysis found that all the body size, carcass traits, serum biochemical parameters negatively correlated with age at first egg except for interpubic distance and serum blood glucose content. Collectively, dietary energy levels had effects on sexual maturity of chicken, which may be achieved by affecting body weight, gonad development, endocrine and the mRNA expression of genes related to hypothalamus-pituitary-gonad axis. These results further set our understanding of how dietary energy regulates sexual maturity.

INTRODUCTION

Sexual maturity is important for poultry production. Both early and delayed sexual maturity has adverse effects on the uniformity and egg production of broiler and layer. In the production of indigenous chickens, it is a central task to improve the egg-laying performance of breeder chickens. The onset of lay is a sign of sexual maturity of female chicken, which was confirmed to determine the production performance, such as total egg production, egg weight at onset of laying and feed conversion ratio for egg (Lewis and Gous, 2006; Alvarez and Hocking, 2009; van der Klein et al., 2020). Therefore, enhancing the egg production performance of poultry through selective breeding of onset of lay is a feasible approach, especially for breeds with late sexual maturity.

The sexual maturity of animals is regulated by various factors, such as genetics, nutrition, and environment. Among them, the relationship between sexual maturity and nutritional status has always been a subject of study in the field of reproductive biology. The sexual maturity in animals is a highly energy-consuming process, thus it is strictly regulated by the body's nutritional status and energy metabolism. Previous studies have found a correlation between the sexual maturity of animals and body weight and body composition on different animal species. Studies conducted on quails revealed that the correlation between body weight and body composition with sexual maturity (Reddish et al., 2003; Abou Khadiga et al., 2016). Similar results have also been obtained in the study of chickens. Researchers compared the body weight and body composition of chickens at the onset of laying and found that reach a minimum weight was necessary for initiating laying (Dunnington and Siegel, 1984). In addition, for chickens at the same age, the sexually mature pullets had higher body weight and fat storage than the immature pullets (Zelenka et al., 1986). These findings were also confirmed in subsequent studies (Jambui et al., 2017; Heijmans et al., 2023), which suggested that poultry need to reach a critical threshold of body weight or fat storage to enter the egg-laying stage. Nutritional status also has an effect on the development of body frame, which was reported to be related to sexual maturity in mammals and poultry (Vargas et al., 1999; Setoguchi et al., 2011; Afrouziyeh et al., 2021).

Nutritional status exquisitely regulated sexual maturity by influencing the development of reproductive organs and metabolic condition (Yu et al., 1992; Brito et al., 2007; Rizzoto et al., 2019). The morphology and function maturity of reproductive organs is the internal sign of sexual maturity. It was reported that dietary energy level influenced the development of testis, ovary, oviduct and ovarian follicle in different animals (Zhou et al., 2010; do Nascimento Barreto et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2020). The gonad and metabolic organ of chicken developed rapidly close to sexual maturity (Cui et al., 2020; Shi et al., 2020). Likewise, the fluctuation of serum biochemical indexes and hormones were also observed during the sexual maturity (Singh et al., 2013; Cui et al., 2020). It was indicated that adequate energy intake was necessary for reproductive development and hormone synthesis before sexual maturity.

Guangyuan Gray chicken is a famous indigenous breed with features of gray feather and skin. It performs better in meat quality and flavor, which is popular among local people (Li et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2023). However, the low-yield meat and egg production limits the commercial exploitation of this breed. The average body weight of population at 154 d of age is less than 2 kg (Yin et al., 2023). The slow increase in body weight also results in delayed sexual maturity. The average age at first egg of population is around 150 d and varies a lot among individuals, which directly affects the egg production and flock uniformity.

In addition to genetic and environment, it is a simple and effective way to improve the production performance by adjusting the nutrition of diet. The relationship of nutritional status, physical development and sexual maturity of poultry is intertwined. Less is known regarding the mechanism by which these external and internal factors respond individually or in combination to dietary energy level changes. Therefore, in order to investigate the impact of dietary energy levels on the sexual maturity of Guangyuan Gray chickens and to estimate the optimal dietary energy level for this breed, this study focused on the influence of diet with different energy levels on body size, gonad development, egg production performance, blood biochemical parameters, hormone level, and the expression of hypothalamus-pituitary-gonad (HPG) axis-related genes during the rearing period (11–18 wk). The findings of this study provide a deeper insight into the impact of dietary energy levels on the physiological and genetic characteristics of this indigenous breed.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This trial was conducted under the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (DKY2021102011). All research work was implemented in accordance with the guidelines of Sichuan Agricultural University (SAU) Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics.

Experimental Design and Animals Breeding

A total of 3,000 fertilized eggs were collected and subjected to standard incubation procedures. Following hatching, manual sexing was performed on the chicks. A total of 1,000 healthy female chicks were selected to floor rearing with strict control over environmental temperature and humidity. At 10 wk of age, 630 chickens with similar body weights (around 0.61 ± 0.08 kg) were chosen and housed in individual cages (40 × 40 × 22.5 cm, L × H × W) at the same height to light. The LED light source (5 W) was installed every 3 m, 2.5 m above the ground in the closed room. The chickens were randomly divided into 3 groups, each consisting of 210 individuals, and each group had 5 replicates (n = 42). From 11 to 18 wk of age, each group was fed a corn-soybean meal basal diet (Table 1) with 3 calculated metabolizable energy (ME) levels (group L: 2,573 kcal/kg, group C: 2,836 kcal/kg, group H: 3,122 kcal/kg) and same crude protein content (CP: 15%). The diets and the amount of feed (Table S1) provided to chickens per day were formulated in accordance with the recommendations of Feeding Standard of Meat-type Chicken Breeder (2004). From 19 wk of age, 3 groups were fed the same ration with equal energy levels. All chickens were raised under unified lighting (Table S2) and environmental conditions. Sample and Data Collection

During the trial, 25 chickens per group were selected randomly from each replicate for body weight measurement weekly and 10 chickens per group were selected for frame size assessment biweekly. The body slope length, sternal length, comb height, comb length, tibia length, tibial girth, and interpubic distance were measured. Ten chickens per group were randomly selected for blood collection from the wing veins around 10:00 am and then slaughtered for organ development assessment biweekly. The hypothalamus, pituitary, ovary, liver, and abdominal fat were collected. The liver weight, abdominal adipose weight, ovarian stroma weight (after removing the follicles with a diameter ≥2 mm), oviduct length and weight were measured, followed by washing with phosphate buffered saline and frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately. Chickens were raised to 300 d of age. The data of the age and body weight at first egg, first egg weight, daily egg production, and average age of sexual maturity (days of age at 5% production) were recorded until 300 d of age for each group.

Total RNA Extraction, cDNA Synthesis and Real-Time Quantitative PCR

Total RNA of hypothalamus, pituitary and ovary stroma were extracted using Trizol reagent (Takara, Dalian, China). The concentration and purity of RNA were determined by NanoDrop 2000C spectrophotometer (Thermo, San Jose, CA). Then, optimal RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Prime Script RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Real-time quantitative PCR (q-RT-PCR) was conducted for relative quantitation analysis of gonadotropin releasing hormone 1 (GnRH), gonadotropin releasing hormone 1 receptor (GnRHR), gonadotropin inhibitory hormone receptor (GnIHR), luteinizing hormone receptor (LHR), estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), and estrogen receptor 2 (ESR2) mRNA with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as the endogenous control. All primers for q-RT-PCR were designed in Prime Premier 6 according to the sequence in GeneBank and were listed in Table S3. The relative expression of mRNA was calculated according to the 2−ΔΔCT method. The ratio of GnIHR and GnRHR expression was calculated as the relative expression of GnIHR divided by the relative expression of GnRHR.

Serum Isolation and Biochemical Parameters, Hormones Analysis

The blood was centrifuged for 10 min at 3,600 rpm at 4°C to isolate serum. Total cholesterol (T-CHO) (mmol/L) was measured by cholesterol oxidase method. Triglyceride (TG) was measured by glycerol-3-phosphate oxidase and peroxidase method. Blood glucose (GLU) was measured by glucose oxidase method. The commercial kits for TC, TG, and Glu content measurement were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Jiangsu, China). The levels of gonadotropin releasing hormone (GnRH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and estradiol (E2) were estimated by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (mlbio, Shanghai, China).

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 26 (SPSS for Windows, Chicago, IL). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized to examine the statistical differences between the means of groups. Least significant difference (LSD) was performed for multiple comparisons. Partial correlations analysis was performed to assess the relationship between reproductive traits and body size, carcass traits, serum biochemical parameters with the group considered as control variable. P < 0.05 indicates a significant statistical difference of all comparisons.

DISCUSSION

Sexual maturity is a complex biological process regulated by a sophisticated network consisting of the genetics, nutrition, and environment. In poultry production, the control of sexual maturity can be easily achieved through artificial changes in nutrition and environment. In this study, we investigated the effects of energy levels on sexual maturity of Guangyuan Gray chickens by feeding diets with different metabolizable energy levels. As found in the results, the higher energy level in the diet increased the body weight before sexual maturity and advanced age at first egg. These results were congruent with the recent study (Bahry et al., 2023). The body weight at first egg and abdominal fat weight were also found to be negatively correlated with the age at first egg. Combined with the above results, it was indicated that dietary energy level regulated sexual maturity by influencing body weight and body composition. The variation trend of first egg weight was consistent with that of body weight among 3 groups, and first egg weight was positively correlated with body weight at first egg. These results were consistent with similar results in previous studies (Lewis et al., 1994; Heck et al., 2004). Overall, increasing the dietary energy level has an effect on the age at first egg and first egg weight.

The related traits and parameters, such as body size, sexual maturity, gonad development, egg production, and HPG axis-related gene expression during the trial, were measured. The results showed that different dietary energy levels had effects on body weight, frame size, organs development, reproductive traits of 3 treatments, which may explain how energy regulates sexual maturity. Different dietary energy levels had effects on body weight and frame size of Guangyuan Gray chicken. The low dietary energy levels resulted in a smaller body weight and body size during the rearing time, while no difference was observed between 3 groups at sexual maturity except for interpubic distance. The similar results that low-energy level depressed the body weight and delayed sexual maturity was also found in previous studies (van der Klein et al., 2018; Bahry et al., 2023). Likewise, there was no significant difference in body weight at sexual maturity in chickens fed different dietary energy contents in previous study (Hocking, 2004). These results indicated that the energy level of the diet can affect body weight of poultry for achieving sexual maturity. A certain body weight was necessary for sexual maturity. In term of Guangyuan Gray chicken, the threshold of body weight for sexual maturity is around 1.67 kg as observed in Table 2, which was larger than previously reported results in layers (Hanlon et al., 2021). It was deduced that such difference may be related to breed and low genetic selection of Guangyuan Gray chicken. The development of comb was thought to be related to sexual maturity of chicken (Chen et al., 2020; Mohammadi et al., 2021). However, only the comb length of 3 groups was observed different at 14 wk of age in this study. It could be speculated that the rapid development stage of comb mainly occurred in a short period before sexual maturity. The tibia length was known as proxies for body frame size. It was reported to be affected by feed restriction (Renema et al., 2007). In this study, the tibia length of the 3 groups was statistically different from 14 to 16 wk of age and the dietary energy levels did have an effect on it, which indicated that 14 to 16 wk may be the critical period when Guangyuan Gray chickens needed adequate nutrients for physical development. Interpubic distance was an easily obtained information to estimate the progression of poultry toward sexual maturity (Yang et al., 2020). In this experiment, the interpubic distance between 3 treatments was observed statistically difference from 16 wk of age to sexual maturity, and the interpubic distance increased with sexual maturity approaching. In summary, body weight is the key factor determining the sexual maturity of chickens. The growth traits, such as tibia length, tibia girth, interpubic distance, was related with sexual maturity and could be used as indexes for evaluating the progression of sexual maturity.

The development of reproductive organs was the physiological basis for sexual maturity. From the onset of sexual maturity, the reproductive organs of animals rapidly develop to maturity, both the morphology and function of the reproductive organs are constantly development for reproduction. In this study, different dietary energy levels had impact on the oviduct development, while no statistic difference was observed in ovarian stroma weight between 3 groups. The previous study confirmed that restricted feeding reduces the ovarian weight and number of large yellow follicle in chickens during sexual maturity (Renema et al., 1999). The results in this study were inconsistent with theirs, which was possibly due to the difference in sampling, as we weighed the ovarian stroma. Therefore, it is speculated that the impact of dietary energy level on ovarian weight is primarily determined by the development of follicles rather than the ovarian stroma. The oviduct of chicken was found to develop rapidly from 16 wk of age (Yu and Marquardt, 1974). The similar result was also observed in this experiment. Moreover, the development of oviduct in group H was more rapid than the other groups from 16 wk of age. To some extent, this result may explain the reason why the earlier onset of laying in the group H observed in this study, which was possibly due to the earlier complete of reproductive organs development. In poultry, liver is a vital organ for fatty acid oxidation and lipid synthesis and metabolism. In this experiment, the diets with different energy levels did not affect the weight of the liver in each group. The same results have been confirmed in previous studies, although they were expressed as a percentage of body weight (Rozenboim et al., 2016). The egg yolk is mainly synthesis in liver. In this study, there was a significantly positive correlation between liver weight and first egg weight. Previous studies have found that the function of the liver varies significantly before and after sexual maturity in hens (Cui et al., 2020). Herein, a significant increase was observed in liver weight from 18 wk to sexual maturity. This result indicated that liver is crucial for the sexual maturity and egg production of poultry. Abdominal fat is an important energy storage organ in animals, which was found to be deposited more in chickens fed high-energy diets (Rozenboim et al., 2016). It was found that the group H exhibited the highest abdominal fat weight of the 3 groups, especially at 12 and 16 wk. In addition, the group with higher abdominal fat weight showed earlier sexual maturity. The results of correlation analysis also showed that abdominal fat weight had a strong negative correlation with age at first egg. The similar results were also found in previous studies (Zelenka et al., 1986; Reddish et al., 2003). These results suggested that abdominal fat does play a vital role in the sexual maturity of poultry. In summary, the dietary energy levels may regulate sexual maturity process by increasing lipid storage in the body and accelerating the development of reproductive organs.

Some serum biochemical parameters such as total cholesterol, triglyceride, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol have been shown to be correlated with sexual maturity (Berenson et al., 1981; Tell et al., 1985; Bertrais et al., 2000; Mascarenhas et al., 2015). The serum T-CHO and TG were observed to be higher in group H, which were also negatively correlated with age at first egg. These results indicated that dietary energy level may regulate the course of sexual maturity via influenced T-CHO and TG content in serum. T-CHO and TG in serum may be a reference to assist in predicting body composition and sexual maturity of poultry. Reproductive hormones are endocrine signals to regulate sexual maturity. In this study, there was no significant effect of dietary energy levels on reproductive hormones was found in this study except for the content of E2 at 14 and 18 wk of age. However, the mRNA of LHR and ESR2 in the ovary and ESR1 in the hypothalamus were found to be different among 3 groups. These results indicate that dietary energy level had effects on the level of E2 in poultry, which may be regulated the synthesis and secretion of E2 by influencing body weight (van der Klein et al., 2019). In addition, the sensitivity of estradiol receptors in different parts to dietary energy level may be different. In the hypothalamus, ESR1 may be more sensitive to energy level, while in the ovary may be ESR2. Briefly, the dietary energy levels may regulate sexual maturity process by influencing the contents of circulating T-CHO, TG and E2, as well as the mRNA expression of LHR, ESR1, and ESR2.

The pulsatile release of GnRH and LH was the sign of sexual maturity initiation (Plant, 2015). In this study, the content of GnRH, LH increased rapidly during sexual maturity. The mRNA expression level of GnRH was consistent with the change of GnRH. These results were similarly like the previous studies, which confirmed that the changes of GnRH and LH were closely related to sexual maturity in poultry. In previous studies, it was found that estradiol can directly bound ESR1 and ESR2 in GnRH neurons to regulate GnRH neurons activity through positive and negative feedback (Mahoney and Padmanabhan, 2010; Kermath et al., 2014). The content of E2 in group H and C was found higher at 18 wk of age and decreased at sexual maturity, which was consistent with the result observed in previous study (Hanlon et al., 2021). The mRNA of ESR1 and ESR2 expressed in hypothalamus and in ovary showed different variation trends at sexual maturity. It was speculated that E2 may firstly acted on the ovary to stimulate the ovary to produce more E2, and then positively activated GnRH neurons before sexual maturity in chicken. These results may reflect the positive feedback effect of sustained elevated E2 on GnRH and downstream LH during sexual maturity (Moenter et al., 2009). The GnIH/GnRH receptor ratio was reported to be changed during sexual maturity in avian species, which resulted in sensitivity of pituitary switched from inhibition to stimulation (Shimizu and Bedecarrats, 2010). In this study, the ratio of GnIHR/GnRHR was high before sexual maturity and decreased rapidly from 18 wk of age to sexual maturity. Previous studies have speculated that sustained elevated serum E2 level at the onset of sexual maturity may be a crucial factor determining the change in pituitary GnIHR/GnRH ratio (Maddineni et al., 2008; Bedecarrats et al., 2009). In this research, the E2 was increased at 18 wk of age and decreased at sexual maturity in group H and C, which was contrary to the variation trend of GnIHR expression. These results verified the regulation effect of E2 on GnIHR. In summary, the dietary energy level had more effects on the related genes expressed in the hypothalamus and ovary instead of genes in the pituitary. Estradiol may be involved in the regulation of sexual maturation by regulating GnRH release in the hypothalamus and GnIHR/GnRH ratio in the pituitary.

CONCLUSIONS

The regulation of dietary energy levels on sexual maturity in chickens was further demonstrated in this study. Higher dietary energy levels implemented during rearing stage induced the early occurrence of sexual maturity in chicken. This process may be achieved by regulating body weight, gonad development and endocrine factors. Higher energy level in diet advanced the age at first egg, increased body weight before sexual maturity and first egg weight, promoted oviduct development, increased the content of TCHO, TG, and E2 in systemic blood, and altered the expression of reproductive related genes in HPG axis. However, there was no significant impact was observed in the majority of frame size, liver weight, ovarian stroma weight, the content of GLU, GnRH, and LH in systemic blood.
