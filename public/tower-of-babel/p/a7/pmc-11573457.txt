Geranylgeranylated SCFFBXO10 regulates selective outer mitochondrial membrane proteostasis and function
Authors: Sameer Ahmed Bhat, Zahra Vasi, Liping Jiang, Shruthi Selvaraj, Rachel Ferguson, Sanaz Salarvand, Anish Gudur, Ritika Adhikari, Veronica Castillo, Hagar Ismail, Avantika Dhabaria, Beatrix Ueberheide, Shafi Kuchay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYCompartment-specific cellular membrane protein turnover is not well understood. We show that FBXO10, the interchangeable component of the cullin-RING-ligase 1 complex, undergoes lipid modification with geranylgeranyl isoprenoid at cysteine953, facilitating its dynamic trafficking to the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM). FBXO10 polypeptide lacks a canonical mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS); instead, its geranylgeranylation at C953 and interaction with two cytosolic factors, cytosolic factor-like δ subunit of type 6 phosphodiesterase (PDE6δ; a prenyl-group-binding protein) and heat shock protein 90 (HSP90; a chaperone), orchestrate specific OMM targeting of prenyl-FBXO10. The FBXO10(C953S) mutant redistributes away from the OMM, impairs mitochondrial ATP production and membrane potential, and increases fragmentation. Phosphoglycerate mutase-5 (PGAM5) was identified as a potential substrate of FBXO10 at the OMM using comparative quantitative proteomics of enriched mitochondria. FBXO10 loss or expression of prenylation-deficient FBXO10(C953S) inhibited PGAM5 degradation, disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis, and impaired myogenic differentiation of human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and murine myoblasts. Our studies identify a mechanism for FBXO10-mediated regulation of selective mitochondrial proteostasis potentially amenable to therapeutic intervention.

INTRODUCTION

The ubiquitin proteasome system (UPS) controls protein turnover throughout the cell, including the cytoplasm, nucleoplasm, and diverse membrane compartments.1,2 However, the operation of the UPS within specific membrane compartments, such as mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), plasma membranes (PMs), and other sub-cellular membrane compartments, is not well understood. Within this framework, approximately 600 predicted E3 ubiquitin ligases (E3s), as encoded in the human genome, serve as principal nodes for substrate specificity by directly recruiting protein targets for turnover.1,3,4 The membrane compartment-specific substrate recruitment and turnover by E3s is not completely clear. In particular, the full complement of E3s operating from membrane compartments and the mechanism(s) by which E3s are targeted to specific membrane compartments to enable ubiquitylation of organellar substrates are not established. The processes by which E3s, especially non-transmembrane E3s, are delivered to specific sub-cellular membrane compartments to sustain their spatially restricted distribution, whether dynamic or static, and recognize membrane-associated substrates are unclear.

One mode of protein targeting to membrane compartments is through post-translational lipid modifications.3,4 Prenylation, a type of lipid modification, is essential for endo- and peripheral membrane compartment associations.5,6 Four known mammalian prenyltransferases (PTs), Ftase and GGtases 1–3, enable prenylation of a large class of proteins containing the canonical C-terminal CaaX motif signature (C, cysteine; a, aliphatic; X, any amino acid) and atypical CaaX motifs (CxxX, CCxX, xCCX, xxCC, xCxC, and CCxxX).7,8 PTs catalyze the attachment of either a farnesyl (15-carbon) or a geranylgeranyl (20-carbon) isoprenoid lipid via an irreversible thioether linkage to the cysteine residue in the CaaX motif of the substrate prenyl-protein,9,10 which may undergo further processing for efficient membrane association.6,11 Although prenylation is essential for target protein-membrane association, it alone does not ensure the correct delivery of CaaX proteins to specific sub-cellular membrane compartments.6,11 For example, PM-targeted prenyl-proteins generally contain what are termed as second signal sequences, usually juxtaposed to CaaX motifs.12,13 Moreover, cytosolic factor-like δ subunit of type 6 phosphodiesterase (PDE6δ) binds isoprenoid groups of prenyl-proteins, allowing transport within the aqueous milieu of the cytoplasm between various membrane compartments.14,15

Mitochondria are distinctive double membranous organelles with their own genome (mtDNA), and as such, mitochondria-driven mechanisms impact a wide spectrum of cellular processes, including differentiation.16 Remarkably, mitochondria are highly plastic organelles organized as dynamic networks undergoing constant morphological remodeling (fusion/fission cycles, intracell mobility) in response to cues and stressors. Abnormal mitochondrial dynamics compromise their functional efficiency, triggering mitophagic degradation.17 However, such destruction and recycling of whole mitochondria are not suitable for the targeted regulation of specific mitochondrial proteins. While basal mitophagy ensures an efficient pool of healthy mitochondria, persistent mitophagy may be counterproductive. Trafficking of the mitochondrial proteome (~1,100 proteins, 98% nuclear encoded) and its distribution to sub-mitochondrial compartments (outer mitochondrial membrane [OMM], inner mitochondrial membrane [IMM], matrix [M], and intermembrane space [IMS]) depend on mitochondria-targeting sequences (MTSs) integral to polypeptides and mitochondria-specific chaperones, such as heat shock protein 90 (HSP90), that dock on OMM receptors to deliver the client for distribution within the sub-compartments.18,19 Mitophagy (e.g., via Parkin and FBXL4 E3s) results in non-selective wholesale mitochondrial degradation20,21; however, the UPS-regulated selective protein ubiquitylation at each submitochondrial compartment involved in fine-tuned mitochondria-driven homeostatic functions is unclear. Here, we report that FBXO10 is a geranylgeranylated-CaaX-protein E3, functional at the OMM. Our studies demonstrate the critical importance of the maintenance of the dynamic OMM distribution of FBXO10 for selective OMM proteostasis and function.

RESULTS

FBXO10 maintains mitochondrial proteostasis

F-box proteins (69 in humans) are the substrate receptors of CRL1 (CUL1-RING protein ligase) complexes (aka SCF for SKP1, CUL1, F-box protein complex).1,2 A close inspection of the coding sequences of FBXO10 and its paralog FBXO11 revealed that FBXO10, but not FBXO11, which is a nuclear protein,22 terminates with an atypical CaaX motif sequence, CTIL, highly conserved across species orthologs with a cysteine residue at position 953 (C953) (Figures 1A and S1A). CaaX-motif-containing proteins, upon prenylation, are known to associate with cellular membrane compartments.8,9 Therefore, we hypothesized that FBXO10 is a membrane-associated F-box protein. We swapped the C953 residue to serine (C953S) to generate mutant FBXO10(C953S) to interrogate the CaaX-motif-dependent sub-cellular distribution and functions of FBXO10 (Figure S1B). The live-cell confocal imaging with organelle-specific markers demonstrated that GFP-FBXO10 decorates the mitochondrial network (Figures 1B, row 1; 1K, top; and S1C, row 1). In contrast, mutation of cysteine953 in the CaaX motif rendered GFP-FBXO10(C953S) cytosolic, as revealed by a homogeneous fluorescence with negatively visualized organelles (Figures 1B, row 2; 1K, bottom; and S1C, rows 2–4). To monitor the PM and motile vesicular compartment (VC), we designed a fluorescent probe, PMV-SeQ (Figure S1D), based on our recent work with another PM-localized E3, SCFFBXL2.12 GFP-FBXO10 and GFP-FBXO10(C953S) do not decorate the PM and VC (Figures 1B, row 3, and S1C, row 3). Analysis of the ER compartment revealed that ER localization was indistinguishable between GFP-FBXO10 and GFP-FBXO10(C953S) (Figures 1B, row 4, and S1C, row 4). Because post-prenylation CaaX motif processing occurs at the ER/Golgi, it may not be surprising for GFP-FBXO10 to temporarily localize in this fraction before its final mitochondrial destination. Sub-cellular fractionation and flow-cytometry-based quantification validated the exclusive enrichment of FBXO10 in mitochondria (Figures 1C, lanes 2 vs. 5, top row, and 1D). In contrast, FBXO10(C953S) delocalized away from the mitochondria (Figures 1C, lane 3 vs. 6, top row; S1J; and 1D).

FBXO10 coimmunoprecipitated with SKP1 and CUL1 (Figure S1E) from enriched mitochondrial fractions, suggesting an authentic FBXO10-CRL1 associated with mitochondria. From cell lysate, FBXO10(C953S) also coimmunoprecipitated with SKP1 and CUL1, with a detectable difference in CUL1 neddylation compared to wild-type FBXO10 (Figure 1E). This result is consistent with the notion that FBXO10 may engage substrates that promote cullin neddylation23,24 for ubiquitylation at mitochondria.

We exploited the delocalized FBXO10(C953S) mutant to investigate the importance of the maintenance of the sub-cellular distribution for the substrate degradation at a specific sub-cellular membrane organelle, the mitochondrion. Accordingly, we analyzed perturbations in the mitochondria-associated proteome by label-free quantitative mass spectrometry analysis (LFQ-MS/MS) of enriched mitochondrial protein fractions purified from doxycycline-inducible cells stably expressing FLAG-FBXO10, FLAG-FBXO10(C953S), and empty vector (EV) at low or near-endogenous levels (Figures S1F and S1G). Proteins whose abundance was reciprocally impacted, i.e., increased by FBXO10(C953S) expression but decreased upon FBXO10 expression, were considered as potential targets of FBXO10, either direct or indirect. Our unbiased quantitative mass spectrometry data unmasked the role of FBXO10 in selective proteostasis at mitochondria and revealed dysregulated proteostasis of mitochondria-associated proteins by the expression of the FBXO10(C953S) mutant (Figures 1F, 1G, S1H, and S1I; Table S1). As we found that FBXO10 resides in the OMM but not the IMM or the M (refer to Figures 2F and 2G), we reasoned that direct substrates of FBXO10 should also localize to the OMM. We shortlisted 18 OMM and/or OMM-associated protein targets that showed significant reciprocal changes in protein levels (≥2-fold change, 5% false discovery rate [FDR]) (Figures 1F and 1G; Table S1). Moreover, functional clustering of the dysregulated proteins (deregulation ≥2-fold, FDR 5%) indeed showed the highest enrichment score for mitochondria, as expected, and revealed that FBXO10-mediated protein turnover at the OMM impacts a range of cellular processes (Figure S1I).

CaaX-mutant FBXO10(C953S) and FBXO10 loss dysregulate mitochondrial ATP production, membrane potential, and morphological dynamics

To examine the functional consequences of impaired FBXO10 sub-cellular distribution and dysregulated protein turnover at the OMM, we investigated mitochondrial ATP production, mitochondrial membrane potential (MPP), and mitochondrial morphological dynamics. In addition to FBXO10(C953S), we also generated an FBXO10(ΔF-box) mutant that is unable to assemble into a functional SCF yet able to localize correctly at mitochondria (Figures S1J and S1K) and hence is expected to sequester substrates away from proteasomal degradation.25 Mitochondrial ATP production and mitochondrial respiratory capacity was inhibited by FBXO10(C953S) and FBXO10(ΔF-box) expression, but not by wild-type FBXO10 (Figures 1H and S2A), suggesting that FBXO10(C953S) and FBX019(ΔF-box) act in a dominant-negative manner. Interestingly, proportionally glycolytic ATP content increased in cells expressing delocalized FBXO10(C953S) and FBXO10(ΔF-box) (Figure 1H). FBXO10 deletion (via CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing) and/or depletion (via two different small hairpin RNAs [shRNAs]) inhibited mitochondrial ATP production but enhanced the proportion of glycolytic ATP (Figures 1I, S6E, and S1M). MPP was inhibited by delocalized FBXO10(C953S) and FBXO10(ΔF-box) expression, but not by wild-type FBXO10 (Figures 1J and S1L). FBXO10 gene deletion also inhibited MPP (Figure 1J).

Wild-type FBXO10 promoted hyperfused spaghetti-like mitochondrial networks and clustering of mitochondria around perinuclear regions compared to non-transfected cells under the same conditions (Figures 1K, top row; 1B, row 1; and S1C, row 1). In contrast, mitochondrial networks in FBXO10(C953S)-expressing cells were visualized as largely non-reticulate, scattered, and irregular networks, apparently revealing mitochondrial fragmentation (Figures 1B, row 2; 1K, bottom row; and S1C, row 2) compared to typical reticulate, well-spread tubular networks in non-transfected cells (Figure 1K, refer to white arrows). Analysis of organellar shape descriptors with quantifications, such as mean mitochondrial area, perimeter, and branching, is consistent with the induction of mitochondrial fragmentation upon expression of delocalized FBXO10(C953S) and perinuclear mitochondrial clustering when wild-type FBXO10 is expressed (Figure 1K, scatterplots). FBXO10 silencing (two different shRNAs) showed that, while in non-targeting shRNA-treated cells mitochondria appeared as typical reticulate networks, FBXO10 silencing showed a reduction in reticulate mitochondria content, appearing as dispersed, consistent with mitochondrial damage and fragmentation (Figure S1M).

FBXO10 is geranylgeranylated on cysteine953 for dynamic sub-cellular distribution to the OMM

We tested the hypothesis that cysteine953 is a prenyl isoprenoid acceptor site in the human FBXO10 CaaX motif (Figures 1A–1C) and sought to determine whether FBXO10 is a candidate for farnesylation and/or geranylgeranylation. Treatment with farnesylation and geranylgeranylation inhibitors (FTi and GGTi) revealed that GGTi-2418, but not FTi-lonafarnib, redistributed GFP-FBXO10 away from mitochondria into the cytosol (Figure 2A), phenocopying the cellular distribution shown by the cysteine953 mutant GFP-FBXO10(C953S) (Figure 1B). Interestingly, treatment with lovastatin (statins are widely used drugs in clinics to lower blood cholesterol)26 led to the redistribution of GFP-FBXO10 away from mitochondria into the cytosol, suggesting FBXO10 is a target of statins (Figure 2A, row 4). Sub-cellular fractionation and flow cytometry quantifications reveal that mitochondrial enrichment of FBXO10 was inhibited by GGTi-2418, but not FTi, treatment, akin to the delocalized cysteine953 mutant FBXO10(C953S) (Figures 1C, 2B, and 2C). These results show that FBXO10 undergoes geranylgeranylation and cysteine953 is an isoprenoid acceptor site necessary for sub-cellular mitochondrial distribution. Metabolic labeling of mammalian cells with tritiated [3H]-mevalonate and clickable geranylgeranyl-azide showed that FBXO10, but not FBXO10(C953S), is prenylated, as assayed directly by two orthogonal assays, autoradiography and Click-IT chemistry (Figures 2D and S2B–S2D).

To determine the exact sub-mitochondrial localization of FBXO10, we performed a trypsin protease protection assay in which we exposed intact double-membranous mitochondria expressing Strep-FBXO10 to trypsin protease. We found that, just like the OMM marker protein TOM70, the FBXO10, along with its SCF cognate subunits SKP1 and cullin-1, was shaved off, whereas resident protein markers of the IMM (SDH), IMS (cytochrome c), and matrix (PDH) remained refractory to trypsin protease digestion (Figure 2E). These results demonstrated that geranylgeranylated FBXO10 resides at the OMM. Flow-cytometry-based quantifications confirmed OMM targeting of geranylgeranylated FBXO10 and that the entire peak of fluorescent ptd-Tomato-FBXO10 was sensitive to trypsin digestion, rendering it to baseline (Figure 2F). To examine if geranylgeranylated FBXO10 associates with the OMM in a permanent or dynamic distribution pattern, we carried out fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) of GFP-FBXO10 focally at mitochondria (Figures 2G and S2E), revealing a dynamic trafficking pattern of geranylgeranylated FBXO10. FRAP of GFP-FBXO10 at mitochondria plateaued at significantly lower levels (Figure 2G), suggesting that fluid-phase diffusion may not be the sole means to account for the FBXO10 trafficking to mitochondria. Wild-type FBXO10 is delocalized away from the OMM in a dose dependent manner upon FBXO10(C953S) mutant coexpression (Figure 2H), suggesting the dominant-negative function of FBXO10(C953S) mutant may result from the delocalization of endogenous FBXO10.

Geranylgeranylated FBXO10 is a client of PDE6δ-mediated transport to the OMM

PDE6δ copurified with FLAG-FBXO10 in immunoaffinity purifications from enriched mitochondrial fractions, as identified by mass spectrometry (Table S2). We validated the mass spectrometric results using coimmunoprecipitation (coIP) of endogenous PDE6δ with Strep-FBXO10 from whole-cell lysates (Figure 3A, top row, lane 2). Of the 11 F-box proteins tested in a coIP assay, including FBXO11, the paralog of FBXO10, only one that bound endogenous PDE6δ possessed the C-terminal CaaX motif, i.e., FBXO10 (Figures 3B and 1A). Reciprocally, endogenous FBXO10 coimmunoprecipitated with PDE6δ (Figure 3C, lane 2). Lovastatin treatment, by inhibiting prenylation of FBXO10, abrogated coIP of endogenous FBXO10 (Figure 3C, lane 3), validating the specificity of the endogenous FBXO10 detection in coIP. PDE6δ protein turnover measurements demonstrated that PDE6δ is not a substrate of FBXO10 for the ubiquitin-mediated degradation but rather a regulator client of FBXO10 (Figures S3A and S3B). Geranylgeranylation-deficient Strep-FBXO10(C953S) did not bind with PDE6δ (Figure 3A, top row, lane 3), demonstrating the critical importance of the geranylgeranyl isoprenoid modification at C953 for the engagement with PDE6δ. Finally, deltarasin, a drug that blocks the isoprenoid-accommodating pocket of PDE6δ, significantly inhibited the interaction of FBXO10 with PDE6δ (Figure 3D). Thus, rather than a target for degradation, PDE6δ may serve as a regulator client of geranylgeranylated FBXO10.

PDE6δ enables active client prenyl-protein transport through the aqueous milieu of the cytosol between membrane compartments.15,27 We tested the hypothesis that geranylgeranylated FBXO10 is a client for PDE6δ-mediated dynamic transport to the OMM. Extraction of client prenyl proteins (e.g., FBXL2, NRAS, and KRAS4B) from their steady-state membrane compartments upon forced expression of PDE6δ provides the assessment of PDE6δ function.12,28 We found FBXO10 to be extracted from the mitochondria/OMM and rendered to the cytosol upon overexpression of PDE6δ, as evident from the homogeneous fluorescence of GFP-FBXO10 in cells cotransfected with mCherry-PDE6δ (Figure 3E). Deltarasin, by blocking the interaction between PDE6δ and prenyl-protein (e.g., FBXL2, KRAS4B), paradoxically prevents prenyl-proteins from associating with membrane compartments due to the interruption in the regulated constant cycling between intended membrane compartments essential for steady-state sub-cellular localization.12,28 Depletion of PDE6δ using small interfering RNA (siRNA) silencing and/or pharmacological inhibition using deltarasin disrupts GFP-FBXO10’s localization to the mitochondrial membrane association (Figures 3F, 3G, S3C, and S3D). Taken together, the results show that PDE6δ is a cytosolic factor essential for the dynamic transport of geranylgeranylated FBXO10 to the OMM.

HSP90 and PDE6δ orchestrate the specific delivery of geranylgeranylated FBXO10 to the OMM

The primary structure of FBXO10 polypeptide lacks a typical MTS. We investigated the molecular determinant(s) necessary and sufficient for the specific delivery of geranylgeranylated FBXO10 to the OMM. Client prenyl-proteins are delivered to the PM and endomembrane by PDE6δ via a “regulated second signal.”12,13 In the absence of an MTS and obvious second signal sequences in the FBXO10 polypeptide, it is unclear how PDE6δ, although necessary (Figures 3A–3F), delivers geranylgeranylated FBXO10 specifically to the OMM. Cytosolic molecular chaperones HSP90 and HSP70 mediate the mitochondria-specific protein targeting by docking onto the integral OMM protein receptor TOM70.19 By mass spectrometry analysis of enriched mitochondrial fractions, we found that HSP90, HSP70, and TOM70 copurified with FLAG-FBXO10 (Table S2). We validated the binding of endogenous HSP90, HSP70, and TOM70 with Strep-FBXO10 (Figure 4A). Protein turnover of HSP90, HSP70, and TOM70 was affected neither by CRISPR-Cas9-mediated FBXO10 gene deletion nor by forced expression of FBXO10 (Figures S4A and S4B), showing that they are rather regulator clients. Interestingly, inhibition of HSP90, but not HSP70, resulted in delocalization of FBXO10 away from mitochondria, as assessed by three independent assays, i.e., confocal immunofluorescence microscopy (Figure 4B), flow cytometry of enriched mitochondrial fractions (Figure 4C), and sub-cellular biochemical fractionations (Figure 4D). Depletion of HSP90 and HSP70 with targeted siRNAs further confirmed the redistribution of GFP-FBXO10 away from mitochondria upon reduction of HSP90 protein levels, but not HSP70 (Figures 4E and S4C). Thus, our results reveal a predominant role for HSP90, but not HSP70, in specific mitochondrial targeting of FBXO10. Furthermore, whereas FBXO10 interacted with HSP90 and HSP70 and TOM70 (integral OMM receptor), geranylgeranylation-deficient FBXO10(C953S) failed to interact with TOM70 but maintained binding to HSP90 and HSP70 comparable to geranylgeranylated FBXO10 (Figure 4A, top row, lane 3). Loss of binding to TOM70, an integral OMM protein, is consistent with the delocalization of FBXO10(C953S) away from mitochondria (Figures 1A–1D), suggesting that the absence of geranylgeranylation at CaaX motif C953 (Figures 2D and 2E) and consequent lack of PDE6δ-mediated transport (Figures 3A–3G) thwarts stable mitochondrial association even in the presence of bound HSP90 and HSP70. Accordingly, CCT018159 treatment interrupted the mitochondrial localization of FBXO10 (Figures 4B–4D), despite no detectable impact on bound PDE6δ (Figure 4F, lane 3 vs. 2). Conversely, deltarasin treatment also interrupted the mitochondrial localization of FBXO10 (Figure 3G) without significant impact on bound HSP90 (Figure 4F, lane 4 vs. 2). Our results demonstrate that, although both PDE6δ and HSP90 are necessary, individually neither is sufficient for steady-state OMM distribution of geranylgeranylated FBXO10 (Figures 3 and 4). Thus, PDE6δ and HSP90 coordinate for the specific delivery of geranylgeranylated FBXO10 to the OMM and maintenance of steady-state OMM distribution. In support of the coordination between PDE6δ and HSP90, we found PDE6δ that coimmunoprecipitated with HSP90 and TOM70 in the presence of FBXO10, but not FBXO10(C953S), demonstrating an OMM multiprotein complex of geranylgeranylated FBXO10, PDE6δ, HSP90, and TOM70 (Figures 4G and S4D). Independent of the geranylgeranylation status of FBXO10, simultaneous inhibition of PDE6δ and HSP90 resulted in efficient redistribution of prenyl-FBXO10 away from OMM, as assessed by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) (Figure 4H) and confocal imaging (Figure S4E). Our results show coordinated roles of PDE6δ and HSP90 for the OMM distribution of prenyl-FBXO10, wherein PDE6δ enables transport of geranylgeranylated FBXO10 through the aqueous environment, and HSP90 directs FBXO10 docking on the mitochondrial receptor TOM70. Because HSP90, but not PDE6δ, maintains interaction with the dominant-negative FBXO10(C953S) mutant (Figures 3A and 4A), we investigated if sequestration of endogenous HSP90 may be responsible for delocalization of wild-type FBXO10 seen upon coexpression of the FBXO10(C953S) mutant (Figure 2H). Coexpression of wild-type FBXO10 and FBXO10(C953S) results in the loss of endogenous HSP90 binding to wild-type FBXO10; however, the binding to PDE6δ was not affected (Figure 4I, lane 3 vs. 4), These data show that HSP90-mediated OMM docking of FBXO10 under such conditions is compromised. Thus, we argue that the endogenous FBXO10 is similarly delocalized away from the OMM in the presence of FBXO10(C953S) and propose HSP90 sequestration (mitochondrial docking chaperone) by the FBXO10(C953S) mutant as the mechanistic basis for the dominant-negative functions of FBXO10(C953S).

Geranylgeranylation-deficient FBXO10(C953S) impairs mitochondria-driven myogenic differentiation in iPSCs and murine myoblasts

Muscle dysfunction in patients on statins forces suspension of statin therapy.26 Interestingly, our results demonstrate that lovastatin inhibits C953-geranylgeranylation-dependent FBXO10 trafficking to the mitochondria (Figure 2A). Skeletal muscle (SM) is highly enriched in mitochondria, and as such, SM is critically dependent on efficient mitochondrial function. We examined if the maintenance of the dynamic OMM sub-cellular distribution of geranylgeranylated-FBXO10 impacts mitochondria-driven myogenesis. We generated human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) stably expressing FBXO10 and geranylgeranylation-deficient FBXO10(C953S) and vehicle lentiviral-infected controls (EV) with comparable expression in engineered iPSCs (Figure S5A). During a 20-day myogenic differentiation program, we monitored the impact sequentially at progenitors, myoblasts, and final SM myotubes. Detectable pluripotency potential (OCT4 immunostaining) remained unaffected (Figure S5B), and no detectable differences were observed up to the myoblast stage as assessed by MyoD immunostaining (Figure S5C). Remarkable differences were observed at the final SM myotube stage as assessed by the myotube marker myosin heavy chain (MyH) immunostaining (Figure 5A). Whereas myotubes were visualized as denser and longer networks upon FBXO10 expression (Figure 5A, middle vs. top row), in contrast, expression of geranylgeranylation-deficient FBXO10(C953S) completely blocked the formation of normal myotubes (Figure 5A, bottom row).

Similar to the human iPSC model, analysis of myogenic differentiation using murine C2C12 myoblasts also revealed remarkable defects in the derived myotubes due to the expression of geranylgeranylation-deficient FBXO10(C953S) (Figures 5B, S5D, and S5E). Whereas FBXO10 promoted formation of oversized myotubes, in contrast, the FBXO10(C953S) expression inhibited myogenic differentiation and resulted in the formation of abnormal myotubes visualized as round and multinucleated (nuclei either clustered and/or malorganized) (Figures 5B and S5E). FBXO10-expressing myotubes show longer and broader sizes with enhanced MyH immunostaining (Figures 5C–5E). In contrast, insignificant MyH immunostaining in abnormal myotubes expressing FBXO10(C953S) precluded their size measurement. Similarly, inhibition of FBXO10 prenylation by lovastatin treatment blocked normal myotube formation (Figure S5F).

To examine if the defective myogenic differentiation resulted from dysregulated mitochondrial morphological dynamics and/or mitochondrial damage, we found, during the differentiation program from myoblasts through myotubes, notable changes in mitochondrial dynamics (Figure S5G). Whereas mitochondria in myoblasts appear like a typical reticulate network spread across the myoblasts in sparse and shorter sub-networks, in contrast, the mitochondria in myotubes appear fused and organized in dense and long networks (Figure S5G, top vs. bottom). Interestingly, the FBXO10-expressing myotubes contained hyperfused, round, and larger mitochondria appearing clustered around perinuclear regions compared to typical networks visualized in controls (Figure 5F, middle vs. top row). In contrast, the abnormal myotubes due to geranylgeranylation-deficient FBXO10(C953S) expression contained strikingly altered mitochondrial morphologies, visualized as small, fragmented, and irregularly organized (Figure 5F, bottom row). FBXO10-expressing myotubes contained mostly healthy intact mitochondrial network markers (MitoTracker red, healthy; MitoView green, mitochondrial mass of both healthy and damaged) (Figure 5F, middle row). Instead, the overlap between markers was limited in abnormal myotubes expressing FBXO10(C953S), with significant portions stained only green, revealing either damaged or fragmented mitochondrial networks (Figure 5F, bottom row). The MPP and respiratory capacity were also inhibited by the expression of FBXO10(C953S) (Figures 1J, S1L, and S2A). Prompted by the induction of mitochondrial damage and fragmentation, we analyzed mitophagy of damaged mitochondrial networks. Mitochondria-targeted mt-Keima imaging assay (a fluorescent probe for measuring mitophagy in vivo based on pH sensitivity)29 revealed that mitophagy in FBXO10-expressing myotubes was distinct from what we observed in EV controls (Figure 5G, top vs. middle row); in contrast, FBXO10(C953S)-expressing abnormal myotubes were undergoing enhanced mitophagy (Figure 5G, bottom row), consistent with their damaged and fragmented mitochondrial networks (Figures 5F, 1J, and 1K).

FBXO10 loss impairs myogenic differentiation and PGAM5 degradation at the OMM during myogenic differentiation

To determine if FBXO10 is required for myogenic differentiation, we silenced FBXO10 with two independent shRNAs in C2C12 myoblasts (Figures S1M and S6A) and analyzed their differentiation into SM myotubes. Both shRNAs (shCDS and sh3′UTR) resulted in a defective myogenic differentiation compared to a non-targeting shRNA control (Figure 6A). Whereas typical and organized myotubes were visualized in non-silenced controls, in contrast, shCDS and sh3′UTR silencing resulted in a striking reduction of intact/organized myotubes (Figure 6A, rows 1–3 and bar graph), and MyH-stained abnormal myotubes appeared sharply reduced and malorganized (Figure 6A, top vs. second and third rows from top). Interestingly, exogenous supplementation with FBXO10, but not FBXO10(C953S), rescued abnormal myotube formation significantly, although incompletely (Figures 6A, bottom row and bar graph, and S6A and S6B), and mitochondrial fragmentation (Figure S6C) in sh3′UTR-silenced myoblasts. Mitochondrial ATP production was completely rescued in sh3′UTR-silenced myotubes supplemented with exogenous FBXO10 (Figure 6B). Next, we analyzed mitophagy using mt-Keima assay, revealing basal/limited mitophagic mitochondrial networks with a typical morphological network profile, dense and long, in control C2C12 myotubes (Figures 6C, top row, and S1M). FBXO10-silenced myotubes showed mitochondrial networks clustered in perinuclear regions with a marked increase in mitophagy, consistent with their mitochondrial damage and fragmentation (Figures 6C and S1M).

Among the 18 OMM putative substrates of FBXO10 shortlisted by the comparative mass spectrometry screen (Figures 1F and 1G), phosphoglycerate mutase-5 (PGAM5; a mitochondrial phosphatase) is implicated in the regulation of stem cell differentiation, mitochondrial morphological dynamics, and mitophagy,30–33 i.e., phenotypes impacted by FBXO10 depletion and/or expression of geranylgeranylation-deficient FBXO10(C953S). We confirmed that PGAM5 indeed is a mitochondrion-associated protein (Figure S6D). Expression of FBXO10, but not FBXO10(C953S), resulted in significant loss of PGAM5 immunofluorescence specifically in transfected cells compared to surrounding non-transfected cells (Figure S6D). Treatment with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 and/or CUL-neddylation inhibitor MLN4924 increased the PGAM5 protein levels in differentiating C2C12 myoblasts (Figure S6F). We examined if PGAM5 is targeted for degradation via FBXO10 during the myogenic differentiation program. By unbiased mass spectrometry analysis of FBXO10 immunocomplexes, purified from enriched mitochondria, we found PGAM5-specific peptide enrichment (Table S2). To validate our mass spectrometry data, we confirmed that, indeed, FBXO10 binds endogenous PGAM5 (Figure 6D). The binding of PGAM5 to FBXO10 is dependent on OMM distribution, as FBXO10(C953S), which distributes away from OMM, shows significant loss in PGAM5 binding (Figures 6D and S6G). PGAM5 specifically engages FBXO10, as tested for 10 F-box proteins, including its paralog FBXO11 (Figure 6D). PGAM5 protein levels fluctuate during myogenic differentiation, with noticeable turnover during the myogenic differentiation program (Figures 6E and S6I). However, FBXO10 deletion via CRISPR-Cas9 editing in C2C12 myoblasts prevented PGAM5 protein degradation during the C2C12 differentiation program (Figures S6H, 6E, and S6I). FBXO10 promoted polyubiquitylation of endogenous PGAM5 at the OMM, whereas basal PGAM5 polyubiquitylation and polyubiquitylation upon expression of FBXO10(C953S) were comparable as assayed by ubiquitin-binding TR-TUBE assay (Figure 6F). Finally, we examined two independent FBXO10-knockout myoblast clones (CRISPR #1 and CRISPR #2) during myogenic differentiation to myotubes. FBXO10-gene-deleted myoblast clones are strikingly defective in SM myotube formation (Figure 6G), consistent with shRNA-mediated FBXO10 silencing (Figures S6J and 6A).

DISCUSSION

Protein turnover at specific sub-cellular membrane compartments via E3-ubiquitin ligases is not well understood. For example, the steady-state sub-cellular distribution of most E3-ligases and the underlying mechanisms to maintain their spatial profile are unknown. A comprehensive understanding of how E3s are spatially restricted to accomplish the compartment-specific protein turnover is warranted. The emergence of E3-ligase-mediated targeted protein degradation strategies, such as PROTACs/molecule glues for therapeutic purposes,34–36 highlights the need for understanding such mechanisms with potential for compartment-specific therapeutics. FBXO10, a substrate receptor of a CRL1, was uncovered by our studies as a dynamically trafficked E3 selectively to the OMM (Figure 2; Figures 1B–1D), and the mechanism for its specific delivery and maintenance at the OMM was demonstrated. We gained pertinent insights into functional consequences by the redistribution of FBXO10 away from the OMM, as an example to underscore the importance of the spatially restricted ubiquitylation process, which may be relevant to disease/biological processes.37–41 FBXO10 primary structure inspection revealed an atypical CaaX motif, CTIL, conserved across orthologs. We examined whether FBXO10 is a candidate for prenyl isoprenoid modification (i.e., either 15-carbon farnesyl or 20-carbon geranylgeranyl) to regulate its OMM association. Our data show, indeed, that FBXO10 is modified by a geranylgeranyl isoprenoid (Figures 2D and S2D). The acceptor site for geranylgeranylation is C953, located in the C-terminal CaaX motif of FBXO10 (Figures 1A, 1B, 2D, and S2D). By swapping C953 with S953, precluding isoprenoid modification in the FBXO10(C953S) mutant, we demonstrated that the integrity of C953 is indispensable for the OMM distribution of FBXO10 (Figures 1A–1D, S1C, and 4G). Geranylgeranylation-deficient FBXO10(C953S) was redistributed away from the OMM to the cytosol, as well as nuclear compartments (Figures 1A–1D and S1C), a profile similar to those of soluble non-prenylated proteins.42,43 Whereas geranylgeranylated FBXO10 at the OMM assembled with its CRL subunits CUL-1 and SKP1, so did the soluble FBXO10(C953S) (Figure 1E). However, reduced neddylatedcullin-1 associated with soluble FBXO10(C953S) (Figure 1E). The neddylation/deneddylation cycle of CRLs is regulated by substrate engagement.23,24 Thus, FBXO10 may engage substrates at the OMM; however, the redistribution away from the OMM precludes FBXO10(C953S) from substrate engagement at the OMM.

We exploited geranylgeranylation-deficient FBXO10(C953S) to expose potential substrates of FBXO10 at the OMM by comparative quantitative mass spectrometry. One of the predicted OMM candidates for selective degradation via FBXO10 included PGAM5, a mitochondrial phosphatase. Functionally, our studies suggest that the timed PGAM5 degradation via FBXO10 promotes the myogenic differentiation program. PGAM5 activity promotes the mitophagic clearance of damaged or fragmented mitochondria, ensuring constant rewiring and biogenesis of only healthy mitochondrial networks during stem cell differentiation.32 We propose that FBXO10-mediated degradation of PGAM5 may serve to dampen and/or terminate non-selective mitophagy, enabling proper progression through a differentiation program. Interestingly, PGAM5, when in the cytosol, is a target of KEAP1, a CRL3 adaptor.44,45 Since KEAP1 is predominantly a cytoplasmic E3, it is not entirely clear if PGAM5 is degraded in the cytosol before its targeting to mitochondria or post-proteolytic processing at mitochondria. FBXO10 targets PGMA5 only at the OMM, as demonstrated by the geranylgeranylation-deficient FBXO10(C953S), which is redistributed away from the OMM (Figures 6D and S6G). Nonetheless, it may be argued that cytosolic KEAP1 regulates events before the initiation of the mitophagy program, and FBXO10 regulates events at the OMM to terminate mitophagy, for example, during myogenic differentiation. Thus, the FBXO10-mediated degradation of PGAM5 at the OMM represents an acute and selective mitochondria-driven degradation event to maintain a rapid check of non-selective mitophagy. Consistent with this notion, PGAM5 protein levels fluctuate, in part dependent on FBXO10-mediated degradation, during the myogenic differentiation program (Figures 6E and S6I). Increased PGAM5 levels, either by FBXO10 loss or expression of FBXO10(C953S), correlate with mitochondrial damage and persistent mitophagy, which counteracts appropriate progression through the myogenic differentiation program (Figures 5 and 6). There are likely additional physiologic functions of FBXO10 due to other substrates at the OMM, e.g., AKAP1/MIRO1 and hexokinases, perhaps also important for the differentiation process (Table S1).

We believe that geranylgeranylation itself, although essential for membrane association, is insufficient to dictate OMM specificity. In addition, the lack of classical MTS signal, and no obvious “second signal” sequence in FBXO10 primary structure, prompted us to investigate the molecular determinants necessary and sufficient for the trafficking and maintenance of dynamic OMM distribution of geranylgeranylated FBXO10. We show that the combined actions of two cytosolic factors, PDE6δ and HSP90, orchestrate the specificity of prenyl-FBXO10 toward OMM distribution (Figures 3 and 4). Both cytosolic factors, PDE6δ and HSP90, are necessary, but neither is sufficient to accomplish the OMM trafficking of FBXO10. Thus, these studies uncover a previously unknown function of PDE6δ to direct trafficking to mitochondria and identify prenyl-FBXO10 as the OMM client. There may be additional proteins that are similarly trafficked to the OMM, despite lacking an MTS, via the tag-teaming of PDE6δ and HSP90, opening possibilities for future discoveries.

Functionally, we show that the OMM distribution of geranylgeranylated FBXO10 is critical for mitochondrial homeostasis, whereas a lack of FBXO10 OMM localization or FBXO10 depletion results in impaired mitochondrial dynamics, reduced mitochondrial respiratory capacity/ATP production, and inhibited MPP. Several E3-ligases, such as Parkin or FBXL4,20,21 are known to translocate to the OMM, but they typically trigger mitophagy and wholesale mitochondrial recycling. The precision targeting of selective OMM proteins such as PGAM5 by FBXO10 allows for more fine-tuned responses in which the cellular machinery selectively degrades specific mitochondrial proteins without triggering mitophagy. In this context, FBXO10 spatial distribution at OMM is not static, but rather dynamic, as demonstrated by two independent assays (Figures 3E–3G and 2G). Thus, understanding regulation of ON and OFF recruitment of FBXO10 at the OMM may help us gain insights into the events involved in programmed degradation of various OMM-resident substrates. We posit that FBXO10 may gain access to specific OMM targets, in part dependent on cues regulating FBXO10 traffic to and from the OMM. Decoding specific cues, for example, that promote and/or counteract PDE6δ and HSP90 binding to prenyl-FBXO10 will require follow-up studies. In summary, our findings propose a model of selective mitochondrial proteostasis via FBXO10 (Figure S7), which paves the way for understanding physiological and pathophysiological roles of E3-ligase-mediated selective OMM protein degradation.

Limitations of the study

In our study, we utilized exogenous FBXO10, which allowed us to specifically determine the function of prenylation-dependent trafficking. However, these approaches do not necessarily recapitulate endogenous FBXO10 geranylgeranylation and OMM localization. Furthermore, the potential OMM targets of FBXO10 revealed by proteomic analysis in this study may not be applicable to all cell and tissue types because regulation of trafficking mechanisms and FBXO10 targets may be cell-type dependent. There is thus a need to examine FBXO10 targets and function in additional cell types and tissues. This also raises the broader question of whether impaired FBXO10 function can cause disease, which could be studied using an in vivo FBXO10 deletion model. Although morphological and myogenic differentiation defects were rescued by reexpression of wild type, but not FBXO10(C953S), it remains unclear if all mitochondrial defects due to FBXO10 depletion will be rescued by FBXO10 but not FBXO10(C953S).

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS

Cell lines

All cell lines were grown at 37°C and 5% CO2 concentration in a humidified incubator. HEK293T, HeLa and C2C12 cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1× Pen-Strep. iPSCs were grown on vitronectin coated plates containing PluriSTEM® Human ES/iPS Cell Medium (Sigma).

METHOD DETAILS

Metabolic labeling and prenylation

H3-mevalonolactone based prenylation assay was performed to establish FBXO10 as a prenylated protein46–48. HEK293T stable cell lines expressing FBXO10 and FBXO10 (C953S) were grown in 6 cm dishes and treated with 15 μM lovastatin for about 2 hrs. 2 mL of labeling medium (DMEM containing 5% dialyzed serum, 0.2 mCi/mL of concentrated H3-mevalonolactone and 15 μM lovastatin) was added to the dishes. After labeling overnight, cells were lysed, and FLAG tagged proteins were immunoprecipitated using anti-FLAG M2 affinity gel (Sigma). Elution was carried out with 2X Bolt LDS sample buffer and the samples were blotted onto the PVDF membrane. The membrane was placed under a phosphor screen (Cytiva) in a cassette and incubated at room temperature in dark. The screen was exposed in a phosphor imager (Amersham Typhoon, GE) for [H3] detection. The PVDF membrane was finally immunoblotted for the expression of FLAG-tagged FBXO10 and FBXO10 (C953S).

Geranylgeranyl-azide based Click-IT prenylation assay was carried out in HEK293T cells in 6cm dishes, grown to 70% confluence. Fresh medium containing 20μM lovastatin was added for about 3hr before metabolic labeling with Gerananylgeranyl azide. About 1.5hr after transfection with FLAG-tagged FBXO10 and FBXO10 (C953S) plasmids, Gerananylgeranyl azide was added into cells at a final concentration of 40μM overnight at 37°C. The cells were washed with PBS and harvested, centrifuged at 1,500 × g for five minutes at 4°C and resuspended in PBS. Cells were lysed by sonication and the protein concentration of the lysate was determined using the BCA assay. For in gel fluorescence analysis, lysates (2 mg/mL, 25 μL) were subjected to click reaction, which was initiated by the addition of TAMRA alkyne (Sigma-Aldrich, 50 μM, 25x stock in DMSO), tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP, Sigma-Aldrich, 1 mM, fresh 50x stock in water), tris[(1-benzyl-1H-1,2,3-triazol-4-yl)methyl]amine (TBTA, Sigma-Aldrich, 100 μM, 16x stock in DMSO), and copper(II) sulfate (1 mM, 50x stock in water) to the lysate and incubated in the dark for one hour at room temperature. 4x SDS-PAGE reducing loading buffer was added and proteins were separated using a NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gel. Gels were visualized using a Sapphire Biomolecular Imager (Azure Biosystems), then stained using Coomassie. Alternatively, immunoprecipitations were carried out from post-labeled cells in lysis buffer. A portion of the lysate was taken as input and the rest was precleared using Protein G Agarose (Invitrogen) followed by 2hr immunoprecipitation with anti-fLAG M2 affinity gel (A2220-Sigma). After washing, the immunoprecipitated FBXO10 and FBXO10 (C953S) was subjected to click reaction with biotin alkyne (Sigma-Aldrich, 50 μM, 25x stock in DMSO) rather than TAMRA alkyne as mentioned above. After incubation in the dark for 1hr at room temperature, 4x SDS-PAGE reducing loading buffer was added. The samples were run on Bolt 4–12% Bis-Tris Plus gel (Invitrogen) and transferred onto PVDF membrane (Millipore). The PVDF membrane was immunoblotted with anti-FLAG antibody (F7425-Sigma) and clicked-biotin was detected with Streptavidin-HRP.

Label-free-quantification mass spectrometry

Enriched mitochondria were isolated from HEK293T cells stably expressing FBXO10 and FBXO10 (C953S) and vehicle control (EV) by stepwise centrifugation. Samples were lysed in SDS based lysis buffer (50 mM Tris Ph7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% SDS, 1mM EDTA and 50mM NaF) containing protease inhibitors (Roche) and sonicated. Protein lysates were processed using S-Trap (Protifi) following manufacturers protocol. In brief, samples were reduced with 20 mM DTT for 1 hour at 57°C, alkylated with 50 mM iodoactamide for 45 minutes at room temperature in the dark and subsequently the reaction quenched with another aliquot of 20 mM DTT. The reduced and alkylated samples were acidified with 12% phosphoric acid. Samples were loaded onto the S-trap (Protifi) and washed three times with S-trap binding buffer and subsequently digested with 500 ng Trypsin at 47°C for 1h. Peptides were eluted using a solution of 40% acetonitrile followed by 80% acetonitrile in 0.5% acetic acid. The organic solvent was removed using a SpeedVac concentrator and the samples were reconstituted in 0.5% acetic acid. Peptides were loaded onto an Acclaim PepMap trap column (2 cm × 75 μm) in line with an EASY-Spray analytical column (50 cm × 75 μm ID PepMap C18, 2 μm bead size) using the autosampler of an EASY-nLC 1000 HPLC (Thermo Scientific). Solvent A was 2% acetonitrile in 0.5% acetic acid and solvent B was 80% acetonitrile in 0.5% acetic acid. The gradient was held for 5 min at 5% solvent B, ramped in 60 min to 15% solvent B, in 35 min to 25% solvent B, in 20 min to 40% solvent B, and in 10 min to 100% solvent B. The peptides were gradient eluted directly into a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Eclipse Mass Spectrometer. High resolution full spectra were acquired with a resolution of 240,000, an AGC target of 1e6, with a maximum ion time of 50 ms, and a scan range of 400 to 1500 m/z. All precursors with charge states between 2–7 were considered for fragmentation. All MS/MS HCD spectra were collected in the ion trap using the rapid scan mode, with an AGC target of 2e4, maximum inject on time of 18 ms, one microscan, 0.7 m/z isolation window, auto scan range mode, and normalized collision energy (NCE) of 27. The instrument was set to run at top speed with a cycle time of 3 seconds. Dynamic exclusion was set to 30 seconds. The MS/MS spectra were searched against the Uniprot human reference proteome using the Andromeda search engine49 integrated into the MaxQuant environment using the following settings: oxidized methionine (M), and deamidation (asparagine and glutamine) were selected as variable modifications, and carbamidomethyl (C) as fixed modifications; precursor and fragment mass tolerance was set to 10 ppm. The identifications were filtered using a false-discovery rate (FDR) of <1% at the protein and peptide level using a target-decoy approach. Only proteins with at least two unique peptides were reported. Data analysis was performed using Perseus50. The protein intensities were log 2 transformed and normalized using the median. The results were further filtered for proteins that are identified in three replicates of at least one group. Missing values were imputed from a normal distribution. To identify proteins differing in intensity between the three groups, a Student’s t-test was applied and corrected for multiple hypothesis testing using Benjamini-Hochberg51. A total of 4215 proteins were quantified across all 9 samples. Proteins with a fold change of ≥2 (log 2 FBXO10/FBXO10 (C953S) ≥1 and ≤−1) and an FDR of ≤5 % were considered ‘deregulated’ and Functional Annotation Clustering analysis was performed on deregulated proteins in FBXO10 vs FBXO10 (C953S) using DAVID software (https://david-d.ncifcrf.gov/)52. Terms with significant enrichment scores (ES ≥ 2, P ≤ 0.01) were plotted in a graph. Outer mitochondrial membrane proteins were shortlisted using Human MitoCarta3.0, DAVID software and literature survey. The mass spectrometric raw files are accessible at https://massive.ucsd.edu under accession MassIVE MSV000094240 and at www.proteomexchange.org under accession PXD050382.

Subcellular fractionation

Subcellular fractionation of cells was performed to isolate mitochondrial and cytosolic fractions53. HEK293T cells were harvested and resuspended in the fractionation buffer (225 mM mannitol, 75mM sucrose, 0.1 mM EGTA, 30 mM Tris pH 7.4) containing protease inhibitors. Samples were kept on ice for 20 min and subsequently processed by Dounce homogenization. Cell debris was separated by centrifuging the samples 2 times at 600 × g for 5 min. Crude mitochondria were isolated at 7000 × g for 10 min and washed once with the fractionation buffer. The supernatant was centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h to separate the light membranes (pellet) and the cytosol (supernatant). The required fractions were processed for downstream applications.

Protease protection assay

Cells were harvested and mitochondria were isolated as described in the STAR Methods section. Mitochondria were resuspended in the fractionation buffer without protease inhibitors and treated with trypsin (50μg/mL) for 15 min-1 h on ice. Post trypsin treatment, mitochondria were pelleted down at 7000 × g for 10 min. For western blotting mitochondria were lysed and the lysates were immunoblotted for the expression of FBXO10 and various mitochondrial proteins. For flow cytometry, mitochondria were treated with MitoView Green in the fractionation buffer prior to trypsin treatment. Post trypsin treatment, mitochondria were washed once with the fractionation buffer to remove the residual trypsin, resuspended in the fractionation buffer, and analyzed using CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and FlowJo software.

Seahorse XF Real-Time ATP Rate Assay and Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test

Seahorse XF Real-Time ATP Rate Assay and Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test were performed using the Agilent Seahorse XFe96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer according to the manufacturer’s instructions. C2C12 cells were plated in Agilent Seahorse 96-well XF Cell Culture Microplate. For Real-Time ATP Rate Assay, the sensor cartridge was hydrated in a non-CO2 37°C humidified incubator overnight. On the day of the assay, water in the utility plate was replaced with prewarmed Seahorse XF Calibrant. The sensor cartridge with utility plate was then placed in a non-CO2 37°C incubator for 45–60 minutes. Cells were washed once with XF Real Time ATP Rate Assay Medium and kept in a 37°C incubator without CO2 for one hour. The XF Real-Time ATP Rate Assay template was designed using the Wave software of the XFe96 machine. The hydrated cartridge was removed from the incubator and injection ports A and B were loaded with 10X Oligomycin (10μM) and 10X Rotenone/Antimycin A (5μM each) respectively. The cartridge and utility plate were transferred to the XFe96 machine and cartridge calibration was started using the assay template made previously. After cartridge calibration was complete, cells were washed again with XF Real-Time ATP Rate Assay Media. The utility plate in the machine was exchanged with the cell culture plate and the assay was started. After the completion of the assay, cartridge/cell plate assembly was ejected, and the Wave Results file was saved. For normalization, live cells were counted using Hoechst/PI staining. The data was analyzed using the Wave software. Mito Stress Test was performed in a similar fashion. For this test, injection ports A, B and C were loaded with 10X Oligomycin (15μM), 10X FCCP (10μM) and 10X Rotenone/Antimycin A (5μM each) respectively.

Myogenic differentiation

C2C12 myoblasts to myotubes

For differentiation, growth medium of C2C12 myoblasts was changed to differentiation medium (DM: DMEM containing 2% Horse Serum and 1× Pen-Strep). DM was changed every 1–2 days for 5–7 days. Myotubes were obtained at this stage. 5μM and 15μM concentrations of lovastatin were added to differentiation media to inhibit C2C12 differentiation to myotubes.

iPSCs to myotubes

iPSCs were differentiated to Skeletal muscle myotubes using SKM-KITM - Skeletal muscle Differentiation kit (amsbio) according to manufacturer’s guidelines. iPSCs were plated onto collagen I coated plates and grown in Skeletal Muscle Induction Medium for 6 days with a medium change after every 2 days. Myogenic precursors formed were dissociated using 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA and plated onto collagen I coated plates at 5000 cells/cm2. Cells were grown in Skeletal Myoblast Medium for 6 days with media change after every 2 days. Myoblasts were observed at this stage. Medium was switched to Myotube Medium for 8 days with a medium change every 3 days. Myotubes were obtained at this stage.

CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, gene silencing, and molecular biology

CRISPR/Cas9 deletion

Guide oligos for FBXO10 knockdown were designed using the CRISPOR program. LentiCRISPRv2 vector which also expresses Cas9 was used for cloning guide oligos. Vector was digested with BsmBI restriction enzyme and dephosphorylated using Quick CIP (NEB). Vector compatible overhangs were added to guide oligos for integration. Oligos were phosphorylated by T4 Polynucleotide Kinase (NEB) and annealed. Ligation of oligos into the vector was carried out using T4 DNA ligase (NEB) and transformation was done in NEB® Stable Competent E. coli. Screening of plasmids for integration of oligos was done by sequencing. Lentiviral transductions were carried out to stably express the guide RNA and Cas9. Puromycin was used for selecting clones with genomic integration. PCR amplification from genomic DNA encompassing the PAM site followed by sequencing was used to confirm FBXO10 knockout clones.

Gene silencing, transfections, and viral transduction

HeLa cells were transfected with ON-TARGETplus siRNAs (Dharmacon) and Santa Cruz Biotechnology siRNAs using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. siRNAs were transfected on two consecutive days. Overexpression transfections were done on the second day post second siRNA transfections using Lipofectamine 3000. Silencing was confirmed by immunoblotting using antibodies against the silenced proteins. General transfections were carried out using standard procedures. Transfections of HEK293T and HeLa cells were performed using PEI MAX® and Lipofectamine 3000 respectively. Stable overexpression and stable knockdown cell lines were made using lentiviral/retroviral transductions. For retroviral transductions, retroviral expression plasmid was mixed in optiMEM with viral packaging plasmids Gag-Pol and VSVG in the ratio of 3:1:1. For lentiviral infections, lentiviral expression plasmid was mixed in optiMEM with lentiviral packaging plasmids pΔ8.2 and VSVG in the ratio of 3:3:1. PEI MAX® (Polysciences) was used as the transfection reagent and HEK293T as the packaging cell line. The medium was changed the next day, and the viral titers were collected at 48 h and 72 h post transfection. C2C12 host cells were infected for 4 to 6 h on consecutive days. Polybrene (8 μg/ml) was used as a cell binding agent for viruses. Cells were grown for 1 day after infections and subsequently selected using appropriate selection drugs. For transduction of iPSCs, viral titer was concentrated using Lenti-X Concentrator (Takara) and polybrene was used at 1 μg/ml concentration.

Cloning and site directed mutagenesis

GFP, ptd-Tomato, FLAG and Strep tagged FBXO10 in pCDNA3.1 vector; Strep tagged FBXO10 (ΔF-box) in pCDNA3.1 vector; and FLAG tagged FBXO10 in pTRIPZ lentiviral vector were cloned using standard molecular cloning procedures. GFP, ptd-Tomato, FLAG and Strep tagged FBXO10 (C953S) mutants in pCDNA3.1 were developed using QuikChange Site-Directed Mutagenesis procedure. Flag tagged FBXO10 (C953S) mutant in pTRIPZ was made by digesting out the C-terminal region of FBXO10 in pTRIPZ and swapping with PCR amplified FBXO10 (C953S) mutant digested with same enzymes. Confirmation of mutation or deletion was done by sequencing.

Real time quantitative PCR

RNA was isolated from harvested cells using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA synthesis was conducted using RNA to cDNA EcoDry™ Premix (Oligo dT) (Takara). Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using SYBR green in a CFX Duet Real-time PCR machine (Bio-Rad). Gene expression was quantified by (ΔΔCq) method using GAPDH as the normalization control. Primers specific to human FBXO10 were used for endogenous and exogenous FBXO10 in human iPSCs. Mouse Fbxo10 specific primers were used for endogenous Fbxo10 in C2C12 cells and human FBXO10 specific primers were used for exogenous human FBXO10 in C2C12 cells.

Microscopy and fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)

Live cell and immunofluorescence microscopy

For confocal microscopy studies, HeLa and C2C12 cells were plated on glass bottom dishes. Transient transfections if needed were done using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent. For experiments requiring differentiation, C2C12 myoblasts were differentiated on glass-like polymer bottom dishes. GFP and mcherry tagged proteins, specific membrane trackers and Hoechst nuclear dye were used for live cell microscopy. For immunofluorescence studies, cells were fixed and incubated with either fluorescent dye tagged primary antibody or primary antibody followed by fluorescent dye tagged secondary antibody. Nuclei were stained using Hoechst or DAPI stains. Imaging was performed using Zeiss LSM-700 confocal microscope and images were processed using Zeiss Zen software. For non-confocal live cell microscopy studies, C2C12 cells were grown and differentiated in 6 well plates. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst stain. Imaging was performed using Leica DMi8 microscope and images were processed using Leica LAS X software.

FRAP

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching experiments were performed using a ZEISS LSM700 confocal microscope coupled with a temperature-, humidity- and CO2-controlled top-stage incubator for live-cell imaging. Images were acquired using a 63x oil immersion objective (Carl Zeiss). Three regions of interest (ROIs) were defined: ROI-1 was the indicated circular region; ROI-2 was un-photobleached region of similar size; and ROI-3 was defined as background and drawn outside of the cell, where its signal was subtracted from those of ROI-1 and ROI-2. Photobleaching was carried out with 488 nm laser at 100% power for 20 iterations. Images were acquired before and immediately after photobleaching every 1s for 90s. The laser power before and after photobleaching was kept identical. Fluorescence intensity of the ROI (F) at each time point was measured using imageJ software. FRAP curves ((F(t)) were calculated: F(t)=100×(F(t)FRAPregion-F(t)background)(Fprebleach,FRAPregion-F(t)background). The data are normalized to the background corrected prebleach intensity (Fprebleach,FRAPregion) and multiplied by 100 to yield a percentage of recovery. The normalized data was used to calculate recovery rates: (Recoveryrate=F(reachingplateaufor1sttimeafterphotobleaching)-F(bleaching))/area/duration of reaching plateau for 1st time after photobleaching). Data were processed as above and plotted using Prism.

Flow cytometry

HEK293T cells were transfected with ptd-Tomato FBXO10 and treated with various inhibitors as required. Mitochondria were isolated as described in the STAR Methods section and treated with Mitoview Green in the fractionation buffer for gating the mitochondria. FBXO10 peak and shifts due to various treatments were analyzed using CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (BECKMAN COULTER) and FlowJo software.

Cycloheximide chase, immunoprecipitation, and immunoblotting

Transfected HEK293T cells were harvested and lysed in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% TritonX100, 50 mM NaF, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaF) containing protease inhibitors. The samples were sonicated (pulse on: 10 s, pulse off: 10 s, and Amplitude: 30%) in Sonic Dismembrator with Cup Horn (Fisher Scientific). Lysates were pre-cleared using Protein G Agarose (Invitrogen) by rocking for 1–2 h at 4°C (16 rpm). Immunoprecipitions were done using anti-FLAG M2 (Sigma) or Strep-Tactin Sepharose 1h-overnight and the samples were eluted using 3X-FLAG peptide or Strep-Tactin elution buffer respectively. Samples were denatured in Bolt LDS sample buffer at 70°C for 10 min and processed for immunoblotting using standard protocols. For cycloheximide chase, HEK293T cells were transfected with EV and FBXO10 expressing constructs. Approximately 2 days post transfection, cycloheximide was added at a concentration of 100 μg/ml for different time periods. Cells were lysed and subjected to immunoblotting. C2C12 cells were differentiated for 2 days for myogenic program prior to cycloheximide treatment.
