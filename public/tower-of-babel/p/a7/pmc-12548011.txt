Complete mesocolon excision versus conventional colectomy for resectable colon cancer
Authors: Atalel F Awedew, Zelalem Asefa, Mahteme Bekele Muleta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To evaluate the benefits and harms of complete mesocolon excision (CME) compared to conventional colectomy in people undergoing surgery for resectable colon cancer, focusing on survival, recurrence, postoperative complications, and quality of life.

Background

Description of the condition

Colorectal cancer is a substantial contributor to cancer‐related mortality, morbidity, economic burden, and disability on a global scale [1, 2, 3]. It is the second leading cause of disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) attributed to cancer and the third leading cause of cancer‐related deaths [2]. The Global Burden of Disease study estimated that there were 2.17 million new cases, 24.3 million DALYs, and 1.09 million deaths in 2019 [1]. A substantial increase in the global burden of colorectal cancer by 2040 can be projected from data from GLOBOCAN [4], with an estimated 3.2 million new cases per year (a 63% increase) and 1.6 million deaths per year (a 73% increase), mainly due to increasing population size and aging. While incidence rates are three to four times higher in high‐income countries than in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), mortality rates show less variation due to a relatively higher case‐fatality rate in LMICs [5]. Projections indicate that over 80% of new cases in 2040 will occur in countries with high or very high levels of the Human Development Index, reflecting their advanced diagnostic and screening capabilities [4]. Of particular concern is the increasing global prevalence of early‐onset colorectal cancer (age ≤ 50 years) [6]. These statistics underscore the need for continued research, prevention strategies, and advancements in treatment modalities to address this significant public health concern [1].

While advances in radiotherapy and chemotherapy have significantly increased survival rates for colon cancer patients, surgical resection is fundamental to colon cancer management and remains the gold standard [7]. Progress in colon cancer survival can be attributed to the development of new surgical techniques, adjuvant chemotherapy, and neoadjuvant chemotherapy over the past 30 years [7]. Despite the accumulation of high‐quality evidence regarding optimal colonic resections, the most effective surgical intervention has remained a topic of debate [8].

Description of the intervention and how it might work

Conventional colon cancer surgery typically involves dissection along the line of Toldt and ligation of named colic arteries, and complete resection of the tumor‐bearing segment of the colon and the associated lymphatic drainage network (D2), often following designated vascular pathways [8]. This procedure primarily focuses on removing the cancerous tissue and associated lymph nodes. However, conventional colectomy often overlooks the potential benefits of central vein ligation, a technique that involves ligating the central vessels supplying the tumor.

In 2009, building upon the principles of total mesorectal excision (TME), a technique used for rectal cancer that involves precise dissection within the avascular mesorectal fascial plane [9], Hohenberger and his team introduced a comparable surgical technique for colon cancer [8]. This novel approach, known as complete mesocolon excision (CME), involves sharp dissection of the mesocolon within the avascular embryological plane to excise the entire mesocolonic fascia, including all lymph nodes, tumor blood supply vessels, central vascular ligation, and D3 lymph node clearance [8, 10, 11]. CME for tumors of the right and transverse colon involves extensive mobilization of the right colon, the duodenum, pancreatic head, and small bowel mesentery to the origin of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA), whereas CME for tumors of the left side is performed through mobilization of the descending, sigmoid, and splenic flexure colon, and the free greater omentum is dissected with division of the phrenic ligament, along with a resection that is about 10 cm proximal and 10 cm distal [10, 11]. Central vascular ligation is performed for complete mesocolon excision extending to the mesenteric vascular root along the ligation within 1 cm from the origin [10, 11].

Previous research has shown that lymph node metastasis along the colon more than 10 cm from the tumor is rare, at only 0.2%; therefore, the 10 cm rule is frequently recommended to determine the proximal and distal margin points for colon resection [12]. Early research has demonstrated that adhering to the 10 cm rule has been beneficial in improving surgical outcomes [8]. While some concerns have been raised regarding the potential for increased morbidity and vessel injury associated with this approach, its positive impact on survival, recurrence rates, and the number of lymph nodes harvested has been well‐documented. However, there is a scarcity of high‐quality evidence directly comparing CME to conventional colectomy.

Why it is important to do this review

An early observational study found that the implementation of CME resulted in a notable reduction in five‐year recurrence rates, from 6.5% to 3.6%, and a corresponding increase in five‐year survival rates, from 82.1% to 89.1% [8]. However, the evidence stemming from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) concerning the effectiveness of CME in the management of colon cancer, particularly in terms of reducing postoperative morbidity and improving survival rates, has yielded inconsistent findings [13, 14]. In addition, there is a lack of evidence about whether CME yields differential effectiveness based on the location of colon cancer – specifically, whether outcomes differ for right‐sided versus left‐sided tumors. This illustrates the need for further investigation into the potential impact of tumor location on the benefits derived from CME.

To address gaps in knowledge, we will conduct a systematic review to evaluate the efficacy of CME in the management of colon cancer, examining its potential benefits and outcomes in comparison to traditional surgical approaches. This systematic review aims to evaluate whether CME offers superior oncologic outcomes compared to conventional colectomy in terms of survival, recurrence, postoperative complications, and quality of life.

Objectives

To evaluate the benefits and harms of complete mesocolon excision (CME) compared to conventional colectomy in people undergoing surgery for resectable colon cancer, focusing on survival, recurrence, postoperative complications, and quality of life.

Methods

We will conduct this systematic review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) statement [15] and recommendations in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Intervention [16].

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include RCTs and quasi‐randomized controlled trials (quasi‐RCTs) that compare complete mesocolon excision (CME) with conventional colectomy for colon cancer [17, 18], including unpublished studies for which the data collection phase is complete. For the purpose of this review, quasi‐RCTs are defined as studies where participants are allocated to intervention or comparator groups (CME versus conventional colectomy) using a method that, strictly speaking, is not random, e.g. by alternation or date of birth. While quasi‐RCTs aim to create comparable groups, they are susceptible to selection bias as the allocation sequence may be predictable. Crucially, our identification of a study as quasi‐randomized will be based on a thorough examination of its methods section to ascertain the allocation sequence generation, rather than solely on the authors' explicit labeling of their study.

We will consider identified studies for inclusion regardless of their publication date, location, or language. We may consider abstracts if they provide sufficient data, including information on study design, sample size, and intervention details.

We will not include cluster‐RCTs or cross‐over studies.

Types of participants

We will include all adults (16 years of age and above) diagnosed with histologically confirmed resectable colon cancer, without restrictions based on sex, race, or geographic location. We will include studies that involve children if they make up less than 10% of the sample. There will be no exclusion criteria relating to participants' existing health conditions or site of the surgery. However, we will exclude people with unresectable or recurrent cancer from this systematic review to maintain the focus on resectable colon cancer.

Types of interventions

We will include studies that compare CME with conventional colectomy for resectable colon cancer.

Complete mesocolic excision represents a complex surgical procedure that entails the removal of a sufficient segment of the longitudinal bowel (10 cm), ligation of central veins, precise dissection along the avascular embryological plane of the mesocolon, and a comprehensive D3 dissection to ensure adequate retrieval of lymph nodes. To be accepted as CME for inclusion in the review, at a minimum, the intervention must include central vascular ligation (arterial and/or venous), preservation of mesocolon integrity, and dissection along the superior mesenteric vein (SMV).

In contrast, conventional colon cancer surgery typically involves a dissection along the line of Toldt and involves ligation of named colic arteries, complete resection of the tumor‐bearing segment of the colon, along with the associated lymphatic drainage network (D2), often following designated vascular pathways.

Search methods for identification of studies

We will conduct searches according to PRISMA 2020 guidelines and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions recommendations [16, 24, 25] (Supplementary material 1).

Electronic searches

As the first complete mesocolon excisions were performed in the early 2000s in Germany, and the first paper on CME was published in 2009 [8], we will search the following electronic databases from 2000 onwards. We will conduct a comprehensive literature search for MEDLINE Ovid, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Embase Ovid, and Web of Science. We will use the RCT filter for our searches. We have provided the search strategy for MEDLINE in Supplementary material 1. We will adapt this search strategy for other databases.

Searching other resources

We will search the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (www.who.int/tools/clinical‐trials‐registry‐platform) and the US National Institutes of Health Ongoing Trials Register (clinicaltrials.gov) for completed and ongoing studies. We will manually search and review the reference lists of all included articles, as well as relevant systematic reviews identified within those articles, to ensure we have conducted a comprehensive search for relevant studies. Furthermore, we will conduct a forward citation search of the included articles using Google Scholar. We also intend to liaise with relevant organizations, such as regional colorectal surgery societies, to ascertain whether they are aware of any ongoing or completed research that could augment our database searches. We will also search for meeting abstracts via the Conference Proceedings Citation Index.

To maintain the integrity and reliability of the evidence, we will proactively monitor for post‐publication amendments to all included and eligible studies. This will involve systematically checking for errata, corrigenda, expressions of concern, and retractions. We will routinely monitor databases and, where available, use publisher platforms and resources such as Retraction Watch to identify any such amendments. We will review the amendments to determine their impact on the study's data, methods, or conclusions. We will incorporate this information into our data extraction and risk of bias assessment, as appropriate. In the event that a study is found to be fully retracted, we will exclude it from the review unless we deem the retraction to be erroneous and the data to remain valid.

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

Two review authors will independently screen titles and abstracts using Endnote to identify studies for full‐text screening [26]. Any disagreements regarding study eligibility will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consulting a third review author. Following the initial screening, two review authors will independently conduct a full‐text review of the selected articles. Multiple reports or publications related to a single study will be grouped under a single reference ID. The selection process will be documented in detail to facilitate the generation of a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram [15], and we will outline the reasons for excluding studies in a 'Characteristics of excluded studies' table.

Data extraction and management

Two review authors will independently extract trial characteristics and outcome data using a standardized and piloted data extraction form from relevant studies. Every outcome will be separately extracted by two review authors, and any disagreements arising during data extraction will be resolved through discussion and consensus amongst the authors or in consultation with a third review author. Two review authors will use the recommended response options to arrive at a risk of bias judgment for each outcome as either low risk of bias, some concerns about bias, or high risk of bias. We will determine the overall risk of bias based on the assessment of the five individual domains. We will classify a trial as having a high risk of bias if it is judged to be at high risk of bias in at least one domain for the result being judged, or if there are some concerns across multiple domains to such an extent that confidence in the result is substantially lowered. We will make the classification 'some concerns' when the trial is judged to raise some concerns in at least one domain for the result, but is not judged to be at high risk of bias in any domain. Finally, we will classify a trial as low risk for a result if it is judged to be at low risk of bias for all domains.

Measures of treatment effect

We will analyze dichotomous data using relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). However, for the outcome of mortality, we will calculate the risk difference (RD), as we anticipate that many included studies will have no events in either study arm. We will analyze continuous data as mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs. If data are reported in units other than those required for our analysis (e.g. hours instead of days), we will convert them accordingly. Where data cannot be converted into a suitable format for our meta‐analysis, we will present results narratively. When results are reported as medians and interquartile ranges, we will calculate the mean and standard deviations (SDs) using the method described by Hozo and colleagues, utilizing the available median, interquartile range, and confidence interval or range [29]. We will also extract and report hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs for time‐to‐event data. We will extract these time‐to‐event data from regression models for survival data, such as the Cox proportional hazards model, or reconstructed from published Kaplan‐Meier curves [30]. We will analyze these data using generic inverse‐variance methods [31]. We will report continuous data as MDs with 95% CIs if different studies use the same scale to measure an outcome, or standardized mean difference (SMDs) with 95% CIs if they use different scales. We will enter data presented on a scale with a consistent direction of effect across studies. We will interpret the magnitude of the effect using established guidelines for interpreting SMDs, also known as Cohen's d. Specifically, an SMD of less than 0.2 (corresponding to an odds ratio (OR) less than 1.5) will be considered a small effect or weak association; an SMD between 0.2 and 0.79 will be considered a moderate effect; and an SMD greater than 0.8 (corresponding to an OR greater than 5) will be considered a large effect or strong association [32].

Unit of analysis issues

In cases where multiple interventions exist within the included study, we will combine all relevant experimental intervention groups into a single group. Similarly, we will combine all relevant comparator intervention groups into a single comparator group, creating a single pairwise comparison [27]. If it is not possible to create a single pairwise comparison, we will select one specific pair of interventions for comparison and exclude the others. Additionally, if there are sufficient studies involving multiple interventions, we will consider conducting a network meta‐analysis. In instances where a study reports data on an intervention that is not included in our analysis, we will exclude data from that group but will document it in the 'Characteristics of included studies' table.

Dealing with missing data

Our approach involves reaching out to the investigators of the included studies to gather any unreported data, including missing outcomes, means, SDs, and information on any differential dropout observed between the study groups, along with the reasons for participant withdrawal. When results are reported as medians and interquartile ranges, we will calculate the mean and SDs using the method described by Hozo and colleagues, utilizing the available median, interquartile range, and CI or range [29]. We will conduct a sensitivity analysis by excluding studies with more than 20% missing data. We will also conduct best‐case and worst‐case scenario sensitivity analyses for dichotomous outcomes, which are considered extreme case analyses to assess the impact of missing data on the overall effect estimate. When means and standard deviations are not reported in the included studies, we will calculate these statistics, where possible, using methods outlined in Chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbookfor Systematic Reviews of Interventions. If we cannot calculate the SD using the above method, we will impute the SD from other studies in the same meta‐analysis [33]. If data are presented graphically rather than numerically, we will extract data from figures, wherever feasible. We will use an imputation method that will decrease the weight of the studies in the meta‐analysis of MD and shift the effect towards the null for SMD. When a specific outcome exhibits a substantial loss to follow‐up (50% or more), we will make this clear in the text and denote the affected data points with an asterisk. We will check and critically appraise the handling of missing data and the imputation methods employed in this systematic review using the RoB 2 tool.

Reporting bias assessment

We will make a diligent effort to investigate and address potential reporting biases by following the guidelines and recommendations outlined in the Cochrane Handbookfor Systematic Reviews of Interventions [34]. If more than 10 studies meet our inclusion criteria, we will conduct a visual assessment of reporting bias using a funnel plot. This visual representation will illustrate the distribution of studies included in the meta‐analysis relative to the magnitude of effect or precision. Moreover, we will use statistical tests such as the Egger test and Begg test to quantify and detect reporting biases within the included studies [27, 35, 36, 37].

Synthesis methods

We will follow the principles and recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions for the data synthesis in this systematic review [31, 38, 39]. We will commence the analysis with a descriptive summary of the included studies, setting the stage for a thorough narrative and quantitative synthesis. We will pool dichotomous outcomes in meta‐analysis if clinical, methodological, and statistical heterogeneity is sufficiently low. To conduct the data synthesis, we plan to use Review Manager (RevMan) [40]. We will use the random‐effects model since we expect there to be clinical diversity. We will use the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator to estimate between‐trial variance. We will use the Hartung‐Knapp‐Sidik‐Jonkman method to calculate a confidence interval for the meta‐analysis effect estimate when there are at least three studies and the estimate of heterogeneity is greater than zero [31]. In other scenarios (i.e. the pooled analyses of two studies, or where the estimate of heterogeneity is equal to zero), we will use the Wald‐type method [31]. If a meta‐analysis proves to be unsuitable, we plan to synthesize the findings from across the studies using non‐statistical techniques. This synthesis will encompass a visual display of the results alongside a summary of effect estimates, including the range and distribution of the observed effects. We will present all results with 95% CIs.

Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

For clinical heterogeneity, we will consider differences in study settings, participant characteristics, interventions, comparators, and outcomes. We will consider methodological heterogeneity in study design and risk of bias using the Cochrane risk of bias tool (RoB 2) [36]. We will also assess statistical heterogeneity using Cochran's Q test, also known as the standard Chi2 test, and the I2 statistic [27, 31, 35, 36, 36, 37]. A rule of thumb for heterogeneity based on the I2 statistic is that 0% to 40% might not be important, 30% to 60% may represent moderate heterogeneity, 50% to 90% may represent substantial heterogeneity, and 75% to 100% is considerable heterogeneity [31]. We will employ subgroup analysis, meta‐regression, and sensitivity analysis to address significant heterogeneity in our systematic review [31]. If we identify a sufficient number of studies (at least three) to support subgroup analyses, we will conduct these analyses according to the guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [27]. Equity‐related assessment

We will conduct subgroup analyses to examine whether effects differ by several key characteristics. The variables that we will explore include the country in which the study was conducted, participant age group (below 50 years of age and 50 years of age and above), sex, and the Socio‐Demographic Index (SDI [41]; a composite measure of income, education, and fertility) class of the country. By using this approach, we will identify any disparities, which could inform policies aimed at equitable access [42].

Sensitivity analysis
