Acid–Base Status During Venoarterial Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenator Support in Patients With Cardiogenic Shock
Authors: Garima Dahiya, J. Kyle Bohman, Joseph E. Tonna, Dustin B. Hillerson, Katrina J. Augustin, Suraj M. Yalamuri, Kianoush B Kashani, Jacob C. Jentzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prognostic significance of an adverse acid–base milieu despite venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support in patients with cardiogenic shock remains poorly defined. We examined metabolic and respiratory disturbances in the ELSO (Extracorporeal Life Support Organization) Registry among patients who received venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support for refractory cardiogenic shock.MethodsArterial blood gas acid–base values at 24 hours following venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation initiation were analyzed, with patients classified by pH, arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide, and bicarbonate. Multivariable logistic regression and exploratory machine learning analyses were used to assess predictors of in‐hospital mortality.ResultsAmong 15 144 adult patients, 74.6% (10 860) had neither respiratory nor metabolic acidosis, 13.1% (1909) had isolated respiratory acidosis, 11.6% (1684) had isolated metabolic acidosis, and 0.7% (100) had combined acidosis. In‐hospital mortality occurred in 7719 (51.0%) patients and increased incrementally across patients with lower pH (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 2.03 with each 0.1‐unit decrease in pH, using 7.4 as a reference point [95% CI, 1.82–2.26]), bicarbonate (adjusted OR, 1.93 per each 5‐mEq/L <24 mEq/L [95% CI, 1.76–2.12]) or base excess (adjusted OR, 1.90 per each 5‐mEq/L <0 mEq/L [95% CI, 1.75–2.07]) (all P<0.01). Mortality varied significantly, being highest for the low‐pH metabolic acidosis cluster (adjusted OR, 1.90 [95% CI, 1.70–2.12], P<0.01).ConclusionsSurvival decreased progressively with worse acidemia or metabolic acidosis despite 24 hours of hemodynamic support with venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation among patients with cardiogenic shock. Our findings emphasize the prognostic significance of metabolic parameters as potentially modifiable risk factors and underscore the need for early recognition and interventions to mitigate severe metabolic derangements.

Cardiogenic shock (CS) is a state of low cardiac output resulting in impaired systemic oxygen delivery and critical end‐organ hypoperfusion. 1 , 2 Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA ECMO) serves as rescue therapy in refractory CS by providing mechanical cardiovascular and pulmonary support. 3 , 4 However, mortality among patients supported by VA ECMO remains high despite restoration of blood flow due to downstream hemometabolic consequences of CS. 4 , 5 Acid–base disturbances in CS are emblematic of the deleterious cascade linking end‐organ hypoperfusion and multiorgan failure, reflecting the maladaptive balance between pathologic disturbances to cellular homeostasis and buffer mechanisms. 5 , 6 The persistence of an adverse acid–base milieu despite VA ECMO support in patients with refractory CS may result from the initial disease process or treatment resistance with evolving injury. Prior studies have demonstrated a significant association between acidemia (low blood pH) and metabolic and lactic acidosis with short‐term mortality in patients with CS. 7 , 8 , 9 However, this relationship remains insufficiently characterized among patients with refractory CS who receive hemodynamic support with VA ECMO.

Patients with CS often have concomitant respiratory failure due to pulmonary hypoperfusion and parenchymal lung disease, for example, pulmonary edema. 10 , 11 This can limit respiratory compensation for metabolic acid–base disturbances, particularly among patients reliant on mechanical respiratory support. The interplay between metabolic and respiratory acid–base disturbances in VA‐ECMO‐supported patients remains largely unexplored. A comprehensive understanding of these derangements would refine risk stratification, inform clinical decision‐making, optimize resuscitation strategies, and potentially guide tailored interventions among VA ECMO patients to improve survival. Accordingly, we sought to evaluate a large contemporary cohort of patients with CS supported with VA ECMO to describe the association between acid–base abnormalities at 24 hours following cannulation and in‐hospital mortality. We hypothesized that persistent metabolic acidosis despite VA ECMO support would be associated with higher mortality in patients with CS.

METHODS

Study Population

The ELSO (Extracorporeal Life Support Organization) Registry is a voluntary international multicenter database of patients treated with ECMO starting in 1989. More than 100 000 patients from >400 centers make it the largest collection of data on ECMO patients worldwide. 12 Member centers use the ELSO Registry for quality improvement and research, and data accuracy is maintained by real‐time validation. We electronically queried the ELSO Registry for deidentified data on adult patients ≥18 years of age who received VA ECMO as the initial modality for cardiac support from 2017 to 2022 and had a diagnosis of CS (International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision [ICD‐9]785.51 or International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD‐10] R57.0). We excluded patients who did not have data for any of the laboratory values of interest at 24 hours, which were pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), or bicarbonate (ie, all of these were missing). Data used for this analysis were deidentified before release and are exempt from institutional board review; accordingly, informed consent was not required.

The authors declare most supporting data are available within the article and its Supplementary Material. Additional data not included herein are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Data Sources

We extracted physiologic variables (vital signs and laboratory values), demographics, details of the ECMO run, pre‐ECMO hemodynamic support, and in‐hospital outcomes from the ELSO Registry. The registry contains 2 time point values of selected physiologic variables before ECMO initiation and at 24 hours of ECMO support; laboratory values include pH, PaCO2, bicarbonate, partial pressure of oxygen, and arterial oxygen saturation percentage. Discharge ICD‐9/10 codes were used to identify the presence of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), heart failure, and cardiomyopathy. Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions Shock Stage was calculated using data on vasopressors and temporary mechanical circulatory support (MCS) devices as described by Jentzer et al. 4

Exposures and Outcomes of Interest

The primary exposures of interest were arterial blood gas acid–base values at 24 hours after initiation of ECMO support. The primary outcome of interest was all‐cause in‐hospital mortality, with patients who continued to be supported with ECMO at 24 hours considered alive; a secondary outcome of all‐cause 30‐day in‐hospital mortality was examined. Based on pH, patients were classified as having primary alkalemia (pH >7.45), primary acidemia (pH <7.35), and normal (pH 7.35–7.45). Based on PaCO2, patients were further classified as having respiratory alkalosis (PaCO2 < 35 mm Hg), respiratory acidosis (PaCO2 > 45 mm Hg), and normocapnia (PaCO2 35–45 mm Hg). Based on the bicarbonate, patients were classified as having metabolic alkalosis (bicarbonate >28 mEq/L), metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate <20 mEq/L), and normal (bicarbonate 20–28 mEq/L). Arterial base excess was calculated as 0.93 * (bicarbonate – 24.4 + 14.8 * [pH – 7.4]) in mEq/L, with values <0 mEq/L representing an arterial base deficit. Lactic acidosis was defined as a lactate level ≥3 mmol/L, threshold cutoff identified in accordance with the ECLS SHOCK (Extracorporeal Life Support in Infarct‐Related Cardiogenic Shock) trial. 13

Statistical Analysis: Conventional Methods

Continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range [IQR]), and groups were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis rank‐sum test. Categorical variables were summarized as numbers (percentage, %), and groups were compared using Pearson chi‐square tests. Predictors of in‐hospital mortality were evaluated using logistic regression to generate odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI values, before and after multivariable adjustment. Multivariable regression models were adjusted for the following variables, selected a priori based on clinical relevance, limited missingness (<10%), and known associations with mortality in prior studies: age, weight, height, year, diagnosis of AMI/cardiomyopathy, transplant, renal‐replacement therapy, pre‐ECMO cardiac arrest, primary diagnosis of CS, number of vasopressors/inotropes/MCS, and partial pressure of oxygen, systolic blood pressure, and ECMO pump flow at 24 hours. All these selected covariates were significantly associated with in‐hospital mortality (P<0.01) on univariable analysis except for female sex and race, which were added despite P>0.01 on univariable logistic regression due to their sociologic and clinical relevance. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to estimate in‐hospital survival to 30 days, censored at hospital discharge, with groups compared using the log‐rank test.

Statistical Analysis: Exploratory Machine Learning

To identify the most important predictors of in‐hospital mortality, classification and regression tree (CART, decision tree) analysis was performed using 24‐hour values of pH, PaCO2, arterial base excess, and bicarbonate with the goal of creating between 3 and 5 groups; a complexity parameter of 0.01 (which was the optimal value) yielded 3 groups, whereas a complexity parameter of 0.001 yielded 9 groups. CART analysis was performed using a 65/35 training/validation cohort stratified by in‐hospital mortality, with model building in the training cohort and mortality evaluation in the validation cohort. Random forest models were built using 24‐hour acid–base values and covariates from the logistic regression models, with 1000 trees and mtry = 5 (the square root of the number of feature variables, rounded to the nearest integer). Variable importance from the final random forest model was evaluated using mean decrease in the Gini coefficient (also known as mean decrease in impurity) and mean decrease in accuracy values, with higher values reflecting greater importance. To identify potential occult subphenotypes, we performed consensus k‐means clustering based on 24‐hour acid–base values using 2000 replications and 10 starting seeds with between 2 and 8 groups. Before clustering, we trimmed extreme values to the 1st and 99th percentiles, then ln‐transformed, centered and scaled the resulting values to get a log Z‐score that was used for clustering. The final number of clusters (n=3) was selected based on elbow plots of the within‐cluster sum of squares, noting an apparent visual inflection point at 3 clusters. All analyses were performed using BlueSky 10.3.1 Pro (BlueSky LLC, Chicago, IL).

RESULTS

Study Population

A total of 17 440 patients within the ELSO Registry had a diagnosis of CS and received VA ECMO for cardiac support from 2017 to 2022. We excluded 2296 (13.2%) patients who had missing data for all 3 laboratory values of interest; 598 patients had data for 1 or 2 of these laboratory values (pH, 29 [0.2%] missing; PaCO2 389 [2.6%] missing; bicarbonate, 208 [1.4%] missing) and were included. The final study population included 15 144 patients with a median age of 58.2 (46.5–66.2) years; 4827 (31.9%) were female, and 8745 (57.7%) were White (Table 1). The primary discharge diagnosis was CS in 9953 (65.7%). Diagnoses of AMI (25.0%), heart failure (33.1%), and cardiomyopathy (10.6%) were common; 19.0% underwent a documented cardiothoracic operation. The median hospital length of stay was 20 (9–37) days, including 134 (77–218) hours on ECMO support. Before ECMO initiation, 34.6% had a cardiac arrest, 60.7% received a form of MCS, 80.3% received vasopressors, and 42.9% received inotropes. A total of 11.6% of patients received renal‐replacement therapy, and 13.5% received angiography/percutaneous coronary intervention during hospitalization (Table 1).

Acid–Base Status at 24 Hours of ECMO Support

At 24 hours of ECMO support, median laboratory values included pH (n=15 115) 7.42 (7.38–7.47); PaCO2 (n=14 755) 38 (33.6–42) mm Hg; bicarbonate (14 936) 24.4 (22–27.1) mEq/L; base excess (n=14 915) 0.4 (−2.4–3.4) mEq/L; and lactate (n = 11 034) 2.1 (1.3–3.8) mmol/L. The distribution of groups based on pH, PaCO2, and bicarbonate is shown in Figure 1. Among patients with available data at both time points, 74.2% of those who had acidemia at 24 hours had acidemia before ECMO, and 71.7% of those who had metabolic acidosis at 24 hours had metabolic acidosis before ECMO. At 24 hours of ECMO support, nearly three quarters of patients were free from acidosis; baseline characteristics differed across these groups, with higher severity of illness in the metabolic acidosis and combined acidosis groups (Table 2). Lactate levels varied markedly across these groups: no acidosis, 2.0 (IQR, 1.3–3.3) mmol/L; respiratory acidosis, 2.0 (IQR, 1.3–3.3) mmol/L; metabolic acidosis, 5.6 (IQR, 2.3–11.6) mmol/L; combined acidosis, 9.4 (IQR, 4.8–12.3) mmol/L. Lactic acidosis > = 3.0 mmol/L was present in 29.4%, 28.7%, 68.1%, and 87.0% of these groups, respectively (34.4% overall).

In‐Hospital Death Versus Hospital Survivors

A total of 7719 (51.0%) patients died during hospitalization, after a median hospital length of stay of 12 (IQR, 6–22) days and 141 (IQR, 74–241) hours of ECMO support; baseline characteristics differed between hospital survivors and in‐hospital deaths, including older age, more hemodynamic support before ECMO and greater illness severity (Table 1). At 24 hours of ECMO support, metabolic acidosis was modestly worse between in‐hospital deaths and hospital survivors (Table 1): pH, 7.41 versus. 7.43 (P<0.01); bicarbonate, 24. 25 mEq/L (P < 0.01); base excess, −0.1 versus 1.0 mEq/L (P<0.01); lactate, 2.7 versus 1.7 mmol/L (P<0.01); however, PaCO2 did not differ: 38 versus 38 mm Hg (P=0.19). Acidemia, metabolic acidosis, and combined acidosis were all more common among in‐hospital deaths at 24 hours of ECMO support, as was lactic acidosis >=3 mmol/L.

In‐Hospital Mortality and 24‐Hour Acid–Base Values

In‐hospital mortality increased progressively with lower pH (with a threshold effect <7.4), bicarbonate (with a threshold effect <24 mEq/L), or base excess (with a threshold effect <0 mEq/L) after 24 hours of ECMO support; a modest U‐shaped association was seen for PaCO2 (Figure 2). Strong inverse associations were observed between 24‐hour values of pH, bicarbonate, and base excess with in‐hospital mortality, both before and after multivariable adjustment, whereas PaCO2 was only weakly associated (Table 3). Patients with lactic acidosis had higher mortality across the range of bicarbonate or base excess (Figure S1). After 24 hours of ECMO support, higher in‐hospital mortality was associated with each 0.1‐unit pH <7.4 (adjusted OR, 2.03[ 95% CI, 1.82–2.26]) and each 5‐mEq/L bicarbonate <24 mEq/L (adjusted OR, 1.93 [95% CI, 1.76–2.12]) or 5‐mEq/L base excess <0 mEq/L (adjusted OR, 1.90 [95% CI, 1.75–2.07]) (all P<0.01).

Higher in‐hospital mortality was observed for patients with acidemia or metabolic acidosis at 24 hours of ECMO support; patients with alkalemia or metabolic alkalosis were at lower risk even compared with patients without any acid base disturbance (Figure 3). No significant difference was observed for respiratory alkalosis or respiratory acidosis versus normal PaCO2 (Figure 3). Before and after adjustment, patients with acidemia or metabolic acidosis at 24 hours were at higher risk of mortality; patients with alkalemia or metabolic alkalosis at 24 hours were at lower risk. Risk did not differ substantially for patients with respiratory acidosis or alkalosis. A gradient of mortality was observed according to the combination of PaCO2 and bicarbonate group at 24 hours. A strong stepwise increase in mortality was observed for patients with isolated respiratory acidosis, isolated metabolic acidosis, and combined acidosis at 24 hours (Figure 1); these findings were consistent across subgroups of interest stratified by age, sex, race, AMI, heart failure, cardiac arrest, postcardiotomy, and pre‐ECMO Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions Shock Stage (Figure S2). In each of these groups, patients with lactic acidosis ≥3 mmol/L at 24 hours were at higher risk; a gradient of risk was observed when considering the presence of metabolic acidosis and lactic acidosis separately (Figure 4). When all acid–base parameters at 24 hours were included in the same model as continuous variables, they all remained associated with in‐hospital mortality; when categorized, only acidemia and metabolic acidosis/alkalosis remained associated.

Kaplan–Meier Survival Analysis

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated lower 30‐day survival for patients with metabolic or combined acidosis, with no difference between the respiratory acidosis and no‐acidosis groups (Figure 5). Median survival was 13 (11–15) days for patients with metabolic acidosis (30‐day survival 35.0%) and 6 (4–10) days for combined acidosis (30‐day survival 20.4%).

Exploratory Machine Learning Analyses

The CART model using 24‐hour acid–base values identified optimal cutoffs for pH (7.375), bicarbonate (21.75 mEq/L), and base excess (−2.7 mEq/L) that were slightly lower than the commonly recognized normal values. It produced 3 groups with differing mortality (Figure S3); base excess <−2.7 mEq/L was most important, followed by pH <7.4. The random forest model demonstrated that 24‐hour values of base excess (#2), bicarbonate (#4), and pH (#6) were among the most important variables for predicting mortality based on mean decrease in accuracy; using mean decrease in Gini coefficient, base excess (#4) remained the most important acid–base variable.

Consensus k‐means clustering selected three clusters based on the elbow plots. There were clusters with low, normal, and high pH. The low pH (metabolic acidosis) group had average PaCO2 and low bicarbonate, reflecting a high prevalence of metabolic acidosis but a low prevalence of respiratory acidosis. The normal pH (compensated) group had high PaCO2 and high bicarbonate, with predominant respiratory acidosis and metabolic alkalosis. The high pH (respiratory alkalosis) group had low PaCO2 and average bicarbonate, reflecting predominantly respiratory alkalosis with a modest and balanced prevalence of metabolic disturbances. In‐hospital mortality varied by cluster (Figure S4), being higher for the low‐pH (metabolic acidosis) cluster; mortality was higher in each cluster for patients with lactic acidosis ≥3 mmol/L. After adjustment, the low pH (metabolic acidosis) group at 24 hours was at higher risk (adjusted OR, 1.90 [95% CI, 1.71–2.12], P<0.01).

DISCUSSION

Summary of Main Findings

In this analysis of >15 000 adults treated with VA ECMO for refractory CS of mixed causes from the ELSO registry, we identified a strong association between the severity of acidemia and metabolic acidosis at 24 hours with in‐hospital mortality. Survival worsened progressively with lower pH, bicarbonate and base excess after 24 hours of ECMO support, emphasizing the primacy of persistent metabolic acidosis as a potent predictor of mortality in CS. This association persisted after multivariable adjustment and was consistent when stratified by relevant baseline variables, including AMI‐CS, heart failure‐CS, cardiac arrest, number of vasopressors/inotropes, VA ECMO flow, and use of renal‐replacement therapy. No significant difference in mortality was observed among patients with isolated respiratory acidosis or alkalosis. Survival was worst among the minority of patients with combined respiratory and metabolic acidosis. Exploratory machine learning (ML) analyses using CART, random forest, and k‐means clustering further corroborated these findings, emphasizing that acidemia and metabolic acidosis are the strongest adverse predictors. This large‐scale analysis underscores the hazards associated with persistent metabolic derangements despite VA ECMO support in patients with CS.

Acidemia and Hemometabolic Shock

Hemometabolic shock is a phenotype of CS characterized by profound metabolic derangements caused by systemic hypoperfusion and impaired oxygen delivery resulting in multiorgan failure. 5 , 6 , 14 Outcomes in patients with hemometabolic CS are poor even with temporary MCS, and multiorgan failure often results in death despite hemodynamic stabilization. 4 , 15 , 16 , 17 Metabolic acidosis is typical of hemometabolic CS and can be caused by lactic acidosis from tissue hypoxia as well as nonlactic acidosis resulting from renal and other end‐organ failure, which in turn can impair clearance of lactic acidosis. 7 , 8 , 18 The severity of acid–base imbalance and lactate accumulation reflects the magnitude of end‐organ hypoperfusion and has been incorporated into risk‐stratification scores for CS; a bidirectional relationship has been proposed whereby greater shock severity contributes to worse acidosis, which aggravates circulatory failure, resulting in a vicious cycle. 8 , 19 , 20 , 21 However, the importance of persistent acidemia following VA ECMO support remains largely unknown. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association of persistent acidemia, metabolic acidosis, and base excess at 24 hours following VA ECMO support with in‐hospital mortality among adults with refractory CS. Survival rates in our cohort declined progressively with each 0.1‐unit decrease in pH, using 7.4 as a reference point, consistent with the inverse linear association observed across the entire pH range, with bicarbonate <24 mEq/L, and a base excess <0 mEq/L at 24 hours of VA ECMO support highlighting the hazard associated with uncompensated metabolic acidosis. That pH values <7.4 and bicarbonate values <24 were strongly associated with mortality supports our approach, despite use of potentially imperfect cutoffs. Our findings emphasize the prognostic implications of broader acid–base trends in refractory CS, minor shifts in acid–base status may reflect physiologic trajectories in critically ill patients supported on VA ECMO and are best interpreted when considered in conjunction with other metabolic and clinical markers of perfusion.

Profound acidemia impairs myocardial contractility, reduces cardiovascular responsiveness to catecholamines, and further exacerbates end‐organ dysfunction, thereby contributing to refractory cardiovascular collapse via the well‐recognized downward spiral of CS. 5 , 22 , 23 Our observation that most patients with metabolic acidosis had lactic acidosis, and these patients had substantially worse survival, argues in favor of persistent uncorrected hypoperfusion as a key mechanism linking acid–base derangements with adverse outcomes. The strong correlation between persistent metabolic/lactic acidosis at 24 hours and poor outcomes highlights the limitations of VA‐ECMO in fully reversing the metabolic consequences of prolonged hypoperfusion, emphasizing the need for adjunctive strategies such as tailored acid–base management, early recognition of inadequate resuscitation with timely institution of temporary MCS, and rapid escalation of therapies to mitigate ongoing metabolic stress. Future clinical trials examining hemodynamic support strategies for refractory CS should factor in pH and metabolic acidosis as a basis for patient randomization to evaluate their influence on mortality outcomes and treatment strategies. Clinical trials in patients with various forms of critical illness have failed to show a consistent benefit of alkali therapy 24 , 25 ; however, this does not exclude a potential benefit in patients supported with VA ECMO.

Respiratory Compensation on Extracorporeal Support

Respiratory failure in patients with refractory CS may occur due to increased physiologic dead space or intrapulmonary shunting due to an imbalance between lung ventilation and perfusion. 10 , 26 , 27 , 28 These may be caused by parenchymal or pleural disease and hypoperfusion due to CS or pulmonary vascular disease, contributing to CO2 retention. 10 , 27 , 29 This is typically ameliorated by mechanical ventilation and ECMO, which can provide very effective net CO2 clearance. Although respiratory acidosis was not a major predictor of mortality in our cohort, the few patients with concurrent respiratory and metabolic acidosis—representing a failure of compensation—exhibited the worst survival. The observed U‐shaped association between 24‐hour PaCO2 levels and in‐hospital survival suggests that both excessive hypercapnia and hypocapnia may contribute to adverse outcomes, albeit comparatively modestly when compared with metabolic acidosis. Indeed, PaCO2 fluctuations have previously been demonstrated to be associated with lower survival among patients supported on VA ECMO. 30 , 31 , 32 Cavayas et al. observed a similar bidirectional dose–response relationship between changes in PaCO2, and neurological complications including brain death among patients with respiratory failure. 30 Similarly, Tonna et al. demonstrated a nonlinear trend between PaCO2 levels and survival at 24 hours of VA ECMO support among patients with cardiac arrest. 32 Our adjusted analyses suggest that metabolic disturbances are more important than respiratory disturbances for determining prognosis. It is imperative to adopt a measured approach to drive proportionate respiratory compensation in response to severe acidemia or alkalemia among VA ECMO‐supported patients, as overly rapid modulation of sweep gas flow alone may further destabilize physiological homeostasis and increase mortality risk. 31 Strategies should prioritize a controlled, moderate adjustment of total CO2 clearance through the ECMO circuit and ventilator to modulate PaCO2 to facilitate smooth restoration of normocarbia and normal pH. Regular monitoring of blood gases is crucial to guiding these adjustments and avoiding rapid PaCO2 shifts that could exacerbate hemodynamic instability or impair cerebral autoregulation.

Machine Learning Phenotype Identification

ML is a powerful tool for data‐driven exploration of risk prediction that overcomes some limitations of traditional regression methods, such as difficulty handling highly correlated variables. 5 , 6 , 33 , 34 We used basic supervised ML methods to identify the most important predictors of mortality in our cohort. Specifically, CART found that a low base excess (<−2.7) was the most important metabolic derangement, followed by low pH, supporting our main findings. Likewise, the random forest models identified base excess as the most important acid–base variable for the prediction of mortality. Unsupervised ML techniques have allowed the identification of phenotypes in populations with CS, uncovering subgroups of patients with distinct metabolic profiles that correlate with clinical outcomes. 5 , 6 , 34 Our findings align with prior evidence establishing the significance of exploratory ML analyses for CS mortality risk stratification, which have highlighted the hemometabolic CS phenotype with severe (lactic) acidosis as the highest‐risk group. 5 , 6 Grouping patients by metabolic response profiles may provide insights to refine pH correction strategies and management approaches, including extracorporeal and ventilatory support. Further validation in independent cohorts is warranted before such ML‐derived risk stratification approaches can be considered for integration into bedside clinical decision support systems.

Limitations

Our study shares the limitations inherent to a retrospective registry analysis, including selection bias, as all patients in our cohort received VA ECMO support for refractory CS. Patients not placed on VA ECMO due to futility or other contraindications (or those with milder degrees of CS) were not analyzed, potentially skewing mortality associations. Missing data likely exacerbated this effect, as we performed a complete‐case analysis, and patients without any acid–base data were excluded potentially leading to selection bias. Patients who had missing acid–base values appeared to differ from patients with complete acid–base data, suggesting that these data were not missing completely at random. Importantly, patients who died before 24‐hour acid–base values could be obtained would have been excluded from this analysis. Our analysis examined acid–base derangements closest to 24 hours post‐VA ECMO initiation; however, the data collection time points ranged from no less than 18 hours and no more than 30 hours after cannulation, precluding us from accounting for dynamic changes in acid–base balance, including progression or rectification of derangements. Our time‐to‐event analysis should be interpreted with caution, as we had only in‐hospital outcome data and could not capture deaths occurring after hospital discharge; we did not report Cox proportional‐hazards regression models due to violations of the proportional‐hazards assumption in this cohort. Given the complex nature of refractory CS, we could not exclude unmeasured confounders as true mediators of effect, precluding us from discerning causality in our cohort. This raises the crucial unanswered question of whether metabolic acidosis is a plausible target for therapy or simply an epiphenomenon of greater illness severity. Interpretation of respiratory acidosis or alkalosis based on PaCO2 is challenging among patients on VA ECMO support, as it is a function of extracorporeal gas exchange and mechanical ventilation more than intrinsic compensatory mechanisms of the respiratory system; we lacked data on sweep gas flow to understand whether high PaCO2 represented refractory respiratory acidosis or an effect of the ECMO settings. Optimal kidney and liver metabolism is crucial for acid–base homeostasis, including clearance of lactate and other metabolic acids. We lacked data regarding renal function, electrolytes (eg, chloride or anion gap), and liver function (eg, transaminases), precluding us from determining whether metabolic acidosis was a surrogate for end‐organ failure. Likewise, we did not have data on treatments that may have been given to address metabolic acidosis (eg, alkali), so we could not determine if persistent acidosis reflects a lack of treatment, failure of therapy, or a combination of factors. The overall use of coronary angiography and revascularization in our study appears relatively low, particularly compared with randomized trials such as ECLS‐SHOCK. We could not exclude the possibility of inaccurate documentation or procedures performed at a different facility as contributing factors. Finally, the results of our ML analyses should be considered exploratory, as we used ML specifically to support our main findings using traditional analytic methods rather than to build optimal predictive models; for this reason, we derived and tested the models in our entire cohort, did not perform splitting or cross‐fold validation potentially resulting in overestimation of model performance.

CONCLUSIONS

Among adult patients with refractory CS supported with VA ECMO, survival worsened progressively with lower pH, bicarbonate, and base excess after 24 hours of extracorporeal cardiopulmonary support. Our findings emphasize the prognostic significance of metabolic parameters as potentially modifiable risk factors. Early acid–base optimization and correction of persistent severe metabolic acidosis may serve as a key therapeutic target in future studies to optimize the benefits of VA‐ECMO support and improve survival in this critically ill population.
