L-Methionine attenuates methotrexate-induced cardiotoxicity by modulating oxidative stress, inflammation, and dyslipidemia in rats
Authors: Wessam M Abdel-Wahab, Nada S Daifalla, Amina E Essawy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethotrexate (MTX) is a potent chemotherapeutic agent with a wide range of anticancer effects; however, its clinical efficacy is restricted due to its cardiomyopathy. This research examined the protective potential of L-Methionine (L-Met) against MTX cardiotoxicity in rats. Thirty rats were assigned into five groups: control, L-Met 400 (400 mg/kg), MTX (20 mg/kg), MTX + L-Met 300 (20 mg/kg MTX plus 300 mg/kg L-Met), and MTX + L-Met 400 (20 mg/kg MTX plus 400 mg/kg L-Met). L-Met was administered orally for 15 days with a single MTX injection on the 5th day. Serum and cardiac samples were gathered for assessments of cardiac injury markers, lipid profile, atherogenic index markers, redox status markers, and pro-inflammatory cytokines. MTX-induced cardiac damage was demonstrated by elevation in the troponin-I, creatine kinase myocardial band, alkaline phosphatase, lactate dehydrogenase, aspartate aminotransferase, and alanine aminotransferase in the serum. Levels of triglycerides, total cholesterol, high- and low-density lipoproteins, atherogenic index, and cardiac risk ratio increased. Notable increase in malondialdehyde, nitric oxide, and protein carbonyl, alongside suppression in reduced glutathione, glutathione peroxidase, superoxide dismutase, and catalase, were reported in the cardiac tissue. Furthermore, the inflammatory effect of MTX was demonstrated by elevated C-reactive protein, tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1β, and interleukin-6 levels. In a dose-dependent pattern, the two doses of L-Met significantly attenuated the cardiotoxic effect of MTX, with the higher dose being more effective. In conclusion, L-Met protected against MTX cardiotoxicity in a rat model via attenuating oxidative stress and inflammation, representing preliminary preclinical evidence that warrants further investigation.Keywords: Methotrexate, L-Methionine, Cardiotoxicity, Oxidative stress, Inflammation, Dyslipidemia

Introduction

Cardiotoxicity induced by anticancer and chemotherapeutic agents is a significant adverse side effect that may limit or even halt the application of these medications [1]. Methotrexate (MTX) is one of the most widely used chemotherapeutic agents in the treatment of different cancers, including leukemia, breast cancer, and hepato-carcinoma when used in high doses [2]. It is also effective in the treatment of various autoimmune diseases when used at a low dose [3]. The cytotoxic effect of MTX on cancer cells is attributed primarily to its folate antagonistic activity. It interferes with the metabolism of folate by inhibiting dihydrofolate reductase and consequently suppressing the synthesis of purines and pyrimidines necessary for DNA synthesis and cell proliferation [4]. Furthermore, the antitumor activity of MTX is mediated by its apoptotic activity, which triggers a cascade of caspase-mediated DNA cleavage in tumor cells [5], [6]. Unfortunately, the cytotoxicity of MTX may not be confined only to the tumor cells but can expand to normal cells and adversely affect other non-targeted organs, including the heart. Cardiotoxicity of MTX was documented to be associated with arrhythmias, myocardial infarction, and cardiac arrest [7]. MTX was reported to induce damage to cardiac tissue and to cause pathophysiological changes, including heart failure and elevation in the levels of cardiac-specific enzymes [5]. Oxidative stress is considered a primary mechanism in MTX-induced organ toxicities. It can impair mitochondrial function and increase the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which consequently stimulates lipid peroxidation (LPO) and causes membrane damage [8]. It also increases reactive nitrogen species (RNS), which initiate inflammation, an important mechanism involved in MTX toxicity [9]. Cardio-protection against the toxicity of MTX is becoming compulsory to enable expanding its clinical efficacy. The use of micronutrients and nutraceuticals with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects can be an effective strategy.

The sulfur-containing amino acid Methionine (Met) is necessary for several physiological processes, including immune functions, metabolism, and cell division and proliferation [10]. It can also be converted to several functional derivatives, such as S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the major methyl donor in many biological activities [11]. Methionine and its derivatives possess anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions [12]. It can function as a direct and indirect antioxidant, thus affecting the redox balance. The Met-part of proteins can react with free radicals to oxidize residues and form Met sulfoxide (Met-O), an enzyme involved in antioxidant defense [13]. Furthermore, the conversion of Met into its reduced and oxidized forms is a natural scavenging process that protects against oxidants [14]. Methionine can also play a central role in cellular redox homeostasis through its involvement in the Met-homocysteine cycle and its contribution to glutathione synthesis [15]. Following conversion to SAM, Met participates in methylation reactions and is subsequently metabolized to homocysteine, which can enter the transsulfuration pathway to generate cysteine, the rate-limiting precursor for glutathione biosynthesis [16]. Preservation of glutathione homeostasis is critical for modulating oxidative stress-mediated myocardial damage. Deficiency in Met has been associated with cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) [17].

Given the previously mentioned positive effects of Met, it can be a suitable adjunct to improve the clinical results and treatment tolerability associated with MTX. While our previous work demonstrated the protective effect of the L- form of Met (L-Met) against MTX nephrotoxicity [18], its potential role in preventing MTX-induced cardiotoxicity has not been previously investigated. The present study was therefore designed to elucidate the cardiotoxicity of MTX in rats and to investigate L-Met’s cardioprotective potential with an insight into the possible mechanism(s) by which L-Met provides the protection.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

MTX, L-Met, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for assessment of cardiac troponin (cTnI), C-reactive protein (CRP), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). Kits for measuring oxidative stress parameters, myocardial biomarkers, and lipid profile were obtained from Bio-diagnostic Co. (Giza, Egypt).

Animals and ethics approval

Thirty adult male albino rats (Wistar strain) weighing 190–220 g were procured from the animal house of the Medical Research Institute, Alexandria University, Egypt. Rats were housed in a room with good ventilation and under controlled conditions (25°C and a 12-hour light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. The rats were accustomed to the lab conditions for 10 days. This study was carried out in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health (NIH). Approval was granted by the Alexandria University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (ALEXU-IACUC), a member of the International Council for Laboratory Animal Science (ICLAS) (Approval number: AU04221126301 granted on 26/11/2022).

Experimental design

2.L-Met group given a daily oral dose of 400 mg/kg L-Met for 15 consecutive days. The L-Met-only group was included to confirm the safety of L-Met at the highest tested dose (400 mg/kg), representing the maximum exposure used in the study. The 300 mg/kg dose was evaluated only in combination with MTX to assess dose-dependent cardioprotective efficacy; therefore, a standalone 300 mg/kg L-Met group was not included.

3.MTX group received a single IP injection of MTX at a dose of 20 mg/kg on day 5 [19], [20].

MTX group received a single IP injection of MTX at a dose of 20 mg/kg on day 5 [19], [20].

4.MTX + L-Met 300 group administered 300 mg/kg L-Met orally for 15 consecutive days and a single IP injection of MTX (20 mg/kg) on day 5.

MTX + L-Met 300 group administered 300 mg/kg L-Met orally for 15 consecutive days and a single IP injection of MTX (20 mg/kg) on day 5.

5.MTX + L-Met 400 group received 400 mg/kg L-Met orally daily for 15 days and a single IP injection of MTX (20 mg/kg) on day 5.

MTX + L-Met 400 group received 400 mg/kg L-Met orally daily for 15 days and a single IP injection of MTX (20 mg/kg) on day 5.

For the combined-treatment groups (groups 4 and 5), L-Met administration started 4 days before MTX injection, along with MTX administration on day 5, and continued for 10 additional days post MTX. The dose of L-Met (300 mg/kg) was selected based on previous experimental evidence demonstrating antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective effects in rats without observable toxicity [18], [21]. The human equivalent dose (HED) calculation was performed using body surface area normalization. Accordingly, a rat dose of 300 mg/kg corresponds to an HED of approximately 48.6 mg/kg which is achievable through dietary supplementation, supporting the physiological relevance and safety of the selected dose. Furthermore, we used a higher dose of L-Met (400 mg/kg) to evaluate the efficacy of this dose and compare its outcomes with the dose of 300 mg/kg. The HED was calculated using the following formula: HED (mg/kg) = Animal dose (mg/kg) x Km (rat)/Km (human), where Km for rat = 6, for adult human = 37. For MTX, a single dose was selected to induce cardiotoxicity within a short time frame while minimizing mortality and cumulative toxicity. Administration of MTX on day 5 allowed sufficient pre-exposure to L-Met to establish steady-state antioxidant and cytoprotective activity prior to toxic insult. Continued L-Met administration for 10 days after MTX exposure was designed to assess its ability to attenuate ongoing oxidative damage and inflammatory response during the progression phase of MTX toxicity, which typically evolves over several days. This dosing schedule with pretreatment–posttreatment has been widely used in studies evaluating protective agents against MTX-induced organ toxicity.

Blood collection and cardiac tissue preparation

Twenty-four hours following the last treatment, rats were anesthetized using ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) IP before euthanasia. Blood was collected by decapitation and was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min to isolate serum. Aliquots of the collected serum were kept at −20°C for the determination of myocardial injury markers, including cTnI, creatine kinase-myocardial band (CK-MB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and lipid profile as well. The heart was dissected out, washed, and kept in liquid nitrogen for further manipulation. For preparing cardiac lysate, one g of the heart tissue was homogenized in 10 volumes of potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) with 0.1 mM EDTA and was spun for 10 min at 4000 x g at 4 °C. Aliquots of the supernatants were preserved at −80°C for assessing redox state and inflammatory markers.

Assessment of cardiac redox status

The malondialdehyde (MDA) level as a marker of LPO, was determined using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction method that measures the interaction of MDA and TBA to generate a chromatic adduct which absorbs at 532 nm [22]. NO level was measured depending on the reaction of the nitrite with Griess reagent to produce a colored compound that absorbs at 540 nm. [23]. Protein carbonyl (PC), as a marker of protein oxidation, was determined through the reaction with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine and the carbonyl measured at 430 nm [24]. Reduced glutathione (GSH) content was determined using Elman’s reagent (5, 5′-Dithiobis [2-nitrobenzoic acid]) [25]. Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity was assessed through a coupled reaction with glutathione reductase [26]. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was evaluated utilizing nitro blue tetrazolium dye [27]. Catalase (CAT) activity was measured by monitoring the rate of hydrogen peroxide breakdown at 240 nm [28].

Measurement of inflammatory biomarkers in cardiac tissue

The ELISA kits (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were used to measure serum CRP alongside cardiac TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 as mentioned in the manufacturers’ instructions

Assessment of cardiac injury markers in serum

Serum levels of cTnI, CK-MB, ALP, LDH, ALT, and AST were measured to assess cardiac injury. cTnI, as a specific marker of cardiac muscle injury was measured using ELISA kits as described in the manufacturer’s guidelines. The activities of CK-MB isozyme, LDH, and ALP, represented as U/L, were determined according to the methods described by Würzburg et al. [29], Henry et al. [30], and Wright et al. [31], respectively. The activities of ALT and AST were assessed using the available diagnostic kits as described by Reitman and Frankel [32]. The enzyme activities were expressed as U/L. AST/ALT ratio was calculated.

Measurement of lipid profile

Levels of triglycerides (TGs), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were assessed by colorimetric methods using lipid profile test kits (Bio-diagnostic, Egypt) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Calculation of atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) and cardiac risk ratio (CRR)

AIP was calculated as follows: AIP = Log (Triglycerides / HDL cholesterol) [33]. CRR was calculated according to the following formula: CRR = [Total cholesterol]/ [HDL cholesterol] as described by Calling et al. [34].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed using SPSS version 29 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Data were represented as mean values ± SEM. The normality of data distribution was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk test and histogram. Values were transformed to the square root or log10 to fit the normal distribution. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. P values below 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Influence of L-Met on MTX-induced alteration in cardiac redox state

Fig. 1, Fig. 2 illustrate the changes in the heart redox state. The cardiac oxidative state was significantly (P < 0.05) increased, as denoted by 1.5-fold (2.44 ± 0.23 vs. 6.10 ± 0.38), 1.4-fold (18.12 ± 1.48 vs. 43.22 ± 3.40), and 1.3-fold (34.72 ± 2.21 vs. 79.57 ± 3.89) in MDA, NO, and PC levels, respectively, as compared to normal control. Meanwhile, the cardiac antioxidative state was significantly (P < 0.05) decreased as shown by a 0.5-fold (37.70 ± 4.57 vs. 17.92 ± 2.04), 0.5-fold (61.25 ± 4.93 vs. 29.65 ± 3.35), 0.6-fold (161.30 ± 6.77 vs. 58.38 ± 6.22), and 0.7-fold (67.38 ± 5.96 vs. 18.80. ± 2.74) decrease in GSH, GPx, SOD, and CAT, respectively, relative to the control. Supplementation with L-Met 400 notably amended the deterioration in the cardiac oxidative/antioxidative state induced by MTX for all markers except CAT. Also, statistical comparison between the two groups that received MTX with L-Met indicates better improvement with the use of the higher L-Met dose (400 mg/kg) compared to the 300 mg/kg dose. At the lower dose, L-Met decreased the oxidative indices by 0.25-, 0.27, and 0.29-fold for MDA, PC, and NO levels, respectively. Meanwhile, 400 mg/kg L-Met decreased these indices by 0.49-, 0.46, and 0.48-fold, respectively, as compared to the MTX group. Concerning the antioxidant state, GSH, GPx, SOD, and CAT showed a significant increase of 0.48-, 0.64-, 0.9-, and 13.6-fold when using 300 mg/kg L-Met and 0.81-, 0.84-, 15.6-, and 19.8-fold when using 400 mg/kg L-Met, respectively, as compared to the MTX group.

L-Met modulates MTX-induced alterations in inflammatory mediators

To examine the involvement of inflammation in MTX cardiotoxicity, CRP, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 were assessed (Fig. 3). MTX induced a 1.60-fold (110.83 ± 6.56 vs. 282.77 ± 11.49), 2.0-fold (23.07 ± 1.19 vs. 69.30 ± 3.49), 1.2-fold (10.85 ± 0.91 vs. 24.22 ± 1.46), and 1.3-fold (61.93 ± 4.72 vs. 144.78 ± 7.58) increase in the levels of CRP, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, respectively, compared to the control and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.001). Rats receiving L-Met with MTX demonstrated a dose-dependent decrease in inflammatory markers in comparison to the MTX-alone group. Levels of CRP, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 showed a reduction of 0.3-, 0.3-, 0.2-, and 0.3-fold, respectively, when treated with 300 mg/kg L-Met; however, the decrease was not significant except for CRP. On the other hand, 400 mg/kg L-Met induced a statistically significant (P < 0.001) decrease of all inflammatory mediators in the amount of 0.4-, 0.6-, 0.5-, and 0.4-fold, for CRP, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, respectively, relative to the MTX group. In addition, the higher dose restored the levels of TNF-α and IL-1β to normal levels comparable to the control. None of the inflammatory mediators were restored to normal levels using the lower dose of L-Met.

Effect of L-Met supplementation on MTX-induced alteration in lipid profile

The lipid profiles indicies of different groups are shown in Fig. 4. Injection of MTX significantly increased the levels of TGs (1.3-fold, 47.95 ± 4.22 vs. 108.53 ± 7.44), TC (0.8-fold, 88.52 ± 5.67 vs. 161.28 ± 7.57), and LDL-C (3.4-fold, 21.70 ± 2.64 vs. 96.18 ± 7.21), respectively, while the level of HDL-C was significantly decreased by 0.5-fold (51.72 ± 4.13 vs. 27.75 ± 2.83) in comparison to the control (P < 0.01). Notably, MTX exposure significantly increased AIP above normal control (-0.03 ± 0.05 vs. 0.6 ± 0.04) (P < 0.01). Likewise, CRR showed a significant increase of 2.4-fold (1.79 ± 0.19 vs. 6.17 ± 0.73) relative to the control group (P < 0.01) in the MTX group. Supplementation with the two doses of L-Met induced a dose-dependent improvement in MTX-induced deterioration in the lipid profiles. The levels of HDL-C and CRR were restored to normal by the administration of either of the two doses of L-Met, whereas the levels of TGs, TC, and AIP were restored to normal using the higher dose only. Although the level of LDL-C showed significant improvement using either of the two doses of L-Met, it was not completely recovered and still showing a significant difference from the control (P < 0.01). Interestingly, MTX significantly increased AIP of the injected rats as compared to the control (P < 0.001). Injection of L-Met 300 significantly decreased the value of AIP (P < 0.01), whereas only the higher dose notably reduced AIP readings to values comparable to normal (P < 0.001). It is accepted that an atherogenic index less than 0.1 is considered a low risk for cardiovascular disease, a value of 0.11–0.21 is an intermediate risk, and a value greater than 0.21 is a high risk.

Discussion

Although MTX is a potent antineoplastic drug, extended organ toxicity remains a major complication that limits its clinical use. Administration of MTX exhibits cytotoxic effect which is not restricted to cancer cells but can extend to vital organs, including the heart. Approaches to protect against the noxious effects of MTX are urgently needed. In our previous work, we demonstrated that L-Met attenuated MTX-induced nephrotoxicity [18]. However, whether these protective effects extend to the heart, a highly vulnerable organ to chemotherapeutic injury, remains unknown. This study examined the cardiotoxicity of MTX and evaluated the potential cardioprotective effect of L-Met at two different doses in rats. The dosing schedule included administration of L-Met for 15 consecutive days with a single MTX dose administered on day 5 to evaluate both the prophylactic and therapeutic protective effects of L-Met against MTX-induced toxicity models. Key parameters assessed included oxidant/antioxidant indices, inflammatory markers, cardiac-specific biomarkers, and lipid profile. The results confirmed the cardiotoxicity of MTX as indicated by altered cardiac biomarkers, dyslipidemia, disturbed cardiac redox state, and enhanced inflammatory mediators, which collectively represent strong biochemical evidence of MTX-induced cellular injury in vivo. Treatment with L-Met (300 and 400 mg/kg) effectively modulated the cardiotoxicity of MTX, possibly due to its well-recognized antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. While the protective mechanisms of L-Met in this study are similar to those observed in our previous MTX-induced nephrotoxicity, the present study provides novel, organ-specific evidence for the heart confirming that the protective effects of L-Met extend beyond the kidney. The selected L-Met doses are physiologically relevant, as L-Met is an essential amino acid commonly consumed through diet and supplementation. Using body surface area conversion, the administered L-Met doses correspond to HEDs that are attainable through nutritional supplementation. Thus, the findings of this study may have translational relevance for dietary or therapeutic strategies.

Due to the sustained need for energy in the heart, mitochondria are highly abundant in the cardiomyocytes, occupying 30% of the cell volume [35]. The high metabolic activity of myocardium and subsequent increase in the generation of ROS, alongside the reduced antioxidant activity, is believed to be a main contributor to the cardiotoxicity of MTX [5]. The results reported herein illustrated a disturbance in the cardiac redox status which agrees with previous studies [36], [37], [38]. MTX can elevate homocysteine levels, which is auto-oxidized to produce ROS and stimulate NADPH oxidases, a major source of cellular ROS [39]. MTX is also capable of inducing nitrosative stress by enhancing RNS that can promote LPO [5]. An elevated level of NO can promote a proinflammatory effect. This effect can occur indirectly through the rapid interaction of NO with ROS, leading to the synthesis of peroxynitrite, a cytotoxic RNS. Furthermore, NO can activate the transcription factor nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), which is responsible for regulating numerous genes related to inflammation [40]. The MTX-induced increase in the cardiac oxidative status in our study may also be related to an alteration in lipid metabolism. An elevated level of lipid is believed to reduce the expression of antioxidant enzymes, heighten the expression of NADPH oxidase, and elevate ROS levels [41]. The effect of MTX on the antioxidants may stem from the suppression of nuclear factor-erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2), a transcription factor critical for regulating endogenous antioxidants. MTX can hinder NRF2's capacity to bind to its target DNA sequences, thereby blocking the synthesis of antioxidant enzymes [42]. L-Met can eliminate ROS/RNS and neutralize oxidants by transferring electrons with the creation of radical adducts [43]. Furthermore, as a sulfur-containing amino acid, L-Met can readily be oxidized to Met-O, which can be reduced back to Met by Met-O reductases, allowing for the elimination of ROS through repeated neutralization [44]. Moreover, L-Met enhances the transsulfuration pathway, resulting in the production of cysteine, an amino acidnecessary to produce GSH and taurine, both of which can influence cellular antioxidant status [45], [46]. In our study, MTX-induced cardiotoxicity was attenuated by co-administration of L-Met suggesting that modulation of oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways may contribute to its cardioprotective effect. However, definitive causality requires targeted mechanistic interventions such as inhibition of ROS generation and use of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agents. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted within the context of association rather than direct mechanistic proof.

Multiple mechanistic pathways are involved in MTX-induced cardiotoxicity, of which inflammation is prominent. Our results further support this hypothesis which agrees with Mahmoud et al. [9] and Karim et al. [47]. Cytokines released during inflammation can affect the activity and expression of cardiac ion channels, resulting in cardiac remodeling and compromised cardiac function [48]. Oxidative stress and inflammation seem to be interrelated mechanisms and have been associated with CVDs [49]. NF-κB is a key factor for the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes. It is well established that increased ROS can activate NF-κB, which in turn up-regulates genes responsible for producing the pro-inflammatory cytokines [50]. Notably, L-Met supplementation suppressed the synthesis of the inflammatory mediators. The antioxidant effect of L-Met may explain its anti-inflammatory action noted in this study. The down-regulation of NF-κB through down-regulating PI3K/AKT may partly account for L-Met’s anti-inflammatory effect [51]. While remaining consistent with previously published evidence implicating oxidative stress and inflammation in MTX-induced organ toxicity, direct mechanistic involvement requires further investigation.

Troponin, alongside myocardium-specific enzymes, including CK-MB, LDH, ALP, AST, and ALT, is a pathological hallmark in diagnosis of cardiac injury [52]. Normally, these biomarkers are low in non-pathological conditions; however, they become elevated in toxicities, including cardiotoxicity [53]. The present results substantiate MTX-induced cardiotoxicity, indicated by marked increase in cardiac-specific biomarkers. These elevations are indicative of cardiomyocyte injury, plausibly resulting from compromised membrane integrity and leakage of intracellular constituents into the circulation. These results agree with previous reports [47], [54]. Polyunsaturated fatty acids, which constitute the main component in cellular membranes, are highly liable to peroxidation by reactive species. Therefore, MTX-induced increase in LPO and subsequent membrane peroxidative damage to cardiomyocytes may explain the leakage of these markers out of the cytoplasm and the elevation in their levels reported herein. Interestingly, co-administration of L-Met and MTX significantly reduced the cardiac injury biomarkers, which supports the protective effect of L-Met. These findings indicate the preservation of the structural integrity of cardiomyocytes.

Dyslipidemia is a major contributor to CVD. In our study, MTX induced dyslipidemia, as indicated by higher TG, TC, and LDL-C serum levels, as well as lower serum HDL-C. Furthermore, the AIP and the CRR were significantly higher in the MTX group. Similar dyslipidemia caused by MTX has been reported by Elbakry et al. [55] and Ghoneum and El-Gebed [56]. Belinsky et al. [57] found that 35 genes involved in fatty acid synthesis were upregulated by exposure to MTX, which is consistent with a common steatosis induced by MTX. Lipid metabolism is predominantly regulated by the liver. Hepatic dysfunction, including drug-induced hepatotoxicity, is well known to disturb systemic lipid profile which may be correlated with elevated liver enzymes [58]. MTX has documented hepatotoxic effects mediated by oxidative stress, steatosis, and fibrosis which can impair hepatic lipid metabolism [59]. Therefore, the dyslipidemia observed in MTX-treated rats in this study is more likely secondary to MTX-induced hepatic dysfunction rather than a direct cardiac effect. The dyslipidemia observed in our study may also be related to inflammation caused by MTX. In particular, inflammation is commonly associated with a reduction in HDL-C, an increase in TGs, and an increase in LDL and lipoprotein (a) levels [60]. These modifications are part of the body's immune response and are usually considered pro-atherogenic. Inflammation boosts the synthesis of several cytokines, which are known to alter the metabolism of lipids [61]. Additionally, MTX has a hepatotoxic effect, which could explain dyslipidemia [62]. Therefore, our findings support the notion that MTX-induced inflammation and the subsequent dyslipidemia can significantly increase the risk of CVDs. Co-administration of L-Met with MTX significantly improved the lipid profile relative to the control group. The hypolipidemic effect of Met has been reported previously [63]. The lipid-modulating effect of L-Met may be attributed primarily to its systemic and hepatoprotective actions [64], thereby restoring normal lipid metabolism and improving the lipid profile. The hepatoprotective effect of L-Met could indirectly mitigate cardiovascular risk. The cardioprotective effect of L-Met may also be related to its distinguished anti-inflammatory and antioxidant actions, which can modulate the hepatic function and thus improve the lipid profile.

Conclusion and future directives

Collectively, this study advances the current understanding of MTX toxicity by (i) identifying the heart as a distinct and vulnerable target of MTX-induced oxidative and inflammatory injury, (ii) providing the first experimental evidence that L-Met confers cardioprotection against MTX, and (iii) linking L-Met supplementation to improvements in myocardial redox status, inflammatory regulation, and lipid homeostasis. By extending our previous renal findings to the cardiovascular system, the present work broadens the protective relevance of L-Met and highlights its potential utility in mitigating MTX-associated cardiotoxicity. It is important to note that administration of L-Met alone did not cause changes in any of the tested parameters which indicates its safety at the used doses. It is also worth noting that the present study was not conducted in a tumor-bearing model, and hence no conclusions can be drawn regarding the effects of L-Met on MTX anticancer efficacy. Although the present study provides substantial biochemical and inflammatory evidence of MTX cardiotoxicity and L-Met cardioprotection, it has a limitation of lacking histopathological and molecular analyses. Further investigation, including histopathological and molecular-level analyses, is needed to validate the proposed mechanisms and confirm the cardioprotective potential of L-Met in MTX-associated cardiotoxicity. In addition, further pharmacokinetic and cancer-treatment efficacy studies are required before deducing clinical implication.
