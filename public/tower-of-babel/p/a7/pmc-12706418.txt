A Step‐by‐Step Workflow for Performing In Silico Clinical Trials With Nonlinear Mixed Effects Models
Authors: Javiera Cortés‐Ríos, Mindy Magee, Anna Sher, William J. Jusko, Rajat Desikan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn silico clinical trials (ISCT) are computational frameworks that employ mathematical models to generate virtual patients and simulate their responses to new treatments, treatment regimens, or medical devices via simulations mirroring real‐world clinical trials. ISCTs are an important component of the model‐informed drug development (MIDD) framework for optimizing therapies, treatment personalization, informing regulatory decisions, and accelerating overall drug development by enhancing R&D productivity. However, the emergence of complex models, such as quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) models, presents significant challenges for their effective implementation. Guidelines for conducting ISCTs have been published to address these challenges, focusing on algorithms and credibility frameworks for generating plausible virtual patients and calibrating virtual populations. However, it is not straightforward to apply existing workflows to models where parameter distributions and correlations are estimated using nonlinear mixed effects (NLME) population fitting approaches, a common practice in the pharmaceutical industry when individual‐patient‐level data is available. Here, we illustrate a modeling workflow for conducting ISCTs with NLME models, detailing key considerations, methods, and challenges at each step. We demonstrate the practical implementation of this workflow through two examples to showcase its broad applicability: (1) a simple model predicting tumor growth in response to chemotherapy and (2) a more complex mechanistic QSP model of hepatitis B virus infection that captures the physiological mechanisms underlying treatment response with standard‐of‐care therapies.

Introduction

Modeling and simulation have become integral components of drug development due to their cost‐efficiency and speed compared to biological experiments and clinical trials. These methods enable the forecasting of outcomes and optimization of therapies, leading to increased adoption within the scientific community, pharmaceutical industry, and regulatory agencies such as the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [1, 2, 3, 4]. Advances in computational methodologies and the availability of rich datasets have facilitated the transition from simpler empirical models, which reproduce the pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD) of therapies, to more complex models such as Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetics (PBPK) and Quantitative Systems Pharmacology (QSP) models. These advanced models provide a deeper understanding of the mechanisms involved in PK, disease progression, and drug responses [5].

The development of models that capture the behavior of physiological variables in real patients enrolled into clinical studies has enabled predictive clinical trial simulations, known as in silico clinical trials (ISCTs). These simulations use virtual patients (VPs), each represented by a set of parameters that mirror real patient trajectories, allowing for the evaluation of diverse treatment scenarios. ISCTs integrate models of human physiology, disease progression, and drug pharmacology with probabilistic/statistical techniques to predict responses to therapy in VPs, thereby reflecting the behavior of physiological biomarkers in real patients [6, 7]. These simulations facilitate forward predictions of clinical trial responses to mono and combination treatment regimens [8], inform optimal drug dosing and clinical trial designs [6, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14], predict drug–drug interactions [15, 16], and provide a causal understanding of heterogeneity in patient outcomes, thus reducing costs and time by de‐risking drug development [9]. VPs have also been instrumental in simulating drug effects in underrepresented populations, such as pediatric or pregnant patients [17, 18, 19], thereby supporting precision medicine strategies through patient‐individualized simulations [9, 14]. Consequently, ISCTs have been applied across a wide range of medical conditions, including cancer [8, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25], infectious diseases [26, 27, 28, 29, 30], cardiovascular diseases [31, 32], immune‐related disorders [33, 34, 35], neurodegenerative diseases [36, 37], metabolic diseases [38, 39, 40, 41], and orphan/rare and chronic diseases with low response rates to existing therapies [42, 43, 44].

The process of conducting an ISCT generally involves: (1) developing an appropriate disease progression and drug PK/PD model; (2) estimating model parameters using biological and clinical datasets; (3) utilizing quantitative measures of parameter variability and parameter sampling to create a set of VPs known as a virtual population (Vpop) that captures inter‐patient heterogeneity; and (4) subjecting the Vpop to a specific treatment scheme mirroring real‐world clinical trials to predict its safety and efficacy [45]. Each step presents various technical challenges and limitations, including data availability and collection, inter‐assay and inter‐study variability, model scope and assumptions, parameter identifiability and uncertainty, computational cost, identification of predictive biomarkers, virtual patient selection, and virtual population calibration. These factors can significantly impact the uncertainty in forward predictions with ISCTs [45]. Therefore, establishing the credibility of the model and the ISCT simulation framework through model verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification is crucial for robustly supporting clinical decision‐making [46, 47].

Model verification ensures the model's implementation is accurate, with error‐free code, correct equations, and precise calculations that align with the model's assumptions [4, 46, 48]. This step includes model building strategies, such as stepwise fitting or hierarchical parameter estimation to avoid practical non‐identifiability between treatment effect parameters acting on similar pathways. Model validation assesses how well the model's predictions align with observed data, particularly independent datasets not used in model development, and evaluates the appropriateness of the biological assumptions and data used [4, 46, 48]. Uncertainty quantification addresses the inherent variability within the model, considering factors such as parameter sensitivity, uncertainty, identifiability, fixed parameter values, and variability across datasets due to assay‐related limitations, confounding factors, and inter‐study differences. This provides a comprehensive understanding of the model's ability to generate reliable predictions and its limitations [4, 46, 47]. Together, these processes ensure that models are both accurate and robust, supporting their effective application in real‐world scenarios. While general guidelines outlining good practices and common methods for conducting ISCTs have been published [4, 5, 45, 48, 49, 50], these must be tailored to specific technical considerations, such as context of use, model characteristics, the nature of the available data, target population, and the variety of methods applied to different cases. These considerations pose challenges to the development of generally applicable frameworks and computational tools, especially with complex models.

One robust method to address these complexities is population fitting using Nonlinear Mixed Effects (NLME). This approach is well‐established for analyzing individual data, which often includes sparse trajectories and missing data points across patients from multiple studies/sources, each subject to different treatment conditions. The NLME approach estimates both population parameter distributions and their correlations, thus constraining the feasible parameter space and allowing robust parameter estimation [51, 52]. Effective ISCTs with NLME models require appropriate parameter sampling that maintains NLME‐estimated distributions, correlations, and covariates to create cohorts of plausible patients (PPs) [45, 53]. Each PP consists of a parameter vector that generates model dynamics, representing the characteristics of a hypothetical patient [6]. According to specific inclusion/exclusion criteria and their ability to reproduce biologically/clinically feasible trajectories, PPs can be discarded or selected to comprise a clinically relevant Vpop [53]. These criteria can be based on characteristics of the clinical population of interest or measurements from control/placebo arms, allowing a preliminary control arm ISCT simulation to accept/reject PPs who do not conform. Thus, a calibrated Vpop provides a realistic representation of a clinical population of interest, enabling tailored ISCT simulations and population‐level predictions of treatment effects [6]. For validation, independent datasets not used to calibrate the model can be employed to compare ISCT predictions, thereby confirming the accuracy and reliability of the simulation outcomes.

Here, we present a step‐by‐step tutorial for performing tailored ISCTs using NLME models. We illustrate these nuances for effectively conducting ISCTs with two practical examples: (1) ISCTs with a small model describing tumor dynamics during chemotherapy [54], and (2) ISCTs with a more complex mechanistic QSP model to predict treatment effects of standard‐of‐care therapies in patients chronically infected with hepatitis B virus (HBV) [55].

Workflow to Perform Virtual Trials Using NLME Models

Although guidelines to perform model‐based ISCTs have been published [45, 49, 53, 56], most of the described methods for generating virtual patients are designed for models fit to average data or separately fit to individual patient data. In contrast, NLME models are fit simultaneously across individual patients and generate population parameter distributions with inter‐parameter correlations, thus necessitating the development of suitable algorithms for generating virtual patients for ISCTs. This tutorial presents a workflow for Vpop generation and calibration in ISCTs, specifically focusing on NLME models, based on the approach proposed by Craig et al. for performing ISCTs [45] (Figure 1). The workflow details methods to constrain the parameter sampling space, assess model structure, evaluate parameter sensitivity and identifiability, and construct tailored virtual populations to effectively run ISCTs. In the following paragraphs, we describe each step of the workflow outlined in Figure 1. This step‐by‐step explanation is intended to guide users through the key considerations and practical implementation of each phase of the process.

A 7‐step workflow for performing ISCTs using NLME models. **Satisfactory results may vary depending on the specific questions being addressed, necessitating verification (ensuring the accuracy of code, results, and computational solutions), validation (evaluating model assumptions and comparing predictions with independent data and literature reports), and uncertainty quantification (assessing the uncertainty in model predictions, parameter values, and data) to ensure model credibility in the specific context of application.

The first step is creating a model. Depending on the questions to answer, data availability, and knowledge of biological pathways as well as plausible hypotheses, the model(s) can include a description of physiological mechanisms involved in disease progression and the response to treatment. Different model structures are possible, and it is important to select the best one under the constraints of data availability, biological uncertainty, and computational/numerical efficiency. Methods and metrics for model selection include context of use, identifiability and constraints in data, residual analysis, goodness‐of‐fit, parameter confidence intervals, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which are used to choose the most appropriate model capable of robustly describing the data and providing physiological insights to answer the question(s) of interest. In addition, testing the model predictions against an independent dataset or comparing predictions with reference literature values can be used to further validate a model's predictive capability. Therefore, deciding the final model structure is typically the result of an iterative process involving verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification processes outlined in this workflow.

The second step consists of model parametrization and fitting, which is closely intertwined with the model building process. As we add variables in the model reflecting biological mechanisms and drug PK/PD, incorporation of available information to constrain parameters values is critical for reducing uncertainty in model predictions. Parameter values with strong evidence from literature can be fixed, along with parameters exhibiting low sensitivity [45]. Conversely, for sensitive model parameters exhibiting inter‐patient variability, if numerical values are reported in literature, using these values as plausible initial estimates when fitting the model to new data (Bayesian initialization) can help guide parameter estimation [57, 58]. In NLME modeling, parameters are either fixed to typical values in the population (termed as fixed effect parameters) or exhibit inter‐individual variability (termed as parameters with random effects). Parameters associated with inter‐individual variability are assumed to follow a statistical distribution in the population of interest (e.g., normal, log‐normal, logit‐normal), whose characteristics, such as mean and variance, are estimated during the NLME fitting process. This framework enables the estimation of both population mean parameters as well as individual‐specific parameters, while also accounting for potential correlations [59]. Thus, in complex models, the final set of estimated population parameters, random effects and their correlations is the result of iterative rounds of model fitting with different sets of fixed, estimated and a priori estimated parameters.

During the model parameterization process, identifiability and sensitivity analysis are useful tools to establish the parameters that ought to be estimated. However, these generally require predefined parameter ranges and/or model fitting results. One approach that might be useful to establish a strategy for model parametrization is structural identifiability analysis at the outset, using a predefined set of parameters, some to be fixed and others to be estimated (Figure 1). There are two types of identifiability, practical and structural identifiability. Practical identifiability allows determining if the model parameters can be identified using the available data and therefore it requires model fitting, while structural identifiability analysis focuses on the algebraic properties of the model structure and can be assessed analytically [51, 60, 61]. In this context, it is important to note that a model with structurally non‐identifiable parameters, when fit using non‐population fitting approaches, will have practically non‐identifiable parameters, and therefore their confidence intervals will not be effectively determined [60]. However, NLME fitting approach could still allow for effective estimation of structurally non‐identifiable parameters depending on the data set and specific parameter distributions [51]. This is because in the context of NLME models, two parameters are non‐identifiable only if the estimated population distributions are the same [51]. Therefore, even if parameters are structurally non‐identifiable, they may still be practically identifiable with NLME if the data allows for the estimation of significantly distinct parameter distributions. Nevertheless, in complex models it is important to identify potentially non‐identifiable parameters within the model structure to ensure careful parameter estimation. In addition, model parameterization can be set according to multiple experimental conditions, e.g., untreated patient datasets versus treated patient datasets, to hierarchically estimate physiological disease progression parameters and differentiate them from drug effect parameters [45].

After performing model fitting and establishing appropriate parameter ranges, sensitivity analysis can effectively evaluate the impact of parameters on model variables and help in identifying parameters with random effects that are crucial for explaining variability between patients. Thus, the third step involves conducting final sensitivity and identifiability analyses to ensure that the model structure and parameterization robustly describes the data and allows the model to generate physiologically feasible biomarker trajectories. Various methods are available to perform either local or global sensitivity analysis. Typically, local sensitivity analysis is computed by the partial first derivative of the output with respect to the input parameter, commonly implemented using the first‐order finite difference approximation [45]. While local sensitivity analysis assesses the impact of small changes of one parameter on the model outputs, global sensitivity analysis allows evaluation of the impact of a parameter on the interconnected dynamics of the entire system across a broad range of values, with some algorithms also accounting for non‐linearities. Therefore, in the context of complex NLME models, global sensitivity analysis is generally recommended as a more robust method. The most popular global sensitivity analysis methods include correlation based methods such as computing the partial correlation rank coefficient (PRCC) of parameters, and variance decomposition methods [62], such as the extended Fourier amplitude sensitivity test (eFAST) and Sobol indices (Si) [62, 63]. The PRCC method and other correlation based methods are effective for mostly describing linear or monotonic non‐linear relationships between parameters and model outputs [62]. In contrast, variance decomposition methods delve deeper into both the non‐linear and linear effects of parameters on the overall system dynamics [62, 63]. However, these global sensitivity analysis methods are more computationally expensive because they require an exponentially increasing number of simulations based on the number of parameters and the number of points evaluated within the range of parameter values (numerical grid size) for convergence [64].

After sensitivity analyses, practical identifiability is usually evaluated to establish parameter estimation uncertainty. Raue et al. [60] proposed a method to assess practical identifiability by exploiting the profile likelihoods of the estimated parameters. However, in NLME models, the use of this approach is not efficient since it requires multiple rounds of population and individual fitting, which becomes computationally expensive and time‐consuming, especially for complex models. Therefore, practical identifiability in NLME models is generally evaluated by checking similarities of convergence indicators after estimating parameter values with different initial conditions, assessing the covariance matrix and/or by estimating confidence intervals of estimated parameters obtained from the inverse Fisher Matrix Information (FIM) method [51, 65]. Identifying non‐identifiable parameters helps clarify which parameters require estimation and which can be fixed in subsequent model fitting, using reported values or available biological knowledge. This approach reduces uncertainty, simplifies the model, and enhances convergence efficiency [66].

It is important to note that although reducing the number of non‐identifiable parameters can help decrease uncertainty in model predictions, gaps in knowledge or lack of specific parameter values in the literature may lead to the development of models with both identifiable and non‐identifiable parameters. One perspective exploring the use of QSP models with non‐identifiable parameters highlights their value in capturing broader inter‐individual variability among real patients and in the context of sparse quantitative knowledge of a treatment's perturbation to biological pathways [61]. Thus, models with broad biological scope with structurally/practically non‐identifiable parameters can still help to identify potential targets for new drug development and prospectively rank drug candidates, tasks that reduced models might not be able to accomplish [61, 67]. Conversely, reducing the number of unidentifiable parameters based on available data can lower prediction uncertainty [60]. Therefore, the model‐building process and decisions on which parameters to fix or estimate should be guided by the specific context and questions that the model aims to address [65].

Once the model is fit to data and population parameters are defined, the fourth step is performing parameter sampling to create a set of PPs. Since NLME models are constrained by specific distributions and correlations, maintaining these properties in large sets of parameters could be challenging using traditional random sampling methods. On one side, discrete re‐sampling techniques cannot ensure a correlation structure and collinearity between the sampled parameters. On the other side, multivariate random sampling methodologies could help to constrain parameters values while maintaining specific correlations between them; however all the variables would be assumed to follow the same distribution [68, 69]. We next propose a numerical method to generate different parameter distributions while maintaining correlation coefficients between parameters (Figure 2).

Proposed method to obtain plausible patients from NLME models. Correlation coefficients between parameter values of a NLME model may refer to Pearson, Spearman, or Kendall correlation coefficients depending on the software used to train the model. However, only Spearman and Kendall correlation coefficients can be maintained after applying the inversion method to obtain specific parameter distributions.

First, to generate correlated parameter samples, we propose using the copulas statistical method. Copulas are multivariate cumulative distribution functions that define dependence for pairs of random variables, where each variable has a marginal probability distribution in the interval [0, 1] [70, 71]. Introduced by Sklar in 1959, this concept offers a way to model the intercorrelation between random variables. The Gaussian copula, i.e., a bell‐shaped curve relationship between variables, is frequently used for representing multivariate dependence. However, various inter‐correlation patterns between parameters can also be modeled using Clayton, Frank, Gumbel, or t copulas [71]. Therefore, to generate random samples with copulas, we first need to define the intercorrelations. In general, an appropriate intercorrelation structure can be selected through visual inspection of scatter plots. In more complex scenarios, fitting the data to various copula functions and assessing their goodness of fit can guide the choice of the most suitable copula model. In the examples presented in this tutorial, the Gaussian copula closely matched the observed intercorrelations of the estimated parameters. Therefore, it was selected to generate random samples for both examples. Additionally, we must specify the sampling size (N) and the correlation coefficients (ρ) between parameters (correlation matrix of the estimated parameters). If covariates, such as patient demographics, are included in the model structure, they can be treated as additional model parameters during NLME estimation. For categorical variables, the method introduced by Tannenbaum et al., which establishes thresholds for converting continuous variables into categorical ones, can be applied [72]. Once these model elements are defined and multivariable random parameter samples are obtained, we can use population mean parameters, random effect parameters, and their respective inverse cumulative distribution functions—such as normal, lognormal, Student's t, binomial, Poisson, or other computable distributions—to convert the sampled parameters into target distributions. This transformation is carried out using the inversion method [73], resulting in a set of PPs.

The examples provided in this tutorial perform the parameter sampling accounting for parameter variability but do not include residual unexplained variability (RUV). As described by Hu [74], RUV represents intra‐individual uncertainty and measurement noise, and does not form part of the true underlying population parameter distributions quantifying inter‐patient heterogeneity. Consequently, it is intentionally excluded from the construction of confidence intervals (CIs) for population‐level decision metrics. RUV may be considered in other predictive contexts, such as sample size determination and quantifying uncertainty due to measurement noise.

Then, the fifth step is selecting the sets of sampled parameters (PPs) using acceptance/rejection criteria to ensure that the Vpop contains VPs with physiologically feasible trajectories and meets the target Vpop characteristics such as physiologically relevant dynamics and numerical stability. After this step, PPs can be subject of an extra filter or calibration step, where application of computational algorithms can allow constraining the Vpop to specific characteristics of the clinical population of interest. This can be particularly relevant when response to treatment is linked to a specific range of baseline characteristics of the population, such as stage of disease progression, virology biomarkers at start of treatment, development of treatment resistance or tolerance and other inclusion/exclusion criteria relevant to clinical trials. To incorporate these criteria a simple if—else statement might suffice in straightforward cases. However, for situations involving multiple constraints or when the Vpop must be tailored to reflect a target clinical population with specific variable distributions, more sophisticated computational algorithms may be necessary [49, 75]. These methods use the estimated probability of VP selection (probability density distribution of the data divided by the estimated plausible population density) to accept or reject sampled PPs. Therefore, information about the shape (probability density function) of the target population distribution is necessary to implement this method.

When specific information about the target variable distributions is unavailable, incorporating multiple constraints into the patient population (PP) selection process can help guide the acceptance of PPs. Cortes‐Rios et al. [55] (see Material S1) proposed an algorithm that calibrates a Vpop using clinically relevant information and model simulations, ensuring the imposed constraints are met while maximizing the number of selected PPs to enhance variability and adhere to the specified criteria (Figure 3). The method establishes a Mixed‐Integer Linear Programming (MILP) optimization framework to select PPs, where the percentage of PPs that fall within the range of values of the variable of interest (bin) is constrained to match the percentage of the target distribution in the corresponding bin. The optimization problem is defined with two parameters: the maximum matching error per bin (epsbin) and the total number of bins (Nbins). Subsequently, a multi‐objective optimization is conducted to identify the parameters epsbin and Nbins that maximize Vpop size while minimizing the total mean error of the calibrated distributions. This process aims to generate a set of optimal solutions for Vpop by minimizing the total error between the calibrated Vpop and the target discrete distribution(s) and maximizing the Vpop size (VPtotal). Importantly, this method enables the calibration of discrete distributions with or without raw data from the target distribution, as only percentages per bin of the target distribution(s) are needed. In conclusion, the selection of the method used to calibrate the Vpop essentially depends on the available information of the target clinical population.

Virtual population calibration algorithm for discrete distributions (adapted from Cortés‐Ríos et al. [55]). This algorithm consists of five main steps: (1) First, the target distribution(s), from real patient data, is used to categorize PPs within defined number of bins (Nbins) per variable. (2) The MILP optimization problem selects a subset of PPs that maximize VPtotal while satisfying the imposed constraints. Each PP (ppi) can be included (1) or discarded (0), keeping the proportion of selected PPs per bin close to predefined targets within a tolerance epsbin. (3) After VPtotal is obtained, the differences between the calibrated and target distribution(s) are computed and used to calculate the total mean error of the calibrated distribution(s). If the MILP solution is optimal, the results are saved; otherwise, a new search is initiated. (4) A multi‐objective optimization (genetic algorithm) optimizes epsbin and Nbins to maximize VPtotal and to minimize the total mean error. This process constructs a Pareto front between these two competitive objectives until a termination criterion is reached. (5) From the resulting Pareto front, a final virtual population can be selected based on the desired balance between minimizing total mean error and maximizing VPtotal.

The sixth step consists of running the ISCT and performing statistical analysis using the calibrated Vpop. For this step, it is important to check if the final Vpop size is enough to capture the inter‐patient variability and effectively estimate confidence intervals of the model predictions. Alternatively, multiple cohorts of VPs could be subject to virtual clinical trials to effectively compute prediction uncertainty. For diseases with low response rates, high sample sizes should be considered, which can make performing virtual clinical trials computationally demanding. Parallel computing could help in these cases to obtain faster results.

Lastly, the seventh step is answering questions of interest. Techniques such as bootstrapping or simple mean/median and standard deviation metrics of the ISCT simulations can be used to answer specific questions about %VPs achieving treatment response, while simulations together with pathway sensitivity analysis can help to answer questions related to mechanistic insights of the drug response. Notably, while the proposed workflow follows sequential steps, it is essential to review the results after each step and repeat previous analyses or steps if needed to achieve satisfactory outcomes. This involves model credibility assessment by performing verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification at each step of the process [4, 46, 48]. Ultimately, the number of iterations and the process duration to develop a functional model will depend on factors such as model complexity, available data, methods used, the nature of the simulations to be conducted, and the requirements for model evaluation to inform decision‐making.

Practical Tutorial: Conducting Virtual Clinical Trials Using NLME Models

Next, we describe a step‐by‐step guide on setting up and executing ISCTs with a simpler tumor dynamics model for lung cancer [54] and a more complex fit‐for‐purpose QSP model for hepatitis B virus disease progression using an NLME fitting approach [55].

Thus, the obtained sample of parameters (Parameters) follow the established distributions and correlations (Figure 4B). Note that when transforming multivariate dependent variables to a non‐normal distribution, the Pearson correlation determined during NLME may not apply. However, rank correlation coefficients, like Spearman or Kendall, are preserved. This is commonly encountered when heavy‐tailed distributions (e.g., uniform random) or normal distributions are correlated with thin‐tailed distributions (e.g., log‐normal or logit‐normal), where tail values can manifest as low linear correlation coefficients with large sample sizes. Therefore, using correlations coefficients like Spearman or Kendall should be preferred when applying this method. Multivariate dependent parameters samples with specific population values and distributions were constructed for the tumor burden model (Figure 4B). In this example, since all PPs exhibited trajectories within expected physiological ranges and there was no intention to match specific variable distributions, no PPs were rejected. Consequently, each set of sampled parameters (g, k and f) corresponds to a VP, with all VPs collectively constituting the Vpop (i.e., Parameters generated in the provided code).

To answer the first question, we look at a temporal snapshot of tumor size trajectories after 18 weeks of treatment or placebo (Figure 5B), where we observe a median normalized tumor size of 1.18 for the control arm, versus a median of 0.99 for the treatment arm. Therefore, the predicted percentage of median tumor size decrease under chemotherapy compared with no treatment for the studied population is around 16% 1.18−0.991.18×100. Since we enrolled the same virtual patients in each arm, we can also compute the individual percentages of decrease under chemotherapy compared with no treatment by subtracting the normalized tumor diameter of the control arm and the treatment arm for each VP at 18 weeks. This gives us the opportunity of computing uncertainty of the prediction, obtaining a median of 16.9% with 90% confidence interval (CI) of [0.8%–76.7%]. This confidence interval was computed by estimating the 5th and 95th percentiles of the simulated virtual patients' trajectories at the end of the treatment period. Alternatively, bootstrapping of the ISCT simulation results or multiple cohorts of virtual patients can also be used to obtain CIs.

The second question can be addressed by establishing a threshold for the tumor size that can be detected. Assuming a minimum tumor size detection limit of ~7 mm in modern clinical PET scanners [77] and an approximate tumor size at baseline of 5 cm for the studied population, we obtained a detection threshold of ~14% from the baseline tumor size 0.75×100. Thus, the virtual patients with baseline‐normalized tumor diameter < 0.14 are defined as responders to chemotherapy, and this percentage at 6, 12 and 18 weeks can be computed. Since responders are relatively rare, we need a large Vpop to obtain an acceptable range for confidence intervals. In this case, we applied bootstrap to compute the CIs, sampling 1000 virtual patients 100 times from the constructed Vpop to calculate the percentage of responders. Thus, the median and the 5th–95th percentiles of the computed values allow us to obtain the median percentage and 90% CIs of VPs with complete response, which were 0.6% [0.3–1.1] at 6 weeks, 1.6% [1–2.5] at 12 weeks, and 2.5% [1.8–3.3] at 18 weeks (Figure 5C). Finally, if information is available, it is important to compare the predicted percentages with actual values. In this case, a study evaluating the effect of platinum‐doublet chemotherapy in patients with non‐small cell lung cancer reported a complete response rate of 2.2% [95% CI: 0.6%—5.6%] after 9 weeks of treatment (Q3W, three cycles) and surgery, with optional adjuvant chemotherapy (up to 4 cycles) thereafter [78]. Although not directly comparable, this result provides a useful benchmark, indicating that our estimation of treatment response at 18 weeks is within a plausible range. However, a more closely aligned clinical study would be necessary to rigorously validate the model's predictive accuracy.

Hepatitis B Virus Infection Model

Next, a mechanistic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection clinical model comprising 11 ordinary differential equations describing disease progression (including virus lifecycle and host‐immune responses) and effects of standard‐of‐care treatments—nucleos(t)ide analogues (NA) and pegylated interferon (IFN)—is used to illustrate the workflow for performing ISCT with more complex models. The model was calibrated in Monolix 2023R1 (Lixoft SAS, a Simulation Plus company) and contains a total of 30 parameters, 22 fixed and 8 estimated using NLME from the individual‐level dataset of 386 patients. In this case, we are using this QSP model to explain the mechanistic relationship between serum hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) at baseline and functional cure (FC)—defined as suppression of two serum virological biomarkers, HBV DNA and HBsAg, below detection for at least 24 weeks post‐treatment—with standard‐of‐care therapies. To include inter‐patient variability and actual patient characteristics, we use ISCT simulations inspired by a real‐world clinical trial (the Everest study [79, 80]) to differentiate between responders and non‐responders to standard‐of‐care therapies and elucidate the physiological mechanisms that explain the relationship between baseline HBsAg and FC. Following parameter estimation, 100,000 PPs were generated, maintaining estimated correlation coefficients between sampled parameters. This large Vpop sample size is intended to ensure enough VPs to accurately compute the prediction uncertainty of the rate of response to standard‐of‐care therapy, which, in the case of nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs), typically falls within 0%–3% [81, 82]. Accordingly, with the methodology described in Figure 2, parameter sampling was performed using the copularnd function of the Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox of MATLAB [76], and the inverse transformation method was employed to retain parameter distributions and correlations across VPs.

With complex models, a rejection/acceptance procedure before performing ISCT could be necessary to discard parameters that generate model variable values out of plausible physiological ranges or present numerical integration problems. Generally, this can be done by running simulations with all the PPs and discarding or accepting them according to a prespecified criterion. In this example, the HBsAg baseline distribution of the Everest clinical trial (NCT04035837) was used as a target population distribution to calibrate the HBsAg baseline distribution of the Vpop. Post‐calibration, we achieved similar parameter distributions and correlations (Figure 7B), and an exact match between the HBsAg baseline distributions of the Vpop and the Everest clinical trial population was obtained (Figure 7C). The final size of the Vpop was 32,000 VPs, which represents approximately one‐third of the initially sampled size of PPs. Furthermore, due to the imposed constraints on HBsAg baseline values, the distribution of HBsAg at baseline shifted from an almost symmetric distribution to a negatively skewed distribution representing clinical observations (Figure 7D).

Once the Vpop was established, it was used to conduct an ISCT that mimicked the Everest trial (Figure 8A). The Vpop was subdivided into 5 cohorts of 6400 VPs each that were randomly assigned between two 48‐week treatment arms in a proportion rate of 20/80 for “switch to” and “add on” IFN treatments. Since the subjects enrolled in the Everest trial were NA‐suppressed patients (> 1 year under NA treatment), the ISCT included a 4‐year period of NA treatment before starting the trial. Subsequently, the rate of VPs experiencing HBsAg below the quantification limit (HBsAg loss) at the end of treatment, as well as the FC 6 months post‐treatment, was quantified and HBsAg loss and FC rates for the 5 cohorts stratified by HBsAg at baseline are shown in Figure 8B. The ISCT results reproduce the inverse relationship between HBsAg at baseline and HBsAg loss (tightly correlated with FC) observed in the preliminary results of the Everest trial (Figure 8C). ISCT simulation results identified differences in immune responses between responders and non‐responders as key drivers of FC, a hypothesis emerging from this analysis (see mechanistic details in Cortés‐Ríos et al. [55], Material S1). In summary, this example shows the utility of ISCT to predict and understand physiological mechanisms underlying drug response in populations with high inter‐patient variability of treatment responses.

Conclusions

The workflow for conducting ISCT using NLME models presented in this tutorial provides a detailed, step‐by‐step explanation of both standard and newly developed methods. Applied examples include a simple tumor dynamics model and a more complex Hepatitis B QSP model, with the latter aimed at predicting and understanding the mechanisms of treatment response. However, the scope of this ISCT approach extends beyond these examples, providing opportunities to optimize dosing regimens and clinical trials, select biomarkers, discover new therapeutic targets, and stratify patients for more personalized treatment strategies. Consequently, the methods and considerations outlined have the potential to significantly influence multiple stages of the drug development process and advances in personalized medicine.
