Explainable machine learning prediction of functional independence measure scores and gain in subacute stroke survivors
Authors: Yuta Miyazaki, Junna Oba, Tetsuo Ishikawa, Michiyuki Kawakami, Kunitsugu Kondo, Akiko Hirabe, Masahiro Tsujikawa, Kaoru Honaga, A Gordon Robertson, Scott E Woodman, Tetsuya Tsuji

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional regression models struggle with non-linear data, limiting their ability to predict health outcomes such as activities of daily living (ADL) in stroke survivors. Machine learning (ML) methods offer improved accuracy but often lack interpretability. Background

In recent years, many studies have used machine learning (ML) to predict Activities of Daily Living (ADL) outcomes in stroke survivors [1]. The growing trend can be attributed to the limitations of multiple regression analysis, which has traditionally been employed in this field. Since multiple regression analysis assumes linear relationships, its predictive performance often plateaus when applied to complex, non-linear clinical data [2]. In contrast, ML is better suited to capture non-linear patterns, offering improved predictive accuracy over conventional regression approaches [3].

Despite these advantages, many ML algorithms are criticized for their “black box” problems, as the learned models are often difficult for clinicians to interpret [4]. This lack of interpretability can be especially problematic in clinical settings, where understanding the rationale behind predictions is crucial.

Traditional methods, such as multiple regression and decision trees, provide interpretable predictive models that are helpful in identifying prognostic factors, although they may be less accurate than ML. In fact, some studies have preferred multiple regression analysis over high-accuracy ML approaches to focus on the interpretability of prognostic factors in stroke outcome models [5]. Thus, there has historically been a trade-off between improving predictive accuracy with ML and retaining interpretability to explore prognostic factors using more transparent approaches such as multiple regression or decision trees.

Recently, explainable machine learning (XML) methods have emerged, offering the ability to analyze non-linear data while maintaining model interpretability [6]. XML enables both high predictive accuracy and the exploration of important prognostic factors. Among these, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) has become prominent for quantifying the contribution of each explanatory variable within predictive models [7]. By leveraging SHAP values, researchers can both maintain high accuracy on non-linear data and identify clinically meaningful prognostic factors, thereby mitigating the “black-box” concern.

In this study, we hypothesized that SHAP-based XML would facilitate the development of robust ADL outcome predictive models for subacute stroke survivors while revealing key prognostic indicators. Furthermore, we aimed to compare these identified factors with those reported in previous studies, focusing on key predictors of functional improvement of post-stroke rehabilitation. Therefore, the aim of this study was to develop XML-based prediction models that provide transparency in clinical decision-making. Rather than solely focusing on maximizing accuracy, we sought to demonstrate how XML can reveal the relative importance and non-linear effects of diverse prognostic factors, thereby offering more nuanced understandings of functional recovery than traditional linear-based or aggregate-based models.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective, cross-sectional, single-center study aimed to predict the outcomes of inpatient rehabilitation in subacute stroke survivors. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [8] and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tokyo Bay Rehabilitation Hospital (approval number 267-7). This study was not pre-registered in a public registry; however, the study was conducted based on a research protocol approved by the Ethics Committee. Tokyo Bay Rehabilitation Hospital is a specialized facility providing inpatient rehabilitation to stroke survivors in the subacute phase [9], transferred from acute care hospitals.

Participants

This study included subacute stroke survivors admitted to our hospital between March 2015 and September 2019. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) history of stroke, (2) transfer to the rehabilitation hospital more than 90 days after stroke onset, (3) hospital stay of less than 28 days or more than 180 days, (4) transfer back to an acute care hospital, (5) missing data, and (6) refusal to participate [5]. All patients received a standard rehabilitation program comprising two to three hours per day of physical, occupational, and speech therapy. Given the retrospective design and use of anonymized data, the ethics committee waived the requirement for informed consent. An opt-out procedure was employed, with study details published on the hospital’s website to enable participants to withdraw.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was determined to ensure model stability and avoid overfitting. While a minimum of 10 events per predictor (EPP) has been traditionally recommended [10], more recent studies suggested that an EPP of up to 23 may be necessary to ensure low optimism in model performance [11]. Given our 33 candidate predictors, we adopted the more conservative EPP of 23, resulting in a minimum required sample size of 759 cases.

Dataset

Clinical variables previously reported as predictors of post-stroke rehabilitation outcomes in systematic reviews and multiple regression analyses [5, 12] were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical records. These variables included age, sex, onset-admission interval, stroke type, Functional Independence Measure (FIM) [14] subscores at admission and discharge, Stroke Impairment Assessment Set (SIAS) scores [13], bilateral grip strength, height, weight, and serum albumin level (Alb). The FIM is one of the ADL measures and consists of 13 motor and five cognitive subitems [14, 15]. Trained therapists and nurses evaluated the FIM using the Japanese version at both admission and discharge. FIM gain, defined as the difference between the total FIM score at admission and discharge, served as an indicator of improvements in ADL during rehabilitation [16]. SIAS assesses motor function at affected and unaffected sides, trunk function, and quadriceps strength. Physicians and therapists assessed SIAS and grip strength at admission. Nutritional indices such as Body Mass Index (BMI) and the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) [17] were calculated.

Model development and statistical analysis

In this study, we employed six machine learning algorithms, including Elastic Net [18], Extra Trees [19], LightGBM [20], Random Forest [21], XGBoost [22], and the combination of these approaches (Mixed) to perform prognostic prediction. Elastic Net, a regularized form of multiple regression, was included as a linear regression-based baseline model. Given the relatively large number of explanatory variables and their potential correlations, Elastic Net was used to mitigate multicollinearity and improve the stability of regression estimates by combining L1 and L2 penalties. LightGBM and XGBoost belong to a similar family of algorithms, both integrating decision trees with gradient boosting techniques. However, XGBoost employs a level-wise training approach, resulting in higher computational complexity, whereas LightGBM adopts a leaf-wise approach, offering the advantage of reduced computational burden. On the other hand, Extra Trees and Random Forest are ensemble learning algorithms based on decision trees, differing significantly in feature selection methods and split point determination. Specifically, in feature selection, Extra Trees randomly selects features at each node, while Random Forest identifies the optimal split at each node. Regarding split points, Extra Trees chooses them randomly, whereas Random Forest searches for the optimal split point for the selected feature, leading to greater computational demand. Although these algorithms vary in computational complexity, we sought to identify algorithms that exhibited superior predictive accuracy.

We set six functional outcomes as the target variables: FIM motor scores, FIM cognitive scores, FIM total scores at discharge, FIM motor gain, FIM cognitive gain, and FIM total gain. Explanatory variables included sex, age, days since onset, FIM subitems, SIAS items, bilateral grip strength, nutritional indices (BMI, GNRI), and stroke type (hemorrhagic, ischemic, or subarachnoid).

The study cohort of 980 cases was randomly divided into an 80% development dataset and a 20% test set (Figure S1). Model training was conducted using the development dataset, and evaluation was performed using the holdout method on the test set, which was not used during training.

EvalML (Alteryx, Inc.) is an Automated Machine Learning (AutoML) program designed to automatically explore combinations of pipelines (preprocessing and algorithms) by training various supervised learning models to identify those with high predictive accuracy. In this study, we developed models for the six outcomes using repeated splitting to ensure stable performance evaluation. Specifically, the development dataset was divided 1,000 times with varying seeds, allocating 75% to the training set and 25% to the validation set (Figure S1). For each seed, pipelines were trained using EvalML. Within the training set, we constructed K ensemble pipelines (where K ranged from 1 to 1,000) for each algorithm (Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, Elastic Net, Extra Trees, and their combination). The optimal value of K was determined based on the R² score (coefficient of determination) as the predictive accuracy metric in the validation set, with K = 100 ultimately selected. Predictive accuracy in the test set was then evaluated using the R² score and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE). The R² score is frequently reported in prognostic stroke studies, while RMSE is a widely used evaluation metric in machine learning.

For each ensembled model, feature importance was calculated and visualized as the mean absolute SHAP value of each explanatory variable to address the “black box” problem commonly associated with ML. SHAP is an XAI technique that quantifies the contribution of each feature (explanatory variable) to the prediction of an outcome, providing insight into the decision-making basis of ML models. We computed the mean absolute SHAP values across the patient dimension and generated bar plots (presented in supplementary figures). Additionally, these mean absolute SHAP values were normalized by dividing by the maximum value for each outcome and visualized as heatmaps.

The models were developed using Python version 3.10.13, and the model accuracy was evaluated and analyzed using R version 4.3.

Results

In this study, we established a prognostic prediction model for subacute stroke survivors using XML.

Participants characteristics

A total of 1,552 stroke survivors were admitted during the study period, and retrospective data analysis was conducted. The following cases were excluded: (1) 289 cases with a history of stroke, (2) 9 cases transferred to the rehabilitation hospital more than 90 days after stroke onset, (3) 105 cases with a rehabilitation hospital stay of less than 28 days or more than 180 days, (4) 103 cases transferred to an acute care hospital, and (5) 66 cases with missing data. Consequently, 980 cases were included in this study (Fig. 1, Table 1).

Performance of predictive models for functional outcomes

The performance of the predictive models for functional outcomes is summarized below (Table 2). For the discharge FIM motor score, the XGBoost achieved the highest prediction accuracy, with an R² of 0.776 and an RMSE of 9.21. For the discharge FIM cognitive score, the LightGBM showed the best performance (R² = 0.630, RMSE = 4.26). Regarding the discharge FIM total score, the XGBoost model again demonstrated the highest accuracy (R² = 0.758, RMSE = 12.36). For FIM gain, the highest prediction accuracy for the FIM motor gain was obtained using the Mixed model (R² = 0.604, RMSE = 9.2), whereas, for the FIM cognitive gain, the XGBoost showed the best performance (R² = 0.413, RMSE = 4.33). Finally, for the FIM total gain, the Mixed model achieved the highest accuracy (R² = 0.536, RMSE = 12.21).

SHAP values of explanatory variables

In the heatmap (Fig. 2), variables such as admission FIM bowel management and admission FIM toilet transfer ranked high in terms of variable importance for the discharge FIM motor score. For the discharge FIM cognitive score, the variable importance of FIM cognitive items at admission (problem solving, memory, comprehension) was notably high, particularly in the LightGBM model. Regarding the discharge FIM total score, variables including admission FIM bladder management, admission FIM bowel management, age, and admission FIM grooming exhibited a tendency toward higher SHAP values. For FIM cognitive gain, the variable importance of age and FIM cognitive items at admission (problem solving, memory, comprehension, expression) was consistently high across all pipelines. For FIM motor gain and total gain, variables such as age, admission FIM lower dressing, and admission FIM toileting showed a tendency toward higher SHAP values, while in the Elastic Net model, the SIAS verticality test, which assesses the trunk function, exhibited a high SHAP value. Additionally, in the dendrogram of explanatory variables, variables related to similar functions tended to cluster together. For instance, the FIM cognitive items—problem solving, memory, and comprehension—were observed to group closely adjacent to one another. Overall, the results suggest that, across the different outcomes, the important explanatory variables did not vary significantly between pipelines and were consistent to some extent.

Discussion

In this retrospective single-center study, we developed SHAP-based XML models to predict discharge FIM scores and gains in subacute stroke survivors, thereby overcoming the “black box” problem of conventional ML. By incorporating SHAP values, our approach both improved predictive accuracy and yielded clinically interpretable insights.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to elucidate the importance of explanatory variables used in XML for prognostic prediction of total FIM scores at discharge and FIM gain in subacute stroke survivors. Admission FIM bowel management was the strongest predictor of FIM motor and total scores at discharge. For FIM motor and total gain, age, lower body dressing, and toileting exhibited high SHAP values. For discharge FIM cognitive score and cognitive gain, age and cognitive-related FIM subitems at admission were identified as critical predictors.

A systematic review of ADL prognosis prediction in stroke survivors identified age, discharge FIM total score, and NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) as key prognostic factors [12]. However, this review did not mention the importance of detailed ADL prognosis predictions, such as FIM subitems, making it challenging to interpret their prognostic value given the limited number of studies. Rasch analysis is one method of interpreting FIM subitems because it enables ranking based on difficulty level [23]. By mapping our SHAP-derived importances onto a Rasch-based difficulty hierarchy, we observed that predictors naturally clustered into low-difficulty FIM motor subitems (e.g., Eating, Bladder management), high-difficulty FIM motor subitems (e.g., Stairs, Bathing), FIM cognitive subitems, motor function measures, and participants’ background characteristics. This consistency likely reflects the data-driven nature of both approaches. Since the Rasch hierarchy itself is established through large-sample statistical modeling, the agreement suggests that our XML models successfully captured the existing psychometric structure of the FIM. Moreover, this demonstrates that our XML models learned these clinically plausible relationships and structural properties without predefined inter-variable interaction, thus combining high accuracy with clinical and psychometric validity.

Low-difficulty FIM motor subitems strongly predicted discharge FIM motor and total scores, reflecting baseline ADL capacity in patients with severe hemiplegia. Poor performance on these subitems at admission reliably predicted lower discharge ADL levels. Conversely, high-difficulty FIM motor subitems were more important for predicting FIM motor and total gain, suggesting that the ability to perform more challenging tasks reflects rehabilitation effectiveness and greater functional improvement. Cognitive subitems were highly important for predicting discharge FIM cognitive scores and gains, which is expected due to their direct relevance. Notably, cognitive subitems contributed less to FIM total gain than to discharge FIM total scores, indicating that they influence discharge status more strongly than functional improvement. This may be explained by a ceiling effect, whereby participants with mild cognitive impairment at admission demonstrate smaller FIM gains, yielding values compared to those of more severely impaired participants with limited recovery. As a result, cognitive subitems appeared more strongly associated with discharge FIM scores.

Regarding motor function, trunk function (SIAS verticality test) and grip strength on the unaffected side emerged as significant predictors for discharge FIM motor score, total score, motor gain, and total gain, corroborating prior findings on trunk function’s central role in stroke recovery [5, 12]. Notably, motor function on the affected side consistently exhibited lower feature importance to predict ADL outcomes.

Among other patient background indicators, age and the onset-admission interval were unfavorable prognostic factors, consistent with findings from previous studies using multiple regression analyses [5, 12]. In contrast, nutritional indicators such as GNRI and BMI were less influential than clinical variables like admission FIM subitems and age.

The SHAP-based XML model in our study effectively identified clinically relevant relationships among predictors without explicit expert knowledge about their relationships during training. For example, the models selected predictors in accordance with the clinical difficulty hierarchy of FIM subitems, despite not being informed of this hierarchy. This finding supports the capability of XML models to uncover inherent patterns in clinical data, ensuring both high predictive accuracy and clinical validity.

XML-based approaches can overcome the “black box” problems of conventional MLs, enabling the development of clinically interpretable ADL outcome prediction models and the identification of prognostic factors using SHAP values. Whereas multiple regression analysis may be constrained by assumptions of linearity and lose accuracy with nonlinear datasets [2], XML models effectively capture complex relationships within such datasets. Consistent with the previous multiple regression study that identified trunk function, low-difficulty motor subitems, and age as key predictors of discharge FIM motor scores [5], our XML models similarly highlighted these factors. Moreover, while low-difficulty motor subitems were significant predictors for total scores, high-difficulty motor subitems were critical for FIM gain, reflecting distinct clinical characteristics based on target outcomes. Thus, these findings indicate that XML offers actionable insights for real-world clinical practice.

We employed a heatmap to present SHAP values across algorithms and outcomes to visualize the multidimensional data. Unlike bar plots, which have been used to visualize mean absolute SHAP values, the heatmap offers an intuitive and efficient overview of variable importance.

The predictive performance of our XML models exceeded that of conventional multiple regression for predicting ADL outcomes in subacute stroke survivors. Discharge FIM motor, cognitive, and total scores were predicted with R² values of 0.776, 0.630, and 0.758, respectively, outperforming the mean R² of 0.65 (range: 0.35–0.82) reported in the previous review [12]. Although the previous ML study achieved slightly higher R² values (0.81, 0.74, and 0.80) [3], this likely reflects the use of only age and admission aggregate FIM scores as primary predictors. Aggregate scores have a dominant statistical relationship with discharge FIM outcomes, allowing parsimonious models to capture variance efficiently. Models based on a small number of highly predictive variables may capture this dominant relationship efficiently and thus achieve high accuracy. In contrast, our study utilized 18 granular FIM sub-items and various physical function scores (SIAS). While incorporating a larger number of clinical variables can increase model complexity and may introduce redundancy or noise—reflecting a well-known bias-variance trade-off [24]—this approach is essential for identifying modifiable prognostic factors. The contribution of this study lies not in maximizing predictive accuracy, but in achieving comparable performance while substantially enhancing interpretability through SHAP-based XML, enabling a more detailed examination of clinically relevant prognostic factors.

For the prediction of FIM gain, our study achieved R² values of 0.604 for FIM motor gain, 0.413 for FIM cognitive gain, and 0.536 for FIM total gain. These results indicate substantial improvements compared to the mean R² of 0.22 (range: 0.08–0.40) reported in the previous systematic review on FIM gain prediction models [12]. Additionally, previous ML study utilizing only patient background and total FIM scores at admission reported R² values of 0.50, 0.39, and 0.54 for FIM motor, cognitive, and total gain, respectively [3]. Thus, our findings demonstrate enhanced predictive accuracy, particularly for FIM motor gain. The use of boosting methods such as XGBoost [22], as well as hyperparameter tuning and optimizing preprocessing combinations using EvalML and repeated splitting approach, likely contributed to the enhanced performance observed in our study.

In this study, the RMSE values ranged from 9.20 to 9.67 for discharge FIM motor score and gain, 4.26 to 4.50 for discharge FIM cognitive score and gain, and 12.21 to 12.88 for discharge FIM total score and gain. A systematic review of prognostic prediction in post-stroke rehabilitation reported that the RMSE for FIM prediction in previous studies ranged from 16.32 to 18.78 [25]. These comparisons suggest that the predictive accuracy of our models may be superior to that reported in previous studies. However, direct comparison of RMSE values across studies should be interpreted with caution, as prior reports varied in outcome definitions, FIM domains, prediction horizons, and study populations.

The variation in predictive accuracy across algorithms and outcomes suggests that the optimal method for extracting useful information from explanatory variables depends on the specific nature of the target outcome. Upon closer examination of the R2 values, Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT) algorithms, such as XGBoost and LightGBM, as well as the Mixed model, outperformed Elastic Net, which served as the regularized linear regression baseline in this study. This superiority is likely attributable to the higher model capacity of boosting algorithms and their ensemble combinations in capturing complex, non-linear interactions among the 33 granular clinical variables—relationships that may be overlooked by linear models. Furthermore, while utilizing individual FIM sub-items and SIAS components increases the complexity of the feature space compared to using aggregate scores (as in the previous study 3), this approach provides the clinical granularity necessary for identifying specific prognostic factors through SHAP-based interpretability.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although our models established relatively high predictive accuracy, this was a single-center retrospective study. Consequently, potential selection bias due to missing data and limitations regarding external generalizability remain. Therefore, caution is required when applying these results to real-world clinical settings. Second, although we applied XML techniques, we did not evaluate other advanced algorithms, such as deep learning, which may offer further improvements. Third, the prediction of discharge-based outcomes does not correspond to a fixed time horizon, which may limit interpretability of the predicted values, because discharge timing varied across participants.

Conclusion

In conclusion, XML-based models demonstrated high accuracy and interpretability in predicting ADL outcomes in subacute stroke survivors. Maximum R² values for discharge FIM motor, cognitive, and total scores were 0.776, 0.630, and 0.758, respectively. Those for FIM gain were 0.604, 0.413, and 0.536, respectively. The XML models demonstrated improved predictive accuracy compared to conventional multiple regression models, with particularly substantial improvements observed for FIM gain. Remarkably, without predefining variable interaction, XML discerned clinically plausible inter-variable relationships, such as FIM difficulties. These findings support the utility of XML as a valuable tool for both accurately predicting ADL outcomes in subacute stroke patients and identifying key prognostic factors, thereby providing practical insights for post-stroke rehabilitation.
