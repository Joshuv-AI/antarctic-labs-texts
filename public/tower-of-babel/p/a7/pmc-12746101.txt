Structurally related phage helicases trigger type III-A3 retron-mediated anti-phage defense across diverse tailed phage families
Authors: Fernando M García-Rodríguez, Francisco Martínez-Abarca, Alejandro González-Delgado, David J Wen, Seth L Shipman, Max E Wilkinson, Nicolás Toro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRetrons are tripartite bacterial systems composed of non-coding RNA (ncRNA), reverse transcriptase (RT), and effector proteins with diverse enzymatic domains. Here, we characterized Retron-Eco11, a type III-A3 retron associated with a phosphoribosyltransferase-like effector, and demonstrated that it mediates anti-phage defense and can be harnessed for genome editing. All three components—ncRNA, RT, and effector—are essential for defense. Retron-Eco11 protects Escherichia coli against multiple phages and is specifically activated by structurally related, phage-encoded helicases, including UvsW and D10 from phages T4 and T5. We show that these helicases trigger effector-mediated toxicity, leading to abortive infection, which is associated with phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate depletion due to PRTase activation, thereby altering nucleotide metabolism. UvsW interacts directly with multicopy single-stranded DNA, and effector protein activation requires the catalytic activity of phage-encoded helicases. Our data indicate that activation occurs without detectable effector release or measurable changes in the composition of the Retron complex. Beyond its defensive role, Retron-Eco11 enables targeted genome editing in bacterial and eukaryotic cells, underscoring the biological relevance and biotechnological potential of type III-A3 retrons.

Introduction

Bacterial retrons are tripartite systems composed of a cognate non-coding RNA (ncRNA), a reverse transcriptase (RT), and an additional (effector) protein or an RT-fused domain with an extraordinarily diverse range of predicted enzymatic functions [1–5]. Retrons are currently classified into 13 types on the basis of these three components, including additional variants [3]. Retron RTs use part of the ncRNA (msdRNA) as a template for multicopy single-stranded DNA (msDNA) synthesis, typically forming a DNA–RNA hybrid containing a 2′–5′ phosphodiester bond between the branching G of the RNA (msrRNA) component and the first nucleotide of the complementary DNA (cDNA) [6–10].

Although retrons were discovered over 40 years ago, their biological function remained elusive until recently. They have been reported to function as anti-phage defense systems [1, 2], employing an abortive infection (Abi) strategy. In addition, at least some retrons encode tripartite DNA-regulated toxin–antitoxin systems in which the RT and msDNA form an antitoxin unit that binds directly to the effector-associated protein, preventing its toxicity [4]. The retron defense system recognizes phage infection, possibly through the DNA stem loop of the msDNA [5], triggering activation of the effector protein via an unknown mechanism. The recently determined cryo-electron microscopy structure of Ec86 (Retron-Eco1: type II-A3, the first retron discovered in Escherichia coli) [5] has provided direct evidence that the effector protein stably associates with the other two components (msDNA and RT) of the Retron complex, forming a supramolecular assembly known as the retron-effector filament [11, 12].

Despite recent advances in retron biology, the mechanisms by which the Retron complex senses and recognizes invading phages, modifying the retron-associated effectors, remain unclear. We investigated a novel type III-A3 retron system, Retron-Eco11, which is associated with a phosphoribosyltransferase (PRTase)-like effector protein. We aimed to elucidate the role of Retron-Eco11 in anti-phage defense and explore its potential as a precise genome-editing tool. We identified the structural and functional counterparts of UvsW and D10 helicases from phages T4 and T5, respectively, as specific triggers of the Retron-Eco11 system. UvsW binds directly to msDNA, while effector protein activation depends on the catalytic activity of phage-encoded helicases. This activation results in phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP) depletion, potentially limiting nucleotide availability and leading to an abortive infection. Together, our results reveal both the defensive function and biotechnological potential of type III-A3 retrons.

Materials and methods

Bacteria, phage strains, and growth conditions

Phages T3, T5, T7, λ, M13, Qβ, and ϕX174, as well as E. coli strains LE 392, Hfr 3000 U 432, MG1655, B, and BW25113, were obtained from the DSMZ-German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH. Phages T2, T4, P1, MS2, and ϕV-1 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. The BASEL collection (Supplementary Table S1) [13], which consists of 68 newly isolated phages infecting the model organism E. coli, was obtained from Dr Alexander Harms.

E. coli strain MG1655 was used as a host for phages T2, T4, T5, T7, P1, and ϕV-1. For phages M13, MS2, Qβ, and ϕX174, strain Hfr 3000 U 432 was used as the host. E. coli K-12 MG1655 ΔRM was employed for assays with phages from the BASEL collection. E. coli strain LE392 was used for λ phage assays, and E. coli B was used for T3 phage assays. The antibiotics used in all experiments were ampicillin (100 μg/ml) and chloramphenicol (200 μg/ml).

Plasmid construction

Retron-Eco11 from E. coli EC2608, along with other type IIIA3 retrons, was synthesized by GenScript Corp. and provided cloned into pUC57mini to generate the corresponding ampicillin-resistant p57mA3 plasmid derivatives. Mutants of the Retron-Eco11 targeting the PRTase catalytic site, the ncRNA branching G, the RT catalytic site, and the Y405A and Y406A substitution in the PRTase were also obtained from GenScript and delivered cloned into pUC57mini as p57mA3_PRTase, p57mA3_C, p57mA3_RT, and p57mA3_AA, respectively. RT-Eco11 was C-terminally tagged with a 3×FLAG tag using the NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly kit (New England BioLabs Inc., NEB). Plasmids were digested with the MscI restriction enzyme, and the following oligomers were used for assembly: 3C-3xFlag-5′ (5′-GTAAATATAATGAGATTAATTGGCCATTTCTAGAGGTTCTCTTTCAAGGGCCAGCAGACTACAAAGACCATGACGGTGATTATAAAGATCATGACATCGATTACAAGGATGACGATGACAAGTGATTTTTGGGTGTTGTATAAGTG-3′) and 3C-3xFlag-3′ (5′-CACTTATACAACACCCAAAAATCACTTGTCATCGTCATCCTTGTAATCGATGTCATGATCTTTATAATCACCGTCATGGTCTTTGTAGTCTGCTGGCCCTTGAAAGAGAACCTCTAGAAATGGCCAATTAATCTCATTATATTTAC-3′). Cloning was performed according to manufacturer’s instructions to generate p57mA3-RTCF and p57mA3mRTCF, the latter containing a mutation in the catalytic site of the RT that alters the YSDD motif to YSAA.

For complex purification, the Retron-Eco11 operon was amplified from p57mA3-RTCF with primers 3A3_PRTase_F (5′- TGTTTAACTTTAAGAAGGAGATATACATGAGCTCTCATGAAATAACATTAGATAAG-3′) and 3C-RT_R (5′- TGCGGATGGCTCCAGCTAGCTGCTGGCCCTTGAAAGAGAA-3′) and subcloned with NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly into a modified pET45b (+) vector such that the RT contained a C-terminal TwinStrep tag (WSHPQFEKGGGSGGGSGGSAWSHPQFEK), generating pMW261.

Phage helicases UvsW and D10 were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and cloned into the pBAD33 vector under the control of an arabinose-inducible promoter to generate the p33UvsWwt and p33D10wt plasmids. The uvsW gene was amplified with primers uvsW5’ (5′-CTGGTACCATGGATATTAAAGTACATTTTCACG-3′) and uvsW3’ (5′-CTGTCGACTTAAAAGCTTTCTTCTACTTCATC-3′), with total DNA extracted from T4 phage serving as the template. The D10 helicase was amplified from total DNA isolated from phage T5, with the primers D10-5′ (5′-CTGGTACCATGAAGGTTGTTATATCTAATAAAG-3′) and D10-3′ (5′-CTGTCGACTTATGAGCTGTTGCCAAATGC-3′). For p33UvsWmut6, the mutant uvsW gene was amplified with primers uvsW5′ and uvsW3′ from total DNA isolated from the T4 mutant 6 phage and subsequently cloned into the pBAD33 vector. The p33D10mut13 plasmid was constructed in a similar manner, with primers D10-5′ and D10-3′ used to amplify the mutant D10 gene from T5 mutant 13 phage DNA. The K141R substitution in UvsW was generated by overlapping PCR using a two set of primers: uvsW5’ and K141R Rv (5′-TGAATTAAAGATCTACCTGCAGATG-3′) for the first PCR and uvsW3’S (5′-CAAGCTTGCATGCCTGCAGG-3′) and K141R Fw (5′-CATCTGCAGGTAGATCTTTAATTCA-3′) for the second PCR. In both reactions, p33uvsWwt served as the template. A third PCR was then performed with the uvsW5’ and uvsW3’S primers, using 1 μl of the first and second PCRs as the template. The D10 R389N mutant was synthesized by GenScript Corp. C-terminally 3xFLAG-tagged UvsW, UvsW K141R, D10, and D10 R389N were synthesized by GenScript Corp., resulting in plasmids p33uvsW3F, p33uvsWk3F, p33D10-CF, and p33D10R-CF.

The PRTase gene was amplified using primers PRTaseWH Fw (5′-CTGGTACCATGAGCTCTCATGAAATAACATT-3′) and PRTaseWH Rv (5′-CTGTCGACTTATGGCCTCCTCAACGTTG-3′), with p57mA3 serving as the template. The resulting PCR product was cloned between the KpnI and SalI sites of pBAD30 to yield p30PRTase. C-terminal 3xFLAG-tagged PRTase (p30PRTase-CF) was synthesized by GenScript using the p30PRTase construct.

Constructs p30nc3 and p30nc4 were generated by amplifying the fragment containing the ncRNA-RT from Retron-Eco11 wild type and from Retron-Eco11 carrying a mutation in the catalytic YSDD motif of the RT. Amplifications were performed using primers ncRNA A3 (5′-CTGGTACCTAATGTTTCATTTAGCGTTAACAC-3′) and A3RTRv (5′-CTGTCGACCTAAAATGGCCAATTAATCTCA-3′). The resulting PCR fragments were cloned into the KpnI-SalI sites of the pBAD30 vector.

Plasmids used in the bacterial recombineering assay were constructed in the pET-21(+) (Novagen) backbone under the control of T7/lac inducible promoters. Wild-type Retron-Eco11 was synthesized by Twist Biosciences HQ. Plasmids containing Retron-Eco1 (pAGD111), Retron-Sen3 (pSLS.618), and Retron-Kpn1 (pSLS.619) were obtained from a previous study [14]. CspRecT and mutLE32K required for the recombineering process were expressed from pORTMAGE-Ec1 plasmid, which was generated previously [14]. Back-to-back primers used to clone the 70 bp rpoB editing donor (S512P) into different positions of the DSLb stem are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Following PCR with these primers, a KLD reaction (NEB) was used to generate the different plasmids used in the bacterial genome editing assay (Supplementary Table S2).

msDNA isolation and visualization

msDNA was extracted from cell lysates using alkaline lysis from 10 ml of culture of cells expressing Retron-Eco11 (p57mA3). Cells were harvested at an OD600 of 0.8–1, and the pellets resuspended in alkaline solution I, transferred into 1.5-ml Eppendorf tubes, and treated sequentially with alkaline solutions II and III. Following centrifugation (14 000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C), the supernatants were extracted twice with phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1, pH 8), and nucleic acids were precipitated overnight at 4°C with isopropanol. Precipitated nucleic acids were centrifuged at 14 000 rpm for 60 min at 4°C, and the pellets were washed once with 1 ml of 70% ethanol, followed by a second centrifugation under the same conditions. Pellets were air-dried for 15 min, resuspended in 10 μl of distilled water containing RNase A (20 μg ml−1), and incubated at 37°C for 60 min. Samples were then stored at −80°C until further use.

msDNA was isolated from FLAG immunoprecipitates as follows: 50 μl of immunoprecipitate was extracted with phenol:chloroform:isoamyl-alcohol (25:24:1, pH 8), and nucleic acids were precipitated overnight at −80°C with 2 volumes of 100% ethanol and 1/10 volume of 3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2). The precipitated nucleic acids were collected by centrifugation at 14 000 rpm at 4°C for 60 min. Pellets were air-dried and resuspended in 10 μl of distilled water containing RNase A/T1, followed by incubation at 37°C for 30 min. Samples were stored at −80°C until use.

For msDNA visualization, samples were mixed with TBE (Tris-Borate-EDTA) loading buffer, heated at 75°C for 5 min, and then cooled on ice for 5 min. Samples were loaded onto a 10% denaturing polyacrylamide gel and electrophoresed in 1× TBE for 90 min at 20 W. Gels were stained by incubating for 15 min in a GelRed bath.

Identification of the 3′ end of Retron-Eco11

msdDNA cDNA was isolated from acrylamide gels, and 100 ng was used to extend the 3′ end with dCTP or dGTP using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT, NEB), following the manufacturer’s instructions. For each tailing reaction, 5 μl of the mixture was subjected to PCR amplification using primers A3-3′ (5′-CGAAGGGGTAAGTAAAATCG-3′) and PoliGH (5′-GGGGGGGGGGGGH-3′) in the presence of dCTP, or A3-3′ and PoliCD (5′-CCCCCCCCCCCCD-3′) in the presence of dGTP. The resulting PCR products were gel-purified, cloned into pGEM-T, and 10 clones from each tailing reaction were sequenced to characterize the 3′ end.

Phage plaque assays

Double-layer plaque assays [15] were performed. E. coli host strains were grown to stationary phase at 37°C in LB. A total of 200 μl of the culture was mixed with 10 ml top agar (LB, 0.5% agar, 5 mM MgSO4, and the appropriate antibiotic) and poured onto 12 × 12 cm LB-agar plates containing 5 mM MgSO4 and the corresponding antibiotic. Ten-fold serial dilutions of phages in liquid LB (10 μl) were spotted of onto the plates, and plaques were counted after overnight incubation at 37°C. When individual plaques were too small to count, the most concentrated dilution at which no plaques were visible was recorded as containing a single plaque.

Isolation and amplification of mutant phages

To isolate mutant phages capable of escaping Retron-Eco11 defense, phages were plated on a lawn of MG1655 bacteria expressing the Retron-Eco11 defense system (p57mA3) using a double-layer plaque assay. Five individual plaques were picked into 100 μl of LB and incubated t at room temperature for 1 h, with occasional vortexing to release the phages from the agar. The samples were centrifuged at 3200 × g for 10 min to remove agar and bacterial cells, and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube.

The ability of the collected phages to escape the Retron-Eco11 defense system was assessed in plaque assays using both a strain expressing the system and a strain lacking it as a negative control. Ten-fold serial dilutions were prepared for the ancestral phage (WT phage used in the original assay) and for the phages recovered from plaques formed on the defense-expressing strain. Phages showing reduced susceptibility to the defense compared to the ancestral phage were further propagated by picking a single plaque formed on Retron-Eco11-expressing cells into a 10 ml liquid culture of Retron-Eco11-harboring cells grown in LB with 5 mM MgSO4 at an OD600 of 0.3. The culture was incubated for 6 h at 37°C with shaking at 175 rpm. The resulting lysate was centrifuged at 3200 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.2 μm pore filter.

Phage sequencing and analysis of mutants

Phage DNA was extracted from 500 μl of a high-titer phage lysate (>107 PFU/ml). Samples were treated with DNAse I (20 μg/ml) for 1 h. DNA was then purified using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit, following a protocol beginning with proteinase K treatment. Sequence libraries were prepared for Illumina sequencing, and reads were mapped to the respective phage reference genomes [GenBank accession numbers: NC_000 866 (T4 phage) and NC_005 859 (T5 phage)] with Geneious Prime software.

Spot growth tests

E. coli MG1655 (BW25113 for assays with p30PRTase) harboring the retron sytem or control plasmids, together with arabinose-inducible helicase constructs or empty plasmid, was grown overnight in LB supplemented with ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and 1% glucose. Cultures were subjected to 10-fold serial dilution, and 10 μl of each dilution was spotted onto an LB agar plate containing the appropriate antibiotics, with or without 0.2% arabinose. Plates were incubated at 28°C overnight.

Affinity purification

E. coli MG1655 harboring plasmids expressing FLAG-tagged RT or the untagged version of the same protein as a negative control was grown by inoculating 200 ml LB supplemented with the appropriate antibiotics with 2 ml of an overnight culture. Cultures were incubated at 37°C until an OD600 of 1.2 was reached. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 12 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was washed with 50 ml phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and resuspended in 8 ml of lysis buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and 1% Triton X-100] supplemented with protease inhibitors. Samples were sonicated in four pulses of 15 s each and centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The pellet was discarded, and 100 μl Anti-FLAG M2-Agarose Affinity Gel (prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions) was added to the supernatant, which was incubated overnight at 4°C with gentle shaking. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 8200 × g for 30 s, and the supernatant was discarded. The resin was either used for co-incubation experiments (see below) or processed for Retron complex analysis. For the latter, the resin was washed three times with 0.5 ml wash buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl). Proteins were eluted, by incubating the resin with 100 μl of wash buffer supplemented with 150 ng/μl 3×FLAG peptide for 30 min at 4°C with gentle shaking, followed by centrifugation at 8200 × g for 30 s. The supernatant containing the eluted proteins was stored at −20°C.

Retron complex purification

The Retron complex was overexpressed from plasmid pMW261 in BL21(DE3) cells in autoinduction media containing ampicillin. Cells (2 L) were grown at 37°C for 8 h, then at 18°C for 16 h. For coexpression with UvsW, BL21(DE3) cells were co-transformed with pMW261 and either p33UvsWwt or p33UvsWK141R and cultured overnight in the presence of ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and 0.2% glucose. We then used 10 ml of the culture for the innocuation of 1 l of LB containing ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and 2 mM MgCl2, and the cells were cultured at 37°C to an OD600 of 0.6–0.8, at which point 0.5 mM IPTG (Isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside) was added to induce retron expression. After 2 h at 37°C, 0.1% arabinose was added to induce UvsW expression, and after a further 2 h, cells were harvested and frozen at −80°C. Retron complexes were purified in the same way for all three sets of conditions: cells were suspended in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 74, 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 5 mM beta-mercaptoethanol) and lysed in a LM20 microfluidizer (Microfluidics). Cleared lysate was bound to Strep-Tactin Superflow Plus resin (Qiagen). The resin was washed first with lysis buffer and then with RNP buffer (20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.9, 250 mM potassium acetate, 2.5 mM magnesium acetate) before elution in RNP buffer containing 5 mM desthiobiotin. Protein-containing fractions were pooled and concentrated in a Vivaspin 20 centrifugal concentrator (50 000 MWCO; Sartorius). Purified complexes were analyzed by spectrophotometry (Nanodrop) and all had A260/A280 ratios of 1.8, indicating the copurification of nucleic acids. The concentrated fractions were extracted with phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1), precipitated with ethanol, and suspended in RNase-free water. We mixed 200 ng of nucleic acids from each complex in 1× DNase I buffer (NEB) with RNase-free DNase I (NEB), 100 ng RNase A, or with no enzyme, as a control, and incubated the mixtures at 37°C for 30 min. Reactions were heated at 95°C for 150 s, placed on ice, and run on a precast 10% acrylamide TBE-Urea gel (Invitrogen) at 400 V in 1× TBE running buffer preheated to ∼50°C. The gel was stained with SYBR Gold (Invitrogen) in 1× TBE for 15 min and visualized with ChemiDoc (Bio-Rad).

Co-incubation experiments

E. coli BW25113 harboring p33uvsWwt or p33uvsD10wt was grown by inoculating 200 ml LB supplemented with chloramphenicol with 2 ml of an overnight culture. Cells were grown at 37°C until an OD600 of 0.3 was reached. Arabinose was added to a final concentration of 0.2%, and growth continued until an OD600 of 1.2 was reached. Cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 12 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was washed with 50 ml PBS and resuspended in 8 ml lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 1% Triton X-100) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Samples were sonicated in four pulses of 15 s each and centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was mixed with the resin obtained as described in the affinity purification section and incubated overnight at 4°C with gentle shaking. Samples were then centrifuged at 8200 × g for 30 s, and the supernatant was discarded. The resin was washed three times with 0.5 ml wash buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl). For elution, the resin was incubated with 100 μl wash buffer supplemented with 150 ng/μl 3×FLAG peptide for 30 min at 4°C with gentle shaking, followed by centrifugation at 8200 × g for 30 s. The supernatant containing eluted proteins was stored at −20°C.

Proteomic analysis

Proteomic analysis was performed at the Proteomics Service of Instituto de Parasitología y Biomedicina “López-Neyra” (CSIC, Granada). Supernatant from affinity purification containing 1.5 μg of protein was run for 10 min in a 4% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gel, which was subsequently stained with Coomassie blue. The band of interest was excised and subjected to in-gel tryptic manual digestion for 18 h at 30°C. Peptides were extracted from the gel using 0.2% trifluoroacetic acid in 30% acetonitrile, dried, and stored at −20°C until further use. Prior nLC-MS/MS analysis, samples were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid (FA) and 0.3% acetonitrile. Peptides were separated on an Easy n-LC II chromatography system (Proxeon) connected to an Amazon Speed ETD mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics). Chromatography was performed on a C18 reverse-phase analytical column (15 μm × 15 cm, 2.6 μm, 100 Å) over a 180 min organic gradient from 5% to 30% buffer B (buffer A: 0.1% FA in H2O; buffer B: 0.1% FA in acetonitrile) with a flow rate of 300 nl min−1. The ion trap was set to analyze the survey scans in the mass range m/z 390–1400. Search criteria included carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification and oxidation of methionine as a variable modification. Fold-enrichment of pull-down proteins was calculated, and statistical significance was evaluated with R.

Metabolomic analysis

Metabolomic analyses were performed by Creative Proteomics Services (www.creative-proteomics.com). Samples of E. coli cells were quantitatively analyzed to measure purine and pyrimidine biosynthesis intermediates using UPLC-MRM/MS. Each sample (~20–80 µl) was brought to a final volume of 100 µl with water. Samples were homogenized on an MM 400 mill mixer at 30 Hz using two metal beads for 2 min. Subsequently, 400 µl of methanol was added to each tube, and samples were homogenized again for 3 min, followed by centrifugation at 21 000 × g for 10 min. The clear supernatants were diluted fivefold with water for LC-MS analysis. Cell pellets were harvested for standardized protein quantification using a BCA assay on a UV spectrometer. For LC-MRM/MS, 10 µl aliquots of the samples and standards were injected into a C18 UPLC column. Analyses were performed on a Waters Acquity UPLC system coupled to a Sciex QTRAP 6500 Plus mass spectrometer with negative-ion detection. A tributylamine buffer solution and acetonitrile were used as the binary-solvent mobile phase for gradient elution under optimized LC separation and MS detection conditions. Linear-regression calibration curves for individual metabolites were constructed using data from their calibration solutions. Concentrations of the metabolites detected in the cell samples were calculated by interpolating the calibration curves using data from the sample solution. For metabolites without standard substances, peak areas were recorded. The results were normalized with protein content of each sample.

Phylogenetic tree construction

Multiple sequence alignments were generated using MAFFT software [16] with progressive methods. An unrooted phylogenetic tree was constructed from an alignment of 2564 sequences carrying the phosphoribosyltransferase domain (IPR000836) and 127 sequences from the retron type III-A associated protein cluster 67_3 [3]. The tree was inferred using the FastTree program [17] with the WAG evolutionary model and a discrete gamma model with 20 rate categories.

Detection of msDNA binding to UvsW

E. coli MG1655 harboring plasmids p57mA3_PRTase, expressing Retron-Eco11 with a mutated PRTase effector from its native promoter, and either p33UvsW3F or p33UvsWk3F, expressing UvsW or its mutant K141R, both C-terminally tagged with 3×FLAG, were grown overnight at 37°C in LB medium supplemented with ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and 1% glucose. Cultures were diluted 1:100 into 200 ml of fresh LB containing ampicillin and chloramphenicol and grown at 37°C until an OD600 of 0.3 was reached. Arabinose was then added to a final concentration of 0.2%, and cultures were incubated for an additional 30 min. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 12 000 × g and subjected to FLAG immunoprecipitation, followed by nucleic acids extraction from the immunoprecipitated material (see earlier). Nucleic acids were separated on a 6% denaturing polyacrylamide gel in 1× TBE for 90 min at 20 W. For Southern blotting, the gel was transferred to a Hybron-N+ membrane for 1 h at 200 mA in 1× TBE. Nucleic acids were UV-crosslinked and hybridized overnight at 42°C with a radioactively labeled DNA oligonucleotide A3-antisense (5′-TTTTACTTACCCCTCCGGGGTCAAA-3′). The membrane was sequentially washed for 15 min at 42°C with 2× SSC/SDS 0,1%, 1× SSC/SDS 0,1%, and 0,1× SSC/SDS 0,1%. Finally, the membrane was dried, wrapped in plastic foil, and exposed to a phosphor screen, followed by imaging with a phosphor-imager after 24 h of exposure.

Determination of protein expression levels

To assess protein expression, 3×FLAG-tagged versions of the proteins were used. E. coli strains carrying plasmids p33UvsW3F, p33UvsWK3F, p30PRTase-CF, p33D10-CF, or p33D10R-CF were grown overnight at 37°C in LB medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 μg/ml) for p30-based plasmids or chloramphenicol (200 μg/ml) for p33-based plasmids. Fresh cultures were then inoculated 1:100 in LB medium with the appropriate antibiotic and grown at 37°C until reaching an OD600 of 0.3. Protein expression was induced by adding L-arabinose to a final concentration of 0.2%, followed by 30 min incubation at 37°C.

Cells were harvested by centrifugation, and total protein extracts were prepared. Samples (3 μg total protein per lane) were separated by SDS–PAGE on 4%–15% gradient polyacrylamide gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (0.45 μm, Amersham) using a semi-dry blotting system (TE77PWR, Amersham Biosciences) at 2 mA/cm2 for 50 min. Membranes were pre-activated in methanol and then blocked for 1 h in 2% blocking reagent (ECL, Amersham) in TBS-T buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 180 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20).

FLAG-tagged proteins were detected using a monoclonal anti-FLAG antibody (Sigma–Aldrich, F7425; 1:50 000 dilution) followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (Sigma–Aldrich, A9044; 1:100 000 dilution) in TBS-T for 1 h at room temperature with gentle shaking. Membranes were washed six times for 5 min each in TBS-T, and signals were visualized using ECL detection reagent (Amersham) on a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Genome editing using type III-A3 retrons

Retron cassettes encoded in a pET-21(+) plasmid and the CspRecT and mutLE32K in the plasmid pORTMAGE-Ec1 were co-transformed into E. coli bMS.346 strain by electroporation. bMS.346 strain was generated from E. coli MG1655 strain by inactivating the sbcB and recJ genes with early stop codons (Addgene #220588). The assay was performed in a deep 96-well plate using 500 μl of culture. Three individual colonies of every retron architecture were grown in LB with proper antibiotics for 16 h at 37°C. A 1:1000 dilution of these cultures was grown overnight into LB supplemented with 1 mM IPTG (GoldBio), 0.2% arabinose (GoldBio), and 1 mM m-toluic (Sigma–Aldrich) acid with shaking at 37°C. Twenty-five microliters of culture was collected, mixed with 1:1 with H2O, and boiled for 10 min at 95°C. Two microliters of the lysed culture were used as a template for PCR reactions with primers containing adapters for Illumina sequencing preparation. An Illumina NextSeq instrument was used to sequence the amplicons, and resulted reads were processed Geneious 2023.2.1 software to quantify the editing percentages.

Mammalian cell culture conditions

HEK293T cells with a constitutive Cbh Cas9 expression cassette at the AAVS1 locus (GeneCopoeia, SL502) was used for human cell editing experiments. HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium + GlutaMax (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10566016) + 10% FBS, fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10082147) in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2.

For transfections, ~100 000 HEK293T-Cas9 cells were seeded in a single well of a 24-well plate. The next day, when cells were 60%–70% confluent, media were replaced with media containing 0.5 µM AZD7648 (Selleck Chemicals, S8843) and 3 µM PolQi2 (MedChem Express, HY-150279). Then, cells were transfected with 2 µg plasmid DNA encoding the retron editor and Cas9 guide RNA and 2.5 µl Lipofectamine 3000. After 24 h, media was replaced with media containing 0.5 µM AZD7648 and 3 µM PolQi2 to remove Lipofectamine. Three days after transfection, cells were washed 1× with PBS (Thermo Scientific 14190250) and lysed by the addition of 100 µl cell lysis buffer [10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0; 0.05% SDS; 25 µg ml−1 proteinase K (NEB); 0.1% Triton-X] and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. Results

The Retron-Eco11 tripartite system

The Retron-Eco11 system is a type III-A3 retron that was identified [3] in the whole-shotgun sequence of E. coli EC2608. Retron-Eco11 is a tripartite system comprising a RT from clade 9, a ncRNA gene, predicted to fold into a conserved type IIIA structure, and a gene encoding an effector protein that belongs to protein cluster 67_3 (Fig. 1A). This effector protein is characterized by an N-terminal phosphoribosyltransferase (PRTase)-like domain and a C-terminal RuvB winged helix DNA-binding domain.

(B) Schematic representation of the predicted pair of inverted repeats located at the 5′ and 3′ ends of the ncRNA gene. The stop codon of the effector protein, in which the two adenosines appear as bulged nucleotides in the predicted secondary structure, is indicated by a dashed line ending with a vertical bar, and the translational start codon of the RT is indicated by a dashed arrow. The predicted branching G is indicated by the red circle. (C) Predicted secondary structure of the msDNA-Eco11, according to the model reported for Ec86 [5]. The predicted (Supplementary Fig. S1D and E) flexible DSLb hairpin loop is framed by a dashed-line rectangle.

The Eco11 ncRNA gene overlaps the 3′ and 5′ ends of the effector and RT genes on the same strand. It contains two distinct regions, the msr and msd regions, and a pair of inverted repeats (IRa2 and IRa1) located at the 5′ and 3′ ends (Fig. 1A and B). This pairing spans 25 base pairs, comprising an outer arm of 14 base pairs (IRa2_1/IRa1_2) and an inner arm of 11 base pairs (IRa2_2/IRa1_1), separated by two bulged adenosine residues. Notably, the inner arm of 11 base pairs (IRa2_2/IRa1_1) is sufficient for msDNA generation, which is essential for the biological activity of the retron system (Supplementary Fig. S1A–C).

The IR region is followed by unpaired G residues, and the msrRNA rG28 (branching G) and msdDNA dT1 are predicted to form a 2′–5′ phosphodiester bond. Apart from the single-stranded RNA segments, the predicted msrRNA region, which is not reverse-transcribed, comprises three short RNA stem-loops and one DNA–RNA duplex. According to the predicted structure and the identified 3′ end of the msdDNA, the RT-Eco11 appears to produce a 101-base msdDNA that is predicted to fold into two DNA stem-loop structures, DSLa and DSLb, comprising 13 and 69 nucleotides, respectively. The predicted secondary structure includes a stable, conserved 18-base pair stem at the 5′ end of DSLb, with the hairpin loop displaying greater structural flexibility (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S1D and E). These findings are consistent with previous reports suggesting that a segment of the long stem-loop in msDNA can serve as a template for precise genome editing through homology-directed repair (HDR) [19, 20].

As anticipated, the sequences of RT-Eco11 and RT-Ec86 (RT-Eco1), both of which belong to clade 9, were well aligned (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Furthermore, a superimposition of the predicted 3D structures of RT-Eco11 generated by AlphaFold2 (Supplementary Fig. S2B) and RT-Eco1 associated with its cognate msDNA (Ec86 complex, Protein Data Bank: 9V9U) gave a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 2.55 Å over 320 Cα atoms, indicating structural similarities between these two proteins.

Type I PRTases function both as enzymes and regulatory proteins in nucleotide synthesis and salvage pathways, thereby potentially modulating nucleotide availability. They are characterized by a conserved PRPP (5-phospho-α-D-ribose-1-diphosphate) binding motif and common core structure featuring five-stranded parallel β-sheets flanked by three or four α-helices, a structural arrangement that closely resembles the canonical Rossmann dinucleotide-binding fold [21]. The Eco11 effector contains the PRPP-binding motif VVILTDTIGSGNQ (residues 148–160), with the binding site (underlined) located in the PRPP loop between β4 and α7. Notably, PRTase-like proteins associated with retrons feature a distinctive conserved motif, Gx14PNNxP [3], located in the loops flanking the β9 strand of the Eco11 effector (Fig. 2A and B). This motif may reflect a common biochemical function of these proteins.

Retron-associated PRTase-like effectors may occur alone or in conjunction with a protein containing a predicted winged-helix DNA-binding domain encoded by a downstream gene. In certain type III variants, such as A3, A4, and A5, this DNA-binding domain is fused to the C-terminus of the putative PRTase (Fig. 2A). Sequence comparisons revealed that proteins within cluster 67_3 possess a short conserved tail of 24 amino acids, referred to as the CTT. This CTT can be further divided into two regions: a hypervariable CTL (spanning residues 390–404 in Retron-Eco11) and a conserved CTP YYPxxLRxx (residues 405–413 in Retron-Eco11) (Fig. 2A and B). As expected, the predicted structure of the Eco11 effector features a characteristic PRTase β-core sheet (β3, β2, β4, β5, β7) sandwiched between α6, α7, and α8 on one face and α5 on the opposite face of the β-sheet (Fig. 2B and C).

A FATCAT search for structurally similar proteins [22] revealed that the Eco11 effector most closely resembles the xanthine–guanine–hypoxanthine PRTase (with an RMSD of 3.07 Å over 130 Cα atoms) and the purine PRTase (with an RMSD of 3.09 Å over 132 Cα atoms) (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). The predicted 3D structure of the C-terminal winged HTH domain aligns with that of transcriptional regulators, with an RMSD of 2.00 Å over 72 Cα atoms (Supplementary Fig. S3C). Interestingly, in the predicted 3D structure of the Eco11 effector, the C-terminus forms a disordered coil–coil region. Superimposition of the C-terminal winged HTH domain of the Eco11 effector onto that of the retron Ec86 effector yields an RMSD of 2.55 Å over 73 equivalent positions (Supplementary Fig. S3C). The C-terminus of the Ec86 effector also contains a structurally similar terminal peptide, flanked by tyrosine (residue 299) and proline (residue 307), which is required for effective phage defense and appears to interact with its cognate msrRNA and msdDNA [5]. These observations suggest that the CTT of the Eco11 effector may be accessible for binding or modifications by other proteins, as previously suggested for the Ec86 effector [5].

Phylogenetic analyses further support that the retron-associated effector in cluster 67_3 represents a distinct and previously unreported lineage within type I PRTases (Fig. 2D), suggesting a specialized function that remains to be determined.

The Retron-Eco11 tripartite system protects against phage infection

We tested the function of Retron-Eco11 as an anti-phage defense system by synthesizing the retron system of EC2608, including its native promoter, and expressing it in the E. coli MG1655 strain, which lacks retrons. Retron-containing bacteria were then tested against a panel of 12 phages. Significant anti-phage activity was observed against E. coli phages T2, T4 (Straboviridae subfamily Tevenvirinae) and T5 (Demerecviridae subfamily Markadamsvirinae), and a decrease in plaque size was observed for phage T7 (Autographviridae subfamily Studiervirinae) (Fig. 3A).

The data displayed are representative results from three independent replicates. For phage T7, a marked reduction in plaque size was observed, indicated as “S” for smaller plaques. Fold protection was determined in serial-dilution plaque assays comparing infection efficacy in strains carrying the retron system versus control strain lacking it. The phages used in these assays are indicated. (B) Requirement of individual Retron-Eco11 components for phage resistance. For the sake of simplicity, only plaque assays with phage T5 are displayed. We investigated which components of the system are essential for the observed defense activity by testing mutants carrying modifications in the PRPP motif of the effector protein (152TDTIGSG158/152AATIAAA158), the catalytic site of the RT (D251A/D252A), and the predicted branching G (changed to C) of the ncRNA. All of these mutations abolished the ability of Retron-Eco11 to protect against phage infection (Fig. 3B). Mutants in both conserved tyrosines (Y405A and Y406A) in the CTP also cause the defense system to lose its ability to defend against viruses (Fig. 3B). These findings confirm that the msDNA and both the protein components are indispensable for the protective function of this retron system.

We then investigated the diversity of the phages recognized by the Retron-Eco11 system, using the BASEL collection [13], which consists of 68 newly isolated phages infecting the model organism E. coli. Our findings indicate that this retron system can provide various degrees of defense against a wide range of tailed phages (Supplementary Table S1), including those from the subfamilies Tempevirinae, Queuovirinae (Nonagvirus and Seuratvirus), Markadamsvirinae (Eseptimavirus and Tequintavirus), and Tevenvirinae (Tequatrovirus and Mosigvirus).

Isolation of phage scape mutants from Retron-Eco11 defense reveals mutations in T4 UvsW and T5 D10 helicases

T4 and T5 are bacteriophages with contrasting genome sizes, genetic contents, and host ranges [24–28]. We investigated the ways in which Retron-Eco11 specifically recognizes and responds to phage infections by conducting experiments to isolate mutants of T4 and T5 phages capable of evading this defense system. For each phage, we successfully isolated five mutants capable of disabling retron defense activity.

Although additional mutations were detected in other genes, only genetic alterations consistently associated with escape from Retron-Eco11 defense were found in helicase genes—UvsW, common to all five T4 escape mutants, and D10, shared by all five T5 escape mutants (Supplementary Tables S3 and S4). Therefore, we focused our analysis on helicases as common determinants of phage escape. All the T4 mutants presented two deletions of different sizes in the UvsW RNA–DNA-encoded helicase [29–32]. These deletions led to frameshift mutations resulting in prematurely truncated proteins in the N-terminal RecA-like motor domain. The T5 mutants displayed a different pattern. They had single-point mutations leading to the replacement of individual amino-acid residues in the C-terminal RecA-like domain of the D10 helicase [33] (Supplementary Fig. S4). D10 is a functional homolog of UvsW, and both proteins belong to the SF2 helicase family [34, 35].

We hypothesized that the truncated UvsW proteins, if produced, would have no helicase activity, as they lack conserved sequence motifs II and VI, essential for ATP binding and hydrolysis, and motifs IV and V, crucial for nucleic-acid binding. However, as the crystal structure of the D10 helicase had not been determined, uncertainties remained regarding the effect of the single amino-acid substitutions found in the mutants on D10 biochemical properties. We used AlphaFold2 to predict the structure of D10 and explore the potential functional implications of these residues. The predicted structure of D10 aligned with that of UvsW over 432 equivalent positions, with a RMSD of 2.78Å (Fig. 4A), indicating that these two proteins are structural homologs. The amino-acid substitutions in D10 (S375R, N379T, L382P, Q415L, and W428G) were located in the cleft facing the conserved N-terminal RecA-like helicase domain (Fig. 4B). Most of the substituted residues were in close contact with either motif V or motif VI, with a direct hydrogen bond observed between residues L382 and L386 in motif VI (Supplementary Fig. S5A and B). These structural observations suggest that the identified mutations may impair D10 helicase activity.

Phage-encoded helicases activate Retron-Eco11 and type III-A3 defense systems

Experiments were performed to confirm that T4 and T5 phages activate the Retron-Eco11 defense system via UvsW and D10 helicases. To this end, wild-type and mutant forms of UvsW and D10 were expressed in E. coli cells carrying either the wild-type or mutant Retron-Eco11 systems, or in cells lacking the system. When expressed independently of an arabinose-inducible promoter, the effector protein did not exhibit detectable toxicity (Fig. 5A). Immunoblot analysis showed that the 51 kDa full-length protein was processed into a ∼15 kDa fragment (Supplementary Fig. S6A), which may explain its lack of toxicity.

Bacterial cell viability was assayed in the presence (p30PRTase) or absence (pBAD30) of the PRTase effector, expressed under the control of an arabinose-inducible promoter in pBAD30. (B) Expression of the T4 UvsW and T5 D10 helicases from an arabinose-inducible promoter results in toxicity in a strain containing the Retron-Eco11 system. The PRTase (PRTase*) mutant (Fig. 3) was also included in some of the assays. All experiments shown in the figure are representative results from at least three independent replicates. In the presence of the complete retron system, the expression of wild-type UvsW or D10 helicases resulted in toxicity (Fig. 5B). In contrast, no growth impairment was observed when mutant helicases were expressed, in cells lacking the retron system, or in the presence of a retron carrying a mutated effector protein. These results provide strong evidence that the UvsW and D10 act directly as triggers of the Retron-Eco11 defense system. Furthermore, they indicate that effector protein toxicity is induced upon recognition of a phage-derived signal by the Retron complex, supporting the idea that Retron-Eco11 responds to specific phage-related cues or activities.

To investigate whether the Retron-Eco11system recognizes the enzymatic activity of helicases, we tested a mutant form of UvsW (K141R), carrying a substitution in the Walker A motif, and a mutant form of D10 (R389N) in motif VI. The K141R mutation is known to strongly impair ATP hydrolysis and translocation (branch migration), while retaining the ability to bind Holliday junction (HJ)-containing structures [30]. Similarly, the R389N mutation is known to almost completely abolish ATPase activity [33], while retaining DNA-binding activity equivalent to that of the wild type. Both UvsW and D10 mutants were present at higher levels than the wild-type helicases (Supplementary Fig. S6B), likely due to increased expression or stability. Interestingly, expression of these mutants did not cause toxicity in cells containing the Retron-Eco11 system (Fig. 5B), suggesting that Retron-Eco11 responds to the functional enzymatic activity of the helicases rather than mere DNA binding.

The Retron-Eco11 system protects against a broad range of tailed phages. We therefore investigated whether it also responds to helicases from other phage families. A BLAST search of Queuovirinae phages from the BASEL collection identified a 658-amino-acid superfamily II DNA/RNA helicase in Nonagvirus and Seuratvirus, showing only distant sequence similarity to UvsW and D10 (14.0% and 12.8% pairwise identity, respectively). Expression of this helicase, designated Heli22 from phage Bas22 (Seuratvirus), in E. coli carrying the Retron-Eco11 system caused toxicity (Fig. 5C), suggesting that Heli22 can trigger retron defense similar to UvsW and D10. In addition, five type III-A3 retrons from different bacterial species (Supplementary Table S5), with pairwise sequence identities ranging from ∼45% to 98%, were activated by UvsW to varying degrees (Supplementary Fig. S7A and B). Together, these results suggest that while UvsW can act as a general trigger, these retrons may be differentially tuned to respond to helicases from phages infecting their native hosts.

Activation of the Retron-Eco11 PRTase alters nucleotide metabolism via PRPP depletion

PRTases are key modulators of intracellular nucleotide availability. To assess whether activation of the Retron-Eco11 PRTase effector by the phage-encoded helicases impacts nucleotide metabolism, we performed metabolomic profiling to quantify purine and pyrimidine biosynthesis intermediates in bacterial cells carrying the Retron-Eco11 defense system and expressing the UvsW helicase. A retron variant with an inactivated PRTase served as a control.

Metabolic differences between the two conditions were first explored using sparse Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (sPLS-DA). The sPLS-DA score plot revealed a clear separation between the two groups (Fig. 6A), with PRPP emerging as the most discriminative metabolite (Fig. 6B), showing an ~50% decrease upon PRTase activation (Fig. 6C). Among the metabolites analyzed, 14 yielded quantifiable values, and PRPP was the only one showing a statistically significant change (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. S8). The marked depletion of PRPP suggests that Retron-Eco11 PRTase activation disrupts nucleotide homeostasis, potentially limiting nucleotide biosynthesis during phage infection, and thereby promoting abortive infection.

The Retron-Eco11 defense mechanism depends on specific characteristics of the UvsW and D10 phage helicases

The UvsW helicase functions similarly to the bacterial RecG helicase, resolving stalled replication forks and facilitating branch migration of Holliday junctions [36]. To determine whether Retron-Eco11 responds to general helicase activities shared by UvsW and RecG, we examined its behavior in a ΔrecG mutant. Transformation of ΔrecG cells with a plasmid encoding Retron-Eco11 was efficient and did not affect growth. Furthermore, co-expression with UvsW or D10 helicases, including their mutant derivatives, produced a phenotype comparable to wild-type E. coli cells (Supplementary Fig. S9). These results support that Retron-Eco11 specifically senses unique features of the UvsW and D10 phage helicases, possibly related to their structure or conformation, which may facilitate access to the Retron complex, rather than general helicase activity.

UvsW helicase interacts with msDNA-Eco11

Retron-Eco11 appears to respond to the activity of the phage helicases. Based on this, we hypothesize that recognition of the phage helicases by the retron occurs via a direct interaction with the msDNA, which subsequently triggers a response dependent on helicase activity. To test this, we performed immunoprecipitation assays using FLAG-tagged UvsW and its mutant derivative, UvsW K141R, in the presence of Retron-Eco11. Importantly, the tagged UvsW remained functional and was still capable of triggering the defense system (Supplementary Fig. S10A). Following immunoprecipitation, nucleic acids were extracted and analyzed via Southern blotting to detect msDNA-Eco11. Our results showed that both UvsW and mutant UvsW K141R successfully pulled down msDNA-Eco11 (Fig. 7A).

Immunoprecipitation assays with these constructs confirmed that both UvsW and the mutant UvsW K141R still interact with the modified msDNA, indicating that binding does not rely on the DSLb nucleotide sequence but may instead be driven by structural features. Taken together with our previous findings, these results suggest that helicase binding and activity may induce conformational rearrangements in the Retron complex, thereby modulating the PRTase effector and triggering its toxicity. We therefore examined next the composition and interactions of the complex.

Retron-Eco11 complex interactions

To further investigate the interactions within the Retron complex, we fused a 3×FLAG tag to the C-terminus of RT-Eco11. This modification did not impair the anti-phage defense activity of the retron (Supplementary Fig. S10B). To define the components of the Retron-Eco11 complex, we performed immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry-based proteomics analysis using the tagged RT. As expected, the only protein significantly enriched together with RT was the PRTase effector (Fig. 8A and B). This suggests that, unlike in other retron systems [4], the presence of msDNA is essential for effector protein binding in Retron-Eco11. We next examined whether the expression of phage helicases UvsW and D10 alters Retron complex composition. Co-incubation of immunoprecipitated Retron complexes using a tagged retron-RT with extracts expressing UvsW or D10 helicase did not affect complex composition (Fig. 8A and B). Similarly, co-expression of the retron with either UvsW or the UvsW K141R mutant helicase did not alter the stoichiometry of the complex. The migration profiles of the RNA and DNA components on a denaturing acrylamide gel were also unchanged (Fig. 8C). Together, these results suggest that activation of the effector protein, and the resulting toxicity, likely occur within the complex itself.

Co-immunoprecipitation assays using C-terminal 3×FLAG-tagged UvsW or the UvsW K141R mutant detected both RT and the PRTase effector. However, only PRTase was significantly enriched in both samples, with higher enrichment observed for the K141R mutant (Fig. 8D). The relative abundance of the tagged K141R helicase was higher than that of the wild-type (Fig. 8E), likely due to its increased expression or stability. Consistent with this, RT and PRTase levels were significantly higher in the K141R mutant (Fig. 8E). While an interaction with RT cannot be ruled out, the association with the PRTase effector is more pronounced in this mutant. Although helicase-inactive, the K141R variant can still bind msDNA, which likely stabilizes its association with the Retron complex and allows us to better detect the interaction that also occurs with the wild-type helicase. Together, these results suggest that, in addition to msDNA, the phage helicase preferentially interacts with the PRTase effector.

Precise genome editing using Retron-Eco11

Retrons have been repurposed to precisely engineer a wide variety of target genomes, including bacteria, phages, yeast, and human cells [19, 20, 38–43]. The recombineering technology developed for bacterial editing is based on the reverse transcription of a modified msDNA that contains an editing donor (RT-Donor), a single-stranded annealing protein which, together with the host single-stranded binding (SSB) protein, enables editing of the target genome during bacterial replication [20]. Recent studies have characterized dozens of retrons for bacterial recombineering, identifying several retrons with higher editing rates compared with Retron-Eco1, the standard to date [44]. Moreover, this survey indicates that clade 9 retrons are generally good candidates for genome editing, highlighting the relevance of analyzing type III-A3 retrons for biotechnological purposes.

Thus, we examine the recombineering capabilities of Retron-Eco11 together with two additional phylogenetically related candidates that have been previously shown msDNA production in E. coli, retron-node 1352 (Retron-Sen3) and retron-node 1353 (Retron Kpn1) [3, 44]. These retrons were engineered to contain a 70-bp donor when reverse transcribed to make a precise edit in the rpoB gene. Furthermore, three different positions within the DSLb stem were selected to place the donor, allowing us to analyze the impact of donor position in editing rates (Fig. 7B). The retron recombineering assay revealed that editing efficiencies of the type III-A3 retron architectures tested in this work are comparable to those observed with Retron-Eco1 (Fig. 9A), which, for context, is among the best retrons for prokaryotic editing [45]. In addition, positioning the donor at different locations within the DSLb stem does not significantly affect editing efficiencies (Fig. 9A). Finally, to evaluate the extension of this system from prokaryotic to eukaryotic editing, we used Retron-Eco11 to produce editing RT-Donors in human HEK293T cells. These donors are used alongside CRISPR Cas9, serving as a repair template following a double-strand break to introduce a precise mutation. Retron-Eco11 enabled templated Cas9-mediated human genome editing at rates similar to that of Retron-Eco1 at the EMX1 gene, where a G > A edit and PAM mutation were installed (Fig. 9B). This is consistent with a previously tested set of 13 III-A3 retrons for human editing, which yielded editing rates that were, on average, 2.2 times higher than those of Retron-Eco1 [44]. To contextualize these editing rates, Retron-Eco1 is not among the best retrons for eukaryotic editing, and retrons from the closely related III-A2 family have been shown to support editing at >50× the rates of Retron-Eco1 [44]. Overall, these results support the use of type III-A3 retrons as potent tools for precise genome editing of prokaryotic, which are also capable of editing in eukaryotic organisms.

Discussion

We describe a new retron system, Retron-Eco11 (type III-A3), which mediates anti-phage defense by recognizing specific phage helicases that activate the retron response. Retron-Eco11 protects E. coli against both T-even and T-odd phages through recognition of structurally and functionally related helicases, exemplified by the T4 UvsW and T5 D10. The effector of this retron system is a PRTase-like protein that becomes toxic when phage helicase activity is sensed by the Retron complex, ultimately leading to PRPP depletion and abortive infection.

Despite recent advances in retron biology, the precise mechanisms by which the Retron complex detects and responds to invading phages remain unclear, and several observations are still puzzling. Recent studies have identified multiple phage triggers that activate the retron-defense system [46]. For example, inhibition of RecBCD by phage proteins activates retron Ec48 (Retron-Eco6, type IV), leading to growth arrest and, ultimately, cell death [2]. A similar activation mechanism involving a different effector protein appears to operate for Retron Sen72 (Retron-Sen1, type V). The RTs encoded by these retrons share a common phylogenetic origin, both belonging to Clade 3.

However, not all retrons with RTs of the same phylogenetic origin followed identical activation patterns. Retron-Eco8 (type I-B2), also belonging to clade 3, is activated by the expression of SSB proteins from phages of the Podoviridae family [46]. Moreover, different phage proteins can trigger distinct retron systems. In retron Ec67 (Retron-Eco2, type I-C1), which encodes a clade 8 RT, activation appears to involve early-expressed phage proteins A1 and DenB, both of which participate in DNA degradation [46]. Similarly, the toxicity of retron Ec86 (Retron-Eco1, type II-A3) appears to be triggered by the phage protein Dcm, which modifies msDNA [12]. These examples suggest that while phage recognition mechanisms may be broadly conserved among retrons, variations in their components can lead to distinct modes of defense.

Retron Ec86 and Retron-Eco11 both encode clade 9 RTs, yet their msDNA components differ structurally and are linked to distinct effector proteins [3]. Consequently, it was anticipated that the phage-triggered defense mechanism of Retron-Eco11 would differ from that of Retron-Eco1. With the exception of retron Ec48, whose defense is suppressed by phages λ and T7 through inhibitors of the RecBCDE complex [2], previous studies have provided no conclusive evidence that a given retron or retron type consistently responds to similar structural or functional triggers across different phage families.

We showed that Retron-Eco11 recognizes helicases from multiple phage families as triggers for bacterial defense. Although these helicases lack extensive primary amino acid sequence conservation, they are structurally and functionally conserved. Our findings further suggest that activation by these phage-encoded helicases is broadly conserved among type III-A3 retron systems, which may have adapted to recognize variant helicases encoded by phages that infect their bacterial hosts.

UvsW is an ATP-dependent DNA helicase that is essential for unwinding DNA structures, such as R-loops, during phage infection and DNA recombination. We hypothesized that UvsW might activate Retron-Eco11 by modifying the retron msDNA, an RNA-DNA hybrid molecule. Consistent with this hypothesis, we found that both UvsW and the ATPase-deficient mutant UvsW K141K interacted with msDNA-Eco11. Interestingly, although UvsW K141R retains msDNA-binding ability, it fails to activate the retron system. A similar observation was made for the D10 R389N mutant, confirming that Retron-Eco11 activation requires helicase activity, rather than msDNA binding alone. Despite its functional similarity to these phage-encoded helicases, E. coli RecG helicase does not activate the retron system, suggesting that activation specificity depends on how phage helicases interact with the Retron complex.

As in Ec86, mutations in the catalytic active site of the RT and in the branching G of the retron ncRNA abolished Retron-Eco11 defense activity. Mutations affecting branching G also disrupted PRTase effector binding to RT, demonstrating that msDNA is indispensable for proper Retron complex formation. Interestingly, it has been suggested that the long hairpin structure of msDNA may play a role in phage recognition and interact with the effector protein, potentially inhibiting its activity [5]. We replaced the DSLb stem in msDNA-Eco11 and found that UvsW and the mutant UvsW K141R were still able to interact with the modified msDNA, suggesting that the interaction does not rely on a specific DSLb sequence and may instead be driven by structural features.

Our findings demonstrate that Retron-Eco11, similar to other known retrons, forms a complex composed of three essential components: RT, msDNA, and PRTase effector. Similar to the Ec86 effector, which is nucleoside deoxyribosyltransferase-like, the predicted structure of the Retron-Eco11 effector suggests a two-lobed architecture. The N-lobe contains the core PRTase-like domain (α1–α14 and β1–β9), while the C-lobe contains the winged-helix HTH DNA-binding domain (α15–α21). It has been proposed that the Ec86-effector-bound structure may represent the inactive state of this potentially toxic effector. Previous studies have shown that the Ec86 retron effector interacts directly with both the RT and msDNA, with msDNA wrapping around the positively charged surface of RT [5]. Co-immunoprecipitation assays using FLAG-tagged UvsW or the UvsW K141R mutant indicated that the phage helicase interacted with both msDNA and the Retron-Eco11 PRTase effector, although the interaction with RT could not be excluded. Moreover, our results did not reveal changes in the composition of the Retron complex upon activation. Instead, the process may involve subtle supramolecular rearrangements within the complex, which warrant further investigation.

Together, our findings provide compelling evidence that type III-A retron-mediated anti-phage defenses operate through similar mechanisms, regardless of phage type. These results lay the foundation for future studies aimed at elucidating how retrons sense and respond to phage infections. Furthermore, our research highlights the potential of type III-A3 retrons as precise genome editors in both bacterial and human cells. Continued characterization and optimization of these retrons could expand the genome editing toolkit and open new avenues for biotechnological applications.
