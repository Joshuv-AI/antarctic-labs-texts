Hydrazone–heterocyclic hybrids as promising anticancer agents: a comprehensive review
Authors: K. Nagaraj Pai, K. Subrahmanya Bhat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrazones have gained significant attention in the field of medicinal chemistry because of their ease of preparation, crystalline nature, structural flexibility and broad-spectrum biological activity. Additionally, N-containing heterocycles have always contributed to anticancer drug discovery, with 73% of FDA-approved anticancer drugs based on them. Recently, the idea of hybrid drug design that synergistically combines two or more pharmacologically active molecules has become an effective strategy in discovering new anticancer drugs. Hence, combining two bioactive fragment hydrazones and N-containing heterocycles can be considered an effective strategy for discovering potent anticancer drugs. Among these, heterocycle–hydrazone hybrids derived from quinoline, indole, triazole, benzimidazole and isatin have displayed promising anticancer activities, with some of the studies even approaching in vivo evaluation. Overall, this review focuses on the anticancer activities of these hydrazone hybrids, their in vitro cytotoxic results, the proposed mechanism of action of the most active compound, cell death pathway results, docking studies, and cell cycle-arrest results. In addition, various synthetic strategies of hydrazones, including conventional methods, microwave and ultrasonication irradiation techniques, diazonium salt-derived methods and solvent-free reaction conditions, have been reported. Furthermore, the kinetics of hydrazone synthesis is described in brief.

Introduction

Cancer is a major global health problem, and India has the third-highest cancer cases worldwide after China and the USA, according to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN), which estimates a 57% rise in the number of cancer cases by 2040.1 Cancer is caused when the abnormal cells divide uncontrollably in the human body, which is not only due to genetics but also caused by poor lifestyle or habits.2 DNA damage is caused by various exogenous and endogenous processes, such as exposure to chemicals, exposure to radiation, generation of free radicals, and topological changes, and is another important condition for the generation of cancerous cells.3 What makes cancer the deadliest disease is its diverse symptoms in diagnosis, with its signs being similar to those caused during mild illness.4 In particular, the current state of knowledge in tumour biology over the last decade has positively impacted the discovery of anticancer drugs.5 However, the drug resistance and lack of selectivity of the drug molecules, as reported in several studies6 and clinical trials, have urged the discovery of new bioactive molecules that are capable of exhibiting cancer-specific cytotoxic activities, and the discovery of anticancer drugs is still a topic of interest among researchers.

Hybrid drug design linking two pharmacologically active molecules has become an effective strategy in the field of medicinal chemistry.7 The incorporation of two biologically active fragments into a single molecule can exert synergistic effects by enhancing pharmaceutical applications via targeting multiple receptor sites.8 Among the various fragments that can be used to scaffold heterocycles, hydrazones (–C <svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" version="1.0" width="13.200000pt" height="16.000000pt" viewBox="0 0 13.200000 16.000000" preserveAspectRatio="xMidYMid meet"><metadata> Created by potrace 1.16, written by Peter Selinger 2001-2019 </metadata><g transform="translate(1.000000,15.000000) scale(0.017500,-0.017500)" fill="currentColor" stroke="none"><path d="M0 440 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z M0 280 l0 -40 320 0 320 0 0 40 0 40 -320 0 -320 0 0 -40z"/></g></svg> N–NH–) provide proven benefits, such as easier synthetic methods, flexibility, and the ability to interact with various target proteins and display broad-spectrum biological applications.9 The free rotation around the N–N single bond and the presence of a carbonyl group maintaining molecular planarity are the characteristic features of their azomethine linkage. In addition, hydrazones are not just the final products of the reaction, but they can also be used as intermediates to prepare heterocycles.10

Heterocycles are cyclic compounds containing at least one heteroatom, such as oxygen, nitrogen, and sulphur.11 Owing to their importance in drug discovery, more than 85% of FDA-approved drugs are made of heterocycles.12 More specifically, approximately 75% of FDA-approved drugs contain N-heterocycles, highlighting their importance in drug discovery.13 Combining these heterocycles with hydrazone hybrids enhances the biological activities of the resultant hybrids.14 Previously, Popiolek has studied the antimicrobial activity of various hydrazone derivatives.15,16 In addition, the authors studied the antimicrobial, antifungal, antitumor, antidepressant, insecticidal and enzyme inhibition activities of benzene–sulfonyl hydrazone derivatives.17 Mali et al. studied various biological activities such as antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, and antitubercular functions including synthesis aspects.18 Socea et al. reported the synthesis, spectral characterization, and antitumour, antimicrobial, antiviral, antiparasitic, anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, antioedematous, antidiabetic and antioxidant action of various acyl hydrazones.19 Tafere et al. reported anticancer and antimicrobial activities of copper, silver and gold complexes of hydrazones.20 In addition to their antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and anticancer applications, Wahbeh et al. reported the role of hydrazones in the prevention of platelet aggregation.21 Omidi and Kakanejadifard studied the biological activities of hydrazones of curcumin.22 Angelova et al. studied the anticonvulsant activity of acetyl-, benzoyl-, GABA-, nicotinic acid-, indole-, thiophene-, 2-chloroquinolinyl, coumarin-, chroman- and chromene–hydrazone derivatives in animal models.23 Furthermore, reviews addressing the anticancer activities of quinoline,24 indole,25 triazole,26 benzimidazole27 and isatin28 have been extensively reported, but a review collectively explaining the anticancer activities of these heterocyclic hydrazones has not yet been conducted.

While there is growing research interest in heterocyclic–hydrazone hybrids in anticancer drug discovery, understanding the current developments in the synthetic methodologies of hydrazone derivatives and the biological applications of heterocycle–hydrazone hybrids is vital. In this context, this review summarizes various synthetic strategies and kinetic effects of catalyst, solvent, pressure and irradiation techniques on hydrazone synthesis. In addition, the recent advances in the anticancer activities of various N-containing heterocycle–hydrazone hybrids, particularly quinoline, indole, benzimidazole, triazole and isatin hydrazones, are summarized along with docking studies to understand their binding interactions with target proteins (such as EGFR, topoisomerase, ERα, and various kinase domains), briefly describing the enzyme inhibition results wherever reported in the anticancer study, the mechanism of action of the most active compounds, and cell-cycle arrest results to collectively present the results of various studies and provide readers with a collective overview of this area of research. With these insights, structure–activity relationship studies have been conducted to help the researchers to understand different substitution patterns and structural arrangement to design potent bioactive molecules with effective anticancer activity.

Synthetic methodologies of hydrazones

Classical condensation of hydrazides and aldehydes

Başaran et al. synthesized acyl hydrazones by the initial conversion of ester 1 to hydrazide 2 using hydrazine hydrate in ethanol at RT. Subsequent treatment of these hydrazides with various aldehydes yielded hydrazones 3a–g in good yields of 76–89% (ref. 29) [Scheme 1].29

Using acid catalysts

Udhayanithi et al. prepared hydrazones 6a–e in 80–90% yield by condensing hydrazide 4 with aromatic aldehydes 5a–e in ethanol at reflux using acetic acid (catalyst)30 [Scheme 2].30

Synthesis of hydrazones 6a–e using acid catalysts.

Using a base catalyst

Karimatu et al. synthesized hydrazone 9 by refluxing 2,4,5-trimethoxyacetophenone 7 and 4-nitrophenylhydrazine 8 in ethanol in the presence of sodium hydroxide31 [Scheme 3].31

Synthesis of hydrazone 9 using the base catalyst.

From diazonium salts

Salah et al. synthesized hydrazone 15 from an aryl diazonium intermediate 12, which was synthesized from chloroanthranilic acid 10 using an aqueous solution of sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid. To this, a solution of acetylacetone 13, sodium hydroxide, water and ethanol was added. Subsequently, the solid precipitate was filtered to yield the target hydrazone32 [Scheme 4].32

Synthesis of hydrazone 15via an aryl diazonium intermediate.

Microwave-assisted synthesis

Magda et al. conducted microwave-assisted synthesis of hydrazones 18a–o by dissolving 2-phenylacetohydrazide 16 and various benzaldehydes 17a–o in ethanol and subjecting the reagents to microwave radiation (300 W of power, for 7 min at 155 °C). The reduced reaction time and sustainable approach in the synthesis serve as an important advantage of such reactions33 [Scheme 5].33

Microwave-assisted synthesis of hydrazones 18a–o.

Solvent-free reactions

Abu-Melha et al. reported solvent-free synthesis of hydrazone 21 employing a grinding technique in the condensation of ketone 19 and hydrazide 20 using acetic acid (catalyst)34 [Scheme 6].34

Grinding technique to synthesize hydrazone 21.

Ultrasonic irradiation technique

Rezki et al. utilized an ultrasound irradiation technique to synthesize the desired hydrazone 24 from isoniazid 22 and 4-fluorobenzaldehyde 23 using acetic acid (catalytic) in ethanol35 [Scheme 7].35

Ultrasonic irradiation technique employed to synthesize hydrazone 24.

Effect of pressure

Costa and coworkers synthesized hydrazones 33a–i [Scheme 10]38 by condensing hydrazides 32 and aldehyde 31 at room temperature without employing solvent with increasing pressure from 0.001 to 3.8 kbar using atmospheric pressure as the control and found that the reaction occurring at the lowest pressure of 0.001 kbar provided a yield of up to 80%. Increasing the pressure up to 0.7 kbar increased the yield by 11%, providing over 90% yield. A successive increase in pressure had a minimal effect on increasing the yield without a notable increase, terming that it could only favour the reaction conditions. Since there was no further increase in yield after 1.4 kbar, further investigation with this pressure being used in 10 cycles yielded 96% yield, which could be treated as the optimal condition for the synthesis. In addition, as depicted in Scheme 10, reactions proceeded under high hydrostatic pressure and provided a higher yield than that obtained in the control conditions.38

Effect of pressure on the percentage yield of hydrazones 33a–i.

Effect of irradiation

Al-Rasheed et al. used ultrasonic radiation to enhance the rate of hydrazone formation and compared the yield and reaction time to the conventional technique [Scheme 11].39 The ultrasonic irradiated reactions were carried out at 40 °C, whereas conventional reactions were performed at reflux temperature in ethanol. The reactions carried out using ultrasonic radiation significantly improved the yield (>90%) at time intervals of 30 to 60 minutes, while conventional reactions gave approximately 70% yield at 4–5 h.39

Quinoline hydrazide–hydrazones and their anticancer activities

Quinoline is a fused heterocyclic molecule with the molecular formula of C9H7N, comprising a pyridine ring fused with a phenyl ring.41 These pharmaceutically active molecules are less toxic and their diverse biological applications have gained significant attention from researchers, making them important heterocycles in the field of medicinal chemistry.42 The presence of a nitrogen atom (−I effect) contributes to its anticancer properties, since it can bind to various amino acid residues in target proteins.43 There are various quinoline-based anticancer drugs such as bosutinib,44 cabozantinib,45 and topotecan,46 as depicted in Fig. 1. Furthermore, introducing a hydrazone functionality into the quinoline group can enhance the structural adaptability and biomolecular interactions.47 Hence, due to this synergistic combination, quinoline–hydrazones have gained prominence in tumor studies.

Quinoline-based anticancer drugs.

Bingul et al. synthesized various quinoline hydrazones 44a–f and nonquinoline hydrazones 45a–c and investigated their antitumor activities against various cancer cells using cell viability assays.48 Compound 44d, bearing two methoxy substituents at the 5th and 7th positions of the quinoline ring, exhibited the highest cytotoxicity against both breast (33% and 38% cell reductions with respect to MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cell lines, respectively) and neuroblastoma cell lines (82% and 96% cell reductions in SH-SY5Y and Kelly cell lines, respectively). Compound 44c demonstrated the second-best cytotoxicity, with 68% and 88% of SH-SY5Y and Kelly cancer cell reductions, respectively. The three nonquinoline hydrazones displayed lesser activity than the quinoline hydrazones, with cell reductions of less than 10% against MCF-7 cancer cell lines, and being even less active towards neuroblastoma cells, thus highlighting the importance of the quinoline ring in cytotoxic activity. In the dose-response experiments, compound 44d displayed IC50 values of 1.3 and 2.9 µM against the Kelly and SH-SY5Y cells, respectively. In addition, compound 44d reduced only 30% of normal lung fibroblast, displaying their selectivity towards cancerous cells. Further, it reduced the cell count in the S phase and G2/M phase, which indicates that compound 44d prevents cell proliferation and induces cell cycle arrest by upregulating p27kip1 (ref. 48) (Fig. 2).48,49 Jia-Xing Lu et al. synthesized quinoline-based dihydrazones, and the in vitro cytotoxic study revealed that compound 45b displayed pronounced anticancer activities against BGC-823 (IC50 = 9.12 ± 0.19 µM), BEL-7402 (IC50 = 10.22 ± 0.12 µM), MCF-7 (IC50 = 7.05 ± 0.05 µM) and A549 (IC50 = 7.32 ± 0.05 µM) cancer cells, even superior to that of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) (IC50 values = 15.18 ± 0.05, 15.81 ± 0.01, 11.32 ± 0.78, and 11.77 ± 0.89 µM, respectively); however, it was less cytotoxic than the standard sorafenib. It displayed less cytotoxicity towards normal cell lines (IC50 > 100 µM) than 5-FU (IC50 = 20.83 ± 0.05 µM), indicating better selectivity. Compound 45c displayed potent cytotoxicity against BEL-7402 (IC50 = 11.14 ± 1.02 µM) and MCF-7 (IC50 = 10.39 ± 0.52 µM) cancer cell lines, superior to that of 5-FU, with better selectivity (IC50 > 100 µM against normal cells). These two compounds had a phenyl group on the pyrimidine backbone, which enhanced the conjugation, which can be attributed to their increased anticancer effects. In addition, compound 45a (IC50 = 7.01 ± 0.26 µM) displayed superior cytotoxicity against MCF-7 cancer cells relative to the standard. The docking results revealed that compounds 45a and 45b had BEs of −7.9 kcal mol−1 and −7.2 kcal mol−1, respectively, indicating stronger binding with DNA. Compound 45b formed H-bonding interactions with DG-8 and DG-34 DNA base pairs, while compound 45c formed H-bonding interactions with DG-8 DNA base pairs. Molecular docking studies carried out on the CDK2 active binding site, which is an enzyme that plays an important role in cell division, revealed that compounds 45a and 45b displayed better binding interactions with the enzyme than the rest of the derivatives, which was in accordance with the in vitro results. Notably, the N-atom in the hydrazone linkage in 45a displayed strong H-bonding interaction with the Lys-88 amino acid residue, while 45b formed H-bonding interaction with the Arg-199 residue. The quinoline ring formed π–π stacking interactions with Ala-201 and Asp-92 residues49 (Fig. 3).49,50 Puskullu et al. studied the cytotoxic activities of the synthesized quinoline hydrazones against A549 and MCF-7 cancer cell lines by an MTT assay. At 100 µM, compound 46 displayed better cytotoxicity by reducing the percentage viability to 59.28% in A549 cancer cells50 (Fig. 3).51 Özcan et al. synthesized quinoline-based sulfonyl hydrazones and studied their cytotoxicity against breast cancer cell line using the MTT assay. Compounds were found to be more active against the ER-negative MDA-MB-231 cells. More specifically, compounds 47a (IC50 = 6.8 µM) bearing chloro at the 2nd and 4th positions, 47b (IC50 = 6.96 µM) bearing a trifluoromethyl group and 47c bearing a chloro substituent (IC50 = 8.6 µM) emerged as potent anticancer agents, displaying potent activity against MDA-MB-231 cancer cells, similar to cisplatin (IC50 = 6.93 µM). Hence, their selectivity was also evaluated by treating them against the normal fibroblast cell line L929, and it was found that compounds 47a (IC50 = 42.64 µM), 47b (IC50 = 297.6 µM) and 47c (IC50 = 96.2 µM) displayed much lower cytotoxicity than the standard (IC50 = 18.36 µM). Hence, compounds with electron-accepting halogens displayed better cytotoxicity than those bearing EDGs, primarily, disubstituted halogens. In addition, compound 47b (p < 0.001) with higher cytotoxicity and selectivity induced apoptotic cell death more efficiently than the other two compounds, as visualized using a fluorescence microscope in the TUNEL assay. The docking studies involving these three compounds in the active site of CDK2 were studied, and it was found that the sulfonyl oxygen and fluorine groups formed H-bonding interactions with the Leu83 and Gln85 residues, respectively. Additionally, it formed certain hydrophobic interactions through quinoline and trifluoromethyl rings. These interactions justify the importance of quinoline and sulfonyl hydrazone groups in imparting the cytotoxic behaviour. Compound 47b can be important in this context, not only for the interactions it displayed, but also for its appearance as a stable ligand in the active site of CDK2 when investigated in MD simulations. The simulation results revealed stable hydrophobic interactions of the quinoline, phenyl and trifluoromethyl groups, along with H-bonding interactions of fluorine, quinoline nitrogen and –NH of the hydrazone fragment with various amino acid residues51 (Fig. 4).52 A. E. El-Helw et al. synthesized various benzo[f]quinoline-based hydrazones and evaluated their cytotoxicity against HCT-116 and MCF-7 cancer cells using the MTT assay. Compounds 48 (IC50 = 7.39 ± 0.5 µM against HCT-116, IC50 = 9.24 ± 0.7 µM against MCF-7) and 49 (IC50 = 13.46 ± 1.1 µM against HCT-116, IC50 = 16.43 ± 1.3 µM against MCF-7) demonstrated potent cytotoxicity against both cancer cell lines, but were eventually less cytotoxic than the standard doxorubicin (IC50 = 5.23 ± 0.3 µM against HCT-116, and IC50 = 4.17 ± 0.2 µM against MCF-7). Compound 48 formed hydrophobic interactions with VAL 18 (4.07 Å), VAL 18 (4.33 Å), GLN 131 (4.82 Å) and ALA 144 (4.37 Å), while compound 49 formed H-bonding interactions with HIS 84 (3.30 Å), LYS 89 (3.54 Å), LYS 89 (3.14 Å) and LYS 20 (3.72 Å) in the target protein CDK-5. In addition, compounds 48 (0.9477 Å) and 49 (1.4889 Å) had much lower RMSD values than the cocrystallised ligand (1.9624 Å) indicating stable docking pose in the active site of the protein52 (Fig. 5).

Božić et al. synthesized mono- and bis-quinoline-based (thio)carbohydrazones and tested their antiproliferative activity against acute monocytic leukemia (THP-1) and pancreatic adenocarcinoma cancer stem cells (AsPC-1).53 Thiocarbohydrazones displayed superior anticancer activity to carbohydrazones against THP-1 cells, and thiocarbohydrazones followed the apoptotic cell death pathway, while carbohydrazones followed the necrotic pathway, which can trigger side effects if used as medications. Most importantly, compounds 51d (ED50 = 2.4 ± 0.2 µM) and 51a (ED50 = 8.4 ± 0.3 µM) displayed better cytotoxicity. With respect to AsPC-1 cells, carbohydrazones provided better results with compounds 50b (ED50 = 35.2 ± 0.7 µM) and 50e (ED50 = 57 ± 2 µM), displaying lower ED50 values than thiocarbohydrazones 51b (ED50 = 75 ± 3 µM) and 51e (ED50 = 74 ± 5 µM). Compounds 50b and 51b had 8-quinolinyl groups, while compounds 50e and 51e had 8-hydroxy-2-quinolinyl groups, and thus they were structurally similar apart from containing sulphur and oxygen53 (Fig. 6).54 Abd El-Lateef et al. synthesized (quinoline-2-carbonyl)hydrazide derivatives and studied their in vitro anti-proliferative activity against MCF-7 cancer cell lines using the MTT assay. Compound 52c (IC50 = 2.71 ± 0.09 µM), bearing a nitro group at the 4th position of the benzene ring, displayed potent cytotoxicity against breast cancer cells, superior to that of the standard drug doxorubicin (IC50 = 6.18 ± 0.20 µM). Compounds 52a (IC50 = 3.39 ± 0.11 µM) and 52b (IC50 = 5.94 ± 0.17 µM), bearing 4-methyl and 3,4-dimethoxy groups displayed superior anticancer activities to those of the standard drug. The most potent compounds 52a and 52c were studied for EGFR-TK inhibition, since they regulate cell-proliferation and apoptosis activation. Compound 52c (IC50 = 0.22 µM) displayed the strongest inhibition comparable to lapatinib (IC50 = 0.18 µM). In addition, at IC50 = 2.71 µM, compound 52c displayed cell cycle arrest in the G1 phase and a decrease in the cell population in the S and G2/M phases. Further, compound 52c increased primary apoptosis from 0.39% to 9.55%, while 0.11% to 17.78% late apoptosis was observed with respect to the DMSO control, which signifies an apoptotic induction pathway in the anti-proliferation of compound 52c (ref. 54) (Fig. 7).

Cytotoxic quinoline hydrazones 52a–c.54

Shailesh et al. prepared various quinoline-1,3-oxazole hybrids and studied their anticancer potential against NCI 60 cancer cells.55 The authors studied the cytotoxicity at a single dose concentration (10 µM) for the preliminary screening and found that nine of the synthesized compounds (53b, 53c, 53d, 53e, 53f, 53g, 53h, 53i and 53j) displayed cytotoxicity against 60 human cancer cells. Further, five dose anticancer screening at 10-fold dilutions revealed that compound 53d displayed the highest antiproliferative activity against leukemia K-562 cells (GI50 = 0.26 µM) and leukemia SR cancer cells (GI50 = 0.33 µM). The results indicated that the EDGs had higher cytotoxicity than the EWGs55 (Fig. 8).56 Vladimir et al. synthesized various quinoline–thiazolyl–hydrazone hybrids and investigated their antiproliferative activity against five different cancer cells using the MTT assay. They synthesized hydrazones based on three different quinoline aldehydes such as 8-quinolyl, 2-quinolyl and 8-hydroxy-2-quinolyl groups, among which the compounds derived from the 8-hydroxy-2-quinolyl scaffold displayed pronounced anticancer activity. Compound 56 (bearing a methoxy substituent) displayed the highest anticancer activity among the three compounds containing an 8-hydroxy-2-quinolyl group, against all the cancer cells; A549 (GI50 = 7.59 ± 0.7 µM), HCT-116 (GI50 = 5.19 ± 0.91 µM), MCF-7 (GI50 = 6.35 ± 1.63 µM), U-2 OS (GI50 = 6.15 ± 0.74 µM), Hep-G2 (GI50 = 1.85 ± 0.14 µM) and HEK-293 (GI50 = 8.44 ± 1.21 µM), were superior to that of 5-FU (GI50 = 8.12 ± 1.01, 5.78 ± 0.38, 8.41 ± 0.98, 13.02 ± 0.03, 18.98 ± 1.98, and 11.09 ± 1.53 µM, respectively). Compound 54 displayed superior anticancer activity against HCT-116 (GI50 = 4.43 ± 1.40 µM), MCF-7 (GI50 = 6.12 ± 2.39 µM), U-2 OS (GI50 = 6.25 ± 0.43 µM), HepG2 (GI50 = 1.17 ± 0.29 µM) and HEK-293 (GI50 = 7.14 ± 0.34 µM) cancer cells. All three compounds displayed potent anticancer activity, superior to that of 5-FU, against HCT-116 and Hep-G2 cancer cells. In addition, compound 56 resulted in the accumulation of cells in S-phase, in HCT-116 cells (p < 0.0001), suggesting DNA damage. Hence, γ-H2AX analysis was conducted, which further supported the results by displaying DNA double-strand breaks in HCT-116 cells after 24 h treatment56 (Fig. 8).

Cytotoxic quinoline hydrazones 53a–l, 54, 55 and 56.55,56

Abbas et al. prepared various quinoline–chalcone hybrids and evaluated their cytotoxicity against different human cancer cells (NCI-60).57 In a single-dose assay conducted against nine different cancer cells, compounds 57 (G.I. = 73.03%) and 58 (G.I. = 73.98%) displayed significant growth inhibition of most of the tumor cells (GI50 = 0.3–52 µM). Both compounds displayed superior cytotoxicity against A549 and K-562 cancer cells, with GI50 values of 0.3–6 µM. Further, compound 57 displayed potent cytotoxicity against A549 (IC50 = 3.91 ± 0.31 µM) and K-562 chronic myelogenous leukemia cells (IC50 = 1.91 ± 0.21 µM), and was superior to that of cisplatin (IC50 values of 15.3 ± 1.87 and 2.71 ± 0.34 µM, respectively). Compound 58 displayed comparable cytotoxicity against the two cancer cells (IC50 = 5.29 ± 0.19 and 2.67 ± 0.27 µM, respectively) as that of the standard employed. A comparable structural feature that both compounds possessed is the presence of an electron-accepting chloro group on the first ring and an electron-donating inductive effect of methoxy and methyl substituents on the third ring. Further, A549 cells treated with compound 57 caused a significant increase in the population up to 76.2% at a concentration of 6 µM in the G2/M phase, while compound 58 increased the population in the G2/M phase up to 59.6% at identical concentrations, indicating cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase. Similarly, compounds 57 and 58 induced cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase in K-562 cells with populations of cells of 49.3% and 41.7%, respectively, at 6 µM. In addition, compound 57 displayed potent PI3K-γ isoform inhibition (IC50 = 52.03 nM), which suggests the potential ability of compound 57 in anticancer drug discovery57 (Fig. 9).58 Georgiana et al. synthesized various heterocyclic hydrazone hybrids comprising phenyl, quinoline, pyridine, benzothiazole and imidazole rings and tested their anticancer activities against a panel of NCI-60 cancer cells. In the single-dose screening, compounds 59, 60, 61, 62, 63 and 64 displayed superior cytotoxicity against all the tested cancer cells. In five-dose screening, compounds 60, 61, 62, 63 and 64 emerged as potent cytotoxic agents with GI50 values in the sub-micromolar range of 120 nM. More specifically, compound 63 emerged as the most potent cytotoxic derivative with cytotoxicity in the range of 0.2–0.46 (leukemia cells), 0.37 (A549), 0.18–0.76 (colon cancer), 0.27 (CNS cancer), 0.92 (prostate cancer) and 0.95 (breast cancer) µM. All these compounds had a 2-choroquinoline moiety linked to the indole ring via a hydrazone linkage in their structure, which could be associated with their higher cytotoxicity. The compounds in which the 2-chloroquinoline ring was replaced with dihydroimidazole and di-aryl rings were inactive, which indicates that the quinoline group is the key contributor to cytotoxicity58 (Fig. 9).

Cytotoxic quinoline hydrazones 57–64.57,58

Purvi et al. prepared various 8-hydroxyquinoline hydrazones and evaluated their cytotoxicity against HeLa, MCF-7, A-549 and MDA-MB-231 cancer cells using the MTT assay.59 Compound 65, bearing a fluoro substituent, displayed the highest cytotoxicity against all the cancer cells with IC50 values ranging from 26.3 to 38.77 µM, but was eventually less cytotoxic than docetaxel (IC50 = 3.37–4.46 µM). Further, it induced the apoptosis in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells59 (Fig. 10).60 In a study conducted by Atamjit et al., again, compound 66, with a fluoro substituent, displayed 93% MCF-7 cancer cell growth inhibition at 100 µM concentration. The importance of electron-withdrawing (−I effect) halogen derivatives was further evidenced by the higher growth inhibition displayed by compounds 67 and 68 bearing chloro and bromo substituents, displaying 85% and 79% inhibition, respectively. Compound 66 further displayed higher selectivity towards MCF-7 cancer cells (GI50 = 4.36 µM) without much effect on L929 normal cells (GI50 = 52.42 µM) with an SI value of 12.02. In addition, it resulted in cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase (50.1% accumulation). Further, docking study results revealed that the chloroquinoline moiety directs the molecule to the hydrophobic pocket of ERα by interacting with various amino acid residues. The hydrazone group formed a H-bonding interaction with the Asp351 residue, which was responsible for the strong binding of the molecule in the active site. In addition, the fluorine atom attached to isatin interacted with Met528 through halogen-type interaction, isatin formed π-donor H-bonding interaction with Cys530, and H-bonding interaction of morpholine oxygen with Met528 residues were also observed, which justifies the superior cytotoxicity60 (Fig. 10).61 Abdullah et al. synthesized various quinoline–amidrazone derivatives and evaluated their anticancer activity against A549 and MCF-7 cancer cells using the MTT assay. Compound 69 displayed the highest cytotoxicity against both A-549 (IC50 = 43.1 µM) and MCF-7 (IC50 = 59.1 µM against A549) cancer cells, but was less effective than cisplatin (IC50 = 22 µM) and doxorubicin (IC50 = 5 µM against MCF-7). Further, the docking studies conducted on c-Abl kinase revealed that compound 69 formed three H-bonding interactions with Glu316, Met318 and Glu286 residues via phenolic –OH and –NH of amidrazone, with an additional π–π stacking interaction of the quinoline ring with the ASP381 residue61 (Fig. 10).62 Lakhnotra et al. prepared various quinoline-triazole hybrids linked via hydrazone fragments and evaluated their cytotoxicity using the MTT assay. Compound 70 exhibited the most potent cytotoxicity against MCF-7 (IC50 = 0.31 ± 0.078 µM), A549 (IC50 = 0.14 ± 0.058 µM), and A2780 (IC50 = 1.36 ± 0.024 µM), superior to that of the positive control etoposide (IC50 = 2.11 ± 0.024 µM against MCF-7, 3.08 ± 0.135 µM against A549 and 1.31 ± 0.27 µM against A2780, respectively). In addition, compounds 71 (IC50 = 0.58 ± 0.072 µM against MCF-7 and 0.34 ± 0.042 µM against A549), 72 (IC50 = 0.96 ± 0.082 µM against MCF-7, 0.69 ± 0.053 µM against A549, and 0.12 ± 0.021 µM against Colo-205) and 73 (IC50 = 1.32 ± 0.022 µM against MCF-7 and 1.10 ± 0.082 µM against A549) displayed potent antitumour activities compared with the positive control following compound 70. Indole hydrazide–hydrazone anticancer activities

Indole is a bicyclic compound comprising a phenyl ring fused with a pyrrole ring, with ten delocalizing electrons resonating between the two rings, making it susceptible to electrophilic substitution.64 This heterocycle is found in various natural and bioactive compounds because of its ability to interact with more than one receptor, making it pharmacologically important.65 Sunitib, alectinib, osimertinib, and nintedanib are some of the FDA-approved anticancer drugs consisting of indole moieties66 as depicted in Fig. 12. Moreover, the inclusion of hydrazone linkages, which are versatile bioactive scaffolds that enhance pharmaceutical applications and biointeractions,67 in heterocyclic molecules like indole enhances the pharmacological potential of the resultant hybrid, the synergistic effect of which can be prominent in anticancer drug discovery.

Indole-based anticancer drugs.

Compound 80, bearing a trimethoxy substituent, displayed potent cytotoxicity against PC3 (IC50 = 3.89 µM), LnCaP (IC50 = 5.31 µM), MCF-7 (IC50 = 0.87 µM) and PaCa2 (IC50 = 6.62 µM) cancer cell lines. In addition, compound 81, bearing an electron-withdrawing chloro substituent, displayed potent antitumor activities against a breast cancer cell line (IC50 = 0.42 µM)68 (Fig. 13).69 Salama et al. synthesized various indole-based hydrazones and conducted in vitro and in vivo anticancer studies, along with their kinase inhibition activity. Triazole hydrazide–hydrazones anticancer activities

Triazole is a five-membered heterocyclic compound containing three nitrogen atoms, and is well-known for its anticancer activity.86 Triazoles exist in two isomeric forms, namely, 1,2,3-triazole and 1,2,4-triazole, and there exist effective triazole-based anticancer drugs letrozole, anastrozole, talazoparib and vorozole,87 as depicted in Fig. 19. Hence, triazole–hydrazone hybrids are evolving bioactive scaffolds in the field of anticancer drug development.

Triazole-based anticancer drugs.

Muhammed et al. studied in vitro and in vivo antiproliferative activities of triazole-hydrazone hybrids derived from (S)-naproxen.88 Compound 126 (IC50 = 26.0 µM) displayed superior cytotoxicity against PC3 cancer cells than cisplatin (IC50 = 39.9 µM). It displayed less cytotoxicity against normal prostate epithelial cell lines (107.1 µmol L−1) than cisplatin (43.3 µmol L−1), indicating its cancer-specific cytotoxicity. Treatment of compound 126 with PC3 cells at a concentration of 40 µM for 12 h confirmed the rise in Bax, Caspase 3, and Caspase 9 expression levels in a time-dependent manner, and the decrease in Bcl-2 mRNA levels in RT-PCR analysis. These results indicated that compound 126 increased apoptotic markers and decreased antiapoptotic markers. Further, docking studies conducted on the MetAP2 enzyme suggested that compound 126 formed stable binding interactions with a binding energy of −10.38 kcal mol−1, through H-bonding interactions between the methoxy group and the ARG337 residue, the –NH group and the ASP262 residue, and the nitro and HIS339 residues. In addition, the fluoro group displayed a halogen interaction with the PHE383 residue, which contributed to its efficient binding, which validates the in vitro activity of compound 126. Benzimidazole hydrazide–hydrazone anticancer activities

Benzimidazole is a bicyclic fused N-containing heterocycle comprising a benzene ring and an imidazole ring, which can form various noncovalent interactions with the protein target.98 Numerous anticancer drugs such as bendamustine, abemaciclib, dovitinib, selumetinib, binimetinib and glasdegib contain a benzimidazole moiety in their molecular structure99 (Fig. 24). Hence, studies of the anticancer activities of hydrazone hybrids of this bioactive heterocycle are regularly listed with the aim of discovering new anticancer drugs bearing benzimidazole.

Isatin hydrazide–hydrazone anticancer activities

Isatin (1H-indole-2, 3-dione) is a natural, plant-derived organic molecule that is capable of exhibiting anticancer activity.111 Among the diverse biological applications exhibited by isatins, their anticancer activities have been studied extensively, with several isatin-based anticancer drugs, namely, toceranib, semaxanib and orantinib (Fig. 29) known to exhibit superior anticancer effects with semaxanib and orantinib still under clinical trials,112 which makes it important to develop anticancer drugs containing isatin groups, with isatin–hydrazones being a suitable choice moving forward.

Isatin-based anticancer drugs.

Ibrahim et al. synthesized bis-isatin hydrazones and evaluated their cytotoxicity against HCT-116, MCF-7 and HepG2 cancer cells.113 Compound 178, bearing a methoxy substituent, displayed potent anticancer activity against MCF-7 (IC50 = 1.84 ± 0.29 µM) and HCT-116 (IC50 = 3.31 ± 0.83 µM) cancer cells, superior to that of doxorubicin (IC50 = 2.57 ± 0.43, 3.70 ± 0.18 µM, respectively). It was also the most potent cytotoxic derivative against HepG2 cells among the other compounds, with an IC50 value of 6.99 ± 0.88 µM. Further, compound 178 restricted cell proliferation in the pre-G phase by 5-fold in MCF-7 cells at its IC50 concentration coupled with the G2/M phase arrest, and it induced apoptosis in MCF-7 cells by displaying a 6.7-fold increase in apoptotic cells compared with the control113 (Fig. 30).114 In a cytotoxic study conducted by Munir et al., using the MTT assay against MCF-7 cells, compounds 179 and 180 displayed prominent cytotoxicity with the percentage of cell viability values of 17.37% and 19.13%, respectively, with IC50 values of 15.8 ± 0.6 and 16.8 ± 0.9 µM, respectively. In addition, compounds 179 (75.23% viability) and 180 (78.14% viability) displayed less cytotoxicity against MCF-10 A normal cells (IC50 > 50 µM). Docking studies performed on EGFR kinase receptor protein justified the in vitro results with compounds 179 and 180 displaying the highest docking scores of −7.561 kcal mol−1 and −4.19 kcal mol−1, respectively, through a key H-bonding interaction with the M769 residue, predicting a plausible mechanism of these two compounds for their anticancer activity114 (Fig. 30).115 Mehran et al. synthesized three isatin–hydrazone hybrids using nanocatalyst (SBA–Pr–N–Is–Bu–SO3H) and evaluated their anticancer activities using the MTT assay. The in vitro results were further supported by in silico docking studies performed on the ERα receptor protein, where compound 181 showed the highest BE of −7.12 kcal mol−1, through various hydrophobic, halogen bonding and van der Waals interactions with different amino acid residues, which could be a plausible mechanism for its anticancer action115 (Fig. 30).116 Marwa et al. studied the anticancer activity of isatin–hydrazone hybrids against a panel of 59 human cancer cells and found that MCF-7 cells were more sensitive. Further, CDK2 enzyme inhibition assay results revealed that compounds 189 (IC50 = 0.2456 µM) and 190 (IC50 = 0.3006 µM) displayed effective enzyme inhibition activities than the reference inhibitor imatinib (IC50 = 0.1312 µM), which could be a plausible anticancer mechanistic approach. Further, docking studies conducted on the CDK2 target protein suggested that compound 189 (−6.5 kcal mol−1) bound to the active site of the CDK2 domain more effectively than compound 190 (−5.9 kcal mol−1), through various H-bonding, π–alkyl, halogen and π–σ interactions with various amino acid residues, thus supporting the in vitro results118 (Fig. 31).119 Israa A. Seliem synthesized various isatin–thiazole hybrids and evaluated their cytotoxicity against breast cancer cells. Compound 191 (IC50 = 10.85 ± 1.19 and 14.45 ± 1.89 µM) displayed the most potent cytotoxicity against MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells and was superior to that of doxorubicin (IC50 = 25.14 ± 1.67 and 36.59 ± 2.12 µM, respectively). In addition, compound 191 (IC50 = 112.4 µM) displayed potent α-glycosidase inhibitory effects superior to that of the reference inhibitor acarbose (IC50 = 393.7 µM). Further, compound 191 (IC50 = 0.0998 µM) displayed potent AChE enzyme inhibition activity compared with donepezil (IC50 = 0.1655 µM). The docking studies conducted on Bcl-2 antiapoptotic protein (PDB ID: 4LVT, 2XAO) suggested that compound 191 displayed the most effective binding with BE values of −7.8 kcal mol−1 and −10.7 kcal mol−1 in the two proteins, respectively, higher than navitoclax (−5.4 kcal mol−1 in 4LVT) and tamoxifen (−8.3 kcal mol−1 in 2XAO), forming H-bonding interactions through hydrazone nitrogen and coumarin oxygen atoms with LYS C:57 and SER C:60 residues, respectively, with the isatin group involved in various hydrophobic interactions, thus improving the binding affinity119 (Fig. 31).

Comparative structure–activity relationship studies of N-heterocyclic hydrazone hybrids

A comprehensive review of the anticancer activities of various heterocyclic–hydrazone hybrids reveals certain structural features that consistently enhance cytotoxicity, such as the influence of electron-withdrawing and electron-donating groups, and heterocyclic systems.

Effect of electron-withdrawing substituents

It was evident from this comprehensive study that most of the quinoline–hydrazone hybrids displayed superior cytotoxicity when electron-withdrawing groups such as halogens and nitro group were bound to the aromatic core. Quinoline hydrazones bearing electron-withdrawing halogens or nitro groups displayed superior anticancer activity compared with the standard employed.50–52,54,59,60,62

A similar effect was observed for indole-based hydrazones where compounds bearing electron-withdrawing halogens displayed superior cytotoxicity.71,74,75,77,78,81,82,84 Although some of the compounds in triazole–hydrazone hybrids88,94,96,97 and benzimidazole–hydrazone hybrids101,106,107 containing electron-withdrawing substituents displayed superior anticancer activity, the majority of these derivatives consisted of electron-donating groups.

Finally, isatin–hydrazone derivatives containing electron-withdrawing substituents displayed prominent cytotoxic activities.115,117,118 Overall, across various heterocyclic–hydrazone hybrids, electron-withdrawing halogens, primarily chloro and fluoro, displayed superior cytotoxicity, which could be a positive take when designing anticancer drugs.

Effect of electron-donating substituents

Most of the quinoline hydrazones displaying significant cytotoxicity consisted of electron-withdrawing groups as discussed above. While a significant number of indole–hydrazone hybrids bearing electron-withdrawing halogens displayed superior cytotoxicity, the effects of electron-donating hydroxy, methoxy, methyl and amine groups were equally prominent, with superior anti-proliferative activity.68,70,72,76,80,82 Most of these derivatives consisted of methoxy substituents as the electron-donating group, and attached to the 4th position of the aromatic ring. With respect to substitution in the indole ring, the methoxy group occupied the 5th or 6th position. In addition, triazole–hydrazones consisted of hydroxy or methoxy groups as electron-donating substituents, primarily occupying the 3rd or 4th position of the benzene ring.89–92,95

The electron-donating substituents primarily contributed to the anticancer activity with respect to benzimidazole-based hydrazones. These derivatives consisted of methoxy and hydroxy substituents linked to the aromatic rings and benzene ring of the benzimidazole moiety. The methoxy and hydroxy substituents were primarily found at the 3rd and 4th positions of the aromatic ring, and these substituents were present at the benzylidene part, which could be considered a positive approach when designing benzimidazole–hydrazone-based anticancer drugs.101,103–105,107,108,110

In addition, substituting isatin-based hydrazones with electron-donating groups seems to increase the anticancer activities of the resulting compounds. Isatin hydrazones consisting of electron-donating methoxy and hydroxy substituents enhanced the anticancer activities of the resulting compounds.113,116,117,120–122 Similar to the observations of benzimidazole hydrazones, these electron-donating substituents occupied the 3rd and 4th positions of the benzene rings.

Effect of heterocycles

Quinoline hydrazones have displayed superior cytotoxicity compared with nonquinoline hydrazones, owing to the importance of the quinoline ring.48 In addition, bis quinoline hydrazones displayed significantly higher cytotoxicity as reported in one of the studies.49 Furthermore, quinoline combined with various heterocycles such as oxazole,55 thiazole,56 indole,58 isatin60 and triazole62 has displayed superior cytotoxicity to the standard employed in the in vitro assays.

Bis-indole moieties linked through hydrazone linkages were studied for indole-based hydrazones, where the derivatives displayed superior cytotoxicity.69,80,82,84 In addition, indole was linked to heterocycles, such as triazole and benzothiazole groups,83,85 and was used as a positive approach in designing anticancer drugs. Furthermore, triazole-based hydrazones linked to heterocycles such as furan, isatin, benzothiazole and indole88,89,93,95,97 displayed superior cytotoxicity. Benzimidazole groups linked to thiophene, thiazole and furan groups displayed significant anticancer activity in the in vitro assays.100,102,106,107 Furthermore, bis-isatin113 (104), and isatin groups linked to pyridine,114,116,120 thiazole,119 quinoline and quinazolines121 displayed enhanced cytotoxicities. Hence, combining two similar heterocycles or with heterocycles such as thiazole, furan and triazole groups has been used frequently among the different N-containing heterocycles that have been listed in this review, which could be considered a positive molecular design in the development of anticancer drugs.

Selectivity

Among various N-heterocycles, most of the cytotoxic triazole-based hydrazones displayed cancer-specific activity.91,92 Furthermore, the triazole group combined with heterocycles, such as isatin,84,95 furan88 and imidazo[2,1-b]thiazole94 displayed significant cancer-specific cytotoxicity results. Furthermore, owing to the cancer-specific cytotoxicity of isatin derivatives, which are not limited to the above-mentioned isatin–triazole hybrids, isatin–indole84 and isatin hydrazone individually115 displayed significant selectivity. In addition, bis-quinoline hydrazone49 and benzimidazole hydrazone linked to thiophene102 and thiazole106 groups displayed cancer-specific cytotoxic results.

Overall, quinoline-, indole- and isatin-based hydrazones with electron-withdrawing groups such as chloro and fluoro groups displayed superior anticancer activities, while benzimidazole hydrazones with electron-donating substituents such as hydroxy and alkoxy groups enhanced the antiproliferative activity. With respect to heterocycles, bis-indole hydrazones and isatin–pyridine hydrazone hybrids displayed excellent cytotoxicity. In addition, thiazole, a heterocycle, was combined with all the N-containing heterocyclic hydrazones reviewed, which resulted in a positive approach in enhancing the cytotoxicity. Furthermore, triazole- and isatin-based hydrazones have displayed significant cancer-specific activities, which can be useful in the designing of anticancer drugs. In addition to the heterocyclic scaffolds, the hydrazone linkage is involved in various binding interactions with essential amino acid residues in the target protein in different molecules, marking the importance of this molecular fragment in targeting receptor sites and enhancing the anticancer activities.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in recent years, significant work evaluating the anticancer potential of N-heterocycle–hydrazone has been conducted, and these hybrid molecules have displayed promising anticancer activity. Hydrazones are combined as a bioactive scaffold with N-heterocycles because of their ease of synthesis, easy isolation due to crystallinity and structural flexibility. Overall, this review presents various synthetic strategies involved in the synthesis of hydrazones including conventional condensation, acid- and base-catalysed reactions, from azo compounds, microwave and ultrasonic irradiation techniques. The kinetic effects of solvents, catalysts, pressure and irradiation techniques have been briefly presented to understand their effects on the reaction rate and yield. Most importantly, a comprehensive explanation of the anticancer activities of various N-heterocyclic hydrazones including quinoline-, indole-, triazole-, benzimidazole-, and isatin–hydrazones has been presented in detail, along with the mechanism of action, cell-cycle arrest results, docking interactions and cell death pathway results to provide the readers with combined information on these aspects, which can help in understanding the current state of development and future drug designs.

Among all the derivatives displaying potent in vitro antiproliferative activity, compound 82, a bis-indole hydrazone derivative, and compound 126, a triazole hydrazone linked to furan and naphthalene groups, comprising fluorine, methoxy and nitro groups, were the stand-out molecules because of their significant pre-clinical in vivo results. Compound 82 reduced 46% solid tumour mass in Ehrlich carcinoma (breast cancer)-bearing mice, comparable to 5-fluorouracil (58% growth inhibition). Compound 126, following its superior in vitro anticancer activity against prostate cancer cells (PC3 cells), displayed significant cancer-specificity by accumulating in the prostate of mice after 6 h when the fluorescence intensity of urogenital organs was studied.

The structure–activity relationship analysis revealed the positive influence of electron-withdrawing halogens, primarily fluorine and chloro substituents and nitro groups, and electron-donating substituents, primarily methoxy and hydroxy groups, with this observation being consistent across all the hybrids. Further, enzyme inhibition studies marked a plausible mechanism of action. The docking studies conducted on various target proteins (EGFR, ERα, CDK, tubulin, VEGFR, etc.) displayed various binding interactions between heterocyclic and halogen fragments with various amino acid residues at the active site of the protein contributing to the stable binding interactions. Further, the most active compounds listed induced apoptosis with cell cycle arrest in the G0/G1 and G2/M phases, preventing DNA replication.

Despite these advances, several challenges remain. First, detailed work covering drug design, bioavailability, toxicity profiles, in vitro efficacy studies, and enzyme inhibition studies to provide mechanistic insights, docking studies, simulations, QSAR models, cell-death pathways, cell cycle arrest and in vivo study results is still lacking in the literature. Further optimization should target success in clinical trials and commercialize the drug molecule to contribute to cancer treatment.

Based on structure–activity relationship studies, linking two heterocyclic moieties into a single molecule using hydrazone linkage has resulted in positive results with respect to anticancer activity due to synergistic effects. Further, researchers can concentrate on trying different combinations of these heterocycles to obtain positive results. In addition, incorporating electron-withdrawing groups into quinoline-, indole- and isatin-based hydrazones and electron-donating groups into benzimidazole hydrazones resulted in significant enhancement in the anticancer activity of the resulting compounds. Most of the molecules contained chloro and fluoro substituents as EWGs, and hydroxy and methoxy substituents as EDGs. Hence, researchers can consider these structural arrangements in designing potent anticancer drugs with respect to N-heterocyclic hydrazones.
