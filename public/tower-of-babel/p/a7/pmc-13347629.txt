A Porcine Model of Intervertebral Disc Injury Recapitulates Human Discogenic Pain Via Notochordal Cell Loss and Pain‐Inducing Nucleus Pulposus Cell Emergence
Authors: Giselle Kaneda, Jacob T Wechsler, Melissa Chavez, Julia Sheyn, Karandeep Cheema, Chushu Shen, Dante Rigo De Righi, Lixia Wang, Yin‐Chen Hsu, Pablo Avalos, Yibin Xie, Wafa Tawackoli, Candace Floyd, Debiao Li, Dmitriy Sheyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLower back pain (LBP) is one of the most common causes of disability, with up to 40% of LBP cases attributed to intervertebral disc (IVD) degeneration. While small animal models are widely used to study IVD degeneration and LBP, the small size of their IVDs limits translational and biological relevance. Large animal models more accurately emulate human disease; however, methods of measuring LBP are not well established. The porcine model has been questioned for LBP research, due to notochordal cells (NCs) persistence through life, unlike humans. Here, we developed a comprehensive porcine model with quantitative measures of discogenic pain via biobehavioral testing (BBT), MRI, and multi‐omics tissue analyses of the IVD and DRGs.MethodsUtilizing a previously established porcine annular injury model of IVD degeneration, pigs underwent longitudinal MRI and biobehavioral testing to monitor degenerative changes in the IVD and pain development. At the study endpoint, IVD and dorsal root ganglia (DRG) tissues were collected for multi‐omic analysis.ResultsMRI demonstrated the progression of IVD degeneration beginning at 4 weeks post‐injury. BBTs showed the development of significant pain responses as early as week 2 post‐injury, supported by transcriptomics of injury‐matched DRGs. Single‐cell transcriptomics, trajectory, and cell–cell communication analyses suggest that, with injury, NCs are differentiating to nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs). Furthermore, NPCs showed upregulation of cellular stress, neural outgrowth, and inflammation pathways, consistent with pain‐inducing distress signals found in human samples.ConclusionsThis study establishes novel MRI and BBT‐based methods for quantifying LBP in pigs and supports its translational relevance to human discogenic LBP. The identification of LBP‐associated clusters mirrors our previous findings in humans. Moreover, the shift of NC to NPC phenotype further supports that the porcine model is relevant to human pathology, as the injury induced accelerated aging and loss of NCs with IVD degeneration and discogenic pain.Keywords: biobehavioral tests, discogenic pain, intervertebral disc degeneration, low back pain, notochordal cells, nucleus pulposus, porcine model

Introduction

Lower back pain (LBP) is the greatest cause of disability worldwide, with an estimated 619 million people suffering globally [1]. This number is expected to rise to 843 million by 2050 due to population aging [1]. Up to 40% of LBP cases can be attributed to intervertebral disc (IVD) degeneration [2, 3]. However, the relationship between disc degeneration and pain is complex, with many cases of disc degeneration being asymptomatic. The disconnect between structural pathology and clinical symptoms has resulted in therapeutic development for LBP treatments falling behind other conditions. Current treatments focus on symptom management and include pharmacological therapies, such as non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs or opioids, and invasive surgical procedures [4]. Thus, there is a clinical need for LBP treatments that alleviate symptoms and reverse disc degeneration.

Discogenic LBP is a multifactorial disease with a variety of factors implicated in its development, including vascularization, inflammation, and innervation [5]. The mechanisms underpinning LBP development have yet to be fully elucidated. The IVD is composed of 3 distinct tissues: the cartilaginous endplate, which adheres the IVD to the adjacent vertebral bodies; the annulus fibrosis (AF), which is the outer portion of the IVD made of concentric rings of fibrocartilage; and the nucleus pulposus (NP), the jelly‐like center of the IVD that develops from the embryonic notochord [5]. During the progression of IVD degeneration, insult to the IVD can induce cell stress and change the mechanical and biochemical homeostasis of the IVD. Stressed cells can induce nociceptive and pro‐inflammatory signaling, thus triggering pain and inflammatory cascades within the IVD, further altering IVD composition [6]. With sustained degeneration, NPCs begin to die off, further exacerbating disruptions to the IVD micro‐environment [7].

In vivo LBP studies are primarily performed in small animal models due to the low cost [8] and well‐established biobehavioral testing methods that can quantify various forms of pain. However, small animal models lack the translational relevance to advance preclinical treatments into clinical trials. Specifically, the smaller IVD size in rodents results in markedly different nutrient diffusion patterns, biomechanical loading conditions, and cellular microenvironment compared to human IVD, limiting the relevance of findings to human pathophysiology [9]. In comparison, large animal models offer superior translational potential due to their closer anatomical similarities to humans [10, 11]. A handful of large animal models have been developed, including canine [12], ovine [13, 14], caprine [15, 16], and porcine [17, 18] to model IVD degeneration. Among these models, the porcine model, specifically the Yucatan minipig, is uniquely suited to model discogenic LBP. In particular, minipigs provide an optimal platform for the development of quantitative measures of LBP due to their overall size, docility, and similar key anatomical and physiological traits, including IVD size [19], biochemical composition [20], and organization of nociceptive pathways. Furthermore, pigs are one of the most widely used models for behavioral and pain studies.

Although healthy porcine discs are known to retain notochordal cells into adulthood, which differs from the human phenotype, this feature enables the investigation of notochordal cell loss and phenotypic transition during disc degeneration, which parallels key aspects of human disc pathophysiology.

In this study, we have comprehensively analyzed a large animal model of IVD degeneration and LBP in Yucatan minipigs [17, 21, 22, 23]. We have utilized our previously established qCEST MR imaging sequences [17, 21, 23, 24, 25] with biobehavioral testing (BBT) to quantify pain. We have also characterized the cellular and molecular landscape of the IVD with injury and confirmed the model's relevance to human discogenic LBP despite anatomical differences such as the presence of NCs in non‐injured porcine discs. Finally, we observed the development of pain‐inducing cells, reflecting what has been observed in human samples [6].

Methods

IVD Degeneration Animal Model

Appropriate Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approval (protocol No. 008066) was obtained prior to conducting any experiments. Due to safety concerns, namely aggression and handling difficulties associated with boars, only female pigs were used in this study to ensure staff safety and experimental feasibility. Survival surgery was performed on female 4–5‐month‐old Yucatan minipigs (Premier Biosource, Ramona, CA). All nine pigs underwent IVD injury at the L3–L6 levels, with non‐injured IVDs from the same animals serving as intra‐animal controls. Behavioral testing to monitor pain development was performed 1 week prior to injury and every other week post injury. MRI imaging was also performed prior to injury and once a month post‐injury to assess IVD degeneration.

Under fluoroscopic guidance, the L3‐L4, L4‐L5, and L5‐L6 were identified and percutaneously punctured with a 17G needle using a dorsolateral approach of approximately 45 degrees from vertical (Figure 1A,B). The needle was advanced to the center of the nucleus pulposus and confirmed in two orthogonal planes (anteroposterior and lateral views). Following confirmation of disc placement, the needle was rotated 360 degrees three times and slowly removed.

At 12 weeks post‐disc injury for pigs harvested for scRNA‐seq, or 16 weeks for all other samples, pigs were euthanized. Following confirmation of euthanasia, injured NP (L3‐L6) and non‐injured controls (L1‐L3, L5‐S1) were harvested. For DRGs, the L2‐L5 levels were harvested. Exact endpoints and allocation of tissues for assays are detailed in Table S1.

Naïve control DRG and NP samples for gene expression analysis were generously donated by Dr. Eugenio Cingolani from pigs undergoing unrelated terminal cardiac procedures. Six female and three male pigs aged 9 to 11.3 months and weighing between 44 and 54 kg were harvested as naïve controls. Pigs either underwent a single dose of experimental exosome treatment (n = 3), oral casein/chitin administration (n = 2), or underwent a cardiac ablation using radiofrequency (n = 4). All pigs underwent identical drug regimens for sedation, anesthesia, and euthanasia as the injured pigs. Tissue collection was performed using the same procedure as the experimental samples. IVD levels L3–L5 and DRG L2–L5 were collected as naïve controls.

Throughout the manuscript, tissues are categorized as follows: “injured” refers to IVDs subjected to needle puncture; “non‐injured” refers to IVDs harvested from uninjured spinal levels within the same animals; and “naïve controls” refers to IVD and DRG tissues obtained from animals undergoing unrelated procedures. DRGs harvested from spinal levels anatomically corresponding to injured and non‐injured disc levels are referred to as “injury‐matched” and “non–injury‐matched,” respectively.

Biobehavioral Testing (BBT)

Biobehavioral testing (BBT, n = 6) was utilized to quantify the development of pain following IVD injury. Tests were adapted from established porcine spinal cord injury models and selected to assess different components of the pain phenotype.

Prior to injury, pigs underwent 4 weeks of socialization and tester training to minimize adverse behaviors and stress‐related responses. One week prior to injury and every other week post injury, pigs underwent a full battery of BBTs.

Testing was performed during morning feeding to minimize behavioral variability and reduce interest in the tester or test procedures. Upon tester arrival, pigs underwent general welfare and pain assessment using the Glasgow Pain Scale (Table S2). After all animals were assessed, pigs were separated into individual kennels and randomly taken to the testing room and tested one at a time. Pigs remained in the testing room for approximately 25 min, with 10 min of acclimation to the testing room, followed by 15 min of testing. Following completion of the test, pigs were returned to single housing with any remaining food. After all pigs were evaluated, they were returned to their original housing conditions.

Glasgow Pain Score

The Glasgow pain scale was adapted from a goat model to measure acute pain [26]. Each pig was assessed once daily on each testing day while still in their home pen, prior to separation for feeding and testing, on five criteria as outlined in Table S2. Scores assigned to each criterion were summed to generate a total pain score for each animal. Daily scores collected within a given testing week were then averaged to calculate the final mean weekly score (Table S3).

Wind‐Up Ratio (WUR)

The wind‐up ratio test assessed temporal pain summation. Each pig was led to the testing room one at a time. Pigs were enclosed in a testing area with their full food bowl placed in the middle of the arena. Following acclimation to the room, pigs underwent testing in a control area innervated by non‐injured DRGs located between the pig's shoulder blades and an injury‐matched DRGs innervated “testing area” adjacent to the spine just above the iliac crest.

A pin prick stimulus was applied to the target area for 2 s, followed by 2 s off, repeated 10 times. The number of stimuli with a positive response was recorded. Three repetitions were performed per area, alternating between the two.

Pig Evoked Pain Score (PEPS)

Pain‐related responses to sensory stimuli were quantified using the Pig Evoked Pain score (PEPS), a novel system which integrates both reflexive (spinal) and cognitive/affective (supraspinal) behavioral responses. Each stimulus was assigned a score from 0 (no response) to 10 (extreme pain) based on the criteria listed in Table S4.

Supraspinal responses to stimuli included vocalizations, interruption of eating behavior, and escape behavior. Vocalizations were included as an affective indicator based on evidence that pigs, as socially complex animals, use vocal communication to convey meaningful information during distressing experiences [27, 28, 29]. Interruption of eating behavior was adapted from human clinical pain research and was used as an ethological correlate of pain‐related activity disruption [30]. To avoid potential confounding effects of hunger on pain perception, pigs were provided with daily rations during testing [31]. Escape behavior was included based on its established association with high‐intensity nociceptive stimuli in pigs [32, 33, 34, 35] and was scored towards the higher end of the PEPS scale. Reflexive responses were evaluated based on limb withdrawal and whole‐body withdrawal from the stimuli, as performed in rodent pain models [36].

PEPS was utilized to quantify pain intensity during WUR testing. Video and audio recordings collected during each session were analyzed post hoc to assign a PEPS using a behavioral ethogram by investigators naïve to the treatment condition, as previously described. Each stimulus was independently assessed by two trained observers who reviewed all available camera angles. Individual PEPS were averaged to produce a final score for each stimulus.

MR Imaging

Prior to MRI, pigs were sedated with intramuscular Telazol (4‐6 mg/kg). Anesthesia was induced via intravenous administration of propofol (1‐2 mg/kg) and maintained with 2%–3% inhaled isoflurane via an endotracheal tube.

MRI was performed at baseline (pre‐injury) and every 4 weeks post injury, up to 16 weeks post injury on the lumbar spine (L1‐L6 levels, n = 3 injured IVD and n = 2 non‐injured IVD). Imaging included traditional (T1 and T2) and novel qCEST sequences to assess disc degeneration and pain development as previously described [21, 23, 24]. In addition, mean disc height was quantified from axial T2‐weighted images by calculating the average disc height at each timepoint and level. Adjacent vertebrae (n = 18) were independently assessed for Modic changes by two experienced radiologists, using standardized grading criteria adapted from Fields et al. [37] Each disc was scored on a scale of 0–3, with 0 indicating no signal change in vertebral bone marrow and 3 indicating hyperintensity suggestive of severe endplate degeneration.

Cell Preparation and scRNA‐Seq Library Preparation

The NP and surrounding transition zone were isolated from injured and non‐injured IVD (n = 3/group) and enzymatically digested to generate a single‐cell solution. Cell viability was assessed prior to library preparation. Single‐cell libraries were generated using a 10× Genomics Chromium platform targeting ~10 000 cells per sample and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus. Detailed dissociation, library preparation, and sequencing protocols are provided in the Supporting Information Methods.

scRNA‐Seq Bioinformatic Analysis

Raw sequencing data were processed using the 10× Genomics Cell Ranger pipeline and analyzed in R using Seurat for normalization, integration, dimensionality reduction, and unsupervised clustering. Cell identities were assigned based on the expression of established lineage and phenotype‐specific marker genes. NCs and NPCs were assigned based on the expression of NC lineage genes TBXT, CD24, KRT8, KRT18, and KRT19 as previously reported [38, 39, 40, 41]. Because both NCs and NPCs share NC lineage markers, the markers ACAN [22, 42] and SOX9 [43, 44] were used to differentiate between NCs and NPCs. dNPCs were characterized using a combination of fibrosis‐associated markers (COL1A1 [45], FN1 [46, 47], MGP [48], CILP2 [49], CHI3L1 [50]), inflammatory markers (IL1R1, IL6R, IL16, IL18, TNFRSF1B, IER3), and cell stress markers (HMOX1, HMGA1 [47], S100A2 [47], SOD2 [47]).

NPC Pseudotime trajectory analysis and cell–cell communication analysis were performed using R packages Monocle 3 [51, 52], and CellChat [53] respectively. The full bioinformatic and analysis pipelines, software versions, and parameter settings are described in the Supporting Information Methods.

RNA Isolation and Gene Expression

Total RNA for the nucleus pulposus and dorsal root ganglia was collected using a RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's protocol. The isolated RNA was transcribed into cDNA using a high‐capacity reverse transcription kit (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA). Pig Taqman gene expression assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific), as detailed in Supporting Information Methods, were used for analysis (Table S5). All assays were normalized to the 18S housekeeping gene.

Bulk RNA‐Seq Library Preparation and Sequencing for IVDs and DRGs

Bulk RNA‐seq libraries were prepared from low‐input of 3 ng of RNA for IVDs using a total RNA approach and from standard‐input of 400 ng of RNA for DRGs using a ribodepletion‐based workflow. Following stranded library construction, libraries were quantified, quality‐controlled, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (1 × 75 bp) to a depth of ~50 million reads per sample. A detailed workflow is provided in the Supporting Information Methods.

Bulk RNA‐Seq Bioinformatic Analysis

Raw reads obtained from RNA‐Seq were aligned to the transcriptome using STAR (version 2.6.1) [54]/RSEM (version 1.2.28) [55] with default parameters, using a custom pig Sscrofa11 transcriptome reference downloaded from https://useast.ensembl.org/, containing all protein‐coding and long non‐coding RNA genes based on Sscrofa11.1.104 annotation.

Differential gene expression was performed using a negative binomial framework implemented in DESeq2, with multiple testing correction applied to identify significantly regulated genes. Variance‐stabilized data were used for visualization and multivariate analysis.

Proteomic Analysis of NP Tissue and Cells and Plasma

Proteomic analyses were performed on plasma as well as intact NP tissue and NP‐derived cells to characterize both the overall tissue proteome and lower abundance intracellular proteins. Briefly, samples were lysed, reduced, alkylated, and isolated using an S‐Trap–based proteomic workflow followed by tryptic digestion. Peptides were analyzed by data‐independent acquisition (DIA) LC–MS/MS on an Orbitrap mass spectrometer coupled to nanoLC. Protein identification and quantification were performed using DIA‐NN against the porcine reference proteome. Differential protein analysis was conducted using MetaboAnalyst, following normalization and multiple‐testing correction. Full experimental and analytical details are provided in the Supporting Information Methods.

ELISA

Serum protein content was quantified using a Pierce BCA kit according to manufacturer protocol (Thermo Fisher, Walthan, MA). Serum IHH content was measured using IHH ELISA (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA) following manufacturer protocol. Absorbance was measured using a SpectraMax M3 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA).

Histological and Immunofluorescent Staining

IVDs were harvested for histological and immunofluorescent analysis (n = 4 per group), fixed, decalcified, paraffin embedded in the sagittal plane, sectioned, and stained with H&E and Alcian blue/Picrosirius red to assess tissue morphology and matrix organization. Slides were imaged using whole‐slide scanning microscopy. Semi‐quantitative histological grading was performed according to the criteria established by Lee et al. for large animal models of IVD degeneration [56].

For immunofluorescence, paraffin sections underwent antigen retrieval, blocking, and staining with primary antibodies (Table S6), followed by fluorescent secondary antibodies and nuclear counterstaining. Images were acquired using fluorescence microscopy.

DRGs were harvested for immunofluorescent analysis (n = 2 per group), cryosectioned, fixed, and stained using standard immunofluorescence protocols with antibodies listed in Supplementary Table S6. Imaging was performed using confocal and fluorescence microscopy. Negative controls omitting primary antibodies were included.

Detailed staining protocols, imaging parameters, and antibody information are provided in the Supporting Information Methods.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in RStudio (v4.5.0) and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA). Outliers greater than two standard deviations from the mean were excluded. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 for all analyses.

MRI outcomes and selected behavioral measures were analyzed in RStudio using linear mixed‐effects models to account for repeated measures. MRI models included Group and Time as fixed effects, with animal and disc nested within animal as random effects. WUR and Glasgow pain scores were analyzed with Time as a fixed effect and animal as a random effect.

Post hoc comparisons were performed using estimated marginal means with multiple‐comparison corrections. Holm–Bonferroni correction was applied for MRI outcomes. For WUR, Šídák‐adjusted comparisons were used to compare groups at each time point, and Dunnett‐adjusted comparisons were used to compare time points with baseline. For Glasgow pain scores, comparisons versus baseline were performed using Dunnett‐adjusted tests.

Highest PEPS scores were analyzed in Prism 10 using the Friedman test with Dunn's multiple comparison test.

All gene expression analyses were performed in Prism. DRG samples were analyzed using either a two‐way ANOVA or a mixed‐effects model, as appropriate, followed by Šídák multiple‐comparisons tests for post hoc analysis. For IVD, normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were analyzed using unpaired t‐tests, whereas non‐normally distributed data were compared using Mann–Whitney U tests.

Results

Confirmation of IVD Degeneration Following Annular Puncture

Gross comparison of injured and non‐injured IVD showed reduced NP size and loss of gelatinous consistency, at 12 weeks post injury (Figure 1C). MR imaging of the injured and adjacent non‐injured IVD showed significant changes in IVD morphology starting at 4 weeks post injury (Figure 1D–F). Type I and II Modic changes in the adjacent vertebral bodies were detected at week 4 post injury, with all adjacent vertebrae demonstrating Type III changes by 16 weeks post injury (Figure 1E, Figure S1). Significant reductions in disc height were observed between injured and non‐injured IVDs (significance indicated by *), injured compared to baseline (significance indicated by $), and with the previous timepoints (significance indicated by #) at all timepoints post injury (Figure 1F). Longitudinal and transverse relaxation time, represented by T1 and T2 respectively, and CEST MTR, which indirectly quantifies glycosaminoglycan content, showed similar trends (Figure 1F).

Gene expression analysis of the IVDs at 16 weeks post injury demonstrated significant upregulation of select degeneration (MMP3), neuronal innervation (PGP9.5 [57, 58]), and CGRP receptor pathway (CALCRL [59, 60, 61], CGRP [59, 60, 61], RAMP1 [60, 61]) genes in injured IVD compared to naïve control discs (Figure 1G). Select markers for inflammation (IL1β [62], TNFα [63]) as well as NGF‐related (NGF [64, 65], TrkA [66]) and nociceptive peptide/channel markers (TRPV1 [67], TAC1 [68, 69]) demonstrated a similar trend of increased expression with injury (Figure S2).

Histological staining with H&E and AB/PR shows a marked loss of ECM organization with accumulation of dense ECM consistent with fibrotic remodeling and scar tissue replacement (Figure 1H,I). Histological scoring of injured and non‐injured IVD demonstrated development of moderate IVD degeneration following injury (Table S7).

Evaluation and Validation of Chronic Pain Development

MR imaging using qCEST sequence has previously been validated in human patients [24, 70] and correlated to low pH in a pig model [21, 23]. Therefore, we have used this method to validate pain outcome measures in this study. Here, the qCEST indicated a significant decrease in pH starting at week 4 and continuing until euthanasia at week 16 in injured IVD compared to non‐injured controls (Figure 2A). Acute pain, as assessed by the Glasgow pain scale, was found to be significantly higher compared to baseline at week 10 post‐injury and continued to increase up until euthanasia at week 16 (Figure 2B). Temporal summation of pain, as measured by WUR, was found to be significantly higher in injury‐matched dermatomes compared to non‐injury‐matched dermatomes (significance denoted by *) and compared to baseline (significance denoted by #) starting at week 2 and continuing up to 16 weeks post injury (Figure 2C). Furthermore, the highest PEPS score in the injury‐matched dermatomes was found to be significantly higher than week 0 measurements (Figure 2D).

Gene expression analysis of pain (NGF [64, 65], CGRP [59, 60, 61], TAC1 [68, 69]) and neuroinflammation (GFAP [71, 72, 73]) in naïve control and injury‐matched DRGs demonstrated significantly higher pain and neuroinflammation in the injury‐matched DRGs compared to non‐injury‐matched naïve controls (Figure 2E). Further comparison of the injury (L2 and L3) and non‐injury matched levels (L4 and L5) demonstrated that upregulation occurred only in injury‐matched DRGs at the levels associated with disc injury (Figure S3). Bulk transcriptomics of the DRGs from injury‐matched and non‐injury‐matched dermatomes demonstrated distinct transcriptional profiles (Figure 2F). Multiple genes associated with neuroinflammation (CCN3 [74], TGFβ‐2 [75]), neural development (NET1 [76], ZIC2 [77]), and pain/sensitization (CRABP1 [78], SLC6A13 [79, 80], SLC7A11 [81]) were found to be enriched in the injury matched DRGs (Figure 2G). IPA pathway analysis predicted upregulation of pathways associated with pain, cell stress, inflammation, apoptotic signaling, and neural outgrowth in the injury‐matched DRGs compared to non‐injury‐matched (Figure 2H).

Immunofluorescent (IF) staining of injured and non‐injured IVD showed the presence of substance P and GAP43 in the injured IVD and absence of such in non‐injured IVDs (Figure 2I, Figure S4). Similarly, IF of the DRG showed relatively higher expression of GAP43 and CGRP as well as TRPV1 (Figure 2J, Figure S4) in the injury‐matched DRGs compared to naïve control DRGs.

TRIM29, ENO3, CAVIN1, RAB3B, and IHH Identified as Potential Biomarkers of Degeneration

Assessment of IVD transcriptomics (Figure 3A–C) and proteomics (Figure 3D–F) showed that injured and non‐injured IVD were significantly different. Interestingly, injured IVD samples tended to be more variable than non‐injured IVD samples in transcriptomics, while proteomics showed the opposite trend (Figure 3B,E). For proteomics, comparison of tissue and cell samples from injured and non‐injured IVD demonstrated that though disc health status was the primary determinant of sample clustering, sample type (cells vs. tissue) also influenced sample similarity, with non‐injured and injured tissue samples tending to be more similar than non‐injured and injured cell samples (Figure 3D,E). Furthermore, differentially expressed genes (DEGs, Figure 3C) and differentially expressed proteins (DEPs, Figure 3F) from RNA‐seq and proteomics identified four markers that may be promising biomarker candidates (Figure 3G). TRIM29, which is linked with DNA damage and immune response [82], was found to be downregulated in injured IVD in both transcriptomics and proteomics (Figure 3G). ENO3, which is involved in metabolism [83], was found to be significantly downregulated in injured IVD transcriptomics but upregulated in proteomics (Figure 3G). Finally, CAVIN2 [84] and RAB3B [85, 86], which are associated with vesicular trafficking and signaling, were found to be downregulated in injured IVD at both the transcriptomic and proteomic levels (Figure 3G).

In addition to IVD, proteomics was performed on plasma collected at baseline, week 12, and week 16 from the pigs throughout the experiment, which identified no overlapping DEP with NP tissue at either week 12 or 16 with IVD (Figure 3H). Comparison of plasma from week 12 and 16 to baseline found moderate overlap in overall sample similarity and DEPs between samples (Figure S5).

Interestingly, Indian Hedgehog (IHH) was identified as a DEP in plasma at 12 and 16 weeks post injury by proteomic analysis (Figure 3H, Figure S5). Further quantification across all timepoints with ELISA revealed a significant decrease in systemic IHH concentration at 4 weeks post injury, followed by a gradual increase at later timepoints (Figure 3I).

Injury Induces NC Differentiation Into NPC

Single‐cell transcriptomic analysis (scRNAseq) identified 21 cell clusters from non‐injured and injured porcine discs collected at 12 weeks post injury (Figure 4A). Clusters were categorized as NC, degenerative NPC (dNPC), NPC, proliferating NPC (pro‐NPC), or endothelial cells (EC), based on canonical marker expression as described in the methods (Figure S6–S8). The prevalence of cells from non‐injured and injured IVD varied among the clusters, with some clusters, such as NC 2, primarily originating from non‐injured IVD, while others, like dNPC 1, stemming primarily from injured IVD (Figure 4B).

(A) scRNA‐seq UMAP colored and annotated by cluster, (B) Proportional distribution of cell originating from non‐injured (red) and injured (blue) discs of each cluster, (C) Summary of major cell identities and their major contribution across the dataset, (D) Comparison of cell type proportions between non‐injured (left) and injured IVD (right), (E) Relative outgoing and incoming intercellular communication strength of clusters, (F) Selected enriched intercellular communication pathways, (G) Trajectory analysis from left to right colored by: Pseudotime, cell type, KRT18 expression and COL1A1 expression.

Closer examination of captured populations demonstrated that a majority of cells exhibited an NC (40.0%), NPC (15.4%), or dNPC (42.5%) phenotype (Figure 4C). However, when stratified by disc type, a majority of NCs were found to originate from the non‐injured IVD, with 63.2% characterized as NCs compared to only 2.7% of cells from the injured IVD (Figure 4D). In contrast, 89.7% of the cells captured from the injured IVD and only 13.5% of cells from the non‐injured IVD were identified as dNPCs (Figure 4D).

Analysis of intercellular communication via the CellChat package found that clusters characterized as NPC or dNPC had high intercellular communication compared to NCs (Figure 4E). Pathways known to be associated with NC to NP differentiation, such as TGFβ, Notch, FGF, and BMP signaling, were predicted to be enriched in NP clusters (Figure 4F).

Trajectory analysis with Monocle 3 predicted that NCs tended to be located earlier in pseudotime compared to NP (Figure 4G). Expression of characteristic markers of NC and IVD degeneration aligned with pseudotime results, with Keratin 18 (KRT18) found to be located earlier in pseudotime, while degenerative markers such as Collagen type 1A (COL1A1) were located later in pseudotime, suggesting a transition from NCs to NPCs as a response to injury (Figure 4G, Figure S9).

Differentiated NPCs Express Increased Pain Inducing “Help Me” Signaling

Interestingly, clusters identified as dNPCs had comparatively higher expression of degenerative, inflammatory, and pain‐related markers when compared to other cell types (Figure 5A). CellChat analysis further identified multiple angiogenesis, inflammation, and neurogenesis/pain pathways that were differentially enriched in the NPC and dNPC clusters, such as VEGF [87, 88], NCAM [89], and prostaglandin [90, 91] signaling (Figure 5B). IPA analysis of the dNPC clusters identified that these cells were predicted to have high levels of senescence, cell stress, neural outgrowth, inflammatory signaling, leukocyte activation, and various pathways associated with differentiation (Figure 5C). Comparison of the injured and non‐injured pig IVD with previously reported human LBP and asymptomatic IVDs [6] found that pig injured IVD were more similar to human LBP IVD than human asymptomatic IVD or pig non‐injured IVD (Figure 5D). Comparison of predicted enriched pathways from the NPCs of injured and non‐injured pig IVD, as well as human LBP and asymptomatic IVD, demonstrated that injured pig and human LBP samples were more enriched in cell stress and death and neural signaling pathways compared to non‐injured pig and human asymptomatic IVD (Figure 5E). Conversely, non‐injured pig and human asymptomatic IVD were predicted to have greater enrichment in metabolic and stem cell/developmental pathways (Figure 5E).

Discussion

This study served as both a proof‐of‐concept and validation, demonstrating that biobehavioral testing in pigs can be used to quantitatively assess the development of LPB. To date, there are no well‐established large animal models of LBP with systematic behavioral assessments of pain. This work aims to fill this critical gap by establishing a translational model that combines a clinically relevant LBP model with quantifiable pain outcomes. In addition, this study characterizes the cellular and molecular changes that accompany disc injury, providing insight into the pathophysiological mechanisms contributing to pain (Figure 6). These results were cross‐referenced with previous findings in humans [6], therefore validating this model as clinically relevant for development of therapeutics. Finally, several candidate biomarkers for degeneration and LBP were identified, offering a valuable opportunity to investigate these biomarkers under controlled conditions.

Proposed mechanism of disc degeneration and LBP development.

An important aim of this study was to develop reliable methods for assessing and quantifying pain‐related behaviors in the porcine disc injury model. Biobehavioral testing was able to quantify and detect the pain responses observed in this model (Figure 2B–D) and was validated by qCEST (Figure 2A) [24, 70], gene expression analysis (Figure 2E), and bulk transcriptomic analysis (Figure 2F–H) of the DRGs. While qCEST does not directly measure pain, it provides a noninvasive surrogate of biochemical changes in the IVD, including pH, that have been associated with painful disc degeneration. There is substantial literature identifying intradiscal pH drop as a characteristic feature of IVD degeneration [92, 93]. Previous work from our group has demonstrated that qCEST measurements correlate with intradiscal pH25. Furthermore, qCEST metrics have been correlated with LBP in human patients [24]. In a similar injury model as used in this study, we have demonstrated that IVD injury results in a decrease in intradiscal pH in addition to increased qCEST signal [21]. In addition, we were able to detect neuronal and nociceptive markers within the IVD itself via gene expression analysis (Figure 1G), bulk RNA‐seq (Figure 2F–H), and IF (Figure 2I), suggesting nerve ingrowth and increased nociceptive signaling within previously aneural disc tissue [87, 94, 95]. The ingrowth of nerves into the inner IVD has previously been observed in both human patients [87, 95, 96, 97] as well as other animal models [98, 99], suggesting that our disc injury model recapitulates key pathological mechanisms of human IVD degeneration and LBP, thus making it a clinically relevant platform for translation of future LBP treatments.

Unlike humans who lose a majority of their NCs by age 10 [100], Yucatan minipigs are generally reported to retain their NCs beyond skeletal maturity [38], though the extent of NC persistence may vary by breed. Thus, another goal of this study was to confirm that this model can faithfully recapitulate the cellular changes seen in human IVD degeneration. Our scRNA‐seq revealed a near‐complete loss of NCs and a corresponding shift towards NPCs following injury, which closely mirrors the cellular landscape seen in degenerative human IVD (Figure 4D). Notably, a small population of NCs persisted in the injured IVD (Figure 4D). This finding is consistent with our and others' previously published work demonstrating that, contrary to the assumptions that NCs are completely lost post‐maturity, some NCs are retained in the adult human IVD and can be detected even in individuals as old as 70 [101, 102, 103].

Beyond its translational value, our study also offers valuable insight into the cellular dynamics underlying IVD degeneration. Multiple lineage tracing studies have demonstrated progressive differentiation of NCs into NPCs with age and degeneration [104, 105, 106]. Our data align with these findings, with trajectory analysis predicting that NC‐rich non‐injured IVD are located earlier in pseudotime (Figure 4G), indicating a less differentiated, progenitor‐like cellular state compared to NPC‐dominated injured IVD.

Furthermore, cell–cell communication analysis identified multiple pathways involved with NC‐to‐NPC differentiation in NPC and dNPC clusters, including Notch, TGFβ, BMP, and FGF (Figure 4F). Among these, the Notch and TGFβ pathways are particularly notable for their well‐established and essential role in IVD development, growth, maintenance, and degeneration [107]. Notch regulates differentiation and tissue homeostasis by maintaining progenitor cells in an undifferentiated, proliferative state and directing their transition to differentiated phenotypes [108]. It is essential for NC‐to‐NPC differentiation [109], elevated in degenerative musculoskeletal disorders, including IVD degeneration [108, 110], and promotes NPC survival by inhibiting apoptosis [111]. Notch signaling is also influenced by proinflammatory cytokines such as IL1β and TNFα [112, 113], suggesting a potential link between inflammation and cell fate within the IVD.

TGFβ is likewise critical for NC‐to‐NPC differentiation during development and postnatal IVD maintenance [114]. TGF‐β promotes upregulation of NP markers expression, ECM synthesis, and phenotypic stability by enhancing proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis [115]. It also regulates cellular responses to mechanical and inflammatory stress, thereby contributing to tissue homeostasis [116, 117]. However, dysregulated TGFβ signaling is also associated with fibrosis, aberrant matrix remodeling [118], and impaired regenerative potential [115]. Enrichment of these pathways supports the hypothesis that injury induces NC‐to‐NPC differentiation and is consistent with developmental and degenerative transitions within the IVD.

Due to this close temporal and spatial relationship, there has been growing interest in studying NCs as both a key regulator of disc homeostasis and as a potential candidate for regenerative therapies. However, our understanding of NCs has been limited due to the limited availability of NCs in human tissue, thus making it difficult to study in clinically relevant contexts. In addition to advancing our understanding of IVD degeneration, elucidating the mechanisms governing NCs' fate could inform the development of NC‐based therapies for IVD degeneration and discogenic LBP.

In addition to exploring the NC niche, we demonstrated the presence of highly transcriptionally active NPCs, which we previously observed and described in human LBP samples [6]. Our scRNA‐seq demonstrated similar upregulation of degeneration‐, inflammation‐, and innervation‐associated markers in dNPC clusters (Figure 5A). Further analysis of these clusters via CellChat and IPA demonstrated enrichment of signaling pathways related to innervation, vascularization, and inflammatory pathways (Figure 5B,C). To evaluate the translational relevance of our model, we compared the transcriptomic profiles of our pig IVD with our previously published human asymptomatic and LBP IVD. This analysis revealed that injured pig IVD were more transcriptionally similar to human LBP IVD than human asymptomatic IVD (Figure 5D). Moreover, pathways associated with cellular stress, altered metabolism, neural signaling, and stem cell development were consistently stratified between the injured/LBP and non‐injured/asymptomatic IVDs. This suggests that injury‐induced changes in the porcine IVD recapitulate not only the cellular alterations but also the molecular pain‐associated phenotype observed in human discogenic low back pain. These findings reinforce the suitability of this model for studying both IVD degeneration and discogenic LBP.

We identified several differentially expressed biomarkers of interest, which are promising avenues of future study. Proteomic and transcriptomic comparison of upregulated markers identified four markers as significant at both levels of expression: ENO3, RAB3B, CAVIN2, and TRIM29. In particular, RAB3B and CAVIN2 are markers of caveolar and vesicular trafficking and have been previously linked to cellular signaling regulation, membrane dynamics, and mechano‐transduction [119, 120]. Although RAB3B has previously been reported to be downregulated in degenerating IVD [121] and CAVIN2 has been studied as a target to enhance extracellular vesicle uptake in NPCs [122], their potential roles in LBP pathogenesis have not, to our knowledge, been explored. Interestingly, examination of cellular communication and signaling mechanisms within the IVD using scRNA‐seq revealed that NC clusters, which are prominent in non‐injured IVD, highly expressed genes associated with caveolae‐mediated signaling (Figure S10). In contrast, dNPC clusters, which are predominately in the injured IVD, showed increased expression of genes involved in constitutive signaling and secretion, with marked downregulation of caveolae signaling genes (Figure S10). These findings demonstrate not only that RAB3B and CAVIN2 are promising biomarkers for IVD degeneration and potentially LBP, but also that cellular communication and signaling mechanisms change with degeneration. Notably, there is a lack of comprehensive studies examining how cell–cell communication networks shift in the context of IVD degeneration and LBP, making it a promising area for future study.

In addition to IVD local markers, we also identified IHH as a plasma DEP at 12 and 16 weeks post injury. IHH, which is more widely known for its role in cartilage endplate homeostasis and degeneration, is also involved in IVD development and homeostasis. Specifically, IHH plays an essential role in NC maintenance and the structural organization of the developing IVD [123] and is typically highly expressed in healthy, non‐degenerated discs. However, several studies have shown that loss of IHH expression is associated with pathological transitions, including the loss of NCs and the emergence of chondrocyte‐like NPCs [124, 125]. Notably, a study by Bach et al. utilized a canine model, reporting the biphasic pattern of IHH expression observed in our proteomics and ELISA results, namely the high IHH in healthy IVD, reduction with the loss of NCs, and subsequent increase as IVD degeneration progressed to later stages of degeneration [124]. These results suggest that IHH may be a potential biomarker for the early‐to‐intermediate stage of IVD degeneration.

Interestingly, Sonic Hedgehog (SHH), which plays the dominant role in IVD development and homeostasis, was not found to be significantly enriched in either our proteomics or transcriptomic analyses. However, scRNA‐seq identified several hedgehog receptors and downstream targets that were upregulated in dNPCs, suggesting that hedgehog signaling may be active (Figure S11). Because hedgehog ligands share common receptors and downstream signaling pathways, it is not possible to determine whether this signaling originated from IHH or SHH. However, previous studies have reported that hedgehog ligands are often detected at low levels in scRNA‐seq datasets despite evidence of active signaling, suggesting that SHH may still be present but below the detection threshold [126].

There are several limitations to this study. First, we used intra‐pig non‐injured controls for several of our experiments, including bulk and scRNA‐seq, histology, and proteomics, which have the advantage of using the same animal with the same genetic background, but may also be a limitation due to some systemic effect of the injury that could not be completely excluded. Furthermore, we did not analyze any earlier timepoints for our transcriptomic analyses, which limits our understanding of the important initial molecular and cellular changes during early disc degeneration. Finally, due to the relative scarcity of pig‐specific bioinformatic resources in biomedical research, many of the databases, such as CellChat and IPA, were analyzed using existing human settings, which may affect the accuracy of these analyses.

To assess structural contributors to pain, adjacent vertebrae were evaluated for Modic changes, which are present in 18%–58% of LBP cases [127]. Type I and, to a lesser extent, Type II Modic changes are most commonly associated with pain, due to inflammation and sensitization [127]. In our model, although Type I changes appeared at earlier timepoints, acute pain was not detected until week 10, when most vertebrae exhibited Type II or III changes. This suggests that factors beyond Modic changes may contribute to LBP. However, distinguishing discogenic from vertebrogenic pain remains challenging due to the anatomical and functional integration of the IVD and adjacent vertebra.

This study employed two behavioral pain assessments, the modified Glasgow Composite Pain Scale and the Wind‐Up Ratio test, which specifically evaluate acute nociceptive responses and temporal summation, respectively. While these assays provided valuable information on pain sensitivity and central sensitization, they primarily capture evoked pain responses and do not fully represent the complexity of pain IVD injury may induce. A more comprehensive evaluation of discogenic pain would benefit from the inclusion of additional assessments targeting spontaneous behaviors, affective components of pain, and longer‐term functional outcomes.

Pain scoring methods are commonly used both in human and veterinary medicine. In humans, self‐reported scoring systems like Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) are essential clinical tools to assess and diagnose LBP as individuals can directly communicate their intensity of pain. In contrast, VAS and NRS are less commonly used in veterinary settings due to their dependence on observer interpretation of animal behavior, which is inherently subjective and associated with substantial inter‐rater variability. Furthermore, VAS and NRS are unidimensional scoring methods that primarily capture perceived pain intensity and do not account for the broader behavioral and functional manifestations of pain.

Validated veterinary composite measures, such as Glasgow Pain Scale, incorporate multiple behavioral and physiological indicators of pain, providing a more multidimensional and structured framework for pain assessment in animals. Although these composite scoring systems remain partially limited by observer subjectivity, validation studies on composite large‐animal pain scales have demonstrated strong correlations with observer‐based VAS/NRS scores, with Spearman correlations ≥ 0.85 [128], supporting their translational relevance for preclinical pain assessment. However, while these tools enable meaningful cross‐species comparisons of pain‐related behaviors, they should not be interpreted as a direct one‐to‐one surrogate for human pain perception or clinical grading criteria.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the potential use of a combined novel MRI and biobehavioral testing to quantify pain in a porcine IVD injury model of discogenic LBP and suggests using TRIM29, ENO3, RAB3B, CAVIN2, and IHH as potential biomarkers for LBP. Furthermore, this study demonstrates the appropriateness of this model for translation of LBP therapies towards clinical adoption, as it closely mirrors the degenerative phenotype and symptoms seen in human discogenic pain.
