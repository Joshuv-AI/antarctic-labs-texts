Co-infection of multiple human papillomavirus genotypes and its effects on symptomatic risks and transmission dynamics: an integrated epidemiological and modelling study in Fujian, China
Authors: Yue Tang, Guzainuer Abudurusuli, Jingwen Xu, Ruiqin Tu, Shishi Hong, Huiming Ye, Tianmu Chen, Yulin Zhou, Weihong Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGlobal human papillomavirus (HPV) prevalence among women rose significantly from 14% (2019) to 24% (2024), underscoring the need to understand transmission dynamics and public health impact. Although multi-genotype infections are increasingly documented, evidence remains limited on their combined effect on cervical lesion severity and transmission, especially in regional populations. Using clinical datasets from Xiamen, China, this study evaluates co-infection patterns through integrated statistical and dynamical models to quantify associations with histological severity and transmissibility.MethodsData were sourced from positive HPV nucleic acid tests at two hospitals in Xiamen. Genotyping employed multiplex PCR-based flow fluorescence hybridisation. Cumulative link models (CLMs) were used to assess associations between multi-genotype infections and cervical lesion severity. Concurrently, an ordinary differential equation transmission model was developed to estimate reproduction numbers, comparing transmission potential across infection types.ResultsOf 1 33 438 samples, 15 939 (11.9%) were HPV-positive, covering 27 genotypes. HPV 16, 52 and 58 were the most prevalent high-risk types. Co-infections involving these genotypes showed strong inter-hospital correlation in pairing patterns (Pearson’s r=0.851). Co-infection severity association was context-specific: the number of genotypes predicted severity in the screening population (eg, quadruple infections OR=1.47, 95% CI 1.23 to 1.71, p<0.01) but not in the referral population. Conversely, co-infections exhibited consistently higher relative transmissibility indices in both settings (eg, high-low-risk co-infection median model-derived : 2.57–6.82).ConclusionsHPV co-infection impacts are modulated by patient population: relative transmission potential is broadly elevated, whereas histological severity effects are marked in screening cohorts but minimal in referral groups. Context-aware public health strategies—adapting co-infection screening and interventions to clinical setting—are urged for more effective and efficient disease control.Keywords: Epidemiology; Disease Transmission, Infectious; Basic Reproduction Number; Public Health

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Current understanding of human papillomavirus (HPV) multi-genotype co-infections remains fragmented. Key gaps include unresolved contradictions regarding the nature of genotype interactions (synergistic vs independent), a scarcity of mathematical models addressing co-infection dynamics and inconsistent clinical evidence on age-dependent severity—necessitating integrated analyses of genotype combinations, transmission patterns and age-stratified risks to clarify carcinogenic mechanisms and optimise prevention strategies.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study demonstrates that the clinical impact of HPV co-infections is context-dependent. While dual infections consistently exhibit elevated transmission potential due to high transmissibility indices, their association with histological severity is pronounced in general screening populations but becomes attenuated within referral populations. This finding highlights the clinical setting as a critical effect modifier, advancing research beyond the prevailing focus on viral combinatorics alone by integrating patient-population heterogeneity into models of HPV outcomes.

This study compels a strategic shift in HPV management towards context-adaptive interventions. It advocates for tailoring co-infection screening in general populations while prioritising dual-infection control due to their high transmissibility. By revealing discrepant associations with severity across hospital settings, this work catalyses the development of precision public health frameworks that account for patient population heterogeneity across clinical settings.

Background

Human papillomavirus (HPV) consists of a large group of small double-stranded DNA viruses that infect squamous epithelial cells. HPV is classified into high-risk and low-risk types based on its potential to cause lesions. High-risk types, including HPV 16, 18 and several other oncogenic types, are associated with precancerous lesions and cancers, such as cervical, vulvar, vaginal, anal and head and neck cancers.1 2 Cervical cancer, the second most common cancer among women globally, is almost universally associated with high-risk HPV infections. Multi-genotype HPV co-infections are frequent, with between 17% and 55% of patients with high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) exhibiting multi-genotype infections.3

Despite numerous studies, the interaction between different HPV genotypes remains unclear. Some research suggests synergistic carcinogenic interactions between specific types like HPV16 and HPV33,4 while other studies indicate that co-infections may occur randomly and independently lead to cervical disease.5,7 A particularly high incidence of multiple HPV infections has been reported among Chinese women, where co-infections involving types 16, 18, 31, 52 and 58 are notably prevalent [8]. This observation highlights the need for further investigation into the dynamic and public health implications of these co-infections within this specific demographic.

Mathematical models have been developed to describe HPV transmission and disease progression in specific populations, but they typically focus on single-genotype infections and do not address co-infection dynamics.8 9 Although some models explore vaccine cross-protection and stochastic individual interactions, they do not analyse typical multi-genotype combinations.10 11 One simplified model of HPV co-infection considered interactions via host recovery rates, but it was limited to a maximum of five co-infection genotypes per host.12

In addition to genotype interactions, factors influencing disease severity in HPV infections are debated. Studies show that age plays a significant role, with older patients exhibiting a higher prevalence of high-grade cervical lesions and cervical cancer.13,15 Although multi-genotype HPV infections are associated with an increased risk of severe lesions, the evidence regarding their collective impact on the histological grade of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) is inconsistent.5 16 17 Specifically, it is unclear whether the presence of multiple genotypes independently contributes to disease progression (eg, from CIN1 to CIN2/3) or if the observed severity is primarily driven by the presence of a single, highly virulent genotype (like HPV16) within the co-infection. A key source of this ambiguity lies in the unresolved nature of inter-genotypic interactions and varying genotype-specific virulence. The highly carcinogenic potential of HPV16 is well-established, yet its synergistic or antagonistic effects in co-infections with other high-risk types (eg, HPV52, 58) remain controversial.4 7 This gap underscores the need to move beyond merely quantifying the number of types and towards understanding the specific combinations and their biological interplay.

This study aims to examine the risk of histological lesion progression in patients with multi-genotype HPV infections and investigate population-level dynamics. Positive HPV test data from two hospitals in Xiamen, Fujian, China, will be used to build a cross-sex transmission model. By incorporating age and co-infection types as predictors, we will assess their impact on the severity of cervical lesions using a cumulative link statistical model. This research will also explore potential genotype interactions, aiming to contribute insights for improved clinical diagnosis, treatment and public health strategies.

Methods

Study population and data collection

This study utilised data from two primary sources. The first dataset comprises records of 1 14 824 women who underwent HPV nucleic acid typing tests at Xiamen Maternity and Child Healthcare Hospital from January 2013 to April 2021 (Hospital 1). The second dataset comprises records of 18 614 women who underwent HPV nucleic acid typing tests at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Xiamen Branch) from December 2017 and March 2023 (Hospital 2).

The two participating hospitals represent distinct patient populations and healthcare settings in Xiamen, China. Hospital 1 is a specialised maternity and child healthcare centre located in the city’s central old district, primarily serving women for routine gynaecological examinations, prenatal care and cervical cancer screening. Its patient population is thus representative of a general screening cohort. In contrast, Hospital 2 is a large, comprehensive tertiary hospital located in a modern urban development zone. It functions as a regional referral centre, attracting patients with more complex conditions, including those with suspected or confirmed cervical lesions. This difference in patient profiles between a screening population (Hospital 1) and a referral population (Hospital 2) provides valuable heterogeneity for our analysis.

Data encompasses diagnosis dates, results, HPV positivity, patient age and other relevant information (see the Supplementary Material for details of the data inclusion/exclusion criteria and HPV Analysis). Additional demographic data were sourced from the ‘Xiamen Statistical Yearbook’, detailing annual birth and mortality rates along with total population figures for Xiamen.

Patient and public involvement (PPI) statement: No patients or members of the public were involved in the setting of the research question, outcome measures, study design, recruitment, conduct or dissemination of this study. This was a retrospective analysis of de-identified clinical data and thus, direct patient partnership was not feasible.

Mathematical models

We use the cumulative link model (CLM), that is, a generalised linear model with a logarithmic link function for the conditional cumulative PMF of the response variable of lesion severity, to evaluate the relation between multi-genotype co-infection and the severity of cervical lesions. An ordinary differential disease transmission model is further constructed to assess the population growth for co-infection combinations.

Cumulative Link Model

To explore the relationship between HPV infection severity Y and variables such as type of HPV infection X1 and patient age X2, we categorised the severity of cervical lesions Y into ordered levels based on clinical diagnosis documented in medical records, with histological confirmation for cervical lesions when available. The ordinal scale was defined as:

Level 0: No abnormality (normal cytology/histology or routine exam with no cervical lesion).

Level 1: Low-grade cervical lesion (LSIL/CIN1).

Level 2: High-grade cervical lesion (HSIL/CIN2–CIN3).

Level 3: Invasive cervical cancer.

The most severe diagnosis for each patient was used, prioritising histological results over cytological findings when both were available.

In our risk analysis, Level 0 served as the reference category for computing ORs. We constructed two separate sets of CLMs to explore the relationship between cervical lesion severity (Y) and predictor variables. For the analysis of single-genotype infections, the infection type (X1) was treated as a binary categorical variable (single high-risk vs single low-risk). For the analysis of multi-genotype co-infections, the infection type (X1) was treated as a multi-category categorical variable (SHPV, DHPV, THPV and FHPV). In both models, patient age (X2) was included as a continuous variable. This structure enables us to assess how infection type and age influence the progression and severity of HPV-related cervical lesions.

We assume that the accumulation of the probability mass function (PMF) of cervical lesions Y|X=x with the value of the random variable X=def[X1,X2]T given follows a generalised linear model with a logistic link function. Such a model is usually called the CLM.

Ordinary differential disease transmission model

We developed a sex-specific susceptible-infectious-cancer-recovered (SICR) transmission dynamics model to analyse the natural history and epidemiological process of HPV, applying it to individual HPV genotypes and co-infection types. The model incorporates the observation that most HPV infections are transient, with a significant majority (80%) naturally resolving and the remainder (20%) progressing to cancer.18 19 It assumes a uniform natural clearance rate across genotypes, a simplification necessitated by limited genotype-specific longitudinal data and to maintain model tractability for comparing co-infection dynamics.8 11 Furthermore, once recovered, individuals acquire permanent type-specific immunity and do not return to susceptible or infected states, an assumption consistent with SIR-style models that reflects the established, though not absolute, immune response to HPV.9 19 These assumptions allow the model to focus on the core transmission dynamics of co-infections. We used hospital data as a proxy for population-wide data to calculate and estimate the basic reproduction number (R0) for single-type HPV infections. This model intentionally assumes independent transmission, excluding synergistic interactions due to a current lack of empirical data for robust parameterisation. This parsimonious approach establishes a clear transmission baseline for co-infections.

Additionally, to capture the progression of multi-type HPV co-infections within the population, we defined a mathematically theoretical ‘basic reproduction number’ for their transmission dynamics, which derives from the classical epidemiological definition of R0. The basic reproduction number R0 is calculated using the next-generation matrix methods (NGM) according to Van den Driessche and Watmough, which determines the expected number of new cases an infectious individual produces in a fully susceptible population (see the supplementary material for details of the calculation of the basic reproduction number).

To quantify the robustness of our model projections and address potential uncertainties in parameter estimates, we conducted a comprehensive sensitivity analysis. We systematically varied five key parameters—carcinogenic rate (μ), female and male recovery rates (γf, γm) and cancer mortality rates (θf, θm)—across biologically plausible ranges and evaluated their impact on the simulated HPV prevalence over time. The full methodology and results, which confirm the structural reliability of our model and the robustness of our core findings, are detailed in online supplemental figures S2-S11 (see the Supplementary Material for details of cross-gender SICR model parameter sensitivity analysis).

Clustering for transmissibilities of HPV genotypes

We utilised hierarchical clustering to analyse the time sequences of the basic reproduction number (R0) for various HPV genotypes, employing MATLAB’s linkage/function and the dynamic time warping (DTW) distance (see the supplementary material for clustering for transmissibilities of HPV genotypes).

Results

HPV infection characteristics

To characterise HPV infection patterns across diverse clinical settings, we analysed two distinct patient populations: a general screening cohort (Hospital 1) and a referral population (Hospital 2). A descriptive analysis, with age categorised into groups, revealed the following. Single-genotype infections exhibited distinct temporal trends: HPV31/33 remained stable across both hospitals, while HPV52/58 showed fluctuating declines in Hospital 1 versus rising volatility in Hospital 2 (online supplemental figure S1 A). Multi-genotype co-infections were most frequently identified in individuals aged 21–40, with HPV16/52/58 combinations dominating (Hospital 1: 23%/17%/16%; Hospital 2: 16%/18%/11%), demonstrating strong inter-hospital correlation (Pearson’s r=0.851) in genotype pairing patterns (online supplemental figure S1(B,C,D,E)).

Risk analysis of cervical lesion severity

Cervical lesion severity was defined as an ordinal variable with four levels based on clinical diagnosis, primarily informed by histology: Level 0 (no abnormality), Level 1 (LSIL/CIN1), Level 2 (HSIL/CIN2–CIN3) and Level 3 (cancer), with Level 0 as the reference. The results of CLMs indicate that age significantly increases the severity of cervical lesions associated with HPV infection. Furthermore, compared with single infections (SHPV), HPV co-infections (double, triple and quadruple infections) significantly increase the severity of cervical lesions (see the supplementary material for HPV Infection and Cervical Lesion Severity Risk Analysis). Risk of severe cervical lesions for single genotype infection

CLMs from Hospital 1 and 2 data highlight age as a stronger predictor of HPV-related lesion severity than infection type (table 1). The risk difference between single high-risk and low-risk HPV infections is negligible (OR=0.96, 95% CI (0.88, 1.04), p=0.323 for Hospital 1 and OR=1.02, 95% CI (0.10, 1.94), p=0.659 for Hospital 2), showing no statistical significance. For both hospitals, age shows a significant positive correlation with lesion severity (OR=1.37, 95% CI (1.33,1.41), p<0.001 for hospital 1 and OR=1.41, 95% CI (1.29,1.53), p<0.001 for hospital 2), confirming age as a critical factor in disease progression, while high-risk vs low-risk infection types do not markedly affect outcomes.

Risk of severe cervical lesions for co-infection

CLMs using data from Hospitals 1 and 2 reveal that the severity of HPV-related lesions increases with both the number of co-infections and age (table 1). In Hospital 1, compared with single infections, the odds of increased lesion severity are higher for double infections (OR=1.21, 95% CI (1.15, 1.27), p<0.001), triple infections (OR=1.33, 95% CI (1.23, 1.43), p=0.007) and quadruple infections (OR=1.47, 95% CI (1.23, 1.71), p=0.006). The effect of age is also significant (OR=1.26, 95% CI (1.22,1.30), p<0.001).

In contrast, Hospital 2 shows that double infections (OR=1.10, 95% CI (0.98, 1.22), p=0.485) and triple infections (OR=0.94, 95% CI (0.74, 1.14), p=0.012) are not statistically significant predictors of lesion severity. However, age remains a consistent predictor (OR=1.22, 95% CI (1.21, 1.23), p<0.001). The stark contrast in the association between multi-infections and lesion severity across hospitals highlights that the predictive value of co-infection is not uniform but is fundamentally shaped by the clinical context and the underlying risk profile of the patient population.

Discussion

Distribution of co-infections and dominant strains

In China, the incidence of multi-genotype HPV infections is reported at 32.29%, with HPV16 frequently co-occurring with HPV52 and HPV58,[8,21] which aligns with the dominant combinations identified in our study across both hospital sites. The strong inter-hospital correlation (Pearson’s r=0.851) in genotype pairing patterns suggests a stable ecological landscape of HPV co-circulation at the population level. Previous research has also documented HPV16 in frequent co-infections, often with types like HPV18 and HPV31,20 21 underscoring the non-random nature of these associations.

Clinical context modulates the association between co-infection and histological severity

A pivotal finding of our study is the profound role of clinical context in shaping the risk profile for HPV-related disease. While increasing age was a consistent predictor of lesion severity across both hospitals, the role of multi-genotype infections was not.

In the general screening population of Hospital 1, we observed a clear dose–response relationship, where an increasing number of HPV genotypes significantly elevated the odds of severe histological outcomes. This aligns with prior studies conducted in screening cohorts, which report that multi-genotype infections compound the risk of high-grade lesions,222,25 highlighting the importance of monitoring infection complexity in this setting.5

In stark contrast, this association was absent in the referral population of Hospital 2. We posit that in a tertiary care setting, patients present with a high pre-test probability of severe disease, often driven by persistent single-type infections or other strong risk factors. In such a context, the incremental risk contribution from multiple concurrent genotypes may be masked or become relatively less important. This discrepancy is unlikely to be fully explained by unmeasured confounders alone; rather, it underscores effective measure modification by clinical context. The predictive value of a co-infection is therefore not an intrinsic property but is conditional on the patient population in which it is measured.

High transmissibility of co-infections across diverse settings

In contrast to the context-dependent effects on severity, the elevated relative transmissibility of co-infections was a consistent finding. Double infections (DHPV), particularly high-low-risk combinations, exhibited the highest model-derived reproduction numbers in both hospitals, confirming their potential for heightened transmission within sexual networks.5 26 27 The specific dominance of HHHPV in Hospital 1 vs HLHPV in Hospital 2 may reflect temporal shifts in circulating strains or local epidemiological dynamics,28 but the overarching pattern of enhanced spread for co-infections remained robust. This suggests that while the clinical consequences of co-infection may vary by setting, their epidemiological role in fueling transmission is universal.

Implications for public health and future research

Our findings compel a move beyond one-size-fits-all HPV prevention strategies. In screening populations, our data support the value of co-infection screening as a marker for intensified surveillance. Conversely, in high-risk or referral populations, resources may be more effectively focused on managing identified high-grade lesions rather than solely on genotypic multiplicity.

Furthermore, the consistently elevated transmission potential of dual infections advocates for vaccination strategies with broad cross-protective efficacy to disrupt the transmission of high-risk combinations. Future research should develop context-specific risk prediction models and more complex transmission dynamic models that explicitly incorporate the patient care pathway (screening vs referral) as a key determinant of disease progression.

Limitations

Data and representativeness

Our study has several limitations. First, the generalizability of our findings is constrained by the hospital-based sampling strategy. Although the inclusion of two distinct hospital types—a maternity care centre (Hospital 1) serving a general screening population and a tertiary referral hospital (Hospital 2) treating patients with more advanced conditions—enhanced the internal heterogeneity of our sample, it may not fully represent community-level HPV epidemiology or other clinical settings. Variations in patient demographics, healthcare-seeking behaviour and clinical management protocols between the two sites may have introduced selection bias, as suggested by the observed discrepancies in age-specific ORs and the relationship between co-infection and cervical lesion severity. Additionally, the absence of detailed vaccination history and behavioural data (eg, on sexual activity or smoking) limited our ability to control for these potential confounders.

Model assumptions and simplifications

Second, our modelling approaches entailed necessary simplifications. The CLMs focused on the number of co-infecting genotypes rather than specific genotype interactions, which may obscure important variations in virulence. The transmission model incorporated assumptions common in compartmental frameworks; the use of a uniform clearance rate across all HPV genotypes may not capture known variations in persistence. Additionally, the assumption of permanent, non-waning immunity prevents the model from accounting for possible re-infections, which could lead to an underestimation of long-term transmission potential. The sensitivity analysis presented in Supplementary Section S5 demonstrates that our primary comparative conclusions regarding co-infection transmissibility are robust to the uncertainties in these specific parameter assumptions. Future iterations of the model could be enhanced by incorporating genotype-specific parameters and waning immunity as more precise data becomes available.

Unmeasured confounding and future directions

Finally, our analysis was unable to incorporate socioeconomic or broader public health confounders due to data availability. Future research would benefit from community-based cohorts, the integration of detailed behavioural data and more complex models that can account for specific genotype interactions and waning immunity to provide a more comprehensive understanding of HPV co-infection dynamics.

Conclusions

Our multi-centre study, encompassing distinct clinical populations, demonstrates that advancing age is a consistent and significant risk factor for severe HPV-associated disease. More importantly, it reveals that the public health implication of HPV co-infections is context-dependent. Co-infections consistently exhibit elevated relative transmission potential across settings, with dual-genotype infections being particularly consequential for community-level transmission. However, their association with increased histological severity is pronounced in general screening populations but attenuated in high-risk referral cohorts. These findings underscore that a subgroup of co-infections disproportionately drives community transmission, while their role in individual disease progression varies by clinical setting. Consequently, we advocate for a strategic evolution in public health practice towards context-aware interventions. This includes tailoring co-infection screening and management based on the patient population and prioritising vaccination strategies that disrupt the transmission of high-risk co-infection complexes.
