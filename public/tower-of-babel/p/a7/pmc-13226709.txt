New inventory of glaciers in Tajikistan from 3 m PlanetScope imagery (2024): the Tajikistan Glacier Inventory (TGI)
Authors: Ruonan Li, Junli Li, Majid Gulayozov, Mustafo Safarov, Ardamehr Halimov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlaciers in Tajikistan serve as a crucial water source in Central Asia. However, existing glacier data products suffer from several limitations, including outdated temporal coverage, coarse spatial resolution, and inadequate mapping of debris-covered glaciers. To address these issues, we have developed the Tajikistan Glacier Inventory (TGI), a high-resolution national-scale glacier dataset derived from 3 m resolution PlanetScope imagery acquired in 2024. Using a deep-learning model enhanced with an attention mechanism, we achieved precise glacier mapping, with validation confirming high overall accuracy (F1 = 92.1%, IoU = 85.4%). The model performed robustly in classifying both clean ice (F1 = 93.3%) and debris-covered ice (F1 = 90.8%). The TGI identifies 12,325 glaciers covering an area of 7,720.26 km2. It provides a systematic, glacier-level distinction between clean and debris-covered ice nationwide. This new inventory indicates an approximate 11% reduction in glacierized area relative to the Randolph Glacier Inventory, which aligns with the significant glacier loss observed over the past two decades. Released as an open-access resource, the TGI establishes a timely and reliable baseline for assessing regional water security, glacier-related hazards, and climate-change impacts across Central Asia.

Background & Summary

Increased glacier instability, triggered by rapid glacier melting, has emerged as a significant environmental challenge, particularly in alpine regions1–3. This process not only elevates the risk of periglacial hazards but also substantially intensifies freshwater scarcity4–7. Glaciers serve as sensitive indicators of climate change and act as “solid reservoirs” in arid and semi-arid regions8–10, and their meltwater provides critical water resources for ecosystems, agriculture, and human livelihoods11,12. Especially in Central Asia, many large rivers receive substantial glacier meltwater from their headwaters, this meltwater is particularly important during summer, when irrigation demand is highest, and during drought years13,14. Tajikistan, known as the “Water Tower of Central Asia”, contains some of the largest glacier systems in the region, and their meltwater sustains downstream rivers and regional water resources15. However, under ongoing climate warming, glaciers in Tajikistan are retreating rapidly, posing increasing risks to regional water-supply stability16.

Despite this importance, accurate assessment and prediction of glacier changes in Tajikistan remain constrained by limitations in existing glacier inventories. Global datasets, such as the Randolph Glacier Inventory (RGI) and the Global Land Ice Measurements from Space (GLIMS) database17,18, provide essential baselines for broad-scale glacier studies. However, their application to contemporary glacier assessment in Tajikistan is limited by differences in acquisition time, source imagery, spatial resolution, and delineation standards. In Central Asia, many glacier outlines in these inventories are based primarily on imagery acquired during the 2000s, and therefore cannot fully provide a temporally coherent benchmark for present-day glacier conditions. Moreover, because these datasets integrate outlines produced by different regional centers using different interpretation protocols, boundary accuracy may vary substantially in complex mountain environments such as the Pamir Plateau, particularly where debris cover, steep terrain, and topographic shadows complicate glacier delineation19,20.

A further challenge is the widespread occurrence of debris-covered ice in Tajikistan’s glacier systems.. The ablation behavior and hydrological response of debris-covered ice differ markedly from those of clean ice. Thin debris layers can enhance melt, whereas thick debris layers can insulate the underlying ice and reduce ablation19,21,22. These contrasting processes make it essential to distinguish clean ice from debris-covered ice when evaluating glacier extent, melt contribution, and hydrological response23,24. However, existing glacier datasets often do not explicitly separate these two glacier surface types and may show limited accuracy in debris-covered zones, where glacier surfaces are spectrally similar to adjacent moraines, bedrock, and other non-glacier terrain. This limitation is particularly pronounced when medium- to low-resolution imagery, such as Landsat data, is used25,26.

In addition to global datasets, several regional or high-mountain Asia glacier inventories are relevant to the Pamir and broader Central Asian region, notably the GAMDAM inventory, the Karakoram and Pamir Glacier Inventory (KPGI), and the recent Central Asian inventory by Guo et al.27–29. These datasets have significantly advanced glacier mapping by providing quality-controlled outlines, incorporating both optical and synthetic aperture radar (SAR) data, and, in some cases, updating regional glacier conditions to 2022–2023. However, they remain constrained by medium-resolution source imagery, uncertainties along debris-covered margins, inconsistent minimum mapping thresholds, and differences in delineation conventions. Therefore, despite these advances, there remains a need for a nationwide, temporally coherent, high-resolution glacier inventory for Tajikistan that is based on recent imagery, represents contemporary glacier conditions, and explicitly distinguishes clean ice from debris-covered ice.

To enhance the consistency and precision of glacier monitoring, we integrated 3-meter high-resolution PlanetScope satellite imagery, multispectral information, topographic variables, and deep learning methods30–39. This approach enables automated and high-resolution extraction of clean ice and debris-covered ice, providing a contemporary benchmark for glacier conditions in Tajikistan. The resulting inventory provides critical data to elucidate the hydrological implications of debris-covered ice, supports water-resource management and climate-change adaptation in Tajikistan and Central Asia, and advances glacier monitoring methodologies in terms of both accuracy and efficiency17,33.

Methods

Our methodology for producing a high-resolution glacier inventory for Tajikistan is illustrated in Fig. 1. The workflow consists of four primary stages: (1) preprocessing of multi-source remote sensing data; (2) creating high-quality training samples deep learning selected from within the entire territory of Tajikistan; (3) training the model and applying it for automated glacier delineation; and (4) post-processing. Input data and preprocessing

PlanetScope imagery

We used PlanetScope SuperDove eight-band surface reflectance product(spatial resolution approximately 3 m; bands: Coastal Blue, Blue, Green I, Green, Yellow, Red, Red edge, NIR), as the primary data source for glacier delineation. The use of surface reflectance products ensured radiometric consistency across all scenes acquired from the PlanetScope platform (https://www.planet.com/).

PlanetScope imagery acquired during the 2024 melt season, from June to October, was selected for glacier delineation. Priority was given to late-ablation-season observations (August to October) when seasonal snow cover was minimized and glacier margins were most clearly exposed. A total of approximately 3,316 PlanetScope scenes corresponding to 508 selected scene footprints were visually inspected and screened for glacier delineation (Fig. 2). Scene selection adhered to the following criteria: (1) minimal cloud and cloud-shadow contamination over glacierized terrain; (2) minimal seasonal snow cover in glacier forefields and surrounding mountain slopes; and (3) adequate radiometric quality for visual interpretation and deep learning model input. In instances where no cloud-free scenes provided comprehensive coverage of a glacierized area, a multi-temporal mosaic was generated by combining the best available cloud- and snow-free observations to ensure spatially continuous coverage.

During preprocessing, Band 10 digital numbers (DN) were converted to top-of-atmosphere (TOA) brightness temperatures through radiometric calibration. Subsequently, the TIRS data were co-registered to the PlanetScope reference grid and resampled to the 3 m working resolution using bilinear interpolation. It should be emphasized that this resampling does not create new fine-scale thermal detail; rather, it allows the thermal signal to be spatially aligned with the higher-resolution multispectral and topographic inputs in the deep learning framework. The resampled TIRS layer was used only as a spatially aligned auxiliary predictor within the multi-source segmentation framework, especially in areas where spectral contrast alone was insufficient.

Thermal infrared information provides complementary contrast among clean ice, debris-covered ice, and adjacent non-glacier terrain. However, the magnitude and even the direction of thermal differences may vary with debris thickness, surface moisture, illumination conditions, topographic shading, and acquisition time. Consequently, the TIRS channel was employed exclusively as an auxiliary predictor within the multi-source segmentation framework, rather than serving as an independent criterion for boundary delineation. An ablation analysis was conducted to quantify the contribution of the TIRS channel to glacier segmentation performance, particularly for the debris-covered ice class.

DEM and terrain variables

Topographic information was derived from the Advanced Land Observing Satellite (ALOS) 12.5 m digital elevation model (DEM), obtained from the Alaska Satellite Facility (ASF) Distributed Active Archive Center (DAAC) via the Vertex data portal (https://search.asf.alaska.edu/). The DEM served two primary purposes.

First, it provided terrain-related predictor variables for the deep learning model. Using a 3 × 3 moving window, we derived a suite of terrain metrics from the DEM: elevation, slope angle, profile curvature, tangential curvature, unsphericity, and the Slope-Azimuth Divergence Index (SADI)30. To ensure spatial consistency across all inputs, these DEM-derived variables were resampled to the 3 m working resolution and co-registered to the PlanetScope reference grid. Incorporating these variables provided essential topographic context, which improved the model’s ability to discriminate glacier surfaces from the surrounding terrain and helped identify local convergent and divergent landform structures relevant to glacier margins.

Second, the DEM supported the subdivision of glacier complexes into individual glacier units. We used hydrologically conditioned watershed analysis and ridge-line extraction to separate adjacent glaciers in topographically complex terrain, following established glacier inventory protocols40.

Glacier mapping framework

This study developed a glacier mapping framework that integrates deep learning, multi-source remote sensing data, automated glacier-complex segmentation, and standardized post-processing to produce the Tajikistan Glacier Inventory (TGI). The overall workflow consisted of five main steps: (1) sample collection; (2) training and application of the SEBDeepLabv3+ semantic segmentation model to generate an initial three-class glacier map; (3) post-processing of the model outputs to remove noise, refine objects, and convert raster predictions into glacier polygon;(4) automated segmentation of glacier complexes into individual glaciers using DEM-derived divides; and (5) final manual quality control (QC) under a predefined interpretation protocol to produce the final inventory.

This workflow was designed to preserve the efficiency of automated extraction while ensuring that the final glacier inventory met the geometric and interpretation standards required for glacier inventory production.

Sample collection

The model input consisted of 15 channels: eight multispectral bands from PlanetScope (Coastal Blue, Blue, Green I, Green, Yellow, Red, Red edge, and NIR), one thermal band from Landsat TIRS (Band 10), and six DEM-derived terrain variables (elevation, slope angle, profile curvature, tangential curvature, unsphericity, and SADI). Representative image patches from non-glacierized terrain were intentionally included in the training dataset to provide background samples and improve the model’s ability to distinguish glaciers from exposed bedrock, moraines, shadowed terrain, seasonal snow, and other spectrally confusing mountain surfaces.

The source training dataset consisted of 87 rigorously screened patches measuring 3000 × 3000 pixels. All samples were manually annotated into the three classes: clean ice, debris-covered ice, and background. The dataset was split into training, validation, and test sets at a 7:1:2 ratio, with no spatial overlap to prevent data leakage. Pipeline-based data augmentation, including random cropping from the 3000 × 3000 patches, rotation, flipping, mirroring, and HSV color-space transformations, was applied, resulting in an augmented training set of 1,462 image-label pairs with a tile size of 1024 × 1024 pixels.

Model description and implementation details

We developed a semantic segmentation method that integrates deep learning with an attention mechanism to improve glacier mapping from multispectral remote sensing imagery. The method uses DeepLabv3+ as the backbone architecture and integrates a channel attention mechanism, the squeeze-and-excitation block (SEBlock), to dynamically weight and fuse multi-band features. This integration enables the identification and mapping of glacier boundaries and glacier surface classes. The decision to use DeepLabv3+ is based on its strong performance in semantic image segmentation, especially for processing high-resolution remote sensing imagery26,31,32. The model utilizes atrous convolutions and a multi-scale context aggregation module (ASPP) to capture multi-level features while effectively preserving important boundary details and class discrimination. This helps address issues such as resolution loss and contextual information deficiency that are common in traditional convolutional networks in complex glacier terrain. These characteristics make the method suitable for fine-scale glacier inventory mapping30,37.

The SEBlock model (Fig. 3) first adaptively learns band weights using global average pooling and fully connected layers. It then enhances the original input features through multiplication. The encoder employs an improved ResNet-50 backbone with a block configuration of [3, 4, 6, 3]. This architecture extracts multi-level features while preserving spatial resolution, producing feature maps that combine high-level semantic information with low-level spatial details. The ASPP module consists of four atrous convolutional branches with dilation rates [1, 12, 24, 36] to capture contextual information across scales, from local to global. These features are integrated with scene-level features from global average pooling, yielding a rich, multi-scale feature representation. The decoder operates with an output stride of 8 and employs a fusion strategy to integrate high-level semantic features with low-level detail features. Initially, channel reduction is applied to the low-level features using a 1 × 1 convolution, and the high-level features are simultaneously upsampled by a factor of 4 using bilinear interpolation to match the corresponding scale. Subsequently, the combined features are further refined with a 3 × 3 convolution. Finally, the network restores the input image resolution through upsampling. It employs a Softmax activation function to generate a probability distribution for each pixel across three classes: background, clean ice, and debris-covered ice, achieving end-to-end glacier classification.

The SEBDeeplabv3+ network architecture used in this study.

Our implementation utilized the AGRS_semantic_segmentation framework (https://github.com/spAurora/AGRS_semantic_segmentation), which has demonstrated good adaptability for large-scale remote sensing mapping tasks41,42. All experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer43 with an initial learning rate of 0.0001 and a batch size of 32. To address class imbalance, Focal Loss was combined with class-specific weights of 1.7966, 3.1948, and 5.8123 for the three target classes (background, clean ice, and debris-covered ice), respectively44. Training was conducted for 369 epochs, with 522 iterations per epoch, and validation was performed at the end of each epoch. The final model was selected based on the highest validation F1-score.

Post-processing

Following semantic segmentation, a post-processing workflow was applied to convert the initial model outputs into the final glacier inventory. First, the pixel-wise softmax predictions were converted into a discrete three-class map (background, clean ice, and debris-covered ice). Second, isolated noise patches and spurious fragments were removed using connected-component filtering and a minimum mapping unit threshold of 0.001 km2. Small internal holes were filled where necessary to improve polygon completeness. Third, the classified raster was converted into vector polygons and topologically cleaned to remove sliver polygons, correct invalid geometries, and ensure non-overlapping glacier boundaries. Fourth, glacier complexes were partitioned into individual glaciers using DEM-derived divides extracted from the automated watershed-based workflow. Finally, the resulting outlines were subjected to standardized manual quality control, focusing on complex debris-covered termini, proglacial lake contacts, calving fronts, and ambiguous glacier margins. This post-processing chain ensured that the final TGI product was geometrically consistent, topologically valid, and suitable for inventory analysis.

Glacier complex segmentation

We developed an automated workflow to improve glacier delineation by extracting topographic divides for segmenting glacier complexes. This approach builds on the methodology of the Second Chinese Glacier Inventory (CGI-2)40. First, we hydrologically conditioned the DEM45 and extracted watershed boundaries46. Next, we identified actual ridge lines by applying a positive-negative relief mask and an aspect-difference filter, with a minimum angle difference (Δθ_min) set to 30°. This filtering technique, consistent with established glacier inventory practices47,48, effectively differentiates two-sided ridge tops from valley bottoms and uniform slopes. The divides were then smoothed using the “midpoint” method from CGI-2 and used for segmentation (Fig. 4). This parameter-driven workflow, implemented in Python/GDAL, enhances reproducibility and efficiency compared to manual techniques by specifying parameters such as minimum contributing area and Δθ_min.

Demonstration of glacier-complex segmentation using DEM-derived divides.

Manual correction and quality control

A final manual quality-control step was conducted after glacier-complex segmentation. Manual correction focused on areas where automated mapping was likely to be affected by cloud, cloud shadow, seasonal snow, topographic shading, debris-covered glacier termini, proglacial lakes, calving fronts, ice-contact water bodies, and spectrally similar moraines or bedrock4.

All manual edits adhered to predefined interpretation protocols. Delineation of glacier boundaries was based on clear spectral, textural, topographic, or geomorphological indicators discernible in the imagery. Isolated seasonal snow patches without evidence of perennial ice or connection to glacier bodies were excluded. Very small glacier polygons were distinguished from seasonal snow patches based on late-ablation-season persistence, topographic setting, continuity with glacier ice, and geomorphological context. Rock glaciers and dead ice were excluded where landform morphology, surface texture, topographic position, ridge-and-furrow structure, or discontinuity from active upslope glacier ice indicated that the feature was not part of an active glacier body.

For debris-covered areas, manual interpretation integrated multiple geomorphological indicators, including spectral tone and texture of supraglacial debris, distribution of supraglacial ponds, hydrological morphology at the glacier terminus, local topographic setting, and the contrast between glacier-surface debris and adjacent moraines or valley-side debris40 (Fig. 5). In ambiguous cases, interpretation relied on combined evidence from PlanetScope multispectral imagery, Landsat TIRS thermal information, DEM-derived terrain context, and high-resolution imagery available through Google Earth.

Criteria for delineating debris-covered ice boundaries: (a) color and texture characteristics of supraglacial debris; (b) distribution of supraglacial ponds and lakes; (c) hydrological characteristics of glacier termini; (d) topographic and hydrological features along glacier margins.

Validation

Model segmentation accuracy

The SEBDeepLabv3+ model was assessed using an independent test dataset prior to the implementation of manual corrections. Evaluation metrics, including Precision (PC), Recall (RC), F1 score, and Intersection over Union (IoU), were computed with annotated test labels serving as the reference standard35,44. The metrics were calculated separately for clean ice, debris-covered ice, and the aggregated glacier class. This class-specific evaluation is important because the combined glacier metric may be dominated by the relatively high accuracy of clean-ice mapping and may therefore overestimate the model performance for debris-covered ice. Therefore, the debris-covered ice class was reported independently, as this class represents one of the main sources of uncertainty in glacier inventory compilation.

The spatial distribution of glaciers is strongly controlled by topography and solar radiation. A pronounced aspect asymmetry is observed, with most glacier area located on north-facing slopes, including north, northeast, and northwest aspects, where reduced incoming solar radiation favors ice preservation. In contrast, south-facing slopes contain a much smaller proportion of glacier area.

The inventory shows a characteristic pattern of “many small glaciers and few large glaciers.” Glaciers smaller than 1 km2 account for 89.8% of the total glacier number but only 25.6% of the total glacierized area. In contrast, glaciers larger than 1 km2 represent 10.2% of the glacier number but account for 74.4% of the total area. This size-frequency distribution exhibits marked spatial heterogeneity, particularly a pronounced west-to-east gradient in glacier dimensions. The average glacier area increases eastward toward the core of the Pamir Mountains, with the Mughob River basin showing the largest average glacier area, whereas western basins such as Kofarnihon contain many smaller glaciers.

The dataset does not provide a formal morphological classification for each glacier, such as valley glacier, cirque glacier, or hanging glacier. Instead, its primary classification distinguishes clean ice from debris-covered ice. This distinction is essential for hydrological and glaciological applications because debris-covered surfaces often have different melt characteristics from clean ice.

Technical Validation

To evaluate the reliability of the Tajikistan Glacier Inventory (TGI), we conducted technical validation from three perspectives: (1) evaluation of the automatic glacier segmentation model, (2) assessment of post-processing and manual quality control, and (3) comparison with existing regional and global glacier inventories. Model accuracy, manual correction, and final inventory uncertainty were evaluated separately to avoid mixing the performance of the automatic deep learning model with the quality of the manually refined final inventory.

Model performance assessment

Precision, Recall, F1-score, and Intersection over Union were used to evaluate the automatic segmentation performance of the model on the held-out test dataset. These metrics were calculated based on the semantic segmentation results before the final manual quality-control step. Class-specific evaluation was performed for clean ice and debris-covered ice. In addition, the two glacier surface classes were merged into a single glacier class to evaluate the overall glacier extraction accuracy.

As summarized in Table 4, the final multi-source model integrating PlanetScope imagery, DEM-derived variables, and Landsat TIRS thermal information resampled to 3 m achieved high overall glacier mapping accuracy, with an F1-score of 92.1% and an IoU of 85.4%. The model maintained high Recall for both clean ice and debris-covered ice, reaching 94.1% and 92.9%, respectively. This indicates that the model was able to effectively extract glacier areas, including debris-covered glacier surfaces where spectral confusion with surrounding moraines and bare terrain commonly occurs.

Model accuracy evaluation and ablation analysis.

To quantify the contribution of Landsat TIRS data, an ablation analysis was conducted using a baseline model trained only with 3 m features, including PlanetScope optical bands and DEM-derived variables. The comparison shows a typical trade-off associated with multi-resolution feature fusion.

Without TIRS, the model achieved slightly higher accuracy for clean ice, with Precision, Recall, F1-score, and IoU values of 94.1%, 94.5%, 94.3%, and 89.2%, respectively. This result is reasonable because clean-ice boundaries are generally well captured by high-resolution optical imagery, and excluding coarser thermal information helps preserve sharper spatial details along glacier margins.

In contrast, the inclusion of TIRS data improved the identification of debris-covered ice. Compared with the baseline model, the debris-covered ice Recall increased from 91.6% to 92.9%, the F1-score increased from 90.1% to 90.8%, and the IoU increased from 82.0% to 83.2%. These improvements suggest that thermal information provides useful physical constraints for distinguishing supraglacial debris from spectrally similar moraines and surrounding non-glacier terrain, thereby reducing omission errors in debris-covered glacier areas.

Although the incorporation of TIRS data caused a slight decrease in clean-ice accuracy, probably due to minor boundary-smoothing effects introduced by the coarser thermal pixels, it improved the performance for debris-covered ice and slightly enhanced the overall glacier extraction accuracy. Specifically, the whole-glacier Recall increased from 92.7% to 93.5%, the F1-score increased from 91.9% to 92.1%, and the IoU increased from 85.0% to 85.4%. Meanwhile, the whole-glacier Precision decreased slightly from 91.2% to 90.8%, indicating that the TIRS-integrated model tended to reduce omission errors at the cost of a marginal increase in commission errors.

Therefore, the TIRS-integrated model was retained as the final model not because it produced a large improvement in overall accuracy, but because it improved the recognition of debris-covered ice, which represents one of the main sources of uncertainty in glacier delineation, while maintaining comparable overall glacier extraction performance. This trade-off is considered acceptable for glacier mapping in debris-covered environments, where reducing omission errors in debris-covered glacier areas is particularly important.

Data quality control and assessment

The total inventoried glacier area for Tajikistan is 7,720.26 km2, with a corresponding total absolute uncertainty of ±168.63 km2, translating to a relative uncertainty of ±2.18%. A decomposition of the uncertainty reveals that clean-ice areas are the dominant contributor to the estimated uncertainty, contributing ±147.32 km2, mainly because they account for most of the glacierized area and boundary length, which accounts for 87.36% of the total national uncertainty. Debris-covered ice contributed ±21.30 km2, accounting for 12.63% of the total national uncertainty.

This uncertainty exhibits significant spatial heterogeneity across major river basins (Table 5). Small basins such as Sirdaryo (Rel. uncertainty: ±6.52%) and Shirkand (±3.66%) show the highest relative values due to their limited glacier area. Among major basins, Kofarnihon (±3.50%) and Marghob (±3.09%) exhibit relatively higher uncertainty. In contrast, basins dominated by extensive clean ice, such as Mughob (±1.62%) and Khovarkul (±1.92%), exhibit markedly lower relative uncertainty. The Mughob basin, while having the lowest relative uncertainty, contributes the most considerable absolute uncertainty (±35.95 km2, 21.3% of the national total) due to its vast area.

Comparison with existing inventories

To assess the consistency and methodological improvement of the Tajikistan Glacier Inventory (TGI), we performed a systematic cross-validation against established glacier datasets: the Randolph Glacier Inventory (RGI v7.0) which includes outlines derived from imagery acquired during different periods, with many Central Asian outlines based on imagery from the 2000s50, the GAMDAM inventory (derived from 1999–2003 imagery)51, the GLIMS database52, and the Karakoram and Pamir Glacier Inventory (KPGI) compiled by Mölg et al.53, which is based on Landsat data from around 2000.

Compared with inventories derived mainly from imagery acquired in the early 2000s, TGI better represents contemporary glacier extent and terminus positions in Tajikistan under present-day glacier conditions. The TGI documents 12,325 glaciers covering 7,720.26 km2, derived from high-resolution PlanetScope imagery in 2024, showing a smaller glacierized area than legacy inventories, consistent with glacier retreat since the early 2000s but also reflecting differences in source imagery, spatial resolution, mapping thresholds, and delineation rules. Specifically, TGI shows an 11.03% decrease relative to RGI (which documented an area of 8,677.03 km2 and 13,755 glacier entities), a 6.44% decrease relative to GAMDAM (which reports 8,251.68 km2 of glacier area and 13,568 glacier entities). However, it is important to recognize that the observed area differences represent a combination of genuine climate-induced glacier retreat and methodological variation. The improved spatial resolution of TGI allows for more accurate delineation of debris-covered ice and the exclusion of non-glacier features previously misclassified in older inventories, such as inactive ice bodies, rock glaciers, and steep headwalls that are not physically connected to active glacier ice. An independent validation against a recent (2022–2023) regional compilation for Tajikistan, reporting an area of 7,624.48 km2, reveals close agreement with the TGI findings, differing by only +95.78 km2 (+1.26%)29,49,54. This provides independent support for the ability of TGI to represent contemporary glacier extent. The remaining minor discrepancies are likely related to differences in the interpretation of complex debris-covered margins and the minimum area thresholds applied (e.g., the strict 0.001 km2 threshold in TGI versus 0.01 km2 in legacy datasets).

When comparing these datasets, it is important to clarify the relationship and differences between RGI and GLIMS (Fig. 7). The GLIMS database is a multi-temporal repository containing glacier outlines contributed by various investigators and derived from imagery acquired in different years. In contrast, RGI is a globally standardized inventory representing a specific epoch, which often incorporates and harmonizes regionally compiled outlines, including GLIMS-derived data. Therefore, quantitative and spatial discrepancies among TGI, RGI, and GLIMS should not be interpreted simply as differences between entirely independent inventories, but rather as the combined result of temporal mismatches, distinct source imagery, differing minimum mapping units, and evolving delineation conventions.

Visual comparisons between TGI and existing inventories in representative areas (Fig. 7) highlight both physical glacier changes and methodological variations. Quantitative differences in total glacier area between the TGI and legacy inventories (e.g., RGI, GLIMS) represent a combination of actual climate-driven glacier shrinkage over the past two decades and mapping discrepancies. These methodological discrepancies primarily stem from differing interpretations of debris-covered margins, steep headwalls, and inactive ice bodies.

While boundaries derived from early 2000s imagery in RGI and GLIMS often extend further down-valley, the TGI outlines align with current glacier termini based on the 2024 basemap (Fig. 7(a,c,e,g)). Compared with manual digitization, which may introduce interpreter-dependent differences and generalized boundaries over complex moraines, the deep learning classifier provides a more consistent delineation of active debris-covered ice margins—the use of a deep learning classifier provides a highly consistent delineation of active debris-covered ice margins (Fig. 7(b,d,f)).

Furthermore, variations in glacier area among datasets are strongly influenced by the conceptual definition of a glacier entity. In this study, TGI applies a transparent mapping convention: it deliberately includes steep headwalls (slopes > 40°) when they are visually and topographically connected to the glacier body, acting as active accumulation zones. We acknowledge that this is a definitional choice, as inventories like GAMDAM systematically omitted them (Fig. 7(d,i,j)). Conversely, TGI systematically excludes inactive ice bodies to maintain physical consistency. Rock glaciers and dead ice were differentiated from active glaciers based on landform morphology (e.g., typical ridge-and-furrow structures), surface texture, and the absence of clear topographic continuity with active upslope ice (Fig. 7(g)).

Finally, the high-resolution PlanetScope imagery enhances mapping capabilities in complex terrain. This fidelity significantly improves the detection of small, high-elevation glaciers based on a strictly applied minimum area threshold of 0.001 km2 (Fig. 7(h)). To minimize commission errors, these very small glacier bodies were distinguished from seasonal snow patches by evaluating their late-ablation-season persistence, clear topographic boundaries, and geomorphological context.

Accurately mapping debris-covered ice remains a primary challenge and a major source of uncertainty in global inventories17 (Fig. 8). By effectively distinguishing active debris-covered ice from stable morainic deposits, the deep learning model reduces commission and omission errors compared with conventional inventory methods19, producing less generalized and more geomorphologically consistent outlines. Building upon these improved delineations, TGI offers an important structural advancement: it explicitly classifies clean ice and debris-covered ice into separate polygons—a distinction not consistently provided in RGI and GLIMS. This separation provides a physically realistic framework essential for distributed hydrological and mass-balance modeling under varying ablation regimes. Furthermore, compared with specialized regional datasets like the KPGI by Mölg et al.28—which utilized SAR coherence images to distinguish debris cover but was constrained by coarser spatial resolution—TGI provides higher-resolution and more consistent delineation of complex debris-covered extents.

In summary, the comparison with existing inventories demonstrates that the TGI provides an improved and internally consistent glacier inventory for Tajikistan. By assimilating 3 m PlanetScope imagery, auxiliary thermal infrared data, DEM-derived terrain variables, and systematic quality control procedures, the TGI provides an up-to-date and internally consistent glacier inventory for Tajikistan in 2024. This dataset addresses the temporal lag in legacy datasets by establishing a modern baseline, while the deep learning approach reduces interpreter-dependent variability, yielding more precise boundary delineations and aiding in the detection of previously omitted small glaciers. While the TGI does not resolve all conceptual differences in glacier-entity definition across existing global inventories, it successfully applies a transparent and highly consistent delineation scheme. Together, these attributes make the TGI a useful contemporary benchmark to support future glacier monitoring, hydrological analysis, and climate-impact studies across the broader Central Asian region.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present inventory should be acknowledged. First, although late-ablation-season PlanetScope imagery substantially improves the delineation of small glaciers and complex glacier margins, some uncertainty remains in shadowed terrain, debris-covered termini, and topographically ambiguous transition zones. Second, the integration of Landsat TIRS data provides useful auxiliary information for debris-covered ice mapping, but its native 30 m resolution is much coarser than that of 3 m PlanetScope imagery and therefore cannot resolve fine boundary details independently. Third, the final inventory includes a manual quality-control stage, which improves the consistency of the product but may introduce some interpreter-dependent uncertainty. Finally, the reported inventory-scale area uncertainty is based on a pragmatic boundary-based approximation and should not be interpreted as an exact local boundary error for every glacier margin. Despite these limitations, the TGI provides an up-to-date, high-resolution, and internally consistent national-scale glacier inventory for Tajikistan.
