A Retrospective Observational Study on COVID-19 Patients Receiving Treatment with Nirmatrelvir/Ritonavir (PAXLOVID)
Authors: Mariam M Murad, Stephen L Atkin, Pearl W Wasif, Alwaleed Abdulaziz Behzad, Aamal M J Abdulla Husain, Florence Lefebvre d’Hellencourt, Jean Joury, Mohamed Abdel Aziz, Hammam Haridy, Julia Spinardi, Lu Wang, Fiona Boland, Moe H Kyaw, Manaf Al-Qahtani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study was undertaken in COVID-19 patients treated with and without Paxlovid™ (Paxlovid) in an outpatient setting to determine hospitalization from the community.MethodsThis retrospective secondary data observational cohort study was conducted between February and November 2022. All patients diagnosed by polymerase chain reaction for COVID-19 and at risk of COVID-19 disease progression were offered Paxlovid in the outpatient setting. Potential associations between Paxlovid use and likelihood of hospitalization, antibiotic use, and other clinical outcomes were explored using regression models as appropriate (i.e., logistic, multinomial, ordinal), adjusted for World Health Organization risk classification.ResultsOf 3011 COVID-19 patients offered Paxlovid, 2005 (67%) were treated with Paxlovid and 1006 (33%) chose not to take treatment. There was no evidence of a difference between groups in terms of vaccination status, viral load, age, or gender, although more patients of Arab ethnicity chose not to take treatment (p < 0.001). There were fewer hospital admissions in the group that took Paxlovid (p < 0.001). Some evidence of a reduction in the number of hospitalizations was found when adjusted for World Health Organization risk category (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 0.60, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.45–0.77), and a reduction was similarly found for the number of hospitalizations among patients with readmission (aOR 0.47, 95% CI 0.26–0.84). A reduction in the odds of antibiotic use was observed in the treated group for any cause (aOR 0.80, 95% CI 0.67–0.94) and specifically for COVID-19 (aOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.44–0.77).ConclusionsPaxlovid treatment reduced hospitalization and antibiotic use, indicating its benefit in reducing severe outcomes and healthcare burden and supporting its use for reducing severe outcomes in COVID-19 patients.Trial registration ClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT06291831.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s41030-025-00316-z.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, COVID-19, Paxlovid, Nirmatrelvir/ritonavir

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? COVID-19 continues to pose a major health and economic challenge worldwide, especially for high-risk populations, with minimal real-world data from Arab countries.There is a lack of real-world evidence on how Paxlovid affects hospitalization rates and antibiotic use—key factors for healthcare system planning and antibiotic stewardship. What was learned from the study? Patients who received Paxlovid had significantly lower odds of hospitalization (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 0.60, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.45–0.77) and a notable reduction in the use of antibiotics for COVID-19 treatment (aOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.44–0.77) compared to those who did not receive the treatment.Paxlovid treatment significantly reduced both hospitalizations and antibiotic prescriptions, suggesting it could ease the strain on healthcare systems and help limit inappropriate antimicrobial use in clinical practice. However, its underutilization among elderly and high-risk groups points to a critical need for better education, awareness, and targeted strategies to boost antiviral acceptance and adherence.

Introduction

In December 2019, COVID-19 was identified as a new, potentially fatal, respiratory infection caused by the novel coronavirus, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). The World Health Organization (WHO) declared COVID-19 a public health emergency of international concern [1] on January 30, 2020, and further characterized the disease outbreak as a pandemic on March 11, 2020 [2]. The clinical presentation of COVID-19 varied widely, ranging from an asymptomatic infection [3] to critical illness characterized by respiratory failure, septic shock, and other multiple organ dysfunction or failure [4]. Although most cases are asymptomatic or mild, patients who were hospitalized with COVID-19 may have had significant morbidity and mortality [5]. Between February 2020 and September 2021, the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimated that of a total of 146.6 million infections, 85% of patients were symptomatic and only 5% required hospitalization for treatment [6].

According to the 2021 National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines, management of non-hospitalized patients with acute COVID-19 should include supportive care, advice on when to contact a healthcare provider and seek an in-person evaluation, and COVID-19-specific therapy for patients who have a high risk of disease progression [7]. More specifically, non-hospitalized patients with mild-to-moderate COVID-19 who are at high risk of clinical progression may receive antiviral therapy in the form of nirmatrelvir (PF-07321332)/ritonavir (Paxlovid), sotrovimab, remdesivir, or molnupiravir [8].

Paxlovid was granted emergency use authorization by the Food and Drug Administration in December 2021 for individuals at high risk of developing severe illness [9]. On January 1, 2022, the National Health Regulatory Authority (NHRA) in the Kingdom of Bahrain approved the emergency use of Paxlovid for high-risk mild–moderate cases of COVID-19, and subsequently, Paxlovid was established as the first-line therapy along with sotrovimab in the Kingdom of Bahrain.

Paxlovid, the combination of nirmatrelvir and ritonavir, acts as a protease inhibitor, disrupting the viral replication process [10]. In the EPIC-HR trial, Paxlovid treatment initiated within 5 days of symptom onset reduced the risk of hospitalization or death by 88% compared with placebo in non-hospitalized adults with risk factors for severe disease and laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection [8]. Other efficacy data reported for COVID-19 therapies include sotrovimab (85% relative reduction reported) [11], remdesivir (87% relative reduction reported) [8], and molnupiravir (30% relative reduction reported) [12]. Furthermore, in vitro analyses suggest that Paxlovid is active against the B.1.1.529 (Omicron) variant of concern [13]. In real-world settings, a meta-analysis based on 14 studies comparing Paxlovid to standard treatment without antivirals found a 59% reduction in the odds of death (odds ratio [OR] 0.41; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.35–0.52; moderate certainty of evidence), and based on 11 studies, although certainty of evidence was low, found a reduction in the odds of hospitalization of 53% (OR 0.47; 95% CI 0.37–0.60; low certainty of evidence) [14]. Based on a composite measure of mortality and/or hospitalization reported in five studies, there was also evidence of a reduction for Paxlovid versus standard treatment or no antiviral treatment (OR 0.44; 95% CI 0.31–0.64). Its effectiveness was observed regardless of age (except for mortality in those under 60 years of age, where there was no evidence of a difference) or vaccination status, with a reduction of 21–92% in the odds of COVID-19-related hospitalization and mortality [15].

COVID-19 has put significant pressure on the healthcare system worldwide and imposed an enormous economic burden on society. While patient characteristics, healthcare resource utilization (HRU), and healthcare costs of patients hospitalized with COVID-19 have previously been described in US studies [16, 17], such data are limited among non-US countries [18]. Furthermore, while the majority of current research focuses on hospitalized COVID-19 patients, little is known about COVID-19 patients identified in an outpatient setting. In addition, there is a growing need to complement clinical trial findings and address data gaps by generating real-world evidence that may help inform healthcare decision-making globally. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including pre-existing comorbidities, between adult COVID-19 patients treated with and not treated with Paxlovid in Bahrain.

Methods

This was a retrospective observational cohort study of patients between February and November 2022 in the Kingdom of Bahrain. Data were gathered from the I-Seha electronic medical records and recorded in electronic case report forms. Patients aged 18 years or older, with a polymerase chain reaction (PCR) diagnostic confirmation of COVID-19, treated in an outpatient setting (e.g., receiving medical treatment without being admitted to a hospital for COVID-19), and offered Paxlovid were eligible for inclusion. Paxlovid was offered to patients in the Kingdom of Bahrain if they were at high risk of complications from COVID-19 due to any of the following: age ≥ 50 years, body mass index (BMI) ≥ 35, a medical history of chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] ≥ 30 ml/min), diabetes mellitus, immunosuppressive disease or treatment thereof, neurodevelopmental disorders, cardiovascular disease or hypertension, chronic lung diseases, or sickle cell disease [19]. The medical records for all patients eligible for Paxlovid were reviewed, and all patients who were offered and took Paxlovid and those who were offered but chose not to take Paxlovid, with complete data, were extracted for analysis. Following Paxlovid treatment initiation, patients were followed up by a telephone call at 5 days. For those who did not take Paxlovid treatment, electronic medical records were reviewed to determine whether further healthcare contact had occurred, including admission to hospital or death.

The national COVID-19 task force ethical approval was obtained in June 2023 for this retrospective study. The study was conducted according to the guidelines and approved by the National COVID-19 Task force ethical approval, obtained in June 2023 for this retrospective study (CRT-COVID2022-160). A waiver which aligns with Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidelines permitting the use the participants’ information for this study, including for publication purposes, was obtained from the National COVID-19 Taskforce ethics committee, allowing us to proceed without additional consent.

Statistics

Continuous variables such as age and BMI were reported as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), depending on the distribution of the data. Categorical variables, including gender, nationality, and comorbidities, were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between patients treated with Paxlovid and those not treated were compared using a chi-squared test, independent-samples t-test, or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additionally, all patients were classed into categories of risk (low, moderate, and high) based on the World Health Organization classification. The World Health Organization guideline on COVID-19 drugs includes the following risk groups for progression to severe COVID-19: high risk (immunocompromised); moderate risk (at least one of the following: ≥ 65 years of age, obesity, diabetes, chronic cardiopulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, active cancer, disabilities, comorbidities of chronic disease); and low risk (people who are not at high or moderate risk). To assess the association between Paxlovid treatment and the likelihood of hospitalization, antibiotic use and other clinical outcomes, unadjusted and adjusted (adjusted for World Health Organization categories of risk) logistic or multinomial regression models, as appropriate, were constructed and the results reported as odds ratios (ORs), or relative risk ratios (RRR), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). As some variables were ordinal in nature (e.g., number of hospitalizations, days of antibiotic use), generalized ordinal logistic regression models with no assumption of proportional odds (as this assumption was tested and violated) were also explored. As a sensitivity analysis, propensity score matching was performed using a logistic regression model including age, gender, BMI, nationality, and ethnicity. One-to-one nearest neighbor matching was applied to match those treated and those not treated with Paxlovid. All analyses were repeated in the propensity score-matched cohort to assess the robustness of the findings. Adverse events reported by patients receiving Paxlovid were categorized and analyzed descriptively. Frequency and percentage of specific adverse events were reported. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata v18 (StataCorp. 2023. Stata Statistical Software: Release 18. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC, USA).

Results

A total of 3011 COVID-19 patients were included: 2005 who were offered and treated with Paxlovid within 1 to 3 days after a positive COVID-19 test, and 1006 COVID-19 patients who were eligible and offered Paxlovid treatment but chose not to take it. Demographic data are shown in Table 1. There was no evidence of a difference between the groups in gender or BMI; however, there were fewer patients aged > 75 years in the group who chose to take Paxlovid (p < 0.001) and more patients of Arab ethnicity who chose not to take Paxlovid treatment (91% vs. 83%, p < 0.001).

For those at low risk (11% of total), fewer were in the group treated with Paxlovid (5% vs. 8%, p < 0.001). For those at moderate risk (82% of the total), there was a similar distribution across groups (83% vs. 82%, treated and untreated, respectively). For those at high risk, a higher percentage of high-risk individuals were in the untreated group (10% vs. 5%). In the group that choose not to take Paxlovid, there was evidence of a higher prevalence of chronic cardiopulmonary disease (50% untreated vs. 31% treated, p < 0.001) and chronic kidney disease (38% untreated vs. 30% treated, p < 0.001), as shown in Table 2. However, there was evidence of a lower prevalence of diabetes (1% untreated vs. 3% treated, p < 0.001). For patient-reported COVID-19 symptoms at baseline, patients who chose to take Paxlovid reported more stuffy or runny nose, sore throat, cough, fatigue, myalgia, headache, chills/shivering, nausea or vomiting, loss of sense of smell, and loss of taste (p < 0.001). There was no evidence of differences in shortness of breath or diarrhea, as shown in Supplemental Table 1.

There were more patients with comorbidities (p < 0.001) in the group that chose not to take Paxlovid, and more had chronic kidney disease, crystal arthropathies, or rheumatologic or other inflammatory conditions (p < 0.05), as shown in Supplemental Table 2.

Healthcare utilization in the 24 months following COVID-19 is shown in Table 3. Rates of hospitalization (at least one hospitalization) were lower in the treated group (7% vs. 12%, p < 0.001), and, after adjustment for World Health Organization risk classification, there was evidence of a 40% reduction in the odds of hospitalization (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 0.60, 95% CI 0.45–0.77). There was also evidence of a difference in the number of hospitalizations, with statistically significant reductions in the risk of one and two hospitalizations, compared to zero hospitalizations for those in the treated group (Table 3). Furthermore, ordinal logistic regression showed that those treated, compared to non-treated, were less likely to have ≥ 2 hospitalizations compared to ≤ 1 (aOR 0.47, 95% CI 0.26–0.85; Supplementary Table 3). There was also a reduction seen for ≥ 3 hospitalizations, although only 1% of the patients had ≥ 3 hospitalizations, and this was not statistically significant (aOR 0.43, 95% CI 0.15–1.22; Supplementary Table 3). There was also evidence of a 53% reduction in the odds of hospitalization among patients with readmission (aOR 0.47, 95% CI 0.26–0.84, p < 0.001). Furthermore, there was some evidence of a difference in length of non-intensive care unit (ICU) hospital stay (non-ICU based on oxygen saturation and oxygen concentration required according to standard practice); patients who took Paxlovid, compared to those who chose not to take Paxlovid, were less likely to experience a non-ICU hospitalization of 1–7 days (RRR 0.55, 95% CI 0.42–0.73, p < 0.001) when compared to no hospitalization. There was no evidence of a difference seen for 8–14 days (RRR 0.74, 95% CI 0.32–1.71, p = 0.481) or 15+ days (RRR 1.67, 95% CI 0.42–6.65, p = 0.464), although only 1% of the patients had a length of stay > 8 days.

There was evidence of a lower use of antibiotics in the treated group for any cause (26% vs. 32%, p = 0.002) and specifically for COVID-19 (6% vs. 10%, p < 0.001) (Table 3). Logistic regression analysis, adjusted for World Health Organization risk category, showed a reduced odds of antibiotic use in the treated group for any cause (aOR 0.80, 95% CI 0.67–0.94) and specifically for COVID-19 (aOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.44–0.77).

In both groups, there were no deaths and very low ICU admission rates (n = 1 (< 1%) treated vs. n = 3 (< 1%) untreated; Table 3). There was no evidence of a difference in ICU admission or emergency room (ER) visits in the 24 months following COVID-19.

The sensitivity analysis generally produced effect sizes and confidence intervals that were directionally consistent with the original analyses but often showed some attenuation of effect sizes and wider confidence intervals (Supplementary Table 4). Notably, antibiotic use in the treated group for any cause was no longer statistically significant (aOR 0.94, 95% CI 0.78–1.14, p = 0.518); however, there was still evidence of a reduced odds of antibiotic use in the treated group specifically for COVID-19 (aOR 070, 95% CI 0.51–0.95, p = 0.025). Overall, the pattern of results remained similar across both approaches, supporting the robustness of the main findings, while highlighting that some associations may be sensitive to analytic assumptions.

Adverse events were passively reported for those treated with Paxlovid (recorded at day 5) and are shown in Table 4. Impaired sense of taste was reported by 85% of patients, diarrhea in 26%, and muscle aches in 6%, with other minor events—nausea/vomiting, headache, and dizziness—also being noted in accord with symptoms of COVID-19 disease.

For those who were treated with Paxlovid, 277 (14%) indicated during follow-up phone interview that they were not compliant with treatment, and the main reasons mentioned were “being afraid” (n = 160, 39%), “felt there was no need or symptoms improved” (n = 125, 31%), “had side effects,” or “interfered with other medications” (n = 117, 29%). When outcomes were stratified by treatment adherence, there were small but statistically significant differences in supplemental oxygen use (2% in the compliant group vs. 0% in the noncompliant group, p = 0.046) and antibiotic use (31% vs. 23%, respectively, p = 0.023). No significant differences were observed for other clinical outcomes.

Supplementary Table 5 shows the vaccination uptake, with 93% of untreated individuals with Paxlovid having either BBIBP-CorV (Sinopharm) or BNT162b2 (Pfizer), compared with 90% of those having Paxlovid treatment (p < 0.001).

Discussion

Our study provided several important insights into the demographics, clinical features, and treatment outcomes of COVID-19 patients within a well-defined population in the Kingdom of Bahrain. While the impact of Paxlovid therapy has been reported in other populations, this is the first study in a Bahrani Arab population, a demographic that has been underreported, and there is a need for real-world data that are lacking. This study sheds light on COVID-19 management and the clinical benefits of Paxlovid as a treatment option in the outpatient setting.

The study's demographic data reveal a balanced gender distribution, with the majority of participants (82%) being Bahraini nationals, showing that this study specifically addressed an Arab population, and this ethnicity is less frequently reported in the literature [18]. While there was no difference in gender, there was a difference in ethnicity, with more patients of Arab ethnicity refusing treatment than those of non-Arab ethnicity, although the reasons for this are unclear and need further investigation. The main risk factors for severe COVID-19 identified in the study were age ≥ 50 years, chronic kidney disease, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and chronic lung diseases in accord with the literature [20]. When stratified for risk of severe COVID-19, based on the World Health Organization classification, fewer treated patients were in the low-risk and high-risk groups, while those in the moderate-risk group (90% of patients) showed a similar distribution between treated and untreated groups. This highlights that undertreatment of high-risk patients represents an unmet need that must be addressed. Indeed, more patients aged 75 years refused to receive Paxlovid, which represents an unmet need and a problem, as older age is the strongest risk factor for severe COVID outcomes, with a risk of death 140 times higher in those aged 75–84 [21].

There were significant reductions in the number of hospital admissions in the Paxlovid group, suggesting that Paxlovid reduced COVID-19 disease progression; however, this needs to be viewed with some caution, as more patients had comorbidities in the non-Paxlovid-treated group, which may have contributed to this. Logistical regression for Paxlovid therapy showed that there was a decrease in hospitalizations and a decrease in the number of hospitalizations among patients with readmissions. This is in accord with a study that showed that adults had a 51% lower hospitalization rate than those who were not given Paxlovid within 5 days of a COVID-19 diagnosis [22]. Very few patients (one in the non-Paxlovid-treated and three in the Paxlovid-treated groups) were admitted to the ICU, so no conclusions can be made on whether Paxlovid decreased disease progression to that requiring intensive intervention therapy, although logistic regression suggested a reduced length of stay. As noted, there were no differences in emergency room visits, ICU admissions, ventilator use, or oxygen administration in those treated or not treated with Paxlovid. This appears to differ from a study that showed that Paxlovid was associated with a lower risk for death, ICU admission, and need for supplemental oxygen or ventilation [23]. However, the difference reflects the severity of COVID-19 infection. Our real-world study involved ambulatory outpatients with COVID-19, a population in which infection would unlikely lead to death, ICU admission, oxygen, or ventilation with or without any treatment, and hence those outcomes were not affected by Paxlovid therapy. Conversely, in the study of hospitalized patients with a baseline of more severe disease, Paxlovid decreased death, ICU admission, and need for supplemental oxygen or ventilation [23].

An additional key finding was the low percentage of patients receiving antibiotics (7%) and a median of zero days of antibiotic use for those who did, indicative that these patients received antiviral rather than antibiotic therapy for their appropriate treatment. This has the potential benefits of circumventing unnecessary antibiotic therapy to help prevent antibiotic resistance.

Significantly more patients in the Paxlovid-treated group had symptoms prior to Paxlovid therapy that included a stuffy or runny nose, sore throat, cough, fatigue, myalgia, headache, chills/shivering, nausea or vomiting, loss of sense of smell, and loss of taste, suggesting that these symptoms were associated with more severe symptomatic disease that may have encouraged these patients to take Paxlovid rather than refusing it. These symptoms are in accord with the literature for COVID-19 [24]; however, dysgeusia and diarrhea are common side effects of Paxlovid and were much higher in this study than reported elsewhere, although it is important to note that those were passively reported [25]. Significantly more patients not taking Paxlovid were vaccinated, perhaps contributing to the increase in symptoms seen at baseline in the patients accepting Paxlovid therapy, fewer of whom were vaccinated. No patient had severe adverse reactions with Paxlovid, but rare adverse events such as pancreatitis [26] were reported, which highlights the need for vigilance, especially in persons with severe renal (GFR < 30 ml/min) or hepatic impairment [27].

Noncompliance with Paxlovid therapy was reported in 14% of cases at follow-up at day 5, which may have impacted the continuity of care and monitoring. Stratified analyses suggested that compliant patients were more likely to receive supplemental oxygen and antibiotics, although these differences may reflect underlying disease severity rather than treatment effects. Reasons for noncompliance included improvement of symptoms, fear of side effects, and interference with other medications; however, comparison with noncompliance to Paxlovid therapy in the literature is scant. A total of 21% of cases did not complete the antiviral treatment course at day 5, potentially affecting the efficacy of the treatment. These findings offer insights into the clinical status, treatment adherence, and outcomes of COVID-19 cases in the study population at day 5, contributing to a better understanding of the disease progression and management strategies.

Questions as to the efficacy of Paxlovid have been raised regarding the development of resistance to nirmatrelvir, one of the components of Paxlovid [28], and studies have identified pathways through which SARS-CoV-2 can develop resistance to nirmatrelvir [28]. Reports have documented rebound COVID-19 infections and symptoms 2–8 days after completing a 5-day course of Paxlovid, although this may also occur with molnupiravir treatment [29].

Cost-effectiveness analyses have been conducted to evaluate the economic impact of Paxlovid in reducing severe COVID-19 cases and mortality rates. One study assessed the cost-effectiveness of Paxlovid in China, which suggested that cost-effectiveness was highest in unvaccinated patients over 80 years of age and lowest in the 40–59-year-olds who were vaccinated [30]. Others report that Paxlovid in those aged < 65 years reduced the risk of hospitalization and/or death, and in older patients with COVID-19, reduced in-hospital disease progression, overall composite disease progression, and viral load, but did not improve the outcomes of death and hospitalization [31]. The high cost of Paxlovid has been identified as a significant barrier, particularly for low-income nations, leading to unequal access to treatment.

There are some limitations of this study. Those who refused Paxlovid had only baseline data and no data at 5 days to directly compare with those who took Paxlovid. This study did not have a prospective control group to assess the effectiveness of Paxlovid; therefore, data on the reduction of hospitalization and other clinical outcomes should be interpreted with caution. It was not possible to determine whether those who took Paxlovid, but did not finish the course, had a worse outcome than those who were compliant. Older and higher-risk patients (≥ 75, chronic cardiopulmonary disease) were less likely to take Paxlovid; therefore, care needs to be taken in the interpretation of these results, as there is a possibility that it could lead to bias in outcome comparisons. Furthermore, all the adverse events noted were in accord with the symptoms of the infection, and indeed had been prespecified in the protocol; thus, there was no causality to Paxlovid therapy; however, adverse events such as taste disturbance and diarrhea were passively collected, and this may have led to an underestimate of the overall adverse events that occurred. The study population included mainly Arabs; therefore, the generalizability of findings may be limited. In addition, to collate post-acute data that limits its long-term utility in determining subsequent long-term outcomes, it was not possible to account for COVID-19 outcomes. The uniqueness of this study lies in its focus on the Bahraini Arab population, and it was evident that treatment refusal was more common among patients. There may be several reasons for this that have been found in other populations and highlight the need for future research to explore these in this Bahrani population to enhance treatment uptake and ensure effective healthcare outcomes. The reluctance to undertake treatment that may be beneficial is likely to be multifactorial but includes a concern that they are being experimented on with the use of a new medication, cultural norms that emphasize community decision-making, concerns with participation such as time, or simply fear of the unknown and preferring not to take a medication due to a greater concern over side effects than the current disease process [32]. Financial concerns were not an issue, as Paxlovid was given free to all subjects. In our study, refusal and nonadherence were most apparent among older adults and those at highest risk—precisely the groups who stood to gain the most. This paradox suggests that greater vulnerability may increase hesitation, rather than encourage treatment. To address this, there is a clear need for health communication that speaks to the realities of Arab communities—open, transparent conversations about risks and benefits, engaging family members in decision-making, and delivering messages in culturally sensitive ways. Although these points remain speculative, they highlight important directions for future research to better understand and overcome these barriers, ultimately supporting wider acceptance and real-world impact of antivirals in Arab and similar populations. Finally, it should be noted that only those with complete data were included, and there was no adjustment for multiple comparisons in the analysis, as the primary goal of the study was exploratory, not confirmatory.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in this real-world study with an Arab population, Paxlovid treatment was associated with reduced hospitalization and antibiotic use, indicating its potential benefit in reducing severe outcomes and healthcare burden, supporting its use in reducing severe outcomes in COVID-19 patients.
