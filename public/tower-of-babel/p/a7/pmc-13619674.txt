Beyond Single-Class Therapy: Real-World Combined Use of GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and SGLT2 Inhibitors in Type 2 Diabetes and Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease
Authors: Georgios Vournas, Anastasia Sarvani, Maria Mavridou, Anastasios Tentolouris, Michael Doumas, Evangelos N Liberopoulos, Kalliopi Kotsa, Theocharis Koufakis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) and sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) exert complementary metabolic effects and have independently demonstrated cardiovascular benefits in individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). However, despite growing evidence suggesting potential additive benefits in high-risk populations, real-world data on dual therapy remain limited. This study aimed to identify clinical factors associated with the use of combined GLP-1RA/SGLT2i therapy in routine practice.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 202 adults with T2DM and established ASCVD admitted to a tertiary care hospital between November and December 2025. Patients were categorized according to their pre-admission outpatient regimen into four groups: neither therapy, GLP-1RA only, SGLT2i only, and combined therapy. Clinical characteristics and comorbidities were recorded at admission. Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) represents one of the most significant global health challenges, with rapidly increasing prevalence and substantial clinical and economic burden [1]. Beyond hyperglycemia, the disease is characterized by a complex interplay of metabolic, inflammatory, and vascular mechanisms that accelerate the development of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), heart failure (HF), and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [2]. Individuals with T2DM are at markedly increased risk of myocardial infarction (MI), stroke, and cardiovascular mortality, while the coexistence of renal dysfunction further amplifies adverse outcomes [3]. This interconnected spectrum of cardiovascular and renal complications has led to the conceptualization of a unified cardiorenal–metabolic continuum, underscoring the need for therapeutic strategies that extend beyond glycemic control alone [4, 5].

Over the past decade, sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) have fundamentally reshaped the management of T2DM. Initially introduced as glucose-lowering agents, both drug classes have demonstrated robust cardiovascular and renal benefits in large-scale outcome trials [6]. SGLT2i have shown consistent reductions in hospitalization for HF and progression of CKD, largely through hemodynamic and renal mechanisms [7]. In contrast, GLP-1RA primarily exert anti-atherosclerotic and metabolic effects, leading to significant reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events [8]. These complementary mechanisms position the two classes as cornerstone therapies within a modern, risk-based approach to diabetes management [9].

Despite this strong evidence base and clear guideline recommendations from major international societies, the real-world implementation of these therapies remains suboptimal. Numerous observational studies have demonstrated persistent clinical inertia, with under use of both SGLT2i and GLP-1RA even among patients with established cardiovascular or renal disease [10, 11]. Treatment decisions in routine clinical practice often continue to be driven predominantly by glycemic considerations, while cardiorenal risk stratification is insufficiently incorporated into therapeutic planning [12]. This gap between evidence and practice highlights the need to better understand prescribing patterns and the factors influencing the adoption of these agents in high-risk populations.

Beyond their individual benefits, SGLT2i and GLP-1RA exert complementary and potentially synergistic effects by targeting distinct pathophysiological pathways within the cardiorenal–metabolic spectrum [13]. Emerging evidence suggests that their combined use may confer additive benefits across cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic domains [14]. Although current guidelines do not advocate routine or universal use of both classes in all patients [15], clinical scenarios in which combined therapy may be appropriate are common, particularly among individuals with overlapping cardiovascular, renal, metabolic, or HF-related risk profiles [16]. In this context, real-world data on the use of combined therapy remain limited, particularly among individuals with established ASCVD. The aim of the present study was to evaluate patterns of dual-class use of these agents in a real-world cohort of patients with T2DM and established ASCVD, and to identify clinical factors associated with combined therapy.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This research was conducted as a retrospective observational analysis of adult patients and represents a prespecified secondary analysis of a previously published cohort derived from the same study population [12], addressing a distinct research question. Whereas the parent study focused specifically on factors associated with GLP-1RA use versus non-use using a propensity score–matched approach, the present analysis evaluated therapeutic stratification across four treatment categories (neither therapy, GLP-1RA only, SGLT2i only, and combined GLP-1RA/SGLT2i therapy), with particular focus on factors associated with dual cardioprotective therapy. The study utilized data from patients admitted for any indication to the internal medicine and cardiology departments of a tertiary care hospital during a predefined 2-month period (November–December 2025). All study variables were documented at the time of admission and reflected patients’ clinical profile and ongoing outpatient management prior to hospitalization, thereby providing a snapshot of routine care independent of in-hospital therapeutic interventions.

Ethical Approval

The study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval for retrospective data collection and analysis was obtained from the Bioethics Committee of the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki (approval code: 26; approval date: 11 November 2025). Patient data were anonymized prior to analysis, with removal of all direct patient identifiers and replacement by non-traceable study codes accessible only to the investigators involved in data collection. In accordance with local institutional requirements and national practice in Greece for retrospective clinical data analyses, all patients provided written informed consent at the time of hospital discharge for the use of their anonymized data for research purposes [17]. No interventions or treatment decisions were influenced by study participation. As this was a retrospective observational study based exclusively on anonymized routinely collected clinical data without prospective intervention or protocol-driven treatment allocation, formal registration on a clinical trial platform was not required.

Study Population

Adult patients with T2DM and concomitant ASCVD were included. ASCVD was defined by documented evidence of prior MI, ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack, or symptomatic peripheral arterial disease (PAD), in accordance with contemporary clinical definitions [18]. Patients were included consecutively to minimize selection bias. Individuals with documented contraindications to GLP-1RA or SGLT2i (e.g., end-stage CKD; recurrent or complicated urinary tract infections in the case of SGLT2i; or history of pancreatitis or severe gastrointestinal disease for GLP-1RA), as well as those with a history of adverse events related to these agents during prior treatment, were excluded. For the purposes of the present analysis, patients were stratified into four groups according to their pre-admission glucose-lowering therapy: those receiving neither GLP-1RA nor SGLT2i, those receiving GLP-1RA only, those receiving SGLT2i only, and those receiving both drug classes.

Data Collection

Clinical and treatment-related data were extracted from medical records at the time of hospital admission. Variables collected included demographic characteristics, cardiovascular comorbidities, and concomitant glucose-lowering pharmacotherapy, while laboratory parameters comprised serum creatinine, lipid profile, and hematological parameters. HF was defined based on a documented clinical diagnosis in the medical records at the time of admission, irrespective of ejection fraction phenotype. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated from serum creatinine using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) 2021 equation. CKD was defined as an eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 [19]. Patients with acute kidney injury at admission were excluded, and, whenever available, admission renal function was cross-checked against previous laboratory measurements to support the chronic nature of the kidney dysfunction. Data on glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), albuminuria, and body mass index (BMI) were not systematically available, reflecting the limitations of routine clinical documentation in real-world hospital settings, as demonstrated in multicenter studies across European countries [20]. Given the incomplete availability of anthropometric data, the triglyceride-to–HDL cholesterol (TG/HDL) ratio was calculated as a lipid-based marker associated with insulin resistance and adverse metabolic phenotype [21]. To better characterize overall cardiovascular burden, a composite variable of polyvascular disease was constructed, defined as the presence of disease in at least two vascular territories (coronary, cerebrovascular, or peripheral arterial).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Comparisons across the four treatment groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. To evaluate independent correlates of treatment patterns, a multinomial logistic regression model was constructed using “no treatment with either drug class” as the reference category. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and included age, sex, HF, polyvascular disease, CKD, insulin use, and metformin use. These variables were chosen to capture demographic characteristics, cardiovascular burden, renal status, and treatment intensity, while avoiding collinearity and over-adjustment. Although several additional laboratory parameters were explored descriptively, these variables were not incorporated into the primary multivariable models in order to preserve parsimony, minimize overfitting, and avoid inclusion of biologically overlapping covariates. Given the relatively small subgroup sizes, an additional parsimonious sensitivity analysis was performed to reduce the risk of overfitting. This model included age, HF, CKD, and insulin use, which were selected to capture demographic profile, overt cardiorenal comorbidity, renal status, and treatment intensity while limiting the number of covariates.

A secondary binary logistic regression analysis was conducted among patients receiving at least one of the two drug classes, with the dependent variable defined as use of both agents versus use of a single agent. This analysis was specifically designed to identify factors associated with escalation to combination therapy, independent of those influencing the initial prescription of either class. The same set of covariates was retained to ensure consistency across models. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11) with the pandas and statsmodels libraries. Formal sample size calculations were not performed because the study was designed as an exploratory retrospective cross-sectional analysis without a prespecified primary outcome. Post hoc power estimation was not performed, as it is considered to provide limited additional interpretative value in exploratory observational analyses [22].

Results

Study Population

A total of 202 patients were included in the analysis. The mean age of the overall cohort was 76.2 ± 9.0 years, and 31.7% of participants were female. The mean eGFR was 68 ± 25 mL/min/1.73 m2, with 41.6% of patients classified as having CKD. Regarding cardiovascular comorbidities, ischemic heart disease was present in 46.5% of patients, cerebrovascular disease in 32.2%, PAD in 14.9%, and HF in 19.3%. Polyvascular disease was identified in 30.2% of the cohort. Hypertension and dyslipidemia were highly prevalent, affecting 74.8% and 75.7% of patients, respectively. In terms of glucose-lowering therapy, 35.6% of patients were treated with insulin and 52.0% with metformin.

Differences Between Treatment Groups

Among the 202 patients, 110 (54.5%) were not receiving either GLP-1RA or SGLT2i, 22 (10.9%) were treated with GLP-1RA only, 43 (21.3%) with SGLT2i only, and 27 (13.4%) with both drug classes.

Significant differences in demographic and clinical characteristics were observed across the four treatment groups (Table 1). Age differed markedly, with patients receiving combined therapy being the youngest (70.3 ± 10.2 years), followed by those receiving GLP-1RA only (73.8 ± 9.4 years), SGLT2i only (76.5 ± 8.3 years), and those receiving neither therapy (78.3 ± 7.8 years) (P < 0.001). The prevalence of HF increased progressively across treatment categories, from 10.0% in patients receiving neither therapy to 18.2% in those receiving GLP-1RA only, 27.9% in those receiving SGLT2i only, and 37.0% in those receiving both drug classes (P = 0.003).

Insulin use demonstrated a strong gradient across groups, being highest among patients receiving combined therapy (81.5%), compared with GLP-1RA only (54.5%), SGLT2i only (20.9%), and neither therapy (27.3%) (P < 0.001). Metformin use differed significantly across groups (P = 0.01), with lower use among GLP-1RA recipients. In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed in sex distribution (P = 0.64), ischemic heart disease (P = 0.87), cerebrovascular disease (P = 0.71), PAD (P = 0.95), or polyvascular disease (P = 0.78). CKD showed a numerical but non-significant increase among GLP-1RA users (P = 0.11). No significant differences in TG/HDL ratio were observed across treatment groups (P = 0.15).

Factors Associated with Treatment Patterns

In multinomial logistic regression analysis using patients receiving neither drug class as the reference category, distinct patterns emerged for each treatment group (Table 2). GLP-1RA use alone was independently associated with younger age (OR 0.87 per year, 95% CI 0.78–0.99, P = 0.02) and insulin use (OR 4.32, 95% CI 2.12–15.31, P < 0.001). No statistically significant independent associations were observed for HF or polyvascular disease. SGLT2i use alone was independently associated with HF (OR 3.24, 95% CI 1.24–8.48, P = 0.01), while no significant associations were observed for age, renal function, or polyvascular disease.

Combined therapy with both drug classes demonstrated the strongest and most distinct associations. Younger age was independently associated with combined therapy (OR 0.89 per year, 95% CI 0.84–0.95, P < 0.001), as were HF (OR 9.64, 95% CI 2.65–35.04, P < 0.001) and insulin use (OR 10.99, 95% CI 3.12–38.72, P < 0.001). Neither CKD nor polyvascular disease was independently associated with combined therapy.

In a secondary analysis restricted to patients receiving at least one of the two drug classes, combined therapy was independently associated with younger age (OR 0.92 per year, 95% CI 0.86–0.99, P = 0.01), HF (OR 5.11, 95% CI 1.27–20.64, P = 0.02), and insulin use (OR 17.57, 95% CI 4.22–73.16, P < 0.001). Consistent with the primary analysis, polyvascular disease and CKD were not associated with the likelihood of receiving both therapies (Supplementary Table 1).

To address the possibility of model instability related to limited subgroup sizes, a parsimonious sensitivity analysis was performed. In the reduced multinomial model, combined therapy remained independently associated with younger age (OR 0.89 per year, 95% CI 0.84–0.95, P < 0.001), HF (OR 7.97, 95% CI 2.41–26.30, P = 0.001), and insulin use (OR 8.93, 95% CI 2.84–28.11, P < 0.001), whereas CKD was not associated with combined therapy (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.29–2.41, P = 0.73). In the reduced binary model restricted to patients receiving at least one of the two drug classes, combined therapy remained associated with younger age (OR 0.93 per year, 95% CI 0.87–0.99, P = 0.03), HF (OR 3.63, 95% CI 1.05–12.54, P = 0.04), and insulin use (OR 11.85, 95% CI 3.39–41.42, P < 0.001), while CKD remained non-significant (OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.12–1.22, P = 0.10).

Discussion

In this retrospective real-world study of patients with T2DM and established ASCVD, we evaluated patterns of combined administration of GLP-1RA and SGLT2i. We observed that dual therapy was infrequent and exhibited a distinct clinical profile compared with single-class or no use, whereas more than half of the patients were not receiving a cardioprotective glucose-lowering drug. These findings should be interpreted within the context of a hospitalized tertiary-care cohort and may not be fully generalizable to the broader outpatient population with T2DM and ASCVD.

The association of combined therapy with younger age, HF, and insulin use provides insight into how these agents are currently implemented in routine care. Younger age may reflect therapeutic selectivity, with clinicians being more inclined to intensify treatment in patients perceived as having longer life expectancy or greater potential to benefit from preventive strategies [23]. However, this may not fully reflect the evidence base, as modern therapies such as SGLT2i have been shown to provide substantial benefits in older individuals, likely driven by their higher baseline risk [24]. The strong association with HF is consistent with the well-established benefits of SGLT2i in this setting, and suggests that HF may act as a key driver for treatment escalation [25, 26]. The robust association with insulin use may reflect a phenotype of more treatment-intensive or potentially more advanced diabetes; however, this interpretation should be considered cautiously given the absence of systematically available data on HbA1c, diabetes duration, and other markers of glycemic burden. In addition, the absence of differences in TG/HDL ratio across treatment groups further supports the notion that prescribing patterns were not driven by underlying metabolic burden, but rather by factors related to glycemic management. Nevertheless, the lack of systematically available anthropometric and metabolic variables limits definitive conclusions regarding the relative contribution of adiposity, glycemic burden, or diabetes severity to treatment allocation. Notably, the absence of an association with polyvascular disease suggests that therapeutic choices may not be systematically guided by overall atherosclerotic burden.

At the same time, while HF emerged as a strong and consistent predictor of both SGLT2i use and combined therapy, CKD was not associated with dual-class treatment. This finding is noteworthy given the robust evidence supporting the renal and cardiovascular benefits of both drug classes in patients with impaired kidney function [27]. One possible explanation is that HF, often characterized by overt symptoms and frequent hospitalizations, is more readily recognized and prioritized in clinical decision-making. In contrast, CKD may remain underdiagnosed or underappreciated, particularly in its earlier stages, despite its high prevalence in patients with T2DM [28]. Alternatively, therapeutic inertia or concerns regarding drug tolerability in the setting of reduced renal function may contribute to this discrepancy [29]. Collectively, these findings suggest that, in real-world practice, treatment decisions may be disproportionately influenced by clinically overt conditions such as HF, while more insidious yet equally prognostically important conditions such as CKD are less consistently incorporated into therapeutic strategies [30]. This highlights the need for improved detection and systematic integration of renal risk in the management of patients with T2DM and established ASCVD. However, this interpretation should be considered cautiously given the absence of albuminuria data and the incomplete characterization of CKD severity.

Real-world studies have consistently demonstrated delayed initiation and suboptimal uptake of SGLT2i and GLP-1RA, even among patients with clear cardiovascular or renal indications [31, 32]. Our analysis extends these observations by suggesting that, even when these agents are used, their combined administration is not systematically aligned with overall cardiorenal risk, but rather reflects selective treatment patterns. These findings suggest that clinical inertia may involve not only under use of cardioprotective glucose-lowering therapies overall but also selective implementation of combination strategies in patients with overlapping cardiorenal–metabolic risk profiles [33]. Although current guidelines advocate a shift toward a cardiorenal risk-based approach, in which both classes play a central role in high-risk patients, our results suggest that this paradigm has not yet been fully implemented in routine practice. Addressing barriers such as therapeutic complexity, tolerability concerns, cost, and fragmented care, and promoting a more integrated and proactive treatment strategy, may help ensure that patients derive the full benefit of these therapies [34].

The strengths of this study include the use of consecutive patients in a real-world clinical setting, the detailed characterization of cardiovascular comorbidities, and the application of a multinomial analytical framework allowing simultaneous evaluation of multiple treatment patterns. Importantly, although derived from the same underlying cohort as a previous publication [12], the present analysis addressed a distinct clinical question focused on combined cardioprotective therapy rather than isolated GLP-1RA use, thereby providing complementary insight into real-world treatment patterns among high-risk individuals with T2DM and ASCVD.

However, several important limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design precludes causal inference and is subject to residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. In addition, the study population consisted of hospitalized patients admitted for any indication to internal medicine and cardiology departments, who may differ substantially from the broader outpatient T2DM and ASCVD population in terms of frailty, multimorbidity, acute illness burden, polypharmacy, and treatment eligibility. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted primarily as reflecting prescribing patterns within a high-risk tertiary-care setting rather than routine outpatient diabetes care in general. The sample size, particularly within subgroup analyses, was modest, which may limit statistical power for some associations. This may have increased the risk of sparse-data bias, model instability, and overfitting, particularly for the smaller treatment subgroups. Although the main findings were directionally consistent in parsimonious sensitivity analyses, the regression results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Data on certain clinical parameters, including HbA1c, BMI, albuminuria, duration of diabetes, and duration of exposure to therapy, were not systematically available, and treatment decisions could not be linked to physician-level or system-level factors. Although patients with AKI were excluded and prior laboratory measurements were reviewed whenever available to support the chronic nature of renal impairment, CKD classification was primarily based on eGFR values obtained at admission. Therefore, renal risk may not have been fully characterized in all patients. Information regarding HF phenotype, drug-specific treatment characteristics, frailty status, medication intolerance history was unavailable. In addition, detailed admission indications were not systematically available for all participants. Although acute clinical context may influence the use or temporary discontinuation of specific therapies—particularly SGLT2i—the present analyses were based on pre-admission outpatient treatment regimens and therefore primarily reflect chronic prescribing patterns rather than therapeutic decisions made during hospitalization. However, incomplete characterization of factors influencing prescribing decisions cannot be excluded. These limitations likely reflect broader gaps in real-world inpatient diabetes documentation and management across Europe rather than being specific to the present study [35]. Although a lipid-based marker associated with insulin resistance and metabolic risk (TG/HDL ratio) was available, it was not included in the main models to preserve parsimony and avoid over-adjustment; consistent with this approach, no significant differences were observed across treatment groups. Socioeconomic variables were not recorded; however, within the Greek healthcare system, access to newer glucose-lowering therapies is broadly available, including for individuals without insurance coverage, which may mitigate potential disparities related to treatment access [36]. Finally, the single-center nature of the study may limit generalizability to other healthcare settings.

Conclusions

In this real-world cohort of patients with T2DM and established ASCVD, combined use of GLP-1RA and SGLT2i was relatively uncommon and appeared to be driven primarily by selected clinical features, such as HF and treatment intensity, rather than by the overall cardiorenal risk profile. These findings highlight potential gaps between evidence-based recommendations and real-world practice, particularly regarding the overall use of cardioprotective glucose-lowering therapies and the selective implementation of combination treatment in appropriate high-risk patients. Future studies should explore barriers to adoption, evaluate the impact of integrated care models, and assess whether broader and more systematic use of dual therapy can translate into improved cardiovascular and renal outcomes in routine clinical practice.

Patient data were anonymized prior to analysis, with removal of all direct patient identifiers and replacement by non-traceable study codes accessible only to the investigators involved in data collection. In accordance with local institutional requirements and national practice in Greece for retrospective clinical data analyses, all patients provided written informed consent at the time of hospital discharge for the use of their anonymized data for research purposes. No interventions or treatment decisions were influenced by study participation. As this was a retrospective observational study based exclusively on anonymized routinely collected clinical data without prospective intervention or protocol-driven treatment allocation, formal registration on a clinical trial platform was not required.
