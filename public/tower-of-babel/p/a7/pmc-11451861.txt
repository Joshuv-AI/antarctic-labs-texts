Compound Odontoma Associated with Multiple Odontogenic Abnormalities in the Mandible of a Child: A Case Report and Review of Literature
Authors: Shraddha Budia, Nilesh Rathi, Lalit Patil, Sunnypriyatham Tirupathi, Rashmi Singh, Vishwas Patil, Humeera Mulla, Muskaan Sachdev
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOdontomas have been described as a dental abnormality that develops due to the overgrowth of certain components of the dental lamina, which eventually leads to the formation of a poorly structured calcified mass. The occurrence has been attributed to local trauma, infection, congenital defects, odontoblastic hyperactivity, or changes in the genetic material. Clinically, odontomas are largely asymptomatic and are diagnosed during routine examinations and procedures. However, they might frequently be associated with delayed eruption, impacted teeth, or missing teeth, warranting early diagnosis and management. This current paper describes the localization and management of a compound odontoma associated with multiple odontogenic abnormalities diagnosed in a 10-year-old girl. Eventually, surgical excision was planned and performed, and the defect was packed with PRF to promote healing and regeneration of the large defect.How to cite this articleBudia S, Rathi N, Patil L, et al. Introduction

Odontomas are an abnormal kind of dental abnormality occurring from the development of both epithelial and mesenchymal components of dental lamina fragments, leading to the formation of a calcified mass with poor structural organization.1 They are regarded the most commonly found odontogenic tumors in the oral cavity that are depicted to be hamartomatous formations. Paul Broca published the first description of odontomas in 1867. He referred to all odontogenic tumors together as “odontomas,” described as tumors that develop whenever there is overgrowth of complete dental tissue.2 The tissues can take the shape of several perfectly formed tooth-like structures known as compound odontomas or nondescript masses of dental tissues called complex odontomas.3 The World Health Organization (WHO) has defined a compound odontome as “A malformation in which all dental tissues are represented in a more orderly pattern than in the complex odontoma, so that the lesion contains many tooth-like structures. Most of these structures do not morphologically resemble the teeth in the normal dentition; however, enamel, dentin, cementum, and pulp are arranged as in the normal tooth.”4 Although the exact cause of development of an odontoma is unknown, it is thought to be caused by local trauma, infection, congenital defects, odontoblastic hyperactivity, or changes in the genetic material. Hitchin proposed various modes of occurrence of odontomas, attributing it to inheritance, mutant genes, or interferences in the genetic control of tooth development occurring postnatally.5 They have also been associated with syndromic conditions such as basal nevus cell syndrome, Gardner syndrome, familial colonic adenomatosis, Tangier disease, Herrmann syndrome, or odontoma–dysphagia syndrome.6 They are relatively common, with a mean age of detection of 14.8 years, and the prevalent age being the 2nd decade of life. The majority of the lesions are asymptomatic, with a higher predilection for occurrence in the maxilla (67%) compared to the mandible (33%), and a high incidence of occurrence in the anterior maxillary region (61%).7 Being asymptomatic, they are generally found on routine radiography, and the diagnosis is confirmed by additional evaluations such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT).8 In rare cases, unprompted eruption can occur with the appearance of the odontoma in the oral cavity. In such cases, it may be symptomatic, with accompanying pain, signs of localized inflammation, or infection with suppuration.9 Here, we present a case of a compound odontoma that was discovered on radiography during a routine examination of the patient.

Case Description

A 10-year-old girl reported to the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry, Dr. D. Y. Patil Dental College and Hospital, Pune with a chief complaint of pain in the lower left back region of the jaw for 2 months. The pain was dull aching and continuous in nature, relieved upon taking medication. No relevant medical history was reported by the patient or parent. On intraoral examination, there was a deep carious lesion with respect to 36 (left permanent mandibular molar) with no gingival findings (Fig. 1). Upon radiographic examination, periapical changes were observed with respect to 36, suggestive of chronic apical periodontitis, for which root canal treatment was planned. On further evaluation, a radiopaque mass was observed apical to the left deciduous first molar, consisting of multiple calcified tooth-like structures with additional multiple flecks of calcifications. It was also observed that the permanent successor, that is, the left first premolar tooth bud, was missing. The patient was then advised for a CBCT evaluation (Fig. 2) which revealed an expansile, mixed, hyperdense lesion in the left mandibular premolar region, measuring approximately 16.6 mm buccolingually, and 15.3 mm supero-inferiorly (Fig. 3). The root form of one of the tooth-like structures was seen embedded in the cortex of the inferior border of the mandible, with displacement of the inferior alveolar nerve canal inferiorly and labially in the mental foramen exit region (Fig. 4). The CBCT report was suggestive of a complex odontoma in the left mandibular premolar region, along with a vertically impacted 33. Chair-side surgical removal of the mass was planned and scheduled after explaining the condition to the parent. Other preoperative investigations were also advised, including routine blood tests such as complete blood count (CBC) and clotting time.

Surgical Procedure

The required surgical armamentarium was arranged for the procedure after obtaining written informed consent from the parent. The perioral structures were disinfected with Betadine solution, following which an inferior alveolar nerve block was administered on the left side. A crevicular incision was made from 32 to 36, along with a vertical incision along the mesial third of 32 (Fig. 5) using a 12-number blade. Following this, a mucoperiosteal flap was raised from 32 to the mesial part of the first permanent molar (Fig. 6). The first deciduous molar was then extracted using lower molar deciduous extraction forceps to gain access to the mass. However, there was bony tissue over it, which was removed using a straight slow-speed handpiece bearing a round tungsten carbide bur under normal saline irrigation. Once access was gained (Fig. 7), the calcified structures were removed stepwise using elevator and root forceps. They were observed to be embedded in a calcified matrix, which was removed using an ultrasonic scaler tip. As the calcified matrix was being removed, more access was gained to remove the tooth-like structures. This procedure was repeated until all calcifications were removed. This was confirmed using multiple radiographs with different angulations (Fig. 8). The walls were then curetted using a surgical bone curette. One particle was transferred immediately into a formalin jar for histopathological analysis. Irrigation with saline was performed, and the patient’s blood was withdrawn for formulating plasma-rich fibrin (PRF). Once PRF was obtained (Fig. 9), it was cut into multiple pieces and placed carefully into the defect with the aim of obtaining accelerated healing. The flap was then repositioned and sutured using nonresorbable sutures (Fig. 10). Postoperative instructions, including not spitting anything for a day, maintaining oral hygiene, and avoiding hot and spicy food, were given to the patient and parents. The patient was prescribed a weight-based calculated dose of antibiotics and analgesics. On the follow-up visit after 7 days, sutures were removed. The patient has been under follow-up for 6 months (Fig. 11) and has been advised to pursue further orthodontic treatment concerning the vertically impacted 33.

Histopathology Evaluation

Macroscopically, a single bit of soft tissue and multiple bits of hard tissue were submitted in 10% formalin. Microscopic examination of the hematoxylin and eosin-stained decalcified sections of hard tissue shows dentin-like material exhibiting few dentinal tubules surrounded by dentin-like structures (Fig. 12A). The mature enamel was lost during the process of decalcification. The ground section of the given hard tissue showed normal tooth structures—enamel, dentin, and pulp-like tissue—arranged as seen in normal tooth structure (Fig. 12B). A diagnosis of compound odontoma was therefore given.

Discussion

Odontomas are odontogenic tumors frequently seen in the oral cavity, but due to their limited and slow growth, they are usually asymptomatic and can be embedded within bone for many years without presenting clinical manifestations.6 The current case involved a 10-year-old female patient in whom an asymptomatic odontoma was detected in the premolar region of the left side of the mandible on routine radiography. In this case, a rare site of occurrence of the odontoma in the mandible was observed. From a clinical viewpoint, it is often seen associated with missing permanent tooth, possibly impacted permanent tooth10 and/or delayed tooth eruption and rarely associated with a primary tooth. In rare cases, it may also be seen associated with cystic lesions such as calcifying odontogenic cyst11 and dentigerous cyst.12 Torul et al. in 2020, reported that 37–77% of eruption disorders are associated with odontomas.13 Consistent with this evidence, in our case, the odontoma was found to be associated with a missing premolar and had also led to the vertical impaction of the canine, similar to the case reported by Das et al. Although the etiology is unknown, a few cases have been attributed to local trauma, infection,14 and genetics.15 Evidence also suggests the role of inheritance through postnatal mutant gene interference that controls tooth development, causing mutation of the persistent dental lamina cells and triggering their in-built capability to transform into the formative stages essential for tooth development.1 Odontomas have been described as two types: compound odontoma and complex odontoma. Radiographically, a mature compound odontoma demonstrates the appearance of a disorganized assortment of multiple radiopaque, tiny tooth-resembling structures, termed denticles, surrounded by a fine radiolucent halo.16 In contrast, a complex odontoma appears as an irregular mass of calcified material with a smooth periphery.1 In the present case, the radiographic appearance was that of a mature compound odontoma with bony expansion of the buccal cortical plate. The lesions seen in compound odontoma are unilocular and are differentiated from normal bone tissue by a well-defined cortical bone formation.17 They may seldom become large, producing bony expansion and causing pathologic alterations in the neighboring teeth, such as devitalization, malformation, aplasia, malposition, or impaction. In the present case, there was slight expansion of the buccal cortical plate along with impaction of the permanent canine. The grade of morphodifferentiation and histodifferentiation of the mineralized tissue is crucial for a definitive diagnosis, which can be made after a histopathological assessment postsurgical excision, as was done in our case.1 Previously, odontomas, being tumors of odontogenic origin, were managed with radical resection of the affected area. However, the evolution of the understanding of odontomas as hamartomatous malformations has shifted the management strategy to a conservative approach involving careful excision of particles with emphatic enucleation of connective tissue capsules. Residues left behind can act as predisposing factors for cystic change, leading to interference with the eruption of permanent teeth and substantial resorption of bone due to recurrence.1 It has been found that the recurrence of odontoma is minimal, but in younger children, close monitoring is necessary.18 The number of denticles in compound odontoma varies, occurring either as single or multiple. Hoang et al. reported a giant compound odontoma in the right mandibular canine region, which comprised >100 denticles.19 Sharma et al.15 and Sharifi Rayeni et al.20 reported 37 and 62 denticles of compound odontoma in the right upper maxillary area. In the current case, surgical removal was executed with the complete removal of 70 calcific structures from the site, which were a combination of both denticular and particulate masses (Fig. 13). Due to the large defect, the application of PRF was performed to promote healing and regeneration. On subsequent appointments, satisfactory healing was achieved. The patient has been followed up for 6 months and has been advised to pursue orthodontic treatment concerning the impacted 33.

Conclusion

Odontomas are not part of routine clinical practice, but it is extremely important to have knowledge of their clinical and radiological features. Although asymptomatic, they have the potential to lead to various orthodontic disturbances and cystic changes, which warrants timely removal and management. Early diagnosis allows for the implementation of a less complex and affordable treatment approach and plays a vital role in increasing success and avoiding relapse.

Clinical Significance

The prompt diagnosis and management of an odontoma hold clinical importance due to its potential for malignant transformation. Since it commonly presents with impacted teeth, delayed removal may lead to occlusal imbalance, which can cause long-term problems.
