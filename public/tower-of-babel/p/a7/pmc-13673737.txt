Reciprocal changes in electrophysiological and behavioral metrics reflecting empathy impairment among adolescents with major depressive disorder
Authors: Fangfang Chen, Sheng Li, Lianzi Wang, Zhongming Yang, Bin Xuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmpathy, encompassing cognitive mentalizing and affective sharing, is pivotal for navigating social interaction during adolescence. However, the characteristics and its electrophysiological mechanisms of different empathic dimensions impairments in adolescents with MDD remains unclear.MethodsThe study enrolled 26 adolescents with MDD (age:15.15 ± 1.62) and 19 healthy controls (age:15.25 ± 1.89) to investigated the neurophysiological underpinnings of empathy impairments by analyzing event-related potentials (ERPs) and event-related spectral perturbations during a pain empathy task. A conventional pain empathy task and the Chinese version of Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI-C) were used to measure cognitive and affective components of empathy.ResultsAdolescents with MDD demonstrated increased Perspective-Taking (PT) scores, decreased Personal Distress (PD) scores and accuracy in judging painful stimuli compared to healthy controls (HCs). Electrophysiological findings revealed reduced P3 amplitudes and theta-band oscillation among adolescents with MDD, particularly in the pain task. Correlation analyses showed that P3 amplitude was negatively associated with PT score, while theta-band oscillation inversely correlated with Empathic Concern (EC) score within adolescents with MDD. ConclusionsThese findings suggest that adolescents with MDD exhibit pain empathy impairments, specifically manifested as impaired emotional sharing and aberrant cognitive evaluation. The observed behavioral and electrophysiological alterations may indicate that excessive concern for others may hinder emotional resonance, while high PT demands might deplete cognitive resources, leading to diminished late cognitive evaluation. Future clinical interventions probably can target the modulation of PT and the alleviation of excessive empathic distress to restore social-cognitive functioning in this population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

The rapid physiological and psychological changes during adolescence can increase individual vulnerability to emotional erosion [22] and the risk of major depressive disorder (MDD). Indeed, MDD incidence increases sharply during adolescence [39, 41]. A meta-analysis of the incidence of MDD among adolescents worldwide reported a pooled 12-month prevalence of 8% and long-term prevalence of 19% [48]. Due to this relatively high prevalence, MDD are recognized as the leading causes of illness and overall functional disability among adolescents, which is associated with pervasive consequences including interpersonal difficulties and academic underachievement, as the most frequent cause of overall functional disability among adolescents, might manifest as poor academic performance, interpersonal difficulties, and an elevated risk of comorbid other psychiatric conditions [5, 8]. For improve therapeutic management of this patient groups, it is essential to elucidate the distinct pathological characteristics of MDD during adolescence, particularly those differentiate it from adult-onset MDD.

Impaired social functioning is a common feature of MDD that can exacerbate depressive symptoms, impede recovery, and increase recurrence risk [26, 28]. The capacity to understand the feelings, emotions, and beliefs of others, collectively termed empathy, is essential for normative social interaction [12]. Deficient empathy is considered a potential mechanism underlying the social impairments observed in MDD [20]. There is consensus in the literature that empathy is a multidimensional construct that comprises both cognitive and affective components [13]. Specifically, cognitive empathy is defined as the ability to identify and understand the emotional states of others through perspective taking, without necessarily sharing that emotion. This is a top-down process, which relies on high-order cognitive functions. Affective empathy, in contrast, as a bottom-up process involves the automatic, vicarious experience of another’s emotional states (such as emotional contagion, affective resonance), which refers to the ability to sharing others emotions through observation or imagination of another’s experiences [11]. Converging evidence from neuroimaging and lesion studies have shown that the involvement of the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), temporo-parietal junction (TPJ) and medical temporal lobe (MTL) are reported to be associated with cognitive empathy, while anterior insula (AI), inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), and the inferior parietal lobule are identified to be related to affective empathy [45]. In addition, adolescence is a particularly critical period for the development of cognitive and affective empathy [4]. Basic effective empathy emerge in childhood and tends to stabilize form adolescence to adulthood, whereas the development of cognitive empathy lags behind affective empathy and may develop dramatically during adolescence [37, 59]. Therefore, it is clinically meaningful to examine both cognitive and affective empathy in adolescents with MDD, as these two dimensions are supported by partially distinct neural systems and follow different developmental trajectories.

Empathy deficits in patients with MDD have been confirmed by previous studies. While some studies reported impairments both in cognitive and affective empathy among adults with MDD [10, 28, 51]. Alternatively, a recent meta-analysis concluded that depression was positively related to affective empathy but not empathy in general or cognitive empathy based on behavioral measurement tools, such as the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI), the Questionnaire Measure of Emotional Empathy, and Mind in the Eyes Test [57]. Studies focusing specifically on adolescent samples have reported that depressive symptoms are positively associated with affective empathy, whereas adolescents recovering from depression exhibited elevated overall empathy scores [20, 52, 57]. Thus, broad consensus that patients with MDD display heightened affective empathy (especially personal distress) in response to others’ emotions [2, 44, 46], and that excessive reactive responses to others’ affective states may trigger or amplify their own negative emotional experiences [49]. Despite the development salience of different components of empathy during adolescence [37], few studies have directly examined the neural mechanism underlying different empathy components impairments in adolescents with MDD, which may be a gap with important implications for early intervention targeting social interaction deficits and relapse prevention.

Shared-representation theory of empathy posits that the similar neural representations between direct experience and its observation when individuals observe others experiencing suffering states, thereby enabling an empathic understanding of the other’s state [31, 33]. Pain empathy, as a well-controlled experimental paradigm, refers to the ability to perceive, share and understand another person’s suffering. It is a complex phenomenon that incorporates sensory, cognitive, and effective processes [16, 40]. According to recent large-scale meta-analytic evidence showed that pain empathy is underpinned by a dual-component neural architecture: a somatosensory component involving the thalamus and inferior parietal lobule (IPL) that facilitates embodied simulation through the mirror neuron system (MNS), and an affective-motivational component involving the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and anterior insula (AI) that mediates vicarious distress. Crucially, this neural dissociation clarifies that pain empathy is not merely a subset of affective empathy. Instead, it represents a context-specific manifestation in which affective sharing (involving the AI/ACC) is tightly coupled with sensorimotor resonance (involving the IPL/IFG), while cognitive empathy (e.g., mentalizing networks) functions as a top-down modulator to prevent self-other confusion during pain observation [30, 63]. Thus, pain empathy depends on multi-level shared neural representations, spanning from pain-specific to domain-general processes (e.g., negative affect, arousal). Selective dysregulation of these neural representations may impairs prosocial behavior to varying degrees in social interaction, which is an crucial characteristic of MDD [42]. Prior system review has revealed that empathic abilities may be impaired in depression, and may experience increased personal distress when encountering other suffering [44]. Another review focus on the neural correlates of empathy also found that patients with MDD exhibit complex, potentially state-dependent abnormal neural activity, encompassing both increased and decreased activity patterns in the bilateral AI and IFG [7]. Functional neuroimaging studies on empathy for pain among adults with MDD (both active and remitted) have reported reduced activation in the aMCC and right somatosensory cortex during pain empathy processing, suggesting that structural or functional alterations in both the affective and sensorimotor dimensions of the pain empathy network may contribute to empathy deficit [19, 43]. However, neuroimaging methods have limited temporal resolution so that cannot capture the rapid and sequential processing stages involved in empathy for pain.

Event-related potentials (ERPs), with millisecond-level temporal resolution, enabling researchers to capture the temporal characteristics underlying empathy for pain [17]. According to previous electrophysiological studies, the temporal dynamics of pain empathy have been characterized by a two-stage for a early processing stage for automatic empathic arousal represented by frontal N1 and N2 components, and a late processing stage for cognitive reappraisal represented by centro-parietal P3 component [17, 24]. Specifically, the N1 (peaking around 100–150 ms) is thought to reflect the brain’s early automatic detection to unique stimuli, while the N2 (150–250 ms) is considered to be related early automatic processing of stimuli and emotional sharing in pain empathy [6, 54]. The P3 (> 300 ms), in contrast, reflects cognitive evaluation and mentalizing processes [60]. A previous study in adults with MDD exhibited reduced N1 amplitudes but enhanced P3 amplitudes relative to healthy controls when viewing pain-depicting pictures in a laterality task that required the viewer to identify the site of pain (Xue Wang [56]). In healthy adolescents, pain-depicting pictures elicited larger N110 (analogous to N1) amplitudes than in adults, suggesting heightened early reactivity to to affective stimuli during adolescence [37]. However, it remains unclear whether temporal dynamics of pain empathy, particularly the characteristics of early emotional sharing and late cognitive evaluation, are disrupted in adolescents with MDD.

Brain electrophysiological signals reflect both ERP waveforms and event-related oscillations [34]. In most ERP studies, waveforms of interest are isolated by signal averaging time-locked to repeated stimulus presentations. However, recent advances in time-frequency analysis allowing for the decomposition of dynamic neuronal oscillations within continuous signals into distinct temporal and spectral components could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the neural activity patterns underlying cognitive processing [62]. A rat model study reported that hippocampus-dependent theta-band oscillations within the cingulo-amygdala circuit of the right hemisphere may be fundamental to the cognitive processes driving empathic fear [53], while a human study reported that theta-band oscillation power was linked to emotional sharing during empathy for pain processing [38]. However, it is unknown if and how these oscillatory activities contribute to empathy for pain among adolescents with MDD.

The present study therefore aimed to investigate the neural substrates of empathy for pain in adolescents with MDD using both ERP waveform and time-frequency analysis. Based on prior behavioral findings, we hypothesized that adolescents with MDD would achieve higher scores on personal distress (PD) and lower scores on perspective taking (PT) on the IRI compared to healthy controls. Regarding electrophysiological results, given the heightened automatic reactivity to painful stimuli observed in healthy adolescents and the absence of consistent evidence for early-stage deficits in adolescents with MDD [37], we hypothesized that N1 and N2 amplitude would be no difference in N1 between adolescents with MDD and healthy controls, and adolescents with MDD exhibited reduced N2 than healthy controls. However, we also expected that adolescents with MDD would exhibit impaired cognitive evaluation for painful stimuli, manifesting as reduced P3 amplitude relative to healthy controls. Finally, we hypothesized that adolescents with MDD would show reduced theta-band oscillation during pain empathy, reflecting diminished emotional sharing and top-down regulatory engagement. Finally, given that theta-band oscillation and P3 amplitude are respectively related to emotional sharing and cognitive reappraisal in empathy for pain processing, we also hypothesized P3 amplitude is positively correlated with cognitive empathy, and theta-band oscillation is positively correlated with affective empathy among adolescents. Taken together, the present study may preliminarily demonstrate empathy impairments at neurophysiological and behavioral level, and explain poor social interaction in adolescents with MDD.

Methods

Participants

The study cohort comprised adolescents diagnosed with MDD, recruited from both outpatient and inpatient departments at the Fourth People’s Hospital of Wuhu. Prior to participant enrollment, G*Power 3.1 software was employed to determine the required sample size, ensuring sufficient statistical power [18]. This calculation specially targeted the critical repeated-measures ANOVA effects that are central to our hypotheses. To determine a justifiable effect size, the present study referred to previous developmental ERP research using a highly similar pain empathy task [37]. Although the study by Mella et al., reported moderate-to-large interaction effects for key components, such estimates might be inflated by sampling variability [32]. Therefore, adhering to methodological guidelines for electrophysiological research [32], the present study adopted a conservative a-priori approach and set the threshold to detect a small-to-medium effect (Cohen d’ = 0.25, α = 0.05, 1-β = 0.95, Repeated ANOVA interaction effects). The calculation yielded a minimum required sample size of 36 subjects. Ultimately, 45 individuals were enrolled, and a post-hoc sensitivity analysis confirmed that the study’s findings remain robust even if the actual effect size is as low as 0.22 (Cohen d’ = 0.22). Patients were diagnosed by at least one associate chief psychiatrist using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) criteria. The severity of depressive symptoms was evaluated via the Chinese version of the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) [64]. Inclusion criteria for the group of adolesecents with MDD included: (1) a primary diagnosis of MDD confirmed by a senior psychiatrist; (2) a HAMD-17 score exceeding 17; (3) age range of 12–18 years; (4) a stable regimen of oral sertraline hydrochloride for at least four weeks prior to participation; and (5) right-handedness with normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Healthy controls (HCs) were recruited via online and offline advertisements. Their psychiatric status was verified by a qualified psychiatrist to ensure they met the following criteria: (a) no current or historical record of mental disorders; (b) no family history of psychiatric conditions in first-degree relatives; (c) a HAMD-17 score below 7; and (d) absence of substance abuse or severe neurological/medical illnesses. The final sample consisted of 26 adolescents with MDD (Mean age = 15.15 ± 1.62, male/female: 4/22) and 19 healthy controls (Mean age = 15.25 ± 1.89, male/female: 4/15).

This research was conducted in alignment with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Fourth People’s Hospital of Wuhu (Approval No. [2021]-KY-29). All participants and their legal guardians provided written informed consent prior to the commencement of the study.

Materials

Chinese version of interpersonal reactivity index (IRI-C)

The IRI-C was used to affective empathy and cognitive empathy, which is a 22-item questionnaire with four subscales for perspective taking (PT), fantasy (FS), empathic concern (EC), and personal distress (PD). EC and PD subscales are thought to related to affective empathy, while PT and FS related to the mentalizing ability and therefore represents cognitive empathy. Furthermore, the IRI-C has credible psychometric properties and its internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.750) and test-retest (r = 0.737) reliability are in the acceptable ranges [61]. The Cronbach s α coefficient in the present study was 0.807.

Stimuli and procedure

Pain empathy task

Consistent with the prior studies on pain empathy, the present study utilized a well-established empathy for pain task implemented via E-Prime 2.0 software (Xue Wang [56, 58]). All stimuli used in the experimental paradigm were real-life digital color photographs, which were shot form a first-person perspective and showed only body parts, specifically hands or feet. An independent cohort consisting 33 healthy subjects rated the pain intensity of each picture on a 5-point scale (1=’not painful at all’ to 5=’extremely painful’) to ensure the validity of the experimental materials. The validation analysis confirmed a significant difference between the painful pictures and neutral pictures [(3.38 ± 0.37) VS (1.21 ± 0.24)], confirming that images used in the pain empathy paradigm were clearly differentiated in terms of perceived pain intensity (t = 30.21, P < 0.01) in previous study [58]. Painful stimuli and neutral stimuli were carefully matched in terms of context, brightness and contrast, for example, painful stimuli is a photograph showing a hand is scratched by glass with bleeding, and matched neutral stimuli is a photograph showing a hand is next to glass in a similar context without any injury or pain being inflicted (see Fig. 1).

The formal experimental paradigm featured 140 digital images depicting common daily-life scenarios (e.g., a hand caught in a door), categorized into four distinct conditions with 35 trials each: (i) painful situations involving the left body part, (ii) painful situations involving the right body part, (iii) neutral situations involving the left body part, and (iv) neutral situations involving the right body part. The experiment was structured into two main blocks, the pain task and the laterality task, with each block comprising 120 trials (an equal distribution of 60 painful and 60 matched neutral stimuli). Each trial began with a 400 ms white fixation cross presented against a black background, followed by a 400 ms blank screen. Subsequently, a target image (painful or neutral) was displayed for a randomized duration of 1000 ms. During the pain block, participants were instructed to indicate whether the individual in the image was in pain by pressing the “F” key on the keyboard for pain and the “J” key on the keyboard for non-pain. In the laterality block, they were asked to identify whether the body part shown was on the left or right side by pressing the “F” key on the keyboard for left side and the “J” key on the keyboard for right side. Responses were recorded during a subsequent blank screen, which lasted between 1500 and 1700 ms. Prior to the experimental procedure, participants completed a 20-trial practice session to ensure they were familiar with the task requirements and could respond with optimal speed and accuracy. The task illustration is outlined in Fig. 1. Participants were required to rate the level of pain felt by the person in all photographs immediately after the EEG recording using a 5-point scale from 1 (“not painful at all”) to 5 (“extremely painful”) to measure sensitivity to others’ pain in adolescents with MDD and healthy controls.

EEG recording and analysis

EEG signals were recorded throughout the pain empathy task. Data were acquired using a 64-channel elastic cap configured according to the international 10–20 system, utilizing a Brain Products recording system (actiCHamp, Brain Products GmbH, Germany). The system was grounded via a forehead electrode, and the FCz electrode served as the online reference. Raw EEG data were amplified with a band-pass filter of 0.01–80 Hz and digitized at a sampling rate of 500 Hz per channel. Electrode impedances were consistently maintained below 10 kΩ throughout the recording sessions.

EEG data preprocessing

Offline EEG data processing and analysis were conducted using the EEGLAB toolbox within the MATLAB environment. Raw EEG signals were re-referenced offline to the average of left and right mastoids (TP9 and TP10). To eliminate baseline drift and ensure robust Independent Component Analysis (ICA) performance, a high-pass filter (0.5 Hz cutoff, via the pop_eegfiltnew function; finite impulse response, 26 dB) was applied. Continuous data were then segmented into epochs, ranging from 1000 ms pre-stimulus to 2000 ms post-stimulus onset. Baseline correction was performed using the 1000 ms pre-stimulus interval to enhance the reliability of component extraction. Epochs containing artifacts were identified and excluded based on the following criteria: (i) abnormal spectral characteristics (rejspec; 20–40 Hz; < −35 or > 35 dB); (ii) linear trends (rejtrend; slope > 200 µV with R2 > 0.3); (iii) extreme amplitudes (threshold ± 500 µV); (iv) improbable data distributions (joint probability; 8 SD for individual channels, 4 SD for all channels); and (v) abnormal kurtosis (rejkurt; 8 SD for individual channels, 4 SD for all channels). Electrodes contributing to the rejection of more than 10% of total epochs were excluded from further analysis. Subsequently, the cleaned epoch data were decomposed into maximally independent components using the extended Infomax algorithm implemented via the runica() function. To ensure data purity, artifact-related components, primarily those arising from electrooculographic (EOG) and electromyographic (EMG) sources, were detected and discarded via the EEG_SASICA plugin, which facilitates a semi-automated objective rejection process.

On average, 43.42 (95% CI, [41.63 45.17]) components remained in the group of adolescents with MDD and 40.84 (95% CI, [38.94 42.69]) in the healthy controls. In the pain task, while 54.54 (95% CI, [51.48 57.04]) trials were retained in the painful stimuli and 55.35 (95% CI, [52.90 57.41]) trials were retained in the neutral stimuli in the group of adolescents with MDD. 59.32 (95% CI, [58.93 59.67]) trials were retained in the painful stimuli and 59.11 (95% CI, [58.33 59.67]) trials were retained in the neutral stimuli in the group of healthy controls. In the laterality task, adolescents with MDD retained 54.12 (95% CI, [51.72 56.20]) trials in the painful stimuli and 54.88 (95% CI, [52.58 56.91]) trials in the neutral stimuli. The group of healthy controls retained 56.05 (95% CI, [49.77 59.40]) trials in the painful stimuli and 55.84 (95% CI, [48.96 59.35]) trials in the neutral stimuli.

Time–frequency representations of the EEG signals were generated via Morlet wavelet decomposition, implemented through the newtimef function in the EEGLAB toolbox. Spectral power was estimated across 50 logarithmically spaced center frequencies (ranging from 3 to 50 Hz) and 200 linearly spaced time bins within each epoch. To optimize the trade-off between temporal and spectral resolution, wavelet parameters were set to [3, 0.8], corresponding to three cycles at the lower frequency bound (3 Hz) and ten cycles at the upper frequency bound (50 Hz). Finally, spectral power was baseline-normalized using the decibel (dB) transformation, defined as [dB power = 10*log10 (power/baseline)].

Following artifact rejection, continuous EEG data were segmented into epochs spanning from 200 ms pre-stimulus to 1000 ms post-stimulus, with time-locking to stimulus onset. ERPs were averaged for painful and neutral stimuli within both the pain and laterality tasks. Based on the two-stage model of empathy for pain and relevant literature, we quantified the mean amplitudes of the N1 and N2 components within the latency windows of 110 to 160 ms and 180 to 300 ms, respectively. The P3 component was measured within the 400 to 600 ms time window. Furthermore, event-related spectral perturbations (ERSPs) and inter-trial coherence (ITC) in the theta frequency band (4–6 Hz, 200–400 ms) were computed and averaged across participants for each stimulus type and task condition. In line with the documented involvement of the fronto-parietal network in social cognition [58], statistical analyses focused on electrode clusters derived from previous studies: a frontal cluster (FC3, FC4, FCz, C3, C4, Cz) and a parietal cluster (CP3, CP4, CPz).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The chi-square test was conducted to assess the gender difference between the groups. Independent sample t-tests were performed to assess the group differences in age, pain rating, MoCA, HAMD and IRI scores. Two-way mixed design ANOVA was conducted to analysis the reaction time (RT) and accuracy (ACC) of pain empathy task between the two groups (adolescents with MDD group vs. healthy controls group), with different stimuli valence (painful vs. neutral) and task types (Pain task vs. Laterality task) as within-subject factors. Repeated-measure ANOVA was conducted to analyze the average amplitude of N1, N2, P3 and theta-band oscillation power with different stimuli valence (painful vs. neutral), task types (Pain task vs. Laterality task) and electrodes (FC3, FC4, FCz, C3, C4, Cz, CP3, CP4, CPz) as within-subjects factors, and group (adolescents with MDD group vs. healthy controls group) as between-subject factors. Repeated-measure ANOVA of covariance was conducted to analyze the average amplitude of P3 amplitude, with MoCA scores as covariance due to cognitive function might influence the modification effect of P3 amplitude. In addition, given participants were predominantly female in the present study sample, gender (male vs. female) also as between-subject factor to examine gender effects within adolescents with MDD group and healthy controls respectively. Partial correlation analyses also after controlling for gender were conducted to examine the strengths of the associations among HAMD scores, IRI scores, behavioral performances, event-related potentials (N1, N2 and P3 amplitudes) and event-related oscillations (theta-band oscillations) indicators to assess the association between the clinical symptoms and pain empathy impairments. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant difference in all tests. The Bonferroni method was used to correct for multiple comparisons in post hoc tests. The Greenhouse-Geisser correction was used to adjust the degrees of freedom. Partial eta squared (ηp2) values were obtained to examine the effect sizes in the ANOVA models, where 0.05 indicated a small effect, 0.1 indicated a medium effect, and 0.2 indicated a large effect.

Discussion

High resolution ERP and oscillation measures during a pain-depicting image task revealed abnormal pain empathy processing among adolescents with MDD compared to healthy controls. Behavioral performance results of the present study found that adolescents with MDD reported higher scores on PD but lower scores on than healthy controls. Adolescents with MDD also exhibited lower ACC and pain rating for painful stimuli than healthy controls. Neuroelectrophysiological results showed that adolescents with MDD exhibited reduced P3 amplitude and theta-band oscillation than healthy controls, which might suggest abnormal early emotional sharing and late cognitive reappraisal of pain empathy processing. Moreover, the correlation analysis also found that there was a negative association between P3 amplitude and PT scores, while theta-band oscillation also showed negative relation with EC scores among adolescents with MDD. Thus, these findings provide a preliminary understanding of the characteristics and its underlying electrophysiological mechanisms of cognitive and affective empathy impairments among adolescents with MDD.

As hypothesized, the present study found that adolescents with MDD reported higher scores on PD and lower scores on PT than healthy controls, a result consistent with previous studies [2, 49]. According to multidimensional construct of empathy, PT represents cognitive empathy, which reflects the tendency to understand another’s thoughts and feelings by adopting their point of view and also related to mentalizing ability. PD is thought to related to affective empathy and reflects self-oriented uneasiness and anxiety when facing tense interpersonal situations, and higher scores on PD suggest that individuals may experience more personal suffering when witnessing the distress of the other [11, 49]. Specially, empathy is more emphasized the ability to share and recognize another emotional state while maintaining a self-other distinction. In other words, distinguishing self from others is a critical aspect of the empathic response [23]. Individuals with MDD have reported to be showed aberrant self-other distinction, characterized by hyper-processing of self-relevant stimuli and a preferential negativity bias, reflecting an exaggerated self-focus [35]. Given the positive association between scores on PD and HAMD among all adolescents in the present study, the more intense feelings of distress brought on by witnessing the distress of the other might increase the risk of depression in adolescents [57]. There results may suggest that, without effective self-other distinction, individuals’ emotions may be excessively influenced by the emotions of others (e.g., causing intense emotional contagion) may increase personal suffering and result in vulnerability to depression during adolescence [29]. The present study also found that adolescents with MDD showed lower accuracy for painful stimuli than healthy controls in pain task, which suggests adolescents with MDD may unable to recognize other’s painful states effectively and would judge painful stimuli as painless. Moreover, there was positive correlation between accuracy of painful stimuli and cognitive empathy. Therefore, decreased cognitive empathy (especially PT) might lead to adolescents with MDD unable to recognize others’ emotional states accurately and increased affective empathy (especially PD) would further push them into a vortex of negative emotions. Certainly, excessive attention to suffering related to self also might leading to blunted processing for judgment of other’s painful experience [47, 51].

The two-stage model posits that the neural mechanisms involved in pain empathy can be divided into an early, automatic, bottom-up stimulus-related affective response and a delayed top-down cognitive evaluation process [17]. Although N1 and N2 components may vary with different stimuli or paradigms, the current consensus is that N1 reflects early sensory and attentional processing to salient stimuli, while N2 is related to early automatic emotional sharing and affective cognition in pain empathy processing [6, 54]. It is not completely consistent with hypothesis that the study didn’t detect significant differences both in N1 and N2 amplitudes between adolescents with MDD and healthy controls. Previous studies have reported that adults with MDD exhibited lower N1 amplitude than matched healthy controls, and adolescents are reported to exhibit greater early automatic empathic arousal for painful stimuli than adults ([37]; Xue Wang [56]). On the one hand, the reason for this result may be that the N2 component is not a stable indicator of emotional sharing, which vary with different stimuli or paradigms. On the other hand, the greater maturation state of the limbic system compared to prefrontal control systems may result in adolescents generally exhibit heighten brain reactivity to emotional information, especially negative information [1]. Thus, there is no difference between two groups in N1 and N2 amplitude may suggest that heighten emotional reactivity and sensitivity for other’s painful experience may be a general characteristic of adolescents.

Neural oscillation is also defined as a more stable and direct indicator of brain function, supporting fundamental mechanisms of information processing in neural networks [27]. Thus, although the present study don’t detect a difference in N2 amplitude between adolescents with MDD and healthy controls, adolescents with MDD exhibit lower theta-band oscillation compared to healthy controls, especially in the pain task. Theta-band oscillations are driven by coordinated hippocampal and ACC activities in humans and are sensitive to the dynamic processing of emotional stimuli and emotional sharing in the pain empathy task [25, 38]. In addition, the present study also found that, contrary to the hypothesis, there was a negative association between scores on EC and theta-band oscillation induced by painful stimuli in the pain task among adolescents with MDD. EC reflects an other-focused affective response that stems from comprehending another’s emotional state or condition, which may be a motivational facet of empathy may drive an individual’s desire to alleviate other’s suffering [9, 15, 36, 55]. These results of the present study may indicate that excess eagerness to alleviate others’ suffering in adolescents with MDD might actually reduce the motivation and ability for emotional sharing during pain empathy processing.

According to two-stage model, P3 amplitude is considered related to late cognitive evaluation and reappraisal during the processing of pain empathy [60]. As hypothesized, the present study found that adolescents with MDD showed reduced P3 amplitudes relative to healthy controls, suggesting impaired top-down cognitive evaluation and control of empathy for pain [14, 60]. On the one hand, despite Thoma et al., have reported that the behavioral empathy of depressed patients may depend on the functional integrity of executive control systems [51], the present study also found that evaluated P3 amplitude induced by painful stimuli than neutral stimuli among adolescents with MDD after controlling for general cognitive function. Given human beings instinctively experience the suffering of others as this response can enhance survival, and painful stimuli would allocate more attention and cognitive resources to help adolescents with MDD regulate the subjective suffering caused by perceive others’ pain [50, 60]. Therefore, this result may suggest a specific impairment in adolescents with MDD during the late cognitive evaluation and reappraisal of pain empathy. On the other hand, there was a negative association between P3 amplitude and PT scores among adolescents with MDD contrary to the assumption. PT as a core component of cognitive empathy, and understanding others’ emotions and thoughts may consume cognitive resources unavailable to individuals with executive control dysfunction, such as depressed patients, thereby reducing the ability of cognitive empathy [13, 51]. Thus, adolescents with MDD may possess insufficient spare capacity for efficient late-stage evaluation and processes for maintaining a high-level of cognitive empathy [21].

However, the study also has several limitations. Firstly, the sample was relatively small, which may overestimate actual effect sizes while providing insufficient statistical power for detection of other important differences. In addition, there were relatively few males enrolled and most studies have reported greater empathy by adolescent females than males [3]. Gender-specific statistical analysis in the present study also suggests gender differences in early shared sensory and arousal processing of pain empathy. Thus, a much larger and representative sample (particularly with an increased number of male participants) is needed to expand upon these findings. Secondly, previous studies have suggested that empathy is dependent on executive control function, and executive functions are disrupted in patients with depression [51]. Although the present study also found reduced P3 amplitude among adolescents with MDD compared with healthy controls after controlling MoCA scores, MoCA scale can only be measured general cognitive function roughly. control ability was not assessed. Thus, future study needs to further explore the dynamic relationships between different specifical cognitive control functions and empathy among adolescents with MDD. Thirdly, antidepressants also have reported influence event-related amplitudes and neural oscillations. However, statistical analysis of medication effect in the present study didn’t found any significant effects of the sertraline on neuroelectrophysiological components. This may be because the study have only explored drug dosages and ignored the potential effects of medication duration. Future research needs to comprehensively incorporate information on medication dosage, duration and other more detailed information to help better understand the effects of antidepressants on electrophysiological components. Finally, empathic capacity changes dynamically across lifespan, and adolescence is a critical period for the development of empathy. Thus, the cross-sectional design may limit the exploration of short-term and long-term changes in empathy impairments among adolescents with MDD. Longitudinal studies are warranted to explore the development of trait and state empathy impairments among adolescents with MDD.

Despite these limitations, the present study may provide preliminary evidence that empathy deficits among adolescents with MDD, specifically abnormal emotional sharing and limited cognitive evaluation capacity. In the present study, adolescents with MDD exhibited lower accuracy for identifying painful stimuli than healthy controls. While there were significant differences both in the theta-band oscillation and late-phase P3 amplitude between adolescents with MDD and healthy controls, suggesting impaired affective sharing and deficient cognitive reappraisal for others’ suffering among adolescents with MDD. Given the reciprocal trends between behavioral performance and electrophysiological component changes, which may suggest that the social avoidance of adolescents with MDD may reflect impaired ability rather than will. Thus, reducing excessive empathic concern for others’ suffering and enhancing cognition related to perspective taking may improve the social functioning impairments among adolescents with MDD.

Conclusion

The present study examined impairments in pain empathy and the underlying neuroelectrophysiological mechanisms among adolescents with MDD during a conventional pain empathy task. Adolescents with MDD exhibited reduced theta-band oscillation power and P3 amplitude in response to painful stimuli. Reciprocal trends also exist between behavioral performance and electrophysiological component changes, which may suggest empathy impairments despite normal motivation. Reducing excessive empathic concern for others and improving perspective taking may be a direction for future interventions to improve empathy and social functioning among adolescents with MDD.
