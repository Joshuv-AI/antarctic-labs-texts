Goslings with a reduced quality score can be reared as effectively as those with a full quality score in a semi-intensive production system
Authors: Krzysztof Damaziak, Wojciech Wójcik, Adrian Stelmasiak, Dawid Goska, Julia Riedel, Dariusz Gozdowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to compare parameters influencing the rearing efficiency of geese that received either a full quality score or a reduced quality score on their first day of life. A sample of 480 birds was randomly selected from goslings assigned by hatchery staff to the full and reduced quality score categories, with 240 birds (120 males and 120 females) per group. Goslings were evaluated using several specific criteria: level of activity, navel area, presence of remaining yolk, development of legs, appearance of eyes and fluff, and status of the remaining membrane. Goslings in the full quality score group received the maximum total score of 100 points. Goslings in the reduced quality score group had lower scores, with abnormalities predominantly associated with the navel area (>90% of birds). Geese were assigned to six replicates of 80 birds each (40 birds with a full quality score and 40 birds with a reduced quality score) and reared in a semi-intensive production system until they reached 20 weeks of age. During the rearing period, body weight (BW) and mortality rate were monitored, and feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated. At 20 weeks of age, 72 geese (36 from each subgroup) were slaughtered and subjected to a comprehensive carcass analysis. The reduced quality score group exhibited a decelerated growth rate until approximately 5 weeks of age (P < 0.05). However, from 6 to 20 weeks of age, the BW of geese of both sexes in both experimental groups was comparable (P > 0.05). The reduced quality of goslings on the first day of life did not affect FCR or mortality over the entire rearing period. Female geese from the reduced quality score group had a higher proportion of leg muscles in BW and carcass weight (P = 0.002 and 0.016, respectively) and a lower proportion of intestines in BW (P = 0.038). The full and reduced quality score groups showed no significant differences in other carcass traits (P > 0.05). In conclusion, goslings classified by hatchery staff as second-grade quality, i.e., reduced health quality but not posing a direct threat to survival, can be successfully reared in a semi-intensive production system.

Introduction

The substantial increase in poultry production has led to a high demand for the simultaneous hatching of chicks. Consequently, to meet the growing requirements of producers, it is imperative for hatcheries to improve their egg storage technologies to accumulate sufficiently large batches for mass incubation (Tainika et al., 2024; Pennings et al., 2025). An extended storage duration together with a higher quantity of simultaneously incubated eggs poses critical challenges to maintain optimal temperature and humidity conditions, which are crucial for incubation efficiency and quality of hatched chicks (Bassareh and Rezaeipour, 2021; Melo et al., 2021).

Contractors need hatcheries to exclusively deliver healthy chicks. Compliance with this stipulation requires not only the removal of malformed chicks or those with a poor likelihood of survival but also the exclusion of chicks that may survive but exhibit low body weight, have a lean appearance, have incompletely absorbed yolk sacs, or have other defects, as these factors could lead to poor growth. The diminished individual production performance of this latter group may have detrimental consequences on the entire flock through a lower growth rate, increased susceptibility to infections, and a higher energy requirement (Willemsen et al., 2008; van de Ven et al., 2012).

In broilers and laying hens, all chicks are classified into two main categories: fully healthy chicks suitable for rearing and those showing any deviation from the normal phenotypic traits, which are immediately slaughtered. However, the cost of producing chicken chicks is comparatively lower than that incurred in the breeding of other poultry species. This issue is more evident in species where chicks are expensive because of their limited availability and incubation-related difficulties.

Goslings are the most challenging poultry chicks to obtain and are therefore the most expensive group (Tai et al., 2001; Rosiński et al., 2006). The high cost associated with goslings can be attributed to several factors, including seasonal egg laying, limited number of eggs produced by breeding geese, and a high proportion of unfertilized eggs, particularly toward the end of the reproductive period (Salamon, 2020; Yan et al., 2025).

The incubation technology used for goose eggs necessitates a greater energy input, as these eggs are typically incubated for approximately 30 days, which is about one-third longer than that for chicken eggs, and require periodic cooling and shell humidification (Kucharska-Gaca et al., 2016). Consequently, goose hatcheries classify chicks into three distinct categories instead of the standard two groups. Along with the two extreme categories, namely fully healthy chicks and those requiring immediate euthanasia, a third category includes goslings that exhibit abnormal quality but are not at a direct risk of death (Lin et al., 2018). Goslings from this third category are frequently sold at a reduced cost to small backyard farms, where they are intended for personal use or limited direct seasonal sale. However, there is a lack of comprehensive data on the effectiveness of rearing substandard goslings. To date, only Lin et al. (2018) demonstrated that the survival rate of substandard goslings can be improved with good care when they are reared separately from full-score goslings at 0–4 weeks of age.

However, private backyard farms do not necessarily conform to veterinary supervision or strict inventory management. Moreover, rearing conditions and husbandry levels substantially vary across these farms. Consequently, there is a lack of reliable data on the survival rates and growth performance of these birds. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess the outcomes of semi-intensive rearing of goslings with reduced health-related quality and classified as unsuitable for commercial production but capable of survival by hatchery staff.

MAterials and methods

Ethics statement

The animals were treated in accordance with the principles stated in Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament and of the Council on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes, as implemented in Poland by Act 2015/266/RP of 15 January 2015 on the protection of animals used for scientific or educational purposes (Directive of the European Parliament and of the Council, 2010). The following practices were implemented for experimental animals: (1) equivalent to practices undertaken for the purpose of recognized animal husbandry; (2) equivalent to practices undertaken for the primary purpose of identifying an animal; and (3) practices not likely to cause pain, suffering, distress, or lasting harm equivalent to, or higher than, that caused by the introduction of a needle in accordance with good veterinary practice. Hence, the present study did not require ethical approval from an institutional review board in accordance with Art 2.5.e-f of the Directive 2010/63/EU and Art.1.2 of the Act 2015/266/RP (Directive of the European Parliament and of the Council, 2010).

The staff of hatchery (Waterfowl Hatchery, NaturAgra Drób Szlachetny Sp. z o.o., Nowy Glinnik 50, 97-217 Lubochnia, Poland) assigned the geese to a specific quality category on the basis of their ample experience. Chicks with very poor survival likelihood were slaughtered by individuals trained in farm animal slaughter, including Krzysztof Damaziak (NR 7/2021, PolLASA 21-25.09.2015) and Adrian Stelmasiak (PIW-P-BŻ.530.90.2018 z dn. 24.08.2018).

Hatching and quality control of goslings

Goslings (White Koluda W31) were obtained from Waterfowl Hatchery. All goslings were hatched from eggs originating from the same breeder goose flock with the peak of lay in the second year of production. All eggs were stored for 4 days at 13–16°C and 55–65% relative humidity. Virocid F at 1% concentration was sprayed on the eggs to disinfect them after collection (at the reproduction farm) and after transport to the hatchery. Egg incubation was performed in Royal Pas Reform SmartSetPro incubators (days 1–27; 37.4–37.8°C, 55% relative humidity, and 45° turning every 1 h) and Royal Pas Reform SmartHatchPro hatchers (days 28–30; 37.0–37.3°C, 80% relative humidity). From day 10 of incubation, all eggs were cooled to a shell temperature of 30°C and wetted with water containing disinfectant (Virocid F) at 24°C by using a backpack sprayer (RY18BPSB-0 18 V, Ryobi Limited, Tokyo, Japan).

Following hatching, all goslings were classified by hatchery staff into one of three quality categories: (1) chicks without any abnormalities intended for commercial production, (2) chicks of reduced quality but capable of survival intended for small private customers, and (3) chicks of very poor quality with no likelihood of survival, which were euthanized immediately after removal from the hatching baskets (Figure 1). From the first and second categories, 240 goslings per group (120 males and 120 females) were randomly selected for the study, yielding 480 goslings for the experiment. During quality control, the body weight (BW; ±1.0 g) of each gosling was measured using a precision balance PCB 1000–2 (KERN, Bellingen, Germany), and each bird was fitted with a flexible leg band bearing an individual identification number. From each group, six replicates of 40 goslings each (20 males and 20 females) were randomly created. These goslings were placed in transport crates containing Chick Pro hydrogel (Alivira Animal Health Limited, Mumbai, India) (Figure 1) and transported to six selected farms.

Experimental design and geese rearing conditions

The experimental design involved comparing all analyzed traits between geese assessed on the first day of life at full and reduced quality scores. Each farm received 80 goslings: 40 each from the full quality and reduced quality score groups. All farms were located in the Mazovian Voivodeship (Poland) and situated approximately 50–70 km apart. Before gosling transport, each farm was equipped with two large metal coops (goose houses) measuring 20 m (L) × 10 m (W) × 6.56 m (H) (Thanaddo, Yantai, China). After the houses were assembled, both shorter sides (10 m), one longer side (20 m), and the entire roof were covered with trapezoidal metal sheets. The second longer side was covered with rigid galvanized mesh, fitted with two closable exits (0.5 × 0.4 m). Each goose house contained four wooden brooding pens (3.0 × 3.0 m), lined with finely chopped straw mixed with sawdust and equipped with one reversible drinker and one semi-automatic feeder.

After arrival at the farms, goslings from the full quality and reduced quality score groups were placed in separate goose houses. Next, 10 randomly selected goslings from a group (full quality score or reduced quality score) were placed in a single brooding pen. Brooding temperature was regulated by adjusting the height of a 250 W infrared heater, with one heater installed per pen. The temperature beneath the heater was maintained at 30–32°C during the first 7 days and subsequently reduced by increasing the heater height and adjusting its power. The temperature was regulated primarily based on gosling behavior.

Goslings remained in the brooding pens from day 1 to day 28 of age. Subsequently, they were allowed access to the entire goose house. The houses had unpaved floors covered with a thick layer of triticale straw. Both groups had access to four gravity feeders (22 kg capacity) and four reversible drinkers (15 L capacity). Lighting was provided by two 11 W LED bulbs. The birds were initially exposed to artificial lighting during the first week after their release from the brooding pens and subsequently relied solely on natural daylight.

Outdoor access was provided from day 42 of age. Each group had access to a separate adjacent paddock of 220 m² area (5.5 m² per goose). The paddocks provided ad libitum access to green forage composed of perennial ryegrass (50%), red fescue (30%), Kentucky bluegrass (15%), and common yarrow (5%). Rearing was conducted until 20 weeks of age.

All geese were fed the same starter diet from day 1 to the end of week 5 and a grower diet from weeks 6 to 17. Table 3 shows diet ingredients, composition, and feeding schedules. Composite feed samples for analysis were randomly collected from different bags, three times each for the starter and grower diets. Metabolizable energy (ME; kcal/kg) was calculated based on the analyzed chemical composition by using conversion factors specified in the Ordinance of the Minister of Agriculture and Rural Development (NRC, 1994), according to the following formula:ME(kcal/kg)=4.31×(CP×DCG)+9.28×(CF×DCG)+4.14×(NFE×DCG) where CP = crude protein, CF = crude fat, NFE = nitrogen-free extract compounds, and DCG = proper factors given in the NRC (National Research Council, 1994).

In weeks 16 and 17 of age, geese had access to whole oat grain, whose composition was also analyzed according to AOAC (2025) methods and presented in Table 3. From weeks 17 to 20, no concentrate diet was provided, and only oat grains were fed ad libitum. Dragon variety oats (Kp 2539/75) were used for feeding. Geese had unlimited access to outdoor paddocks until the end of the rearing period.

Individual BW was measured using an electronic scale (Steinberg SBS-PT-50/2, Germany) every 7 days from weeks 1 to 3 of age and subsequently at 2-week intervals. Intake of concentrate feed was monitored from day 1 of life, while intake of oat grains was monitored from week 17 onward. Feed conversion ratio (FCR; kg/kg) was calculated separately for concentrate diets and oat grains. Bird mortality and culling were monitored continuously.

Slaughter and dissection analysis

At 20 weeks of age, during the final BW measurement, six males and six females from each experimental group were randomly selected from each farm. A total of 144 geese were selected for slaughter (12 geese × 2 experimental groups × 6 farms). The selected birds were transported in crates to the experimental slaughterhouse of the Warsaw University of Life Sciences (RZD Wilanów-Obory 101). The transport duration was approximately 1.5 h, depending on the farm, and the transport was conducted at night to reduce stress and avoid high temperatures. Following entry into the slaughterhouse, the birds were rested for 8 h in littered pens without feed but with free access to water.

Immediately before slaughter, geese were weighed again (BW1) and stunned using an STZ 6 electrical stunner (PPHU KOMA Sp. z o.o., Świdnica, Poland) at 50 Hz for 4 s. Subsequently, the birds were slaughtered by severing the carotid artery and bled for approximately 5 min. After bleeding, the head was removed at the second cervical vertebra. The head and entire body after bleeding were weighed (BW2).

Scalding was performed in water at 75°C for 3 min. After scalding and defeathering, the birds were weighed again (BW3). Blood weight and feather weight were calculated as BW1 − BW2 − head weight and BW2 − BW3, respectively. Feet were removed at the hock joints and weighed. The entire gastrointestinal tract was removed, and the liver without the gallbladder, the gizzard without digesta and keratinous epithelium, and the intestine with the esophagus were weighed separately. The heart and trachea were also removed and weighed.

Carcasses were weighed and stored at 4°C for 24 h. After chilling, carcasses were reweighed and dissected. Lungs, kidneys, and abdominal fat were removed and weighed individually. Breast muscles were excised and weighed with surrounding skin and subcutaneous fat; subsequently, skin and fat were removed, and the muscles alone (fillets) were weighed. The same procedure was applied to leg muscles after removing the femur and tibia. Necks and wings were also removed and weighed. Finally, the total weight of bones and skin with fat was recorded.

The collected data were used to estimate the weight (g) and proportion (%) relative to BW or carcass weight of edible components (breast muscles with and without skin and subcutaneous fat; leg muscles with and without skin and subcutaneous fat; wings; neck; gizzard; liver; and heart) and inedible components (skin with fat, bones, head, feet, feathers, and blood). BW loss during post-slaughter processing and dissection was also calculated.

Statistical analysis

Growth data were analyzed by fitting three nonlinear growth models (Gompertz, Logistic, and von Bertalanffy) by using nonlinear least-squares regression.

Gompertz model: W(t) = A × exp(-exp(-k × (t - c)))

Logistic model: W(t) = A / (1 + exp(-k × (t - c))) von Bertalanffy model: W(t) = A × (1 - exp(-k × t))^c where W(t): weight at time t (days); A: asymptotic weight (maximum weight); k: growth rate coefficient; c: scaling or time-shift parameter (depending on the model); and t: time (in days)

For each model and group (sex and gosling quality score), the asymptotic weight (A), growth rate (k), and curve-specific parameter (c) were determined along with their standard errors. Model fit quality was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²) and root mean square error (RMSE). Inflection-point characteristics, including inflection-point weight (IPW), maximum daily increment (MI), and age at the inflection point (IPA), were derived analytically from the parameter estimates. To determine the predictive performance of the fitted models, estimated BWs at 20 weeks of age were compared with the actual measured values for both gosling groups. The studied variables were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The one-way ANOVA model was as follows:Yij=μ+Ai+eijwhere Yij is the observed value of the trait, μ is the overall mean, Ai is the fixed effect of gosling quality (full score vs. reduced score), and eij is the random error.

Results are expressed as mean (LS) ± standard error (SE). A P-value of <0.05 for group comparison was considered significant. Statistical analyses and growth curve fitting were performed in the Google Colab environment using Python libraries, including pandas, NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn and Matplotlib. Mean values between the groups were compared using Statistica 13.3 software (TIBCO Software Inc., 2017)

Results

Goslings in the reduced quality score group included those with a reduced quality of the navel area (99.1%), accompanied by the presence of a partially unabsorbed yolk sac (95.4%). These abnormalities reduced the maximum score for these traits (maximum 20 points) by 2 to 8 points and were therefore considered moderate. It is likely that goslings with a score below 12 points for both these traits were classified as third-grade goslings, i.e., not capable of survival (Table 2). Among the remaining quality traits, goslings were designated nonvaluable mainly due to poor down quality (25%) and the presence of eggshell or membrane remnants adhered to the down region (15%). Considerably fewer goslings exhibited reduced activity (5%) or leg deformities (1.6%); no eye defects were recorded (Table 2).

The growth analysis revealed that the von Bertalanffy model provided the most appropriate description of goose growth. This finding was supported by very high R² values (0.999 for females, males, and both sexes combined in both experimental groups; Table 4, Figure 3). The Gompertz model showed comparable R² values, except for females in the full quality score group (R² = 0.998). However, a comparison of actual and estimated BW values revealed that predictions based on the von Bertalanffy model were markedly closer to the measured data than those obtained using the Gompertz model (Table 5, Figure 3). Discussion

Concurrent incubation of a large number of eggs can inevitably cause quality defects in chicks. These defects may vary in frequency and severity and can occur due to multiple factors, including genetic abnormalities and nongenetic congenital variability. In both cases, the defects are frequently lethal (Okumura et al., 2013; Jomaa et al., 2020). Other factors include errors in incubation and hatching technology or issues related to egg storage duration and variability in egg quality, such as egg weight and shell thickness (Reijrink et al., 2010; Dang et al., 2023). The simultaneous incubation of multiple eggs of varying weight and shell quality under identical microclimatic conditions causes differences in water loss, which possibly impairs albumen and yolk sac resorption and hinders with the hatching process (Liao et al., 2013).

The resulting quality defects may be severe, contributing to suffering of the birds and a low probability of their survival, or these abnormalities could be moderate to mild, reducing vitality and negatively affecting appearance without posing an immediate threat to life. In practice, hatcheries primarily aim to produce chicks of the highest possible quality, as only such chicks can be sold to large commercial producers (Decuypere and Bruggeman, 2007). The most common defects that disqualify chicks from the highest quality category include remnants of the unabsorbed yolk sac, protruding or abnormal navels, and dirty or disheveled appearance. Such chicks are generally considered to have a low likelihood of survival and may pose a risk to the remaining flock because of a higher susceptibility to infection. Thus, most goslings classified as second-grade quality by hatchery staff exhibited abnormalities in the navel area. Small remnants of the yolk sac can reduce the vitality of chicks and virtually eliminate their suitability for survival under commercial large-flock rearing conditions.

Fasenko and O’Dea (2008) reported that chicks with navel defects exhibit delayed growth, suggesting that this delay may be associated with subclinical yolk sac infections. Kawalilak et al. (2010) further showed that chicks with navel abnormalities had reduced villus height in the duodenum and ileum during the early growth phase. However, Noy and Sklan (1999), followed by van de Ven et al. (2012), showed that immediate access to feed and water after hatching prompted intestinal development and mitigated the negative effects of suboptimal navel quality. This may explain the compensatory growth observed in the present study in goslings from the reduced quality score, wherein over 90% of goslings exhibited reduced navel quality.

Goslings from the reduced quality score group had lower BW up to approximately 5 weeks of age. After this period, they showed a tendency toward higher weight gain compared to the full quality score goslings. This finding can be explained by better access to feed and water in small groups (10 goslings), which facilitated faster recovery and eliminated the underlying causes of the initial defects. All goslings originated from the same genetic background and the same breeder flock, indicating that they had a similar growth potential. The initial reduction in BW associated with lower quality might have been compensated by appropriate environmental conditions, including unrestricted access to feed, water, and adequately warm resting areas.

This compensatory growth has been rarely reported, as the few studies that addressed reduced quality chicks focused mostly on chickens, which have a considerably shorter production cycle, typically up to 42 days (van de Ven et al., 2012). Dang et al. (2023) reported that goslings with a score of 84.80 points on the Tona scale (Tona et al. (2003) exhibited slower growth compared to those with a score of 97.20 points, but only up to 14 days of age. Similarly, Lin et al. (2018) observed slower growth only up to 28 days of age, which is consistent with the results of the present study.

The relationship between navel quality and intestinal morphology observed by Kawalilak et al. (2010) may explain the lower proportion of intestines relative to BW in female geese, which persisted until 20 weeks of age. Female geese typically complete somatic growth earlier, which may have halted further compensation before complete equalization of the intestinal mass between the full quality score and reduced quality score groups. In contrast, male geese did not show a similar phenomenon. An important, albeit unexpected, finding of the present study was the significantly higher proportion of leg muscles in female geese from the reduced quality score group, with a similar tendency observed in male geese. These results have not been reported earlier, probably because the rearing performance of geese or ducks with reduced quality has not been evaluated up to slaughter age.

Chickens are slaughtered considerably earlier; moreover, leg muscles follow different development patterns in gallinaceous poultry and waterfowl. In general, muscle development occurs in two stages: differentiation of muscle fibers during the embryonic period and post-hatch muscle fiber growth (Chal and Pourquié, 2017). Thus, all muscle fibers are formed exclusively during embryogenesis, while post-hatch muscle growth depends on genetic potential and the extent to which this potential is favored by specific environmental conditions (Ismail and Joo, 2017). According to Weng et al. (2025), in geese between 5 and 28 days post-hatch, the proportion of slow-twitch fibers in leg muscles gradually decreases, whereas the proportion of fast-twitch fibers dynamically increases. Muscle growth dynamics are also strongly influenced by physical activity, as indicated by comparisons between more active slow-growing birds and less active fast-growing birds (Sales, 2014; Wu et al., 2018).

Goslings with a reduced quality score likely made greater efforts to access feed and water and to move within the rearing environment during the first days of their life compared to those with a full quality score. This increased physical activity may have elevated energy expenditure and initially slowed down their growth, while simultaneously providing greater stimulation of muscle fibers, accelerating hypertrophy, and increasing the proportion of fast-twitch fibers. This early phase may have been critical in shaping subsequent muscle growth dynamics after access to outdoor paddocks. However, this hypothesis requires validation in future studies incorporating behavioral observations with activity monitoring and concurrent morphological analysis of leg muscle tissues, for example, by using histological methods.

The willingness of small-scale farmers to rear goslings with a reduced quality score depends primarily on the expected production outcomes. The economics of gosling rearing are influenced by gosling price, survivability (including the timing of potential losses), FCR, and BW. Depending on the marketing strategy, carcass weight and the mass of key components in geese, particularly breast muscles with skin and leg muscles with skin, may also be important. Oat-fattened geese may also be desirable because of their high fat content, which is considered a beneficial component of the human diet (Friend et al., 1983). Goslings with a reduced quality score are typically sold at a lower price than those with a full quality score; however, a higher mortality rate is expected.

Lin et al. (2018) reported that weak goslings had a 4.65% higher mortality rate up to 28 days of age, with most losses occurring within the first 2 weeks of age. In the present study, the mortality rate of the reduced quality score group was only slightly higher than that of the full quality score group (by 1.25%) over the 20-week rearing period, with the highest mortality occurring during the first week of rearing and, in one case, on the first day. These findings are critical because the overall mortality rate was low; moreover, early losses lead to smaller economic losses. Mortality in goslings with a reduced quality scope largely depends on the accuracy of classification by hatchery staff; however, precise classification methods are currently lacking.

Geese producers may apply the 100-point Tona scale developed by Tona et al. (2003) or the five-point Pasgar© score (van de Ven et al., 2012), although both methods were designed for chick quality assessment in chickens. Waterfowl chicks substantially differ in morphology from chicken chicks; moreover, incubation and hatching present additional challenges in geese rearing. Consequently, the classification of goslings largely relies on the experience and accuracy of hatchery staff. Performing highly precise classification could lower the mortality rates of both first- and second-grade goslings.

A notable finding of this study is the absence of differences in BW at 20 weeks of age, carcass weight, FCR, and the proportions of breast and leg muscles with skin and subcutaneous fat between the full quality score and reduced quality score groups. To date, Dang et al. (2023) alone evaluated differences in FCR between high- and low-quality goslings only up to 14 days of age and found no significant differences in FCR. This is likely because chicks that are too weak to consume feed tend to deteriorate rapidly and die, whereas chicks that can initiate feeding despite quality defects consume feed at a similar intensity as high-quality chicks.

In the present study, the intake of concentrate feed and oats should be considered approximate, as geese had continuous access to pasture for most of the rearing period. In geese, the proportion of fat and skin could also be an interesting aspect. Both fat and skin are commonly rendered into goose lard, which, following oat fattening, is characterized by a high content of unsaturated fatty acids (approximately 60–65%) (Friend et al., 1983). Although fat with skin was classified as an inedible component in the present study, this classification is conventional. Notably, the level of fatness did not differ between the full quality score and reduced quality score geese, regardless of sex.

Conclusion

In summary, rearing reduced quality score geese in a semi-intensive system enables to achieve the same BW at 20 weeks of age as that of full quality score geese, despite the slower growth rate of the former group until 5 weeks of age. Mortality, FCR, and carcass composition do not differ between full quality score and reduced quality score geese. However, better leg muscle performance can be expected from female geese in the reduced quality score group. Therefore, dividing geese into three quality classes at the hatchery is a beneficial approach because it improves production economics by avoiding unnecessary culling. In addition to economic benefits, this practice also has key ethical implications for poultry production. A limitation of the use of reduced quality score geese is the insufficiently developed categorization scheme for individual quality classes, which currently depends largely on the experience of hatchery personnel.
