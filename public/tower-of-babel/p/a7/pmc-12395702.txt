Fusion model integrating multi-sequence MRI radiomics and habitat imaging for predicting pathological complete response in breast cancer treated with neoadjuvant therapy
Authors: Shaojie Xu, Yushi Ying, Qilan Hu, Xingyin Li, Yulin Li, Hao Xiong, Yanyan Chen, Qing Ye, Xingrui Li, Yue Liu, Tao Ai, Yaying Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to develop a predictive model integrating multi-sequence MRI radiomics, deep learning features, and habitat imaging to forecast pathological complete response (pCR) in breast cancer patients undergoing neoadjuvant therapy (NAT).MethodsA retrospective analysis included 203 breast cancer patients treated with NAT from May 2018 to January 2023. Patients were divided into training (n = 162) and test (n = 41) sets. Radiomics features were extracted from intratumoral and peritumoral regions in multi-sequence MRI (T2WI, DWI, and DCE-MRI) datasets. Habitat imaging was employed to analyze tumor subregions, characterizing heterogeneity within the tumor. We constructed and validated machine learning models, including a fusion model integrating all features, using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) and Precision-Recall (PR) curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and confusion matrices. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) analyses were performed for model interpretability.ResultsThe fusion model achieved superior predictive performance compared to single-region models, with AUCs of 0.913 (95% CI: 0.770–1.000) in the test set. PR curve analysis showed improved precision-recall balance, while DCA indicated higher clinical benefit. Confusion matrix analysis confirmed the model’s classification accuracy. SHAP revealed DCE_LLL_DependenceUniformity as the most critical feature for predicting pCR and PC72 for non-pCR. LIME provided patient-specific insights into feature contributions.ConclusionIntegrating multi-dimensional MRI features with habitat imaging enhances pCR prediction in breast cancer. The fusion model offers a robust, non-invasive tool for guiding individualized treatment strategies while providing transparent interpretability through SHAP and LIME analyses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40644-025-00929-2.Keywords: Breast cancer, Neoadjuvant therapy, Magnetic resonance, Radiomics, Heterogeneity

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) management now includes surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy [1]. Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) has become the standard of care for locally advanced BC, aiming to downstage tumors, expand surgical options, and reduce the risks of recurrence and metastasis to improve long-term outcomes. Consequently, primary surgery is no longer considered the optimal treatment option [2–4]. However, BC exhibits high intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH), resulting in vastly different responses to NAT. Although most patients can benefit from NAT, a small subset exhibits a poor response to NAT and must be treated with modified chemotherapy regimens in subsequent treatment. This variability presents a significant clinical challenge and may compromise the long-term survival rate [5, 6]. The effective stratification of patients for optimal treatment decisions remains elusive yet essential in clinical practice. Early assessment and prediction of NAT response are essential for guiding subsequent treatment strategies. The gold standard for assessing pathological complete response (pCR) is through pathological examination of post-NAT surgical specimens, which is inherently limited by time delays and the inability for dynamic monitoring. Non-invasive, real-time, and accurate preoperative assessment of pCR remains a challenge. The current in-practice evaluation of NAT response is routinely guided by the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 [7]. However, due to BC’s complex heterogeneity, reliance solely on the tumor’s maximum diameter as a measure of treatment effect can be misleading and subject to variability among observers [8]. This approach may also overlook critical microscopic information within the tumor.

Breast MRI is recommended for detecting tumor response to NAT. Medical imaging-based machine learning models show great potential in accurately predicting pCR before surgery. Radiomics features extracted from multiparametric MRI can detect tumor response to NAT in BC [9–11]. Previous studies have predominantly focused on intratumoral features to assess the NAT response in BC [12–15]. However, these approaches often neglect the tumor’s complex internal architecture and surrounding microenvironment, including changes in vascular density induced by NAT. These changes lead to variations in heterogeneity among different patients during the treatment of NAT. In this context, a simplistic reliance on intratumoral features for characterizing the heterogeneous nature of BC is inadequate. Therefore, increasing effort has been dedicated to characterizing the tumor heterogeneity with the peritumoral region and habitat imaging as methods [16–18]. Unlike traditional radiomics approaches that treat the tumor as a monolithic entity, habitat imaging divides the tumor into subregions, often referred to as ‘habitats’. These subregions, characterized by parameter variations such as blood flow, cell density, and necrosis, have distinct prognostic implications [17, 19]. Furthermore, habitat imaging provides novel insights into how tumor heterogeneity manifests at the imaging level and has demonstrated potential for enhancing the assessment of NAT efficacy [20].

This retrospective study aims to develop a radiomics model based on multiparametric MRI images, incorporating tumor heterogeneity parameters, to improve pCR prediction in BC patients undergoing NAT. We hypothesize that the fusion of these imaging biomarkers will enhance predictive accuracy over conventional radiomics approaches. Additionally, the study will conduct an interpretability analysis of the integrated model’s predictive logic. Materials and methods

Study population

Patients with newly diagnosed BC at our institution from May 2018 to January 2023 were initially included in this study. The study was approved by the Huazhong University of Science and Technology Tongji Medical College Affiliated Tongji Hospital Medical Ethics Committee (Approval No. TJ-IRB20231173). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with primary lesion(s) confirmed as breast cancer via core needle biopsy prior to NAT; (2) Patients who underwent standard NAT before surgery and received a breast MRI approximately one week prior to the initiation of NAT; (3) Patients with complete clinical, pathological, and imaging data. The exclusion criteria were: (1) Patients with a history of other malignant tumors (s); (2) Patients with incomplete or non-standardized NAT treatment or surgery; (3) Patients with poor image quality, indistinct lesion presentation, or difficulty in ROI delineation. Finally, a total of 203 patients were enrolled in the study for further analysis. Subsequently, patients were allocated to the training and test sets in a ratio of 8:2 using computer-generated randomization. Specifically, 162 patients were assigned to the training set, while 41 were assigned to the test set. In addition, patients’ clinical data was extracted and gathered from the electronic medical records of the hospital information system (HIS).

Breast MRI acquisition

All patients underwent full-protocol breast MRI examinations using a 3.0 Tesla MRI scanner (Skyra, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a specialized 16-channel breast coil. Patients were scanned in a prone position, with both breasts naturally positioned within the coil. The coverage extended from the axilla to the lower margin of the breasts. The standard breast MRI acquisition technique includes fat-saturated T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and T1-weighted fat-suppressed dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI). For DCE-MRI, a dual-barrel high-pressure injector was employed for intravenous administration of the contrast agent (Omniscan, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI) with a dosage of 0.1 mmol/kg at a rate of 2.5 mL/s, followed by a 20 mL saline flush. Specific imaging parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Pathological evaluation

The Miller–Payne (MP) grading system was utilized for the evaluation of pathological responses to NAT [21]. The Miller–Payne scoring encompasses five distinct grades: Score 1 indicates either no reduction or only minimal reduction in tumor size; Score 2 signifies a reduction of up to 30%; Score 3 represents a reduction spanning from 30 to 90%; Score 4 signifies a reduction exceeding 90%, accompanied by some remaining invasive (or axillary) disease; Score 5 designates the absence of any residual invasive carcinoma (or axillary) metastasis. MP Grades 1 through 4 are grouped under the category of pathological partial response (pPR), while MP Grade 5 is categorized as pCR.

Tumor segmentation

All T2WI, DWI, and DCE-DWI images were transported into the ITK-SNAP software (version 3.8.0. Available at http://www.itksnap.org) to segment the tumor lesions manually. The whole lesion of each tumor was segmented by a radiologist with more than five years of experience in breast MRI interpretation while avoiding the regions of tumor necrosis, cysts, and calcifications. Subsequently, Python programming language (version 3.12.0. Available at http://www.python.org) was employed to expand the lesion ROIs outward by 3 mm, effectively embracing the peritumoral regions. Meanwhile, another radiologist with over ten years of experience in breast MRI interpretation randomly selected 50 patients for independent ROI delineation. These delineations were utilized to assess feature consistency and inter-observer variability analysis.

Feature engineering

To mitigate biases arising from variations in image field strengths and scanning protocols, preprocessing steps were applied to all images, including image normalization, N4 bias field correction, and resampling to an isotropic voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm³. T2WI, DWI, and DCE-MRI sequences were registered to create pre-clustered images. For each voxel within the tumor ROI, its intensity value in the pre-clustered image was used as the spatial coordinate, with equal weight assigned to each sequence. Clustering was performed using the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm (k = 3), determined by the elbow method, resulting in three distinct tumor habitat regions.

Five metrics were extracted to characterize the clustered regions: Inertia, Calinski-Harabasz index (CH index), Silhouette coefficient, Separation, and Davies-Bouldin index (DB index), referred to as heterogeneity features. These metrics assess intra-cluster compactness, cluster quality, sample similarity, inter-cluster separation, and cluster similarity, respectively.

Radiomics features were extracted from the images using the open-source Pyradiomics module in Python, yielding 1197 features from T2WI, DWI, and DCE-MRI, including shape-based attributes, first-order statistics, texture descriptors, and transformation features. These features underwent mean-variance normalization, and interclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated for reproducibility, excluding features with ICC < 0.90. For deep learning features, the largest cross-sectional ROI slice from each sequence was manually cropped and resampled to 400 × 400 pixels. The images were input into the ResNet50 model for training, extracting 2048 features per sequence from the final fully connected layer.

Dimensionality reduction techniques were applied according to feature type. Radiomics features were selected using the Mann-Whitney U test (p < 0.05) and correlation analysis (coefficients > 0.9). For deep learning features, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with a threshold of 0.9 for explained variance was used. After initial selection, the radiomics and deep learning features were combined into a composite feature matrix. Feature importance was ranked using six tree-based models, and the top 180 features from each model were intersected. Finally, recursive feature elimination (RFE) was applied, and the results were further refined through an additional intersection step.

Model construction and validation

The samples were divided into training and test sets using a random number table method. The model was built using the training set and evaluated using the test set. We constructed the tumor-adjacent model (TA-Model) using the optimal feature subset derived from the tumor and surrounding regions. Additionally, heterogeneity features were utilized to build the heterogeneity model (H-Model). We also developed a feature fusion model (F-Model) by combining the features from the individual models. For each type of model, we applied a variety of powerful classification algorithms, including Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Logistic Regression (LR).

The models were assessed based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) along with its 95% confidence interval (CI). Furthermore, key performance metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were computed. To enhance the interpretability and clinical transparency of the model, we applied SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) after model development. SHAP, rooted in game theory, provides global feature importance by quantifying each feature’s contribution to model predictions, allowing identification of key predictors. LIME, in contrast, builds local surrogate models to approximate the original model’s behavior for individual cases, offering insight into patient-specific prediction logic. Together, these techniques facilitate understanding of the model’s decision process and bridge the gap between algorithmic output and clinical interpretation. The workflow of this study is shown in Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

The baseline data of the patients were analyzed using SPSS (version 26.0) and Python statistical packages. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. The Levene test was used to evaluate the homogeneity of variances. Inter-group differences were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. The Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was applied to compare categorical variables. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated using the bootstrap method with 1,000 iterations. The DeLong test was performed to compare the AUCs of different models.

Feature selection

After the radiomics feature extraction, 1197 features were obtained from the T2WI, DWI, and DCE-MRI sequences. Following ICC consistency evaluation, 3214 features were retained (representing 89.5%). Mann-Whitney U test and correlation analysis were used for feature selection, resulting in 107 radiomics features. Deep learning features underwent PCA for dimensionality reduction, retaining 123 features. Finally, the dimension-reduced radiomics and deep learning features were integrated into a composite feature matrix, which was then used for feature selection through RFE.The intersection of the top 180 ranked features from each of the six tree-based models resulted in a total of 64 shared features (Fig. 2a). Figure 2b shows the application of RFE to fused features, aiming to select the optimal feature subset and reduce redundancy.The results indicate that most models stabilize in performance with around 10 features, with additional features providing limited improvement, highlighting 10 features as the key point for feature selection. However, these 10 features varied across different tree models, suggesting different feature preferences among the models. To address this, we conducted an intersection analysis of the selected features from the six tree models and identified four common features (Fig. 2c): DCE_LLL_DependenceUniformity, DWI_Log_Skewness, DWI_LHH_Skewness, and PC72. These features were defined as the final optimal subset, referred to as Tumor-Adjacent features.

Discussion

NAT combined with surgery has become an effective strategy for treating locally advanced BC. Early prediction of NAT efficacy is of significant clinical importance for guiding treatment decisions and improving prognosis. However, no widely accepted non-invasive method for early prediction of pCR to NAT in BC exists. We developed an F-model based on multi-sequence MRI and multidimensional features (traditional radiomics and heterogeneous features) using machine learning algorithms. Our findings indicate that incorporating radiomics and heterogeneous features improves the model’s predictive accuracy, providing a novel approach for the non-invasive early prediction of NAT efficacy in BC.

Histopathological examination of surgical specimens has traditionally been the gold standard for evaluating NAT efficacy in BC. However, it is limited by its retrospective nature, as it cannot provide real-time monitoring or promptly reflect changes in tumor response. Currently, the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 guidelines are commonly used, categorizing responses into four stages—complete response, partial response, stable disease, and progression—based on changes in tumor diameter [22]. However, this criterion is problematic as it overlooks the inherent growth characteristics and pre-treatment components of tumors. Consequently, relying solely on RECIST 1.1 criteria may not reflect the ability of anticancer therapy to induce some degree of tumor regression or growth retardation. Additionally, due to differences in tumor biology, the rate of tumor progression may vary not only between different types of malignancies but also among patients diagnosed with the same cancer [23, 24]. Empirical studies have further highlighted the limitations of relying solely on changes in the tumor’s maximum diameter as a predictive metric of NAC efficacy. For example, research has indicated that the correlation between the residual tumor diameter shown on late-stage DCE-MRI in BC NAC and pathological findings is only 0.72, and the specificity of diagnosing pCR for non-enhanced lesions is only 0.54 [25, 26]. Moreover, some scholars have used MRI to assess the size of residual tumors in locally advanced BC patients after NAC and explored whether tumor morphology on MRI affects the accuracy of MRI measurements of residual tumor size. The results indicated an MRI-pathology correlation coefficient of 0.64, with 28% of patients showing inconsistencies between MRI measurements and pathologically determined size. While MRI is an accurate method for measuring post-NAC residual tumor extent, it may overestimate residual lesions, especially in cases with a nest-like or margin tumor pattern, and in cases with ductal carcinoma in situ or multifocal dispersed nodules after NAC [27]. Collectively, these findings underscore the limitations of using maximum tumor diameter as the sole metric for NAC efficacy assessment. Changes in intratumoral heterogeneity are also key factors affecting efficacy, and traditional imaging examinations cannot provide quantitative assessment of tumor heterogeneity. Therefore, a more comprehensive approach, beyond changes in tumor diameter, is warranted for a more accurate and clinically relevant assessment of NAT efficacy in BC.

BC’s high heterogeneity and complexity lead to varying responses to NAT, with tumor heterogeneity evolving during treatment. Traditional imaging assessments based solely on maximum tumor diameter are insufficient to capture these dynamic changes. Recent studies suggest that internal homogeneity and perfusion index (PI%) from contrast-enhanced ultrasound may serve as early biomarkers for predicting pathological response to NAT in BC [28]. Sun et al. have demonstrated that Diffusion Kurtosis MR Imaging (DKI) could be a noninvasive approach for evaluating the effects of NAC in BC [29]. Other multidimensional approaches have been proposed, encompassing methods like 18 F FDG PET-CT, ultrasound elastography combined with serum ATF5, and the expression of LncRNA MIR205HG to refine NAC efficacy assessment [30–32]. To identify more effective biomarkers, some researchers have integrated artificial intelligence (AI) into assessing NAC efficacy in BC. Studies exploring radiomics and deep learning applications have shown promising results. For example, Li et al. combined FDG PET/CT radiomics with clinical features to predict pCR in NAC, achieving AUC values of 0.958 and 0.730 in the training and test sets, respectively, outperforming models based solely on clinical features [33]. Chen et al. analyzed MRI of 158 BC patients and developed radiomics features to predict pCR in NAC. Their model, combining pretreatment MRI features and hormone status, demonstrated high predictive value for NAC pCR [12]. ITH is a complex phenomenon arising from diverse cellular subpopulations within a tumor, each possessing distinct characteristics that ultimately lead to varying phenotypic and functional characteristics during disease progression [34]. This heterogeneity has been widely reported to contribute to treatment resistance and adverse clinical outcomes [35]. In the field of radiomics, conventional methods for quantifying intratumor heterogeneity include first-order feature histograms and second-order image features such as texture analysis [36]. These have shown value in assessing tumor heterogeneity [37, 38]. However, it should be noted that texture analysis methods presuppose homogeneity within the entire tumor and assume that the tumor is heterogeneous but well-mixed. This approach overlooks phenotypic variations in different regions within the tumor, potentially providing an incomplete quantification of intratumor heterogeneity [19]. To address this limitation, we utilized the “Habitat Imaging” technique, a more nuanced approach that identifies subregions—or “habitats”—within the tumor to better characterize its internal heterogeneity [19]. To represent tumor heterogeneity comprehensively, we delineated regions of interest in three dimensions, considering the entire tumor before subregion segmentation. This approach preserved spatial integrity and allowed for the exploration of relationships between subregions in relation to the outcome variable. Using K-means clustering, we identified five heterogeneous features to build a machine learning model. These features effectively capture tumor subregion specifics, including intragroup similarities, and provide a detailed description of tumor heterogeneity. Our study highlights the importance of intratumor heterogeneity subregions in predicting treatment outcomes and prognosis, aligning with prior research [39–41].

Explainability analysis in radiomics helps to understand how models make predictions based on imaging features, enhancing transparency and trustworthiness. It reveals the relationship between imaging features and disease biology, while also aiding in model optimization and improving clinical decision support [42–44]. Overall, DWI_LHH_Skewness was the most critical feature for predicting pCR. This feature, derived from DWI imaging, reflects the asymmetry of pixel intensity distribution within the tumor region, highlighting tumor heterogeneity and structural differences. However, clinically, DCE-MRI is considered a reliable tool for monitoring residual tumors [45], which was also reflected in our individual analyses. Although the performance of the heterogeneity model alone was suboptimal, the fusion model demonstrated significantly improved efficacy. This indicates that heterogeneity features primarily serve a supportive role, enhancing the predictive performance when integrated with radiomics features.

While the findings of this study are promising, several limitations warrant attention. Firstly, the fusion radiomics model developed herein is predicated on retrospective data, which, although informative, may not wholly represent the complex landscape of BC heterogeneity. The external validation of this model using prospective data from randomized clinical trials would substantively augment its clinical validity.

Secondly, the study was confined to a single institution and encompassed a relatively limited sample size, thereby necessitating further validation through larger, multi-center studies to ensure its generalization. Thirdly, we exclusively utilized MRI data to develop the model. The incorporation of additional types of medical imaging, such as pathology whole-slide images or ultrasound scans, could further refine the model’s predictive accuracy.

In conclusion, we leveraged multi-sequence and multidimensional radiomics features from pre-NAT MRI, and combined various machine learning algorithms to construct a fusion model for accurate prediction of pCR in BC. This model holds clinical value for BC patients, assisting surgeons in deciding whether to proceed with breast-conserving surgery and sentinel lymph node biopsy after NAT treatment. Additionally, we aim to explore the clinical utility of tailored treatments in broader regions and populations.
