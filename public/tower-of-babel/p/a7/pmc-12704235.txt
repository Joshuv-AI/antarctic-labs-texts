Immiscible Phase Separation‐Driven Microfabrication of Gelatin Methacryloyl Scaffolds for BMP‐2 Delivery and Osteogenic Enhancement
Authors: Basel A. Khader, Stephen D. Waldman, Dae Kun Hwang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) hydrogels are recognized for their biocompatibility, tunable mechanics, and ability to support cellular functions, making them attractive for tissue engineering. However, achieving uniform, structurally stable micro‐scaffolds for minimally invasive delivery remains challenging. Injectable hydrogels provide targeted delivery but lack the micro‐architectural complexity required for effective regeneration, while 3D printing offers precision yet faces resolution, handling, and mechanical limitations. To overcome these barriers, we developed injectable GelMA micro‐scaffolds (mS‐GelMA) with controlled porosity, stability, and reproducibility using stop‐flow lithography (SFL). This technique enables precise control over shape, porosity, and degradation, surpassing conventional injection moulding and 3D bioprinting in micro‐particle uniformity and reproducibility. Scaffold performance was optimized by incorporating trimethylolpropane triacrylate (TMPTA) into GelMA, enhancing drug delivery and regenerative potential. Cellular assays confirmed high biocompatibility and functionality, with human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) exhibiting excellent viability, migration, and osteogenic differentiation within the mS‐GelMA scaffolds. These findings demonstrate that SFL‐fabricated GelMA scaffolds bridge the gap between injectability and structural complexity, offering a promising platform for minimally invasive tissue engineering. This work highlights the potential of SFL‐engineered hydrogels to advance scaffold‐based regenerative medicine by combining architectural precision, biological performance, and clinical applicability.

Introduction

Synthetic hydrogels represent a highly versatile class of biomaterials extensively utilized in tissue regeneration strategies. Their appeal stems from tunable physicochemical properties that allow for mimicking the native extracellular matrix (ECM). Among the materials employed, Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) is particularly advantageous for constructing scaffolds for ECM because of its intrinsic biocompatibility, adjustable mechanical properties, and inherent bioactivity. However, a fundamental challenge in designing effective GelMA scaffolds is optimizing the balance between structural integrity for mechanical stability and a microporous network, which can impede cell migration and limit nutrient diffusion, thus affecting cellular accessibility [1, 2, 3].

One remedy to optimize this balance is to use GelMA microparticles to construct a microparticle‐based scaffold, particularly in the context of injectable scaffold systems. While bulk injectable GelMA hydrogels facilitate minimally invasive delivery, they lack predefined structural complexity, which can hinder efficient long‐term tissue integration [4, 5, 6]. In contrast, microparticle systems can self‐assemble in situ to form microporous structures that improve cell infiltration, facilitate nutrient and waste transport, and support tissue remodelling. Their inherent shear‐thinning behavior enables minimally invasive delivery, a significant advantage for applications such as bone regeneration, cartilage repair, and soft tissue engineering where precise scaffold placement and adaptability are paramount [7, 8, 9, 10]. Furthermore, non‐spherical microparticle geometries (e.g., crescent‐shaped, anisotropic) when compared to spherical ones offer better control for systematically adjusting void volume fraction and potentially improve cellular infiltration without sacrificing mechanical integrity or injectability [11].

Despite these benefits from GelMA micro‐architectures composed of non‐spherical GelMA microparticles, fabrication of such GelMA microparticles remains challenging, particularly when embedding a tunable porous network with pore sizes exceeding several micrometers within non‐spherical geometries to ensure uniform cell distribution and adequate nutrient diffusion [4, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. While additive manufacturing techniques such as 3D bioprinting offer precise spatial control over complex scaffold geometry, they are often constrained by material processability limitations, including low production rates, nozzle clogging issues, potential for weak interlayer adhesion, and compromised biomechanical performance. Conversely, this inherent trade‐off underscores the necessity for hybrid scaffold systems that synergistically combine the structural fidelity achievable through methods like 3D bioprinting with the practical advantages of injectable hydrogels. The efficacy of such scaffolds critically depends on achieving optimal rheological properties, controlled gelation kinetics, and uniform cell encapsulation. Although 3D bioprinting enables geometric precision, limitations related to achievable resolution, cost, and processing complexity restrict its widespread clinical translation. Hybrid scaffolds aim to circumvent these issues by integrating injectability with structural precision, focusing on optimizing viscosity, crosslinking dynamics, and overall scaffold durability [19, 20, 21].

To overcome these challenges in achieving precise control over microparticle geometry, uniformity, and porosity, stop‐flow lithography (SFL) presents a powerful microfabrication technique for generating structurally well‐defined GelMA‐based micro‐particles [12, 22, 23]. By precisely integrating controlled microfluidic transport with rapid photopolymerization, SFL facilitates the high‐throughput production of monodisperse microparticles possessing tunable geometries, controlled porosity, and tailored mechanical properties. Relative to methods such as traditional 3D bioprinting or molding techniques, SFL offers superior resolution, particle uniformity, and process reproducibility [12, 22, 23, 24]. These attributes position SFL as a highly suitable technology for developing advanced scaffolds where precise architectural control is leveraged to direct cell behavior and effectively support tissue regeneration processes.

SFL, where rapid and controllable UV‐induced phase separation and polymerization occur simultaneously, is especially advantageous for GelMA‐TMPTA systems. Thus, SFL enables real‐time photopatterning of phase‐separated structures with micrometre‐scale resolution, which is difficult to achieve using conventional methods. In our study, the term ‘GelMA microparticles’ refers to these photopatterned units, which, upon TMPTA incorporation, become porous micro‐scaffold GelMA (mS‐GelMA) particles with well‐defined shapes and internal architecture. Notably, SFL allows precise control over particle size and geometry, with typical feature sizes ranging from the low µm to several hundred micrometres depending on flow rate, UV exposure time, and photomask design. To address these existing limitations in scaffold fabrication and validate a new scaffold‐particle design strategy, we employed stop‐flow lithography within a microfluidic platform to fabricate highly porous GelMA‐based micro‐scaffold (mS‐GelMA) particles incorporating immiscible materials, specifically trimethylolpropane triacrylate (TMPTA). By adjusting TMPTA concentration, we precisely modulated scaffold porosity, pore size, swelling behavior, and degradation kinetics. Increased TMPTA levels correlated with enhanced porosity and accelerated degradation, beneficial for drug delivery and protein release applications. Confocal microscopy and live‐dead assays demonstrated high viability and active migration of human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) within the scaffolds. Osteogenic assays further confirmed that BMP‐2‐loaded mS‐GelMA significantly enhanced osteogenesis compared to control conditions, underscoring its potential for advanced tissue engineering applications.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis of Gelatin Methacrylate (GelMA) Hydrogel

GelMA hydrogel was synthesized following a modified protocol from our previous work [12]. Briefly, 10 g of bovine skin gelatin (Type B, 225 bloom; Sigma–Aldrich) was dissolved in 75 mL of Dulbecco's Phosphate‐Buffered Saline (DPBS, pH 7.4) at 60 C. Methacrylic anhydride (MA, Sigma–Aldrich) was added dropwise in 7 mL aliquots over 90 min, with vigorous stirring. The reaction was maintained at 60 C for 3 h, then diluted threefold with pre‐warmed DPBS. To remove unreacted MA and byproducts, the solution was dialyzed against ultrapure water (MWCO 12–14 kDa) for 5–6 days at 60 C, with water changes performed twice daily. The purified GelMA was lyophilized for storage. The degree of acrylation was estimated to be approximately 70%, as determined by the ninhydrin assay following the manufacturer's instructions (Sigma–Aldrich, Ninhydrin Assay Kit).

Hydrogel Preparation

Immiscible materials were employed to ensure uniform TMPTA incorporation to fabricate micro scaffold GelMA hydrogels. GelMA was dissolved in alpha‐MEM culture media (supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin‐streptomycin, and 2 mm L‐glutamine; Gibco) at 60 C to achieve a 10% (w/v) concentration. Trimethylolpropane triacrylate (TMPTA, Sigma–Aldrich) was added to the GelMA solution at volume ratios of 0, 10, 50, and 100 µL per mL of GelMA solution. Lithium phenyl‐2,4,6‐trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP, Sigma–Aldrich) was included as a photoinitiator at a final concentration of 0.05% (w/v).

Fabrication of Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) Microfluidic Devices

Microfluidic devices were fabricated using a refined soft lithography technique [12, 22]. A SU‐8 2050 photoresist master mould with a thickness of ∼70 µm was prepared on a silicon wafer via spin‐coating, UV exposure through a custom AutoCAD‐designed photomask, and profilometric verification. To enable Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) replication, the mold was silanized with trichloro (1,1,2,2‐perfluorooctyl) silane. PDMS microfluidic channels were fabricated by casting a 10:1 mixture of Sylgard 184 elastomer base and curing agent onto the mold, followed by curing at 65 C for 1 h. The cured PDMS was bonded to a partially cured PDMS‐coated glass substrate, and final device curing was completed with an additional 1‐h bake at 65 C.

GelMA Micro‐Scaffold Particles Fabrication

A GelMA/TMPTA prepolymer solution was introduced into the microfluidic channel. The platform was designed to ensure laminar flow and spatial confinement of the prepolymer stream, enabling precise control over particle geometry and reproducibility. The immiscibility between the hydrophilic GelMA and hydrophobic TMPTA induced phase separation during UV exposure, leading to the formation of internal micropores. By tuning the TMPTA concentration, the extent of phase separation, and thus pore size and porosity, could be systematically controlled. The photopolymerization of the scaffold particles was induced by a precisely timed 2 s UV pulse (365 nm) at 45% intensity, delivered through a 10x objective lens and a custom photomask (1150 × 200 µm). The vertical thickness of the particles was defined by the height of the channel and lubrication layers, where no polymerization occurs due to oxygen inhibition, form at the top and bottom of the channel, with each layer approximately ∼ 2.5 µm thick. Since the microfluidic channel which was approximately 70 µm, the particle thickness is approximately 65 µm. A Type‐H LED collimator (Mightex) controlled the UV light intensity and exposure duration. The UV light was directed through an Axio Observer inverted microscope (Carl Zeiss) to ensure targeted sample irradiation. The polymerized scaffold particles were collected from the channel outlet and subsequently purified by serial dilutions in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) to remove unreacted monomers. The TMPTA phase did not contain a photoinitiator and therefore did not undergo significant polymerization during UV exposure. The generated TMPTA droplets remained unpolymerized and were subsequently removed via serial PBS washing steps. Due to their immiscibility and confinement within the scaffold, these droplets gradually diffused out without forming covalent bonds to the GelMA network. Scaffold thickness was governed by the microfluidic channel height and was independent of the TMPTA droplet distribution.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

The microstructure and surface morphology of mS‐GelMA particles were characterized using a JEOL JSM‐6380LV scanning electron microscope (SEM). Prior to imaging, particles were washed three times with Milli‐Q water, snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen for 20 s, and freeze‐dried. To enhance conductivity and image quality, samples were sputter‐coated with a 5 nm gold layer. Imaging was conducted in secondary electron (SE) mode at 20 kV to visualize surface topography and in backscattered electron (BSE) mode at the same voltage to assess scaffold porosity.

Swelling Behavior and Surface Expansion Analysis of mS‐GelMA Particle Hydrogels

The swelling behavior of the mS‐GelMA particle hydrogel was evaluated using the surface expansion (fluid uptake) method. Hydrogels were fabricated as described earlier in this paper. After collection from the microfluidic channel, the samples were placed on a glass slide and dried in an oven at 65 C for 24 h. A solvent (PBS, pH 7.4) was added using a syringe with a 14G needle. The samples were then observed under a Zeiss microscope with a 10x objective, and surface expansion was recorded every second to monitor the swelling process. The swelling ratio was calculated from the recorded data. This method quantifies swelling through changes in projected surface area, rather than mass or volume, due to the microscale size and low individual mass of the mS‐GelMA particles, which make conventional gravimetric or volumetric measurements impractical. Surface area analysis via imaging allowed for reproducible and high‐throughput comparison of swelling behavior across TMPTA concentrations.

Degradability

Hydrogel particles (mS‐GelMA, mean surface area: 1196 µm2) were collected from the outlet of the microfluidic device and individually seeded into wells of a 96‐well, flat‐bottom microplate. Following the removal of the PBS collection medium, the hydrogels were rinsed with fresh PBS to eliminate residual debris. To initiate degradation, each well was supplemented with a PBS solution containing 2 U/mL Collagenase Type II. Degradation kinetics were monitored by capturing images at predetermined time intervals. Degradation was qualitatively assessed based on surface area reduction, as accurate mass measurements were not practical at this scale.

Culture and Treatment of Human Mesenchymal Stem Cells

Human bone marrow‐derived mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) were sourced from RoosterBio Inc. as RoosterVial‐hBM‐1M (Part Number MSC‐003, Lot Number 310305). These cells were obtained from a 20‐year‐old female donor and cryopreserved in a liquid nitrogen vapour phase. The hMSCs were verified to be sterile, mycoplasma‐free, and capable of differentiating into adipogenic and osteogenic lineages, as per the manufacturer's quality assurance standards. The osteogenic differentiation of hMSCs was evaluated through histochemical alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining across four experimental groups: (1) Control group, where hMSCs were cultured without mS‐GelMA particles or BMP‐2; (2) mS‐GelMA group, where hMSCs were cultured with mS‐GelMA particles but without BMP‐2; (3) BMP‐2 group, where hMSCs were cultured in osteogenic media supplemented with 200 ng/mL BMP‐2; and (4) mS‐GelMA + BMP‐2 group, where BMP‐2 (200 ng/mL) was pre‐loaded into mS‐GelMA particles before polymerization, and the particles were introduced into the culture. Cells were initially expanded and maintained in standard growth media (α‐MEM supplemented with 16% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% antimycotic solution (Gibco, 15,240,062), and 1% L‐glutamine). Cells at passage 4 were seeded into 12‐well plates at a density of 6.5 × 10⁴ cells per well. After two days of culture, the media in each well were replaced, and mS‐GelMA particles and BMP‐2 were introduced according to the respective group conditions. For ARS staining, the culture wells were first washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) to remove any residual FBS. The cells were then transitioned to serum‐free osteogenic media supplemented with 25 µg/mL ascorbic acid‐2‐phosphate (AA2P), 5 mM beta‐glycerophosphate (BGP), 1% insulin‐transferrin‐selenium (ITS), 1% antimycotic solution, and 1% L‐glutamine. The media was refreshed every two days after the initial two days of culture. At this point, mS‐GelMA particles and BMP‐2 were introduced into the wells according to the respective group conditions.

Alkaline Phosphatase and Alizarin Red Staining

ALP staining was performed on day four post‐treatment to assess early osteogenic differentiation using a Leukocyte Alkaline Phosphatase Kit (86‐C, SIGMA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. To evaluate mineralization, ARS staining was conducted approximately 14 days post‐treatment. Wells were gently washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h, and then rinsed with deionized water. Subsequently, 1% alizarin red S solution (deionized water, A5533 SIGMA, pH 4.2) was added for 20 min; the excess stain was aspirated, and wells were rinsed twice with deionized water. Stained plates were air‐dried for 24 h before image analysis. Image analysis was performed using Image J software according to the guidelines provided by the National Institute of Health [25]. Briefly, images were converted to 8‐bit grayscale, and integrated pixel values were measured within a region of interest (ROI) covering the entire culture area using the same detection threshold for each multi‐well plate. Data was combined from 3 well plates and represented an average fold change compared to control cells without mS‐GelMA and BMP‐2 stimulation.

Cell Viability and Morphology Assessment

For the assessment of cell viability and morphology, cells were stained after 7 days of culture using Hoechst 33342, WGA‐Alexa Fluor 488, and Live/Dead stain. The culture medium was removed, and the wells were washed with PBS to eliminate any residual medium. Hoechst 33342 (1 µg/mL) was added to the wells for 20 min to stain the nuclei, following the manufacturer's protocol (ab228551). This was followed by the addition of WGA‐Alexa Fluor 488 (3 µg/mL) for 10 min to label the cell membranes at room temperature, following the manufacturer's protocol (Invitrogen, W11261). To assess cell viability, mS‐GelMA microparticles were carefully collected and transferred to a new plate for staining, while the original culture plate was stained separately to evaluate cell viability on the plate bottom and the surface of the mS‐GelMA. Live/Dead stain (2 µL/mL) was applied to both plates and incubated for 15 min at 37 C, following the manufacturer's protocol (ab115347). After incubation, fluorescent images of the stained cells were captured using a fluorescence microscope with appropriate filters to visualize Hoechst (blue), WGA‐Alexa Fluor 488 (green), and the Live/Dead stain. This procedure enabled the evaluation of cell morphology, membrane integrity, and viability.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical significance was determined using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post‐hoc test, analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10.2.3. Each experimental group included a minimum of n = 5 samples, unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Significance levels are indicated as follows: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, and **** p < 0.0001.

Result and Discussion

Microfabrication and Image Processing of mS‐GelMA Particles

This research introduces a novel approach to fabricating mS‐GelMA structures using the microfluidic SFL technique (Figure 1; Figure S1.1). The core principle of this fabrication leverages the immiscibility between the hydrophobic monomer, TMPTA, and the hydrophilic GelMA solution. When mixed, TMPTA formed discrete droplets within the GelMA matrix, which act as porogens. Upon photopolymerization, the GelMA solidified around these droplets, creating a stable, interconnected porous network (Figure 2). Since the photoinitiator (LAP) was only present in the GelMA phase, polymerization was limited to that phase, though limited interfacial crosslinking may have occurred via diffusing radicals. TMPTA was selected for its high immiscibility with GelMA, its compatibility with radical polymerization, and its ease of removal post‐curing, helping stabilize the pore boundaries and generate well‐defined porous features. The TMPTA droplets were subsequently removed through a three‐step washing process using PBS, yielding the final mS‐GelMA scaffold. Importantly, we can precisely control the porosity of the mS‐GelMA by varying the TMPTA concentration, added at volume ratios ranging from 0 to 100 µL per 1 mL of GelMA solution used in this study. This ability to manipulate porosity provides significant control over the scaffold's properties, making it adaptable for a wide range of biomedical applications. The resulting porous structure enhances surface area and mass transport capabilities, both critical for applications such as drug delivery and tissue engineering, where efficient diffusion and cellular interactions are essential.

To demonstrate the versatility of the technique, we fabricated various shapes of mS‐GelMA structures: rectangular, triangular, circular, and square with various porosities (Figure 1), showcasing its ability to generate scaffolds with a wide range of porosities. For the shape control, we simply exchange the photomask pattern design, ensuring consistent scaffold geometry for specific applications. Their axial dimension, determined by the light propagation direction, is defined by the channel thickness, approximately less than 5 µm, while the lateral dimension is defined by the photomask pattern dimension and objective magnification. By systematically increasing the volume of TMPTA added to the GelMA solution (from 0 to 100 µL), the resulting scaffolds exhibit a corresponding increase in porosity, as evidenced by the larger and more numerous pores observed (Figure 3; Figure S1.1).

Although various hydrogels, including PEGDA‐700, were initially tested and yielded similar porous structures (Figure 3), GelMA was ultimately selected for its advantageous properties, such as biocompatibility, degradability, and the presence of protein sequences that promote cellular interactions.

GelMA's biocompatibility, degradability, and natural protein sequences make it highly favorable for biomedical applications, as it supports cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [26, 27, 28]. This SFL approach, within a microfluidic environment, offers significant advantages over traditional 3D printing techniques [29], which often struggle with achieving uniform porosity and may require supportive structures. In contrast, the SFL method provides fine control over porosity and geometry, resulting in a scaffold with predictable and consistent drug and protein release profiles. Overall, this technique offers a cost‐effective, scalable, and customizable platform for creating biomaterial scaffolds tailored to specific biomedical applications.

Microstructural Characterization of mS‐GelMA Particles

The microstructures of mS‐GelMA particles fabricated with varying TMPTA concentrations were analyzed using SEM and quantified for porosity and pore size using ImageJ software [30]. The corresponding pore size distributions are provided in (Figure S1.2), illustrating how higher TMPTA concentrations lead to narrower, more uniform pore profiles. Due to the small size and soft hydrogel composition of the mS‐GelMA particles, standard porosity measurement methods such as liquid displacement and mercury intrusion porosimetry were not suitable. These techniques require bulk, rigid, or precisely weighed samples, which are incompatible with our microscale particles. As an alternative, SEM imaging combined with ImageJ‐based threshold analysis allowed for consistent, non‐destructive quantification of pore size and relative porosity across all TMPTA conditions. While this approach is limited to surface‐level analysis, it was sufficient for comparative evaluation in this study. At 0 µL TMPTA (control, Figure 4a), the scaffold exhibits minimal porosity, with small, irregular pores ranging from 4 to 10 µm and an overall porosity of approximately 15% (Figure 4f). Introducing 10 µL TMPTA (Figure 4b) increases the pore size to a range of 5–12 µm, with a few larger pores reaching 17 µm, resulting in a significant increase in porosity (∼40% compared to 0 µL TMPTA, Figure 4f). Further increasing the TMPTA concentration to 50 µL (Figure 4c) produces pores in the range of 10–13 µm, accompanied by a porosity of ∼75% (compared to 10 µL TMPTA, Figure 4f). At the highest TMPTA concentration (100 µL, Figure 4d), the scaffold achieves the largest and most uniform pore size, ranging from 10–15 µm, and the highest porosity (∼95% compared to 50 µL TMPTA, Figure 4f), demonstrating a highly interconnected and consistent porous network.

SEM images of mS‐GelMA micro‐particles fabricated with varying TMPTA volume ratios: (a) 0 µL, (b) 10 µL, (c) 50 µL, and (d) 100 µL, showing changes in pore size and structure. Scale bars: 50 µm. (e) Heatmap illustrating the average pore size (µm) at each TMPTA concentration. (f) Bar graph of porosity percentage for each TMPTA concentration, with statistical significance indicated (* p < 0.001, ** p < 0.0001). Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 5).

These findings illustrate that TMPTA concentration is critical in modulating pore size and porosity through phase separation. Higher TMPTA concentrations reduce crosslinking density, creating more uniform and interconnected voids within the GelMA matrix. This structural enhancement is highly advantageous for applications such as drug delivery, tissue regeneration, and protein release, where scaffold porosity directly influences cell migration, nutrient diffusion, and fluid uptake [12, 17, 18]. The significant differences in porosity across all TMPTA concentrations (p < 0.001 or higher) highlight the precision and versatility of the SFL technique in tailoring scaffold properties. The uniform porosity and tunable microstructure of the mS‐GelMA scaffolds present a distinct advantage over conventional 3D‐printed GelMA scaffolds, which often lack uniformity and tunability in pore architecture [31, 32]. The mS‐GelMA particles with 100 µL TMPTA concentration offer an optimal balance of porosity and uniformity, making them highly suitable for regenerative medicine applications (Figure 4e). While our analysis relied on SEM imaging and ImageJ quantification to assess porosity and pore size, we acknowledge that these methods primarily provide surface‐level and 2D information. Although micro‐CT imaging could more directly assess 3D pore interconnectivity, the hydrogel nature and small size of the mS‐GelMA particles presented limitations in resolution and contrast. In future work, we aim to explore contrast‐enhanced micro‐CT or alternative imaging approaches, such as multiphoton microscopy, to validate and further characterize the internal pore network. Given that the increased pore size of the mS‐GelMA particles with 100 µL TMPTA promotes enhanced cell migration, we proceeded with further experiments to investigate their potential for tissue regeneration applications.

Swelling Behavior and Surface Expansion

First, before cellular studies using our mS‐GelMAs, we evaluated their swelling behaviors by observing the expansion in surface area over time (Figure 5; Figure S1.3). The results demonstrate that the mS‐GelMA particles swell rapidly and uniformly, achieving complete swelling within approximately 12 s (Movies S1 and S2). From the initial state, the scaffold's surface area expands progressively upon immersion in an aqueous environment, with swelling observable as early as 1 s and continuing until the maximum expansion is reached at 12 s.

The highly porous structure of the mS‐GelMA, created through the immiscibility‐driven phase separation of TMPTA and GelMA during fabrication, significantly contributes to its efficient swelling behavior. Unlike 3D‐printed GelMA samples, which often exhibit limited or uneven porosity due to the layer‐by‐layer construction process, the mS‐GelMA scaffold features a uniformly interconnected pore network. This interconnected porosity allows rapid water infiltration and distribution, enabling the scaffold to achieve full swelling quickly and uniformly. Additionally, the enhanced porosity of mS‐GelMA maximizes its surface area during swelling (Movie S3), providing a greater interaction surface for surrounding fluids and promoting more efficient drug and protein release [33]. In comparison, 3D‐printed scaffolds generally face challenges in achieving the same level of rapid and uniform swelling because of potential pore blockages and variability in layer adhesion, which can limit fluid uptake and slow the swelling process [3, 34, 35]. The consistent and finely tuned porosity of mS‐GelMA, in contrast, supports rapid and controlled swelling, making it highly advantageous for biomedical applications that rely on rapid scaffold expansion, sustained therapeutic delivery, and optimized surface interactions with the surrounding tissue.

Enzymatic Degradation and Surface Transformation

We also evaluated the degradation behavior of the mS‐GelMA particles by examining the effects of increasing TMPTA concentration on degradation rates (Figure 6). Our results indicate that as TMPTA concentration increases, the degradation time of the scaffold decreases significantly. For the control sample with no added TMPTA, the scaffold fully degrades within 65 min. However, with the addition of TMPTA at increasing concentrations, we observe a marked acceleration in degradation, with the fastest degradation time recorded at approximately 15 min for the 100 µL TMPTA concentration.

This accelerated degradation is due to the reduced GelMA content and lower crosslinking density at higher TMPTA concentrations, which diminishes structural integrity and increases susceptibility to enzymatic and hydrolytic breakdown [36]. Compared to previous reports using bulk GelMA hydrogels [36, 37], the shorter degradation times observed here can be attributed to differences in particle size, increased porosity, and overall scaffold composition. The microscale format and TMPTA‐induced porosity significantly enhance enzyme access and fluid diffusion, resulting in faster degradation under identical enzymatic conditions. The ability to modulate degradation rates by adjusting TMPTA concentrations provides an advantage in designing scaffolds with specific lifespans tailored to the needs of therapeutic applications. For instance, higher TMPTA concentrations offer rapid degradation suited to short‐term treatments, while lower concentrations result in a more stable matrix with slower degradation.

Biological Performance of mS‐GelMA Particles

Osteogenic Activity, Mineralization Evaluation, and In Vitro Biocompatibility of mS‐GelMA Particles

We evaluated the osteogenic potential of mS‐GelMA particles fabricated with 100 µL TMPTA using hMSCs cultured under four conditions (Figure 7): (a) control without scaffolds or supplements, (b) mS‐GelMA alone, (c) BMP‐2 alone, and (d) a combination of mS‐GelMA and BMP‐2 (Figure 7 panels a–d). Early osteogenic differentiation, assessed via ALP staining (Figure 7e), and late‐stage mineralization, evaluated using ARS staining (Figure 7f), reveal significant differences among the groups. Quantitative analysis of ALP and ARS staining demonstrates that the combination of mS‐GelMA and BMP‐2 yields the highest osteogenic performance, significantly surpassing all other conditions (Figure 7g,h).

(a–d) Experimental groups: (a) Control, (b) mS‐GelMA, (c) BMP‐2, and (d) mS‐GelMA + BMP‐2. (e) ALP staining shows the highest early osteogenic activity in the BMP‐2‐loaded mS‐GelMA group compared to other groups. (f) ARS staining highlights significantly enhanced mineralization in the BMP‐2‐loaded mS‐GelMA group. (g,h) Quantification of ALP and ARS staining demonstrates significantly higher osteogenic activity and mineral deposition in the mS‐GelMA + BMP‐2 group (**** p < 0.0001). Statistical analysis was performed using one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test (n = 3 for each group in ALP and ARS).

ALP activity, an early marker of osteogenic differentiation, was significantly higher in the BMP‐2‐loaded mS‐GelMA group than in the BMP‐2‐alone group, underscoring the synergistic effects of the scaffold's structural and biochemical properties. Similarly, ARS staining revealed a substantial increase in calcium deposition in the mS‐GelMA + BMP‐2 group compared to all other groups, indicating its superior ability to support late‐stage osteogenesis. While BMP‐2 alone enhanced osteogenic differentiation relative to the control, the inclusion of the mS‐GelMA scaffold amplified these effects, likely because of its highly porous structure. This structure enhances growth factor retention, sustained release, and bioavailability while creating a bioactive microenvironment conducive to cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation [38, 39, 40]. The amino acid groups present in the GelMA backbone provide additional benefits to the scaffold's bioactivity [27, 41]. Specifically, the arginine‐glycine‐aspartic acid motifs mimic extracellular matrix components [41], facilitating integrin‐mediated cell attachment and promoting hMSC proliferation and differentiation [42]. These biochemical properties complement the scaffold's physical characteristics, such as high porosity and tunable degradation. The increased porosity in the mS‐GelMA particle, achieved through immiscibility‐driven phase separation at high TMPTA concentrations, allows for effective BMP‐2 loading and a gradual, controlled release. This prolonged bioavailability of BMP‐2 to hMSCs improves osteogenic differentiation and outperforms BMP‐2 administration alone by preventing rapid growth factor depletion [43, 44, 45].

Moreover, the high porosity facilitates fluid infiltration and nutrient exchange, creating an environment conducive to osteogenesis [46, 47]. The interconnected pore network provides a larger surface area and multiple diffusion pathways for sustained growth factor release [47, 48]. These structural attributes, combined with tunable degradation rates, enable the scaffold to provide mechanical support during early osteogenesis while degrading in sync with new tissue formation [47]. Overall, the synergistic effects observed in the BMP‐2‐loaded mS‐GelMA group highlight the scaffold's unique combination of bioactive amino acid groups, controlled degradation, and tunable porosity. These features collectively make the mS‐GelMA particles an ideal platform for delivering bioactive molecules, promoting cell‐mediated tissue formation, and achieving targeted therapeutic outcomes. Future studies should focus on in vivo evaluations of the scaffold's ability to support vascularization, integration with host tissues, and the optimization of drug release kinetics for broader applications in regenerative medicine.

Cell Viability and Morphology Analysis

To investigate the cellular interactions and migration of hMSCs within the mS‐GelMA particles, we performed confocal microscopy following fluorescent staining. hMSCs were cultured within the mS‐GelMA scaffold and analyzed after staining with Hoechst (nuclear counterstain) and WGA‐AlexaFluor488 (cell membrane stain) (Figures 8 and 9). The confocal images reveal clear evidence of cell migration into the scaffold's porous structure, with both nuclear and membrane staining providing detailed visualization of cell morphology and distribution (Figure 8, panels b–e).

The Hoechst nuclear counterstain highlighted the nuclei of the hMSCs, indicating successful adhesion and proliferation of cells within the scaffold [49]. The WGA‐AlexaFluor488 stain reveals the cell membrane [50], demonstrating that the cells not only adhere to the surface but also penetrate deep into the interconnected pore network of the mS‐GelMA (Figures 8 and 9). This cell migration is facilitated by the scaffold's uniform porosity, a key feature enabled by immiscibility‐driven phase separation during its fabrication. The interconnected pores provide an ideal pathway for cellular infiltration, enhancing the scaffold's capacity to support 3D cell organization and tissue formation. The presence of cells within the scaffold interior highlights the biocompatibility and suitability of the mS‐GelMA for promoting cellular interactions and migration, essential for applications in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. The ability of the scaffold to support both surface adhesion and internal migration is particularly advantageous for applications requiring effective cell delivery and integration, such as bone regeneration or delivering therapeutic cells to targeted areas.

These findings suggest that mS‐GelMA offers improved functionality compared to traditional 3D‐printed GelMA particles, which often exhibit limited pore interconnectivity, restricting cell infiltration [51]. The live‐dead assay results demonstrate high cell viability of hMSCs cultured within the mS‐GelMA particles after 7 days. The majority of the cells within the scaffold are stained green, indicating they are alive and metabolically active, with only a minimal presence of dead cells (stained red) (Figure 9m–r). Importantly, the assay also revealed significant cell migration into the scaffold's porous structure, highlighting the ability of mS‐GelMA to support not only cell survival but also active infiltration. This migration is attributed to the scaffold's uniform and interconnected porosity, which facilitates cell movement and ensures efficient nutrient and oxygen diffusion throughout its structure. The results confirm the biocompatibility of the mS‐GelMA, further enhanced by the presence of amino acid groups in the GelMA backbone, which promote integrin‐mediated attachment and support cellular functions [41]. Overall, the live‐dead assay results validate mS‐GelMA as an effective platform for maintaining cell viability and encouraging migration (Figure S4).

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrates the fabrication of highly tunable mS‐GelMA particles using SFL and immiscible materials (TMPTA), allowing precise control over pore size, porosity, and structural uniformity—critical factors for tissue engineering applications. The incorporation of TMPTA facilitates the modulation of porosity, creating a highly interconnected pore network that promotes cell migration, enhances fluid infiltration, and optimizes the controlled release of bioactive molecules. The BMP‐2‐loaded mS‐GelMA particles significantly enhance osteogenic differentiation, as evidenced by elevated ALP activity and increased mineral deposition, highlighting the synergistic effect of scaffold architecture and biochemical properties. Additionally, the tunable degradation profile of the mS‐GelMA scaffold aligns with tissue formation, ensuring sustained growth factor release over time. The ability to precisely tailor scaffold properties through immiscible phase separation and microfluidic processing sets this system apart from traditional methods. This work establishes a foundation for future advancements in scaffold design, with applications extending beyond bone tissue engineering to include localized drug delivery and other regenerative therapies. These advancements offer significant promise for improving therapeutic outcomes in a variety of medical applications.
