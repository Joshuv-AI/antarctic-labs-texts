Randomized controlled trial on ankle biomechanics in the treatment of functional ankle instability with joint mobilization
Authors: YiKun Yin, Qihan Lin, Jialin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctional ankle instability (FAI) patients often experience restricted ankle dorsiflexion, increased inversion angle, and elevated ground reaction forces during walking, all related to altered kinematics of the talocrural and subtalar joints. This study aimed to investigate the potential positive impact of joint mobilization on FAI patients from a biomechanical perspective. The experimental group (EG, n = 17; Age: 20.06 ± 1.34 years; Height: 1.74 ± 0.07 m; Weight: 69.79 ± 11.20 kg; BMI:22.88 ± 2.63 kg/m2; CAIT:15.59 ± 2.58; M/F: 15/2) received joint mobilization + routine rehabilitation training, while the control group (CG, n = 16; Age: 20.50 ± 0.73 years; Height: 1.73 ± 0.09 m; Weight: 64.59 ± 7.21 kg; BMI: 21.65 ± 2.47 kg/m2; CAIT: 16.75 ± 2.21; M/F: 14/2) only received regular rehabilitation training. Biomechanical tests were performed in both groups after the 4-week intervention. The spatial parameters during walking (including step length, stride length, step width, step time, cadence, step speed, support time, and swing time), ankle flexion and dorsiflexion angle, inversion, and eversion angles, internal and external rotation angles, ankle torque, as well as the vertical ground reaction force were measured before and after the intervention. The results of the two-way ANOVA showed that the main effect of time was significant for step length (P < 0.001), stride length (P = 0.008), step speed (P < 0.001), the sagittal plane angle at touchdown (P < 0.001), maximum dorsiflexion angle (P = 0.005), sagittal plane toe off-ground angle (P < 0.001), peak flexion-dorsiflexion torque (P = 0.033), the first peak vGRF (P = 0.013), and second peak vGRF (P = 0.011). The main effect of Time * Group was significant for step speed (P = 0.044). The EG demonstrated significant improvements in step speed (P = 0.047), maximum dorsiflexion angle (P = 0.047), and the first peak vGRF (P = 0.028) compared to the CG. This study reveals that joint mobilization intervention enhances gait spatiotemporal parameters, kinematics, and kinetics, particularly in step speed, maximum dorsiflexion angle, and the first vGRF peak for the EG compared to the CG. Therefore, the rehabilitation strategy for patients with functional ankle instability should appropriately increase the use of joint movement to promote the functional recovery of FAI patients.Keywords: Joint mobilization, Functional ankle instability, Biomechanical, WalkingSubject terms: Therapeutics, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

An ankle sprain is one of the most common sports injuries, with up to 50% of acute ankle sprain patients not seeking formal medical management, leading to recurrent sprains in the months and years following the initial injury1–3. Even with later systematic rehabilitation, up to 70% of patients experienced various functional impairments within 6 months, developing into functional ankle instability (FAI)4,5. FAI severely restricted patients’ physical function6 and may even lead to post-traumatic ankle osteoarthritis7. Furthermore, repeated ankle sprains in FAI patients could cause irreversible damage to ankle stability8. FAI patients typically exhibited decreased ankle muscle strength, laxity of ligaments, limited joint mobility, decreased proprioception, and neuromuscular control9,10. The new study found that FAI patients might experience three-dimensional shape changes in their ankle joints11. These factors were the main risk factors for recurrent ankle injury. Restricted ankle mobility was associated with changes in tibiotalar and subtalar joint kinematics12, including reduced posterior glide of the talus or changes in its position relative to the ankle socket. During movement, FAI patients demonstrated increased ankle inversion angle, inversion angular velocity13,14, and smaller eversion torque, as well as impaired control mechanisms in the tibiotalar, subtalar, and tarsal joints15,16. In addition, compared to healthy individuals, FAI patients exhibited higher peak vertical ground reaction force (vGRF) during walking, and the time to reach peak vGRF was shortened17. This inability to adjust appropriate ankle joint positioning during landing promptly led to a stiff movement pattern and increased risk of injury17,18. These characteristic changes had been confirmed as risk factors for recurrent ankle sprains in FAI patients.

Joint mobilization techniques have been widely acknowledged for enhancing and restoring both physiological and accessory movements of the joints19. These techniques involved passive activities that stretched the surrounding joint capsule and ligaments, released soft tissue adhesions, adjusted joint positioning, and restored ligament elasticity20. Maitland’s technique, developed by Australian physiotherapist Geoffrey Douglas Maitland, was a crucial diagnostic and therapeutic approach for managing musculoskeletal dysfunctions. It encompassed examination, assessment, and treatment procedures such as rolling, rotating, sliding, and distraction traction, primarily aimed at improving and restoring joint physiological and accessory movements19,21. Previous studies on musculoskeletal disorders consistently highlighted joint mobilization’s positive therapeutic effects, including improved joint mobility, activation of neural pathways, increased proprioception and stability, and alleviation of patient symptoms22,23.

Current research on the biomechanics of the ankle joint in patients with FAI primarily focuses on two aspects: comparative studies of landing, running, jumping, and side-cutting movements between FAI patients and healthy individuals3,14,24 and the effects of interventions such as semi-rigid or elastic ankle braces and kinesiotape on FAI patients25–27. While studies on joint mobilization in FAI patients have reported improvements in ankle dorsiflexion range of motion, proprioception, balance, and pain, there is a lack of research on the biomechanical effects of joint mobilization during walking post-intervention. Consequently, the impact of joint mobilization on ankle kinematics and kinetics during gait in FAI patients remains unclear. This study aims to assess the effects of joint mobilization techniques on patients with FAI. Specifically, it investigates the improvements in ankle kinematics and kinetics following joint mobilization interventions in FAI patients, providing valuable insights for clinical practice. The hypothesis posits that joint mobilization will positively affect the gait cycle and the kinematics and kinetics of the ankle joint in patients with FAI.

Materials and methods

Research design

This study is a single-blind, randomized controlled trial designed according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) recommendations28. The protocol of this study complied with the Helsinki Declaration and was approved by the Ethics Committees of Guangxi Normal University (No: 20230806001). This study was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration number: ChiCTR2300079263 28/12/2023). It was conducted between January 2024 and February 2024.

In this study, participants suffering from FAI were recruited by distributing questionnaires and posters at Guangxi Normal University. A total of 40 individuals with FAI were initially enrolled, including 3 with bilateral ankle instability and 1 who had previously undergone ankle surgery. Following the exclusion of these cases, a final selection of 36 participants (30 males and 4 females) was included in the study. Before participation, all individuals were provided written informed consent.

Characteristics of participants

One participant in the experimental group withdrew from the study due to a muscle strain, and two participants in the control group withdrew without completing the entire intervention. As a result, the final participant count was 17 in the experimental group and 16 in the control group. No statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the two groups (P > 0.05). Interventions

This study used computer software Stata (version 12.0, www.stata.com, StataCorp, USA) to generate a simple random allocation sequence. Participants were randomly assigned to the experimental group (EG) or the control group (CG) in a 1:1 ratio. Before the implementation of the intervention, independent researchers (not involved in the intervention process) prepared sealed opaque envelopes containing the intervention measures, maintaining a 1:1 ratio (Two interventions are involved in the envelope, namely: (1) Routine rehabilitation training combined with joint mobilization; (2) Routine rehabilitation training. Participants were instructed to randomly select an envelope, which would determine the intervention they would receive. Furthermore, participants were instructed not to discuss the intervention details with the researchers. The intervention consisted of a 4-week program, with 3 sessions conducted per week, totaling 12 sessions. The control group underwent routine rehabilitation training, while the experimental group received Maitland joint mobilization therapy and routine rehabilitation training. The Maitland joint mobilization technique is categorized into four levels. Grades I and II aim to alleviate pain, while grades III and IV focus on releasing tissue adhesion around the joint, promoting joint mobility, and enhancing the range of motion. Rehabilitation therapists administered all treatments with a minimum of three years of experience.

Balance Stability Training

Balance Stability Training

Balance stability training consists of stable surface and unstable surface training. Stable surface training occurred on flat ground, while unstable surface training involved using a balance pad. The primary objective of this training was to enhance the balance capabilities of the affected lower limb. The training exercises encompassed single-leg standing with eyes open, in which participants had to maintain an upright position with both upper limbs abducted at a 90-degree angle and the unaffected limb elevated to the level of the affected knee joint. The inner side of the unaffected ankle should have aligned with the unaffected knee joint. Participants were required to maintain a forward gaze, ensure body stability, and sustain this position for one minute. Similarly, single-leg standing with eyes closed followed the same procedure as single-leg standing with eyes open. Once participants achieved stability with their eyes open, they were instructed to close their eyes, maintain body stability, and perform single-leg standing for one minute. Each exercise was repeated three times, with a 10-s rest between repetitions29.

(2)Rehabilitation Muscle Strength Training

Rehabilitation Muscle Strength Training

Muscle strength training for rehabilitation comprised resistance training for plantar flexion, dorsiflexion, inversion, and eversion. This training was conducted standing, utilizing elastic bands at the ankle joint. Resistance training for inversion and eversion was executed in the plantar flexion position. Similarly, resistance training for inversion and eversion was performed in the dorsiflexion position. Each muscle strength training exercise in every direction entailed achieving the maximum range of joint motion without inducing pain. Each direction was repeated 8 times to ensure effectiveness, with 3 training sets and a 3-min rest period between sets30.

In this study, Maitland joint mobilization was executed using the Grade III manipulation technique, 30s/group, with 1-min rest between groups, in 3 groups.

Ankle biomechanical characteristics

The testing equipment comprised the infrared light spot high-speed motion capture testing system (MCU500, Qualisys, Sweden) and the three-dimensional force platform from AMTI (Length × width × height: 1800 × 400 × 82.55 mm, AMTI, USA, Model: 2 m). The testing was conducted at the Laboratory of Movement Performance Research, Guangxi Normal University. A sampling frequency of 200 Hz was used for data collection. To facilitate tracking, fluorescent markers with a diameter of 14 mm were placed according to the CAST scheme. These markers were positioned on various anatomical landmarks, including the left and right anterior superior iliac spine, posterior superior iliac spine, medial and lateral epicondyles of the femur, medial and lateral malleolus, calcaneus and Achilles tendon insertion point, as well as the dorsal edge of the first, second, and fifth metatarsal heads. For secure fixation, cluster markers were utilized on the lateral side of both thighs and shanks. In total, 36 marker points were employed during the testing process32,33. See Fig. 2.

Schematic diagram of markers. Thirty-six light grey markers are used as capture points to define human motion in the model. Light blue markers are primarily used to define the ground coordinate system or to construct the joints of the human model during data processing and analysis.

To ensure accurate gait testing, certain requirements were implemented. Firstly, all subjects were instructed to walk barefoot, as this eliminated variations in ground reaction force buffering caused by the cushioning properties of different shoes. Before the testing commenced, subjects underwent a 5-min warm-up period to prepare their muscles, followed by familiarization with the designated testing pathway. Subsequently, subjects were instructed to walk back and forth along a 10 m pathway, maintaining a comfortable pace. During this process, it was emphasized that only one foot should be entirely placed on the force platform while walking. Three sets of gait data were collected for each subject to ensure data validity. If any marker became dislodged during data collection, dynamic and static data collection were repeated to ensure accuracy.

Experimental parameters:

Gait spatiotemporal parameters: step length, stride length, step width, step time, cadence, step speed, support time, and swing time.

Kinematic parameters and kinetic parameters: During the foot support phase of walking, the ankle flexion and dorsiflexion angle, internal and external rollover angles, internal and external rotation angles, ankle torque, as well as the vertical ground reaction force (vGRF). All parameters were measured and exported using Visual 3D software. All kinetic parameters were normalized relative to body weight to eliminate the influence of body weight on the results. Angle at touchdown: The angle when the heel of the tested foot touches the ground34; Toe off-ground angle: The angle when the toes of the tested foot leave the ground35.

Data acquisition and statistical analysis

Kinematic and Kinetic data were analyzed using inverse dynamics via the gait analysis software Visual 3D (v5, C-Motion, Inc., Germantown, MD, USA). Kinetics data was filtered with a cut-off frequency of 50 Hz17,36. Kinematic data was filtered with a cut-off frequency of 8 Hz via a fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter37. Further processing was done with MATLAB software (version R2016a, MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA).

Statistical analysis was performed using the statistical software SPSS (version 22; SPSS, Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). The data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). The normality of all results was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Independent t-tests were used to compare the baseline between the two groups. To control the overall Type I error, results were analyzed using two-way (time and intervention) ANOVA. If a significant interaction was detected, post hoc Bonferroni tests were conducted. Partial eta squared (ηp2) was used to represent the effect sizes of the main effects and interactions in the two-way ANOVA. The threshold p-values for ηp2 were: small effect 0.01–0.06, medium effect 0.06–0.14, and large effect greater than 0.1438. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.0538.

Results

Gait spatiotemporal parameters

There were no statistically significant differences in gait spatiotemporal parameters between the two groups before the intervention (P > 0.05). Compared to pre-intervention measurements, the differences in step length (F = 16.002, P < 0.001), stride length (F = 5.727, P = 0.023), and step speed (F = 18.778, P < 0.001) in the EG were statistically significant. Post hoc tests showed that the step speed of the EG increased to 1.39 ± 0.08 (m/s), which was significantly higher than that of the CG (F = 4.292, P = 0.047). The results of the two-way ANOVA indicated that the main effect of time was significant for step length (F = 17.062, P < 0.001, ηp2 =0.355), stride length (F = 8.131, P = 0.008, ηp2 = 0.208), and step speed (F = 15.501, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.333); the main effect of Time * Group was significant for step speed (F = 4.440, P = 0.044, ηp2 = 0.124). Joint kinematic

There were no significant differences in kinematics between the two groups before intervention (P > 0.05). Compared to pre-intervention measurements, significant differences were observed in the sagittal plane angle at touchdown (F = 8.675, P = 0.006), maximum dorsiflexion angle (F = 9.449, P = 0.004), and sagittal plane toe off-ground angle (F = 8.790, P = 0.006) in the EG were statistically significant; and in the sagittal plane angle at touchdown (F = 7.870, P = 0.009) and sagittal plane toe off-ground angle (F = 6.697, P = 0.015) in the CG were statistically significant. Post hoc tests indicated that the maximum dorsiflexion angle in the EG increased to 14.32 ± 3.24°, which was significantly higher than that in the CG (F = 4.276, P = 0.047). The results of the two-way ANOVA showed that the main effect of time was significant for the sagittal plane angle at touchdown (F = 16.519, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.348), maximum dorsiflexion angle (F = 8.914, P = 0.005, ηp2 = 0.223), and sagittal plane toe off-ground angle (F = 15.381, P < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.332). Discussion

This study aimed to explore the potential effects of joint mobilization interventions on gait cycle, ankle kinematics, and kinetics among patients with FAI. Following four weeks of joint mobilization intervention, the EG demonstrated remarkable enhancements in Step speed, maximum dorsiflexion angle in the sagittal plane, and first vGRF peak, compared to the CG. While no statistical disparities were observed between the EG and CG in other measures, the improvements observed in the EG were significantly superior to those seen in the other two groups.

Research demonstrated that gait alterations in individuals with FAI may have contributed to an increased risk of re-injury39. Previous studies revealed that FAI patients exhibited significantly reduced step length, swing time, and prolonged support time, compared to healthy individuals. These findings suggested a potential lower degree of walking automatization among FAI patients40. Discrepancies in spatiotemporal gait parameters between FAI patients and healthy individuals could be attributed to variations in motor neuron quantity, type, and discharge frequency, ultimately leading to muscle weakness, decreased walking speed, and stride length during the ambulation process41. Remarkably, this study found that after 4 weeks of joint mobilization intervention, the improvement in gait spatiotemporal parameters in FAI subjects was superior to conventional rehabilitation training. The intervention stimulates accessible nerve endings and mechanoreceptors around the ankle joint, leading to changes in the motor potentials of the surrounding muscles and increasing the excitability of the motor units in the spinal dorsal horn42,43. This process enhances the afferent neural signals, thereby improving the automation of walking in FAI subjects.

Limited ankle range of motion was a primary hallmark of FAI. Prior investigations revealed that gait analysis comparing FAI patients to healthy individuals demonstrated a conspicuous inversion position of the ankle joint during heel landing. In addition, ankle dorsiflexion range of motion disorder was frequently observed in patients with FAI during ambulation44,45. The analysis of FAI patients in the standing stage after gait initiation found that the degree of inversion foot increased. Due to this increase in inversion, the activity of the peroneal longus muscle was prolonged and intensified after the initial contact of the foot during walking46. Due to the anterior displacement of the talus relative to the ankle mortise in FAI patients, the talus cannot effectively glide posteriorly during movement, thereby increasing the risk of ankle instability47. In addition, the subtalar joint, as a "mitered hinge," was also one of the crucial joints of the lower limbs, and the limitation of the subtalar joint was also a potential factor affecting ankle kinematics. The movement of the subtalar joint usually occurs in three planes of the foot simultaneously, called three-plane joint movement. During the movement, ankle dorsiflexion and abduction were caused by the interconnection of subtalar joint pronation and supination with calf rotation48,49. The ankle would be sprained if the interconnection was broken. Maximum dorsiflexion of the ankle joint caused the talus to rotate outward, which limited the talocrural joint’s ability to slide medially. Maximum dorsiflexion would reduce bone flexibility, meaning a higher risk of ankle inversion sprain49. Research demonstrated that joint mobilization positively impacted the dorsiflexion range of motion in the early stages of FAI50. Furthermore, it effectively enhanced ankle joint mobility in treating subacute/chronic lateral ankle sprain. A randomized crossover study conducted by Matthew C. revealed a significant increase of 0.42 cm in the ankle dorsiflexion range of motion (DFROM) following talocrural joint mobilization using the Maitland III technique21. This study found that after 4 weeks of joint mobilization intervention, the improvement in ankle joint mobility in FAI patients was superior to traditional rehabilitation training, especially in the maximum dorsiflexion angle during walking. Joint mobilization facilitates the posterior glide of the talus by loosening the talocrural joint, adjusting the position of the talus in the ankle mortise, and restoring ankle dorsiflexion mobility51.

In a comparative analysis of gait kinematics between individuals with FAI and healthy controls, Piming et al. discovered notable alterations in ankle joint torque during walking. Specifically, insufficient torque was observed in ankle plantar flexion and abduction, potentially indicating deficiencies in relevant muscle function52. However, Moisan et al. presented research results indicating the absence of significant differences in ankle joint torque between individuals with FAI and healthy controls, irrespective of walking speed53. Torque, a comprehensive measure of muscle activation, was determined by the vGRF and the lever arm. By increasing torque, muscles’ activation time and intensity could be effectively enhanced, thus potentially protecting the ankle joint54. In this study, the improvement in torque among patients with FAI following a 4-week joint mobilization intervention was more pronounced compared to the CG. However, upon comparison, no statistically significant difference was observed. This lack of significance may be attributed to the relatively short intervention duration. Previous studies have indicated that patients with FAI exhibited limited ankle dorsiflexion activity and reduced muscle strength around the ankle during walking, leading to an inability to adapt to the impact forces generated by the lower limbs on the ground55. Consequently, this increased vertical ground reaction force, braking ground reaction force, and propulsive ground reaction force. The inability to effectively cushion these ground reaction forces led to a higher vertical ground reaction force in the ankle, which may adversely affect the metabolism and health of the ankle cartilage56 and elevate the risk of re-spraining55. The present study demonstrated that the improvement in vGRF was more pronounced in the EG compared to the CG. Hall et al. found significant improvement in ankle dorsiflexion, inversion, and eversion muscle strength after 6 weeks of strength training in FAI patients57. According to the findings of Yin et al., the incorporation of joint loosening with strength training in patients with FAI yielded superior improvement in perianal muscle strength compared to strength training alone. These results suggested a synergistic effect between joint mobilization and conventional training, resulting in a more effective enhancement of perianal muscle strength9.

This study had several limitations that needed to be addressed. Firstly, there were instances of subject dropouts during the research process, and the subjects were not classified by gender, potentially influencing the accuracy of our results to some extent. Secondly, the absence of a long-term follow-up period posed challenges in accurately determining the duration of the observed effects. Moreover, the study solely focused on a biomechanical perspective, thus failing to evaluate muscle activation clearly before and after intervention. Lastly, the absence of a detailed breakdown of the walking cycle restricted our ability to explore potential differences across different phases. To enhance the rigor and diversity of experimental data, future investigations should consider combining surface electromyography (sEMG) with a more comprehensive breakdown of the gait cycle, and group discussion of subject gender should be conducted in future studies.

Conclusion

This study aimed to investigate the potential impact of joint mobilization on foot and ankle kinematics and kinetics in individuals with FAI. The findings of this study revealed notable improvements and enhancements in the gait cycle, kinematics, and kinetics following joint mobilization, specifically in variables such as step speed, maximum dorsiflexion angle, and vGRF peak when compared to the control group. To elucidate the role of joint mobilization in FAI, future experimental designs should encompass midterm and long-term follow-up investigations, and an increased number of participants to ensure more robust testing methodologies.
