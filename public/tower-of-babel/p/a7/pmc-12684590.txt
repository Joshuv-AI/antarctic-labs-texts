Use of a Generative Pretrained Transformer to Answer Questions and Facilitate a Large Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Devin L Brown, Kayla Novitski, Joelle B Sickler, Rebecca M Hankla, Faria Khattak, Emily Philpot, Valerie Durkalski‐Mauldin, Sima Sayyahmelli, Christine Patino, Flora Arevalo‐Bermudez, Mitzi Roberts, Allegra Sahelian, Ronald D Chervin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenerative artificial intelligence may help facilitate clinical trials. We sought to determine whether a customized generative pretrained transformer (GPT) could assist clinical trial sites within a randomized, controlled trial with rapid responses to protocol and procedure‐related questions.MethodsWithin a large clinical trial, Sleep SMART (Sleep for Stroke Management and Recovery Trial), we developed, tested, and implemented a customized GPT designed to answer, in real‐time, procedure‐related questions. This support was offered to all active trial sites and questions and responses were monitored by the central study team. An anonymous survey also queried primary study coordinators about their experiences with the GPT.ResultsOf the 785 questions entered during a 10‐month implementation period, 75% were able to be answered per the GPT. On manual review, of the 588 that the GPT reportedly answered, only 13 (2%) were not helpful responses, 12 (2%) were incomplete responses, 5 (1%) were misleading, and 3 (1%) contained the correct response but also provided some conflicting messaging. Of 95 primary study coordinators to whom the survey was sent, 45 (47%) responded. Of those, 21 (47%) reported having used the GPT. Of the 19 who provided more detailed information, 89% found it to be very helpful (n=11) or helpful (n=6). Most (79%) found the responses to be accurate (n=15) or partially accurate (n=2), and 89% were very satisfied (n=10) or satisfied (n=7) with the GPT.ConclusionsThis novel use of a customized GPT suggests it could be valuable in support of clinical sites within a large trial. Further research should confirm equivalent accuracy and safety through a direct comparison to human support.RegistrationURL: https://clinicaltrials.gov/; Unique Identifier: NCT03812653.Keywords: clinical trials, generative artificial intelligence, natural language processingSubject Categories: Ischemic Stroke

Clinical trials are often complex, and include large volumes of instructive text, including protocols, manuals of procedures, and data collection guidelines. Study teams may struggle to locate relevant instructions, require quick answers, and turn to trial leadership for support.

Growing evidence suggests that large language models implemented through customized generative pretrained transformers (GPTs) can be used to make clinical trials more efficient. Large language models have been used to assist in clinical trial design 1 and screening for eligible patients. 2 , 3 , 4 They might play a role in safety monitoring. 5 We sought to determine whether a customized GPT could assist clinical sites within a randomized, controlled trial through rapid responses to protocol and procedure‐related questions.

METHODS

Data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The methods for the randomized, controlled trial titled Sleep SMART (Sleep for Stroke Management and Recovery Trial) have been previously published. 6 Briefly, the trial enrolls patients who have had an ischemic stroke, during their acute or rehabilitation hospitalization and implements a 2‐night inpatient protocol followed by 6 months of randomized treatment. Participants are tested for obstructive sleep apnea and if eligible are randomized to automatically adjusting continuous positive airway pressure plus usual care versus usual care alone. The multicenter trial is implemented through the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke‐funded clinical trial network, StrokeNet. Procedures include use of a sleep apnea test, transfer of test data via a proprietary online system, selection of continuous positive airway pressure masks, a continuous positive airway pressure run‐in night, randomization procedures, and ultimately outcome assessments at 3 and 6 months. Data collection is through an online data collection system managed by the National Data Management Center at the Medical University of South Carolina (WebDCU), and through a proprietary online system managed by a sleep medicine technology company (Nox Health).

Trial enrollment began in May 2019 and is ongoing. Support for trial sites has included near‐real‐time responses (8 a.m. to 9 p.m. ET, or later, daily) from the central team to an email “hotline” for questions related to eligibility criteria, responses from Nox Health to technical questions via phone and email (7 a.m. to 7 p.m. ET daily), a 24/7 randomization hotline, and responses from the central team to emails sent to them individually. Most systems were staffed from morning to night eastern time but were not staffed overnight. Trial sites spanned pacific to eastern time zones.

In the winter of 2023/spring of 2024, we explored options to create a customized GPT designed to answer protocol and other procedural questions for clinical trial sites. Our goal was to identify an option without additional costs incurred for individual sites/users, and where we would have visibility of actual questions asked (anonymously) and responses provided so we could monitor GPT performance and update the GPT for any issues identified. We tested a couple of reasonably priced options including Microsoft Copilot, Botsonic, and a University of Michigan‐based system. However, in each case the GPT performance was suboptimal, with incorrect responses or inability to answer most questions, or had logistical barriers such as exclusion of external users. We ultimately selected a commercial product with a monthly fee (and small token use‐related costs), DocsBot, after formative training and testing.

We provided information to the GPT related to the protocol, data collection guidelines, and some study‐related procedures. This included uploading documents (Table 11) and study website links, and additional source material in the form of a Q&A section, which prompted the study team to add anticipated questions (free text) and appropriate responses (free text). Document text was modified to capture information otherwise presented as figures and tables, as these formats were unable to be processed by the specific GPT at that time (this was since updated). Redundancies across documents were also reduced as overlapping material appeared to result in inaccurate or incomplete responses. When documents required revisions, updates were manually provided. Reindexing of websites was performed manually. The GPT provided responses in real time (within moments) to questions asked, identified the source(s) used to provide the response and provided a link to documents requested from the study website (eg, the study brochure). The GPT considers the question answerable when the response can be derived from the available training content. When the GPT was unable to respond based on the source materials, it indicated that the context did not provide information about that specific topic, but often supplemented its responses with related generic information. Users could then either rephrase the question or abandon the line of questioning with the GPT. Iterative updates to the documents, and additions to the Q&A were made based on testing by the central team. On April 5, 2024, testing by Nox Health and the National Data Management Center staff began. On April 29, 2024, primary study coordinators at 13 clinical trial sites were asked to perform beta testing. Updates were made in response to each of these phases of testing. In total, 463 questions were asked across all phases of testing. The study‐wide rollout to the trial sites occurred on May 13, 2024. Users were instructed to confirm responses with study documents because the GPT could make mistakes. They were also instructed to avoid entering protected health information (PHI), and “No PHI please!” was posted on the GPT website. We provided information to sites about the GPT through webinars, emails, newsletters, and other communications. We also posted the GPT link on our trial website.

The original GPT used GPT‐3.5. When GPT‐4o became available, because the study GPT was live, we duplicated the GPT to test the 4o capabilities. No new issues were identified by the account holder (a study principal investigator) and the 4o function seemed improved with respect to response clarity and ability to answer more questions compared with the 3.5 response. On May 15, 2024, we switched our study GPT from 3.5 to 4o. All questions, except for those entered by the account holder, were submitted anonymously. Responses have been monitored continually since the roll out, and small updates have been made to improve the ability of the GPT to respond to a wider array of questions.

Questions and responses from May 13, 2024 to March 13, 2025 were downloaded in batches prospectively, and concatenated. Questions entered by the account holder were excluded as they were for the purpose of GPT revisions. The time stamp for each question was converted from coordinated universal time to eastern time accounting for daylight savings time. The GPT classified its responses on the backend as “could answer” yes/no. Responses to questions it “could answer” were also reviewed by one of the study principal investigators (D.L.B.) to evaluate the usefulness of the response, and categorize post hoc as incomplete (statements were correct but missing relevant information), misleading (responses were inaccurate but inconsequential, or technically correct but could have led to the wrong conclusion), conflicting (contained correct and contradicting information), or unhelpful (accurate information that was not responsive to the question). In February/March of 2025 we also administered an online, anonymous survey (Data S1) to study staff of trial sites open to enrollment. Descriptive statistics are presented. The survey was given an exemption designation by the University of Michigan Medical School’s Institutional Review Board. The first author had full access to all data and takes responsibility for its integrity and analysis.

RESULTS

Of the 785 questions entered during the 10‐month time period, 75% were able to be answered per the GPT. On manual review, of the 588 that the GPT reportedly answered, 13 (2%) were not helpful responses, 12 (2%) were incomplete responses, 5 (1%) were misleading, and 3 (1%) contained the correct response but also provided some conflicting messaging. The misleading responses included reporting: incorrect study principal investigator, incorrect instructions for ordering supplies (n=2), suggesting use of a witness for consent when it was not necessary, and reporting that COVID was not exclusionary (it is not per se, but respiratory precautions are an exclusion). Of the 95 primary study coordinators at 99 open clinical trial sites to whom the survey was sent, 45 (47%) responded. Of those, 21 (47%) reported having used the GPT. Of the 19 who provided more detailed information, 1 reported asking the GPT 1 question; 11, 2 to 5; 6, 6 to 10; and 1 >15 questions. Most (89%) found it to be very helpful (n=11) or helpful (n=6). Only 1 reported that it was unhelpful, and 1 reported that it was neither helpful nor unhelpful. Most (79%) found the responses to be accurate (n=15) or partially accurate (n=2); 1 was unsure, and 1 individual who reported that it was very helpful reported “no” to accuracy. Most (89%) were very satisfied (n=10) or satisfied (n=7) with the GPT. Only 1 was dissatisfied. Eleven respondents confirmed all responses with source documentation; 7 confirmed some but not all responses, and 1 reported not confirming any responses. Only 4 reported confirming the answer with a study team member. The reported advantages to use of the GPT were immediate responses (n=15), ability to find information more quickly than through reference documents (n=14), availability after hours (n=11), confirmation of suspected answers (n=10), and ability to ask questions anonymously (n=8). Among the 45 total respondents, including the 24 who had not used the GPT, 20 (44%) planned to use the GPT in the future, 17 (38%) were unsure, and 8 (18%) did not plan to use it.

DISCUSSION

We report the novel development and use of a customized GPT to provide protocol and procedure support to clinical trial site staff within a large, multicenter trial. Anticipated challenges included concerns that responses might contain significant errors that pose risks to participants, the possibility that study teams could inadvertently disclose PHI despite instructions, and a lack of adoption. The GPT in practice provided helpful answers to most questions and site staff were satisfied with the tool. However, interpretation of the survey results was limited by low GPT use and number of survey respondents. No “dangerous” responses were identified and limited PHI was entered by study sites. Despite the availability of relatively prompt human‐based daytime and evening support, an average of 79 questions were directed to the GPT per month. These results suggest that a customized GPT can be a useful tool in a clinical trial to support clinical sites. We are unaware of other published work on this topic.

Use of the GPT provided an opportunity for immediate responses to questions any time of the day or night including on weekends and holidays; although, most questions were asked between 8 a.m. and 5 p.m. ET. The GPT supplemented the human support available in the trial, rather than replacing all human support. We are unclear whether use of the GPT reduced questions addressed to the central teams. We lack information on whether staff confirmed the GPT responses, as we advised, using source documentation. Staff may have asked the GPT questions they otherwise would have tried to address locally, as they noted in the survey that the GPT was a more efficient alternative to looking through source documents.

The GPT’s self‐reported percentage of “could answer” may have been misestimated. Some questions it could not answer were asked multiple times in quick succession suggesting a rephrasing of the same question by a single study team member. On the other hand, the GPT offers sample questions for users, and some users may have entered these to test the GPT function rather than to ask a “real” question. To optimize accurate responses, we performed upfront and continual testing and retraining. GPT performance without this investment is unclear. The effect of the GPT on accuracy of protocol implementation is unknown, and the usefulness of the GPT, acceptability of its performance, and comparative safety of its responses cannot be confirmed without direct comparison to human performance. In our study, only a single evaluator assessed the usefulness of the responses. Finally, the rapid technological evolution of LLMs may affect the generalizability of the results but may facilitate similar applications of customized GPTs in the future.

Conclusions

Little published research has addressed protocol support for clinical trial enrollment sites. Based on the current study, customized GPTs may play a role in this support. Caution should be exercised to avoid entering PHI in these systems. We speculate that success and safety of our GPT may have been enhanced by the decision to use a tool that mostly relied on resources provided, rather than a tool that used the internet more broadly to answer questions. Advancement in technology conceivably could improve GPT utility for clinical trial support. For example, although our GPT gave an indication of what sources were used to answer a specific question, it did not provide links that take the user directly to the relevant sentences. This GPT could not give the reader any sense of the estimated confidence or reliability of the answer. Simple modifications to programming could automatically forward unanswered responses to the study team; let the user know the question has been transmitted for further (human) consideration; and serve as a conduit for the human response then provided by a study team member. Future research should directly compare GPT and human performance in provision of trial protocol and procedure support.
