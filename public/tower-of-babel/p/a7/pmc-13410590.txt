Association between continuous phenylephrine infusion and hemodynamic stability in patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy under remimazolam sedation: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ye Tian, Yun Zhang, DanShi Feng, HongYing Wang, ZiHuan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypotension is an important complication in the procedural sedation for the gastrointestinal endoscopy despite the use of remimazolam, which has superior hemodynamic stability compared to propofol. Prophylactic vasopressor therapy may preserve cerebral perfusion and enhance the outcome of recovery. This study evaluated the association between continuous infusion of phenylephrine and hemodynamic stability and recovery of cognition in patients undergoing endoscopy under remimazolam sedation.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed clinical records of 108 adult patients (American Society of Anaesthesiologists I-III, age 18–75 years) who underwent elective gastroscopy and/or colonoscopy at Pangang Group General Hospital between January 2022 and December 2024. Patients were classified into two groups based on whether they received prophylactic phenylephrine infusion (30 µg/min) throughout the procedure (phenylephrine group, n = 54) or standard care without prophylactic vasopressor support (control group, n = 54). All patients received sufentanil 0.1 µg/kg and remimazolam 0.15 mg/kg for sedation induction. Primary outcomes were hemodynamic parameters and the incidence of hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or more than 20% decrease from baseline). Secondary outcomes were recovery time, orientation recovery time and adverse events. Hemodynamic variables were measured at baseline and at several intervals after induction. Statistical analyses were performed using independent samples t-tests, repeated measures ANOVA and multiple regression models with adjustments for baseline imbalances.ResultsThe phenylephrine group showed significantly better hemodynamic stability (hypotension, defined as systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or a decrease > 20% from baseline, occurred in only 1.8% of patients compared with 61.8% of control group patients; relative risk 0.03, 95% CI: 0.00-0.20, P < 0.001; number needed to treat 1.67). Systolic blood pressure was 12–14 mmHg higher in the phenylephrine group at all post induction time points (all P < 0.001). Maximum blood pressure reduction was significantly reduced (9.94 ± 11.51% vs. 22.47 ± 7.75%, P < 0.001). Recovery time was significantly reduced in the phenylephrine group (8.48 ± 1.63 vs. 11.67 ± 2.21 min, mean difference 3.19 min, 95% CI: 2.45–3.92, P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.64), a difference of 27.3%. Orientation recovery time was also statistically significantly accelerated (9.11 ± 1.55 vs. 14.63 ± 3.70 min, mean difference 5.52 min, 95% CI: 4.45–6.59, P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.94), indicating a 37.7% improvement. Severe hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 80 mmHg) occurred in 5.5 vs. 40.0% of patients (P < 0.001). Gender-stratified analyses and multiple regression analyses adjusting for baseline imbalances confirmed the robustness of the observed associations in every subgroup. Adverse events were significantly less in the phenylephrine group (9.3% vs. 74.1%, P < 0.001), with no serious adverse events in either group.ConclusionsContinuous phenylephrine infusion during remimazolam sedation was associated with better hemodynamic stability and appeared to be related to a lower incidence of hypotension and faster recovery in patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy. These results suggest the possible benefit of prophylactic vasopressor support to both increase the safety and efficiency of procedural sedation. However, the retrospective nature of this study precludes causal inference. Further prospective randomized studies in various patient populations and clinical settings are warranted in order to confirm these observations and to determine optimal dosing strategies.Trial registrationNot applicable. Introduction

Procedures that investigate the inside of the digestive system with instruments constitute one of the most common medical methods performed across the world; more than 51 million procedures take place annually in the United States alone [1]. The widespread use of sedation during these procedures has altered the experience from one that was uncomfortable to one that is well tolerated for both diagnostic and treatment purposes and has resulted in significant improvement in patient satisfaction, improved operating conditions for the endoscopist, and enhanced diagnostic and therapeutic outcomes of the procedures [2, 3].

Propofol has become the major sedative agent used for such procedures because of its favorable properties, quick onset, and quick recovery properties. However, the effects of propofol on the cardiovascular system are issues of significant safety concern, especially the tendency of propofol to produce low blood pressure due to a combination of reductions in resistance in blood vessels throughout the body and cardiac output. Recent large-scale analyses have shown that hypotension occurs in about 36% of patients undergoing examination of the colon with propofol sedation, with episodes lasting longer than 10 min in many cases and with levels of hypotension reaching those associated with organ injury in surgical populations [4, 5]. The incidence of hypotension during procedures varies significantly depending on the patient’s characteristics, and elderly patients, patients with cardiovascular diseases and patients with complex treatment procedures have been reported to have a particularly high risk [6].

Remimazolam is a novel, ultra-short-acting medication belonging to the class that induces sedation through particular receptors and was approved for use during procedures in 2020. This is a potentially safer medication alternative to propofol and is a novel, ultra-short-acting medication [7]. Unlike traditional medications in this class which are broken down through pathways in the liver enzymes, remimazolam is broken down quickly by enzymes in the tissues to inactive forms, leading to predictable and rapid recovery, regardless of the length of time administered or function of the liver and kidneys [8]. Studies comparing treatment approaches and studies combining multiple studies have shown that remimazolam is significantly superior to both midazolam and propofol in terms of faster recovery times, reduced incidence of low blood pressure and respiratory depression, and superior stability in terms of cardiovascular measures, with procedure success being in excess of 97% [9, 10].

Remimazolam has superior effects on the heart and circulation than propofol but low blood pressure remains the concern during procedures requiring sedation. The mechanism is the interaction of receptor-mediated inhibition in the nervous system, decrease in sympathetic tone, and direct effects on blood vessels [11]. Even with rapid metabolism and good properties, vasodilation during the sedation procedure can impact on blood flow to the brain, especially if combined with pain relief medication often provided during procedures looking at the digestive system [12].

Providing medication to maintain blood pressure before low blood pressure occurs represents a possible strategy to be used to maintain stability during procedural sedation. Phenylephrine, which acts on particular receptors, constricts the blood vessels without significant effects on heart rate, and is therefore theoretically appropriate to counteract the hypotension caused by sedation [13]. While phenylephrine has been investigated intensively in relation to spinal and general anesthesia, its prophylactic use during procedural sedation with remimazolam has not yet been systematically evaluated. The rationale for hemodynamic support is not just to maintain the pressure, but the maintenance of blood flow to the brain during sedation may have an effect on cognitive recovery, procedural efficiency and overall outcome for patients [14].

The present study aimed to address this gap by evaluating the association between continuous phenylephrine infusion and hemodynamic stability and recovery characteristics in patients who underwent gastrointestinal endoscopy under remimazolam sedation. Through retrospective analysis of clinical records from a single institution where prophylactic phenylephrine infusion was adopted by some anesthesiologists as part of routine practice, this study examined whether patients who received continuous phenylephrine infusion exhibited a lower incidence of procedural hypotension and faster cognitive recovery compared with those who received standard care without prophylactic vasopressor support. Understanding the relationship between prophylactic hemodynamic support and novel sedative agents like remimazolam is important for optimizing procedural sedation protocols and improving patient safety in the growing field of outpatient procedures.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This was a retrospective cohort study conducted at Pangang Group General Hospital. Clinical records of patients who underwent elective gastroscopy and/or colonoscopy under remimazolam sedation between January 2022 and December 2024 were reviewed and analyzed. This work was examined and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Pangang Group General Hospital (No. PGZYY-LLKY-2024013). The requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and followed Good Clinical Practice guidelines. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reporting cohort studies.

Study population

Clinical records of patients who received elective gastroscopy and/or colonoscopy under remimazolam sedation during the study period were screened for eligibility. The study included individuals between 18 and 75 years with classification from the American Society of Anesthesiologists showing physical status of I to III and body mass index between 18.5 and 25.0 kg/m². These individuals required fasting for at least 8 h before procedures and required preparation of the large intestine following standard approaches.

The study did not include individuals using pain medication on a regular basis or individuals showing dementia or showing intellectual disability. It also did not include individuals with known reaction to sedation medications or to components in study medications. Individuals with heart rate below 50 beats per minute at the start and individuals using receptor blockers for blood pressure or using enzyme blockers for blood pressure on a regular basis were not included. The study did not include individuals with severe heart disease or with blood pressure showing poor management or with severe breathing disorders. It also did not include individuals showing liver problems with Child-Pugh class C or showing kidney failure requiring dialysis. Individuals who were pregnant or providing milk to infants were not included and individuals in other studies within the past 3 months were not included.

Patient grouping

Eligible patients were classified into two groups based on their clinical management records. Patients who received prophylactic continuous phenylephrine infusion during the procedure were assigned to the phenylephrine group (n = 54), whereas patients who underwent the same sedation protocol without prophylactic vasopressor support were assigned to the control group (n = 54). The decision to administer prophylactic phenylephrine infusion was made by the attending anesthesiologist based on clinical judgment and institutional practice patterns during the study period. At our institution, the practice of prophylactic phenylephrine infusion during remimazolam sedation was introduced and gradually adopted during the study period, with some anesthesiologists routinely incorporating this approach while others maintained standard practice without prophylactic vasopressor support.

Sample size

A post-hoc power analysis was performed to evaluate the statistical adequacy of the sample. Based on the observed hypotension incidence of 1.8% in the phenylephrine group and 61.8% in the control group, with 54 patients per group, the achieved power exceeded 99% at a two-sided significance level of 0.05. The analysis was conducted using PASS 15.0 software (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA).

Anesthetic management

Preoperative preparation

According to institutional protocol documented in the clinical records, individuals in the study were positioned in the left lateral position on the side. Monitoring that is standard was established and this monitoring included continuous measurement using methods for heart activity, blood pressure measurement that does not involve entering the body, measurement of oxygen in blood using a method on the finger, and monitoring of breathing rate. Vital signs at the initial point were recorded following a period of five minutes for stabilization. Access to veins in areas outside central regions was secured using a catheter of eighteen gauge or twenty gauge. Oxygen provision before the procedure was provided using a mask on the face at eight to ten liters in a minute for three to five minutes before induction of agents for anesthesia.

Anesthetic induction

Per the institutional standardized sedation protocol, individuals in both groups received sufentanil at a dose of 0.1 micrograms per kilogram of body weight. This was given intravenously over thirty seconds. Following this, remimazolam at 0.15 milligrams per kilogram was given as a slow intravenous injection over sixty seconds. Loss of consciousness was defined as loss of the eyelash reflex. It also included failure to respond to verbal commands.

In the phenylephrine group, a continuous infusion of phenylephrine was initiated at the same time as remimazolam administration. Phenylephrine at ten milligrams was diluted in normal saline. This produced a concentration of 125 micrograms per milliliter (0.0125%). The initial infusion rate was set at 30 micrograms per minute (14.4 milliliters per hour). This used a calibrated infusion pump (Graseby 3400, Smiths Medical, UK). The infusion was continued throughout the procedure. It was discontinued upon completion of the endoscopic examination.

Individuals in the control group received sufentanil and remimazolam as described in the previous section without prophylactic vasopressor infusion.

Anesthetic maintenance

Following the initial dose of remimazolam, depth of sedation was assessed during the procedure. The assessment used observation of patient movement, observation of changes in vital signs, and observation of patient response. If the patient required additional sedation, supplemental doses of remimazolam 2.5 mg were provided. These supplemental doses were provided through the same approach. The supplemental doses were provided at intervals. These intervals were at least 2 min. The procedure limited supplemental doses to five doses. This limit applied within any period of 15 min. The total dose of remimazolam was recorded for each patient. The total of supplemental doses was recorded for each patient.

During the procedure, patients maintained spontaneous breathing. This breathing occurred through a face mask. The mask provided supplemental oxygen at 5 L per minute. Support for breathing was provided if the rate of breathing decreased below 8 breaths per minute. Support for breathing was provided if SpO₂ decreased below 90%. This decrease in SpO₂ was measured despite supplemental oxygen that was provided.

Management of changes in blood pressure and heart rate

Hypotension was defined as systolic blood pressure below 90 mmHg or mean arterial pressure below 60 mmHg or a decrease of more than 20% from baseline. In the control group, hypotension was treated using phenylephrine given intravenously in bolus doses of 50 micrograms as needed. Bradycardia, defined as heart rate below 50 beats per minute, was treated using atropine given intravenously at a dose of 0.5 milligrams. Hypertension, defined as systolic blood pressure greater than 180 mmHg or an increase of more than 30% from baseline, was managed by reducing the rate of phenylephrine infusion or by temporarily stopping the infusion. All interventions administered were documented in the anesthetic records and extracted during data collection.

Postprocedural management

At the completion of the endoscopic procedure, all sedative agents were discontinued. Patients were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit and monitored continuously until full recovery. Supplemental oxygen was provided until oxygen saturation remained above 95% on room air. Readiness for discharge was assessed using standard criteria, including stable vital signs, full orientation to person, place, and time, ability to tolerate oral fluids, and absence of significant pain or abdominal discomfort.

Data collection and outcome measures

All data were extracted retrospectively from electronic medical records, anesthetic records, and post-anesthesia care unit documentation by two trained investigators. Data extraction was performed independently, and discrepancies were resolved by a third senior investigator.

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes were hemodynamic stability parameters, focusing on the incidence of hypotension and the magnitude of blood pressure changes from baseline. Hemodynamic parameters including systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation were extracted from anesthetic records at the following time points: baseline (before drug administration), immediately post-induction, and at 3, 5, 10, 15, and 20 min after induction. Blood pressure was measured using an automated device calibrated per manufacturer instructions. All measurements were performed on the same arm throughout the monitoring period.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes included recovery profile and adverse events. Recovery time was defined as the interval from the last dose of remimazolam to the time the patient opened eyes spontaneously or in response to verbal command. Orientation recovery time was defined as the interval from the last dose to complete restoration of orientation to person, place, and time, assessed using standard questions regarding the patient’s name, current location, and date. These times were documented in minutes by trained nurses in the post-anesthesia care unit.

Adverse events were documented systematically in the anesthetic and post-anesthesia care unit records. These included hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or decrease > 20% from baseline), severe hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 80 mmHg), bradycardia (heart rate < 50 bpm), tachycardia (heart rate > 100 bpm), hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 90%), respiratory depression (respiratory rate < 8 breaths per minute), nausea, vomiting, injection site pain, paradoxical agitation, allergic reactions, and other unanticipated events. The severity, duration, treatment, and outcome of each event were recorded.

Additional variables extracted from the records included the following. Demographic and clinical characteristics including age, sex, weight, height, body mass index, ASA physical status classification, medical history, concurrent medications, and procedure indication were collected. Procedural variables including procedure type (gastroscopy, colonoscopy, or both), procedure duration, endoscopist-rated difficulty, and patient movement or coughing during the procedure were documented. Patient satisfaction was assessed using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied, 5 = very satisfied) during the postprocedural interview.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and Python version 3.12 with the SciPy library. Tests were two-sided, and P values less than 0.05 were considered significant. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons in secondary outcomes.

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of distribution plots. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared between groups using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median and interquartile range and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell frequencies were less than 5.

Hemodynamic parameters at each time point were compared between groups using independent samples t-tests. Percentage change from baseline was calculated as (value at time point − baseline value) / baseline value × 100%. Repeated measures analysis of variance was performed to assess the effects of treatment group, time, and group-by-time interaction on hemodynamic variables. The Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied when the assumption of sphericity was violated. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction when significant main effects or interactions were identified.

Recovery time and orientation recovery time were compared between groups using independent samples t-tests. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d, where values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 represented small, medium, and large effects, respectively. Mean differences with 95% confidence intervals were reported.

Adverse event incidence was compared between groups using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Relative risks with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. The number needed to treat was calculated as the reciprocal of the absolute risk reduction for hypotension.

To account for potential confounders including the gender difference and baseline heart rate difference between groups at baseline, several additional analyses were performed. First, gender-stratified analyses were conducted to evaluate treatment effects within male and female subgroups separately. Second, multiple linear regression models were constructed with recovery outcomes as the dependent variable and group, gender, age, baseline heart rate, and BMI as independent variables. This approach enabled assessment of whether the group effect remained significant after adjusting for potential confounders. Propensity score matching was considered but was not feasible due to the limited sample size. Estimates with and without covariate adjustment were reported to evaluate consistency of findings.

Additional subgroup analyses were performed across age groups (< 60 years vs. ≥ 60 years) and BMI categories (using cutoffs of 22 and 24 kg/m²) to evaluate potential effect modification.

Results

Patient selection and baseline characteristics

A total of 215 patients who underwent elective gastroscopy and/or colonoscopy under remimazolam sedation during the study period were identified from the electronic medical records. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 132 patients were eligible for analysis. Of these, 24 patients were excluded due to incomplete medical records (n = 14) or missing key outcome data (n = 10). The remaining 108 patients were included in the final analysis, with 54 patients in the phenylephrine group and 54 patients in the control group. The patient selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were largely balanced between the two groups (Table 1). The mean age was 50.4 years in both groups with no significant difference (P = 0.975). However, a statistically significant difference in gender distribution was found with the phenylephrine group having a higher proportion of male patients (54.5% vs. 30.9%, P = 0.020). Body mass index was similar between groups (22.96 ± 1.83 kg/m² in the phenylephrine group and 22.59 ± 1.87 kg/m² in the control group, P = 0.309). Similarly, diastolic blood pressure was significantly greater in the phenylephrine group at all post-induction time points. The difference was between 8.5 and 9.5 mmHg at all measurement points; all the comparisons were significant (P < 0.001). Heart rate patterns revealed an interesting divergence between the groups. The percentage change from baseline in systolic blood pressure showed marked differences between groups (Table 3). In the phenylephrine group, systolic blood pressure decreased by only 4.74 ± 4.62% immediately post-induction compared with a 15.72 ± 8.12% decrease in the control group (P < 0.001). This pattern was consistent across time points, where the control group showed blood pressure reductions ranging between 15.05 and 16.63% and the phenylephrine group showed blood pressure within 4.40 and 6.25% of baseline values (all P < 0.001). Analysis of maximum decrease in systolic blood pressure during the procedure showed that the maximum systolic blood pressure decrease was 9.94 ± 11.51% in the phenylephrine group compared to 22.47 ± 7.75% in the control group, representing a highly significant difference (mean difference 12.53%, P < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.28).

Repeated measures analysis of variance showed significant main effects of group (F = 186.4, P < 0.001), time (F = 24.7, P < 0.001) and a significant group-by-time interaction (F = 8.9, P < 0.001) for systolic blood pressure, indicating that the group effect on blood pressure differed at different time points. Similar patterns were found for diastolic blood pressure and heart rate.

Recovery outcomes

Recovery profiles differed significantly between the two groups (Table 4). The mean time from the last dose of remimazolam to eye opening, defined as recovery time, was 8.48 ± 1.63 min in the phenylephrine group, compared with 11.67 ± 2.21 min in the control group, representing a clinically and statistically significant difference of 3.19 min (95% CI: 2.45 to 3.92 min, P < 0.001). The effect size was large (Cohen’s d = 1.64).

These results suggest that the better hemodynamic stability observed in the phenylephrine group might have contributed to improved cerebral perfusion and more rapid emergence from sedation.

Adverse events

The incidence of adverse events was significantly lower in the phenylephrine group compared with the control group (Table 5). Hypotension, defined as a systolic blood pressure reduction greater than 20% from baseline or less than 90 mmHg, occurred in only 1 patient (1.8%) in the phenylephrine group compared to 34 patients (61.8%) in the control group (relative risk 0.03, 95% CI: 0.00 to 0.20, P < 0.001). This corresponds to an absolute risk reduction of 60.0% and a number needed to treat of 1.67.

Severe hypotension (defined as systolic blood pressure less than 80 mmHg) occurred in 3 patients (5.5%) in the phenylephrine group compared to 22 patients (40.0%) in the control group (relative risk 0.14, 95% CI: 0.04 to 0.43, P < 0.001). The number needed to treat to prevent one episode of severe hypotension was 2.9. Bradycardia occurred rarely in both groups with no events in the phenylephrine group and 1 event (1.8%) in the control group (P = 1.000). Tachycardia was reported in 2 patients (3.6%) in the control group but not in any patients in the phenylephrine group (P = 0.495). One episode of transient hypoxemia (SpO₂ less than 90%) occurred in the phenylephrine group, which was rapidly corrected with supplemental oxygen and jaw thrust maneuver, and no hypoxemic events occurred in the control group (P = 1.000).

There were no serious adverse events (events requiring hospitalization, causing permanent disability, or life-threatening events) in either group. No patients had allergic reactions, paradoxical agitation, or severe respiratory depression requiring mechanical ventilation. Minor adverse events such as injection site discomfort were rare and did not differ significantly between groups. A total of 5 patients (9.3%) in the phenylephrine group and 40 patients (74.1%) in the control group experienced at least one adverse event. The total number of adverse event episodes was 5 in the phenylephrine group and 59 in the control group, as some patients in the control group experienced multiple concurrent adverse events (e.g., hypotension accompanied by nausea or tachycardia).

The rescue medication use reflected the differences in hemodynamic stability between groups. In the control group, 34 patients required rescue bolus doses of phenylephrine for treatment of hypotension, with a median of 2 doses (range 1–5 doses) per patient. No rescue vasopressor was required in the phenylephrine group. One patient in the control group required atropine for bradycardia. No patients required treatment for hypertension in either group; however, the phenylephrine infusion was temporarily reduced in 2 patients (3.7%) in the phenylephrine group because of systolic blood pressure greater than 160 mmHg, which resolved without further intervention.

Age-stratified analyses (< 60 years vs. ≥ 60 years) and BMI-stratified analyses (using cutoffs of 22 and 24 kg/m²) similarly demonstrated consistent group differences across all strata, with no evidence of effect modification (all P for interaction > 0.20, data not shown). These extensive sensitivity analyses provide evidence that the observed associations between continuous phenylephrine infusion and improved outcomes were consistent across different patient characteristics.

Procedural characteristics

Procedural characteristics were comparable between groups. The mean duration of endoscopy was 18.3 ± 5.2 min in the phenylephrine group and 17.9 ± 4.8 min in the control group (P = 0.673). The distribution of procedure types (gastroscopy only, colonoscopy only, or both) was similar between groups (P = 0.582). Total remimazolam dose, including the loading dose and supplemental doses, was 10.2 ± 2.1 mg in the phenylephrine group compared to 10.5 ± 2.3 mg in the control group (P = 0.472), indicating comparable depth of sedation requirements. The number of patients requiring supplemental remimazolam doses did not differ significantly between groups (38.9% vs. 42.6%, P = 0.698).

Endoscopist satisfaction, assessed using a 5-point Likert scale, was high in both groups (phenylephrine group: 4.6 ± 0.5, control group: 4.5 ± 0.6, P = 0.391). Patient satisfaction scores were also comparable (phenylephrine group: 4.7 ± 0.5, control group: 4.6 ± 0.6, P = 0.284), with the majority of patients in both groups reporting very high satisfaction with the procedure. Patient movement or coughing during the procedure occurred rarely and did not differ between groups (phenylephrine group: 3.7%, control group: 5.6%, P = 0.652).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study demonstrates that continuous phenylephrine infusion was associated with improved hemodynamic stability and faster recovery of cognitive function in patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopy under remimazolam sedation. The results show that the incidence of hypotension was 1.8% in the phenylephrine group compared with 61.8% in the control group, and that recovery was faster in the phenylephrine group. These findings support the potential role of prophylactic vasopressor therapy during procedural sedation with remimazolam, addressing the important clinical concern of maintaining adequate cerebral perfusion while facilitating efficient patient throughput in endoscopy settings.

The hemodynamic stability observed in the phenylephrine group is consistent with evidence indicating that remimazolam provides better cardiovascular effects than propofol [15, 16]. Meta-analyses have shown that remimazolam reduces the incidence of hypotension by approximately 50% compared to propofol across various surgical settings [17, 18]. However, the control group in this study showed a hypotension incidence of 61.8% even with the favorable profile of remimazolam, suggesting that GABA_A receptor-mediated sedation produces substantial vasodilatory effects. The mechanism by which continuous phenylephrine infusion may provide hemodynamic protection involves multiple pathways. Phenylephrine, as a selective α₁-adrenergic receptor agonist, produces arterial and venous constriction, increasing systemic vascular resistance and raising mean arterial pressure without directly affecting the myocardium [19]. Studies examining hemodynamic patterns have demonstrated that phenylephrine-induced venoconstriction increases venous return, thereby augmenting cardiac output through a preload-dependent mechanism [13]. This preload-dependent mechanism may explain the observation that the phenylephrine group maintained both blood pressure and adequate tissue perfusion throughout the procedure. The observed decrease in heart rate in the phenylephrine group was statistically significant but values remained within clinically acceptable ranges, requiring no intervention. This heart rate reduction represents a baroreflex-mediated response to increased arterial pressure, constituting a normal regulatory mechanism rather than a pathological finding [20]. The reduction in heart rate may also contribute to improved diastolic filling and coronary perfusion, potentially conferring additional cardiovascular benefits in susceptible populations.

The key finding of this study is the substantial association between phenylephrine infusion and improved recovery time (27.3% reduction) and orientation recovery time (37.7% reduction), with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 1.64 and 1.94, respectively). These findings suggest that maintaining hemodynamic stability during sedation may affect not only the duration of sedation but also the quality and rapidity of cognitive restoration. In addition to the cerebral perfusion hypothesis, improved hemodynamic stability could also lead to better renal and hepatic perfusion, thereby facilitating the clearance of co-administered anesthetic agents such as sufentanil. Although remimazolam is primarily metabolized by tissue esterases and its elimination is unlikely to be substantially affected by changes in organ perfusion, enhanced clearance of sufentanil through improved hepatic blood flow may represent an alternative or complementary mechanism for the faster recovery observed in the phenylephrine group. Notably, the comparable total remimazolam doses between the two groups (10.2 ± 2.1 vs. 10.5 ± 2.3 mg, P = 0.472) is a strength of this study, as it suggests that the observed differences in recovery were not attributable to differences in sedative exposure.

The observed relationship between maintained blood pressure and faster cognitive recovery may be mediated through preserved cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP). While cerebral autoregulation theoretically maintains constant cerebral blood flow across mean arterial pressure ranges of 60–160 mmHg, this protective mechanism can be significantly impaired during general anesthesia and sedation [21, 22]. Recent studies using cerebrovascular monitoring have demonstrated that anesthetic-induced hypotension, even when transient, leads to measurable reductions in cerebral blood flow velocity and cerebral oxygen delivery [14]. The rapid blood pressure fluctuations that commonly occur during procedural sedation may exceed the capacity of temporal autoregulatory compensation, resulting in periods of relative cerebral hypoperfusion [23].

Benzodiazepine-type agents including remimazolam exert their effects through positive allosteric modulation of GABA_A receptors, facilitating chloride ion conductance and neuronal inhibition [24]. This process produces reductions in cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen consumption, but the coupling between cerebral metabolism and cerebral blood flow may be disrupted when hypotension occurs [25, 26]. Animal studies have demonstrated that these agents can reduce cerebral metabolic rate by as much as 55%, with proportional cerebral blood flow reductions when blood pressure is maintained at normal levels [27]. However, when hypotension occurs concurrently, the metabolism-flow coupling is disrupted, potentially creating a mismatch between cerebral oxygen supply and demand even though overall cerebral metabolism is decreased. The finding that patients who received phenylephrine showed faster functional recovery suggests that maintaining adequate cerebral perfusion pressure during the procedure may have preserved aerobic cerebral metabolism and prevented accumulation of anaerobic metabolic byproducts. This interpretation is supported by evidence demonstrating that intraoperative cerebral desaturation and hypotension are independent predictors of delayed emergence and postprocedural cognitive disturbance [28]. The brain’s capacity for rapid emergence from sedation depends critically on maintaining oxidative metabolism and preventing lactate accumulation, processes requiring stable cerebral blood flow even when overall metabolic rate is reduced.

Although this study examined immediate postprocedural outcomes, the findings have potential relevance to broader concerns regarding postoperative cognitive dysfunction and perioperative neurocognitive disorders [29, 30].

Emerging evidence suggests that intraoperative hemodynamic perturbations represent a modifiable risk factor for postprocedural cognitive dysfunction, particularly in older adults [31–33]. Multiple studies have demonstrated associations between intraoperative hypotension, including brief episodes, and increased rates of postoperative delirium and delayed cognitive recovery. The pathophysiological mechanisms linking intraoperative hypotension to cognitive outcomes involve multiple pathways, including cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury, blood-brain barrier disruption, neuroinflammatory responses, and impaired clearance of anesthetic agents. Older adults demonstrate reduced autoregulatory capacity and increased vulnerability to hypotensive episodes, with studies showing rightward shifts in autoregulatory curves and diminished dynamic autoregulatory responses. The patients in this study, with a mean age of 50.4 years, represent a population in which age-related cerebrovascular changes are beginning to manifest, making perfusion optimization particularly relevant. The superior postprocedural recovery profile observed in association with continuous phenylephrine infusion may reflect not only maintenance of cerebral perfusion during sedation but also prevention of subclinical cerebral injury. Advanced neuroimaging studies have shown that brief intraoperative hypotensive episodes can produce detectable changes in cerebral microstructure visible on diffusion-weighted MRI. Although this study did not include long-term cognitive follow-up, the substantial reduction in orientation recovery time suggests preserved higher cortical function, which could potentially reduce the risk of subsequent cognitive complications.

The use of prophylactic vasopressor therapy during endoscopic procedures represents a paradigm shift from reactive to preventive hemodynamic management. The data show that continuous infusion maintained blood pressure within 5–6% of baseline values throughout the procedure, in contrast to the 15–16% decreases observed in the control group. This stable hemodynamic profile eliminates the blood pressure oscillations associated with bolus dosing and may reduce the workload of anesthesia providers while improving procedural workflow. The safety profile observed in this study, with no episodes of severe hypertension requiring intervention and minimal dose adjustments (3.7% of patients), suggests that standardized infusion protocols can be implemented safely. The continuous infusion approach offers pharmacokinetic advantages over bolus administration, producing steady-state plasma levels that match the duration of sedation [34]. Given phenylephrine’s elimination half-life of approximately 5 min, discontinuation of the infusion at procedure completion results in rapid normalization of hemodynamic parameters. From a healthcare efficiency perspective, the faster recovery times translate to meaningful improvements in suite throughput and resource utilization. The 3.2-minute reduction in recovery time and 5.5-minute reduction in orientation recovery time, when extrapolated across the hundreds of procedures performed weekly in high-volume centers, represent substantial efficiency gains. The prevention of hypotension-related complications, including the need for extended monitoring and urgent interventions, likely offsets the modest additional cost of the infusion.

The present findings are particularly relevant to sedation with remimazolam. Remimazolam offers advantages over propofol in terms of hemodynamic stability, organ-independent metabolism, and the availability of flumazenil for reversal. Data from the control group demonstrate that hypotension remains a significant concern with this agent [35]. The hypotension rate in the group receiving remimazolam alone was 61.8%, which, although lower than rates reported in propofol studies, indicates that the ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine mechanism alone does not eliminate sedation-induced vasodilation. The pharmacokinetic profile of remimazolam, characterized by rapid onset, predictable offset, and tissue esterase metabolism to inactive metabolites, makes it an ideal agent for ambulatory procedures [36]. The combination with sufentanil in the protocol used in this study provided excellent procedural conditions but likely contributed to the observed hypotension through synergistic vasodilatory and sympatholytic effects. Integration of phenylephrine infusion as a prophylactic measure appears to maintain the clinical benefits of remimazolam while mitigating its hemodynamic limitations.

Several limitations require consideration in the interpretation of these findings. Most importantly, the retrospective observational design of this study precludes establishment of causal relationships between phenylephrine infusion and the observed outcomes. The assignment of patients to the phenylephrine or control group was based on attending anesthesiologist preference and institutional practice patterns rather than randomization, introducing the possibility of selection bias. Anesthesiologists who chose to administer prophylactic phenylephrine may have differed systematically in their overall clinical management approach, which could have influenced outcomes independently of the phenylephrine infusion itself. Although multiple regression analyses and subgroup analyses were performed to adjust for measured confounders, unmeasured confounders cannot be excluded in an observational study. Additionally, the retrospective data collection relied on the completeness and accuracy of existing medical records, and information bias cannot be fully ruled out despite the use of standardized institutional documentation protocols and independent data extraction by two investigators. The imbalance observed at baseline in gender distribution and heart rate represents an additional concern. Sensitivity analyses including regression models adjusting for these variables confirmed that the observed associations were robust, and gender-stratified analyses demonstrated consistent findings across subgroups. The single-center design and focus on patients classified as ASA I-III undergoing elective endoscopy limit the generalizability of findings to higher-risk populations and emergency settings. The study did not include long-term cognitive assessment, preventing evaluation of whether the improved immediate recovery translates to reduced incidence of postprocedural cognitive complications at one week or beyond. Future research should employ prospective randomized designs with comprehensive neurocognitive assessment batteries at multiple time points to establish causal relationships and evaluate sustained cognitive benefits. Mechanistic studies using cerebral oximetry or transcranial Doppler monitoring would provide direct evidence of improved cerebral perfusion with phenylephrine infusion. The optimal dosing regimen for phenylephrine remains to be definitively established. The protocol of 30 µg/min provided excellent hemodynamic stability, but individualized dosing based on baseline blood pressure, patient characteristics, or real-time hemodynamic monitoring might further optimize outcomes. Comparative studies evaluating different infusion rates and titration strategies would refine clinical protocols.

In conclusion, this retrospective cohort study demonstrates that continuous phenylephrine infusion during remimazolam sedation for gastrointestinal endoscopy was associated with a lower incidence of hypotension and faster cognitive recovery. The findings suggest that cerebral perfusion may play an important role in the process of emergence from sedation and highlight the potential importance of hemodynamic management in procedural sedation. While these results are promising, the observational nature of this study warrants cautious interpretation, and prospective randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings and establish optimal implementation strategies. As the use of remimazolam increases in clinical practice, prophylactic vasopressor support represents a potentially evidence-based strategy to improve both safety and efficiency of outpatient gastrointestinal endoscopy.
