Molecular and clinical characterization of a pedigree with factor XI deficiency, including a novel missense variant (p.Cys200Phe): a case report
Authors: Ruimin Cai, Yi Li, Ping Liu, Yeke Tan, Fei Li, Wenyang Wang, Qiang Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCoagulation factor XI (FXI) deficiency is a rare bleeding disorder, we aimed to identify potential variants of F11 gene in a pedigree with hereditary FXI deficiency and explore the molecular pathogenesis.Case presentationA pedigree with FXI deficiency was included in this study. The proband was an 80-year-old Asian woman who showed a significantly prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), along with markedly decreased FXI activity and antigen levels.Direct DNA sequencing of all F11 gene exons identified compound heterozygous variants (p.Trp246* and p.Cys200Phe) in the proband. The daughter was heterozygous for p.Cys200Phe, who had an FXI coagulant activity (FXI: C) of 41.0% and FXI antigen level (FXI: Ag) of 48.0%. Her son and elder granddaughter were heterozygous for p.Trp246*, presenting FXI: C of 35.0% and 34.2%, and FXI: Ag of 43.3% and 53.0%, respectively. Bioinformatics analysis and protein modeling demonstrated that both variants were likely pathogenic, impairing the structure and function of the FXI protein and contributing to FXI deficiency in this pedigree.ConclusionIn summary, we identified compound heterozygous variants (p.Trp246* and p.Cys200Phe) as the likely genetic cause of FXI deficiency in this family. The missense variant p.Cys200Phe has not been reported previously. This study expands the genotypic spectrum of the F11 gene, which is beneficial for enhancing the accuracy of molecular diagnosis, optimizing clinical risk assessment, and facilitating individualized clinical management and genetic counseling for patients with FXI deficiency.

Introduction

The coagulation factor XI gene (F11) is mapped to chromosome 4 (4q35) and consists of 15 exons and 14 introns. To date, approximately 272 variants have been recorded in the FXI mutation database, with missense and nonsense variants accounting for the majority of these genetic variants [1].

FXI is a 160-kDa glycoprotein serine protease encoded by the F11 gene. It forms a homodimer composed of two identical 607-amino-acid subunits linked via disulfide bonds [2]. These identical polypeptide chains possess a conserved domain structure, containing four tandem apple (Ap) domains (AP1 to Ap4) at the N-terminus (each with 90–91 amino acid repeats) and a trypsin-like serine protease (SP) catalytic domain at the C-termFXI [3]. Synthesized by hepatocytes and megakaryocytes, FXI serves as a vital serine protease participating in the intrinsic pathway of blood coagulation [4]. It enhances thrombin generation on the surface of activated platelets and thereby plays a crucial role during the consolidation phase of coagulation [5]. Additionally, FXI can be activated by activated factor XII, thrombin, or autolysis, and subsequently activates factor IX (FIX) [6].

Genetic variants in the F11 gene lead to congenital FXI deficiency, a rare bleeding disorder also termed hemophilia C or Rosenthal syndrome, which was first documented by Rosenthal et al. in 1953 [7].

This disease is predominantly prevalent in specific ethnic populations, especially Ashkenazi Jews and French Basques. Patients with FXI deficiency manifest variable bleeding tendencies following trauma or surgical procedures, particularly when the injury occurs in fibrinolytic-rich tissues including the urinary tract, nasopharynx, and oral cavity. Nevertheless, a considerable number of affected individuals remain asymptomatic [8].

Notably, the plasma FXI level shows a weak correlation with the clinical bleeding risk of patients [3]. In terms of disease severity, patients with homozygous or compound heterozygous variants exhibit severe FXI deficiency, with coagulation factor activity lower than 20% of the normal level. In contrast, heterozygous individuals usually present intermediate FXI activity ranging from 20 to 60% of the standard value [9].

In the present study, we identified compound heterozygous variants in the F11 gene in a Chinese pedigree diagnosed with FXI deficiency. Genetic sequencing identified two heterozygous variants, p.Cys200Phe and p.Trp246*. Of these, the missense variant p.Cys200Phe is reported for the first time worldwide.

Case presentation

The proband was an 80-year-old Asian woman admitted to the hospital due to intermittent stabbing chest pain and chest distress for one year, with acute exacerbation and syncope one day before admission. She had a documented history of hypertension and no history of severe bleeding following trauma, tooth extraction, obstetric procedures, or other relevant conditions. No family members had a history of abnormal bleeding, thrombosis, or liver and kidney dysfunction.

Six family members across three generations, including her son, daughter, daughter-in-law, elder granddaughter, and youngest granddaughter, were enrolled in this study (the proband is indicated by an arrow in Fig. 1). Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood cells of the proband and her family members. All exons and exon–intron boundaries of the F11 gene in the proband were amplified by PCR, purified, and analyzed via Sanger sequencing bidirectionally (both forward and reverse strands). Discussion

Hereditary FXI deficiency is an inherited coagulation disorder that follows autosomal recessive or dominant inheritance patterns, caused by pathogenic variants in the F11 gene that impair normal blood clotting function [11, 12]. In this study, the proband exhibited a significantly prolonged APTT, with both FXI:C and FXI:Ag markedly reduced. The proband's son, daughter, and older granddaughter demonstrated normal APTT, while their FXI:C and FXI:Ag levels were reduced to approximately half of the normal range. Based on phenotypic and genotypic analyses, the compound heterozygous variants p.Cys200Phe and p.Trp246* were determined to cause severe reductions in both FXI:C and FXI:Ag levels. However, when either the p.Cys200Phe or p.Trp246* variant exists alone, it results in normal APTT with only mild synchronous decreases in both FXI:C and FXI:Ag levels. Measurement of FXI:C and FXI:Ag enables the phenotypic classification of mutations as either Type I (low FXI:Ag and low FXI:C) or Type II (normal FXI:Ag and low FXI:C). All family members in our study affected with FXI deficiency are classified as Type I [12, 13]. The limitation of this case is that an APTT mixing study and lupus anticoagulant testing were not performed, which would help exclude acquired causes of prolonged APTT.

Sequence alignment analysis revealed that Cys200 is highly conserved across various species, highlighting its critical role in maintaining the normal function of the FXI protein. In this study, TEG results revealed a prolonged R time in the proband, indicating reduced factor activity in the intrinsic coagulation pathway. In our TGA assay, tissue factor (5 pM TF) was used as the trigger. We describe the intriguing phenomenon of preserved thrombin generation in the proband with severe FXI deficiency, and elaborate that residual FXI activity together with complementary coagulation pathways may account for the maintenance of thrombin generation capacity. Taken together, thrombin generation via the extrinsic pathway is preserved at a level comparable to that in the control. Owing to the difficulty of recruiting matched healthy volunteers in this rare familial coagulation disorder, the number of healthy controls was limited.

FXI deficiency is associated with variable bleeding manifestations after trauma or surgery, Nevertheless, many affected individuals remain asymptomatic. Notably, all affected family members in this pedigree were asymptomatic. Most pathogenic mutations responsible for FXI deficiency are missense mutations, among which the Ap3 and SP domains harbor the greatest number of such mutations [4]. In our study, the novel variant c.599G > T (p.Cys200Phe), identified in the proband and her daughter, shows striking similarity to the previously reported variant c.599G > A (p.Cys200Tyr) [14]. Located within the Ap3 domain, the p.Cys200Tyr substitution is predicted to either directly disrupt or allosterically affect conserved disulfide bonds. Structural homology modeling analyses suggest that this cysteine-to-tyrosine substitution may induce protein misfolding, ultimately leading to a type I phenotype via impaired molecular stabilization [4, 15, 16].

The heterozygous nonsense variant c.738G > A (p.Trp246*), located within the AP3 domain, was first reported in 2003 by Wu et al. [17]. Subsequent studies have revealed its relatively high prevalence in the Chinese population. Notably, Shao et al. [3] analyzed 57 Chinese families with FXI deficiency and confirmed that this is the most prevalent pathogenic variant. It represents a population-specific hotspot variant specific to East Asian populations [6]. The p.Trp246* variant gives rise to a truncated protein with marked instability that is rapidly degraded [3]. Studies have indicated that this premature termination codon may also induce rapid degradation of the corresponding mRNA via nonsense-mediated decay (NMD). This pathway selectively eliminates transcripts containing premature stop signals, thereby ultimately reducing circulating FXI levels [18]. However, we acknowledge that the limitation of a single pedigree and the lack of in vitro functional experiments moderately reduce the novelty of our study, our current study only adopted bioinformatics prediction and structural modeling to assess the pathogenicity of p.Cys200Phe, and lacked in vitro functional verification such as recombinant protein expression and activity detection.

Conclusion

This study analyzed the phenotypes and genotypes of a family with hereditary FXI deficiency. Two heterozygous variants, p.Trp246* and p.Cys200Phe, were identified, among which p.Cys200Phe is reported here for the first time worldwide. It may explain the biallelic variants lead to severe FXI deficiency, whereas heterozygous carriers may exhibit partial or mild-to-moderate laboratory deficiency, with uncertain clinical penetrance. However, The allelic imbalance observed via Sanger sequencing is not completely attributable to standard heterozygosity, and copy number variation as well as other structural genomic variants remain plausible alternatives that cannot be ruled out with current sequencing evidence, the specific underlying mechanism remains to be further explored.
