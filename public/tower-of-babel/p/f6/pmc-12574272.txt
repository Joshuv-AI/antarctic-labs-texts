MDG-DDI: multi-feature drug graph for drug-drug interaction prediction
Authors: Wenjun Li, Yiting Zhou, Wanjun Ma, Weijun Liang, Xiwei Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrug–drug interactions (DDIs) frequently occur in combination therapy and may cause adverse effects or reduced efficacy. Existing computational approaches often fail to capture both the semantic information in drug sequences and the structural properties of drug molecules, limiting predictive power.ResultsWe propose MDG-DDI, a deep learning framework that integrates a Frequent Consecutive Subsequence (FCS)-based Transformer encoder with a Deep Graph Network (DGN) to extract complementary semantic and structural features. These representations are fused and fed into a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) for DDI prediction. Experiments on three benchmark datasets under transductive and inductive settings show that MDG-DDI consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods, with particularly strong gains when predicting interactions involving unseen drugs.ConclusionBy jointly modeling substructure-level semantics and molecular graph structure, MDG-DDI achieves robust and accurate DDI prediction. The framework demonstrates improved generalization and offers potential for enhancing drug safety assessment and discovery.Keywords: Drug-drug interaction, Graph convolution network, Feature extraction, Deep learning, Substructure mining, Molecular representation

Introduction

Drug-Drug Interaction (DDI) prediction is a key area of research in bioinformatics. In the treatment of complex diseases, a single drug often fails to achieve the desired therapeutic effect, making the use of multiple drugs a necessary approach. Combining medications is common for managing patients with intricate conditions [1, 2]. However, this concurrent use can lead to adverse effects known as drug-drug interactions [3]. For example, the serum concentration of dofetilide decreases when taken with dabrafenib but increases with dalfopristin [4]. Understanding the frequency of DDIs and the drugs most commonly involved can improve the assessment of their clinical significance [5]. Predicting potential DDIs can help minimize unexpected interactions, reduce drug development costs, and optimize the drug design process [6].

Predicting drug-drug interactions (DDIs) is essential in pharmacology and clinical practice, significantly influencing patient safety and therapeutic effectiveness. Accurate DDI predictions can minimize adverse drug reactions (ADRs) and improve the overall safety of drug regimens. By identifying potential interactions between concurrent medications, healthcare professionals can make informed decisions to avoid contraindications, adjust dosages, and implement alternative treatment strategies. This proactive approach reduces the risk of adverse health outcomes and supports personalized medicine by customizing treatment plans to individual patient profiles. Additionally, DDI prediction aids drug development by identifying potential interaction issues early in the research phase, allowing for the design of safer and more effective pharmacological interventions. Traditionally, DDI predictions relied on extensive experimental testing (in vitro) and clinical trials, both of which are costly and time-consuming. Nowadays, computational (in silico) methods offer an effective and cost-efficient alternative for predicting DDIs.

In recent years, various learning-based methods, including machine learning [7] and deep learning techniques [8] techniques, have demonstrated promising results in predicting drug-drug interactions (DDIs) [9–12]. Ryu et al. [13] introduced the DeepDDI model, the first to apply deep learning to DDI prediction. Building on this, Deng et al. [14] proposed a polymorphic deep learning model that utilizes filtered complete information for training. Liu et al. [15] developed the DANN-DDI framework, which employs deep attention neural networks to integrate various drug feature networks, enhancing the prediction accuracy of unobserved drug-drug interactions and improving the efficiency and safety of drug research. Notably, these methods often converge on local optimal solutions, prompting some researchers to incorporate attention mechanisms. Ren et al. [16] introduced BioChemDDI, which utilizes Natural Language Processing (NLP) [17] for chemical sequence data, Similarity Network Fusion (SNF) for biological function similarities, and Hierarchical Representation Learning for Networks (HARP) [18] to extract deep network structure information. This model combines biochemical and network features using a self-attention module and predicts interactions through a Deep Neural Network (DNN), achieving high prediction accuracy.

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are powerful tools for analyzing complex patterns in graph-structured data, particularly at the molecular level. Marinka et al. [19] introduced Decagon, a two-layer heterogeneous graph model designed to predict polypharmacy side effects for drug pairs targeting specific proteins. Nyamabo et al. [20] developed the SSI-DDI model, which improves the prediction of adverse drug-drug interactions by focusing on chemical substructure interactions rather than entire drug structures. Unlike traditional methods that rely on manually engineered molecular representations, SSI-DDI utilizes raw molecular graph data to extract detailed features, specifically analyzing pairwise interactions between drug substructures to enhance prediction accuracy. Li et al. [21] proposed DSN-DDI, a dual-view drug representation learning network that increases prediction accuracy by integrating local and global representation learning modules, examining drug substructures from both individual drugs and drug pairs. Recent methods such as MR-GNN [22], GoGNN [23], and DSIL-DDI [24] also emphasize the importance of drug substructures and utilize GNNs’ advanced feature extraction capabilities.

The rapid expansion of large-scale genomic and phenotypic data, combined with information on regulatory-approved small molecular compounds, has significantly advanced drug-drug interaction (DDI) prediction [25]. This process typically uses various drug characteristics and existing knowledge of known DDIs. Key databases in this field include DrugBank [26], Drug Repurposing Knowledge Graph (DRKG) [27], Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [28], Bio2RDF [29], TWOSIDES [30], SIDER [31], PubChem [32], and DrugCentral [33].

In this paper, we present a deep learning method called MDG-DDI(Multi-feature Drug Graph for Drug-Drug Interaction prediction), which extracts substructure features and employs a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) for drug-drug interaction (DDI) prediction. Unlike other methods, MDG-DDI uses two distinct encoders for feature extraction. The main contributions of our model are:

Two unique feature extraction modules: (a) An augmented transformer encoder that effectively identifies and captures semantic relationships among substructures extracted from large-scale, unlabeled biomedical datasets from MolTrans [34]. (b) A Deep Generative Network (DGN) drug-embedding module that generates a representation for each node in a molecular graph [35].

We evaluate our model using three real-world datasets: the Drugbank dataset, which includes 1,635 drugs and 556,757 drug pairs; the ZhangDDI dataset, containing 572 drugs and 48,548 known interactions; and the DS dataset proposed by Han et al. We conduct experiments in a transductive setting where the training and test sets share the same drug pairs for all three datasets. Additionally, to assess the model’s performance on unseen drugs, we perform experiments in an inductive setting using the Drugbank dataset, where the drugs in the training and test sets differ.

FCS and transformer-based embedding modules

SMILES, or Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System, is a notation system that represents chemical structures in a linear text format. It encodes a molecule’s structure-atoms, bonds, and connectivity-into a concise string. For instance, the SMILES notation for ethanol is "CCO". This system allows for the storage and exchange of chemical structures in a simple, compact format. We input the drug’s SMILES sequence, decompose it into substructure sequences using a decomposition algorithm, and then feed these sequences into the augmented transformer embedding module to obtain enhanced contextual embeddings for each substructure.

FCS mining module

To analyze DDI at the substructure level of a drug, we first decompose the drug’s molecular sequence into substructures using the FCS (Frequent Consecutive Sub-sequence) algorithm.

The FCS algorithm functions as follows: It begins by initializing the SMILES string dataset A, labeling it, and creating a labeled set B. Then, it iterates through set B to identify the most frequently contacted markers (M, N). Each pair (M, N) is replaced with a new marker (MN), which is added to dataset A. This process of traversal, identification, and updating continues until no frequent marker exceeds a threshold h or the size of A reaches a predefined maximum. Consequently, we obtain a token that includes all frequent subsequences and a shorter token for those that are insufficiently frequent. For example, the SMILES sequence ‘CCO’ (ethanol) can be decomposed into frequent subsequences such as ‘CC’ and ‘CO’. From this, it can be seen that compared with fingerprint methods that generate overlapping substructures, FCS can identify recurring fragments.

Ultimately, the FCS algorithm produces a collection of substructures of size n, represented as , where each substructure is derived from dataset A.

The FCS method offers two key advantages:

Improved Explainability: Traditional sub-structure fingerprinting methods often create complex, overlapping sets of sub-structures, making it difficult to pinpoint which ones affect specific outcomes. For example, techniques like PubChem encoding can yield around 100 granular sub-structures for a small molecule, many of which overlap, complicating interpretation. In contrast, FCS deconstructs drug molecules into distinct, manageable sub-structures, enhancing clarity. This clear representation supports better analysis and more effective use of extensive unlabeled data for sub-structure discovery. The Transformer Encoder focuses on encoding input sequences, generating contextual representations through multiple layers of self-attention and feed-forward networks. This architecture is widely used in natural language processing and is applied here for molecular representation learning.

GCN-based DDI prediction module

Drug-drug interaction (DDI) prediction is fundamentally a node-pair classification problem on graph-structured data. In the final stage of our model architecture, we employ a classical variant of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)-the Graph Convolutional Network (GCN)-to model potential interaction patterns between drugs. Unlike traditional Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), which are primarily designed for Euclidean data (e.g., images or text), GCNs can directly operate on non-Euclidean structures like molecular graphs, social networks, and knowledge graphs. Recently, GCNs have shown exceptional performance in tasks such as molecular property prediction and protein interaction identification. Since the DDI task naturally forms a graph structure among drugs, we utilize GCNs to capture these complex relational patterns.

Interaction modeling and prediction

After passing through the GCN layers, each drug node’s representation combines structural and semantic features from its neighborhood. To predict drug-drug interactions (DDIs), we concatenate the representations of two drug nodes and input them into a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) classifier. This classifier predicts whether an interaction exists between the drugs. The prediction task can be framed as a binary classification problem (interaction/no interaction) or extended to a multi-class classification setting to identify specific interaction types.

Experiments

Dataset

We evaluated our model using several widely recognized benchmark datasets for DDI prediction tasks. DrugBank, a comprehensive and publicly accessible biomedical database (version 5.1.10), integrates detailed information on drugs and their targets, sourced from journals, databases, and textbooks. It currently contains 16,565 drug entries, including 2,761 approved small molecule drugs, 1,610 biologics, 135 nutraceuticals, and 6,723 experimental compounds. The database also features 5,302 non-redundant protein sequences related to drug targets, enzymes, transporters, and carriers. All data in DrugBank undergo rigorous validation and consistency checks to ensure completeness and accuracy. Each drug entry includes chemical structure data, pharmacological attributes, mechanisms of action, metabolic pathways, and ADME properties (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion). Additionally, detailed descriptions of drug-target interactions, such as inhibition or activation, are provided. It should be noted that in the initial Drugbank dataset, there were only positive samples. We generated negative samples by randomly pairing drugs to construct a balanced dataset. We adopted a positive and negative ratio of 1:2. However, this sampling strategy inevitably leads to an imbalance in categories.

DS is a dataset curated by Han et al., with DDI records sourced from the TWOSIDES database, which includes 1,918 drugs and 211,990 known DDI instances. After mapping the drug entries to a knowledge graph (KG) and filtering for valid SMILES sequences, the dataset was refined to 103,938 DDI pairs across 1,314 drugs.

ZhangDDI [38] is a compact benchmark featuring 572 drugs and 48,548 DDIs. Although it has fewer drugs, molecular fingerprints are available for all entries, making it suitable for structure-based DDI prediction.

Experimental setup

We implemented our proposed model, MDG-DDI, using PyTorch [39] and PyTorch Geometric [40]. The drug feature are set to (1, 64) for the FCS module and (1, 96) for the DGN module. The model is trained using the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of and a batch size of 131,072 DDI pairs, maintaining a positive-to-negative sample ratio of 1:2. The dataset is divided into training, validation, and test sets in a 17:2:1 ratio. Training runs for 4,000 epochs in the transductive setting and 600 epochs in the inductive setting. In the transductive setting, drugs in the training and test sets may overlap. Data partitioning and negative sample generation occur before training. In the inductive setting, the drugs in the training and test sets are distinct, requiring the model to generalize from the training data to accurately predict new drug pairs, which poses a greater challenge than the transductive setting. Hyperparameters are tuned based on the validation set using grid search.

Analysis of results

Our model outperforms all metrics on both datasets, DS and ZhangDDI. In the transductive setting of the DDI prediction experiment on Drugbank, while the model demonstrates strong accuracy (ACC), this does not fully reflect its true effectiveness for several reasons:

Category Imbalance: The Drugbank dataset displays category imbalance, which can distort the ACC and misrepresent the model’s performance. In such cases, ACC may be skewed by majority class samples, leading to an overestimation or underestimation of the model’s true capabilities. In contrast, metrics like AUC, AP, and F1 scores offer a more comprehensive evaluation of model performance on imbalanced data, as they account for the model’s ability to distinguish between positive and negative classes and its predictive performance across various thresholds.

Threshold Sensitivity: ACC depends on a specific classification threshold, and its value can vary significantly based on this choice. In our experiment, the model outputs probabilities, enabling metrics such as AUC, AP, and F1 to evaluate performance more effectively across different thresholds. Relying solely on ACC can obscure the model’s strengths in other areas.

Limitations of Accuracy as a Single Assessment Metric: Accuracy only reflects the proportion of correct predictions made by a model, overlooking essential metrics like precision and recall. In drug-drug interaction (DDI) prediction, it’s crucial to evaluate the model’s ability to identify positive samples and distinguish them from negative ones. Metrics such as AUC, AP, and F1 scores offer a more comprehensive assessment of the model’s performance in these areas.

While the accuracy metric performs reasonably well in the transductive setting on DrugBank, its significance in this experiment is limited. The strong performance of the model across AUC, AP, and F1 metrics indicates its robust ability to accurately identify drug-drug interactions. In the inductive setting, our model excels in AUC, accuracy, and F1 metrics, particularly in predicting unseen drug combinations. Specifically, the AUC, accuracy, and F1 scores of MDG-DDI improve by 0.524.39%, 0.966.58%, and 0.3921.79%, respectively, compared to other models. This showcases our model’s superior accuracy with unseen data, reduced false positives, and enhanced capacity to capture genuine DDIs.

Our model effectively combines the advantages of multiple feature fusion with the strong structure learning capabilities of GCN, demonstrating impressive performance in DDI prediction during experiments.

This highlights the effectiveness of the MDG-DDI strategy, which integrates two feature extraction methods-FCS-Transformer and DGN-along with an optimally designed number of GCN layers to capture the structural information of drugs and model complex drug-drug interactions. By utilizing multidimensional feature extraction and graph convolutional networks, MDG-DDI shows enhanced generalization capabilities with unseen drug combinations, improving the differentiation between positive and negative samples, as well as accuracy and precision-recall balance. In contrast, model variants that either increase the number of GCN layers or depend solely on one feature extraction method do not adequately learn the complex interaction patterns among drugs, resulting in lower AUC, ACC, and F1-score.

Figure 2 presents the experimental results for each model in the transductive setting. We first analyzed how the number of GCN layers impacts model performance. As illustrated, the metrics for all three variants initially increase before declining, with performance dropping after three GCN layers. Although accuracy (ACC) shows slight improvements at certain points, both the area under the curve (AUC) and average precision (AP) significantly decrease. This trend may result from deeper networks capturing more patterns and features, enhancing accuracy on specific training data. However, the decline in AUC and AP indicates that, despite improvements in ACC, the model struggles to maintain reliable classification boundaries on imbalanced data. ACC primarily reflects overall prediction accuracy but may not adequately represent the model’s ability to distinguish minority classes in scenarios of class imbalance. In contrast, AUC and AP evaluate the model’s accuracy in classifying positive and negative samples; their decline suggests decreased precision in assessing minority class samples. This issue may stem from the oversmoothing [47] effect of deeper GCN layers, where increased depth leads to convergence of node representations, diminishing the model’s ability to capture local features and resulting in poorer performance on complex or hard-to-distinguish samples. Consequently, a slight improvement in accuracy may not compensate for the reduced AUC and AP, suggesting that the model’s reliability in practical applications could be compromised. Therefore, the experiment confirms that a 2-layer GCN provides the best balance and is the most suitable configuration.

Next, we evaluated the model’s performance using only one feature extraction method: FCS-Transformer or DGN. As illustrated in Fig. 2, neither method alone matched the performance of the full MDG-DDI model, which integrates both approaches. Specifically, FCS-Transformer resulted in a 3.25% decrease in AUC, while DGN led to a 3% reduction in F1-score. This indicates that single feature extraction methods struggle to capture both global and local information about drug molecules. In contrast, the combination of FCS-Transformer and DGN in the full MDG-DDI model allows for a more thorough extraction of drug features, improving overall DDI prediction accuracy. The integration of two modules fills in the supplementary information that cannot be captured by a single path. The improvement in predictive performance is not merely the simple superposition of models, but rather stems from this complementary effect.The ablation experiments clearly demonstrate that the full MDG-DDI model outperforms all other variants, particularly in AUC and F1-score.

In conclusion, these experiments underscore the contributions of each module in the MDG-DDI model to its overall performance. The carefully selected number of GCN layers was essential for effectively modeling the complex relationships between drugs, while the integration of multiple feature extraction methods further enhanced the model’s predictive capability for drug-drug interactions (DDIs). These findings strongly support the design of the MDG-DDI model and validate the distinct roles of each component in improving performance based on experimental data.

Discussion

In this study, we present the MDG-DDI framework for predicting drug-drug interactions (DDIs). Our experimental results demonstrate the method’s effectiveness and robustness. In practice, MDG-DDI can be applied to early-stage drug screening, off-target risk assessment, and clinical decision support for polypharmacy. By employing two distinct feature extraction modules, FCS-Transformer and DGN, we extract rich, meaningful features from the SMILES sequences of drugs. When these features are fed into a graph convolutional network (GCN), the model shows superior predictive capabilities compared to existing DDI prediction methods, indicating that the MDG-DDI framework effectively captures the complex relationships between drug molecules.

However, some limitations persist. First, the MDG-DDI framework depends on SMILES sequences for input features. While this method excels in structural representation, it may underperform for drugs without standard SMILES expressions or those with complex molecular structures. Second, although GCNs effectively capture information in graph structures, they struggle with ultra-large-scale graphs. Future improvements may necessitate more efficient graph neural network architectures to enhance computational efficiency. Additionally, our experimental data primarily comes from the comprehensive DrugBank dataset; further external validation is crucial for practical applications. Currently, we evaluated generalization only within datasets. A valuable future direction is cross-dataset validation (e.g., training on DrugBank and testing on DS or ZhangDDI), which would better demonstrate the robustness of MDG-DDI. It is worth noting that in our work, no specific case studies have been conducted. This is because case-based validation requires a large amount of manual management of biomedical literature evidence, which is beyond the scope of our current research. We plan to supplement quantitative assessment by combining case studies in the future. While MDG-DDI demonstrates promising improvements in predictive performance, further validation on external clinical datasets is necessary before the framework can be reliably applied in real-world practice.

In the future, we can enhance our research in several areas. First, we should integrate additional drug characteristics, such as protein-drug interactions (PDI) and drug metabolism information, to improve the model’s predictive accuracy and applicability. The Knowledge Graph Embedding (KGE) method has been effectively used in biomedicine to construct drug-protein-target interaction networks, capturing complex biological interactions. Techniques like TransE, RotatE, and R-GCN can uncover potential relationships between drugs and their targets, enhancing the model’s feature representation. By combining these embedded features with our existing drug-drug interaction (DDI) prediction framework, we can enrich drug representation and potentially boost prediction performance.

Secondly, we should address the modeling challenges associated with complex molecular structures and the computational efficiency of optimizing Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN). While GCN excels in molecular graph modeling, increasing network depth can lead to over-smoothing, which results in information loss. In addition, the integration of high-dimensional features can consume excessive computational resources. Future studies should explore more lightweight GCN variants (such as GraphSAGE and GIN) or adopt efficient training strategies (like sampling methods and pruning techniques) to enhance computational efficiency and reduce model complexity.

Additionally, expanding the model’s applicability and validating its performance in realistic scenarios are crucial for future research. Many current DDI predictive studies primarily use laboratory datasets, while real-world clinical drug interactions are more complex and influenced by factors such as individual patient differences, dosage, and time dependence. Therefore, incorporating real-world data (RWD), along with electronic health records (EHRs) or real case data, could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the model’s feasibility and generalization ability. Investigating the model’s performance in inductive learning scenarios will further enhance its potential applications in new drug discovery and drug retargeting tasks.

MDG-DDI stands out from existing methods due to its innovative approach to feature extraction and relationship modeling. However, traditional machine learning and cheminformatics techniques still perform competitively with certain datasets or simpler drug pairs. While MDG-DDI provides a new perspective on drug-drug interaction (DDI) prediction, there is considerable potential for further optimization and expansion.

Conclusion

In this paper, we introduce MDG-DDI, a novel deep learning framework designed for drug-drug interaction (DDI) prediction. The framework consists of two distinct modules for feature extraction: FCS and DGN, which capture complex features from input drug SMILES sequences. These features are concatenated and fed into a graph convolutional network (GCN) that effectively models drug relationships and predicts potential interactions.

Experimental results from three datasets demonstrate MDG-DDI’s strong performance, significantly surpassing previously proposed methods. Notably, it excels in both transductive and inductive settings, underscoring its generalizability across various learning environments.

Ablation studies further confirm MDG-DDI’s robustness, indicating that each component contributes meaningfully to its overall performance. This robustness, combined with high accuracy, positions MDG-DDI as a reliable and powerful tool for DDI prediction, with promising applications in drug safety and development.
