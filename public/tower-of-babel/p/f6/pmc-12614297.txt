A cross-sectional study of macronutrients, vitamins, minerals, and bioactive components in human milk of approved milk bank donors from low-, middle-, and high-income countries
Authors: Maryanne T Perrin, Kimberly Mansen, Kiersten Israel-Ballard, Lars Bode, Daniela Hampel, Setareh Shahab-Ferdows, Lindsay H Allen, Francisca Cofré Maggio, Emily M Njuguna, Hoang Thi Tran, Aleksandra Wesolowska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrently, clinical care of preterm infants is limited by a dearth of information on the nutritional and bioactive composition of donor human milk (DHM).ObjectivesThe primary objective is to assess and compare the macronutrients, vitamins, minerals, human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs), and antimicrobial proteins in human milk from approved milk banks in a variety of low-, middle-, and high-income settings. The secondary objective is to explore factors that influence composition, including gestational stage (preterm compared with term), lactation stage, maternal age, lifetime donation volume, and storage duration.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of human milk collected systematically from unique, approved milk bank donors (n = 600) in Chile, Kenya, Poland, the United States, and Vietnam. True protein, total fat, lactose, HMOs, vitamins, minerals, and antimicrobial proteins were assessed. Analysis of variance and Tukey’s tests were used to evaluate normally distributed data; Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn’s tests were used for nonparametric data. Multiple regression was used to explore factors associated with milk composition.ResultsThe only site with predominantly preterm donors was Kenya (26/50). Kenya also had an early donation model (mean lactation stage 1.6 ± 1.1 wk), whereas the remaining sites had a mature donation model (mean lactation stage ranged 14.2–21.4 wk). Most nutrients differed significantly (P < 0.05) between geographies. For instance, true protein in Kenya was 1.3 ± 0.3 g/dL compared with 0.8 ± 0.2 at all United States banks. Many minerals were higher in the early donation model whereas lactose and several water-soluble vitamins were higher in the mature donation model. Although the lactation stage typically had the most predictive value, most nutrient variation (>60%) remained unexplained.ConclusionsClinical protocols for feeding DHM to preterm infants, including setting-specific fortifier products, may need to take into consideration the local donor milk banking model. Multidonor pooling warrants further investigation as a tool for milk banks to ensure minimum nutrient standards for DHM.

Introduction

When preterm infants do not have access to their mother’s own milk, donor human milk (DHM) is the recommended feeding alternative due to evidence of reduced rates of necrotizing enterocolitis compared with infant formula [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Despite increasing use of DHM globally [5], there is a dearth of information on its nutritional composition, which impedes the ability to optimize nutritional care for the vulnerable neonate. A recent review on the composition of DHM from nonprofit milk banks or commercial entities identified 14 studies [6]. Reviewed studies were predominantly small (<50 samples); represented milk from a single milk bank in high-income settings (United States or Europe); focused on protein, fat, and carbohydrates; and did not use established reference methods for measuring macronutrients, which can lead to systematic over-reporting of protein and available energy. Collectively, the reviewed studies suggested that the protein, fat, and energy content of DHM can vary by 2-fold or more. This agrees with recent studies of donor milk composition in the United States and Australia [7,8]. Noticeably absent in the published literature on DHM composition are studies on vitamins, minerals, and bioactive factors, including lactoferrin, Ig A, and human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) that have been associated with improved infant health outcomes [9,10].

The dearth of information on the nutritional and bioactive composition of DHM—and how donor attributes (e.g., gestation term, lactation stage, maternal age, lifetime donation volume) are associated with composition—is a major barrier to improving DHM feeding therapies for preterm infants. The aim of this study is to assess and compare the macronutrients, vitamins, minerals, HMOs, and antimicrobial proteins in human milk from approved milk banks in a variety of low-, middle-, and high-income settings. A secondary aim is to explore factors that influence composition including gestational stage (preterm compared with term), lactation stage, maternal age, lifetime donation volume, and storage duration.

Methods

Study overview

We conducted a cross-sectional study of approved milk bank donors from 8 geographically diverse settings. An overview of the study design has previously been published [11]. Briefly, we partnered with 8 established milk banks in geographically and income-diverse settings and collected milk samples from 600 approved milk bank donors. The planned settings were: Banco de Leche Hospital Dr. Sótero del Río (n = 50); Pumwani Maternity and Referral Hospital Human Milk Bank (n = 50); Milk Bank at Mother's Memorial Hospital Research, Lodz Institute (n = 50); Da Nang Hospital for Women and Children Learning & Research Center for Human Milk and Newborn Care (n = 50); Mothers’ Milk Bank of the Western Great Lakes (n = 100); Northwest Mothers Milk Bank (n = 100); Mid-Atlantic Mothers’ Milk Bank provided through the Human Milk Science Institute and Biobank (n = 100); and Mothers’ Milk Bank of North Texas (n = 100). The sample size was determined based on multiple factors to support the study’s collective aims. On the basis of findings from the INSPIRE study [12], a sample size of 40–43 would be needed to detect country-level differences in HMOs; thus, our initial goal was to collect 50 samples per site. For United States sites, our goal was to collect a snapshot of 1–2 mo of milk available for donor milk production to enable simulation of different donor pooling scenarios; thus, our final sample size for United States sites was 100 unique donors per site. Sample collection

Each sample reflected milk from a unique, approved milk bank donor that had been collected over multiple days (minimum of 3) and then was pooled together into a single donor pool. This sampling lens is reflective of how most milk banks collect and process DHM, making it generalizable to our study question [13]. Milk banks were instructed to use their normal thawing process, thoroughly mix the single donor pool for 30 s immediately after pooling, and collect a 20 mL sample of raw milk in a tamper-evident specimen containers provided by the study team (P/N 22-039-430; Fisher Scientific). To account for the impact of Holder pasteurization, 50 matched pairs of 100 mL were collected and pasteurized at the Mothers’ Milk Bank of North Texas. A flowchart summarizing sample collection is provided in Supplemental Figure 1, and a flowchart of the overall study schematic has previously been published [11].

Sample collection occurred between October 2021 and January 2023. We experienced unexpected delays with sample collection in Lodz, Poland due to the milk banks involvement in supporting an influx of refugees from the war in Ukraine; thus, we expanded our sample collection in Poland to 2 additional milk banks in Gdańsk and Warsaw to meet our sample targets within the planned study period. Additionally, midway through sample collection at 1 of our partner milk banks, we noticed a dramatic reduction in the fat composition of the samples. On investigation of the collection site, we learned that there had been a deviation in the sample collection process, and samples were being collected at the end of the day after the single donor pools had sat in the refrigerator for several hours. Therefore, these samples were removed from the study, and additional samples (n = 60) were collected using the planned collection protocol. All samples were shipped on dry ice to the University of North Carolina Greensboro where they were thawed using a 35°C shaking dry bath, mixed with a magnetic stirrer, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C for future analysis.

For each sample, we collected meta-information using a sample collection log including: sample collection date; sample ID; donor ID, first expression date in the single donor pool, last expression date in the single pool, maternal birth year, infant birth MMYY; birth term (preterm or term), and lifetime donation volume. Deidentified biospecimens were collected for the study, and this project was classified as nonhuman subjects research by the Institutional Review Board at the University of North Carolina, Greensboro (protocol IRB-FY21-68).

Sample analysis

We assessed a wide range of macronutrients, vitamins, minerals, HMOs, and bioactive proteins in the study samples as previously described [11]. Analysis occurred at Eurofins using Association of Official Analytical Collaboration (AOAC) methods for crude/total protein (AOAC 991.20), nonprotein nitrogen (AOAC 991.21), and true protein (AOAC 991.23) using the modified Kjeldahl technique; University of California, San Diego for HMOs; United States Department of Agriculture, Agriculture Research Service—Western Human Nutrition Research Center for vitamins and minerals; and University of North Carolina, Greensboro for lactose, fat, lactoferrin, and IgA as previously described [11]. Secretory status was determined based on the presence or near absence (<1 μmol/mL) of the HMO 2’FL. To improve rigor and reproducibility, assays were analyzed in replicates, or a subset of blinded replicates was measured. We also included control samples on assays that occurred over many months to monitor potential temporal changes in the assays. Lactoferrin and IgA activity were assessed over 8–9 mo using reagents from the same lot. Significant trends in the control sample were observed (correlation coefficient of 0.83 and −0.64, respectively), suggesting systematic changes in assay reagents over time; therefore, a correction factor was applied to each plate that adjusted the control sample to the overall control mean. A summary of the analytical methods and quality control efforts is available in Supplemental Table 1 [[14], [15], [16]].

Data and statistical analysis

Several values were computed from the sample collection log. The number of days in the pool was computed as (last expression date – first expression date + 1). The average expression date of the sample was computed as (first expression date + (last expression date – first expression date)/2). The lactation stage of the sample was computed as (average expression date - infant birth MMYY) with an assumed day of 15, given that actual birthdate was not collected to ensure deidentification of milk samples. Maternal age was computed as (year of the average expression date of the sample – maternal birth year). Storage duration was computed as (date the sample was received in the laboratory and stored in the −80°C freezer – average expression date).

Data for individual nutrients were available for 595–600 of the samples, depending on the analyte, due to some sample loss that occurred during sample transportation and analyses. This limited amount of missing data still allowed us to reach our target sample size, so the missing data were simply omitted from the analysis for the specific nutrient. Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables. If significant changes were seen in nutrients after Holder pasteurization, raw values were adjusted based on the average change seen during Holder pasteurization. Differences between milk bank locations were assessed with an analysis of variance and a Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons when data were normally distributed, or with Kruskal–Wallis tests and Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons when data were nonparametric. Data were considered to have an approximate normal distribution when skewness was within ± 2 and kurtosis was within ± 4 [17]. Categorical data were evaluated with a chi-square test. A Benjamini–Hochburg correction with a false discovery rate of 0.25 was used, given the large number of outcome variables that were measured in each milk sample (n = 29). This resulted in an adjusted P value of 0.0086 for the first variable when ranked in ascending order of P values.

Stepwise multiple linear regression was used to explore relationships between nutrient composition and variables known to milk banks including lactation stage, birth term, maternal age, lifetime donation volume, and storage duration. A significance level of 0.10 was used for variables to stay in the model, and a type III sum of squares test was used to evaluate variable significance. All analyses were conducted with SAS 9.4 (SAS Corporation), GraphPad Prism 10 (Dotmatics), and Excel v2410 (Microsoft).

In stepwise multiple regression analysis to characterize how common variables known to milk banks—including birth term, lactation stage, maternal age, lifetime donation volume, and storage duration—predict nutrient composition of donors’ milk, we found that for all nutrients, most variability (>60%) was unexplained (Figure 1). Discussion

Macronutrients

In this global study of milk from approved milk bank donors, we observed significant differences in the composition of macronutrients, vitamins, minerals, and bioactive factors that were primarily associated with whether milk banks collected donations in the early postpartum period (1–2 wk after giving birth) or whether they collected mature donations, highlighting the strong impact of lactation stage on the composition of human milk. Declining protein and total HMOs, and increasing lactose and fat concentrations in the early postpartum period are well supported in the literature [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. In our study, Kenya’s donors, which provided donations in the early postpartum period, had higher true protein and total HMOs, and lower lactose compared with sites with a mature donation model, which agrees with the literature. However, we did not observe lower fat concentrations in Kenya, which may be because samples reflected pooled milk across multiple days, and because milk donations may not be collected like research studies in maternal milk composition, where a full-breast expression is often requested to capture the higher fat concentrations in hindmilk [23].

The concentrations of macronutrients that we observed were also relatively similar to those reported in the literature for mothers’ milk, when considering lactation stage and birth term. For example, in a systematic review by Gidrewicz and Fenton [20], preterm milk at 2-wk postpartum averaged 1.9 g/dL crude protein, 1.5 g/dL true protein, 5.7 g/dL of lactose, and 3.5 g/dL of fat. In comparison, we reported 1.8 g/dL crude protein, 1.3 g/dL true protein, 5.9 g/dL lactose, and 2.9 g/dL fat from Kenya’s donors who donated at 1.6 wk postpartum and had predominantly given birth preterm. Our results of mature milk donations from predominantly term donors were also similar to those reported by Gidrewicz and Fenton, with fat being the nutrient that differed the most (3.4 g/dL in Gidrewicz compared with 2.9–3.3 g/dL in our study). As discussed above, a possible explanation for the lower fat that we observed may be related to differences in how milk is typically collected for research studies (full expression of the breast) compared with for milk donations.

Minerals

There is a dearth of information on the micronutrient composition of donor milk [6,24], which is an important contribution of this study. Several minerals were higher in samples from Kenya compared with the other locations including potassium, sodium, copper, iron, selenium, and zinc. Our findings of higher concentrations of many minerals at milk banks with an early donation model agree with reviews on longitudinal changes in human milk composition [25,26], and with a recent longitudinal study by Bilston-John et al. of 81 healthy Australian mothers who gave birth at term. Bilston-John et al. [27] presented downward trends during the first year postpartum in calcium, copper, iron, phosphorus, potassium, selenium, sodium, and zinc, with sodium and selenium increasing again in late lactation. We did not observe higher calcium concentrations in the milk of Kenya donors, which was surprising given the much earlier lactation stage of their donations. Ren et al. [26] also did not observe decreasing calcium concentrations across lactation stages in healthy Chinese mothers. The average calcium concentrations we observed in our 8 settings ranged from 235 to 304 mg/L, which is in line with the median concentration of 252 mg/L reported by Dorea et al. [25,28] in a review of 50 y of research on calcium in human milk, where no factors consistently predicted calcium composition. Importantly, the averages for most minerals in our study, with the exception of selenium and iron, were similar to those reported by Bilston-John and Ren, suggesting that the mineral composition of approved donors’ milk, although frequently influenced by the lactation stage of the donation model, generally looks similar to the milk of healthy individuals feeding their own healthy infants.

Vitamins

Several vitamins in our study exhibited the opposite pattern to minerals, with concentrations lower in Kenya’s donors, and higher at locations with a mature donation model. Specifically, thiamin, niacin, B6 and pantothenic acid were lower in the early donation model and higher in the mature lactation models, which agrees with 2 recent reviews on longitudinal changes in human milk composition [25,26]. Retinol, α-tocopherol, and vitamin B12 were higher in the early donation model compared with the later donation model, which also agrees with recent reviews [25,26,29].

We noted some regional differences in vitamins that have previously been reported as being associated with maternal dietary intake [25]. For example, we observed higher riboflavin and lower choline concentrations in milk from United States donors compared with non-United States donors. Within mature donation models, we observed lower B12 concentrations from Poland donors compared with United States donors, and lower thiamine from Vietnam donors compared with other mature donation sites. As we did not collect dietary information from the donors in our study, we were unable to assess the potential impact of maternal diet. Research on the dietary patterns of milk bank donors is limited. A recent study of 110 approved milk bank donors in Spain used a 5-d food record and reported that donors had lower intake of DHA and vitamin B12 compared with the mothers of hospitalized very preterm infants, and that both groups had low Healthy Eating Index scores (61.9–63.6 of 100) driven by intakes of fruit, vegetables, and dairy that were below the recommendations [30]. More research into dietary and supplement-use patterns of milk bank donors is warranted.

Although reliable reference values for the composition of mother’s milk are currently lacking [31], the Mothers, Infants, and Lactation Quality (MILQ) study [32], which is currently underway, will provide important insights on the composition of human milk from 1000 healthy mothers globally. These data can be used in the future to help further contextualize findings from the Global DHM study.

Factors associated with DHM composition

In our study, lactation stage had the strongest predictive value for the composition of many nutrients in multiday pools of human milk from approved milk bank donors, with birth term, maternal age, and lifetime donation volume also associated to a lesser degree with some nutrients. Mills et al. [33] studied macronutrients in 2966 single donor pools collected from 1175 approved donors to a single milk bank in the United Kingdom between 2011 and 2017 and reported that in addition to lactation stage and birth term, small for gestational age (SGA) status was a significant predictor of protein, fat, and total calories. Similar to our study, Mills found that the combined fixed effects of maternal age, lactation stage, birth term, donation volume, and SGA status explained only 8%–27% of the variation in macronutrients. This suggests that the usefulness of milk attributes as a tool for milk banks to manage the nutritional composition of donor milk may be limited and that investigation into other strategies is needed to ensure minimum nutrient standards for donor milk.

Recently, studies have reported on the impact of multidonor pooling to reduce the variation of some nutrients in DHM [[34], [35], [36]]. Given the high percentage of unexplained variation we found across macronutrients, vitamins, and minerals in the milk of approved milk bank donors, further research on the impact of multidonor pooling is warranted. Multidonor pooling is already used within blood banking for some products like platelets [37], which may serve as a benchmark for implementing rigorous multidonor pooling in milk banking. However, in some settings, multidonor pooling may not be culturally appropriate given concerns about potential milk kinship within the Islamic faith that is further complicated with pooling [38].

Strengths and limitations

There were several strengths to our study. We used a sampling protocol reflective of donor milk banking (approved milk bank donors and single donor pools that included milk donated over multiple days). We included low- and middle-income settings, which often have the highest rates of preterm births and are historically missing from the DHM composition literature. We collected samples using a lens that is generalizable to human milk banking and accounted for the impact of Holder pasteurization using volumes and equipment reflective of a milk bank setting. We utilized trustworthy techniques for assessing nutrients and reported on reproducibility. Limitations include that we studied donations from approved milk bank donors, which are the raw inputs for milk banks to produce DHM; thus, our findings might not reflect additional lacto-engineering that occurs within milk banks. Maternal diet was not assessed as part of the study, although all samples came from healthy donors who had passed the local milk bank screening process.

In conclusion, DHM is a critical feeding intervention for the small vulnerable newborn given its ability to reduce the incidence of necrotizing enterocolitis [2,4]. In this global study of milk from approved milk bank donors, we found significant differences in DHM composition across most nutrients, which were primarily associated with whether the milk bank collected donations predominantly from the early lactation period (1–2 wk postpartum) or from the mature lactation period. True protein and many minerals were higher in the early donation model whereas lactose and several water-soluble vitamins were higher in the late lactation model. Clinical protocols for feeding DHM to the preterm infant, including setting-specific fortifier products, may need to take into consideration the local donor milk banking model. Most variation in donor milk nutrient composition remains unexplained; therefore, multidonor pooling warrants further investigation as a tool for milk banks to ensure minimum nutrient standards.
