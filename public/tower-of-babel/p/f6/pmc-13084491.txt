Measuring the Effects of Furosemide on Lung Mechanics Using Oscillometry in Preterm Infants
Authors: Colm P. Travers, Waldemar A. Carlo, Yashaswini K. Kulkarni, Kimberly M. Armstead, Namasivayam Ambalavanan
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Very preterm infants are at high risk for chronic lung disease, and approximately 85% of preterm infants in US neonatal intensive care units with lung disease receive furosemide despite limited data on benefits and risks [1]. Infant pulmonary function tests, which would enable quantitative measurements of lung disease severity and treatment responses, are difficult to perform [2]. As a result, relatively few trials inform loop diuretic use among preterm infants. In a meta‐analysis of five trials among preterm infants (combined n = 54), furosemide improved pulmonary compliance and resistance [3, 4]. The effect of furosemide may be heterogeneous, with some infants failing to respond and other infants rapidly developing tachyphylaxis [5].

A reproducible and quantifiable measure would improve the efficiency of clinical trials of furosemide and other respiratory treatments. In addition, bedside tests could potentially help individualize care. Respiratory oscillometry measures resistance (R) and reactance (X), a measure of pulmonary stiffness [6, 7]. Non‐invasive oscillometry has been used to quantify lung disease in term and preterm infants [8, 9]. This technique can potentially determine disease phenotype and monitor treatment response. The objective of this study was to test the hypothesis that non‐invasive oscillometry would reproducibly measure the effects of furosemide on lung mechanics in preterm infants. Some results of this study were previously reported at a conference [10].

Methods

This was a single‐center prospective pre‐post study at the regional neonatal intensive care unit at the University of Alabama at Birmingham. This study was approved by the University of Alabama at Birmingham Institutional Review Board (IRB‐300000296) and registered on clinicaltrials.gov (identifier: NCT03346343). Preterm infants with a gestational age of ≥ 22 weeks, not on positive pressure respiratory support, and not on furosemide for at least 7 days who were being administered furosemide for clinical indications were included after parental consent. Oscillometry was performed before treatment, 1–2 h after treatment, and 24 h after treatment with furosemide using a Tremoflo N‐100 Airwave Oscillometry System™ (Thorasys, Montreal).

We calibrated the device daily against a 91.76 cmH2O.s/L test load to account for higher impedance in infant airways, a bacterial/viral filter, and an infant face mask [8, 9]. The mask was placed over the mouth and nose with the infant supine, the head neutral midline, and the chin/jaw supported. After spontaneous breathing flows were visualized, we conducted 8 s measurements by superimposing multiple pseudo‐random prime frequency oscillations from 7 to 41 Hz. Bias flow was not used, given the short measurement duration, and the mask was removed after each measurement to reduce rebreathing. Measurements were repeated at approximately 30‐second intervals up to 10 times until at least three satisfactory reproducible measurements were obtained [6]. Each measurement set was then repeated after approximately 5 min. We considered tests technically acceptable if the coefficient of variation (CV) was < 20% and sets were within a 15% threshold of agreement [8, 9].

The primary outcome was the change in the area under the reactance curve (AX) at 1–2 h after treatment. Secondary outcomes included reactance at 7 Hz (X7), resistance at 7 Hz (R7), the difference in resistance between 7 Hz and 19 Hz (R7‐19) at 1–2 h and 24 h, and the AX at 24 h. We recorded changes in the cumulative percentage time with oxygen saturations (SpO2) < 90%, the average respiratory rate (RR) derived using histograms on bedside monitors, and the effective fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2). We estimated thirty infants were required to detect a 250 cmH2O.s/L or 20% change (1030 cmH2O.s/L pre‐ vs 825 cmH2O.s/L post‐treatment) in AX, with a SD of 320 cmH2O.s/L, correlation of 0.7 between pre‐ and post‐treatment values, power of 90%, α of 5%, and 10% attrition rate. Results were analyzed by paired t‐tests with p values < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Testing was technically adequate and complete in 28/30 infants (93.3%). Infants had a mean ± standard deviation gestational age of 26 ± 2 weeks, birth weight of 733 ± 205 grams, and were enrolled on postnatal day 80 ± 33 at a weight of 2278 ± 738 grams from May 2021 to September 2022 (Table 1). The intra‐infant variability for each test set was excellent (R = 0.92; R2 = 0.85; p < 0.001). The majority (89.3%) received a single dose of enteral furosemide 2 mg/kg.

R7‐19 improved significantly at 1–2 h and at 24 h after furosemide treatment. R7 decreased at 1–2 h after treatment, but the difference was not significant at 24 h. While most infants had improved lung mechanics after furosemide, there were six infants with higher AX at 1–2 h after exposure and ten infants with higher AX at 24 h after exposure compared with baseline. Discussion

In extremely preterm infants not on positive pressure support, furosemide improved lung mechanics at 1–2 h but most differences were no longer statistically significant by 24 h. As anticipated, there was a heterogeneous response to furosemide treatment within and between infants, which may relate to differences in underlying disease phenotype, such as the presence or absence of pulmonary edema, as well as medication dosing and metabolism. The enteral dose used in the majority of infants in this study was relatively modest but consistent with standard recommendations in neonatal formularies to reduce the risk of side effects such as ototoxicity. The overall changes in lung mechanics observed in the current study were consistent with previous studies, which demonstrated that furosemide improved pulmonary compliance and resistance [3, 4]. Prior studies also suggested that the effect peaked at 1 h after administration [4]. Our pre‐post study only followed infants for 24 h but previous studies observed ongoing improvements in compliance and resistance with 7–8 days of furosemide therapy, albeit without improvements in respiratory outcomes [3].

Our study provides proof of concept for using oscillometry in neonatal clinical trials alongside traditional respiratory outcomes such as changes in respiratory support, oxygen requirements, and respiratory morbidities such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia. Our study agrees with studies in older populations suggesting that oscillometry is reproducible, can measure the effectiveness of pulmonary treatments, and could potentially be used to personalize therapies [7]. Future studies are needed to establish the relationship between lung mechanics and disease phenotype among preterm infants. Furthermore, it will be necessary to determine whether the results of non‐invasive oscillometry in infancy predict longer‐term respiratory health and pulmonary function in childhood and adulthood.
