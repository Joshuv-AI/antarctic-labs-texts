DUOX2 activation drives bacterial translocation and subclinical inflammation in IBD-associated dysbiosis
Authors: Hajar Hazime, Gloria Michelle Ducasa, Ana M Santander, Nivis Brito, Eddy Ernesto Gonzalez-Horta, Maria A Quintero, Stephen Barnes, Landon Wilson, Yanping Zhang, Fahong Yu, Raad Z Gharaibeh, Christian Jobin, Katerina M Faust, Oriana M Damas, Amar Deshpande, David H Kerman, Siobhan Proksell, Judith Pignac-Kobinger, Irina Fernández, Juan F Burgueño, Maria T Abreu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs) are characterised by dysbiosis and a leaky gut. The NADPH oxidase dual oxidase 2 (DUOX2) is upregulated in patients with IBD, yet its role in driving the disease remains unclear.ObjectiveWe interrogated the functional consequences of epithelial DUOX2 activity for the host and microbiome.DesignDUOX2 function was studied in mice with epithelial-specific DUOX2 overactivation (vTLR4), inactivation (vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO) and wild-type controls. We assessed the effect of dysbiosis on DUOX2 signalling and intestinal permeability (FITC-dextran, serum zonulin, bacterial translocation) with germ-free (GF) mice engrafted with IBD or healthy microbiota. RNA sequencing of colonic mucosa and microbiota and faecal metabolomics were used to characterise the host–microbe interface. Mechanistic studies were conducted in mouse colonoids, IBD biopsies and patient serum samples.ResultsDUOX2 activity increased permeability and bacterial translocation and induced subclinical inflammation in vTLR4 mice. GF vTLR4 mice had increased DUOX2 activity and permeability but no subclinical inflammation. In patients with IBD, DUOX2 expression was positively associated with plasma zonulin levels and negatively associated with ZO-1 expression. Engraftment of GF mice with IBD stool increased DUOX2 activity and triggered low-grade inflammation and permeability defects in mice. DUOX2 activity functionally altered the microbiome, reduced butyrate metabolism and promoted proinflammatory and pro-oncogenic bacterial metabolites. Butyrate and histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors blocked DUOX2 activation and reversed its effects.ConclusionsElevated DUOX2 signalling contributes to epithelial barrier dysfunction, microbiome alterations and subclinical inflammation. Butyrate and HDAC inhibitors reversed these effects, indicating that DUOX2 may be a therapeutic target in IBD.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study demonstrates that chronic DUOX2 activity promotes dysbiosis and subclinical inflammation. We show, for the first time, that DUOX2 directly increases intestinal permeability, an early marker of IBD. Additionally, we demonstrate that the microbial metabolite butyrate, which is reduced in IBD, can block DUOX2 activity and reverse its effects. This positions DUOX2 as a critical therapeutic target for addressing leaky gut and inflammation in IBD.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) are characterised by chronic and relapsing inflammation of unknown aetiology. One key driver in the onset of inflammation in IBD is dysregulation of the host–gut microbiota relationship. Several factors are implicated in this process, including abnormal microbial composition, impaired epithelial barrier function and chronic activation of innate and adaptive immune pathways.1 While the sequence of events preceding IBD is poorly defined,2 increased intestinal permeability3 and specific antimicrobial antibodies in serum predict Crohn’s disease (CD) development.4 These observations suggest that a leaky gut and microbial exposure precede inflammation; however, the inter-relationship of these events is not fully understood.

Gut dysbiosis, an imbalance in the function or composition of the gut microbiome, increases susceptibility to inflammation.1 2 5 IBD-related dysbiosis, which is evident even at the initial diagnosis of IBD,6 is characterised by an expansion of facultative anaerobes from the phylum Proteobacteria at the expense of strict anaerobes from the phylum Firmicutes.7 8 A lower relative abundance of Firmicutes reduces the availability of microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including butyrate, which play immunomodulatory and protective roles in preclinical models of inflammation.9 10 The overabundance of Proteobacteria enhances innate immune receptor signalling, such as by toll-like receptors (TLRs).11

We previously showed that patients with IBD exhibit increased TLR4 expression and that TLR4 signalling can increase susceptibility to IBD and colitis-associated cancer.12 13 One of the most overexpressed genes downstream of TLR4 signalling is DUOX2.14 This epithelial NADPH oxidase interacts with the maturation factor DUOXA2 to catalyse the conversion of oxygen into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2).15 16 DUOX2 plays an antimicrobial role17 18 and impacts physiological functions.19 Increased expression of DUOX2 is an early event in patients with IBD, including newly diagnosed children20 and adults.8 A higher relative abundance of Proteobacteria in the gut is associated with DUOX2 upregulation in IBD patient biopsies.8 We recently demonstrated that colonic epithelial cells (CECs) activate DUOX2 in response to adherent, invasive Escherichia coli (AIEC, phylum Proteobacteria),14 an IBD pathobiont, but not in response to Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (phylum Firmicutes),14 21 a butyrate-producing commensal. Moreover, we showed that chronic activation of DUOX2 increases susceptibility to colorectal cancer.14 However, the mechanisms by which increased DUOX2 activity leads to IBD or IBD-related pathology remain unclear. We posit that DUOX2 activation is a critical early event in IBD that triggers chronic inflammation.

In the present study, we used transgenic and gnotobiotic mouse models to interrogate the functional consequences of chronic DUOX2 activation in the gut. Our findings reveal that dysregulated DUOX2 activity leads to functional changes in the microbiome and the host. We establish a novel association between heightened DUOX2 activity and impaired function of the gut epithelial barrier. The dysbiotic IBD microbiome, in steady-state conditions, can induce dysregulated DUOX2 signalling, low-grade inflammation and epithelial barrier defects in germ-free (GF) mice. We present new evidence that increased DUOX2 expression in biopsies from ulcerative colitis (UC) patients correlates with markers of epithelial barrier dysfunction. Lastly, we demonstrate that the microbial metabolite butyrate, which is reduced in IBD,22 can effectively inhibit DUOX2 both in vivo and in vitro, reversing the observed epithelial barrier defects.

Methods

Human tissue acquisition

Colonoscopic biopsies in RNAlater were taken from five actively inflamed (active) and five non-inflamed (inactive) UC patients and processed to obtain RNA (online supplemental table 1). Stool and serum samples were collected from the same UC patients and healthy subject (HS) controls. Healthy controls were selected based on the absence of any history of gastrointestinal disease or known diagnosis of systemic inflammatory disease. Stool samples were aliquoted in an anaerobic chamber (Coy) on the same day as collection and stored at −80°C.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this research.

Animals

Villin-TLR4 (vTLR4) mice expressing the CD4-TLR4 transgene23 under the villin promoter were generated.24 In this model, constitutive activation of TLR4 drives high DUOX2 expression and activity, as measured by H2O2 production, even in the absence of microbial ligands.14 vTLR4 mice were backcrossed with epithelial specific DUOXA1/A2-KO mice to generate villin-TLR4 DUOXA-knockout (KO) mice as previously described.14 All mice (male and female, aged 8–16 weeks) were age-matched, manually randomised and housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a controlled temperature of 20°C±2°C and provided with free access to food and water.

GF mice and microbial engraftment

GF wild-type (WT) and vTLR4 mice were generated at the University of Miami Gnotobiotic Facility. Microbial engraftment was performed by transferring stool from UC patients or HS controls into recipient GF mice (two mice per patient). Frozen human stool was mixed with sterile Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS; 1:10 ratio of stool weight to HBSS) and filtered with a 40 µm strainer in an anaerobic chamber. GF mice were then given an oral gavage containing 200 µL of the resulting stool slurry and housed in separate iso-cages depending on the donor microbiome. Mice were euthanised 3 weeks after engraftment.

Sodium butyrate supplementation

vTLR4 mice and their vWT littermates were separated and given at least 5 days to adapt prior to starting experiments. The treatment group received 150 mM sodium butyrate (156-54-7, Cayman Chemical) added to their drinking water for 14 consecutive days; this solution was replaced every other day.

Permeability assessment

Mice underwent food restriction for 12 hours before receiving a 600 mg/kg oral gavage of 4 kilodaltons fluorescein isothiocyanate–dextran (FD4). 4 hours later, blood was collected, and fluorescein isothiocyanate fluorescence (485/528 nm ex/em) in plasma was measured in a Synergy H1 fluorometer (BioTek). Plasma or serum zonulin levels were measured in mouse or human samples, respectively, with commercially available ELISA kits (human: Elabscience, ABIN6962693; mouse: MyBioSource, MBS2603528). All samples were assayed in triplicate. Genes associated with intestinal permeability were assessed via quantitative PCR (see online supplemental methods and online supplemental table 2 for the gene list and primer sequences).

Bacterial translocation assessment

Homogenised livers were cultured on tryptic soy agar with 5% sheep blood plates (Lonza) and incubated under aerobic and anaerobic conditions for 24–48 hours. Colonisation is expressed as the average number of colony-forming units per milligram of tissue.

RNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomic analyses

RNA was extracted from colonic tissue and sequenced. Reads were mapped and counted for the determination of differential gene expression and pathway analysis. Untargeted metabolomic analyses were performed with snap-frozen stool samples (see online supplemental methods).

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Data were analysed and figures were generated with GraphPad Prism V.10 (GraphPad Software). Normality of data was determined with Shapiro-Wilk tests. Normally distributed data were log-transformed and subsequently analysed with parametric tests including t-tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and two-way ANOVA; non-normally distributed data were analysed with χ2 and Mann-Whitney U tests. The results are presented as means and SDs, and p values are reported for each analysis and comparison. The threshold for statistical significance was set at a p≤0.05. All authors had access to the study data and have reviewed and approved the final manuscript.

Results

Chronic DUOX2 activity increases gut permeability and promotes bacterial translocation

We hypothesised that DUOX2 activation contributes to increased intestinal permeability, an event associated with IBD risk.3 To test this hypothesis, we took mice with DUOX2 overactivation driven by constitutive TLR4 signalling (vTLR4 mice) and created a functional deficiency of DUOX2 by knocking out its maturation factor in intestinal epithelial cells (vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO mice). Thus, we had mice with epithelial-specific DUOX2 overactivation (vTLR4), inactivation (vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO) and WT controls (vWT) (figure 1A). Next, we orally administered FD4 to these mice and assessed fluorophore leakage from the gut and bacterial translocation to the liver (figure 1B). At baseline, vTLR4 mice had higher plasma levels of FD4 (figure 1C, n=8, p<0.0001) and greater bacterial translocation to the liver (figure 1D, n=8, p<0.0001) than vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO mice and vWT littermates.

To better understand the potential cause of this increased intestinal permeability, we analysed the expression of genes encoding tight junction proteins in the colonic mucosa of our mice, as these genes are surrogates of epithelial barrier integrity.25 vTLR4 mice had lower transcript levels of Jama and Zo-1 than vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO and vWT littermates (figure 1E, n=5, p<0.0001). To support these transcriptional findings with a functional assessment, we measured plasma zonulin levels (positively associated with paracellular permeability).26 27 vTLR4 mice had higher plasma zonulin levels than vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO and vWT littermates (figure 1F, n=8, p<0.0001), suggesting that the epithelial barrier is disrupted in these mice and is rescued on DUOX2 inactivation. Taken together, these data demonstrate the novel finding that DUOX2 activity drives alterations in paracellular permeability, leading to increased translocation of bacteria to distant organs.

Chronic DUOX2 activity induces proinflammatory and pro-oncogenic pathways

To characterise the functional consequences of sustained DUOX2 activation, we performed RNA sequencing of the colonic mucosa of vWT, vTLR4 and vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO mice in steady-state conditions (figure 1A). Differential gene expression analysis revealed that, compared with littermates, the colonic mucosa of vTLR4 mice had increased expression of redox-associated and proinflammatory genes, such as Nos2, Duoxa2, Duox2, Nox1, Saa1, Tnfα, Lcn2 and Cxcl1 (figure 2A). We also observed an upregulation of genes involved in mucosal homeostasis and epithelial barrier integrity, such as Muc1, Reg3g and Myl7 (figure 2A). Interestingly, we observed a marked downregulation of interferon-regulated genes in the colonic mucosa of vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO mice compared with vTLR4 mice (figure 2B), indicating that these factors are directly associated with DUOX2 activation.

We next examined the biological processes and molecular pathways altered in these mice. Differentially expressed genes in vTLR4 mice mapped to redox stress, neutrophil activation and degranulation, proinflammatory cytokine signalling, DNA damage and cytoskeleton reorganisation pathways (figure 2C). vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO mice exhibited downregulation of pathways associated with innate immune responses to cytokines such as type I and II interferons, tumour necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)1β or tyrosine phosphorylation of STAT, and regulation of tyrosine kinases (figure 2D). Consistently, the expression of genes encoding the proinflammatory cytokines Tnf and Il1β and the myeloid cell-recruiting chemokines Cxcl1 and Cxcl2 was upregulated in the CECs of vTLR4 mice and significantly downregulated after abrogation of DUOX2 activity (figure 2E, n=8).

Given these RNAseq findings, we next asked whether vTLR4 mice exhibited greater evidence of inflammation than vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO and vWT littermates. There were no histological signs of inflammation across the groups (online supplemental figure 1). Interestingly, vTLR4 mice had higher plasma levels of serum amyloid A1 (SAA) as well as CXCL1 and CXCL2 than vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO and vWT littermates (figure 2F, n=8–10). Collectively, these findings indicate that persistent DUOX2 activity fosters molecular and biochemical signs of inflammation that may enhance the susceptibility to overt inflammation in response to stressors.

Microbiota are required for subclinical inflammation but not DUOX2-mediated barrier defects

To determine whether the mechanism by which DUOX2 activity leads to a leaky gut in vTLR4 mice is dependent on the microbiota, we reared the same mouse genotypes (vTLR4 and vWT) in GF versus SPF conditions and administered oral FD4 and assessed plasma zonulin levels. H2O2 levels were elevated in vTLR4 mice in both GF and SPF conditions compared with WT mice (figure 3A, n=5). This persistent DUOX2 activation, as reflected by higher H2O2 levels, in GF vTLR4 mice is due to their constitutively active TLR4 signalling in intestinal epithelial cells, which drives Duox2 expression independently of microbial ligands.24 Our results show that GF vTLR4 mice had higher plasma levels of FD4 (figure 3B, n=6, p=0.0006) and zonulin (figure 3C, n=6, p=0.0003) than their GF vWT littermates. These data show that microbial signals are not needed to cause DUOX2-mediated epithelial barrier defects.

To extend these results and exclude developmental effects of the GF state, we asked whether depletion of the microbiota impacted the DUOX2-mediated effects on epithelial barrier function. Antibiotics were used to deplete the microbiome of vWT, vTLR4 and vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO mice (online supplemental figure 2A). Again, we found that microbiome-depleted vTLR4 mice had more H2O2 activity (online supplemental figure 2B) and epithelial barrier defects (online supplemental figure 2C,D) than microbiome-depleted vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO mice and vWT littermates. These data corroborate our findings that dysregulated DUOX2 activity is sufficient to alter paracellular permeability.

We then asked whether a leaky gut is sufficient to activate inflammatory pathways downstream of DUOX2 function in the absence of the gut microbiota. CECs isolated from GF vTLR4 mice showed marked downregulation of Tnf, Il1β, Cxcl1 and Cxcl2 compared with those isolated from SPF vTLR4 mice (figure 3D, n=6, p<0.0001) and similar to those of GF vWT mice. Plasma levels of CXCL1 and CXCL2 were also decreased (figure 3E, n=5–8, p<0.0001). These data demonstrate that DUOX2 activation is necessary for epithelial permeability changes, while microbiota-derived signals are required to amplify cytokine production and drive subclinical inflammation.

DUOX2 expression correlates with impaired epithelial barrier function in patients with IBD

To further examine the relationship between DUOX2 activity and epithelial barrier integrity in IBD, we compared samples obtained from UC patients with inactive and active disease as well as HS controls (figure 4A, online supplemental table 1). Active UC patients had higher faecal calprotectin levels than inactive UC patients and HS controls (figure 4B, n=4–5). Biopsies from active UC patients had higher transcript levels of DUOX2 and DUOXA2 as well as lower transcript levels of ZO-1 than those from inactive UC patients (figure 4C, n=4–5). Serum zonulin levels were significantly higher in active UC patients than in inactive UC patients and HS controls (figure 4D, n=4–5), indicating that DUOX2 upregulation is accompanied by epithelial barrier dysfunction in patients with IBD. Our results showed that DUOX2 expression in colonic biopsies was positively correlated with faecal calprotectin levels (figure 4E, r=0.8468, p=0.002) and plasma zonulin levels (figure 4F, r=0.8322, p=0.005) in UC patients. Taken together, these data indicate that DUOX2 activity is related to epithelial barrier defects in patients with IBD; our study is the first to report this association.

IBD-related dysbiosis induces epithelial DUOX2 and increases epithelial permeability in GF mice

To provide further insight into the causal relationship between dysbiosis and increased mucosal expression of DUOX2, we engrafted GF mice with stool from the same HS controls and UC patients used above (figure 5A). GF mice engrafted with stool from active UC patients had higher levels of H2O2 (figure 5B, n=5–8) and higher expression of Duox2 and Duoxa2 (figure 5C, n=5–8) than GF mice engrafted with stool from inactive UC patients or HS controls. This finding demonstrates that the degree of DUOX2 response reflects donor inflammation status and can be transferred with the microbiota.

To address whether the IBD microbiota recapitulated other early manifestations of IBD, we examined epithelial barrier function in these mice. We found that mice engrafted with stool from active UC patients had higher plasma levels of FD4 (figure 5D, n=5–10) and zonulin (figure 5E, n=5–10) and greater bacterial translocation to the liver (figure 5F, n=5–10) than mice engrafted with stool from inactive UC patients or HS controls. Notably, DUOX2 activity was positively correlated with plasma levels of both FD4 (figure 5G) and zonulin (figure 5H), suggesting that DUOX2 activity contributes to epithelial barrier defects.

Finally, we examined whether the engrafted microbiome influenced inflammation in our mice. Engraftment with UC microbiota did not induce overt inflammation, as determined by histologic assessment (online supplemental figure 3) or myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity (figure 5I, n=5–10). We found no difference in plasma SAA levels (figure 5J n=5–10). Despite the absence of neutrophils by histology or biochemical measures (MPO), mice engrafted with stool from active UC patients had higher plasma levels of CXCL1 (figure 5K, n=5–10) and CXCL2 (figure 5L, n=5–10) than mice engrafted with stool from inactive UC patients or HS controls. Taken together, these data suggest a close interrelationship of the IBD microbiota and DUOX2 induction that leads to the transfer of epithelial barrier defects and low-grade inflammation without the need for other exogenous triggers.

Increased DUOX2 activity drives functional alterations in the microbiome and metabolome

Next, we investigated whether the increased epithelial production of H2O2 by DUOX2 alters the function of the mucosa-associated microbiota (MAM). We analysed the meta-transcriptomic profile of the MAM from vWT, vTLR4 and vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO mice. Differential gene expression analysis of microbial RNA-seq data revealed distinct gene expression patterns in the MAM of each genotype (online supplemental figure 4A,B). Compared with vWT mice, vTLR4 mucosal microbiota exhibited upregulation of genes encoding flagellin and proteins related to the response to H2O2-induced stress in the microbiota, such as catalase and arginine decarboxylase (figure 6A). Microbial genes encoding flagellin and other structural components of the flagellum as well as NADH peroxidases, 3-hydroxybutyryl-CoA dehydrogenase, and OmpA family protein were also upregulated in vTLR4 mice compared with vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO mice (figure 6B). These data indicate that DUOX2 dysregulation is associated with functional changes in the MAM that are linked with pathogenetic mechanisms of IBD.4 28 29

To further investigate functional changes in the microbiota, we analysed the faecal metabolomic profile of these mice. Untargeted metabolomic profiling of stool samples revealed that vTLR4 mice have a metabolomic signature distinct from that of vWT and vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO mice (online supplemental figure 4C,D). vTLR4 stool showed higher proportions of cholic and taurocholic acid(s) as well as lower amounts of inosine than vWT and vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO stool (figure 6C,D). Pathway analysis of the metabolomic data revealed enrichment of arginine and proline metabolism, citric acid biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism and fatty acid degradation in vTLR4 mice compared with vWT mice (figure 6E). Interestingly, pathways associated with the Warburg effect, which confers a growth advantage in tumour cells,30 were also enriched in vTLR4 mice compared with vWT littermates (figure 6E). Compared with vTLR4 DUOXA IEC-KO mice, vTLR4 mice demonstrated enrichment of pathways related to riboflavin, vitamin B6, arginine and proline metabolism as well as downregulation of pathways related to purine, methionine, nicotinic acid/nicotinamide and butyrate metabolism (figure 6F). In general, inactivating DUOX2 shifted the metabolomic signature of vTLR4 mice towards that of vWT mice (online supplemental figure 4E). Taken together, these data demonstrate that sustained DUOX2 activation shapes the microbiome at a functional level. It leads to the production of inflammatory and pro-oncogenic metabolites and alters pathways important for homeostasis.

Butyrate inhibits the DUOX2-mediated H2O2 response and restores barrier integrity

IBD-related dysbiosis reduces the production of SCFAs, including butyrate.8 Our untargeted metabolomic data suggested that SCFA metabolism pathways are decreased in vTLR4 mice. Butyrate can inhibit proinflammatory pathways in the intestinal epithelium31 32; therefore, we asked whether butyrate could repress DUOX2 activity. We preincubated WT mouse colonoids with 1 mM sodium butyrate and subsequently challenged them with a microbial (heat-killed AIEC) or inflammatory (IFNγ) stimulus (figure 7A). Butyrate significantly reduced the release of H2O2 as well as the gene expression of Duox2 and Duoxa2 in response to both stimuli (figure 7B,C, n=6, p<0.0001).

To corroborate these findings in vivo, we supplemented vTLR4 mice and their vWT littermates with 150 mM sodium butyrate for 3 weeks (figure 7D). Butyrate supplementation did not induce histological inflammation (online supplemental figure 5). Strikingly, butyrate supplementation reduced DUOX2 activity (figure 7E, n=5–7, p<0.0001) and expression (figure 7F,G, n=5–7, p<0.0001) and reversed epithelial barrier leakage (figure 7H–I, n=6, p<0.0001), preventing bacterial translocation to the liver (figure 7J, n=6, p<0.0001). Furthermore, butyrate also suppressed the subclinical inflammation of vTLR4 mice by markedly downregulating Tnf, Cxcl1 and Cxcl2 expression in CECs of sodium-butyrate supplemented vTLR4 mice (figure 7K, n=5–7, p<0.0001). These results suggest that bacterial metabolites such as SCFAs modulate DUOX2 expression and activity. Reduction of butyrate in patients with IBD may exacerbate DUOX2 activation and barrier dysfunction downstream of innate immune pathways.

The immunomodulatory effects of butyrate can occur through different mechanisms, including inhibition of class I and class II histone deacetylases (HDACs).33 34 To interrogate the effects of selective HDAC inhibition on DUOX2 modulation, we preincubated WT mouse organoids with the class I and class IIa HDAC inhibitors UF010 and TMP269 before challenging them with AIEC and IFNγ. Inhibition of both class I and class IIa HDACs mimicked the effect of butyrate and decreased DUOX2 activity in response to both microbial and inflammatory stimuli (figure 7L, n=8). In addition, the expression of Duox2 and Duoxa2 transcripts was also downregulated (figure 7M, n=8). These results indicate that butyrate may inhibit DUOX2 induction through HDAC inhibition.

Discussion

IBD is characterised by chronic inflammation driven by dysregulated host–microbiome interactions and epithelial barrier dysfunction.3 4 6 DUOX2 is consistently upregulated in patients with IBD—even at the time of diagnosis,8 20 but its exact contribution to IBD pathogenesis remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that chronic DUOX2 activation drives epithelial barrier dysfunction, dysbiosis and low-grade inflammation. We further show that inhibiting DUOX2 activity restores barrier integrity and reduces inflammation. These findings support the development of translational approaches to modulate DUOX2 to improve gut integrity and treat IBD.

Since DUOX2 is a key downstream effector of TLR4 signalling,14 we used the vTLR4 mouse model. In this model, constitutive activation of TLR4 drives high DUOX2 activity, as measured by H2O2 production, even in the absence of microbial ligands. We compared vTLR4 mice to mice in which we eliminated epithelial DUOX2 function to define this enzyme’s functional impact in an IBD-like context. We demonstrated that DUOX2 activity disrupts epithelial barrier integrity, leading to increased permeability and bacterial translocation to the liver. Notably, histological assessment revealed no overt inflammation, suggesting that DUOX2-driven barrier dysfunction alone is not sufficient to induce spontaneous colitis. However, DUOX2 activation created a primed inflammatory state, marked by elevated levels of CXCL1, CXCL2 and the acute phase protein SAA1. While SAA1 levels were higher in vTLR4 mice (10 ng/mL) than in other groups, they remained far below those observed during overt DSS-induced colitis in C57Bl/6 WT mice, where plasma SAA1 levels can reach up to 2000 ng/mL.35 The mean plasma levels in vTLR4 mice reflect a subclinical inflammatory state, yet this low-grade inflammatory state can set the stage for more severe inflammation in response to additional triggers. In a similar fashion, first-degree relatives of patients with IBD exhibit increased gut permeability without clinical symptoms or overt inflammation.3

To examine how the microbiome influences DUOX2 signalling and barrier function in our model, we used GF and antibiotic-depleted vTLR4 mice. These mice also displayed increased intestinal permeability, confirming that DUOX2-mediated barrier dysfunction can occur independently of the presence of microbes or their products. Despite the presence of barrier defects, GF vTLR4 mice did not exhibit the inflammatory markers (SAA1, CXCL1 and CXCL2) observed in microbiota-intact vTLR4 mice, indicating that while DUOX2 activity alone is sufficient to impair the epithelial barrier, local and systemic microbial exposure is necessary to trigger cytokine production.36 These findings, together with our results in microbiota-colonised mice, reinforce the role of DUOX2 in barrier dysfunction while underscoring the microbiome’s critical role in eliciting inflammation.

To assess the impact of the IBD microbiome on DUOX2 and barrier function, we colonised GF mice with stool from HS and UC patients. Increased epithelial permeability and elevated inflammatory cytokines in the serum were observed only in the UC-engrafted group, confirming that these effects were not simply due to the engraftment process itself. Notably, histological assessment confirmed the absence of significant inflammation, indicating that while UC microbiota transfer does not immediately induce overt colitis, it can promote low-grade barrier dysfunction and systemic inflammation. These findings align with prior studies showing that the IBD-associated microbiota do not necessarily trigger spontaneous colitis but instead contribute to a proinflammatory state, increasing susceptibility to inflammation on additional triggers.5 37 These results suggest that even in the absence of active colitis, UC-associated microbiota can affect barrier integrity, lowering the threshold for inflammation. This highlights the potential benefit of intervening early to correct IBD-associated dysbiosis before it contributes to disease progression.

Using RNA sequencing and metabolomic profiling, we identified distinct functional shifts in the MAM and metabolome of vTLR4 mice. DUOX2 activation was associated with microbial stress responses, including increased expression of catalase and arginine decarboxylase (enzymes that counteract oxidative stress). Additionally, we observed increased flagellin expression, upregulation of genes involved in bacterial virulence (OmpA), and altered metabolic pathways, including elevated levels of cholic and taurocholic acids, which have been implicated in inflammation6 38 and carcinogenesis.3941 Notably, antibodies to flagellin and bacterial outer membrane proteins are found in advance of a CD diagnosis,6 suggesting that the microbial changes driven by DUOX2 activation may contribute to disease-relevant microbial dysregulation. These findings not only highlight how DUOX2 activation drives disease-relevant microbial dysregulation but also underscore the need for microbiome-based therapies that address both microbial composition and host-derived signals. Since DUOX2 activation disrupts barrier integrity and reshapes the microbiome, effective interventions must target both in tandem to restore gut homeostasis.

Finally, given the well-established link between microbial-derived metabolites and epithelial function, we tested whether butyrate, a major SCFA reduced in IBD, could modulate DUOX2 activity. Butyrate supplementation inhibited DUOX2 expression and H₂O₂ production, restored barrier integrity and suppressed inflammatory markers. Mechanistically, we found that butyrate mimicked the effects of HDAC inhibitors, suggesting that butyrate’s impact on DUOX2 may be mediated through HDAC inhibition. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of microbiome-derived interventions that modulate DUOX2 without causing immunosuppression.

Our study had several limitations. Establishing causality in human IBD remains challenging—whether dysbiosis drives DUOX2 activation or vice versa. We propose that a feed-forward loop perpetuates epithelial barrier dysfunction and chronic inflammation, but future studies are needed to further dissect this relationship. Another limitation is the potential influence of IBD medications. While we accounted for the presence or absence of histological inflammation, biologics and small molecules may indirectly affect DUOX2 expression. Overall, larger human studies exploring DUOX2 expression in IBD and its connection to epithelial barrier integrity are warranted

In conclusion, our bedside-to-bench approach establishes a direct mechanistic link between DUOX2 activation and epithelial barrier dysfunction using translationally relevant models, including gnotobiotic mice engrafted with patient stool. These findings provide a foundation for future research into microbiome-based interventions that target DUOX2, offering new avenues for improving gut health in IBD.
