Toxoplasma gondii infection diagnosed by tNGS in a patient with acute lymphoblastic leukemia: a case report
Authors: Fengfan Li, Wei He, Kexin Ren, Jiefei Chen, Jialiang Zhang, Xin Liu, Ziyang Li, Min Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundToxoplasma gondii (T. gondii) is an obligate intracellular protozoan parasite. In the context of hematological malignancies, the immunosuppression induced by chemotherapy and the underlying disease state render patients highly vulnerable to T. gondii reactivation. The consequences of such infections can be extremely severe, often leading to life-threatening complications such as toxoplasmic encephalitis (TE). There are few reports on the detection of T. gondii infection in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) by targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS).Case presentationA 5-year-old girl diagnosed with ALL developed an unexplained fever and cough during the course of chemotherapy. Initial routine laboratory tests failed to identify the causative pathogen. Due to the persistence of symptoms and the patient’s immunocompromised state, further investigations were pursued. tNGS of blood and CSF unexpectedly revealed T. gondii sequences. Brain MRI demonstrated abnormal signals, including ring-line high signal, which are characteristic of TE. Based on these findings, a definitive diagnosis of TE was established. The patient was promptly initiated on a combination therapy of trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (SMZ-TMP) and clindamycin, and the patient responded well to the treatment and subsequently discharged in stable condition and continued on maintenance therapy to prevent relapse.ConclusionsThis case highlights the timely use of tNGS, a new tool with fast speed, high accuracy, and cost-effectiveness that can help identify atypical infections and start early therapy. Moreover, tNGS has great potential in various other clinical scenarios. In critically ill patients with complex infections where time is of the essence, tNGS can provide a comprehensive pathogen profile in a short time.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11771-z.Keywords: Case report, Toxoplasma gondii, Targeted next-generation sequencing, Precision treatment, Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole

Background

For patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), chemotherapy administration is an effective treatment strategy. However, a major concern associated with chemotherapy is the increased risk of immunosuppression, particularly from common bacterial, viral, parasitic, and fungal pathogens, as well as opportunistic infections with low-pathogenic organisms, which may occur due to either newly acquired infections or reactivation of latent infections [1]. Infections caused by microorganisms are life-threatening complications in patients treated for their cancer with chemotherapy and other modalities [2].

T. gondii is an obligate intracellular protozoan parasite that infects nucleated cells of warm-blooded animals, including humans [3]. Human infections are acquired from contaminated food or water, and it is estimated that this parasite persists chronically in 25%–30% of the global human population [4]. Patients with haematological malignancies might develop life-threatening T. gondii [5]. Reactivation of latent cysts is the primary mechanism of toxoplasmosis following ALL. However, identifying the pathogens in central nervous system (CNS) infections using conventional methods such as culture, PCR, and serological tests may be laborious [6, 7]. In addition, atypical symptoms and a lack of knowledge can further increase diagnostic difficulties. tNGS is a novel approach that enables the identification of thousands of pathogens in a single run and enhances sensitivity over untargeted approaches [8]. The role of this innovative detection method in clinical pathogen diagnostics has matured over time. Accumulating evidence has shown that tNGS may help identify the etiological agent when conventional methods have failed [8, 9]. One study [10] showed that in cancer patients with pneumonia, compared with conventional microbiological tests (CMT), tNGS showed significantly higher sensitivity (84.62% vs. 56.92%) and diagnostic accuracy (85.81% vs. 62.16%). The tNGS method identified more pathogens than the CMT method (87.50% vs. 57.14%), whether they were bacteria, fungi or viruses. In addition, tNGS outperforms metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) for cost and turnaround time [11, 12].

To the best of our knowledge, the application of tNGS to detect TE has not been reported in patients with ALL. In this particular research, clinicians employed tNGS to investigate a suspected CNS infection in a child with ALL, and unexpectedly detected T. gondii.

Case description

On June 8th, 2023, a 5-year-old patient was diagnosed with ALL and admitted to our hospital’s Department of Pediatrics for chemotherapy. The VDLD regimen (vindesine, daunorubicin, L-asparaginase and prednisone) was used for induction chemotherapy. She completed the chemotherapy and was discharged on July 16th, 2023. There was no fever, diarrhea, abdominal pain, or abnormal immunophenotype (residual leukemic cells < 104). To continue regular chemotherapy, she went to the emergency department of our hospital. The patient with ALL was admitted to our department for the second time, and the first round of the CAM regimen (cyclophosphamide, cytarabine, 6-mercaptopurine) was started on July 22th, 2023. The patient completed the chemotherapy and was discharged on August 12th, 2023. The patient subsequently had multiple admissions and was treated with the block 1, block 2, and block 3 regimens starting on September 16th, 2023. During chemotherapy, the patient developed herpes zoster, which was treated with acyclovir, vitamin B1 for nerve support, and ibuprofen for pain relief. Topical herpes zoster liniment and Futan cream were alternated between the two. In addition, the patient developed significant bone marrow suppression, with a white blood cell count of 0.18 × 109/L, a neutrophil count of 0.14 × 109/L, a red blood cell count of 3.06 × 1012/L, and a platelet count of 72 × 109/L. The patient was treated with recombinant human granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF), recombinant human interleukin-11 (rhIL-11) to increase white blood cells and platelets, and platelet transfusion was performed at the same time. After the patient’s condition improved and was discharged, she was readmitted on August 18th, 2024 due to recurrent cough and fever, with a maximum temperature of 40 degrees Celsius. Meropenem and teicoplanin were given for anti-infection treatment, voriconazole was administered to prevent fungal infection, and gamma globulin was given to regulate immunity. Laboratory results showed a decrease in white blood cell count (2.66 × 109/L), red blood cell count (3.53 × 1012/L), and neutrophil count (1.29 × 109/L), with normal hemoglobin and platelet counts. Blood biochemistry and CRP were normal. Routine cerebrospinal fluid examination (Table 1) showed a white blood cell count of 4 × 106/L and a positive Pandey’s test. Cerebrospinal fluid biochemistry: chloride 109.60 mmol/L, protein 195.00 mg/L, glucose 3.02 mmol/L; cerebrospinal fluid cytology showed a few mature lymphocytes among leukemic cells. India ink staining of cerebrospinal fluid was negative. A CSF culture was also performed, and the results suggested no bacterial or Candida growth. In addition, the blood sample was negative for T. gondii by RT-PCR. Serologic tests performed during the patient’s hospitalization showed negative results of T. gondii immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies and immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibodies. The above examinations did not provide valuable information. The immune system is severely compromised in ALL patients receiving chemotherapy. Chemotherapy affects both innate and adaptive immune responses. In the case of serological testing, chemotherapy-induced impairment of B cell function leads to insufficient production of specific antibodies against T. gondii. For RT-PCR, low levels of infection in blood samples may not provide sufficient template DNA for successful amplification and false negative PCR results may occur. Furthermore, there was no significant improvement in the patient’s clinical symptoms during this time. To investigate the causative agent, we used tNGS to test blood and CSF samples separately. The results of blood tNGS detection showed that the number of human herpesvirus 5 (CMV) sequences was 88, the number of torque teno virus 16 (TTV) sequences was 158, and the number of T. gondii sequences was 10. The results of tNGS-CSF test showed that the number of sequences of T. gondii (4510) was detected (Table 2). Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a ring-line high signal lesions in the right cerebellar vermis (Fig. 1), and slightly long T1, slightly long T2 signal shadow and high T2WI-FLAIR signal in the right occipital lobe, the left posterior horn of the ventricle, and the right cerebellar vermis. There was no significant diffusion limitation on DWI, no significant enhancement in the right cerebellar vermis on enhanced scan, and slight hyperintensity in the right cerebellar vermis on ADC (Fig. 2). No abnormalities were detected on the chest radiograph. These imaging features are highly consistent with the typical manifestations of T. gondii encephalitis, strongly suggesting inflammatory lesions caused by the parasite’s invasion of the CNS. Due to the high sequence number of T. gondii infection, combined with imaging findings, the child was treated with SMZ-TMP and clindamycin. The plasma concentration of SMZ-TMP was monitored to prevent adverse reactions. At the same time, since the CMV and TTV sequences were detected by tNGS, the patient was given antiviral treatment with acyclovir. After appropriate and timely treatment, the patient’s condition improved significantly, compared with the MRI at admission, the original right occipital lobe, the left posterior horn of the ventricle, the right cerebellar verma, the right frontotemporal lobe, and the insular lobe showed slightly longer T1, slightly longer T2 signal shadows, the range of abnormal signal shadows was significantly reduced, and some of them were nearly absorbed, with the lesions largely resolved. There was no abnormal high signal on T2WI-FLAIR, no obvious diffusion restriction on DWI, no abnormal signal on ADC, and no obvious enhancement on contrast-enhanced scan (Fig. 2). The re-examination of tNGS was negative, and she was discharged with oral medication on September 27th, 2024 (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This is a rare case of ALL with TE in a patient who had previously experienced culture and immunological failure and was not prophylaxis with SMZ-TMP. Toxoplasmosis is a zoonotic disease caused by T. gondii infection [13]. Chemotherapy can significantly increase the risk of infections or infection-related mortality among cancer patients, primarily due to the impaired immunity caused by the cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy on hematopoietic stem cells and immune cells [14]. TE is a significant complication in children with ALL. Global statistics show that about 30–50% of the world’s population is infected with T. gondii, and seroprevalence varies greatly by region (from < 10% to 70%)[15, 16]. In this case report, a 5-year-old child could be infected with T. gondii through several routes. The most common non-vertical transmission route for a child of this age is likely through ingestion of food or water contaminated with T. gondii oocysts. Young children may have habits such as not washing hands properly after playing in potentially contaminated soil or coming into contact with cats (definitive host). Cats excrete oocysts in their feces, and if these contaminate food, water, or surfaces that the child touches and then puts their hands in their mouth, infection can occur. Another possible route could be through contact with undercooked meat, mainly mutton and pork (intermediate hosts) containing T. gondii tissue cysts, although this is less common in young children but still a potential source [17]. Vertical transmission is not relevant in this case as the child was already 5 years old. However, it’s important to note that if the mother had a primary T. gondii infection during pregnancy, the child could have been born with congenital toxoplasmosis, but this was ruled out based on the child’s medical history.

Cancer patients, especially those with immunocompromised status, are at high risk of infections. The main pathogenesis of toxoplasmosis is reactivation. In patients with immunosuppression, bradyzoites can reactivate into rapidly growing invasive tachyzoites, leading to necrosis and inflammation of the affected tissues [18]. There are unmet needs in the early and accurate diagnosis of infections for those patients in clinical practice. The clinical symptoms of TE are diverse, and there are different degrees of neurological manifestations. The most common manifestation is subacute encephalitis, which can be manifested as altered consciousness, fever, headache, seizures, visual impairment, etc [19, 20].

According to the results of the present study, Toxoplasma tachyzoites are rarely detected in CSF by microscopic examination, although this is considered the gold standard for clinical diagnosis. Toxoplasma trophozoites or cysts were detected by microscopic examination with special staining. Immunoperoxidase staining can improve the sensitivity of detection, but the positive rate is low. To make matters worse, this approach is primarily recommended for brain tissue biopsies, which carry high risks and have limited clinical use. Culture can be challenging, as T. gondii is the causative agent, and it may also be insensitive to other CSF tests [21]. Currently, the most commonly used method to confirm the etiology of TE is CSF toxoplasma PCR, which has a high specificity, but different PCR systems have varying sensitivities (35% to 72%) [22]. The negative serology and PCR results despite the presence of active T. gondii infection can be attributed to multiple factors. Firstly, the immunosuppression induced by chemotherapy in this patient severely impaired the function of B-lymphocytes, which are responsible for antibody production. As a result, the patient’s ability to generate detectable levels of IgG and IgM against T. gondii was significantly reduced. Secondly, the sensitivity of the PCR assay used might have been suboptimal, since the sample used in this case was blood. The primers and reaction conditions may not have been optimized to effectively amplify the specific genetic targets of T. gondii in the patient’s samples, leading to false-negative results.

The clinical manifestations of this patient were atypical, which made accurate diagnosis and treatment selection challenging. To avoid delays in critical treatment, clinicians administered multifaceted antimicrobial therapy. In addition, the patient underwent lumbar puncture and CSF testing using the tNGS technique. The incidental detection of T. gondii led to the final medical diagnosis. This case demonstrates that tNGS can be a valuable diagnostic tool for TE in children with leukemia, but so difficult on most cases due to patient underwent lumbar puncture and difficult common application. A study comparing the diagnostic value of tNGS with tissue culture for periprosthetic joint infection showed that tNGS had higher sensitivity [23], which is consistent with our conclusion.

For central nervous system infections, the diagnostic efficiency of imaging, culture and other detection methods commonly used in clinical practice is low. PCR and antibody detection techniques require early identification of specific pathogens or a limited set of pathogens, which greatly limits their use [24]. Even if etiologic cultures yield positive results, there is still the problem of time consumption. Currently, there are no widely accepted antigen, molecular, or nucleic acid-based tests to accelerate pathogen identification in CNS infections, resulting in delays in appropriate treatment. Therefore, the diagnosis of CNS infections remains challenging due to the lack of rapid and reliable tests. Delayed diagnosis and inappropriate treatment may lead to permanent damage or death, which is unacceptable. Therefore, clinicians need a rapid and effective method to isolate and detect pathogens.

However, tNGS is able to identify thousands of pathogens directly from samples in a single run in a high-throughput and culture-independent manner [25]. Thus, the technique can reduce the number of tests required and the time required for detection. Because of these advantages, the detection of CSF by tNGS can be used as an auxiliary diagnostic tool for CNS infections mainly. It can rapidly and sensitively detect pathogens, provide information on thousands of pathogens, narrow the scope of detection, and provide reliable evidence for clinically challenging infections [26, 27]. The choice of tNGS over mNGS in this case was primarily driven by its cost-effectiveness [11]. In addition, tNGS is Not affected by human genome and background flora, and tNGS showed higher accuracy and sensitivity in pathogen detection compared to mNGS [28]. Moreover, the rapid turnaround of tNGS was crucial for the timely diagnosis and initiation of appropriate treatment for the patient, especially considering the critical condition of the child with ALL.

Although CMV and TTV sequences were detected, their clinical relevance in this case was minimal. CMV, with a sequence count of 88, was likely within the range of asymptomatic carriage in immunocompromised patients, as no associated clinical symptoms such as organ-specific manifestations (e.g., pneumonitis, retinitis) were observed [29]. TTV, which is highly prevalent in the human population, had a sequence count of 158. However, it is generally regarded as non-pathogenic or of low pathogenicity [30]. In contrast, the significantly higher sequence count of T. gondii (4510 in CSF) and the patient’s clinical symptoms (fever, abnormal MRI findings) clearly indicate that T. gondii was the primary pathogen responsible for the patient’s current condition.

TE is the most common manifestation of acquired toxoplasmosis in immunocompromised patients and is fatal if left untreated [31]. In our patient, fever and magnetic resonance imaging are atypical features of TE31. The lack of specificity in early laboratory tests and clinical manifestations resulted in no consensus on the initial clinical diagnosis of CNS infection. Therefore, tNGS of CSF samples play a unique role in the rapid and accurate detection of potential pathogens in the initial stages of disease. This approach can help guide subsequent clinical diagnosis and treatment, especially when the disease features are uncertain. The choice of SMZ-TMP and clindamycin over standard therapies like pyrimethamine was based on several practical considerations. Pyrimethamine is typically used in combination with sulfadiazine, but at the time of treatment, sulfadiazine was unavailable in our hospital pharmacy. SMZ-TMP has been proven effective against T. gondii and was readily available. Considering the patient’s young age and potential tolerance issues, clindamycin was added. Clindamycin has excellent tissue penetration capabilities, allowing it to effectively target T. gondii in the central nervous system [32]. There were no contraindications for these two drugs in this patient, and they were well-tolerated throughout the treatment process. In addition, antiviral therapy with acyclovir was administered to prevent opportunistic infections in this patient from immunosuppression after chemotherapy.

tNGS also has unique advantages, rapid and accurate diagnosis is crucial for clinicians. In addition, the parents of this child had high expectations for the treatment. Because of the severity and complexity of the underlying illness, this patient was treated with a comprehensive antiinfective regimen that included all potential pathogens after admission. The tNGS technique used in this study had a turnaround time of approximately 1 day from receipt of the sample. The use of this diagnostic technology can achieve timely and accurate etiological diagnosis, which can help to optimize anti-infection treatment, reduce medical costs, and mitigate the development of drug-related toxicity and drug resistance. This case also indirectly demonstrated the value of the sensitivity and specificity of tNGS in clinical practice.

Limitation

(1) At present, there are few multicenter randomized controlled clinical trials with large sample size, and the value of tNGS in the diagnosis of infection has not been fully recognized; (2) Compared with other specific tests such as PCR, tNGS is only suitable for cases with uncertain direction of early diagnosis; (3) This is a case report limited to cases of T. gondii infection in the central nervous system.

Conclusion

TE is a life-threatening complication for patients with ALL and is challenging to detect promptly, particularly in those exhibiting atypical symptoms. This condition demands heightened clinical vigilance, the prompt use of suitable diagnostic techniques, and timely, targeted anti-pathogen therapy. To diagnose T. gondii infection, PCR detection remains critical for swift and accurate etiological identification. Nevertheless, tNGS often proves to be a more fitting diagnostic tool in cases involving immunocompromised patients suspected of harboring rare pathogens. In this report, tNGS was instrumental in quickly diagnosing TE in an ALL patient presenting with fever, despite the absence of other clinical or laboratory indicators and an unknown infection source.

Overall, current tNGS technology offers promising new avenues for diagnosing TE. In the future, tNGS could be integrated into the initial diagnostic algorithms for immunocompromised patients with suspected infections. For example, in pediatric oncology departments, it could be used as a first-line test to rapidly identify potential pathogens in patients presenting with fever and other non-specific symptoms. Additionally, it could be combined with other diagnostic methods, such as rapid antigen tests or immunofluorescence assays, to enhance the accuracy of diagnosis. This integration would not only expedite the diagnostic process but also enable clinicians to make more informed treatment decisions, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Yet, it is important to recognize that tNGS is still an emerging method requiring further validation through comprehensive studies to ascertain its precision and practicality in detecting T. gondii. Future research could focus on studying the prevalence of T. gondii in pediatric oncology patients. This would help in better understanding the burden of the disease in this specific population and developing targeted preventive strategies. Additionally, research could be directed towards optimizing the tNGS technique for more accurate and sensitive detection of T. gondii, such as improving the library preparation methods and bioinformatics analysis algorithms.
