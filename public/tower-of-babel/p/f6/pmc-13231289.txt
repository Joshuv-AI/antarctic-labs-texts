Housing Attainment With the Psychiatric Assistance for Transitional Homelessness in Shelters Program
Authors: Sheng-Yun Cheng, Chih-Hao Tseng, Hsiao-Yun Hu, Chih-Chiang Chiu, Yu-Ru Lin, Yu-Chi Pan, Ming-Chin Yang, Pesus Chou, Po-Yu Chen, Ming-Chyi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat demographic and clinical factors are associated with successful housing attainment for individuals experiencing homelessness with mental illness under an on-site psychiatric program?FindingsIn this 5-year cohort study of 208 participants, 46.6% achieved housing attainment; older age was associated with greater success, whereas substance or alcohol use disorders were associated with lower likelihood of success. Distinct sex-based differences were also observed in clinical profiles and housing outcomes.MeaningThese findings suggest that tailored interventions considering age, sex, and psychiatric diagnosis are essential to address the diverse needs of and improve housing outcomes among individuals experiencing homelessness in shelter-based, on-site psychiatric services.

Introduction

Homelessness remains a pressing social issue worldwide, prompting both governmental and nongovernmental organizations to develop intervention strategies. The multifaceted nature of homelessness is closely linked to economic challenges, inadequate social support systems, and public health concerns, particularly regarding the high burden of mental illness.1,2 Mental illness is significantly more prevalent among individuals experiencing homelessness than in the general population,3,4,5,6 with prevalence estimated to be as high as 67% to 76%.7,8 The presence of comorbid mental disorders has been associated with worse outcomes, including a 70% increase in mortality risk,9 higher rates of criminal behavior and victimization,10,11 and longer durations of homelessness.12 However, since most mental disorders are treatable, they present a valuable opportunity to reduce health disparities through targeted interventions.13,14

Evidence suggests conventional clinic-based models of care that require unhoused patients to attend mental health appointments regularly and on time are not ideal.6,15 As a result, the current approach for intervention programs in individuals experiencing homelessness with mental disorders has been shifted from the traditional treatment-first model, which required treatment before housing, to the housing-first (HF) model. This newer approach first provides stable housing and then offers recovery-oriented support through assertive community treatment teams, a method that has been shown to more reliably secure long-term housing.16,17,18,19,20 However, studies have shown that no significant differences were observed for psychiatric symptoms severity between HF group and treatment-as-usual group (ie, only receiving existing support and social services).17,21,22 Therefore, the HF model alone may be insufficient, and its success hinges on implementation, specifically the tailored type, and high quality and sufficient intensity of support services, to ensure long-term housing stability.20,22,23,24,25

Implementing the HF model in Taipei City, located in a metropolitan area in Taiwan, faces economic difficulties. In 2024, Taipei ranked 17th globally for housing unaffordability among cities, with a price-to-income ratio of 28.8.26 In this context, the city faces substantial challenges in supporting its estimated 700 to 800 individuals experiencing homelessness. These challenges include a lack of public housing to implement an HF model, limited rental subsidies, and low public support for prioritizing this population over other vulnerable groups. Yuang-Tong House, the only public temporary homeless shelter operated by the Taipei City Department of Social Welfare, is one of the few social housing facilities available for individuals experiencing homelessness, but it previously did not provide psychiatric services. Unpublished internal data from Yuang-Tong House (provided by coauthor Y.C.P., who is the director of Yuang-Tong House) indicate that fewer than one-quarter of its residents were able to achieve housing attainment through the support of social workers alone. This rate is even lower among those with co-occurring mental illness.

Research shows that integrating psychiatric services with housing support substantially improves engagement in care for this population.27 To address this unmet need, the Psychiatric Assistance for Transitional Homelessness in Shelters (PATHS) program was established in 2019. The PATHS program delivers on-site psychiatric services at Yuang-Tong House, aiming to directly address residents’ mental health needs, enhance their access to care, and ensure that their homeless status does not impede treatment delivery. Although factors such as case management assistance and younger age often facilitate shelter exit, others, including chronic homelessness and criminal records, frequently emerge as substantial barriers in international studies.12,28 Against this backdrop, the PATHS program recognizes the bidirectional nature of mental health and housing instability, in alignment with established evidence.29,30,31 However, most existing literature focuses on the general severity of psychological symptoms rather than structured psychiatric diagnostic interviews,32,33,34 leaving the relationship between specific psychiatric diagnoses and housing outcomes largely unexplored. Although determining whether mental illness was the primary driver of the initial onset of homelessness remains challenging, the PATHS program’s longitudinal framework allows for the systematic tracking of participants’ status.

In this 5-year observational cohort study, we followed individuals experiencing homelessness at the shelter to characterize their trajectories and to identify factors associated with shelter exit outcomes. We also examined the forms of housing attainment and identified the variables explaining each outcome. In particular, we assessed each participant’s psychiatric diagnoses, as defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (Fifth Edition), to evaluate the impact of comorbid mental health conditions on shelter exit outcomes. Accordingly, this study aimed to determine the housing attainment rate for individuals experiencing homelessness with mental illness under the PATHS program and to identify the clinical and demographic factors associated with successful housing attainment.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study examined the outcomes of individuals experiencing homelessness and mental illnesses who resided at Yuang-Tong House and received psychiatric services through the PATHS program from October 2019 to October 2024. The PATHS program was established by the Taipei City Hospital, Song-De Branch in October 2019 with funding from the Taipei City Government. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for cohort studies. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board. Given its retrospective observational design, the requirement for informed consent was waived, as all data were deidentified.

Yuang-Tong House

Yuang-Tong House is the only public temporary homeless shelter in Taipei city, which primarily accepts individuals experiencing homelessness through referrals from the Department of Social Welfare. Upon entry, each resident is assigned a dedicated bed, eliminating the need to requeue daily. The shelter provides comprehensive support services, including family tracing, work programs, and social welfare integration. Although residents can leave during the day, they must return to the shelter for the night.

PATHS Program

The PATHS program employs a multidisciplinary approach beginning with an initial screening upon shelter entry. Trained staff use a history-taking questionnaire, designed by the lead psychiatrist (S.Y.C.) based on the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview, to identify potential mental health needs. These residents were then invited to consult with a medical team, comprising a psychiatrist (S.Y.C.) and a psychiatric nurse (Y.R.L.), during biweekly on-site visits. Interventions include direct diagnostic assessments and medication management. The scope of prescriptions includes hypnotics, antidepressants, antipsychotics (both oral and long-acting injectables), and mood stabilizers. For patients with severe conditions, the team facilitates transitions to hospital-based inpatient care upon obtaining patient consent. Beyond direct care, the psychiatrist serves as a clinical liaison for shelter staff to facilitate social welfare eligibility and application processes. To further reduce barriers, the team leverages government grants to waive fees for noninsured treatments (eg, buprenorphine-naloxone and naltrexone). Although standard medical costs are covered by National Health Insurance, administrative and project-specific expenses are supported by the Taipei City Government.

Participants

Participants comprised 231 unhoused individuals at Yuang-Tong House voluntarily enrolled in the PATHS program. We excluded 23 participants from the final analysis: 3 died from physical illnesses, and 20 remained in the shelter at the end of the study. The latter group included 14 recently admitted individuals (mean [SD] length of stay, 73 [49] days) who had not yet reached temporal eligibility for housing transition, and 6 long-term residents (mean [SD] length of stay, 816 [463] days) facing legal barriers, such as guardianship proceedings or litigation related to property or alimony or support.

Data Collection

Baseline demographic variables (age, sex, and education level) and clinical information (psychiatric diagnoses coded according to International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision) was extracted from the electronic medical records maintained by Taipei City Hospital. The shelter exit trajectory was categorized into 3 types as housing attainment, unnotified departure, and eviction. Housing attainment was defined as the successful transition from shelter to a predetermined community-based residence. Unnotified departure was defined as 3 consecutive days of unexplained absence. Eviction referred to the compulsory termination of stay owing to severe violations of shelter regulations, such as stealing, violence, or on-site substance use. For housing attainment, 3 specific types were further classified: residential psychiatric rehabilitation facilities, return to original families, or independent living in rental housing. Illegal substances refer specifically to heroin and methamphetamine, the 2 most prevalent illegal drugs in Taiwan.35

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software version 28.0 (IBM Corp). Categorical variables were compared via χ2 and Fisher exact tests. Multivariable logistic regression was the primary approach; time-to-event models were deferred because of COVID-19 disruptions, administrative delays, and potential measurement bias in shelter transition dates. To control for confounding, full models with all prespecified variables were analyzed before backward stepwise selection. Cox regression served as a sensitivity analysis (eTable in Supplement 1). All tests were 2-sided, with P < .05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics of All Participants and by Sex

Table 1 presents the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population of 208 individuals, including 154 male individuals (74.0%) and 54 female individuals (26.0%), stratified by sex. The median (IQR) age of the entire sample was 54.0 (44.3-62.0) years (male individuals, 54.0 [46.0-62.0] years; female individuals, 53.0 [44.0-62.3] years). Male individuals were more likely to have lower education levels, defined as 9 or fewer years of education (80 male individuals [51.9%] vs 19 female individuals [35.2%]; P = .03). In addition, male individuals exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of alcohol use disorder (AUD) compared with female individuals (71 male individuals [46.1%] vs 3 female individuals [5.6%]; P < .001), whereas female individuals had a significantly higher prevalence of schizophrenic spectrum disorders (27 female individuals [50.0%] vs 42 male individuals [27.3%]; P = .002). These findings highlight notable sex-based disparities within the population.

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics Stratified by Outcome

Table 2 presents differences in demographic and clinical characteristics across 3 types of shelter exit: housing attainment, unnotified departure, and eviction. Among the total population, 97 (46.6%) achieved housing attainment, 53 (25.5%) unnotified departure, and 58 (27.9%) were evicted. Type of Housing After Housing Attainment

Table 3 details the distribution of housing types among individuals who achieved housing attainment. Residential psychiatric rehabilitation facilities were the most common housing arrangement, followed by rental housing and returning to original families. Significant sex-based differences were observed across the housing categories, as male individuals were more frequently represented in residential psychiatric rehabilitation facilities and rental houses compared with those returning to original families (residential facilities, 48 male individuals [84.2%] vs 9 female individuals [15.8%]; original family, 2 male individuals [22.2%] vs 7 female individuals [77.8%]; rental house, 20 male individuals [64.5%] vs 11 female individuals [35.5%]; P < .001). In addition, the proportion of major neurocognitive disorder in individuals was higher in those who transitioned to residential psychiatric rehabilitation facilities compared with those in rental housing or returning to original families (residential facilities, 17 individuals [29.8%]; original family, 0 individuals; rental house, 2 individuals [6.5%]; P = .009).

Factors Associated With Housing Attainment

The multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with successful housing attainment. The results of the full model in Table 4 and the final stepwise selection model in Table 5 both indicated that age and substance use disorders were significant factors. In the stepwise model, age was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of housing attainment (odds ratio [OR], 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01-1.05; P = .04), suggesting that for every 1-year increase in age, the odds of achieving housing attainment increased by 3%. Conversely, individuals with AUD had significantly lower odds of housing attainment (OR, 0.54; 95% CI, 0.29-0.99; P = .045). Furthermore, the presence of opioid or methamphetamine use disorder was the most significant barrier (OR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.14-0.62; P = .001). In the Cox regression analysis, as shown in the eTable in Supplement 1, opioid or methamphetamine use disorder was similarly associated with a lower likelihood of housing attainment.

Discussion

The PATHS program represents Taiwan’s first psychiatric services specifically designed for individuals experiencing homelessness. A central feature of the PATHS program is the provision of low-barrier, on-site psychiatric services alongside social support to facilitate housing for those experiencing homelessness. This model aligns with current literature, which emphasizes that services for homelessness should transcend traditional brick-and-mortar clinics, positioning outreach as a cornerstone.36,37 Correspondingly, to our knowledge, this cohort study is the first to evaluate the association between factors and housing attainment among individuals experiencing homelessness with mental illness in Taiwan.

In this study, we found that 46.6% of participants achieved housing attainment upon exit from the shelter, notably higher than the 23.2% rate observed before the program’s implementation (according to Yuang-Tong House’s internal data provided by coauthor Y.C.P.). Participants’ characteristics, including educational attainment and psychiatric diagnoses, varied by sex. Female individuals were more likely to have higher educational attainment and a schizophrenic spectrum disorder, whereas AUD was significantly more prevalent among male individuals. Older age was associated with a greater likelihood of achieving housing attainment, whereas AUD and heroin or methamphetamine use disorders were associated with reduced odds. Among the housing attainment outcomes, nearly 60% of participants transitioned to residential psychiatric rehabilitation facilities. Notably, male individuals constituted a higher proportion among those entering residential facilities, whereas female individuals were more likely to return to their original families. In addition, major neurocognitive disorder was more prevalent in the residential facility group than in the other groups.

Previous studies7,38,39,40,41 identified AUD as one of the most common psychiatric conditions among homeless populations. However, a significant gap remains in the literature regarding the association between sex and AUD among individuals experiencing homelessness.3 We suggest that chronic alcohol use can increase the risk of homelessness by leading to family discord, declining health, and workplace injuries.42,43 Regarding our findings on schizophrenia, 50.0% of female individuals had schizophrenia, nearly twice the rate observed in male individuals (27.3%) and significantly higher than that in the general population, which is approximately 0.46% with no significant difference between sexes.44 This finding is consistent with previous integrated multicity data, which consistently demonstrated a roughly 2-fold higher prevalence of schizophrenia among women experiencing homelessness compared with men.45 These divergent clinical profiles suggest that men and women experiencing homelessness may represent distinct subpopulations characterized by different etiological pathways into housing instability. Such differences in clinical presentation may also extend to prognostic trajectories, as reflected in the sex-based disparities in housing attainment observed after shelter exit. Our findings underscore the need for individualized interventions addressing the specific psychiatric and reintegration needs of each population, consistent with the existing literature.3,46,47,48 Our findings indicated that older age was significantly associated with housing attainment. This observation contrasts with existing international literature, which identifies older age as a disadvantageous factor in exiting homelessness.12,28,49 Several mechanisms may explain this positive association. First, the physical and psychological toll of aging, such as chronic illness and declining mobility, often reduces endurance for homelessness and increases receptivity to long-term placement.50,51 Second, the Senior Citizens Welfare Act in Taiwan provides extensive resources and dedicated social worker support, which improves the efficiency and quality of welfare applications. Within this framework, the PATHS program plays a critical role by conducting disability assessments to determine eligibility for these welfare services, thereby facilitating housing attainment for older adults.

Substance use contributes to higher eviction rates, often related to disruptive behavior, violence, or substance use within shelters.52 Unnotified departure was also prevalent among these individuals, indicating potential resistance to structured living environments. Such individuals may exit shelters to continue substance use, creating a cycle of shelter disengagement, street homelessness, and worsening substance use.52 We speculate that the underlying factors are closely linked to withdrawal symptoms and intense craving experienced within the shelter. Existing literature indicates that withdrawal and craving can significantly increase impulsivity, leading to aggressive behaviors and reckless decision-making.53,54,55 Although the PATHS program strives to provide withdrawal management, there remains room for improvement in both quality and intensity. Furthermore, anticraving medications, such as buprenorphine-naloxone and naltrexone, have not been covered by Taiwan’s National Health Insurance. This lack of reimbursement has made it difficult to address craving issues among this population experiencing poverty. Fortunately, starting from the fourth quarter of 2025, the PATHS program utilized a Taiwan policy to subsidize 90% of the cost for naltrexone. We hope that this initiative, aimed at promoting pharmacoequity,56 will yield measurable improvements in housing outcomes over the next 5-year study period. Consequently, these individuals may represent a subgroup that needs a holistic approach, including targeted support, affordable addiction treatment, and more flexible shelter policies that accommodate their needs without compromising overall safety.18,38,47,57,58

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the PATHS program was conducted within Taiwan’s specific cultural and health care policy, which may limit the generalizability. Specifically, the prevalence of methamphetamine and heroin in Taiwan differs from Western patterns involving fentanyl or cocaine, which could influence the specific relationship between addiction and homelessness. Second, because posttraumatic stress disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, and personality disorders were not evaluated, their role as potential confounders in housing outcomes remains unknown. Future studies should include a more comprehensive diagnostic assessment to determine how these specific comorbidities affect housing attainment. Third, the duration of prior homelessness was not recorded, as institutional circuitry and recall bias made quantifying cumulative episodes difficult. In addition, housing status was only assessed at the time of shelter exit, precluding conclusions on long-term sustainability. However, since housing attainment is a vital prerequisite for recovery, identifying factors that facilitate this initial transition provides a necessary foundation for future interventions. Fourth, voluntary participation may have introduced selection bias, and owing to ethical constraints, a randomized control group was not feasible, limiting direct causal inferences; thus, the 23.2% historical rate is provided only as a reference point and should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

In this cohort study of 208 individuals with mental illness experiencing homelessness, the PATHS program achieved a 46.6% housing attainment rate by integrating psychiatric services directly within a shelter setting. This model provided a viable alternative or supplement in settings where the HF model faced substantial barriers. Our results highlight significant sex-based disparities and identified age and specific diagnoses as primary factors associated with housing attainment. These findings support the need to move beyond one-size-fits-all models toward tailored interventions.6,59 We hope this study will benefit mental health professionals and policymakers, addressing a need highlighted by leading organizations such as Boston Health Care for the Homeless Program.60
