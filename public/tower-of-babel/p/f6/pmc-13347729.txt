Evaluation of Pediatric Patients Diagnosed with Encephalitis: Single Center Experience
Authors: Burcin Aygun Cosarpinar, Eda Sahin Izci, Ayse Turgut Sahin, Lutfiye Sahin Keskin, Nazan Dalgic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesEncephalitis is a potentially fatal inflammation of the brain parenchyma characterized by neurologic dysfunction. Although the etiology of most encephalitis cases cannot be determined, viruses are the most common known causative agents. In this study, pediatric patients diagnosed with encephalitis were evaluated in terms of etiology, clinical findings, laboratory and imaging results, treatment, and prognosis.MethodsA total of 115 patients aged 1 month to 18 years who were diagnosed with encephalitis between January 2008 and December 2020 were retrospectively evaluated in the Pediatric Infection Clinic of our hospital.ResultsThe study included 115 children, of whom 36% (n=41) were girls and 64% (n=74) were boys. The disease was most frequently observed in children younger than 5 years of age (48%). The most common symptoms at admission were fever (54.8%), convulsions (46%), and impaired consciousness (27%). The etiology was identified in 67% of the patients; viral agents were the most common cause (44%). Varicella zoster virus (VZV) was the most common etiologic agent (26.9%). Acyclovir treatment was initiated in 94% (n=108) of the patients. Sequelae developed in 6% (n=7) of the patients, and mortality occurred in 1.7% (n=2).ConclusionIn the presence of suspected encephalitis, antiviral treatment should be initiated early, considering the mortality and morbidity associated with the disease, and then continued with treatment appropriate to the identified etiology. Studies investigating etiology will be useful in determining the prognosis of encephalitis cases.

Encephalitis is an acute, life-threatening disease defined as inflammation of the brain parenchyma and characterized by various neurologic dysfunctions, such as impaired consciousness, behavioral changes, speech disorders, and seizures.[1,2]

Although the etiology of encephalitis varies regionally according to geographic characteristics, the etiology cannot be determined in most studies (50–70%). Many infectious and non-infectious factors are involved in the etiology, and the most common infectious agents are enteroviruses and herpesviruses.[1,3,4]

Autoimmune encephalitis is included among non-infectious etiologies, and this diagnosis should not be overlooked if the patient has an unexplained neuropsychiatric disorder without additional symptoms.[5]

Clinical findings in encephalitis may vary depending on the agent, central nervous system involvement, patient age, and immune response. Early diagnosis and treatment are important because of the high mortality and neurologic sequelae associated with the disease.[6]

The International Encephalitis Consortium (IEC) criteria are a standard method used in diagnosis.[7] In addition to first-line blood tests, including complete blood count, kidney and liver function tests, and acute-phase reactants, histologic examination, cell culture, and plasma serology are essential for diagnosis. If not contraindicated, lumbar puncture should be performed in every patient to perform cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) serology and CSF polymerase chain reaction (PCR). If clinically indicated, autoantibodies such as anti-NMDAr, anti-MOG, and anti-GAD antibodies should be investigated in CSF or plasma.[8]

Molecular tests for herpesviruses, enteroviruses, and HPeV accelerate the diagnostic process. If fungi or mycobacteria are suspected, CSF samples should be examined for these agents.[1] Pathologic findings are detected on neuroimaging in 60–70% of patients with encephalitis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the preferred brain imaging method because it is more sensitive and specific for encephalitis than computed tomography. Electroencephalography (EEG) is abnormal in 87–96% of children with encephalitis.[8,9]

Encephalitis treatment consists primarily of supportive care, empirical antiviral therapy, and antibiotic therapy administered according to age and the suspected causative agent until CSF culture results are obtained in patients who cannot be distinguished from those with bacterial meningitis.[9] Herpes simplex virus (HSV) is one of the few treatable causes of viral encephalitis, and parenteral acyclovir therapy is recommended until the diagnosis is excluded. Prognosis varies according to the patient’s age, specific causative agent, initial neurologic findings, and time of treatment initiation. In patients at high risk of encephalitis-related sequelae, satisfactory results can be obtained with early diagnosis and treatment. Early aggressive immunomodulatory therapy may also affect prognosis in patients with autoimmune encephalitis, which is known to have a poor prognosis. Therefore, diagnostic studies should be prioritized from the time of admission in patients with encephalitis.[1,10,11]

This study aimed to evaluate the demographic characteristics, etiology, clinical findings, laboratory and imaging results, treatment, and prognosis of pediatric patients diagnosed with encephalitis in our hospital. This study was designed to highlight the importance of early antiviral therapy in units where vaccination coverage is incomplete and molecular testing is unavailable.

Methods

Our study was designed as a single-center, retrospective, cross-sectional study. Before the study, ethics committee approval was obtained from the Health Sciences University Şişli Etfal Training and Research Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision number: 195; Date: 26.05.2021). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Two hundred fifty-two pediatric patients who were followed up with a preliminary diagnosis of encephalitis in the Pediatric Infection Clinic of our hospital between January 2008 and December 2020 were retrospectively screened through electronic medical records. One hundred thirty-seven patients who were hospitalized with a preliminary diagnosis of encephalitis were excluded from the study group because of incomplete data and different diagnoses. The study continued with 115 patients aged 1 month to 18 years who were diagnosed with encephalitis according to the 2013 IEC diagnostic criteria. These diagnostic criteria are as follows: in possible cases, the presence of three or more minor criteria in addition to encephalopathy that cannot be explained by another cause; in definite cases, the presence of two minor criteria in addition to encephalopathy is sufficient for diagnosis. Minor criteria include the following: (1) presence of fever ≥38°C within 72 hours, (2) seizure, (3) focal neurologic findings, (4) presence of pleocytosis in CSF (CSF WBC ≥5/μL), and (5) presence of neuroimaging findings or EEG findings compatible with encephalitis.[7]

The name, surname, file number, age, sex, comorbidity status, clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings, treatment and duration, ward and intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay, and prognosis of all patients were retrospectively collected from patient records. Findings of microbiologic and virologic studies were collected from the microbiology database and recorded on the case follow-up form.

Clinical findings at admission, including fever, headache, vomiting, impaired consciousness, convulsions, focal neurologic findings, psychosis, ataxia, speech disorder, and choreoathetosis; laboratory parameters, including hemogram, acute-phase reactants (C-reactive protein [CRP] and procalcitonin levels), liver and kidney function tests, and electrolyte levels; blood and CSF cultures; HSV PCR; protein and glucose levels and cell count in CSF; CSF anti-NMDAr test results; and nasopharyngeal swab sample results were recorded. CRP levels of ≥5 mg/L and procalcitonin levels of ≥0.5 μg/L were considered high. White blood cell counts of ≥5/μL in CSF were considered pleocytosis. CSF protein and glucose values were classified as low, normal, or high according to age-specific normal limits (CSF glucose normal range: 40–70 mg/dL for 4–8 weeks and 50–80 mg/dL for >8 weeks; CSF protein normal range: 50–80 mg/dL for 4–8 weeks and 15–45 mg/dL for >8 weeks).

In our study, rapid immunochromatographic tests containing monoclonal antibodies specific to influenza A and influenza B were used for the diagnosis of influenza A and B. Serologically examined antibodies were determined using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method. This method was used for the identification of Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), Parvovirus B19, M. pneumoniae, measles, and rabies virus. DNA isolation from clinical samples was performed using the Magnesia Viral DNA/RNA extraction Kit (Anatolia Geneworks, Türkiye) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and amplification of the isolated viral DNAs was performed using the Bosphore HSV 1-2 Genotyping Kit v1 (Anatolia Geneworks, Türkiye) on a Montania 4869 device (Anatolia Geneworks, Türkiye).

It was recorded whether computed tomography (CT), MRI, and EEG showed findings that could be interpreted in favor of encephalitis. The presence of slow waves on EEG, hyperintense areas in cortical or subcortical regions or hemorrhagic findings on cranial MRI, and focal cerebral edema or necrosis on cranial CT were interpreted in favor of encephalitis.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS 15.0 for Windows was used for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were presented as numbers and percentages for categorical variables and as mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, and median for numerical variables. Comparisons of numerical variables between two independent groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test because the variables did not show a normal distribution. Proportions between groups were compared using the Chi-square test. Risk effects were examined using logistic regression analysis. The alpha significance level was accepted as p<0.05.

Results

In the Pediatric Infection Clinic of our hospital, 252 pediatric patients who were followed up with a preliminary diagnosis of encephalitis between January 2008 and December 2020 were retrospectively examined. One hundred thirty-seven patients who were hospitalized with a preliminary diagnosis of encephalitis were excluded from the study group because of incomplete data and different diagnoses, and 115 patients with a definite diagnosis of encephalitis were included in the study. Of these patients, 36% (n=41) were female and 64% (n=74) were male. The mean age was 69 (range, 1–216) months. The disease was most frequently encountered in the <5 years age group (48%). Among the symptoms at first presentation, the most common were fever (54.8%) and convulsions (46%), followed by ataxia (32%) and rash (30%) (Table 1).

Accompanying Diagnoses and Symptoms in Patients

In our study, comorbidities were identified in 61% of the patients (Table 1). Etiology was determined in 67% of the patients, and 50.5% of them were found to be related to viral or bacterial agents (Table 2, Figure 1). VZV, which could not be isolated in CSF but was accepted as a clinical causative agent, was the most common etiologic agent (26.9%), and viruses constituted 44% of the etiologies. Considering the vaccination rates, only 19% of our patients had received the varicella vaccine. None of the patients diagnosed with VZV encephalitis had received varicella vaccination.

WBC count, CRP, and procalcitonin levels were measured as acute-phase reactants. WBC counts, CRP levels, and procalcitonin levels were found to be normal in 76%, 57%, and 72% of the patients, respectively. CSF protein levels were within normal ranges in 70% of the patients, and CSF glucose levels were within normal ranges in 88% of the patients. In two of the four patients with low CSF glucose levels, the causative agent was M. tuberculosis; in one patient, it was HSV; and in the other patient, the causative agent could not be determined. CSF pleocytosis was present in 50.3% of our patients. There were 10 patients with erythrocytes in CSF. When these patients were examined in terms of the causative agent, three had influenza, two had M. tuberculosis, and one had HSV infection; the etiology could not be determined in four. The CSF parameters examined in 40.3% (n=42) of all patients were normal. In our study, six patients were diagnosed with HSV encephalitis through CSF PCR, eight patients were diagnosed with autoimmune encephalitis via a CSF anti-NMDAR test, and influenza A (n=4) and B (n=3) encephalitis were diagnosed through nasal swab samples. The causative agent could not be determined in 33% of the patients. Findings in favor of encephalitis were found in 49% (n=50) of the patients on cranial MRI performed for diagnosis, and a slow-wave pattern compatible with encephalitis was detected in 35.7% of those who underwent EEG (Table 4).

Comparison of laboratory and imaging findings of patients with and without microorganisms detected

Patients with and without causative agents were compared in terms of various parameters. No significant difference was found between the groups in terms of age and sex; however, hemoglobin levels were found to be significantly lower in patients with a causative agent than in those without a causative agent (p=0.018). When the acute-phase reactants (CRP and procalcitonin) and CSF findings (CSF glucose, protein, WBC, and RBC) of both groups were compared, no statistically significant difference was found (p=0.587, p=0.603, p=0.429, p=0.238, p=0.381, p=0.114, p=0.432, and p=0.985, respectively). When patients with infectious encephalitis with and without identified microorganisms were compared, the rates of VZV infection and vesicular rash were significantly higher in those with a causative agent (p<0.001) (Tables 3 and 4). Acyclovir treatment was initiated in 94% (n=108) of the patients. When the duration of acyclovir treatment was compared between patients with and without identified microorganisms, it was found to be significantly shorter in those with a causative agent than in those without a causative agent (p=0.032). Sequelae developed in 6% (n=7) of the patients, and mortality occurred in 1.7% (n=2).

Discussion

Encephalitis is a serious disease requiring rapid diagnosis and treatment because of its high risk of morbidity and mortality.[1] Although viral infections play an important role in the etiology of encephalitis in children, the implementation of vaccination programs has played a critical role in reducing encephalitis cases caused by vaccine-preventable diseases such as varicella, measles, and mumps.[11]

In the literature, the most common infectious causes of pediatric encephalitis are known to be HSV and enteroviruses.[12] In our country, varicella vaccination has been included in the national vaccination schedule since 2013. This has led to a decrease in varicella-related morbidity and mortality in our country. In one study, the rate of hospitalization due to varicella encephalitis decreased after varicella vaccination was introduced into the routine vaccination schedule.[13,14]

In our study, the most common agent was VZV. However, because only 19% of the patients included in the study were vaccinated against varicella, the increased disease burden was one of the reasons explaining the frequency of VZV in the etiology. Another reason was that only HSV PCR could be performed in CSF examination at the time of the study, which led to the inability to isolate other viral agents, such as enterovirus and HHV-6.

In our study, influenza was found to be the second most common etiologic agent. Although influenza was isolated from the respiratory tract in our patients, influenza could not be analyzed in CSF, and central nervous system (CNS) complications of influenza were considered in these patients based on concurrent findings after the exclusion of other etiologies. Although enterovirus is currently reported as the second most common cause of encephalitis in children, its frequency is not known because CSF PCR was not analyzed at the time of our study.[15]

HSV-1 positivity, which ranked third in etiology, was detected by HSV PCR in CSF.

Rabies virus was another rare causative agent in our study. Although rabies is a condition that can be prevented with prophylaxis, one of our patients who developed encephalitis after a dog bite was brought to the hospital late by his family and died because of a lack of prophylaxis.

Another agent detected in our patients was EBV. Although CSF PCR was not performed in this patient, a CNS complication accompanying active EBV infection was considered, and acyclovir treatment was administered. Although there is no specific antiviral treatment for EBV-related encephalitis, antiviral agents such as acyclovir or ganciclovir are used and may be clinically beneficial.[16]

In our study, five patients had concurrent gastroenteritis; rotavirus was detected in two patients and adenovirus in one patient in stool tests. These viruses were considered causative agents in these patients, and symptomatic treatment was administered. Although rotavirus infection is a vaccine-preventable disease, it is still not included in the routine vaccination schedule in our country. Therefore, rotavirus causes various complications in our country.[17]

Neurologic complications of measles virus, which is one of the vaccine-preventable diseases, are rare and may occur as encephalitis or subacute sclerosing panencephalitis during or immediately after the acute illness, or months or even years later. In a study conducted in Australia between 1979 and 2006 on the etiology of acute encephalitis, the number of deaths due to measles encephalitis was observed to decrease over the years, especially after the first national measles vaccination campaign in 1987.[18] In our study, measles virus was serologically positive in two patients, and measles vaccination was found to be incomplete in these patients.

Autoimmune encephalitis and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) are among the diseases with increasing frequency and awareness in recent years. Anti-NMDAR encephalitis is the most common form of pediatric autoimmune encephalitis.[19] There is no specific epidemiologic study on the incidence of autoimmune encephalitis in our country. In the United States, the incidence of anti-NMDAR encephalitis in pediatric cases has been found to be comparable to that of viral etiologies in recent years. Therefore, this points to the need for clinicians to evaluate immunomodulatory treatment options that may improve prognosis.[12] In our study, eight patients (7%) were diagnosed with autoimmune encephalitis based on CSF anti-NMDAR positivity, and 11 patients (9%) were diagnosed with ADEM based on history, clinical findings, and neuroimaging findings. In one study in the literature, autoimmune encephalitis was found at a rate of 5.1%, and NMDA receptor encephalitis accounted for 50% of these cases.[20] In a study by Erikson et al., [21] anti-NMDAR positivity was found at a rate of 13.4%.

When the symptoms of the patients at presentation were analyzed in our study, fever was the most common symptom, occurring in 54.8% of the patients; convulsions were the second most common symptom (46%), and disturbance of consciousness was the third most common symptom (27%). In the literature, similar to our study, fever is the most common symptom, with a frequency of 50%–90%, followed by impaired consciousness and seizures.[22,23]

In a study by Sevilla-Acosta et al.,[24] the most common comorbidities in patients diagnosed with encephalitis were upper respiratory tract infection and acute gastroenteritis. In our study, 31 patients had active varicella lesions and were diagnosed with concurrent encephalitis. Although CNS complications of VZV are rare, the development of encephalitis with active varicella lesions in our patients suggested that VZV was the primary etiologic agent.[25] In a study conducted with 84 patients on CNS complications of VZV, only two patients with a diagnosis of VZV encephalitis confirmed by CSF PCR analysis had no rash.[26] All our patients diagnosed with VZV encephalitis had vesicular varicella lesions.

In the literature, low hemoglobin levels have been reported due to complications such as pneumorrhagia caused by encephalitis.[27] The fact that hemoglobin levels were lower in patients in whom microbiologic agents were detected than in those in whom no agent was detected in our study may be explained by the possibility that the agents causing encephalitis lead to bleeding at the microvascular level. CSF pleocytosis was observed in 50.3% of our patients. There are other studies with a frequency of CSF pleocytosis similar to that in our study.[20,28] However, no significant difference was found between our patients with and without an infectious agent in terms of CSF pleocytosis, whereas Glaser et al. [28] found that patients diagnosed with infectious encephalitis had a higher CSF WBC count than patients diagnosed with encephalitis due to a non-infectious agent. This is thought to be related to the higher rate of bacterial agents among the infectious causes in that study compared with our study. Neuroimaging abnormalities are seen in 60–70% of patients with encephalitis.[29] In our study, findings in favor of encephalitis were found in 49% (n=50) of the patients who underwent cranial MRI, and a slow-wave pattern compatible with encephalitis was detected in 35.7% of those who underwent EEG.[20]

It is recommended that all patients with suspected encephalitis be started on age-appropriate doses of acyclovir in the early stages of treatment. Because HSV and VZV can be fatal, acyclovir should be administered until the diagnosis is confirmed in centers where vaccination rates are low, molecular tests cannot be performed, or results are delayed. As is well known, antiviral treatment initiated within the first 24–48 hours of HSV encephalitis, during the viremia phase, is the most effective.[1] At the time of our study, CSF PCR testing was limited to HSV, and results were delayed. Therefore, empirical acyclovir treatment was started in 94% of our patients because of the possibility of HSV encephalitis, which is associated with high morbidity and mortality, and continued until the results were obtained. Acyclovir treatment was continued only in six HSV-positive patients, and acyclovir treatment was discontinued in the other patients because treatment was switched according to the detected causative agent. In a study conducted using data from more than 7000 pediatric admissions in the US Pediatric Health Information System database, 40% of children were admitted to the pediatric intensive care unit, and the mean hospital stay was 16 days.[30] In our study, our patients were hospitalized in the infectious diseases ward for a mean of 13 days, and only 38 patients (33%) were followed and treated in the pediatric intensive care unit for a mean of 8 days.

Encephalitis is a condition that requires rapid diagnosis and treatment and may result in mortality and morbidity when appropriate treatment is not administered. A meta-analysis showed that 42% of patients had long-term sequelae. Long-term sequelae were observed to develop at a higher rate in patients with HSV encephalitis than in those with other infections, and severe sequelae were reported in 6% of patients and death in 2.7%.[31] In another study, 36% of patients had sequelae at discharge.[20] In our study, seven patients (6%) developed sequelae, and two patients (1.7%) died. The low rate of sequelae in our study may be attributed to the early initiation of antiviral treatment.

Conclusion
