CARK3-mediated phosphorylation of bHLH106 inhibits iron uptake by repressing the expression of IRT1 in Arabidopsis
Authors: Qin Yu, Xiaoyi Li, Juan He, Yihan Feng, Jiangjie Kang, Li Chen, Xinyue Hua, Jianmei Wang, Yi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The maintenance of iron homeostasis is fundamental to plant survival, preventing both iron deficiency and toxicity. This equilibrium is orchestrated by transcription factors (TFs), including those from the basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) family. Numerous bHLH TFs have been identified as positive regulators of plant iron uptake, but negative regulators remain largely unknown. In this study, we demonstrated that bHLH106 can bypass the FIT-centered transcriptional regulatory network and directly bind to the promoter of IRT1 (Iron Regulated Transporter 1), inhibiting its expression. The FIT–IRT1-mediated iron-uptake pathway is activated under iron deficiency, promoting the accumulation of intracellular iron. As intracellular iron levels rise, CARK3 (Cytosolic ABA Receptor Kinase 3) phosphorylates bHLH106 to promote its recruitment to the IRT1 promoter, enhancing the transcriptional repression of IRT1 by bHLH106 and thus limiting iron uptake. Under iron-deficient conditions, bHLH106 expression is suppressed, and bHLH106 protein levels are reduced. The transcriptional repression of IRT1 by bHLH106 is alleviated, facilitating FIT-mediated upregulation of IRT1 expression and promoting iron uptake. In summary, the CARK3–bHLH106–IRT1 module enables plants to adapt rapidly to fluctuations in iron levels.

Introduction

Iron (Fe) is an essential micronutrient that regulates a variety of cellular processes in plants, including chlorophyll production, photosynthesis, respiration, and hormone synthesis. However, its bioavailability is often limited owing to its tendency to form insoluble compounds in alkaline or calcareous soils. To cope with iron deficiency, plants have evolved multiple strategies to enhance iron uptake from the soil by either reducing or chelating Fe3+ (Römheld et al., 1986). Dicot and non-grass monocot species acquire bioavailable iron by reducing Fe3+ (Campos, 2020). In Arabidopsis, the H+-ATPase AHA2 releases H+ into the rhizosphere, creating an acidic environment at the root surface, where ferric iron is reduced to ferrous iron by FRO2 (Ferric Reductase-Oxidase 2) (Robinson et al., 1999; Santi et al., 2009). The resulting Fe2+ is transported into the root epidermal cells by IRT1, a key iron transporter (Vert et al., 2002). However, excess iron triggers the production of damaging hydroxyl radicals by the Fenton reaction, making it detrimental to plant growth and development (Dixon et al., 2014) and underscoring the importance of plant iron homeostasis.

Iron homeostasis is controlled by multiple steps involving the precise transcriptional regulation of genes associated with iron uptake. A variety of transcription factors (TFs) are involved in the iron-deficiency response, including members of the basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH), MYB, and WRKY families (Stringlis et al., 2018; Fan et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2022; Gao and Dubos, 2024a). In particular, the bHLH TF family has emerged as a central player in the intricate network that governs iron uptake, distribution, and storage (Liang, 2022; Gao and Dubos, 2024a). bHLH Ibs (bHLH38/39/100/101) interact with FER-LIKE IRON-DEFICIENCY INDUCED TRANSPORTER (FIT) to form heterodimers (Yuan et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2013), and FIT then positively regulates the transcription of genes associated with iron uptake (e.g., IRT1 and FRO2) to enhance iron uptake in Arabidopsis (Colangelo et al., 2004; Bauer et al., 2007; Schwarz et al., 2020; Gao and Dubos, 2024a). bHLH IVcs (bHLH104/115) directly activate the expression of bHLH121/URI (Upstream Regulator of IRT1) (Lei et al., 2020) and interact with bHLH121 to activate the expression of genes involved in the iron-deficiency response through a direct or indirect mechanism and to regulate iron homeostasis (Gao et al., 2020, 2024b; Lei et al., 2020). In addition, some bHLH TFs negatively regulate iron deficiency. The bHLH TF MYC1 (Myelocytomatosis 1) negatively regulates the iron-deficiency response by competitively interacting with FIT to inhibit the formation of FIT–bHLH38/39 heterodimers and thus suppress transcription of IRT1/FRO2 (Song et al., 2023). In the presence of JA, MYC2/bHLH6 (IIIe subfamily) inhibits the transcription of FIT and bHLH Ibs. At the same time, the JA-responsive regulators bHLH Ⅳas (bHLH18/25/19/20) interact with FIT, promoting its degradation and thereby suppressing expression of IRT1 and FRO2 and limiting iron acquisition. BTS (BRUTUS) interacts with bHLH IVcs to promote their degradation and negatively regulate iron uptake (Long et al., 2010). ILR3 and PYE (POPEYE)/bHLH47 inhibit the expression of a common set of genes, including those encoding ferritin, and the transcriptional inhibitory activity of ILR3 depends on its interaction with PYE (Tissot et al., 2019). PYE inhibits the transcription of bHLH Ibs by binding directly to their promoters (Pu et al., 2023) and also interacts with bHLH IVcs to antagonize their transactivation activity (Pu et al., 2023). bHLH11, a member of the bHLH IVbs, recruits the transcriptional co-repressors TPL/TPR (Topless/Topless-Related) to inhibit the transactivation activity of bHLH IVcs, thereby suppressing the expression of bHLH Ibs (Tanabe et al., 2018; Li et al., 2022b). These findings highlight the versatile roles of bHLH TFs in maintaining plant iron homeostasis. IMA (IRON MAN) peptides are a conserved family of small peptides found throughout angiosperms (Grillet et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2024) that positively regulate iron acquisition and translocation, primarily by modulating the expression of key iron-related genes. IMA peptides competitively interact with the ubiquitin E3 ligase BTS/BTSL, thus protecting bHLH IVc TFs from BTS/BTSL-mediated degradation, which is important for activating the iron-deficiency response in roots (Li et al., 2021; Lichtblau et al., 2022). By contrast, the pattern-triggered immunity response induces IMA degradation in a BTSL-dependent manner, thereby inhibiting the iron-deficiency response (Cao et al., 2024).

Iron homeostasis is also controlled at the post-translational level, in particular by phosphorylation, which influences the stability, localization, and DNA-binding affinity of bHLH proteins. Under iron deficiency, increased calcium ion concentrations activate the calcium sensor CBL (Casitas B-Lineage Lymphoma), which interacts with CIPK11 (CBL-interacting protein kinase 11) to activate itself, enhancing the recruitment of CIPK11 to FIT (Gratz et al., 2019). Phosphorylation of FIT by CIPK11 enhances its interaction with bHLH39, thus promoting the expression of IRT1/FRO2 (Gratz et al., 2019). The activity of bHLH121 is also regulated by iron-dependent protein phosphorylation (Kim et al., 2019). Iron deficiency promotes the accumulation of phosphorylated URI, which forms heterodimers with bHLH IVc TFs to promote the transcription of bHLH Ibs. Various negative posttranslational regulatory mechanisms maintain the balance between iron uptake and iron overload. For example, the E3 ubiquitin ligases BTSL1 (BRUTUS-Like1) and BTSL2 negatively regulate the iron-deficiency response by ubiquitinating and degrading FIT under iron-deficient conditions (Hindt et al., 2017). Post-translational modifications thus provide an additional layer of regulation, enabling plants to fine-tune their response to fluctuating iron levels. To date, only a few protein kinases have been shown to function in iron homeostasis, and their molecular mechanisms remain to be determined.

The CARK cytoplasmic protein kinases are known to phosphorylate ABA receptors and participate in the regulation of ABA signaling (Zhang et al., 2018; Li et al., 2022a). Analysis of sequence homology revealed that CARKs belong to the RLCK VIII family, which comprises 11 members in Arabidopsis. RLCK VIII kinases play important roles in plant responses to biotic and abiotic stresses (Forzani et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2020). Previous studies have demonstrated that CARK1 phosphorylates RCAR3 (Regulatory Components of ABA Receptor 3) and RCAR11–RCAR14, thereby positively modulating ABA signaling in Arabidopsis (Zhang et al., 2018; Li et al., 2019). CARKs also interact with one another to phosphorylate the RCAR/PYR/PYL ABA receptors, resulting in monomerization of RCARs to trigger the ABA signaling response (Li et al., 2022a). Although CARK3 is known to phosphorylate RCARs in response to ABA signaling, its role in the regulation of iron homeostasis is unknown.

In this study, we found that CARK3 physically interacts with and phosphorylates bHLH106, whose expression is downregulated under iron deficiency. Functional studies demonstrated that CARK3 and bHLH106 are involved in negatively regulating iron uptake in Arabidopsis. Further analysis revealed that bHLH106 negatively regulates iron uptake dependent on CARK3-mediated phosphorylation. bHLH106 directly binds to the promoter of IRT1 to repress its expression, and its phosphorylation by CARK3 enhances this inhibitory activity.

Results

CARK3 interacts with bHLH106 in vivo and in vitro

CARK3, a member of the RLCK VIII subfamily, has been reported to play an important role in plant growth and development (Wang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022a; He et al., 2024). To identify CARK3 interaction partners, we performed a yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screen using CARK3 as bait and identified a potential interacting candidate, bHLH106 (Figure 1A). To more precisely identify the region of bHLH106 responsible for its interaction with CARK3, we fused three truncated bHLH106 variants to the GAL4 activation domain of the prey vector and analyzed their interactions with CARK3 using the Y2H system. Full-length bHLH106 and two of its truncated versions (N terminus 1–133 aa and C terminus 56–253 aa) interacted with CARK3, whereas the third truncated version (56–133 aa, DNA-binding domain) did not (Supplemental Figures 1A and 1B). Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays in tobacco leaves revealed that CARK3 interacted with bHLH106 in vivo (Figure 1B), and GST pull-down assays showed that CARK3 interacted with bHLH106 in vitro (Figure 1C). To further confirm the interaction between CARK3 and bHLH106 in planta, we performed bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays. Co-infiltration of YFPN-CARK3 and YFPC-bHLH106 constructs into Nicotiana benthamiana leaves led to strong YFP fluorescence signals in the cytoplasm, whereas co-infiltration of YFPC-bHLH106 with YFPN or co-infiltration of YFPN-CARK3 with YFPC produced no detectable YFP fluorescence (Figure 1D). A subcellular co-localization assay showed that CARK3-GFP and bHLH106-mCherry both localized to the cytoplasm (Figure 1E). Overexpression of bHLH106 disrupts the iron-deficiency response in Arabidopsis

bHLH106 is a member of the bHLH Vb subfamily and is thought to participate in iron homeostasis (Ahmad et al., 2015). To confirm this function, we generated transgenic plants expressing a bHLH106 promoter–driven GUS reporter gene. GUS staining revealed that the GUS reporter gene was highly expressed in roots, lateral roots, shoots, leaves, and anthers (Supplemental Figure 2A). However, GUS activity decreased significantly under Fe-deficient conditions, consistent with the RT–qPCR analysis (Supplemental Figures 2B and 2C). These results indicated that iron deficiency represses the expression of bHLH106. We next identified a transfer DNA (T-DNA) insertion mutant, bhlh106 (SALK_137687), and generated three independent bHLH106-overexpressing transgenic lines (OE-bHLH106 #8, #18, and #19) (Supplemental Figures 3A–3C and 4A). bHLH106 expression was significantly reduced in the bhlh106 mutant (Supplemental Figure 3C), and RT–qPCR revealed constitutive high expression of bHLH106 in OE-bHLH106 plants (Supplemental Figure 4A). OE-bHLH106 plants were hypersensitive to iron deficiency, with a shorter primary root than wild-type (WT) plants, whereas the bhlh106 mutants showed tolerance to iron deficiency, with a longer primary root (Figures 2A and 2B). Iron deficiency induces the activity of ferric-chelate reductase (FCR) and H+-ATPase, which serve as indicators of the iron-deficiency response (Robinson et al., 1999). Here, rhizosphere acidification was observed under iron deficiency. H+-ATPase activity was significantly higher in the bhlh106 mutant, as demonstrated by yellowing around the root in the presence of the pH indicator bromocresol purple, but it was reduced in OE-bHLH106 plants compared with the WT (Figure 2C). Perls-DAB staining revealed that iron accumulation was higher in the bhlh106 mutant but lower in OE-bHLH106 plants compared with the WT (Figure 2D). In addition, FCR activity was increased in bhlh106 mutant plants but reduced in OE-bHLH106 plants compared with the WT under iron-deficient conditions (Figure 2E). There were no significant differences in chlorophyll content among WT, bhlh106 mutant, and OE-bHLH106 plants under iron-sufficient conditions. By contrast, under iron deficiency, chlorophyll content was significantly lower in OE-bHLH106 lines and significantly higher in the bhlh106 mutant than in the WT (Figure 2F). We next analyzed plant biomass in three independent experiments, each of which used 40 seedlings. There were no significant differences in biomass under iron-sufficient conditions, but biomass was higher in the bhlh106 mutant and lower in OE-bHLH106 lines compared with the WT under iron deficiency (Figure 2G). Alkaline soil limits iron availability owing to reduced iron solubility (Gautam et al., 2021). When 2-week-old seedlings were cultivated in neutral soils (pH 6.0–7.0), the growth of bhlh106 and OE-bHLH106 plants was similar to that of the WT. When grown on alkaline soils (pH 7.0–8.0), OE-bHLH106 plants exhibited aggravated chlorosis and growth retardation due to iron deficiency, but these effects were alleviated in the bhlh106 mutant (Supplemental Figure 4B). We measured the Fe content of 2-week-old seedlings by inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) and found that iron accumulation was higher in bhlh106 mutant plants and lower in OE-bHLH106 plants than in the WT under Fe-deficient conditions (Supplemental Figure 4C), but there were no significant differences in iron content when plants were grown on neutral soils. Genes involved in iron homeostasis are induced in response to iron deficiency (Liang, 2022), and we therefore examined the expression of these genes by RT–qPCR. The induction of IRT1 expression by iron deficiency was significantly greater in the bhlh106 mutant than in the WT (Supplemental Figure 5). The expression of bHLH Ibs and bHLH IVcs did not differ significantly between the WT and the bhlh106 mutant (Supplemental Figure 5). However, expression of FRO2, FIT, and bHLH101 was higher in the bhlh106 mutant than in the WT under iron deficiency (Supplemental Figure 5). To further confirm the role of bHLH106 in iron homeostasis, we generated three bHLH106 complementation lines (probHLH106-bHLH106/bhlh106, com #5, #6, and #10) in the bhlh106 mutant background. RT–qPCR revealed that bHLH106 expression in the complementation lines was comparable to that in the WT (Supplemental Figure 6B). There were no differences in root length, FCR activity, or chlorophyll content between the WT and the complementation lines, regardless of Fe concentration (Supplemental Figures 6A, 6C, 6D, and 6E). CARK3 participates in the regulation of iron homeostasis

Because bHLH106 physically interacts with CARK3, it is likely that CARK3 also plays a role in iron homeostasis. To assess this possibility, we generated three CARK3-overexpressing lines (OE-CARK3 #2, #21, and #26) in the WT background and quantified their CARK3 protein levels by Western blotting with an anti-FLAG antibody (Figure 3A). WT, OE-CARK3, and cark3 mutant plants showed no obvious phenotypic differences under iron-sufficient conditions (Figures 3B–3E). However, when grown on iron-deficient medium, OE-CARK3 plants displayed more severe iron-deficiency symptoms than the WT, including reduced root growth, more chlorotic leaves, and lower biomass, whereas iron-deficiency symptoms were much less pronounced in the cark3 mutant (Figures 3B–3E). In addition, iron content was higher in the cark3 mutant (107 μg/g) and lower in OE-CARK3 plants (53 μg/g) than in the WT (67 μg/g) under iron-deficient conditions (Figure 3F). The CARK3–bHLH106 interaction depends on CARK3 kinase activity

We noted that the transcription of CARK3 was not affected by iron deficiency, suggesting that the function of CARK3 in the iron-deficiency response may rely on bHLH106 (Supplemental Figure 7A). RT–qPCR analysis revealed that CARK3 expression was not affected by bHLH106 and, conversely, that bHLH106 expression was not altered in cark3 mutant or OE-CARK3 plants (Supplemental Figures 7A and 7B). The protein level of CARK3 was lower under iron-deficient conditions, indicating that CARK3 was likely regulated by post-translational modification (Supplemental Figure 7C). To assess this possibility, we performed in vitro phosphorylation assays in which the autophosphorylation of CARK3 in the fourth lane served as a positive control and kinase-dead CARK32m (K101R/N208A) in the fifth lane served as a negative control. There was no phosphorylated band of bHLH106 when bHLH106 was added alone (Figure 4A). The results showed that CARK3 phosphorylated bHLH106 in vitro, whereas the kinase-dead CARK32m (K101R/N208A) did not (Figure 4A). We next generated three transgenic lines overexpressing bHLH106 with a Myc tag (OE-bHLH106-Myc #2, #3, and #4). RT–qPCR analysis showed that bHLH106 expression was significantly increased and CARK3 expression was unchanged in the OE-bHLH106-Myc lines (Supplemental Figures 8A and 8B). Western blotting revealed that bHLH106 protein levels were significantly lower under iron-deficient conditions (Supplemental Figures 8C and 8D). OE-bHLH106-Myc plants were hypersensitive to iron deficiency, with a shorter primary root compared with the WT (Supplemental Figures 8E and 8F), but there were no visible differences between OE-bHLH106-Myc and WT plants under iron-sufficient conditions. Rhizosphere acidification was significantly induced in the bhlh106 mutants but was not observed in OE-bHLH106-Myc and WT plants (Supplemental Figure 8G). We crossed OE-bHLH106-Myc plants with cark3 and OE-CARK3 plants to obtain the OE-bHLH106-Myc×cark3 and OE-bHLH106-Myc×OE-CARK3 lines. We then examined the phosphorylation status of bHLH106 in these plants using phosphate affinity Mn2+-Phos-tag SDS–PAGE, in which the phosphorylated protein displayed lower electrophoretic mobility than its corresponding non-phosphorylated form. bHLH106 exhibited low mobility in the presence of CARK3, indicating that CARK3 phosphorylates bHLH106 (Figure 4B). By contrast, high-mobility bHLH106 bands were observed in OE-bHLH106-Myc×cark3 plants (Figure 4B). These results indicated that CARK3 is capable of phosphorylating bHLH106. We next performed co-IP assays to assess the effect of CARK3 kinase activity on the CARK3–bHLH106 interaction. The results showed that bHLH106 interacted with CARK3-FLAG but not with CARK32m-FLAG in planta (Figure 4C). Likewise, GST pull-down assays demonstrated that MBP-bHlH106 was pulled down by GST-CARK3 but not by GST-CARK32m (Figure 4D). To confirm these results, we performed BiFC assays by co-transfecting CARK3-YFPN or CARK32m-YFPN with bHLH106-YFPC in tobacco leaves. The YFP fluorescence produced by co-expression of CARK3-YFPN and bHLH106-YFPC was not observed upon co-transfection of CARK32m-YFPN and bHLH106-YFPC (Figure 4E). These data demonstrate that the loss of CARK3 kinase activity eliminates its interaction with bHLH106, suggesting that the CARK3–bHLH106 interaction depends on CARK3 kinase activity.

CARK3 and bHLH106 jointly regulate the iron-deficiency response in Arabidopsis

To investigate the effect of bHLH106 phosphorylation by CARK3 on iron homeostasis, we generated OE-bHLH106×cark3 lines by crossing OE-bHLH106 (tag-free lines #8, #18, and #19) with the cark3 mutant. Primary root growth assays revealed no significant differences among the lines under iron-sufficient conditions. However, OE-bHLH106×cark3 plants displayed tolerance to iron deficiency and were similar to bhlh106 mutants in root growth (Figures 5A and 5B). The OE-bHLH106×cark3 plants also exhibited markedly greater rhizosphere acidification compared with WT and OE-bHLH106 plants (Figure 5C). Accordingly, chlorophyll content and FCR activity were higher in OE-bHLH106×cark3 plants thafn in WT and OE-bHLH106 plants under Fe-deficient conditions (Figures 5D and 5E). When grown on alkaline soil, WT and OE-bHLH106 seedlings exhibited slower growth and chlorotic leaves, whereas OE-bHLH106×cark3 plants showed much better growth (Figure 5F). Iron accumulation was higher in OE-bHLH106×cark3 plants than in the WT under Fe-deficient conditions (Figure 5G). We next obtained a homozygous double mutant of bhlh106×cark3 by genetic crossing (Supplemental Figure 9A). Seedlings of the bhlh106×cark3 double mutant exhibited iron-deficiency tolerance comparable to that of the cark3 and bhlh106 single mutants in terms of primary root growth and chlorophyll content under Fe deficiency (Supplemental Figures 9B–9D). When grown on alkaline soil for 3 weeks, the bhlh106×cark3 double mutant showed better growth than the WT (Supplemental Figure 9E). bHLH106 negatively regulates iron uptake in an IRT1-dependent manner

To dissect the downstream signaling pathways regulated by bHLH106 and the mechanism by which bHLH106 mediates iron homeostasis, we performed RNA sequencing analysis. We first identified genes that were differentially expressed between WT plants grown under iron-sufficient and iron-deficient conditions, thus establishing a baseline for the complete iron-deficiency response. The results revealed 3210 upregulated and 3442 downregulated genes in response to iron deficiency (Supplemental Figure 10A). Gene Ontology analysis indicated that these differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in pathways that included iron binding, iron homeostasis, and iron transport (Supplemental Figure 10B). We then analyzed the expression of iron-homeostasis-related genes in the bhlh106 mutant. Under iron-deficient conditions, genes associated with iron storage (such as FER1/4) were downregulated to a greater extent in the bhlh106 mutant than in the WT, whereas genes related to iron uptake (such as IRT1) were more highly upregulated in the bhlh106 mutant (Supplemental Figure 10C). The relative transcript levels of IRT1 and FRO2 measured by RT–qPCR in bhlh106 were consistent with the trends in the RNA sequencing data (Supplemental Figures 10D and 10E).

IRT1 is the key iron transporter in the iron-deficiency response network, and its expression is significantly induced by iron deficiency (Vert et al., 2002). We therefore analyzed IRT1 transcript levels by RT–qPCR in OE-bHLH106 and OE-bHLH106×cark3 plants. The iron-deficiency-induced expression of IRT1 was markedly suppressed in OE-bHLH106 plants but significantly higher in bhlh106 and OE-bHLH106×cark3 plants than in the WT (Figure 6A). However, there was no significant difference in IRT1 expression between the bhlh106 mutant and overexpression lines under iron-deficient conditions (Figure 6A). Considering that the expression of IRT1 is closely related to bHLH106, it is possible that the expression of IRT1 is directly regulated by bHLH106. Previous studies have shown that bHLH TFs form dimers to regulate the expression of their target genes. For example, AKS1 forms homomultimers to activate KAT1 expression, and the members of the bHLH IVc subfamily form homo- or heterodimers to activate the expression of bHLH Ibs and PYE (Li et al., 2016; Takahashi et al., 2016; Liang et al., 2017; Gao and Dubos, 2024a). Here, we found that bHLH106 is able to form homodimers (Supplemental Figure 11). To assess whether bHLH106 regulates the transcription of IRT1, we performed dual-luciferase assays. Co-transfection of 35S:bHLH106-Myc and proIRT1:LUC in tobacco leaves repressed LUC activity, which was further inhibited by the addition of CARK3 rather than CARK32m (Figures 6B and 6C). Co-transfection of bHLH106 with CARK3 resulted in a lower fluorescence signal than transfection of bHLH106 alone or co-transfection of bHLH106 with the kinase-dead mutant CARK32m (Figure 6D). To assess this possibility, we performed electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs). Probes containing G-box and E-box elements from the IRT1 promoter were labeled with biotin. Cold unlabeled probes were used for probe competition and cold mutant probes (5′-CANNTG-3′ to 5′-AAAAAA-3′) were used for negative competition. When the biotin-labeled probe was incubated with MBP-bHLH06, we detected bound probe with retarded mobility representing the DNA–protein complex (Figure 6F). Detection of the bound probe was dramatically reduced when excess unlabeled probe was added to the reaction (Figure 6F) but not reduced when the mutant probe was added (Figure 6F). The amount of bound probe increased significantly when bHLH106 was preincubated with CARK3 in phosphorylation kinase buffer prior to addition of the labeled probe (Figure 6G). Discussion

Iron homeostasis is a dynamic, rapidly changing process. Low concentrations of available iron in the soil lead to iron deficiency, but excessive iron is toxic to plants, making it crucial to maintain a balance between iron uptake and iron overload. FIT- and PYE-mediated transcriptional regulation of genes involved in iron uptake and translocation is critical for the iron-deficiency response in Arabidopsis (Zhang et al., 2023). To date, many bHLH TFs have been identified as positive regulators of iron homeostasis, but few studies have focused on the negative regulation of iron absorption. An important module for the negative regulation of iron homeostasis involves PYE, which inhibits the transcription of bHLH Ibs by binding directly to their promoters (Pu et al., 2023). The interactions between bHLH Ibs and FIT are thus repressed, leading to the suppression of IRT1 transcription and reduced iron uptake (Pu et al., 2023).

In this study, we found that bHLH106 negatively regulates iron uptake by directly repressing the transcription of IRT1. bHLH106 belongs to the bHLH Vb subfamily TFs and was previously suggested to participate in iron homeostasis (Ahmad et al., 2015). TOM5 (Target of Monopteros 5), another bHLH Vb TF, positively regulates plant growth under low-phosphate conditions by increasing root hair density (Wendrich et al., 2020). A third bHLH Vb TF, bHLH107, has been reported to respond to cadmium stress in rice (Fan et al., 2021). bHLH Vb TFs thus play important roles in the regulation of micronutrients in plants. Here, overexpression of bHLH106 disrupted the iron-deficiency response, resulting in shorter primary roots and more chlorotic leaves, whereas bhlh106 mutant plants exhibited extreme tolerance to iron deficiency (Figure 2). Complementation with functional bHLH106 abolished the tolerance of the bhlh106 mutant to iron deficiency (Supplemental Figure 6). In addition, iron accumulation was reduced in OE-bHLH106 plants but increased in the bhlh106 mutant (Supplemental Figure 4C).

The IRT1 transporter is responsible for transporting Fe2+ from the soil into the root epidermal cells (Long et al., 2018; Cointry et al., 2019). IRT1 mRNA was detectable in roots after 24 h of iron deficiency and peaked at 72 h of iron-deficiency treatment (Connolly et al., 2002). FIT directly activates the transcription of IRT1 and FRO2 to promote iron uptake in plants (Bauer et al., 2007). However, IRT1 mRNA levels were significantly reduced after 6 days of iron deficiency (Connolly et al., 2002). These data indicate that the transcription level of IRT1 is rapidly fine-tuned in response to dynamic changes in iron concentration (Gao and Dubos, 2024a). The interaction between MYC1 and FIT has been reported to weaken the transactivation activity of FIT toward IRT1, thereby reducing iron uptake (Song et al., 2023). However, the mechanism by which IRT1 expression is directly negatively regulated remains unknown. We found that overexpression of bHLH106 caused the downregulation of IRT1 under iron deficiency (Figures 6A–6D). A previous study revealed that bHLH106 binds to the G-box element in the promoters of target genes related to the salt-stress response (Ahmad et al., 2015). In this study, we found that bHLH106 bound directly to the G-box and E-box in the IRT1 promoter, and enrichment of bHLH106 at the IRT1 promoter declined under iron deficiency (Figures 6H and 6I). In addition, bhlh106×irt1-2 double mutants exhibited sensitive phenotypes similar to that of irt1-2 rather than that of the bhlh106 mutant, suggesting that bHLH106 acts upstream of IRT1 in the iron-deficiency response (Supplemental Figure 12). We noted that the transcript level of bHLH106 was lower under iron deficiency, reducing the bHLH106 protein level and alleviating the transcriptional repression of IRT1 to maintain iron homeostasis (Supplemental Figures 2–7 and 8D). However, the mechanisms by which iron deficiency represses bHLH106 expression remain to be determined. Given the ubiquitous expression of bHLH106 in roots, stems, and leaves, we propose that it may play a role in iron transport from roots to shoots (Supplemental Figure 2A).

Iron homeostasis is also regulated at the post-translational level. Under iron deficiency, activated CIPK11 phosphorylates FIT to promote the interaction with bHLH39 and ultimately increase the expression of IRT1 (Gratz et al., 2019). Protein phosphorylation thus plays an important role in the rapid response of plants to changes in iron content. However, little is known about the protein kinases that negatively regulate iron homeostasis. In this study, we found that the CARK3 kinase directly interacts with bHLH106 in vivo and in vitro (Figure 1). We observed that both the N and the C termini of bHLH106 interact with CARK3, whereas the overlapping region does not. This may be because the overlapping region of the N and C termini in bHLH106 is a bHLH domain, which is responsible for DNA binding rather than protein interaction. Previous studies have shown that CARKs positively regulate ABA signal transduction by phosphorylating RCARs (Wang et al., 2020; Li et al., 2022a; He et al., 2024). CARK3 has also been reported to interact with OXI1, activating the MPK3/6 signaling pathway to enhance pathogen resistance (Anthony et al., 2006). These studies demonstrate that CARKs play an important role in plant growth, development, and stress response. In this study, cark3 mutants exhibited remarkable tolerance to iron deficiency, whereas OE-CARK3 lines were more sensitive (Figure 3). Further experiments demonstrated that CARK3 interacts with bHLH106 in a phosphorylation-dependent manner. CARK3 phosphorylates bHLH106, whereas the kinase-dead mutant CARK32m does not (Figures 4A and 4B). Overexpression of bHLH106 resulted in greater susceptibility to iron deficiency, which was mitigated by the loss of function of CARK3 (Figure 5). These observations indicate that phosphorylation of bHLH06 by CARK3 is indispensable for its negative regulation of the iron-deficiency response. Further analysis revealed that the bhlh106×cark3 double-mutant plants were tolerant to iron deficiency, confirming that both CARK3 and bHLH106 act as negative regulators of iron uptake (Supplemental Figure 9).

BiFC assays showed that the CARK3–bHLH106 interaction also occurred in the cytoplasm (Figure 1). Phosphorylation is one of the mechanisms that regulate the nucleocytoplasmic distribution of TFs. A subcellular localization assay showed that bHLH106 was present in both the nucleus and the cytoplasm of root cells, and its GFP fluorescence was weakened under iron-deficient conditions (Supplemental Figure 13). However, its GFP fluorescence was not affected by the presence or absence of CARK3. These data indicated that the phosphorylation of bHLH106 does not induce its nucleocytoplasmic shuttling (Supplemental Figure 13). The loss of CARK3 function in OE-bHLH106 plants impaired the transcriptional inhibition activity of bHLH106, thus increasing IRT1 transcription (Figure 6A). EMSA and ChIP–qPCR assays showed that phosphorylation of bHLH106 by CARK3 promotes its recruitment to the IRT1 promoter and enhances its transcriptional inhibitory activity (Figures 6B–6G). Phosphorylation of bHLH106 by CARK3 thus promotes its transcriptional repression of IRT1. Phosphorylation is known to influence the stability, subcellular localization, and protein activity of bHLH TFs (Gao and Dubos, 2024a). Given the weakened repression of IRT1 by bHLH106 under iron deficiency, we speculate that the phosphorylation of bHLH106 by CARK3 is impaired under these conditions. Taking these results together, we propose that the phosphorylation of bHLH106 by CARK3 directly enhances its transcriptional repression activity on chromatin rather than acting as a switch to control its entry into the nucleus (Figures 6 and S13). This will be a key focus of our subsequent work. In summary, as iron accumulation increases, CARK3-mediated phosphorylation of bHLH106 directly represses the expression of IRT1 by binding to its promoter. The CARK3–bHLH106–IRT1 module thus functions as a negative regulatory mechanism to fine-tune iron uptake in Arabidopsis (Figure 7).

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The WT and mutant plants were in the Columbia (Col-0) background. The T-DNA insertion mutant bhlh106 (SALK_137687) was obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC). The T-DNA insertion mutant cark3 (SALK_064325) has been characterized previously (Wang et al., 2020). The T-DNA insertion mutant irt1-2 (SALK_097869) was obtained from Dr. Huapeng Zhou (Sichuan University). The double mutants bhlh106×cark3 and bhlh106×irt1-2 were generated by crossing bhlh106 with the cark3 or irt1-2 mutant, respectively, and were identified by PCR using the T-DNA insertion mutant primers listed in Supplemental Table 1. Plants were grown on a mixture of soil and vermiculite at 22°C under 65% relative humidity with a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. Alkaline soil (pH 7.0–8.0) was generated by addition of CaO (7.8 g CaO/kg) as described previously (Kim et al., 2006). For plate experiments, seeds were vernalized in sterile water for 3 days at 4°C, then soaked in 25% NaClO for 12 min and washed six times with sterilized water. For measurement of iron-deficiency phenotypes, 2-day-old seedlings were transferred to half-strength Murashige and Skoog medium (½ MS) with 1% sucrose and 1% agar in the presence or absence of 50 μM FeNa-EDTA (+Fe or −Fe) for 4 days.

Generation of transgenic plants

The full-length coding sequence (CDS) of bHLH106 was cloned into the pEarleyGate100 and pEarleyGate100-Myc vectors and transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 strains to generate OE-bHLH106 and OE-bHLH106-Myc overexpression lines in the WT background. To generate the probHLH06-GUS and complementation lines (probHLH106:bHLH106/bhlh106), we cut the 35S promoter from the pCAMBIA1301 vector. The promoter (∼1000 bp upstream of ATG) of bHLH06 was cloned from WT genomic DNA using the primers ProbHLH06-GUS-F and ProbHLH06-GUS-R (Supplemental Table 1). The promoter fragment and CDS of bHLH106 were then cloned into the pCAMBIA1301 vector to obtain complementation lines in the bhlh106 mutant background (probHLH106:bHLH106/bhlh106, com). In addition, the promoter fragment was cloned into the pCAMBIA1301 vector with the GUS reporter gene. Transgenic plants were obtained by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation using the floral dip method. The full-length CDS of CARK3 was cloned into the pBI121-6FLAG vector by homologous recombination and then transformed into Agrobacterium GV3101 and transferred into WT Arabidopsis to obtain OE-CARK3 plants. The primers used to create transgenic plants are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

RNA extraction and RT–qPCR analysis

RNA was extracted from 30 mg of seedlings using the Trizol reagent (Takara, Otsu, Japan). Single-stranded cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA (YEASEN, China) and was quantified using SYBR Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) on the Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR system. The experiment was repeated three times for each gene. Relative gene expression levels were normalized to that of ACTIN2. Primers for RT–qPCR are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Subcellular localization

The CDSs of bHLH106 and CARK3 were cloned into the pEarleyGate100-mCherry and pEarleyGate100-GFP vectors, respectively. The constructed vectors were transformed into Escherichia coli for plasmid extraction. Protoplasts were isolated from leaves of 2-week-old Col-0 plants as described previously (Wang et al., 2020). The purified plasmids were then co-transfected into the protoplasts and incubated for 14 h. Subcellular localization was observed using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica, DM4B).

Y2H analysis

The full-length CDS of bHLH106 and various truncated versions were cloned into pGBKT7 vectors. Empty vector combinations were used as negative controls. The interactions were assessed by growing yeast cells on SD4 selection medium (−Leu, −Trp, −His, −Ade).

BiFC assays

The full-length CDS of bHLH106 was cloned into the pSPYCE or pSPYNE vector to generate bHLH106-YFPC or bHLH106-YFPN. The construction of CARK3-YFPN/CARK32m-YFPN was reported previously (Li et al., 2022a). The combination of CARK3-YFPN and RCAR12-YFPC was used as a positive control (Li et al., 2022a). All recombinant vectors were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and transiently co-expressed in tobacco leaves. The YFP signals were observed with a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, DM4B).

GST pull-down assays

The CDS of bHLH106 was cloned into the pMal-p2x vector, and the resulting plasmid was transformed into E. coli strain Rosetta. MBP or MBP-bHLH106 was co-incubated with equal amounts of GST-CARK3 or GST-CARK32m immobilized on glutathione S-Sepharose 4B resin with gentle rotation at 4°C for 3 h. The proteins were analyzed by Western blotting with GST and MBP antibodies (TransGen Biotech, China).

Co-IP assays

The pEarleyGate100-bHLH106-Myc and pBI121-6Flag-CARK3 recombinant vectors were transiently expressed in tobacco leaves by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Total proteins were incubated with anti-Myc agarose resin for 4 h. The proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE and detected using anti-Myc or anti-FLAG antibody. Primers for these constructs are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Histochemical GUS staining

probHLH106:GUS was used for the GUS assay. Five-day-old seedlings grown under Fe-sufficient (+Fe) or Fe-deficient (−Fe) conditions were incubated with the GUS histochemical assay kit (Real-Times, China) in the dark at 37°C for 12 h. The seedlings were then decolorized in 75% ethanol and photographed.

Measurement of chlorophyll content

Seedlings were cultured on MS medium and Fe-deficient medium for 5 days. Samples were incubated with extraction buffer (ethanol:acetone:H2O = 5:5:1) at 37°C for 4 h. Total chlorophyll content was calculated from the absorbance of the supernatant measured at 642 and 663 nm (BioTek Instruments).

Ferric chelate reductase assays

About 40 seedlings were used for measurement of FCR activity. The seedlings were weighed, and the samples were prepared using an FCR activity assay kit (Geruisi Bio) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. FCR activity was calculated from the absorbance of the supernatant measured at 535 nm (BioTek Instruments). Experiments were performed independently three times.

Rhizosphere acidification assay

After iron-deficiency treatment, each sample (containing six seedlings) was transferred to a 1% agar plate containing 0.006% bromocresol purple and 0.2 mM CaSO4 (pH adjusted to 6.8 with NaOH), then photographed 24 h later.

Fe concentration measurement

Three-week-old seedlings grown on neutral and alkaline soils were harvested and dried at 65°C for 3 days. Fe concentration was measured by ICP–MS. Three samples were used in each independent experiment.

Perls-DAB staining

Perls-DAB staining was performed as described previously (Grillet et al., 2018). Five-day-old seedlings grown on ½ MS (+Fe or −Fe) were used for Perls staining with DAB/H2O2. Images were collected using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, DM4B).

Mn2+-Phos-tag SDS–PAGE

OE-bHLH106-Myc×OE-CARK3 and OE-bHLH106-Myc×cark3 transgenic plants were used for analysis of bHLH106 phosphorylation in vivo. Protein samples were separated by 10% Mn2+-Phos-tag SDS–PAGE as described previously (Zou et al., 2021), then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane for Western blotting with anti-Myc antibody (Cwbio, China).

In vitro kinase assays

The full-length CDS of bHLH106 was cloned into the pET28a vector and transformed into E. coli Rosetta strain. The recombinant His-bHLH106 protein was expressed with 0.5 mM isopropylthio-β-D-galactoside (IPTG) at 16°C and purified. For the phosphorylation assay, GST-CARK3 or GST-CARK32m was incubated with the substrate His-bHLH106 in kinase buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5], 10 mM MgCl2, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM ATP, and 2 mM DTT) at 37°C for 3 h. The reaction was stopped by the addition of SDS loading buffer and heated at 99°C for 10 min.

Dual-luciferase assays

The genomic sequence of the IRT1 promoter was cloned into the pGreenII 0800-LUC vector, which contains a REN element driven by the 35S promoter and a LUC reporter gene without a promoter. The experiments were performed by Agrobacterium-mediated transient transformation of tobacco cells. Luciferase activity was assessed using the Dual- Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Promega, USA) and measured in a Synergy H1 hybrid multi-mode microplate reader (BioTek, UK). The result was normalized as the ratio of firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity. Three independent experiments were performed. The primers used for cloning are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Luciferase imaging

To observe the activity of firefly luciferase, luciferase imaging assays were performed as described previously (Shi et al., 2022). Two days after infiltration, the tobacco leaves were sprayed with D-luciferin sodium salt solution and imaged with a CCD camera (PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina III).

Split-luciferase complementation assays

The full-length CDS of bHLH106 was introduced into pCAMBIA1300-nLuc and pCAMBIA1300-cLuc. Agrobacterium suspensions containing bHLH106-nLuc and cLuc-bHLH106 were mixed and infiltrated into tobacco leaves. Luciferase intensity was imaged using a CCD camera (PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina III).

EMSAs

Probes containing the G-box or E-box element from the IRT1 promoter were synthesized and labeled with biotin. Cold unlabeled probes were used for probe competition and cold mutant probes (5′-CANNTG-3′ to 5′-AAAAAA-3′) were used for negative competition. MBP-bHLH106 protein was expressed and purified from E. coli strain Rosetta. The reactions of MBP-bHLH106 with probes were performed using the LightShift Chemiluminescent EMSA Kit (Thermo Fisher, USA) according to the manufacturer’s manual. To assess the effect of CARK3-mediated bHLH106 phosphorylation on the transcription of IRT1, bHLH106 was preincubated with CARK3 in phosphorylation kinase buffer prior to addition of the labeled probe. Probe sequences are shown in Supplemental Table 1.

ChIP–qPCR assays

OE-bHLH106-Myc and OE-bHLH106-Myc×cark3 transgenic plants were grown on MS medium for 2 days and then transferred to +Fe or −Fe medium for 10 days. Approximately 2 g of plant seedlings were sampled and cross-linked immediately in 1% (w/v) formaldehyde for 20 min. The cross-linking was stopped by addition of 0.125 M glycine for 10 min, and the plant tissues were washed three times in distilled water and ground in liquid nitrogen. Chromatin was prepared using the EpiQuik Plant ChIP Kit (Epigen Tek, USA) as described previously (Li et al., 2022a). DNA binding ratios were quantified by qPCR with specific primers. Primers used for ChIP–qPCR are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t-test. Asterisks indicate statistically significant differences (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001); “ns” indicates no significant difference compared with the WT or control. Where appropriate, data were analyzed using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test (GraphPad Prism v9). Different letters represent statistically significant differences among groups (p < 0.05).
