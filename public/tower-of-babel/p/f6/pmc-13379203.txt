Gambling Severity and the Use of General Health Care and Social Services: A Nationwide Population Survey of Service Use and Associated Factors
Authors: Jenna Grundström, Tiina Latvala, Maria Heiskanen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGambling-related harms constitute a significant public health concern, with adverse effects ranging from financial difficulties to mental health disorders. Therefore, people experiencing gambling-related harm may need different kinds of health care and social services.MethodsThis study examines the social and health care usage between gambling severity groups. We aim to examine whether people with recreational, risk, or problem gambling use more social and health care services compared to non-gamblers, and what increases the probability of using social and health care services for people who gamble on different levels of risk for harms. The study used data from the population-based Healthy Finland survey (N = 28,153; 46%).ResultsOur results show that individuals with problem gambling more commonly experienced social and health vulnerabilities. Among individuals with problem gambling, 86% had utilized health care services, and 18% social care services during the past year, which was somewhat higher than among individuals who did not gamble. Among different gambling severity groups, it seemed that the use of health care services is less common among men, younger individuals, people with excessive alcohol use, and among those with higher income. Individuals in the problem gambling groups were 2.1 times more likely to utilize health care services than the non-gambling group. Among individuals with problem gambling and low-risk gambling groups, the utilization of social services was more common among those with lower income, individuals experiencing psychological distress and long-standing illnesses.ConclusionsThese results provide new knowledge on how people with different levels of risk for gambling harm use social and health services and support the development of social and health services in responding to gambling problems.

Introduction

The global gambling industry is rapidly growing, with estimates of net losses by consumers reaching 700 billion US dollars by 2028 [1]. Problematic gambling can lead to severe financial, psychological, and social consequences [2, 3] and is increasingly recognized as a significant public health issue [1, 3]. A global meta-analysis found that among adults, 8.7 per cent were classified as engaging in risk gambling, and 1.4 per cent were engaging in problematic gambling [4]. Problematic gambling affects family members and friends, as well as social environments and societies through, for example, social and health care service use [5, 6].

In population studies as well as in clinical practice, the “Problem Gambling Severity Index” (PGSI) is widely used to assess gambling severity, measuring both gambling behavior and associated problems [7, 8]. Gambling severity is classified into five groups: (1) non-gambling; (2) recreational gambling without issues; (3) low-risk gambling with minor negative effects; (4) moderate-risk gambling with more frequent harms; and (5) problem gambling or gambling disorder (GD) causing major personal, social, and financial disruptions. In some cases, gambling problems, “legacy harms” – such as indebtedness or difficulties in social relationships – may persist even after active gambling has ceased [9]. In this manuscript, we use the term “gambling severity” to indicate the intensity or severity of an individual’s gambling behavior, while the terms “gambling problems” or “problematic gambling” are used more broadly to refer to the presence of negative consequences associated with gambling. “Harms” refer to consequences from gambling. In addition, we use the concepts of low-risk gambling, moderate-risk gambling, and problem gambling groups in relation to PGSI1.

Health care and social services have different roles in supporting people experiencing gambling problems. Primary healthcare is an established setting for administering risk prediction, screening tools, direct interventions, or referrals to specialist services related to gambling-related issues [10]. The recommended treatment for GD (International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision, code 6C50) is well-established in research, and many countries have treatment guidelines for it, recommending mainly cognitive-behavioral therapy and motivational interviewing, delivered traditionally by health care professionals [11, 12].

Gambling problems often cause financial and social harms, such as indebtedness, inability to meet financial obligations or relationship disruption [13]. The field of social work has a strong emphasis on helping individuals with financial and social difficulties, including those arising from gambling [14]. Alongside support for issues such as financial hardship, child wellbeing, and housing difficulties, increasing perceived emotional and social support also benefits people experiencing gambling problems [15], further highlighting the importance of social services.

Although these services could help people with gambling problems, the services seem to be vastly underused relative to population estimates: only 1 in 25 people experiencing moderate-risk gambling and one in five individuals with more severe gambling problems seek assistance from friends, family, or services specifically for their gambling-related issues [16]. For example, in England, it was estimated that between 2021 and 2022 just over 7,000 individuals sought treatment for gambling each year, yet it has been suggested that approximately 243,000 adults might have benefited from intensive treatment (cognitive-behavioral therapy) [17, 18].

While the use of gambling-specific services appears low, previous research indicates that individuals experiencing gambling problems tend to use general healthcare and mental health services more frequently than those without such issues. A meta-analysis of data from 10 studies, mainly from New Zealand and the USA, found that around 3% of patients reported significant levels of problem gambling in primary care settings, although rates varied widely across studies, ranging from approximately 1%–15% [10]. Studies in the UK, Australia, and the USA have found that people with gambling problems are overrepresented in general practice, mental health services, and social work [6, 19, 20].

The increased use of general health care services may be due to the co-occurrence of other mental health conditions, such as depression and anxiety, which are common among people with gambling problems. Both somatic and psychiatric diagnoses are more prevalent among individuals with a GD diagnosis than among other patients, according to the Nordic healthcare registers [21, 22]. Other issues affecting treatment-seeking are socioeconomic status, gender, age, alcohol, drug and nicotine use, interpersonal problems, as well as the financial and legal problems caused by gambling [19, 23, 24]. In addition, people with gambling problems have an increased risk of suicide and suicidal ideation [25–27]. As the problems caused by gambling extend across many areas of life, and since overall life situations as well as social, financial, and health-related circumstances vary between individuals, service needs can also be diverse. There is no clear pathway to recovery in GD, as each case is complex [28].

There are also omissions in the recognition and screening of gambling problems both in health care and social services. Screening for gambling is not yet routine in healthcare, as it is often not regarded as an immediate risk, a long-term concern, or a common occurrence [29]. A narrative synthesis revealed that none of the GPs routinely screened for GD [30]. Similarly, social workers seem to have insufficient training and knowledge regarding gambling and are uncertain about how to support people experiencing gambling-related harm. Furthermore, no brief screening tool is currently recommended for use in social care settings [22–24]. If problematic gambling goes undetected in service use, it can lead to overutilization and an unnecessary burden on primary services, with attention focused on addressing the symptoms and consequences of the disorder (e.g., depression, suicidal ideation) rather than the underlying cause, problematic gambling [6, 10].

In summary, previous research shows that both health care and social services could play an important role in supporting recovery from gambling problems. However, the services specially targeted at gambling problems are underused relative to the prevalence of gambling problems in the population. At the same time, individuals experiencing gambling problems tend to have elevated use of general health care and social services. There are many complex reasons behind the use or nonuse of services, but one important structural issue is that the health care and social service systems often fail to detect gambling problems underlying service use and to direct individuals to appropriate treatment and support services.

Countries differ in their social and health care systems, both in their general principles and in how they respond to gambling problems. The public services in Finland are obligated, according to the law, to organize health care and social care services for their residents. The medical treatment of substance use disorders and other addictions is provided within the health care system, while social services are responsible for supporting people with social problems, such as family issues and child welfare, financial difficulties and challenges related to living or housing.

There is a lack of detailed information in Finland and globally on the proportion of clients in health care and social services who experience gambling problems. To estimate the total societal burden of gambling, it is important to have data on the extent and costs of social and health care service use associated with different levels of gambling severity. As gambling opportunities expand in many countries, including Finland, this is likely to lead to an increase in gambling-related problems and greater demand for social and health care services [31]. Also, for the development of health care and social services that respond to gambling problems, it is important to understand which background factors are associated with the use or nonuse of these services.

The present study aims to examine the utilization of general health care and social services across gambling severity groups: (1) non-gambling, (2) recreational gambling, (3) low-risk gambling, (4) moderate-risk gambling, and (5) problem gambling. Specifically, we aim to determine whether individuals with recreational, low-risk, moderate-risk, or problem gambling use more health care and social services compared to non-gamblers, and to identify factors associated with an increased likelihood of service use among individuals with different levels of gambling-related risk.

The study addresses the following research questions:a.Are individuals with higher gambling severity more likely to experience social and health vulnerabilities and to use health care and social services than recreational gamblers or non-gamblers?b.Who are the individuals across different gambling severity groups who use or do not use health and/or social care services?

Methods

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines; the completed checklist is provided as online Supplementary Material (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552564). This study utilizes data from the Healthy Finland Survey, a nationally representative population health survey conducted by the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare between September 2022 and February 2023. The survey targeted adults aged 20 years and above residing in Finland. A stratified random sampling method was employed to ensure both national and regional representativeness. Of the 61,600 individuals invited to participate, 28,153 responded (all respondents included in analyses), yielding a response rate of 45.7%. Data were collected through both online and paper questionnaires, which covered a broad range of topics including health status, health behaviors, use of health and social services, and quality of life. A more detailed description of the dataset is available in another article [32].

To enhance representativeness, the data were weighted to match the demographic structure of the Finnish adult population with respect to age, gender, marital status, education level, first language, and region of residence. Population distributions for these variables were obtained from the Digital and Population Data Services Agency and were also used to evaluate nonresponse and construct the analytical weights [32]. The weights were derived using the inverse probability weighting method, which is commonly applied in population-based surveys and has been shown to effectively correct for nonresponse bias in Finnish survey data [33]. The final weights were applied to all analyses – including descriptive statistics and regression models – to ensure that the results reflect the Finnish adult population.

Measures

Gambling Severity

Gambling severity was assessed using the PGSI. The following cut-off scores were applied: non-gambling, nonproblem gambling (score of 0), low-risk gambling (scores: 1–2), moderate-risk gambling (scores: 3–7), and problem gambling (score: ≥8). The non-gambling category included individuals who – in response to the question “Think about the past 12 months, how often did you gamble?” – reported that they had not gambled at all. PGSI, a nine-item measure developed by Ferris and Wynne [7], has been extensively evaluated and is widely regarded as a reliable, valid, and commonly used “gold standard” instrument for assessing gambling-related harm in general population studies [34]. Psychometric analyses demonstrate strong internal consistency, substantial concurrent validity, and robust ability to differentiate between low-, moderate-, and high-risk gamblers [7, 34]. The PGSI has been used in several population studies in Finland. However, no separate validation study has been conducted in the Finnish context.

Utilization of Health Care and Social Services

Health care use was measured with the question: “Have you used health care (e.g., doctor, nurse, hospital, dentist, dental hygienist) in the past 12 months?” Social service use was assessed with the question: “Have you used social services in the past 12 months?” Both included binary (yes/no) response options. Service use referred to use for any reason, not specifically due to gambling-related harm.

Sociodemographic Factors

The cut-off points for educational level were determined separately for each age and gender group using the distribution of years of education within those subgroups. Because educational attainment varies substantially across birth cohorts and between men and women, subgroup-specific thresholds were used to ensure that the categories reflected comparable relative positions within each demographic group. First, we calculated the distribution of completed years of education for each age group separately for men and women. Second, we used percentile-based thresholds to define low, medium, and high education within each subgroup. Specifically, participants below the 25th percentile were classified as “low education,” those between the 25th and 75th percentiles as “medium education,” and those above the 75th percentile as “high education.” These percentile cut-offs were then converted into the corresponding number of completed years of education for each subgroup.

Household income was assessed with the question: “How large was your household income last year (before tax)?” with response options of the following: EUR <15,000; EUR 15,001–35,000; EUR 35,001–55,000; EUR 55,001–75,000; and EUR 75,001 or over. For analysis purposes, the three highest categories were combined into a single group: “EUR 35,001 or over.”

Family status was derived by combining data on household size and the number of children <18 years, resulting in three categories: single household, couple, and family with children. Sociodemographic variables had no missing values for sex, age, and family status, and only minimal missing data for education (2.2%) and household income (2.5%).

Factors of Social and Health Vulnerabilities

Excessive alcohol use was assessed using the three-item Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test-Consumption (AUDIT-C). The AUDIT-C consists of three standard items assessing (1) drinking frequency, (2) the typical number of drinks consumed on drinking days, and (3) the frequency of consuming six or more drinks on a single occasion. In the survey, respondents selected the option that best reflected their current or typical drinking patterns, with examples provided for clarity. Response options followed the standard AUDIT-C format, ranging from “never” to “four or more times per week” for drinking frequency, and from “1–2 drinks” to “10 or more drinks” for typical consumption, with scores ranging from 0 to 12 [35]. Thresholds for excessive use were defined as ≥6 for men aged 20–64 years, ≥5 for women aged 20–64 years, and ≥4 for adults aged 65 years and older [36]. AUDIT-C is recognized as a key brief tool for detecting alcohol misuse and has been validated across numerous clinical and research settings in Finland and elsewhere [37, 38].

Severe psychological distress was measured using the five-item Mental Health Inventory (MHI-5), which assesses emotional well-being over the past 4 weeks on a 6-point scale. Scores range from 0 to 100, with values below 52 indicating severe distress. The MHI-5 has been widely applied in epidemiological surveys and health monitoring studies, including European and Nordic cohorts, supporting its suitability for use in population-level analyses for detecting depressive symptoms [39–41]. Prior research has shown good internal consistency estimates along with strong convergent validity with established measures of depression and anxiety [40, 42]. This indicator had 4.6% missing data.

Long-term illness or health problems were assessed with the question: “Do you have any long-term illness or health problem?” with response options of “yes” or “no.” Loneliness was measured with the question: “Do you ever feel lonely?” Response options included: “never,” “very rarely,” “sometimes,” “fairly often,” and “all the time.” For this study, respondents who reported feeling lonely “fairly often” or “all the time” were classified as lonely.

Statistical Analyses

Social and health vulnerabilities, as well as the utilization of health and social services by gambling severity, were analyzed using the χ2 test (Table 1). Next, the utilization of health care (Table 2) and social services (Table 3) were examined separately across gambling severity groups, and statistical differences were assessed using the χ2 test. The association between service utilization and gambling severity was further analyzed using logistic regression, with utilization of health and social services as the dependent variable and gambling severity as the independent variable, where non-gamblers served as the reference group. Logistic regression was selected because the outcome variable was binary (service use vs. no service use), and the method allows estimation of adjusted odds ratios to quantify how the likelihood of service utilization varies across levels of gambling severity while controlling for potential confounders. In models 1A and 2A, health care utilization and social service utilization were examined, adjusting for sociodemographic factors and gambling severity. In models 1B and 2B, sociodemographic factors, gambling severity, and indicators of social and health vulnerabilities were all considered. Results are presented as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (Table 4). Weighted data were applied to all analyses to ensure that the results reflect the Finnish adult population. The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.2.0) with the complex sample module to account for the multistage sampling design.

Results

Addressing the first research question – whether individuals with higher gambling severity differ from recreational gamblers and non-gamblers in their social and health vulnerabilities and service use – we observed clear gradients across gambling severity groups. The problem gambling group reported markedly higher levels of social and health vulnerabilities: 55% reported excessive alcohol use, 43% experienced severe psychological distress, 57% had a longstanding illness, and 33% reported loneliness (Table 1).

Service use also varied across groups. Among individuals in the problem gambling group, 86% had used health care in the past year and 18% had used social services. Health care use was somewhat lower among the moderate-risk (81%), low-risk (83%), and non-gambling groups (81%), whereas recreational gamblers showed the same level of use as the problem gambling group (Table 2). Social service use followed a similar pattern: individuals in the problem gambling groups reported nearly double the rate of social service use (18%) compared with the moderate-risk (10%), low-risk (10%), and non-gambling (8%) groups, while the lowest level was observed among the recreational gambling group (6%) (Table 3).

When examining health care use within gambling severity groups, individuals within the problem gambling group did not differ in their service use based on sociodemographic factors or most social and health vulnerabilities. The only exception was that those with a longstanding illness were more likely to have used health care during the past year than those without such illnesses (Table 2). This pattern was consistent across the other gambling severity groups.

Across all groups, 15–20% of individuals had not used any health care during the past year (Table 1). Patterns of non-utilization varied by subgroup. In the moderate-risk, low-risk, and recreational gambling groups, as well as the non-gambling group, those who had not used health care were more often men (Table 2). Younger individuals (<45 years) and those reporting excessive alcohol use were overrepresented among the low-risk gambling group who had not used health care, whereas those in the recreational gambling group who had not used health care were generally older. Income-related patterns also emerged: in the moderate-risk gambling or non-gambling groups, non-utilization of health care was more common among individuals with lower income, whereas in the recreational gambling groups, it was more common among those with higher income.

The use of social services also varied by socioeconomic status. In all gambling severity groups except the recreational gambling group, social service use was more common among individuals with lower income (Table 3). Among individuals in the problem gambling group, social service use was also more common among those reporting a longstanding illness, psychological distress, and loneliness – a pattern that was also observed within the low-risk and recreational gambling groups.

In model 1B, which included all sociodemographic factors as well as social and health vulnerabilities, health care utilization was more common in the problem gambling, low-risk gambling, and recreational gambling groups compared with the non-gambling group (Table 4). The problem gambling group was approximately 2.1 times more likely to use health care services than those who did not gamble. Social service use was more common only in the problem gambling group in model 2A; however, this association was no longer statistically significant after all covariates were added in model 2B.

Discussion

This study examined the use of any health care and social services during the past year between gambling severity groups: (1) non-gambling, (2) recreational, (3) low-risk gambling, (4) moderate-risk gambling, and (5) problem gambling. Among individuals with gambling problems (group 5), 86% had utilized health care services, and 18% had utilized social care services during the past year. Individuals in the problem gambling group were 2.1 times more likely to utilize health care services than non-gambling individuals. Utilization of health care during the past year was lowest in moderate-risk, non-gambling, and low-risk groups, while in the recreational gambling group, it was the same as in the problem gambling group.

Our results are in line with previous research showing that people harmed by gambling are overrepresented in health care services [6, 10, 19, 20]. Our research does not classify whether service use is related to gambling or not. However, these findings highlight that people who experience gambling problems are very often already clients of health care. This finding emphasizes the responsibility of health care professionals and health care systems to identify gambling and gambling problems among patients. According to current evidence from Finland, the prevalence of moderate-risk and problem gambling (PGSI ≥3) was 4.2% in 2023 [43], whereas in 2020 only 0.018% of the population had received a formal GD diagnosis from a medical doctor in health care [44]. This suggests that many individuals with gambling problems are not identified, or at least not diagnosed, in clinical settings.

The mechanisms underlying the elevated health care use in the problem gambling group compared with moderate-risk, low-risk and non-gambling groups may be diverse. Overall, there can be a variety of structural (e.g., those that predispose or enable the service use) and individual factors (such as co-occurring social and health problems) that are related to the over-presentation of health care use among people with gambling problems [6]. Our findings suggest that longstanding illness and psychological distress are key factors associated with increased utilization of health and social services. It is well established that gambling problems often co-occur with both physical and mental health conditions, which may exacerbate service needs and contribute to more frequent or intensive use of services (e.g., [1, 6]).

These factors likely reflect broader and more complex needs among individuals experiencing gambling-related harms. Therefore, the observed associations may not only indicate direct effects of gambling-related harm but also the accumulation of multiple vulnerabilities. A recent study identified four distinct service-use profiles among patients with gambling problems, differing in what treatment and other services they had received [45] – suggesting that people experiencing gambling problems in health care settings have diverse backgrounds and needs. This highlights the importance of integrated and holistic approaches in health and social services to better identify and support individuals with overlapping health, psychological, and social challenges.

Health care structures, e.g., poor availability and accessibility of gambling treatment, may also induce elevated service use: if gambling problems are not recognized, the individual may use many services for the treatment of harms caused by gambling (e.g., mental health disorders), while the underlying condition is not addressed [10]. Structures and practices in health care settings should advance the use of brief screening instruments to recognize gambling problems in the early stages. Furthermore, it has also been suggested that in countries with publicly funded universal health care systems (like Finland), the rates of health care use with gambling problems may be higher, because of the socioeconomic disparities-reducing nature of these systems [10].

Interestingly, the level of health care use among the recreational gambling group was comparable to that of the problem gambling group (86%), and in logistic regression models, they were more likely to use health care compared to the non-gambling group. This supports the interpretation that complex mechanisms underlie health care use, which may relate to other health and social vulnerabilities or to health care structures.

It is also important to look at those who have not reported any health care use during the past year, while experiencing some level of gambling problems according to the PGSI. Notably, 14% of individuals in the problem gambling group had not reported using any health services in the past year. Across gambling severity groups, health care use appeared less common among men, younger individuals, those with excessive alcohol use, and people with higher income, though these patterns varied somewhat between groups. The finding that men use health care services less consistently than women reflects previous research on gender differences in health care use [46]. These results support the targeting of development programs aimed at encouraging younger men with excessive alcohol use and higher incomes to seek help when experiencing gambling problems or any level of associated risk.

The use of social care services was 6–10% within the non-gambling, recreational, low-risk, and moderate-risk gambling groups, but increased significantly among the problem gambling group. Among these people, 18% had utilized social services in the past year – nearly or more than twice the share seen in other groups. Health care use in general is much more prevalent than social care use. The share of social care use in the problem gambling group is, however, comparable to that of the general population of Finland, as 14% of Finnish people were estimated to be clients of social care services in 2024 [47].

Importantly, within the problem and low-risk gambling groups, social care utilization was more common among individuals with lower income, psychological distress, and longstanding illness. This may indicate that at more severe levels of gambling problems, financial and social harms accumulate, and some form of official support is needed. Individuals experiencing gambling-related harm often seek help not directly for gambling, but for its associated consequences such as depression, anxiety, or financial problems [6, 48]. However, as the gambling problem is more prevalent among those in lower-socioeconomic positions and among people with social and financial vulnerabilities [1–3], the elevated social service use among people with gambling problems may partly reflect pre-existing social and financial vulnerabilities rather than gambling problems alone.

However, fewer than one in five people with gambling problems reported having used any social services in the past year. Considering the wide-ranging effects that gambling problems can have on families, children, housing, financial situations, and employment, social service use among people experiencing gambling problems appears surprisingly low. Previous studies have identified stigma, lack of awareness about service options, and difficulty in recognizing gambling as a social issue [49, 50]. In addition, social care professionals often lack training or tools to identify gambling problems, which may contribute to under-identification in social care settings [51, 52]. Development of screening tools for social care is also needed, as well as service structures that advance supporting people with gambling problems from social services.

This study offers valuable insights into the relationship between gambling severity and the use of social and health care services. A key strength of the study lies in its use of a large, population-based dataset, which allows for generalizable findings within the context studied. Furthermore, the inclusion of both health care and social services provides a more comprehensive view of service utilization patterns across different gambling risk levels.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although participants were asked whether they had used health care or social services during the past 12 months, the survey did not capture the specific types of services accessed or the reasons for service use. This lack of detail constrains the interpretability of the findings. For example, we cannot distinguish between visits to general practitioners, mental health services, or financial counselling, nor can we determine whether service use was related to gambling problems or to other health or social concerns. Previous research indicates that individuals with gambling problems often seek help for secondary consequences – such as anxiety, depression, or relationship difficulties – rather than for gambling itself [6, 18]. Consequently, the observed differences in service use across gambling severity groups may reflect broader health and social needs rather than gambling-specific harms.

Second, the number of respondents classified in the problem gambling group was relatively small. This limits statistical power and reduces the precision of estimates for this subgroup. As a result, comparisons involving individuals with gambling problems should be interpreted with caution, as effect estimates may be less stable and associated with wider confidence intervals. This limitation is particularly relevant for the logistic regression analyses, where the relatively small size of the problem-gambling subgroup may affect the robustness of the estimated associations and increase uncertainty in subgroup comparisons.

Third, both gambling behavior and service use were measured using self-reported data. Self-report measures may introduce recall bias, misclassification, and under- or over-reporting, which could influence the observed prevalence of gambling severity and service utilization. In addition, the complexity of integrated service systems may make it difficult for respondents to accurately identify or categorize the services they have used, for example, when social work support is delivered within primary care settings. Such factors may introduce measurement error and potentially attenuate or obscure associations between gambling severity and service use.

Finally, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the ability to determine temporal ordering or causal relationships. It therefore remains unclear whether higher gambling severity leads to increased service use or whether pre-existing health or social vulnerabilities contribute both to gambling problems and to greater service utilization. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the directionality of these relationships.

In conclusion, this study contributes to our understanding of broader social and health care utilization patterns across different levels of gambling severity. The main findings show that first, people with gambling problems often are already patients in health care, but the question remains whether gambling has been recognized and is being treated or not. Second, social service use is more prevalent among problem gamblers than in other groups, while still considerably lower than health care use. The mechanism behind the health care and social services use is complex and likely related to many individual and structural factors, needing further research.
