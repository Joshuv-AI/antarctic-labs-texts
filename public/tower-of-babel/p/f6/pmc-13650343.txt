Organizational Support for Safety and Work Ability in Healthcare Workers: A Longitudinal Study
Authors: Sundus Siddique, Rebecca Gore, Tomas Hemmingsson, Laura Punnett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaintaining healthcare worker (HCW) work ability and continuing employment are critical issues. We examined the association of work ability with the previously unexamined work-environment factors of organizational support for safety (OSS) and surface-acting emotional labor (SaEL).MethodsA self-administered survey covering work-environment features recruited employees of five U.S. public-sector healthcare facilities in 2018-2019 and 2021. Linear regression on the outcome of work ability, both cross-sectionally and longitudinally, focused on OSS and SaEL with additional independent variables.ResultsA total of 1,059 and 1,553 surveys were collected from predominantly female HCWs; 425 answered both questionnaires. Almost two thirds of the HCWs were either non-direct care or unlicensed HCWs. Work ability scores were lower in direct-care workers versus non-direct-care workers. In multivariable regression models, OSS was positively associated with work ability, and SaEL was negatively associated, cross-sectionally at baseline and follow-up. Job strain, back pain, and safety hazards were also negatively associated with work in both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, while social support and civility norms (combined) were positively associated. In the longitudinal analysis, only OSS positively predicted work ability, while SaEL had a borderline negative effect.ConclusionThe finding that OSS predicts work ability is novel. Despite high turnover, there has been little examination of risk factors for low work ability among HCWs. The importance of this organizational predictor justifies organization-level interventions to improve workplace safety. Such actions could simultaneously address and mitigate the additional risk factors of SaEL, job strain, and back pain in order to improve work ability for all groups of HCWs.

Introduction

While the quality of the healthcare work environment has been of high concern for a long time, experiences during the current COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated even more clearly the burden of job demands, lack of resources and emotional exhaustion faced by front-line healthcare workers (HCWs) [1]. Willingness to stay on the job suffers as a consequence and HCW retention is an increasingly urgent problem in many countries [2].

Work ability is a person's capacity to meet the physical, mental and interpersonal challenges at the workplace [3]. It could be considered the positive complement of work disability. Work ability is a useful explanatory lens for understanding the relationship between workers' health and their job status and function [4,5]. Health conditions that decrease work ability are also associated with increased all cause mortality [6] and disability [7]). Further, low work ability can lead to poor job outcomes such as low productivity [8], disengagement [[9], [10], [11]] job dissatisfaction, intention to leave the job, and early retirement [[10], [11], [12]]. In the healthcare sector, individuals' work ability is intertwined with their ability to provide care and the quality of care provided [13,14]. Large-scale empirical studies (e.g. [15]), research reviews [13] and meta-analyses [16] document the associations of work ability in HCWs with job attitudes, worker engagement, and intrinsic predictors such as long-term sickness, returning to work after sustaining an injury, and disability after a work injury [13].

Identifying environmental predictors of work ability that can be intervened upon is critical for supporting workers' physical and mental well-being and enabling people to continue working productively [16]. Job demands (emotional, mental and physical) can be risk factors for decreased work ability [16]. Work ability could potentially be improved by enhancing job resources such as coworker support, supervisor support, decision latitude, and control over work schedules [15,[17], [18], [19]].

Organizational safety practices are another resource. These affect mental and physical health, including acute traumatic injuries and musculoskeletal disorders, all of which can produce disability. Two qualitative studies showed that poor “safety culture practices” (lax enforcement of rules) were associated with lower work ability in blue collar workers/construction workers [20,21]. Brandt et al. reported that two items from the management commitment scale of the Nordic Occupational Safety Climate Questionnaire (NOSACQ-50) tool, predicted lower work ability in Danish blue-collar workers after 2 years [22].

We have previously identified organizational support for safety (OSS) as an important predictor of HCW burnout [23] and depression [24]. In view of these findings, organizational safety practices might also affect HCW work ability [5] but formal analysis of the relationship has been limited.

Healthcare is a service-oriented and emotionally taxing job that requires management of one's emotions. Surface-acting emotional labor (SaEL), or masking one's feelings to perform the job, is associated with adverse mental health in junior doctors, nurses, and nursing home workers [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. However, we have found no study examining its association with work ability. Given that SaEL is a predictor of burnout and depression [23,24], we considered it another plausible risk factor to examine.

As noted above, the consequences of low work ability in HCWs carry heavy repercussions. Hence, there is an urgent need to identify and address preventable risk factors like organizational commitment to safety and SaEL, alongside other antecedents of work ability, in a mixed HCW population.

Hypotheses

The primary hypothesis for this study was that OSS and SaEL were associated with work ability in staff of long-term healthcare facilities, cross-sectionally and longitudinally.

Methods

This study was part of a larger project, “Safety and Health through Integrated, Facilitated Teams” (SHIFT), carried out in five public sector healthcare facilities offering a mix of residential and outpatient services. The study sites had a high number of low-paid unlicensed allied personal and medical assistants, such as mental health workers. All facilities were unionized and had preexisting employee safety and health infrastructure. Participants completed a self-administered questionnaire during personal time and were compensated $10. The questionnaire included a combination of adapted versions of published validated instruments and new instruments developed for this study (Supplementary table 1). Further details about the study sites and methods have been reported [29].

Baseline surveys were conducted in 2018-2019 and follow-up surveys in 2021. For the present analyses, work ability was the study outcome, measured on each occasion as the mean of current physical, mental and interpersonal work ability [3].

The primary variables were OSS and SaEL. OSS was computed from the management dimension of the NOSACQ-50 [30]. This scale has questions about: the presence of meaningful and practical safety routines at the workplace; employee influence on safety practices; employees’ participation in safety-policy-related decisions; collection of accurate information in accident investigations by the management; and judicious inquiry into accidents looking for causes, not guilty persons, when an accident occurs.

SaEL was measured with two items derived from [31].

For the other independent variables, psychosocial job strain was computed as the ratio of psychological job demands to decision latitude [32]; the sum of coworker and supervisor support [32]; civility norms in the workplace [33], and perceived presence of safety hazards (NIOSH 2008; Silver & Boiano, 2019). Back pain of at least moderate severity in the past 3 months was included because of the large literature on HCW turnover due to this condition (details of the questions and their sources are provided in Supplementary Table 1).

Age was dichotomized into younger (18-40 years) and older (≥40 years). Fourteen job titles were provided by the respondents and grouped into direct care of patients/residents or non-direct-care jobs such as dietary, housekeeping, facilities (maintenance) and security (Supplementary Table 2).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were done using SAS 9.4 [34]. Internal consistency of scales was measured using Cronbach's α coefficient. Work ability was compared between subgroups defined by gender, direct care/non-direct care jobs, and study site using t-test and one-way analysis of variance.

Spearman's correlation coefficients were computed among all pairs of variables because most of them had skewed distributions. Variables correlated with work ability at the level of p < 0.05 were retained for regression modeling. Regarding potential collinearity, no absolute correlations greater than 0.5 were observed. The variance inflation factor for all exposures was in the range of 1.02-1.5, suggesting no collinearity [35]. A combined score of coworker and supervisor support and civility norms (sum of the three ratings) was defined to create a comprehensive variable for the social landscape of the workplace and called the “social environment at work.”

Descriptive statistics were generated for demographic and work environment conditions in the baseline survey population, the follow-up population, and the group of workers who participated in both surveys (“overlap group”). Within the overlap group, paired t-tests assessed within person comparisons, i.e., whether these individuals' opinions of any work conditions differed on the two survey occasions. Two independent sample t-tests at both survey occasions assessed whether the individuals answering both surveys gave systematically different ratings than the respondents who were only surveyed once.

Linear regression analysis of work ability used forward stepwise model-fitting. Variables were retained if they had a p value ≤ 0.05 or if they changed the size of another coefficient by 15%. We fitted cross-sectional linear regression models for the baseline and final data sets, and longitudinal regression models with baseline exposures and the outcome at follow-up. Linear and longitudinal regression models were also stratified by direct care (yes/no).

The independent variables were correlated with each other in the range of -0.52 to 0.51 at baseline (Supplementary Table 3) and with similar ranges in the later survey. Work ability was most strongly associated with OSS and the presence of safety hazards. Organizational support for safety was strongly correlated with safety hazards, SaEL, coworker support, and psychosocial job strain. Direct care staff had higher ratings of most physical and psychosocial demands than those in other jobs.

Regression analyses

In multivariable regression models, perceived OSS (positive effect) and SaEL (negative effect) were associated both cross-sectionally and longitudinally with work ability (Table 3), although the effect of SaEL was weak in the overlap group cross-sectionally. In addition, job strain, safety hazards, and back pain were negatively associated with work ability in all three cross-sectional models, while the social environment was positively associated cross-sectionally and longitudinally. Stratified regression analysis

After stratification by direct care (yes/no), OSS had a consistently positive association with work ability in all subsets of the data except for non-direct care workers longitudinally (Table 4). For the other independent variables, some effects were inconsistent between direct care and non-direct-care workers (Table 4). Surface-acting emotional labor at baseline had a negative effect only for direct care workers. However, at follow-up, the associations were more robust and 55% larger for non-direct care workers. There was a negative trend in the longitudinal models; neither coefficient was statistically significant, but this sample was about one-fourth of the size.

Job strain had a negative effect at follow-up and longitudinally for direct-care workers only. Safety hazards generally had a weak negative association. Back pain was consistently associated with lower work ability across the stratified analyses except for non-direct-care workers in the longitudinal analysis.

Discussion

This study of mixed licensed and unlicensed HCWs showed that work ability (combined physical, interpersonal, and psychological dimensions) was higher both cross-sectionally and prospectively with better organizational commitment to safety, after adjusting for other covariates. Surface-acting emotional labor had a consistently negative association but was weaker in two of the three multivariable models. To the best of our knowledge, the associations of work ability with OSS and surface-acting emotional labor are new findings for healthcare workers. Job strain also had a consistent and strong negative effect. Other associations with safety hazards, back pain, and the social environment were also in the expected directions, although they were less robust in the multivariable models.

Several elements of safety climate (from the NOSACQ tool) were previously found to be associated with lower physical and mental work ability after 2 years in Danish blue-collar workers [22]. Our results add to those findings with a methodologically stronger approach by using the complete nine-item scale for perceived organizational commitment to safety as a predictor in HCWs.

The odds of having safe work experiences during the pandemic in HCWs were predicted by prepandemic OSS [36]. The present finding that OSS was the single most important predictor of physical, mental, and interpersonal work ability over four years, at least in these direct-care workers, allows occupational health and safety policymakers a tangible point of intervention.

Direct-care workers reported lower mean work ability than non-direct-care workers, but both groups had highly skewed distributions of this outcome with low variance, and they experienced a similar decreased in work ability during the pandemic. We cannot easily explain why OSS predicted work ability only in the direct-care providers but not in the other group. Perhaps OSS is not as important to workers who do not provide direct care, or perhaps there was simply not enough variance in the outcome in this group. Even though we did not have statistical power to identify predictors of work ability in non-direct care workers, it is possible that OSS would still be relevant to their work ability as well. Since any changes in OSS would likely be made at an organizational level, they could affect the work environment of both direct care and non-direct care workers. Keeping this in mind, the finding that OSS predicted work ability in the longitudinal models is even more important.

According to the Conservation of Resources (COR) Theory [37], employees are motivated to retain and protect workplace resources that are most salient to their roles. It is possible that organizational safety climates serve as key resources for direct care staff, impacting their well-being and work ability. For non-direct care providers, organizational safety may be perceived as less directly relevant to their core work resources, thus contributing to the non-significant association observed in our analysis.

Organizational practices regarding safety should be a resource for employees to mitigate the effect of job demands [38]. Managerial safety practices, such as rule-oriented and participative leadership [21,39] have been associated with positive safety outcomes in other industries. Safe patient-handling programs in nursing homes resulted in reduced back pain, reduced compensation claims, and eventually turnover [40]. Therefore, management commitment to safety can have broader implications beyond injury prevention. The association of back pain with work ability in this material suggests that workplace safety can enhance both the person's performance and their confidence about remaining on the job.

In our study, the association of SaEL with work ability in HCWs shows how the effort of managing and suppressing ones' emotions impairs job performance. Suh and Punnett have demonstrated how SaEL predicts depression longitudinally, showing that SaEL is an important predictor of one's mental health. Primary prevention of SaEL is complicated due to the intrinsic interpersonal nature of healthcare work; however, organizations can take steps to make sure that employees are heard and feel protected when they are in a confrontational situation. This would be similar to some of the practices assessed by the OSS scale. More interventional studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of such efforts.

It is the responsibility of the organization to reduce this strain by implementing safety practices that are both consistent with the official messaging and effective in protecting workers.

Individual-oriented programs which seek to bolster HCW well-being, resilience, and peer support through behavioral interventions have met with limited success in improving mental health [41].

Limitations and strengths

This study utilized self-reported data on both exposures and the outcome; hence, common-method bias may exist. Individuals with reduced work ability might be more likely to exaggerate their levels of job demands and other stressors, leading to differential misclassification of exposures, i.e., information bias, and reverse causality in the cross-sectional analyses. Over one thousand employees were surveyed on each occasion, but the number for whom there were longitudinal data was somewhat limited. Further, the results might not be generalizable throughout the healthcare workforce since the study population consisted of largely of unionized employees from public-sector agencies providing chronic and residential care services.

This study was different from many prior studies of HCW work ability in that it had a moderate sample size, involved multiple sites, and included both clinical and nonclinical employees. Furthermore, we surveyed a wide range of occupational features and examined factors not often studied in relationship to work ability, specifically, OSS, surface-acting emotional labor, and back pain; others have studied the impact of injuries on variously defined work ability cross-sectionally. Measured constructs and scales were adapted from well-reviewed tools, studied for internal consistency, and found to be reliable.

Conclusions and future implications

The present findings support more organization-based interventions, such as improving safety policies and demonstrating commitment to safety in practice and improving the social environment at work by increasing coworker and supervisor support and civility. In our longitudinal results, a one-unit increase in OSS is associated with more than a half-unit gain in work ability, highlighting the practical significance of safety support interventions for direct care employees. These results indicate that, particularly for those with direct-care jobs, organizational safety support is likely to be impactful in real-world settings. Interventions in this area could potentially drive meaningful improvements in workforce capacity and well-being. With reference to the specific elements assessed by the OSS items, our findings support incorporating employee input into safety policies; accident reporting without scapegoating workers; and practicing participatory ergonomics by encouraging employees to take part in decision-making. Further, managerial skills and leadership training could be improved.

It is of great import for the management of healthcare institutions to invest in organizational policies promoting improved managerial commitment to employee safety. This would foster the safety culture of an organization-a cardinal resource for the betterment of worker well-being, thus leading to the building and sustaining of a healthy and resilient workforce. In the postpandemic work environment for HCWs, countries and organizations developing better safety policies and ensuring their implementation would improve the workers' perception of safety; these organizations would also have better work ability as opposed to those with a laissez-faire attitude toward worker safety.
