Predicting COVID‐19 Mortality Risk Among Cardiovascular Disease Patients Using Artificial Intelligence Algorithms: A Retrospective Study on Clinical Data
Authors: Raoof Nopour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimWith the transition of the COVID‐19 outbreak from a pandemic to an endemic state, people are now experiencing COVID‐19. COVID‐19 is associated with severe symptoms in infected populations with chronic diseases, and CVD patients are no exception to this rule. Early prediction of COVID‐19 mortality risk using AI‐based models is crucial for improving prognosis and survival rates by enabling more effective surveillance among CVD populations. This study aims to develop predictive models of COVID‐19 mortality risk and to provide insights into this topic.MethodsThis retrospective study used a single‐center database of 1255 CVD participants admitted to Shariati Hospital, affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS) in Tehran City, from February 2020 to November 2021. Several demographics, patients' clinical histories, clinical features, and laboratory findings were leveraged and assessed using univariate and adjusted correlation analyses to train AI algorithms to predict the risk of COVID‐19 death among CVD patients as the outcome variable. We leveraged the SHAP summary plot to introduce the AI's explainability (XAI).ResultsOur empirical results indicated that XGB achieved a PPV of 0.931, an NPV of 0.926, a sensitivity of 0.917, a specificity of 0.939, an accuracy of 0.929, an F‐score of 0.924, and an AUC of 0.864, outperforming other models. SHAP values indicated that age, pneumonia, ICU admission, type of surgery, and d‐dimer were the most significant predictors.ConclusionThe XGB model demonstrated greater potential to stratify at‐risk CVD patients on admission, particularly for COVID‐19 mortality, by better allocating clinical resources and improving the prognosis of COVID‐19 patients with this chronic condition, thereby achieving greater predictive performance and clinical usability.

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19), which first appeared in Wuhan, China, in late December 2019, has emerged as a highly contagious and pathogenic viral infection caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2), resulting in a global pandemic associated with significant loss of human life [1, 2]. This disease has had a devastating impact on the world, forcing a sharp adjustment to the new reality, including ways of living, working, and communicating. Additionally, the pandemic worldwide has overwhelmed healthcare systems [3]. It has caused more than 6.8 million deaths and 686 million confirmed cases, raising a global public health concern [4]. In addition to its health implications, the pandemic has posed considerable challenges to the environment, education, economy, and human psychology [5].

Nations have established various strategies to combat this pandemic, including social measures such as lockdowns, movement restrictions, government facility closures, quarantines, and individual precautions like maintaining distance and wearing masks [6]. They have adopted vaccines, followed by nonmedical measures, to combat this pandemic and reduce COVID‐19 mortality, as recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) [7]. The challenges posed by COVID‐19 have persisted despite the implementation of various preventive and therapeutic measures [8]. Also, contrary to the WHO declarations at the end of the COVID‐19 pandemic, it now accounts for an endemic affecting global health [9].

COVID‐19 patients with chronic diseases are more likely to experience more severe symptoms. A meta‐analysis by Geng et al. revealed that chronic diseases significantly worsen COVID‐19 severity and increase mortality risk [10]. Another meta‐analysis indicated a significant association between chronic conditions and increased COVID‐19 severity symptoms, accompanied by the need for ICU services [11]. The findings of Aslaner showed that accompanying chronic diseases pose an adverse influence on mortality risk among COVID‐19 patients, in addition to increasing the course of hospitalization and severity of disease [12].

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of death globally [13]. Even though COVID‐19 infection causes a mild illness for most people, some chronic diseases, such as CVD, are severely influenced. Therefore, it is essential to stratify infected individuals among these patients to develop more effective preventive strategies [14]. Concerning the mortality risk, one meta‐analysis by Hesami et al. has brought us insight into a significant association between COVID‐19 death and patients with CVD [15]. Bae et al., in one meta‐analysis, have observed that CVD and its risk determinants, such as diabetes and hypertension, have a strong correlation with fatal outcomes in COVID‐19 across all ages [16]. Also, these studies have mentioned that the risk of death increases several times in CVD patients infected with COVID‐19.

Proper management and monitoring of CVD are highly recommended to decrease mortality in patients infected with COVID‐19 [17]. CVD is considered a risk factor for the progression and prognosis of COVID‐19, diminishing the efficacy of medical therapies, and we are required to pay more attention to CVD patients to prevent extensive deterioration created by the disease [18]. So, early prediction of COVID‐19 death among CVD patients is crucial in optimizing the prognosis and establishing more effective surveillance measures based on these patients' characteristics, eventually leading to better clinical insights into prevention strategies for mortality.

Artificial intelligence (AI), as a highly efficient and promising scientific tool, is vital for optimizing the performance in the diagnosis and treatment of various diseases, including prediction and detection in healthcare [19]. Machine learning (ML), as a subfield of AI, has provided us with efficient predictive insights into various diseases [20]. It also enables software programs to improve forecasting accuracy without complex programming by leveraging historical data to predict new output values. Deep learning (DL), as a branch of ML, involves constructing training models that learn from patterns in sounds, text, or images using neural networks and enables the analysis of large‐scale volumes of data [21, 22].

Establishing an AI‐enabled model for COVID‐19 death risk prediction based on CVD patient parameters would assist healthcare providers with efficient assessment and the redistribution of medical services to enhance overall survival [23]. Previous studies have mainly focused on developing prediction models using DL or ML algorithms to assess the mortality risk of COVID‐19 among the general population or in other conditions, such as chronic kidney disease and heart failure, and limited research has been conducted on specific chronic conditions, such as CVD. Therefore, this study aims to develop predictive models to support the implementation of prevention strategies and enhance COVID‐19 survival among CVD patients.

Methods

This study adheres to the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) reporting guideline, aiming at harmonizing the landscape of forecasting model research and establishing transparent reporting of studies on constructing and validating predictive models using AI‐based algorithms for prediction purposes, regardless of the medical domain, predictors, or outcomes leveraged [24].

Study Design and Population

This study followed a retrospective type on CVD patients infected with COVID‐19, aiming to establish a prediction model for COVID‐19 death risk among these patients, which used a single‐center database. The data used in the current study comprised 1255 CVD patients admitted to Shariati Hospital, affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS), in Tehran City from February 2020 to November 2021. Among 1255 CVD patients who were infected with COVID‐19, 595 cases died after suffering from this disease, worsening symptoms, and hospitalization. Six hundred sixty cases survived after receiving inpatient services and going through a period of illness. The participants received various therapeutic measures, including an IL‐6 receptor antagonist monoclonal, intubation interventions, and RNA inhibitors.

Data Source

A single‐center database in Excel format was used to establish a prediction model. This data set included demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters of CVD patients as input features in the database to build prediction models. From there, we aimed to develop forecasting models for COVID‐19 mortality risk among patients with CVD; hence, the current study should assess the mortality status of all CVD patients infected with COVID‐19. The current research used COVID‐19 mortality status in CVD patients as an outcome variable, categorized into two groups (alive and deceased) and assigned 0 and 1 codes in the database, respectively, for all participants. To maintain the confidentiality of patient information, all records in the database were anonymized, meaning they were devoid of any identifying information, including names and record IDs. A consecutive number was assigned to each patient record in the database, and it did not reveal the patient's identity. This study investigated retrospective data, with variables' values at one point in time (2–3 days after COVID‐19 diagnosis confirmation and 30–35 days after positive diagnosis for the outcome variable).

Features Used to Establish the Prediction Model

The current database comprises 60 features used for data analysis, categorized into four primary types: demographic information, a history of personal diseases and other conditions, clinical factors, and laboratory parameters, as outlined in Table S1.

Preprocessing of the Data Set

We investigated the data set to assess its data quality and cleanliness. First, we excluded duplicate records from the databases that contained the same data for a single participant. Second, we manually accounted for missing values in the data set. In this regard, we reviewed all records for each CVD participant to determine whether any data were lost. The records with more than 10% missing data in their features were excluded from further analysis. In cases where records have less than 10% missing data, and the missing data is qualitative, we used the mode of the corresponding feature to fill the empty cell. Otherwise, we used the average value of the corresponding feature across each class to fill the empty cell in the quantitative data. Second, we endeavored to minimize the impact of noisy and invalid values from the current database for the analysis. Invalid features in the qualitative data were excluded, and missing values were imputed. To minimize the impact of noisy data on findings, we used the Min–Max method to scale features. This way, the ranges of the quantitative data would be transformed into a single prespecified range, for example, 0–1. Using Min–Max to normalize quantitative data preserves the original data's characteristics and stabilizes variance. Still, data would be normalized to standard scales, precluding the dominance of any single feature and mitigating the impact of noisy data.

Feature Selection

As a dimensionality reduction technique, feature selection aims to improve performance efficiency by selecting a subset of original database features, excluding irrelevant, redundant, or noisy features [25]. In this study, we used univariate (Phi and Crammer's V and eta) and multivariate (logistic regression) analyses to identify more critical risk factors for COVID‐19 mortality. initially used correlation‐based techniques to select input features. This way, after consulting with specialists, one less critical feature of the two highly correlated features was excluded from the analysis. Sixty features at this step were investigated using correlation‐based methods, and COVID‐19 mortality was analyzed using the Phi and Crammer's V and eta tests as univariate feature raters. These features were also included in the logistic regression to assess their association with the outcome, using multivariate feature scoring. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for reporting the significance of each feature and for selecting them for model construction.

Model Development and Assessment

The current study used ML and DL algorithms to build predictive models to estimate COVID‐19 mortality risk among CVD patients. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), long short‐term memory (LSTM), and deep neural networks (DNNs) were implemented to establish models using the DL technique. In contrast, random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGB), support vector machine (SVM), and decision tree (DT) algorithms were used as ML approaches.

DL algorithms, such as CNNs, are used to detect associations between features based on spatial characteristics and are more commonly used for image, text, and audio data [26]. They also exhibit optimized performance when handling tabular data and have been utilized in studies with favorable predictive performance [27, 28, 29]. These algorithms preserve a more profound association between features by extracting local features, such as images [30]. Conversely, ML algorithms are more comprehensible because they use simpler configurations and require less tuning effort. ML models exhibit robust predictive performance in handling tabular data, and their interpretability is more achievable than that of DL models [31]. Moreover, the exploratory comparison in the current study showed favorable predictive performance of DL algorithms with tabular data when applied to a structural data set of COVID‐19 deaths with clinical features for CVD patients, so we compared DL and ML algorithms to gain predictive insight into this topic.

Cross‐Validation (CV) and AI's Parameter Adjustment

The CV technique is leveraged to build AI‐based models that mitigate bias and instability during training, making it a preferred approach. In this study, we established prediction models using a 10‐fold CV. In this technique, the data set is randomly split into 10 equal folds. One fold is used for validation, and the other nine for training algorithms. As part of a comprehensive approach, this process is iterated 10 times, and the final algorithm's performance matrices represent the average rate across 10 iterations [32, 33]. To optimize the algorithms' parameters, a grid search CV was used. This parameter adjustment addresses the fit and score model implementation, optimizing it through a cross‐validated grid search over a parameter grid. In other words, an internal CV technique is used to compute the score for each parameter combination in AI algorithms [34]. We optimized the algorithms using Grid search CV to achieve higher training accuracy with a specified parameter combination, and the optimization was stopped when the algorithms reached a higher performance accuracy.

Model Explanation and Clinical Applicability

As black‐box AI‐based algorithms are widely used to make essential predictions in critical contexts, there is an increasing demand from various AI stakeholders for greater transparency to overcome unjustifiable and illegitimate decisions arising from vague AI behavior [35]. Despite the use of advanced AI algorithms and readily available training data, the lack of transparency in AI decision‐making has hindered the widespread adoption of AI in medicine, potentially replacing specialists in certain clinical functions [36]. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has enhanced the clinical applicability of AL algorithms by introducing internal processes and decision‐making mechanisms to diminish their black‐box nature [37]. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, a cooperative game‐theory approach is crucial in optimizing the explainability of AI models. They provide a flexible framework for understanding the minimal impact of specified features when building a prediction model [38]. Features with positive SHAP values influence forecasting positively, while those with negative values negatively impact the prediction task [39]. This method provides a thorough understanding of feature significance across the entire data set [40].

SHAP Summary Plot

As mentioned, the XGB model achieved higher PPV, NPV, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F‐score, and AUC in predicting the mortality risk of COVID‐19 among patients with CVD. Based on the XGB model, a SHAP summary plot was generated to identify the most significant predictors of COVID‐19 mortality risk. Figure 7 indicates SHAP summary plots illustrating SHAP values of individual predictors of mortality risk. As shown by the SHAP values, age, pneumonia, ICU admission, type of surgery, and d‐dimer were the top‐ranking significant factors.

SHAP summary plot: The SHAP value plot is used to explain how a machine learning model arrived at a particular prediction. In this plot, each feature contributes to the model's output, either increasing or decreasing it. A positive SHAP sign indicates that the feature increased the prediction, and a negative sign indicates that it decreased it. The absolute value of the SHAP value indicates the strength of the feature's influence on the model's decision. By examining these values, we can compare the relative importance of features. Each point in the summary plot is a Shapley value for a feature and a sample. The y‐axis and x‐axis are determined by the feature and the SHAP value, respectively. The blue and red colors show each feature value from low to high, with negative and positive impacts on the outcome prediction. The features are ordered by importance from top to bottom.

Discussion

This study used AI‐based algorithms to develop predictive models for COVID‐19 mortality among CVD patients. Several DL and ML models, including CNN, DNN, LSTM, XGB, RF, DT, and SVM, were trained using demographics, personal history, clinical manifestations, and laboratory parameters to achieve more robust predictive models with higher predictive performance. To achieve greater explainability of prediction models and increased responsibility for clinical usability and decision‐making, we leveraged the SHAP summary plot to indicate the importance of individual predictors for prediction purposes in this context. XGB provided satisfactory predictive performance for COVID‐19 death risk among CVD participants, with a PPV of 0.931, an NPV of 0.926, a sensitivity of 0.917, a specificity of 0.939, an accuracy of 0.929, an F‐score of 0.924, and an AUC of 0.864. Additionally, DL models achieved favorable performance criteria, ranging from 0.767 to 0.903, with an average AUC of approximately 0.8. Although DL models yielded more predictive performance and greater reliability in predicting COVID‐19 mortality risk among CVD patients, XGB, as an ensemble ML model, demonstrated higher predictive efficiency and reliability than other AI models.

COVID‐19 is one of the most dangerous diseases in recorded human history due to its high contagiousness and rapid spread. In addition, people with preexisting medical conditions, such as CVD, are at higher risk of serious illness. So, leveraging efficient predictive solutions is crucial to the early identification of patients at increased risk of COVID‐19 death and advocates decreasing death as soon as possible. Moreover, it facilitates optimal decision‐making and provides accurate mortality predictions for COVID‐19 patients with CVD [43]. In the current study, XGB showed an efficient predictive performance in achieving these aims. As an ensemble ML algorithm, XGB is superior to DL algorithms in handling complex data distributions in high‐dimensional feature spaces, thereby enhancing classification performance on structured data sets [44].

The patients with chronic diseases are exposed to a lower quality of life and complicated prognosis and require more attention to decrease death risk, especially when infected with COVID‐19 due to increased related comorbidities and disease severity in COVID‐19 patients [45, 46]. CVD is considered a potential risk factor for the increased risk of COVID‐19 mortality. In one study by Bae et al., it has been shown that CVD and its risk factors, such as hypertension and diabetes, are highly associated with fatal outcomes in patients with COVID‐19 across all ages [47]. Mishra et al. have indicated that CVD harms health status regarding the increased COVID‐19 severity and mortality [48]. Maddaloni et al. have found that CVD has a high risk of COVID‐19 mortality in patients hospitalized due to COVID‐19, considering other comorbidities [49]. Tessitore et al. concluded that a history of CVD is linked to higher in‐hospital death and adverse CVD events in COVID‐19 [50]. Cordero et al. have demonstrated, based on their analysis, that CVD and COVID‐19 increase the risk of death by fourfold [51].

This study used demographics, personal history, clinical features, and laboratory parameters to train AI‐based algorithms to predict the mortality risk of COVID‐19 among patients with CVD. Elshennawy et al. leveraged clinical data to train DL‐based algorithms, including clinical manifestations, preexisting conditions, and demographics of 12,020 COVID‐19 patients. Their results indicated that the IMG‐CNN, with an average accuracy of 94.14%, a precision of 100%, a recall of 91.0%, a specificity of 100%, an F1‐score of 95.3%, an AUC of 93.6%, and a loss of 0.22, was the best model for predicting COVID‐19 mortality risk [52]. The majority of the input features used to establish prediction models in our study, including demographics, medical history, and clinical manifestations, align with those reported in Elshennawy's study among the general population. Like Elshennawy, we found that DL algorithms are efficient for predicting COVID‐19 mortality risk when applied to structured data. Although the current research shows that XGB performs better on this topic, DL models provide more reliable and efficient predictive performance for mortality risk, indicating their clinical usability for predicting COVID‐19 mortality risk when dealing with routine table data and image types. Gao et al. achieved AUC values ranging from 0.9186 to 0.9762 with an ensemble model using these clinical parameters [53]. Leveraging a data set of more than 2,670,000 laboratory‐confirmed COVID‐19 participants from 146 countries, including symptoms, preexisting conditions, and demographics data, Pourhomayoun et al. have attained an overall accuracy of 89.98% using simple NNs [54].

The laboratory parameters enhance the generalizability and predictive efficiency of AI‐based algorithms.

In a predictive analysis conducted by Gao et al. among CKD patients regarding COVID‐19 mortality risk, using laboratory parameters yielded an ROC curve of 0.833, sensitivity of 0.952, and specificity of 0.714, with LASSO coefficients and an ML approach. Also, Li et al., in one retrospective study on ML models for the prediction of COVID‐19 death risk in chronic heart failure (CHF) disease (CHD) patients, obtained several laboratory parameters, such as SPO2, hemoglobin, partial thromboplastin time, and prothrombin time, as the significant factors using the Bourta algorithm [55]. In Amiri et al.'s study on developing ML‐based prediction models for patients with chronic comorbidities, GB models achieved 84.15% accuracy and outperformed other models in predicting COVID‐19 death risk. Despite achieving satisfactory predictive performance, they stated that prognostic factors, such as laboratory indicators, are critical for further improving ML model performance [56]. The current study has yielded significant associations for most laboratory parameters, as indicated by both univariate and adjusted correlation analyses. Therefore, leveraging laboratory factors is crucial for improving predictive efficiency, especially when assessing data on chronic conditions.

XAI enhances clinical decision‐making in the healthcare industry by providing transparency and interpretability into AI‐generated outputs, enabling users to understand and trust them. XAI ensures transparency, accuracy, and accountability of AI models in practice across various medical contexts [57, 58, 59]. Tailored XAI approaches can meet the distinct requirements of different medical domains by enhancing diagnostic precision and supporting treatment decisions [60]. SHAP, as a cooperative game‐theoretic method, focuses on participants' cooperation, assigning each participant a score for achieving the shared goal, and estimating the average level of collaboration required to reach it. This method estimates the contribution of each feature to a black‐box model's output, thereby enhancing the explainability of AI models by improving their comprehensibility and the process by which these predictions are generated [61, 62]. SHAP is advantageous for improving clinical decision‐making in healthcare settings by presenting feature importance and feature influence in a more visually effective manner, thereby promoting physicians' understanding of various clinical outcomes [63]. To enhance the clinical usability and applicability of AI‐based prediction models, we leveraged SHAP values, a state‐of‐the‐art approach for visualizing and demonstrating the clinical importance of individual predictors, to assess COVID‐19 mortality risk among patients with CVD. In the current study, SHAP values indicated that age, pneumonia, ICU admission, type of surgery, and d‐dimer were the most critical factors. Although the type of surgery is considered an important risk factor for this, it may indicate the severity of the underlying disease rather than a direct predictor. However, this factor is associated with increased risk of COVID‐19 mortality in these patients in the current study in addition to the increased risk of the severity of CVD. Age is a significant factor in COVID‐19 severity, and many scientists and media outlets have emphasized age as a key risk factor for COVID‐19 mortality [64]. Kremer and Thurner clearly reported a significant correlation between age and COVID‐19 deaths [65]. Hashem et al., citing two studies in their introduction, noted that age over 65 and comorbidities are more significant prognostic factors for COVID‐19 [66]. Biswas et al. concluded that COVID‐19 patients aged > 50 years are 15.4‐fold at increased risk of mortality compared with those with age < 50 (p < 0.00001) [67]. One study by Yanes et al. on 178,568 COVID‐19 deaths revealed that the incident rate ratio (IRR) was 8.1 times higher (95%CI = 7.7–8.5) in patients with 55–64 years old and more than 62 times higher (95%CI = 59.7–64.7) in patients having more than 65 years old compared with patients with age < 55 years [68], so regarding the clinical decision making as obtained in our study, it is essential that future tailored choices be focused on the demographics of populations, and especially consider the incidence of this disease among participants having more than 65 years old. Wang et al.'s study on the development and interpretation of AI algorithms for COVID‐19 mortality and morbidity showed that being aged 75 years and over is one of three critical factors for predicting COVID‐19 death risk [69]. Two studies also recognized age among four crucial variables for predicting COVID‐19 mortality risk [41, 70].

Pneumonia was considered another significant feature based on the current study's XAI. Bilateral pneumonia, as identified on CT scans and x‐rays, was recognized as a potential risk factor for progression among COVID‐19 patients in Zhang et al.'s study [71]. Nseir et al.'s study of 568 SARS‐CoV‐2 pneumonia patients found that those receiving mechanical ventilation in the ICU were more likely to have a longer mechanical ventilation duration and an increased risk of COVID‐19 mortality [72]. Pneumonia potentially affects mortality risk among COVID‐19 patients and increases the risk of COVID‐19 death by up to 14% due to acute respiratory failure [73]. AI models predicted COVID‐19 mortality risk with an AUC of 0.81 using pneumonia indicators such as the pneumonia severity index (PSI). AI algorithms that use prognostic factors for pneumonia patients can automatically screen them without requiring additional data or disrupting clinical workflow. Patient alerts can be set to desired thresholds for alert sensitivity and specificity, as needed, across various care environments. As a clinical decision‐support tool, AI‐based algorithms using pneumonia indicators can assist clinicians in navigating the complexities of COVID‐19‐associated resource allocation [74]. More specifically, regarding the pneumonia patients' characteristics requiring ventilation measures during hospitalizations, individualized predictive models to assess these patients getting COVID‐19 are crucial in better allocation of very constrained medical resources, designing targeted policies to prioritize vulnerable patients, and decisions on diagnostic tests, hospital admission, and therapeutic measures in the ICU [75].

In the current research, ICU admission has been identified as a top‐ranking predictor of COVID‐19 mortality among patients with CVD, highlighting its importance for improving COVID‐19 prognostication in these patients. ICU hospitalized patients are more exposed to mortality than others due to the existence of some comorbidities and usually chronic conditions in ICU patients, requiring several special therapies for them. Therefore, identifying patients who are at high risk of severe illness and ICU admission is crucial for effective strategic planning and resource allocation [76]. Given an ascending trend of COVID‐19 severity symptoms and its poor prognosis among CVD patients, and also an increased likelihood of ICU admission among this population, paying attention to this risk factor will play an essential role in improving decision‐making by clinicians, managers, and policymakers at different levels of the health industry.

Another significant predictor in this study was the type of cardiovascular surgery specific to CVD. It should be noted that COVID‐19 patients with preexisting CVD are among the most commonly admitted to the ICU and finally suffer from a higher death rate [77]. Due to the high burden of CVD among COVID‐19 patients and the high rate of mortality, proper management and monitoring of these patients for acute cardiac conditions is highly suggested to decrease the mortality and improve the prognosis [78]. Some cardiac invasive interventions significantly impact the pandemic prognosis and increase the likelihood of mortality. Kaplan et al.'s study of 7269 patients who underwent cardiac surgery found a significant association between cardiac surgery and an increased risk of death in COVID‐19 patients (p < 0.001), which may influence higher costs in a resource‐constrained healthcare system [79]. Aksu's study on 1171 infected patients who underwent open‐heart surgery indicated that postoperative COVID‐19 infection is associated with a high rate of pneumonia and mortality among CVD patients, especially non‐vaccinated ones [80]. To reduce the risk of COVID‐19 mortality, particularly among postoperative cardiac patients, we require robust pre‐surgery diagnostic protocols and efficient strategies to establish a COVID‐19‐free environment, which are invaluable for achieving safe and continuous delivery of cardiac surgery [81].

As a laboratory indicator, the d‐dimer test is a crucial predictor of COVID‐19 mortality and progression, as indicated in several meta‐analyses and case‐control studies [82, 83, 84, 85]. In other words, the elevated d‐dimer has a strong relationship with increased COVID‐19 death risk. Additionally, a d‐dimer level on admission higher than 2.0 µg/mL can effectively predict in‐hospital mortality from COVID‐19, indicating that this laboratory parameter is a quick, inexpensive, and easily accessible biological test for enhancing the monitoring of COVID‐19 patients [86, 87]. Measuring and controlling d‐dimer levels in COVID‐19 patients may help clinicians implement rapid medical interventions and accurately predict COVID‐19 prognosis [88]. Regarding the clinical utility of this critical test, several studies aimed at developing prediction models to identify high‐risk patients for COVID‐19‐related death early have found that this factor plays a satisfactory role in improving the predictive performance of AI models [41, 89, 90]. Although d‐dimer has not yet demonstrated strong predictive power in similar studies among CKD patients [91], the current study shows that d‐dimer is among the top‐ranking predictors of COVID‐19 mortality among CVD patients. Therefore, continuous monitoring of CVD patients using this biomarker test is essential for enhancing COVID‐19 prognosis.

Current research shows that AI models efficiently predict COVID‐19 mortality risk among CVD populations. Additionally, the clinical usability of XGB as a high‐performing model was demonstrated using SHAP values and top‐ranking features, which were extracted to evaluate the predictive ability of these risk factors for determining COVID‐19 mortality risk among CVD populations. Additionally, the significance of predictors such as age, ICU admission, pneumonia, and d‐dimer was consistent with that in general populations. Moreover, cardiac surgery type is recognized as a specific risk factor among CVD participants, indicating its clinical applicability and its effect on COVID‐19 prognosis, as discussed in this section.

AI‐based predictive models are advantageous for predicting various clinical conditions, such as COVID‐19 mortality risk in different populations, including CVD patients, by analyzing multi‐source data, including vital signs, laboratory results, and patient records, in clinical practice. They can be used as satisfactory triage tools to prioritize COVID‐19 patients at risk of mortality, predict clinical deterioration, and optimize resource allocation for better prognosis. Integrating AI models as a knowledge source with electronic health records (EHRs) for clinical decision‐making enables real‐time updates to predictions and more efficient clinical decision support for healthcare providers. It provides care providers with more enhanced individual diagnostic and therapeutic measures for these patients, leading to a better prognosis. Also, explainable AI can improve the clinical applicability and interpretability of these models in clinical practice. The Obstacles to implementation in clinical practice include poor record quality, low data quality, and biases in educational data, which hinder the establishment of predictive models using AI algorithms. Organizational challenges include user resistance to accepting AI‐based approaches in practice, additional workloads, and a lack of appropriate technical infrastructure and data governance. Additionally, ethical, legal, and privacy considerations, along with the need for external validation and post‐deployment monitoring, limit sustainable implementation.

Despite obtaining several predictive insights, this study has some constraints that should be addressed. First, we used a single‐center, retrospective database rather than a prospective cohort study, and developed prediction models for COVID‐19 death risk among CVD patients. This type of data limits the generalizability of the established models to some extent, as it relies on data from a single center in a single region. It may lose its performance efficiency in other regions and even other clinical centers. We suggest using data from additional clinical centers in other regions to mitigate this constraint. Moreover, using a prospective study increases the generalizability of model performance by accounting for all relevant risk factors during data collection, which is an important limitation of retrospective studies, which may not capture all relevant factors from a retrospective database. Second, we did not perform an external validation cohort because no external data set from another clinical center was available for these specific populations. For future studies, we recommend using data from other centers to highlight this important aspect and to evaluate the performance, efficiency, and generalizability of the constructed prediction models in other clinical environments.

Conclusion

The current study aims to develop an AI‐based predictive model for COVID‐19 mortality among CVD patients, incorporating demographics, clinical features, medical history, and laboratory parameters. Our experimental results showed that XGB is more competent and reliable for prediction purposes in this context. Additionally, the clinical applicability of XGB was assessed using SHAP values, which indicated that age, pneumonia, ICU admission, and d‐dimer are crucial factors for assessing the usability and clinical decision‐making in predicting COVID‐19 death risk among CVD populations. The XGB prediction model, equipped with a comprehensive set of predictors, can efficiently identify at‐risk patients upon admission, thereby enhancing the prognosis of COVID‐19 patients with chronic conditions such as CVD.
