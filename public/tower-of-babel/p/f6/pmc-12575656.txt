Machine learning integration of multi-modal radiomics and clinical factors predicts refracture risk after percutaneous kyphoplasty in postmenopausal women
Authors: Bin Zheng, Panfeng Yu, Zhenqi Zhu, Yan Liang, Haiying Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the use of radiomic features extracted from preoperative T2-weighted MRI and CT images, combined with machine learning models, to predict the risk of vertebral refracture after percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) in postmenopausal women. We retrospectively collect data from 156 postmenopausal women with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) who underwent PKP (35 refracture cases, 121 non-refracture controls). All patients had preoperative lumbar T2-weighted MRI and CT scans. We extract MRI and CT radiomic features and constructed radiomic signatures through feature selection. Key clinical factors (age, body mass index [BMI], vertebral CT Hounsfield unit [HU] values, smoking history, diabetes history, alcohol use, etc.) are used to build clinical prediction models. Various machine learning classifiers (Support Vector Machine [SVM], K-Nearest Neighbors [KNN], Random Forest [RF], ExtraTrees, XGBoost, LightGBM, Multi-layer Perceptron [MLP]) are trained on the radiomic signatures and clinical factors. Model performance was evaluated on an independent test set using area under the ROC curve (AUC) as the primary metric. Accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and other measures on the test set were compared between radiomic models, clinical models, and a combined model. The refracture group (n = 35, 22.4%) is significantly older (72.09 ± 4.25 vs 70.11 ± 3.31 years, P = 0.002) with lower vertebral bone density (97.00 ± 6.31 vs 102.49 ± 4.68 HU, P < 0.001). Among individual algorithms, the KNN clinical model achieves optimal performance (AUC = 0.74), while the SVM radiomics model demonstrates the best accuracy (AUC = 0.798, accuracy = 0.839, sensitivity = 0.857, specificity = 0.833). The combined model achieves superior performance (AUC = 0.886), significantly outperforming both standalone models. Multi-modal radiomics combined with key clinical factors provides superior prediction of refracture risk after PKP. Introduction

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) are a common and serious complication in elderly women. Percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) can rapidly relieve fracture pain and restore vertebral stability. However, the incidence of new vertebral fractures after PKP remains high—reportedly around 20%1,2. Refractures not only cause patients renewed pain and disability risks but also increase medical costs and mortality3. Therefore, there is an urgent need to predict which patients are prone to refractures either before or shortly after surgery, so that preventive measures can be implemented to improve outcomes.

Traditionally, clinicians assess fracture risk based on risk factors such as bone mineral density T-scores, age, and previous fracture history. However, traditional clinical models show limited accuracy in predicting refracture risk after vertebroplasty. On one hand, individual differences in bone quality among patients are difficult to fully reflect using single indicators like bone mineral density alone; on the other hand, postoperative changes in mechanical distribution and cement effects make risk assessment more complex4–6. Therefore, an objective quantitative method that integrates more information is needed to enhance predictive performance.

Radiomics has emerged as a research hotspot in recent years7. It quantitatively extracts numerous features from medical images that are difficult to perceive with the naked eye (such as texture, shape, intensity distribution, etc.) and uses machine learning to construct models for disease diagnosis, prognosis assessment, and other applications8. In the field of osteoporosis and fractures, radiomics has the potential to reflect bone microstructure and quality9. For example, CT images can provide information about trabecular bone density and distribution10; MRI (especially T2-weighted images) can show changes in bone marrow composition and water content, all of which may be related to bone fragility11. In recent years, radiomics has been applied for OVCF. Yu and Pan applies CT radiomics methods to identify high-risk patients for OVCF12,13. Yang applies CT radiomics to predict adjacent vertebra fracture14. Cai reports MRI radiomics to assessment of the imminent new vertebral fracture after vertebral augmentation15. However, there lacks combination of CT and MR radiomics in OVCF.

This study aims to utilize multi-modal radiomics features from preoperative MRI T2WI sequences and CT images, combined with various machine learning algorithms, to establish a prediction model for refracture risk after PKP in postmenopausal women. The researchers compare the predictive performance of radiomics models with traditional clinical models to verify the added value of radiomics in this field.

Materials and methods

Patient population

This retrospective study is approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Peking University People’s Hospital. This study complies with all principles and regulations of the Helsinki Declaration. All patients involved in the study consent to participate in the study. Written informed consent has been obtained from all the patients.

It includes postmenopausal women patients who undergo PKP for osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures at our hospital from January 2018 to December 2023. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥ 55 years, female, with confirmed postmenopausal osteoporosis diagnosis; (2) Single-segment PKP treatment; (3) CT and plain MRI examination including T2-weighted sequences within 1 month before surgery; (4) Follow-up imaging data for at least 1 year after surgery to determine the presence of new vertebral fractures. Exclusion criteria: (1) Fractures caused by non-osteoporotic reasons such as tumors or infections; (2) Previous history of multiple vertebral fractures affecting assessment; (3) Incomplete or poor-quality imaging data. Patients are divided into refracture and non-refracture groups based on whether new vertebral compression fractures occur during follow-up.

The entire dataset of 156 samples is randomly divided into training and data, we implement the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE). This method is crucial for augmenting the minority class in the dataset by synthesizing new samples, thereby achieving a balance in the class distribution. This balanced dataset is then used to enhance the performance of our classification models.

Image acquisition

The study flowgram is shown in Fig. 1. All patients undergo preoperative MRI and CT examinations. MRI uses a 3.0 T magnetic resonance imaging system for sagittal T2-weighted imaging (T2WI) of the spine with slice thickness of approximately 4 mm; CT examination uses a 64-slice spiral CT for plain scanning with slice thickness of 1.25 mm. MRI and CT images are reconstructed to the same anatomical plane for subsequent feature extraction and registration analysis. For each patient, the fractured vertebra is selected as the research target. This study focuses on the characteristics of the initial fracture and surgical vertebra, so the region of interest (ROI) is chosen as the trabecular bone region of the preoperative fractured vertebra. The contours of fractured vertebrae are manually delineated as ROIs on both MRI T2WI and CT images, trying to exclude interference from adjacent intervertebral discs, appendages, and cortical bone.

Flowchart summarizing patient selection and allocation to the training cohort and test cohort.

To assess segmentation reproducibility, 30 randomly selected cases were independently delineated by two spine surgeons (A and B). Observer A repeated the segmentation after a two-week interval to evaluate intra-observer variability. The reproducibility of ROI delineation was quantified using the Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) for spatial overlap and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for extracted radiomics features. An ICC greater than 0.90 was considered indicative of excellent agreement. The results of DSC and ICC are calculated in Supplementary Table1.

Radiomics feature extraction and selection

High-throughput radiomics features are extracted from each ROI using tools such as Pyradiomics. Features include first-order statistical features (such as intensity histogram mean, skewness, etc.), texture features (based on Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix GLCM, Gray Level Run Length Matrix GLRLM, Gray Level Size Zone Matrix GLSZM, etc.), and features after preprocessing with wavelet filtering, logarithmic transformation, LoG filtering, LBP filtering, etc. Hundreds of candidate features are extracted from MRI and CT for each patient. Features are first standardized using Z-score normalization, then dimensionality reduction is performed using feature selection methods: univariate analysis is first performed in the training set to eliminate features unrelated to refracture, then Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression is used to select the most predictive feature subset from highly correlated features. Based on these selected features, radiomics signatures are constructed through weighted linear combination, i.e., each patient can calculate a radiomics score for risk prediction.

Model construction

This study constructs pure radiomics models and pure clinical models respectively to predict refracture risk. Radiomics models are constructed based on the above radiomics features/signatures using various machine learning algorithms, including Support Vector Machine (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest (RF), Extra Trees (ExtraTrees), Extreme Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (XGBoost), LightGBM Gradient Boosting Machine, and Multi-layer Perceptron neural network (MLP), totaling 7 algorithms (hyperparameters are optimized through fivefold cross-validation on the training set). The cross-validation results are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Clinical models are constructed based on patients’ clinical risk factors. Information such as age, BMI, fractured vertebral CT Hounsfield unit value, smoking history (yes/no), diabetes history, alcohol consumption history, etc., is first recorded for each patient, and univariate analysis is performed to screen candidate variables. Subsequently, the same approach is used to construct various machine learning algorithms for the selected clinical variables. The cross-validation results are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Model training and validation

The total dataset is randomly divided into training set (approximately 80%, n = 125) and test set (approximately 20%, n = 31), ensuring no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two parts. When training various machine learning models on the training set, appropriate undersampling or cost-sensitive learning strategies are adopted to balance positive and negative sample ratios (due to fewer samples in the refracture group). Model hyperparameters are determined through five-fold cross-validation. After the final models are trained on the training set, performance evaluation is conducted on the test set that did not participate in modeling. Model output is the probability of predicting refracture, which is converted to binary classification results (refracture or non-refracture) through optimal thresholds predetermined on the training set. To intuitively compare model performance, ROC curves for each model are drawn on the test set, and AUC values and 95% confidence intervals are calculated.

Radiomic nomogram

Radiomic nomogram is established in combination with radiomic signature and clinical signature. The diagnostic efficacy of radiomic nomogram is tested in test cohort, ROC curves are drawn to evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of nomogram.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 and Python (scikit-learn and other libraries) are used for statistical analysis and model construction. Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and independent sample t-tests are used for between-group comparisons; categorical data are expressed as numbers and percentages, and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests are used for between-group comparisons. Model predictive performance is evaluated using AUC, and sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value are calculated from confusion matrices. DeLong tests are used on the test set to compare whether differences in AUC between different models are statistically significant, with significance level set at P < 0.05.

Results

General patient characteristics

The patients selection process is shown in Fig. 1. Table1 summarizes the baseline chrematistic of included patients. A total of 156 elderly women patients are included, with 35 cases in the refracture group (22.4%) and 121 cases in the non-refracture control group (77.6%). The two groups show significant differences in age distribution at surgery: the refracture group age is 72.09 ± 4.25 years, significantly higher than the control group’s 70.11 ± 3.31 years (P = 0.002). The BMI means of the two groups are 20.59 ± 1.53 and 20.35 ± 1.44 respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.191). Regarding osteoporosis severity, using vertebral CT value (HU) as a bone density indicator, results show that the average CT value of fractured vertebrae in the refracture group is 97.00 ± 6.31 HU, significantly lower than the control group’s 102.49 ± 4.68 HU (P < 0.001). In terms of lifestyle and comorbidities, the positive rates of smoking history (refracture group 28.6% vs control group 15.7%), alcohol consumption history (17.1% vs 8.3%), and diabetes history (11.4% vs 9.9%, P = 0.795) do not reach statistical significance (P = 0.090, 0.127 respectively); proportions are similar in both groups (11.4% vs 9.9%, P = 0.795). These results suggest that refracture patients tend to be older with lower bone density, while BMI, smoking, diabetes, alcohol consumption, and other factors do not show significant associations with refracture risk.

Discussion

In this study, we develop a multi-modal model combining radiomic features from MRI and CT with clinical factors to predict vertebral refractures after PKP in osteoporotic postmenopausal women. Our findings demonstrate that models incorporating radiomic features substantially improved prediction of refractures compared to models using only clinical variables. Moreover, the best performance is achieved by a combined model that fused both radiomic and clinical inputs, highlighting the complementary value of each data type.

Consistent with established knowledge in osteoporosis, we found that patients in the refracture group were older and had lower vertebral bone density (HU values) than those in the non-refracture group. Age-related bone loss and microarchitectural deterioration likely contribute to increased fracture susceptibility16,17. Likewise, a lower HU measurement of the vertebral body on CT—reflecting reduced trabecular density and bone strength—has been associated with higher fracture risk18,19. On the other hand, factors such as BMI, smoking, alcohol use, and diabetes did not differ significantly between the two groups in our study. While these factors can influence bone health and fracture risk in general, their effects may have been less evident in our cohort, possibly due to the relatively homogeneous characteristics of the population (all were older women with osteoporosis) or the limited sample size. Still, our model confirms that age and bone density are important predictors of refracture risk, warranting their inclusion in risk assessment.

The radiomics approach provided additional insights into bone quality that are not captured by conventional measures like mean BMD or HU alone. Advanced imaging texture features can quantify the heterogeneity and structural patterns of trabecular bone, which play a critical role in bone strength20,21. Gray-level textures in CT images can reflect the uniformity of vertebral trabecular distribution, the interface complexity between cortical and cancellous bone, etc22,23. For example, the "Small Area Low Gray Level Emphasis" feature selected in this study quantitatively describes the aggregation degree of low-density small patches in images24. This indicator shows higher values in refracture group vertebrae, suggesting more local low-density areas in bone tissue, possibly corresponding to sparse trabeculae and microstructural deterioration, thereby increasing refracture risk. T2-weighted MRI images mainly reflect bone marrow composition and water content changes25,26. As osteoporosis worsens, bone marrow fat content relatively increases and blood supply decreases, causing changes in T2WI signal characteristics. Some MRI texture and statistical features entering the model (such as energy, aggregation intensity, etc.) may reflect bone marrow signal uniformity and subtle changes27. Overall, multi-modal radiomics combines CT’s bone density structural information with MRI’s bone marrow tissue information, providing comprehensive bone quality assessment and thus outperforming in refracture risk prediction.

As shown in Fig. 5, the most influential features in our model are derived from CT, with both the strongest risk-enhancing and protective factors originating from CT texture metrics. Features such as Small Area Low Gray Level Emphasis and Low Gray Level Zone Emphasis are associated with higher refracture risk, reflecting clustered low-density trabecular regions that indicate structural fragility. In contrast, CT features describing greater uniformity and higher-density trabecular distribution show protective effects, highlighting the stabilizing role of compact microarchitecture. Compared with CT, MRI features contribute less, though they provide complementary information on marrow composition and physiology. These findings underscore CT’s dominant role in capturing trabecular bone integrity, while MRI serves as a supportive modality, and their integration provides a more comprehensive and interpretable assessment of refracture risk after PKP.

In addition to the stand-alone radiomics and clinical models, our study demonstrates that the combined model integrating radiomics features with clinical factors (age and vertebral CT HU) achieves the best overall performance, with an AUC of 0.886 in the independent test set. The superior performance of the combined model underscores the complementary value of clinical and imaging-derived information. While radiomics features capture quantitative descriptors of trabecular microarchitecture and marrow composition that are difficult to measure otherwise, clinical indicators such as age and bone density remain robust, well-validated predictors of skeletal fragility. Their integration enhances both the sensitivity and specificity of refracture prediction, as radiomics features can compensate for the limitations of traditional clinical variables, while clinical data improve the interpretability and stability of imaging-based models. This synergy is particularly important in small-sample, high-dimensional settings, where over-reliance on radiomics features alone may risk overfitting. Thus, the combined model not only provides higher predictive accuracy but also offers greater clinical credibility and translational potential, supporting its role as a practical tool for individualized risk stratification in postmenopausal women undergoing PKP.

The study’s limitations include: First, the test set sample size is relatively small (n = 31), with only 8 cases in the refracture group, which limits the statistical power and reliability of model performance evaluation. The small sample size may lead to wide confidence intervals for AUC values, creating uncertainty in the assessment of model generalizability. In particular, the estimates of sensitivity and specificity may exhibit considerable variability and require further validation in larger independent cohorts. In the future, larger, multi-center cohorts and external validation are required to further confirm the generalizability of our models. Second, several models (such as XGBoost and ExtraTrees) demonstrated perfect or near-perfect performance on the training set but showed significant deterioration on the test set (e.g., XGBoost AUC dropped from 1.000 to 0.565). This strongly indicates the presence of overfitting, which is a critical concern for model generalizability. The dramatic performance gap between training and testing suggests that these complex algorithms may have memorized training data patterns rather than learning generalizable features. Third, fat-suppressed T1WI could also provide valuable information, not all patients had this sequence in our retrospective cohort. To ensure consistency and comparability, we limited the analysis to T2WI. In future, combined T2-MRI and fat-suppressed-MRI could provide more information. Fourth, as a retrospective study, refracture determination relies on follow-up imaging, potentially missing refracture cases due to delayed medical visits, causing biased outcome assessment. Additionally, the researchers currently only select imaging features of preoperative fractured vertebrae, not considering factors such as postoperative cement distribution and adjacent vertebral stress changes on refracture. Future work can incorporate postoperative imaging features, cement-related parameters, etc., into models to further improve predictive ability. Despite these limitations, this study pioneers the application of multi-modal MRI and CT radiomics to refracture prediction after vertebroplasty, achieving clinically meaningful accuracy improvements.

Conclusion

This study established a multi-modal radiomics approach combining preoperative MRI and CT features to predict refracture risk after percutaneous kyphoplasty in postmenopausal women. We developed three prediction models: a clinical model based on age and vertebral CT Hounsfield unit values, a radiomics model using 21 selected imaging features, and a combined radiomics-clinical model. The combined model achieved superior performance (AUC = 0.886) compared to the clinical model (AUC = 0.74) and radiomics model alone (AUC = 0.798), demonstrating the synergistic value of integrating quantitative imaging features with established clinical risk factors. This approach provides clinicians with an objective tool for individualized risk stratification, representing a significant advancement toward precision medicine in osteoporotic fracture management.
