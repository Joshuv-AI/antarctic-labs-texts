Trends and determinants of tuberculosis treatment outcomes among patients at Hargeisa tuberculosis hospital, Somaliland (2019–2024): a multi-year retrospective study
Authors: Conrad Murendo, Faith Ngima, Farhan Mohamed Osman, Norbert Rakiro, Michael Lei Abaasiku, Gagik Karapetyan, Suganya Kimbrough, Abdirahman Muse Alin, Ahmed Abdirahman Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a major public health concern in Somaliland, and evidence on multi-year treatment outcomes is limited. This study assessed temporal trends and factors associated with TB treatment outcomes among patients managed at Hargeisa TB Hospital, Somaliland.MethodsWe conducted a register-based retrospective cohort study using routinely collected data from the Hargeisa TB Hospital treatment registers for patients initiating anti-TB treatment from 2019 to 2024. TB treatment outcomes were dichotomised as successful (cured or treatment completed) versus unsuccessful (death, loss to follow-up, treatment failure). Descriptive analyses were performed to present patient characteristics, while bivariate and multivariate logistic regression models were used to identify variables linked to treatment success. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsAcross the six-year period, treatment success rates remained above 85%. Significant predictors of treatment outcomes varied by year. Between 2020 and 2024, age consistently predicted outcomes, with patients aged ≥ 55 years significantly less likely to achieve successful treatment (AOR range: 0.24–0.44). HIV-negative patients had up to fourfold higher odds of treatment success (AOR = 4.30; 95% CI: 1.76–10.49; p = 0.001).ConclusionTB treatment success consistently met or exceeded the World Health Organization benchmark of ≥ 85% across all study years. However, older age and TB/HIV co-infection remained associated with reduced treatment success even after stratification by TB type. These findings highlight the need to strengthen TB/HIV service integration and to provide enhanced clinical management and adherence support for older patients.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality despite the availability of effective prevention and treatment [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that 10.7 million people developed tuberculosis (TB) in 2024, resulting in 1.08 million deaths globally, including approximately 170,000 deaths among people co-infected with TB and Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) (TB/HIV co-infection deaths) [2].

Between 2015 and 2024, the global TB incidence declined by about 12.3%, well short of the WHO End TB Strategy milestone of a 50% reduction by 2025 [2]. Drug-resistant TB remains a major challenge, with approximately 165,000 new multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) cases identified in 2024 [2]; however, many cases remain undetected or untreated due to weaknesses in health systems, case detection, and treatment access [2].

In Somalia, the TB burden continues to be high, and TB control is constrained by fragile health systems, recurrent conflict, climate-related shocks, and socio-political instability that hinder effective disease control [3]. In 2023, Somalia’s estimated TB incidence rate was 243 per 100,000 population, a modest decline from 262 per 100,000 in 2018 [3, 4]. In 2011, a nationwide survey in Somalia found that MDR-TB affected 5.2% of new cases and 40.8% of previously treated TB cases [5]. TB/HIV co-infection in Somalia is relatively low, with studies and WHO reports estimating prevalence at approximately 1%, indicating a comparatively lower burden of TB/HIV-related complications [2, 6].

Somaliland, an autonomous region of Somalia, has made gradual strides in TB case detection and management; however, underreporting and treatment non-adherence remain key challenges. Hargeisa TB Hospital, located in the capital city, serves as the primary referral centre for TB care with an estimated catchment population of 1.2 million [7]. The facility manages nearly 40% of all TB cases and over 90% of identified MDR-TB cases within Somaliland’s TB program [7]. Services include outpatient and inpatient care, directly observed treatment (DOT), diagnostic testing, and specialised management of drug-resistant TB. Like many facilities in fragile settings, the hospital faces resource constraints, high patient volumes, and challenges maintaining continuity and adherence over the treatment course. Studies have found that younger age and HIV-negative status [8] were associated with successful treatment outcomes [4, 8, 9], whereas others identified older age and HIV co-infection as significant predictors of unsuccessful treatment [10]. Available evidence suggests that treatment success for drug-susceptible TB (DS-TB) in Somaliland is high (around 90%).

However, there remains a notable evidence gap in multi-year analyses of TB treatment outcomes and their associated factors in Somaliland [3, 11], Somalia [4, 10, 12] and other countries [8, 9]. To date, only one study has provided a multi-year assessment of TB treatment outcomes at Hargeisa TB Hospital [3], using a five-year retrospective design up to 2023 to examine predictors but not temporal trends. Furthermore, there has been limited research exploring factors associated with treatment outcomes when stratified by TB type [13]. In contrast, this study conducted a six-year retrospective register-based analysis (2019–2024) to assess temporal trends and factors associated with DS-TB (hereafter, referred to as TB) treatment outcomes among patients. In addition, the study assesses factors associated with treatment outcomes after stratification by TB type. By integrating cross-sectional and longitudinal perspectives, it adds evidence on multi-year trends in treatment outcomes [3, 4] and provides insights to inform quality improvement, resource allocation, targeted interventions, and TB control strategies in Somaliland and similar resource-limited, conflict-affected settings. Throughout the study period, all drug-susceptible tuberculosis cases were managed using a standardized first-line regimen in line with WHO recommendations under the Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course (DOTS) framework [14].

Methods

Study design and setting

A facility-based retrospective study was conducted through a review of the TB treatment register to assess treatment outcomes and associated factors among TB patients treated at Hargeisa TB Hospital in Hargeisa, Somaliland. The hospital serves as the main referral center for TB diagnosis and treatment services in the region. Data on treatment outcomes and associated factors were collected retrospectively from monthly TB treatment registration records for all patients who received treatment between January 1 and December 31 of each year during the study period. The study period extended from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2024.

Study population

This study included all patients diagnosed with tuberculosis who received anti-TB treatment at Hargeisa TB Hospital during the study period. Patients registered for TB treatment between 2019 and 2024 were eligible for inclusion. A total of 1,373 patients were registered in 2019, 1,124 in 2020, 1,209 in 2021, 1,266 in 2022, 1,157 in 2023, and 1,112 in 2024. Participants were included only if both treatment initiation and treatment outcome data were completely recorded in the hospital’s TB treatment registers. Patients with incomplete data were excluded: 241 in 2019, 214 in 2020, 66 in 2021, 54 in 2022, 131 in 2023, and 26 in 2024.

Study procedure

Data for all eligible tuberculosis patients registered between January 1, 2019, and December 31, 2024, were retrospectively extracted using a standardized checklist. Diagnosis and evaluation of treatment outcomes were conducted by hospital physicians. Following treatment initiation, trained personnel documented patient characteristics such as age, sex, TB classification (pulmonary or extrapulmonary), treatment category, smear status, and HIV status in the TB register. Final treatment outcomes were recorded upon completion of therapy. Consistent with the Somali National TB Guidelines, all individuals with suspected TB, including children able to produce sputum samples, were tested bacteriologically using the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay. In this study, the dependent variable was defined as the treatment outcome of tuberculosis. Treatment outcomes were classified as successful if the patient was cured or had completed the prescribed course of therapy [2, 15]. Outcomes were considered unsuccessful for patients who experienced treatment failure, died during treatment, or were lost to follow-up [2, 4, 15]. The independent variables included sex, age, year of treatment, type of TB (pulmonary or extra pulmonary), and HIV status.

Data analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analysed using STATA version 18.0. Descriptive statistical methods, such as frequencies, percentages, cross-tabulations, and graphical presentations, were employed to summarize the characteristics of patients and their treatment outcomes. To identify factors associated with successful TB treatment, bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed. The crude odds ratios (COR) and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Variables with a p-value ≤ 0.05 in the multivariate regression were considered statistically significant predictors of treatment outcome.

Definitions

The WHO treatment outcome definitions were used to define the terms [16, 17]. TB patient category. “New patient” is a person with TB who has never treated for TB before or taken TB drugs for less than 1 month. “Previously treated patients” are individuals who have previously received anti-TB treatment for at least one month. “Pulmonary TB (PTB)” - A person with TB disease involving the lung parenchyma or the tracheobronchial tree. “Extrapulmonary TB (EPTB)” - A person with TB disease involving organs other than the lung parenchyma or tracheobronchial tree (e.g., pleura, lymph nodes, digestive track, genitourinary tract, skin, joints and bones, meninges) [17].

Treatment outcome variables. “Cured” is defined as a pulmonary TB patient who started treatment with bacteriologically confirmed TB and finished the treatment as advised by national policy with evidence of bacteriological response and no signs of failure. “Treatment completed” refers to a patient who has completed the recommended treatment course according to national policy but did not meet the criteria for cure or treatment failure. “Treatment successful” refers to a person with TB disease who was either cured or who completed treatment as defined above. “Died” refers to a patient who died before the beginning of treatment or while receiving it. “Lost to follow-up” refers to a patient who either doesn’t start therapy or halts their treatment for two consecutive months or longer. “Failure” refers to a person with TB disease whose treatment regimen needed to be terminated or permanently changed to a new regimen option or treatment strategy [17]. The WHO treatment outcome definitions apply to both PTB and EPTB; however, the term “cured” is mainly used for bacteriologically confirmed PTB [17]. In EPTB, bacteriological follow-up is often not feasible, so patients are usually classified as “treatment completed” rather than “cured.” Thus, successful treatment in EPTB generally refers to treatment completion, while unsuccessful outcomes include treatment failure, death, loss to follow-up, or not evaluated [17].

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of TB patients

Across the six-year period (2019–2024), males consistently accounted for a higher proportion of TB cases than females, comprising 60–65% of patients, while females represented 35–41% annually (Table 1). The 15–34-year age group contributed the largest share of cases (38–41%), followed by those aged 35–54 years (19–25%) and children aged 1–14 years (17–27%); patients aged ≥ 55 years consistently formed the smallest group (14–17%), indicating a predominance among economically productive age groups. Pulmonary TB accounted for 55–59% of cases, while extra-pulmonary TB accounted for 41–44%. The category ‘Not done’ for smear results comprised extra-pulmonary TB cases and other patients in whom sputum smear examination was not indicated or not conducted. The proportion of smear-positive TB (pulmonary case) increased from 25.3% in 2019 to 43.8% in 2024. TB/HIV co-infection was low and stable (2.4–3.0%), with 97% of patients testing HIV negative.

Treatment outcomes among TB patients (2019–2024)

Table 4 presents the TB treatment outcomes among patients treated at the facility between 2019 and 2024. Overall, treatment success rates (combined cured and treatment completed) remained high throughout the six-year period, ranging between 86% and 92%, which is above the WHO global target of 85%.

The highest treatment success rates were recorded in 2020 (91.9%) and 2024 (91.7%), while the lowest occurred in 2022 (86.4%). The proportion of patients who were cured increased steadily from 23.3% in 2019 to 36.7% in 2024, suggesting improvements in case detection and follow-up sputum testing. Conversely, the proportion of patients who completed treatment without bacteriological confirmation decreased over time, from 65.6% in 2019 to 55% in 2024, which may reflect better documentation and diagnostic verification practices.

The proportion of unsuccessful treatment outcomes comprising death, loss to follow-up, and treatment failure ranged from 8% to 14% over the study period. The highest rate of unsuccessful outcomes was observed in 2022 (13.6%), followed by 2019 (11.1%), 2021 (10.2%), and 2024 (8.3%), indicating some fluctuation but an overall improving trend in recent years. Loss to follow-up showed an increasing trend, rising from 0.6% in 2019 to a peak of 3.1% in 2022, before slightly declining in subsequent years.

Among unsuccessful outcomes, death consistently accounted for the largest proportion, contributing approximately 6% to 10% annually. Across the six-year period (2019–2024), deaths among TB patients fluctuated between pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB without a consistent dominant pattern. In 2019, most deaths occurred among pulmonary TB patients (53.85%). This pattern reversed in 2020 and 2021, where extrapulmonary TB accounted for a slightly higher proportion of deaths (51.43% and 52.94%, respectively). In 2022, pulmonary TB again had a higher share of deaths (54.55%). This shifted back in 2023, when extrapulmonary TB accounted for the majority of deaths (58.33%). Finally, in 2024, pulmonary TB once more had a higher proportion of deaths (57.97%). Overall, the findings show an alternating pattern of mortality distribution between pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB over time, with no sustained dominance of one TB type.

Factors associated with tuberculosis treatment outcomes at Hargeisa TB hospital, 2019

Table 5 shows factors associated with treatment success in 2019. In the bivariate analysis, sex, age, and HIV status showed significant associations with treatment outcomes. After adjusting for potential confounders in the multivariate logistic regression, sex, age category and HIV status were independently associated with treatment success.

Female patients had lower odds of treatment success than male patients (AOR = 0.66; 95% CI: 0.45–0.96; p = 0.029). Compared with children aged 1–14 years, those aged 15–34 years (AOR = 1.64; 95% CI: 1.02–2.64; p = 0.042) and 35–54 years (AOR = 2.13; 95% CI: 1.15–3.96; p = 0.017) had higher odds of treatment success. HIV status was strongly associated with treatment outcome. HIV-negative patients had higher odds of treatment success than HIV-positive patients (AOR = 3.25; 95% CI: 1.41–7.46; p = 0.005). TB type, and smear result were not statistically associated with treatment success after adjustment (p > 0.05).

Factors associated with tuberculosis treatment outcomes at Hargeisa TB hospital, 2020

Bivariate analysis (Table 6, column 5) showed that age was significantly associated with tuberculosis treatment outcomes. After adjusting for potential confounders in the multivariate logistic regression model (column 7), this association remained significant. Patients aged 15–34 years were 2.44 times more likely to achieve successful TB treatment outcomes compared to those aged 1–14 years (AOR = 2.44; 95% CI = 1.06–5.63; p = 0.036). Six-year comparative synthesis (2019–2024): evolving determinants of TB treatment success — Hargeisa TB hospital

Over 2019–2024, several consistent patterns and a few notable shifts emerged. Across the six annual cohorts, older age (≥ 55 years) was the most consistent predictor of reduced treatment success (significant in 2020–2024). Significant associations between treatment success and HIV status were observed in some years. Other covariates (sex, TB type and smear status) showed inconsistent or year-specific associations, which may reflect changing case mix, program factors, and/or random variation across year-specific models.

Age was the most consistent predictor: patients ≥ 55 years had persistently worse outcomes (non-significant in 2019 but strongly and significantly less likely to achieve treatment success in 2020–2024), while younger adults (particularly 15–34 years) showed higher odds of success in 2019 and 2020 but not consistently afterward. HIV status remained an important determinant: HIV-negative patients had substantially higher odds of success in 2019, 2021 and a borderline effect in 2023, while the effect was not significant in some intermediate years, reflecting year-to-year variation likely driven by small numbers of HIV-positive cases and program changes. Pulmonary TB was associated with worse adjusted outcomes in 2022 only; smear-status and sex showed inconsistent effects (female sex was associated with lower odds only in 2019).

Factors associated with TB treatment success by TB type

Table 12 presents results from multivariable logistic regression analyses examining factors associated with TB treatment success, stratified by TB type over the period 2019–2024. Overall, the analysis shows that determinants of treatment success differ between extrapulmonary TB and pulmonary TB, with age emerging as the most consistent predictor across both TB types, while HIV status and smear status show variable associations over time.

Extrapulmonary TB

Across the 2019–2024 period, age emerged as the most stable determinant of treatment success in EPTB. Patients aged 15–34 years generally showed higher odds of treatment success, with particularly strong positive associations observed in 2022–2024, indicating improved outcomes among young adults during the later study period. In contrast, those aged 55 years and above consistently demonstrated reduced odds of treatment success, with statistically significant negative associations observed in several years (notably from 2020 onwards). HIV status showed a non-stable but occasionally very strong association with EPTB treatment success. Most years did not show statistically significant effects; however, in 2023, HIV-negative status was very strongly associated with improved treatment success (aOR = 11.247). In other years, the effect was either weak or not statistically significant, indicating substantial variability over time. Sex of the child did not emerge as a consistent determinant of treatment success in EPTB. The direction of association varied across years, and statistical significance was not sustained.

Pulmonary TB

Age was the most robust and consistent determinant of treatment success in PTB across all study years. The 55 years and above age group consistently showed reduced odds of treatment success from 2019 to 2024, indicating a persistent disadvantage for older patients regardless of year. This pattern was stable and statistically supported across multiple model specifications. HIV status showed a variable pattern of association with PTB treatment success. Significant positive associations were observed in 2019 and 2021, with an exceptionally strong effect in 2021 (aOR = 11.181). However, in other years, HIV status was not statistically significant, indicating inconsistency over time. Smear status showed intermittent significance across the study period. It was not consistently associated with treatment success in earlier years but became more relevant in later years, particularly in 2022–2024, where smear-positive and smear-negative categories showed varying associations with outcomes.

Discussion

This study examined factors associated with tuberculosis (TB) treatment outcomes among patients at Hargeisa TB Hospital between 2019 and 2024. The treatment success rates ranged between 86% and 92%, and was comparable to the WHO global target of 90% by 2030 [18], and the overall TB/HIV coinfection was 3%. The observed variations may reflect differences in program performance, patient adherence, and external factors such as service disruptions or changes in TB case management during the study period. Despite these fluctuations, the overall trend indicates sustained progress toward achieving and maintaining the WHO-recommended treatment success target.

TB/HIV co-infection remained uncommon (around 3% annually) but was associated with lower treatment success in years with sufficient HIV-positive cases. HIV-negative patients had roughly two-to four-fold higher adjusted odds of treatment success than HIV-positive patients in those years. This aligns with evidence that immunosuppression and treatment complexity can worsen TB outcomes among people living with HIV, reinforcing the need for strengthened TB/HIV collaborative services, early antiretroviral therapy initiation, and integrated follow-up.

The findings reveal that treatment success was significantly associated with age, sex, and HIV status, with notable variations observed across the study period. Older patients, particularly those aged ≥ 55 years, consistently demonstrated lower odds of successful treatment outcomes. In 2022 and 2023, these patients were 56–76% less likely to achieve treatment success compared to children aged ≤ 14 years. This pattern mirrors findings from Somalia, Somaliland, Eritrea, and Ethiopia, where advanced age has been linked to poor TB outcomes due to higher prevalence of comorbidities, delayed diagnosis, and reduced treatment adherence [3, 4, 10, 19, 20]. These results underscore the need for targeted clinical monitoring, nutritional support, and tailored adherence counselling for older TB patients.

TB/HIV co-infection also emerged as a critical predictor of poor outcomes across the study period. HIV-negative patients were two to four times more likely to complete TB treatment successfully compared to HIV-positive individuals. This finding aligns with global evidence that TB/HIV co-infection worsens treatment outcomes due to immunosuppression, drug interactions, and higher risk of adverse events [2–4, 20, 21]. Strengthening TB/HIV collaborative services - including routine testing, early antiretroviral therapy (ART) initiation, and integrated treatment follow-up are crucial to improving outcomes among co-infected patients.

Furthermore, the finding that female patients were less likely to achieve successful treatment outcomes in 2019 suggests persistent gender-related barriers such as stigma, delayed care-seeking, and limited access to social support [22, 23]. For example [22], notes that women were associated with unfavourable TB treatment outcome in Ethiopia. Gender-responsive community engagement, psychosocial counselling, and empowerment programs should therefore be integrated into TB control strategies.

Overall, sustaining high treatment success will likely require targeted support for higher-risk groups (older adults and people living with HIV), alongside continued improvements in continuity of care and routine data quality. These study findings emphasize importance of differentiated, patient-centered approaches, particularly for older adults, HIV-positive patients, and women to enhance treatment adherence and sustain high treatment success rates in Somaliland, Somalia.

Across 2019–2024, age was the most consistent predictor of TB treatment success, with clear differences between extrapulmonary TB and pulmonary TB. In EPTB, younger adults (15–34 years) generally showed higher odds of treatment success, particularly in 2022–2024, while those aged ≥ 55 years consistently experienced reduced success, indicating a U-shaped age pattern [24, 25]. In PTB, the adverse effect of older age was more stable, with the ≥ 55 years group showing consistently lower odds of treatment success across all study years, making age the most robust predictor in this subgroup. HIV negative status showed inconsistent effects in both TB types, although it was strongly associated with improved outcomes in specific years, notably 2021 for PTB and 2023 for EPTB, suggesting substantial temporal variation in its influence. Smear status demonstrated limited but emerging associations in later years (2022–2024), particularly among PTB cases, while sex of the child was not a consistent determinant of treatment success in either TB category. Overall, these findings indicate that age, especially older age remains the most reliable predictor of TB treatment outcomes, similar to other studie [24, 25], whereas the effects of HIV status and smear positivity appear context-dependent.

Strengths and limitations

This study examined temporal trends and factors associated with TB treatment outcomes among patients in Somaliland, providing valuable programmatic insights over time. A key strength of the study is the use of routinely collected data, which enhances the real-world relevance of the findings and reflects actual program performance under routine conditions. The analysis of trends across multiple years and differentiation by TB type also enabled assessment of changes in treatment outcomes and associated factors over time, thereby supporting evidence-based decision-making for TB control programmes.

However, several limitations should be considered. The study relied on secondary data derived from an observational design, which limited the availability of certain variables. Important factors that may influence TB treatment outcomes, such as socioeconomic status, adherence levels, comorbidities other than HIV, nutritional status, and behavioural factors, were not consistently captured in the records. As with all observational studies based on routine data, the potential for incomplete records and misclassification bias cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

TB treatment success at Hargeisa TB Hospital consistently met or exceeded the WHO benchmark of ≥ 85% between 2019 and 2024. This six-year analysis identified age and HIV status as the most significant predictors of treatment outcomes, with older adults and HIV-positive patients experiencing persistently poorer treatment outcomes. These associations remained significant after stratification by TB type. These findings highlight the need for targeted, patient-centred interventions. Older TB patients would benefit from enhanced clinical follow-up, including screening and management of common comorbidities such as diabetes. In addition, TB/HIV collaborative services should be further strengthened to ensure routine HIV testing, timely initiation of antiretroviral therapy, and coordinated follow-up for co-infected patients.
