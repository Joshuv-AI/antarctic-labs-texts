Stroke Prevention in Pregnancy and Postpartum in Patients With Patent Foramen Ovale
Authors: Grace B Russo, Stephanie E Purisch, Malamo E Countouris, Lynn M Yee, Imo J Akpan, Robert J Sommer, Eliza C Miller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPregnancy and patent foramen ovale (PFO) are both stroke risk factors in young people. There are currently no guidelines for mitigating PFO-related stroke risk during pregnancy. We summarize the pathophysiology of PFO-related stroke and offer our approaches to primary and secondary stroke prevention for pregnant patients with PFO. We present 5 clinical scenarios: incidentally identified PFO in a pregnant patient without a stroke history; considerations for PFO screening and primary stroke prevention in select patients at unusually high risk of stroke in pregnancy; planning for pregnancy after a PFO-related stroke; PFO-related stroke occurring in pregnancy; and pregnancy planning for a patient with PFO identified after a transient neurological episode of unclear cause. Through these scenarios, we highlight the unique treatment considerations and challenges of primary and secondary PFO-related stroke prevention during this high-risk period. Collaboration between neurology, cardiology, and maternal-fetal medicine is integral for the successful management of these patients.Key words: cerebrovascular accident, paradoxical embolism, patent foramen ovale, postpartum, pregnancy, stroke

Pregnancy and patent foramen ovale (PFO) are both stroke risk factors in young people. Paradoxical embolism from a systemic venous thrombus is the most widely accepted mechanism for PFO-related stroke.1 Exposure to transient prothrombotic states, such as combined hormonal contraception and pregnancy, is associated with an increased risk of PFO-related stroke.2 Knowledge gaps remain in stroke-prevention strategies for this patient population. Here, we review the pathophysiology of PFO-related stroke and discuss our multidisciplinary clinical approach to the pregnant patient with a PFO (Central Illustration).

Multidisciplinary Management of Pregnancy in a Patient With PFO

Multiple factors warrant consideration for multidisciplinary discussion and shared decision-making in the preconception, pregnant, and postpartum periods for patients with PFO. For patients with a history of stroke or TIA, PFO closure should be considered prior to pregnancy. For PFO-related stroke occurring during pregnancy, anticoagulation is suggested and deferral of closure until after pregnancy. For patients without a history of stroke, PFO closure is not recommended; decisions regarding primary stroke prevention with conservative management, aspirin, or in rare cases, anticoagulation would depend on factors such as a history of unprovoked venous thromboembolism, underlying hypercoagulable states, or other risk factors. Routine screening for PFO is not recommended, but screening for right-to-left shunt with transcranial Doppler could be considered for selected patients with high-risk conditions. We do not consider pregnancy a contraindication to agitated saline injection. PFO = patent foramen ovale; TIA = transient ischemic attack.

PFO and stroke

In 25% of people, incomplete closure of the fetal foramen ovale results in a PFO.3 A PFO may facilitate right-to-left shunt (RLS) when right atrial pressure transiently exceeds the left atrial pressure. Concurrent atrial septal aneurysm is associated with larger RLS.1 The magnitude of the shunt can increase during states of increased right atrial pressure (eg, coughing or Valsalva).4 The motion of the fossa ovalis membrane within the septal redundancy may increase the likelihood of local thrombus formation.1

A PFO can become a conduit for microemboli to pass from the venous to arterial system. Among patients aged ≤55 years with cryptogenic stroke, up to 73% have a PFO.5 Patients with PFO-related stroke have a high prevalence of prothrombotic conditions, including exposure to combined hormonal contraception.2 The likelihood that a PFO is the culprit increases when the presence of other stroke risk factors is low.6 This risk is quantified in the Risk of Paradoxical Embolism (RoPE) score, a validated model designed to predict whether a PFO in a patient with cryptogenic stroke is pathogenic or incidental.5 The PFO-Associated Stroke Causal Likelihood (PASCAL) classification system combines the RoPE Score with high-risk PFO features to classify patients with cryptogenic stroke into 3 categories: unlikely, possible, and probable causal relationship.7 Patients with a high RoPE score and “probable” PASCAL classification have a 90% relative risk reduction in recurrent stroke after PFO closure.7

Pregnancy and risk of PFO-related stroke

Physiologic adaptations to pregnancy inherently stress the circulatory system. Hemodynamic changes begin early in pregnancy and continue throughout, typically peaking around 28 to 32 weeks gestation but with an additional significant increase with labor and delivery and returning to baseline by 3 to 6 months postpartum. Significant changes in preload, cardiac output, and systemic vascular resistance may affect the degree of shunting in pregnant individuals with a PFO. However, since all patients with PFO can have right-to-left shunting with a sufficient Valsalva, it may be that physiological/hormonal changes in clotting factors are the primary risk impacting the risk of paradoxical embolism.8,9 The gravid uterus can cause femoral vein and inferior vena cava obstruction,10 resulting in venous stasis in the legs. Delivery is associated with tissue trauma. The highest venous thromboembolism (VTE) risk occurs in the peripartum period when all 3 elements of Virchow’s triad occur simultaneously. Compared with the nonpregnant state, pregnancy is associated with a 5-fold increased VTE risk, and the postpartum period is associated with a 30- to 60-fold increase in VTE risk and 3- to 9-fold risk of stroke.8,11,12 Given these changes, it is possible the relative risk of a PFO-related stroke is higher during pregnancy than in the nonpregnant state. However, this hypothesis has yet to be formally investigated.

Methods for PFO detection

A PFO may be detected by the use of agitated saline injection (“bubble study”) with and without Valsalva, during transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiography, or transcranial Doppler (TCD). There may be some hesitancy to perform these studies during pregnancy. However, the microbubbles employed are unlikely to represent any serious risk for fetal or maternal harm. In patients with RLS, there is no significant increased risk for cerebral ischemia or air embolism. In addition, a recently published study demonstrated no evidence of air embolism or fetal impact in 100 pregnant patients who underwent bubble study.13

Our approach

We do not consider pregnancy a contraindication to agitated saline injection. We favor TCD as the preferred mode for PFO detection, due to its noninvasive nature, lower cost, and high sensitivity (96% compared with transesophageal echocardiography as the gold standard) for both intracardiac and intrapulmonary shunts.14 Where TCD is not available, we favor transthoracic echocardiography in most cases as a less invasive, albeit less sensitive, option. While TCD is highly sensitive, it cannot localize the shunt location anatomically, and the presence of a PFO will need subsequent confirmation.

Clinical scenario 1: An incidentally found PFO in a patient without a history of stroke, pregnant, or planning pregnancy

While the American Heart Association 2024 Guideline for the Primary Prevention of Stroke addresses some aspects of primary stroke prevention in pregnancy, it does not address management of incidental PFO in this population.15

Aspirin is commonly used in pregnancy for indications other than stroke prevention. The US Preventive Service Task Force and American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) recommend initiation of low-dose aspirin at 12 weeks gestation for patients at increased risk of preeclampsia.16,17 Multiple trials demonstrated the safety of low-dose aspirin without an increase in bleeding, adverse fetal outcomes, congenital malformations, or long-term developmental delays.16,18, 19, 20 Low-dose aspirin is not excreted in breast milk,21 and salicylate levels are low in people taking it.22 However, no evidence supports aspirin for primary stroke prevention in pregnant patients with an incidentally discovered PFO.

Our approach

If the PFO was incidentally discovered and asymptomatic, we do not recommend aspirin use for stroke prevention in pregnancy or the postpartum period. We initiate low-dose aspirin during pregnancy for those at risk of preeclampsia, per ACOG and US Preventive Service Task Force recommendations, with discontinuation after delivery per usual obstetric practices. We recommend the use of filtered intravenous lines for patients with known PFO, to reduce the risk of air embolism.

Clinical Scenario 2: High-risk patients without a history of stroke: Considerations for anticoagulation and the role of PFO screening prior to pregnancy

PFO screening in patients with no stroke history, but elevated risk of thrombosis during pregnancy, might allow for a more informed discussion of risk mitigation during high-risk periods. Research is needed to investigate the efficacy and feasibility of PFO screening for certain patient groups.

Thrombophilia and VTE

A previous history of thrombosis is the most significant risk factor for recurrent VTE during pregnancy and postpartum.23,24 Among women with pregnancy-related VTE, 20% to 50% have inherited or acquired thrombophilia.23,25 Thrombophilia is associated with a 16-fold increased odds of pregnancy-associated stroke.26 A concurrent PFO may further increase the risk of cerebral embolism. Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) is the treatment of choice for thromboprophylaxis during pregnancy and postpartum due to its relative safety profile.27 Many professional societies provide guidelines for thrombophrophylaxis in the antepartum and postpartum setting.28, 29, 30, 31 American Society of Hematology guidelines set a 1% to 4% VTE threshold risk for recommending antepartum prophylaxis and a 1% to 3% VTE risk threshold for recommending postpartum prophylaxis.28 In the absence of randomized controlled trials, ACOG recommends that the initiation of pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis should be based on individual risk factors, and discussion of the risks and benefits should take place within a multidisciplinary setting.23,27

Migraine with Aura

Migraine with Aura (MA) is well-recognized as a risk factor for ischemic stroke, particularly in young women.32 The high incidence of PFO in patients with MA is thought to contribute to the 8-fold increased risk of ischemic stroke in MA patients taking combined hormonal contraception, which should be avoided in these individuals.33,34 Migraine is also associated with increased risk of preeclampsia and pregnancy-associated stroke.35 Of note, no evidence supports aspirin for primary stroke prevention in nonpregnant patients with MA.

Our approach

We do not favor routine screening for PFO in all patients with thrombophilia, prior VTE, or MA.

We consider PFO screening in patients planning pregnancy who have a history of prior unprovoked VTE or at high risk of acquired or inherited thrombophilias. For patients found to have a PFO, we suggest consideration of LMWH during pregnancy through at least 6 weeks postpartum per ACOG recommendations and consideration of continuing LMWH for up to 12 weeks postpartum, given data showing elevated risk of thromboembolic events during this extended period.12 We favor including a vascular neurologist and a hematologist in these discussions.

Given the strong association between MA and pregnancy-related stroke, as well as PFO, we occasionally consider screening for PFO in select patients with MA; for example, if focal neurological deficits have occurred in the setting of a migraine. If a PFO is present in a patient with MA, we consider recommending low-dose aspirin during pregnancy and the postpartum period to lower the risk of a first stroke, although no trials have tested this approach.

Recognizing the lack of evidence to guide management in these situations, we favor a shared decision-making approach with the patient and other specialists, as needed.

Clinical scenario 3: A PFO-related stroke that occurs prior to pregnancy

The Canadian Stroke Best Practice Consensus highlighted pregnancy as a unique risk factor for recurrent stroke and recommended counseling all female stroke survivors of reproductive age regarding risk reduction during this time.22 Although there is an overall low recurrence risk for PFO-related stroke (approximately 1% per year in patients treated with medical therapy),36 data are lacking regarding the potential interaction of transient states of hypercoagulability, such as pregnancy, on recurrence risk.

Stroke evaluation for patients with PFO

As many as one-third of RLS found in patients with cryptogenic stroke are estimated to be incidental37; therefore, in any patient presenting with cryptogenic stroke who is found to have a PFO, a thorough investigation of potential alternate etiologies must be completed, including the presence of traditional vascular risk factors, which have increased in patients with pregnancy-associated stroke over time.38 This workup includes vascular imaging, baseline electrocardiography, extended cardiac monitoring, and evaluation for underlying hypercoagulable states.36 Some groups recommend screening for underlying May-Thurner syndrome (MTS) as a potential alternate etiology in patients presenting with cryptogenic stroke and PFO.39,40 MTS is an anatomic variant that results in compression of the left common iliac vein between the right common iliac artery and lumbar spine.41 This condition increases the risk of pelvic thrombi that may be undetectable in standard lower-extremity ultrasound. During pregnancy, the increasing size of the gravid uterus can further increase compression in MTS, resulting in higher risk of pelvic vein thrombi.39 MTS has been reported as an etiology of cryptogenic stroke with a PFO, including in pregnancy.40,42

PFO closure

Five randomized controlled trials investigated the efficacy of PFO closure for secondary stroke prevention (Table 1). All excluded patients who were pregnant or planning a pregnancy. The later trials show compelling evidence for the benefit of closure, particularly for PFO with high-risk features. However, an important knowledge gap remains regarding the question of stroke risk reduction from PFO closure in patients with morphologically “low-risk” shunts who are exposed to states of hypercoagulability and significant hemodynamic change, such as pregnancy.

Our approach

In patients with previous PFO-related stroke planning a pregnancy, after thorough evaluation to exclude alternate stroke etiologies, we favor PFO closure prior to planned conception. Dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin and clopidogrel) is usually given for 1 to 6 months following closure, followed by single antiplatelet therapy indefinitely. Anticoagulation may be considered in specific clinical situations. In patients with closed PFOs, we continue aspirin during the preconception period, throughout pregnancy, and indefinitely thereafter per secondary stroke–prevention guidelines.43 We consider screening for MTS with pelvic magnetic resonance venography as part of the stroke evaluation in patients with cryptogenic stroke and a PFO, and when present, we favor a multidisciplinary decision-making process regarding treatment approaches prior to pregnancy, including hematology, vascular neurology, maternal fetal medicine, cardiology, and vascular surgery.44

Clinical scenario 4: Management of a PFO-related stroke that occurs during pregnancy

In the most recent American Heart Association guidelines for secondary prevention of stroke and transient ischemic attack (TIA; 2021), the role of PFO in pregnancy is not addressed.43 The 2011 guidelines included a focus on pregnancy and recommended the use of therapeutic anticoagulation as secondary stroke prevention for pregnant patients at increased risk of cardioembolic stroke.45 Canadian secondary prevention guidelines suggest low-dose aspirin for pregnant patients with PFO-related stroke, with consideration for anticoagulation with LMWH.22 It is reasonable to extrapolate from the existing literature on the management of VTE during pregnancy when considering the management of a PFO-related stroke that occurs during pregnancy.

Medical therapy

For most cases of VTE diagnosed during pregnancy, systemic anticoagulation therapy with LMWH is the preferred treatment option.11 LMWH does not cross the placenta and is not secreted in breast milk.46,47 There is no evidence of teratogenicity or increased risk of fetal hemorrhage.23 Prophylactic and therapeutic doses of LMWH have not been found to increase major bleeding in pregnant patients, even after operative vaginal or cesarean delivery.48,49 Although heparin-induced thrombocytopenia can affect up to 3% of patients exposed to heparin,50 it is rare during pregnancy and even rarer with LMWH than unfractionated heparin (UFH),51 with various studies demonstrating 0 to 1 case per several 100 patients.48,51,52 However, treatment recommendations for LMWH during pregnancy are based largely on studies of nonpregnant individuals, and therefore do not take into account the altered pharmacokinetics during pregnancy.11 Most societies do not recommend anti-Xa monitoring to guide dosing during pregnancy, although the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends anti-Xa monitoring during pregnancy in cases of extremes of bodyweight and other risk factors such as renal failure or recurrent VTE53; however, certain high-risk scenarios for VTE may require special consideration.48

PFO closure during pregnancy

Since pregnant patients were excluded from all closure trials, there is no evidence regarding the safety and efficacy of closure during pregnancy.22 Canadian guidelines suggest avoiding PFO closure during pregnancy.22 However, there are several case reports of PFO closure during pregnancy after PFO-related stroke in patients at high risk of recurrence or with contraindications to anticoagulation therapy. In one series, 3 patients underwent closure during the second trimester due to either evidence of recurrent events (2 patients) or a contraindication to antithrombotics (1 patient).54 Intracardiac echography was used to avoid general anesthesia and minimize fluoroscopy exposure. The overall estimated fetal radiation exposure during the procedures was <0.001 mGy.54 No periprocedural complications were noted, and all pregnancies resulted in the delivery of a healthy child without further neurologic events.54

Delivery considerations

Delivery mode for pregnant patients with a history of PFO-related stroke warrants special consideration. Valsalva-provoking activities are not found to have been more frequent in patients with PFO-related stroke than in patients with strokes of alternate etiology,55 a counterintuitive finding considering the shunt physiology of the PFO. In addition to increasing the risks of other adverse maternal outcomes, cesarean delivery is associated with 4 times the risk of VTE compared to vaginal delivery.56 The American Society of Anesthesiologists recommends cessation of prophylactic LMWH 12 hours before regional epidural anesthesia and 24 hours for therapeutic dosing.48 Given this, patients may elect for planned induction of labor when obstetrically indicated or at 39 weeks48 to ensure eligibility for epidural anesthesia during delivery. During this time, they can be transitioned to UFH due to its shorter half-life and ability for rapid reversal.11 Following delivery, UFH can be restarted 1 hour following catheter removal, and therapeutic LMWH can be started a minimum of 4 hours following catheter removal.57

Our approach

For patients who experience a PFO-related stroke during pregnancy, regardless of the presence of a deep venous thrombosis, we recommend treatment with therapeutic LMWH for the remainder of the pregnancy and for at least 6 weeks postpartum, along with the use of filtered intravenous lines to reduce the risk of air embolism. PFO closure should be considered after the postpartum period and after a thorough stroke evaluation to exclude alternate etiologies (including MTS) but is not urgent to perform during pregnancy if the patient has no contraindication to anticoagulation. We favor no alteration of route of delivery beyond usual obstetric indications.

Clinical scenario 5: A transient neurologic event and a PFO

A difficult scenario encountered by clinicians is that of a patient presenting with a transient neurologic event, who is found to have a PFO. In young adults without vascular risk factors, the differential diagnosis most often features MA vs TIA. Even among patients without a migraine history, a diagnosis of MA cannot be excluded, as this is a common age of onset. The best management for PFO-related TIA is also not as clear as that for PFO-related stroke. All but one of the PFO closure trials excluded patients with an initial presentation of TIA; the one that included TIA found no difference in the primary endpoint.58 In addition, diagnostic uncertainty may remain regarding the event. Rates of events initially diagnosed as TIA that later receive an alternate diagnosis have been reported to be as high as 60% within routine clinical practice.59 Current guidelines from the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions recommend against routine PFO closure in the absence of documented stroke, including for patients with migraine, atrial septal aneurysm, or thrombophilia.60

Our approach

We recommend that patients who experience a transient neurologic event and are subsequently found to have a PFO be evaluated by a vascular neurologist. If a TIA is felt to be the most likely diagnosis, we recommend discontinuation of combined hormonal contraception if applicable and consideration of PFO closure prior to pregnancy, especially if the PFO is associated with high-risk features. We also recommend initiation of low-dose aspirin, to be continued indefinitely in accordance with current secondary prevention guidelines, including during the preconception, pregnancy, and postpartum periods. If the transient neurological event is thought to represent MA, we favor the primary prevention approach for MA patients as summarized earlier. We do not favor PFO closure for patients with MA without a history of stroke.

Conclusions

The physiologic state of pregnancy should be considered a risk factor for PFO-related stroke. Through these common clinical scenarios, we highlight the unique treatment considerations and challenges of primary and secondary PFO-related stroke prevention during this period. Our empiric approach is summarized in Figure 1; however, numerous knowledge gaps remain, and there is a need for research focused on stroke risk reduction for this patient group. Close collaboration between neurology, cardiology, and maternal-fetal medicine is integral for the successful management of these patients.
