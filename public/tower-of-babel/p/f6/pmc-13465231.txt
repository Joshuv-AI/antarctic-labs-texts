Optimized task allocation in SDN-enabled 5G IoMT networks using fog computing
Authors: Reza Mohammadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) leverages 5G-connected User Equipment (UE) to enable real-time health monitoring, transmitting patient data to hospital clouds for processing. However, this approach faces challenges in latency, resource efficiency, and energy use, critical for meeting 5G’s ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC) demands in healthcare. This paper proposes an optimized task allocation framework for SDN-enabled 5G IoMT networks, integrating fog computing to process tasks closer to the edge. Patient-generated tasks defined by data size, CPU cycles, and deadlines are intercepted by a dedicated fog broker, which applies a multi-objective optimization model to minimize latency (transmission and processing), load imbalance, and energy consumption across fog nodes. A heuristic solves this NP-hard problem, after which the SDN controller installs optimal routing paths between UEs and selected fog nodes. We evaluate the framework using NS3, Mininet, and Ryu simulations, comparing it against Round Robin and Nearest Fog Server baselines. Results show reductions in latency, deadline violations, and energy consumption, alongside improved load distribution. By harnessing SDN’s centralized control and fog computing’s proximity, our approach enhances QoS, scalability, and sustainability in IoMT, offering a robust solution for next generation healthcare systems.Keywords: IoMT, Fog computing, SDN, 5G

Introduction

The Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) has emerged as a transformative paradigm in healthcare, integrating sensors, wearable devices, and 5G-connected User Equipment (UE) to enable continuous, real-time monitoring of patient health conditions [1, 2]. In an IoMT ecosystem, patients generate critical data such as heart rates, glucose levels, or emergency alerts that must be transmitted to hospital clouds for analysis, storage, and decision-making [3]. Leveraging 5G’s ultra reliable low latency communication (URLLC) and massive machine type communication (mMTC) capabilities, IoMT enhances patient care by reducing response times and supporting a massive number of devices [4, 5]. However, offloading these computational tasks directly to distant clouds introduces significant challenges, including high latency, network congestion, and excessive energy consumption, which can jeopardize the stringent quality of service (QoS) requirements of medical applications [6].

Fog computing addresses these issues by deploying computational resources at the network edge, such as base stations or smart routers, closer to data sources [7]. Unlike traditional cloud computing, which centralizes processing in remote data centers, fog computing reduces latency by minimizing data travel distance and alleviates cloud load by distributing computation [8]. For IoMT, this proximity translates to faster processing of health data, enabling timely interventions—e.g., detecting anomalies in vital signs within milliseconds [9]. Additionally, fog nodes can preprocess data locally, reducing bandwidth demands and enhancing energy efficiency by offloading only essential information to the cloud [10].

Complementing fog computing, Software Defined Networking (SDN) provides a centralized programmable approach to manage the network infrastructure connecting 5G UEs to hospital clouds [11]. By decoupling the control plane from the data plane, SDN allows dynamic traffic management, real-time topology awareness, and efficient routing through OpenFlow enabled switches [12]. In the IoMT context, SDN’s benefits include optimized path selection to reduce delays, load balancing across network links, and adaptability to fluctuating traffic patterns—critical for handling bursts of emergency health data. Together, SDN and fog computing form a synergistic framework: fog nodes process tasks locally, while SDN ensures seamless data flow between UEs, fog nodes and the cloud.

Current task allocation strategies in fog-enabled IoMT networks often focus on single objectives such as latency or load balancing. However, they neglect critical factors like energy efficiency and deadline compliance, which are vital for healthcare. To address this gap, we propose an optimized task allocation framework for SDN-enabled 5G IoMT networks. Our approach introduces a dedicated fog broker that intercepts tasks from 5G patient UEs—each defined by data size , required CPU cycles, and deadlines before they reach the hospital cloud. The fog broker employs a multi-objective mathematical model to minimize a weighted sum of latency (transmission and processing delays), load imbalance across fog nodes, and energy consumption. Due to the NP-hard nature of this optimization, we design an efficient heuristic to assign tasks to fog nodes, characterized by processing power, energy rates, and bandwidth. The fog broker then instructs the SDN controller to install optimal routing paths, ensuring efficient task execution.

We implement and evaluate our framework using an integrated simulation environment combining NS3 (for 5G and UE modeling), Mininet (for SDN topology), and Ryu (for SDN control). Compared to baselines like Round Robin and Nearest Fog Server our approach achieves significant improvements in latency, deadline adherence, load distribution, and energy efficiency. These results highlight the potential of combining SDN’s network orchestration with fog computing’s edge processing to meet IoMT’s rigorous demands, enhancing both patient outcomes and system sustainability.

Unlike existing works that typically focus on either fog-based task scheduling or SDN-based traffic engineering independently, this work presents a unified framework that jointly optimizes task allocation and network routing. The novelty of the proposed approach lies in (i) a multi-objective formulation considering latency, energy consumption, and load balancing simultaneously, (ii) the integration of a fog broker with SDN control for coordinated decision-making, and (iii) a lightweight heuristic suitable for real-time IoMT healthcare environments.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related works. Section 3 details the proposed architecture. Section 4 presents experimental results and performance evaluations. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper and discusses future research directions.

Related work

Research combining 5G, Software-Defined Networking (SDN) and fog computing for the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) is scarce, with few studies fully addressing their synergy to meet healthcare’s demanding requirements. Our work focuses on optimizing task allocation in SDN-enabled 5G IoMT networks using fog computing. Here, we review related efforts in edge computing, SDN-based networks and IoMT systems that partially align with our approach.

Several studies leverage fog computing to enhance IoMT performance. Anand et al. [13] proposed a fog-based service composition framework to improve QoS in IoMT, optimizing response time, reliability and energy efficiency. However, it lacks SDN for dynamic traffic management and does not address load balancing or efficient task allocation. Haval et al. [14] developed an IoMT framework integrating WBANs, fog and cloud computing for diabetes prediction, using fuzzy logic and machine learning to reduce latency and execution time. Yet, it overlooks 5G’s low-latency capabilities and fog node resource optimization. Patel et al. [15] introduced FORCE, a fog-based load-balancing framework for IoMT, reducing latency and energy use via region-aware node selection. However, it lacks QoS provisioning and 5G integration, limiting its suitability for next-generation healthcare systems. Sonia and Doha [16] proposed SF-HDRL, combining Federated Learning and Deep Reinforcement Learning for data placement and task offloading in edge-fog-cloud IoMT systems, improving scalability and costs. Nevertheless, it neglects QoS metrics, 5G networks and network simulator validation, reducing its practical relevance. Arafat et al. [17] presented QQAR, a Q-learning-based routing protocol for IoMT-enabled WBANs, enhancing QoS through link reliability and load balancing. However, it omits SDN for centralized control and fog computing for edge processing, limiting its real-time healthcare applicability.

In [18], Kaur and Bhardwaj proposed a Dynamic and Distributed Service Placement Policy (DD-SPP) to address the challenge of efficiently deploying latency sensitive IoT services in edge and fog environments, which suffer from limited resources compared to traditional cloud models. The approach includes three phases: Service Type Estimator (STE), Service Dependency Estimator & Resolution (SDER), and Resource Demand Estimator & Scheduling (RDES) working collaboratively to optimize service placement. Their method achieves notable performance improvements over existing techniques in terms of energy consumption, response time and overall cost. In other research [19], Kaur and Mittal proposed a Matrix-based Task-Fog Pairing (MTFP) algorithm to address the challenge of scheduling real-time, latency sensitive IoT tasks in fog environments with limited resources. By leveraging compatibility and execution time matrices, MTFP efficiently matches tasks to suitable fog nodes to ensure timely execution and improved Quality of Experience (QoE). Experimental results demonstrate that MTFP outperforms existing methods in terms of execution time, delay and energy consumption. However, these studies do not take into account the unique requirements of the Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) and 5G network environments. In [20], Yadav et al. introduced a Lightweight Adaptive Learning Offloading Algorithm to address energy and latency challenges in edge-based smart healthcare systems using IoMT devices. By formulating a joint minimization problem of energy consumption and latency while considering battery constraints and service deadlines the algorithm dynamically adapts offloading decisions using parameters () and a configurable tradeoff target. Simulations in iFogSim2 confirm the effectiveness of their proposed method in reducing both energy usage and latency compared to traditional resource allocation methods. As another study, Yadav et al. [21] proposed a Computation Offloading using Reinforcement Learning (CORL) scheme to address inefficient resource allocation in energy constrained, delay sensitive IoMT devices within edge enabled healthcare systems. The CORL algorithm formulates a joint optimization problem to minimize both latency and energy consumption, while respecting battery and service deadline constraints. By leveraging reinforcement learning, CORL identifies optimal resource nodes for offloading tasks, balancing energy and latency trade-offs. Experimental results using the iFogSim demonstrate improved energy efficiency, reduced latency, and better resource utilization compared to traditional methods. However, their researches omit SDN for centralized control and fog computing for edge processing, limiting its real-time healthcare applicability.

Hassan et al. [22] proposed a resource-aware load allocation algorithm for fog-cloud environments, where edge devices are assigned to fog nodes based on sensing rates and processing capacities. Their approach aims to reduce latency and network consumption by balancing workload across heterogeneous fog nodes. While their simulation results demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach in reducing latency and network usage, their model does not incorporate SDN or 5G-enabled architectures. In another work, Hassan et al. [23] proposed a load-aware resource allocation strategy for heterogeneous fog computing environments. The method estimates the volume of sensed data generated by clusters of edge devices and assigns them to appropriate fog nodes based on available computational resources. Their approach demonstrates improvements in latency and network utilization through extensive simulations on different scales using the iFogSim toolkit. However, although their results confirm the effectiveness of load-aware allocation, the proposed framework does not incorporate SDN or 5G-enabled communication mechanisms, which are essential for dynamic network control and ultra-low-latency requirements in modern IoMT systems.

Recent studies have explored SDN-based reconfigurable edge and fog architectures to improve task allocation and network adaptability. For example, Alam et al. [24] proposed a programmable edge network for IIoT that dynamically reconfigures communication and application modules to reduce actuation latency and improve energy efficiency. Similarly, Alam et al. [25] introduced OptiFog, which predicts fog node resource availability using a stochastic model to optimize task offloading and minimize latency. While these works provide effective predictive and adaptive strategies, they primarily target industrial IoT scenarios and rely on complex prediction models. Tripathy et al. [26] proposed a software defined fog architecture for healthcare IoT using WBANs, optimizing fog node utility to reduce delays and energy use via Lagrangian optimization. However, it ignores 5G, constraining its relevance for low latency IoMT systems. Imanpour et al. [27] developed an SDN-based load-balancing approach for multimedia IoMT, using long short term memory and fuzzy systems to improve QoS and efficiency. Yet, it excludes 5G and fog computing, missing critical support for high-bandwidth and edge processing needs. Patel et al. [28] introduced SDN-care, a deep learning-based SDN approach using CNNs to optimize network parameters for Healthcare 4.0 communication. Despite strong performance, it neglects 5G and fog computing, limiting its ability to meet ultra-low-latency and edge-processing demands.

Existing studies often focus on subsets of SDN, fog computing, or 5G. They typically overlook critical aspects like ultra-low latency or edge processing. In this paper, we aim to bridge this gap by proposing a novel framework that synergistically combines SDN, fog computing and 5G for efficient task assignment in IoMT, ensuring QoS, scalability and real-time responsiveness for modern healthcare applications.

Methods

In this section, we present the proposed architecture for optimized task allocation in SDN-enabled 5G IoMT networks using fog computing. The framework integrates a multi-layered topology with a dedicated fog broker to manage patient-generated tasks efficiently, leveraging SDN’s centralized control and fog computing’s edge processing capabilities.

Heuristic algorithm

Given the NP-Complete nature of the optimization problem [29], we propose a heuristic to assign tasks efficiently. The algorithm prioritizes minimizing the cost function Z while respecting constraints.

Specifically, α prioritizes latency-sensitive healthcare tasks, β controls energy-aware allocation, and γ enforces balanced utilization across fog nodes. The heuristic dynamically evaluates these components for each task-node pair, enabling adaptive decision-making under varying system conditions. If no feasible node is found, the task is marked as unassigned and is forwarded to the hospital cloud. The time complexity of Algorithm 1 is O(nm), where n is the number of tasks and m is the number of fog nodes. This arises from the nested loop evaluating each task against each fog node to compute the cost function . The effectiveness of the proposed heuristic stems from its ability to jointly consider multiple conflicting objectives, namely latency, energy consumption, and load balancing, within a unified cost function. Unlike conventional scheduling approaches such as nearest-node selection or Round Robin, which focus on a single criterion, the proposed method evaluates each task–node pair using a composite metric that captures both system performance and resource utilization. This enables more informed and balanced decision-making, particularly in dynamic IoMT environments. From a computational perspective, the algorithm follows a deterministic greedy strategy with a finite number of tasks and fog nodes, guaranteeing convergence in a bounded number of iterations. Specifically, the algorithm evaluates at most n × m possible assignments, resulting in a predictable execution time suitable for real-time applications. Although the underlying optimization problem is NP-Complete and computing the global optimum is computationally prohibitive, the proposed heuristic provides an efficient approximation with low complexity. Instead of seeking exact optimality, it achieves near-optimal performance in practice, as demonstrated by the experimental results, while maintaining scalability and responsiveness required for healthcare scenarios. For practical IoMT scenarios with hundreds of tasks and tens of fog nodes, the complexity of the proposed algorithm remains manageable, as m is typically small (e.g., 5–10 nodes in hospital settings). To validate scalability, we conducted simulations with up to 25 UEs and 12 fog nodes, as discussed in Section 4, demonstrating real-time performance suitable for healthcare applications.

Performance evaluation

In this section, we conduct a comprehensive performance evaluation of the proposed optimized task allocation framework for SDN-enabled 5G IoMT networks using fog computing. We assess its effectiveness in terms of latency, energy efficiency, task completion rates and scalability, comparing it against four baseline approaches: Random Fog Node Selection, Nearest Fog Node Selection, Round Robin and Cloud-Only processing. The evaluation leverages well-established network simulation tools NS3 [30], Mininet [31] and Ryu [32] to model the 5G IoMT environment, SDN infrastructure and task allocation process.

Simulation setup

We simulate the proposed architecture on a virtual machine equipped with 6 CPU cores and 11 GB of RAM. The simulation environment integrates NS3 to model 5G network nodes, including User Equipment (UEs) representing IoMT devices and the Radio Access Network (RAN) with gNodeBs, mimicking patient-generated health data traffic. The simulation environment also uses Mininet to emulate the SDN-based transport network, consisting of OpenFlow-enabled switches. Moreover, Ryu serves as the SDN controller, managing flow rule installation for task routing between UEs, fog nodes and the hospital cloud.

The simulation parameters are summarized in Table 3. We consider three fog nodes and one fog broker, with fog node characteristics (bandwidth, processing power and energy consumption rate) randomly assigned within specified ranges. Each UE generates tasks with random attributes (data size, required CPU cycles and deadlines) within predefined ranges, sending them to the fog broker. The broker evaluates fog nodes using the proposed heuristic (Algorithm 1) and other baselines to assign tasks, ensuring constraints on capacity and deadlines are met. If no fog node can process a task, it is forwarded to the hospital cloud, which has unlimited processing power but incurs a fixed 5 ms transmission delay and 0.5 Joules per second of processing energy. The total simulation duration is 5 minutes per scenario.

Experimental results

The simulation results demonstrate the superiority of the proposed framework across all metrics, particularly in latency reduction, energy efficiency and task completion rates. Below, we summarize the findings for each metric, with detailed comparisons across the three UE scenarios (5, 10 and 15 UEs).

Total energy consumption

Figure 3 illustrates the total energy consumed in each scenario, as well as the energy consumed per task for all compared methods. As shown, offloading all tasks to the cloud leads to significantly high energy consumption, highlighting the importance of fog-based solutions for energy efficiency. Our proposed method consistently achieves the lowest energy consumption across all scenarios. This is because energy efficiency is explicitly integrated into our optimization objective function, guiding the fog broker to assign tasks to nodes that minimize energy usage. Among the baseline methods, Nearest and Round Robin show comparable energy usage, outperforming the Random method. The poor performance of the Random method is due to its lack of awareness regarding node energy states, often resulting in high-energy nodes being chosen arbitrarily. In contrast, Nearest and Round Robin consider network conditions to some extent, leading to relatively better energy profiles. Although the numerical differences between the compared methods in Fig. 3 are not large, the proposed approach consistently achieves better performance. Practical deployment considerations

Although the proposed framework demonstrates promising performance in simulation, several practical challenges must be considered for real-world deployment. First, fog node heterogeneity is inherently addressed in our model through varying computational resources (e.g., CPU and memory), which are incorporated into the task allocation process. However, real-world environments may exhibit more dynamic and unpredictable resource variations. In addition, network failures and intermittent connectivity can significantly affect system reliability. The integration of SDN in the proposed architecture can facilitate dynamic rerouting and adaptive resource management to mitigate such issues. Device mobility, particularly in IoMT scenarios (e.g., wearable or portable medical devices), introduces additional complexity in maintaining stable connections and low-latency communication. Furthermore, deployment in real hospital environments requires interoperability with existing healthcare information systems, compliance with data privacy regulations, and stringent latency requirements for critical applications. While these aspects are beyond the scope of the current study, they represent important directions for future research and practical implementation.

Discussion

The results highlight the ability of the proposed framework to address the stringent requirements of IoMT. Using SDN’s centralized control and the edge processing of fog computing, it achieves lower latency, less consumption of backhaul bandwidth and reduced energy consumption compared to baselines. The multiobjective optimization of the heuristic ensures a balanced load distribution, preventing fog node overload, a common issue in the Nearest baseline. The scalability of the framework is evident as performance remains robust with increasing UE, unlike Random and Round Robin, which degrade under high loads. Compared to Cloud-Only, the proposed method underscores the advantages of fog computing in reducing latency and energy costs, critical for real-time healthcare applications. This validates the effectiveness of intelligent fog computing strategies in enhancing IoMT system performance. By increasing task processing within the fog layer and reducing reliance on the cloud, our method improves system responsiveness and energy efficiency both critical factors for ensuring patient satisfaction and reliability in healthcare applications.

Although recent studies have explored machine learning and reinforcement learning-based approaches for task offloading and scheduling, such methods often require significant training overhead and computational resources. In contrast, the proposed framework focuses on a lightweight heuristic design suitable for real-time IoMT scenarios with resource-constrained fog nodes. Incorporating learning-based strategies while preserving efficiency remains an important direction for future work.

It is also worth mentioning that in healthcare-oriented IoMT environments, data security and privacy are of paramount importance due to the sensitive nature of patient information and strict regulatory requirements [33]. While the proposed method primarily focuses on efficient task allocation and resource management, the underlying SDN–fog architecture provides a flexible foundation for integrating advanced security mechanisms. In particular, SDN enables centralized and programmable control, which can be leveraged for fine-grained access control, traffic isolation, and real-time anomaly detection [34]. Secure communication can be ensured through encryption protocols and authentication mechanisms between IoMT devices, fog nodes, and cloud layers. Moreover, fog computing allows localized data processing, which reduces the need to transmit raw sensitive data to centralized servers, thereby enhancing privacy. In addition, privacy-preserving techniques such as data anonymization, secure aggregation, and lightweight encryption schemes can be incorporated at the fog layer to further protect patient data. Although a full implementation of these mechanisms is beyond the scope of this work, the proposed architecture is designed to seamlessly support such extensions, which will be investigated in future research.

Conclusions

This paper presents an optimized task allocation framework for SDN-enabled 5G IoMT networks, leveraging fog computing to address the stringent requirements of healthcare applications. By integrating a fog broker with a multi-objective optimization model, our approach minimizes latency, load imbalance and energy consumption while ensuring deadline compliance for patient-generated tasks. The proposed heuristic efficiently solves the NP-hard allocation problem and SDN’s centralized control enables dynamic routing to support seamless task execution. Simulation results demonstrate significant improvements over baselines, including reduction in latency, fewer deadline violations and lower energy consumption, alongside enhanced load distribution. These findings validate the framework’s ability to enhance QoS, scalability, and sustainability in IoMT systems. In practical terms, the proposed approach can reduce patient monitoring delays and optimize resource usage in real-world healthcare deployments. Future work will focus on dynamic fog node orchestration, AI-driven adaptive scheduling, reinforcement learning for predicting task arrivals and prioritizing emergency tasks, and container-based deployment strategies to enable scalable and robust fog infrastructures in real-world IoMT testbeds.
