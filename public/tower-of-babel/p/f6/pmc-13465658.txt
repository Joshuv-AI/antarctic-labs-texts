Development and validation of a culturally relevant tool for advance care planning in India
Authors: Parth Sharma, Muskan Matia, Smriti Khanna, Gayatri Sharma, Smriti Rana, Hardik Dua, Shireen Yachu, Dhvani Mehta, Rajani Surendar Bhatt, Savita Butola, Roop Gursahani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvance care planning (ACP) enables individuals to communicate their values, goals, and preferences for future healthcare. Despite evidence supporting its benefits, ACP uptake in India remains limited due to cultural norms favouring family-centred decision-making, low public awareness, and a lack of culturally relevant educational tools. This study aimed to develop and validate a culturally relevant ACP educational tool for the Indian community.MethodsA structured mixed-methods study was conducted in 3 phases. Phase 1 involved the development of an ACP educational booklet using culturally appropriate language and visual aids. Phase 2 comprised validation by a multidisciplinary panel of 10 ACP experts using the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (PEMAT) to assess understandability and actionability, supplemented by qualitative feedback. Phase 3 involved community validation by 16 educated urban community participants using the same assessment framework. Quantitative data were analysed descriptively. Qualitative feedback was analysed thematically to help refine the booklet.ResultsACP experts reported high overall scores for understandability (95%) and actionability (100%). The educated urban community participants also reported high scores, with understandability at 91.4% and actionability at 93.7%. Qualitative feedback highlighted clarity and cultural relevance as key strengths. Suggestions regarding simplification, length reduction, and clearer next steps were incorporated into the final version.ConclusionThis study presents a systematically developed and validated educational ACP booklet tailored for the Indian community. The tool demonstrates high understandability and actionability, and holds promise for improving public awareness and engagement with ACP. It contributes to the broader goal of advancing patient-centred care in culturally diverse populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09384-4.

Background

Advance care planning (ACP) refers to processes that support adults at any age or stage of health in understanding and sharing their personal values, life goals, and preferences regarding future medical care [1]. ACP is not a one-time event, but a dynamic, proactive process of communication that prepares healthcare providers and caregivers for urgent decision-making according to an individual’s wishes [2]. ACP empowers individuals to make informed decisions about their healthcare preferences, ensuring that their wishes are respected and adhered to by caregivers and healthcare providers, particularly in serious or chronic illness where they might be unable to articulate these themselves [3, 4]. These discussions may be formally documented as an Advance Medical Directive (AMD), which provides legally recognized instructions to guide healthcare decisions when an individual loses decision-making capacity.

ACP has been shown to improve multiple outcomes: concordance between care preference and delivered care [5], reduction in unwanted aggressive treatments, increased use of palliative care services [6], patient satisfaction, and psychological well-being of family members [7]. Despite the benefits, the uptake and implementation of ACP remain suboptimal in many clinical practices worldwide [3]. The barriers to ACP uptake exist on both ends—patients and families, and healthcare providers. The main barriers reported by professionals are a lack of knowledge and skills to undertake ACP, a sense of apprehension towards conversations around death and dying, and the unavailability of time for discussions. Fear of discussing their relatives’ end of life, infrastructural and interpersonal challenges to carry out ACP, and not knowing who is responsible for initiating ACP conversations are the main barriers to uptake of ACP by patients and their family members [8].

In the Indian context, ACP is still an emerging concept. The legal discourse around advance care planning in India has evolved primarily through judicial pronouncements rather than legislation. In the judgment of the Common Cause v. Union of India by the Supreme Court of India, the apex court of the country recognized the right of individuals to execute AMDs or “living wills” and affirmed the legality of withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment under specific safeguards. Subsequent clarifications by the Court in 2023 simplified the procedural requirements for implementing AMDs, making the process more feasible in clinical practice [9].

Despite these developments, multiple cultural and institutional factors hinder the uptake of ACP in India. Traditional decision-making models often assume the family at the core of end-of-life choices, contrasting sharply with the individualistic autonomy typically offered in most Western countries [10]. In India and other South Asian communities, it is commonplace for family members to withhold health-related information from the sick [11]. This prevents the patient from making informed choices about their own treatment. These family-centred cultural norms, combined with the information asymmetry between the public and healthcare providers, present unique challenges to widespread ACP adoption within the Indian communities [10, 12].

While various advance care planning (ACP) tools have been developed in Western countries, a culturally tailored tool for the Indian context was lacking. For instance, the U.S. National Institute on Aging (NIA) Advance Care Planning guide and the MD Anderson Cancer Center ACP workbook heavily emphasize individual autonomy, personal values clarification, legal advance directives, and state-specific documentation processes grounded in the American healthcare and legal systems [13, 14]. These tools assume a relatively individualistic decision-making model with limited integration of extended family consensus, or resource constraints typical of Indian healthcare settings. The Ontario ACP workbook also follows a comparable approach [15].

Such tools are therefore of limited relevance in India, where end-of-life decision-making is typically family-centred, influenced by strong cultural, religious, and filial obligations, and operates within a different legal and healthcare framework. Additionally, ACP awareness remains limited in India. For instance, an online survey conducted on college graduates revealed that 72.1% did not know about AMDs [16]. This highlights the need for ACP-related educational materials suited for the general community to cater to subpopulations at distinct levels of health literacy [17, 18]. Currently, there is no public-facing information, education and communication (IEC) material to simplify ACP in India. Addressing this critical need, the present study aimed to develop and validate a culturally appropriate and easily understandable educational booklet on ACP, specifically tailored for the general community in India.

Methods

Study design

This study is an instrument development and content validation study that uses mixed methods to create an ACP educational booklet tailored for the general community in India. The research was conducted over three sequential phases to ensure theoretical integrity and for the structured improvement of the booklet, facilitated by learning from different phases to enhance the practical applicability of the educational material.

Phase 1: development of the booklet

The development phase consisted of a comprehensive literature review of existing materials on ACP, patient education, and health communication strategies. The following keywords were used in a Google Search to identify relevant patient education materials and informational resources: “advance care planning,” “advance directives,” “living will,” “end-of-life decision making,” “advance care planning patient education,” “advance care planning educational material,” “advance care planning booklet,” “advance care planning brochure,” “advance care planning patient information,” “end-of-life care education material,” and “patient information leaflet advance care planning”. Co-authors with expertise and global experience in advance care planning (SK, DM, RG) were consulted to identify education material missed in Google Search. Effective health communication strategies, particularly those that are culturally sensitive and utilize visual aids, were identified as they are paramount for disseminating complex health information and promoting behavioural change in diverse populations [19–21]. Studies on populations with similarly diverse cultural norms as India were noted [22, 23]. Collaborating with an expert illustrator with a background in emergency care and public health, the initial booklet draft was created (GS). It incorporated simple language, culturally relevant examples, and clear visual aids to enhance comprehension and engagement [19, 20].

Phase 2: ACP expert validation

A multidisciplinary panel composed of 10 specialists of palliative care, public health, and patient advocacy (termed “ACP expert’) independently evaluated the initial draft. The panel strength of 10 experts falls within the recommended range of 6–10 experts [24]. Evaluation was done using the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (PEMAT), a previously published and validated instrument, to assess the understandability and actionability of the educational booklet. [25]. PEMAT scoring is grouped under two domains. The understandability domain assesses whether consumers of varying health literacy levels can process and explain key messages (e.g., content clarity, word choice, organization, layout, and use of visual aids). The actionability domain evaluates whether the material clearly identifies actions the user can take (e.g., explicit steps, concrete instructions, and use of visual cues to support action). Each item is rated as “Agree” (score = 1), “Disagree” (score = 0), or “Not Applicable (N/A)”. Items marked N/A are excluded from the denominator. Domain scores are calculated as percentages using the formula: (Sum of points for “Agree” ÷ Total possible points excluding N/A items) × 100.

Separate percentage scores are then generated for understandability and actionability, with higher scores indicating better performance. Conventionally, scores of 70% or higher are considered indicative of adequately understandable or actionable materials, although this threshold is interpretive rather than absolute. The tool does not generate a composite score; instead, both domains are reported independently to provide a nuanced assessment of material quality.

Alongside PEMAT scores, open-ended feedback on clarity, usability, and overall impression was recorded, aligning with established validation protocols for health resources [26–28]. The iterative feedback process enabled amendments to readability, visual presentation, and cultural resonance, ultimately producing a version ready for community testing [12, 23, 26].

Phase 3: validation by educated urban community participants

Community validation involved 16 English-speaking adults aged over 18 years, without formal medical or legal training, and not living with any life-limiting illness. They had a clear understanding of ACP, assessed through their awareness and comprehension of ACP concepts via a questionnaire. They were chosen because medical professionals, though knowledgeable, might overlook medical jargon in the educational booklet that could be difficult for the general public to understand. This ensured the booklet would be largely accessible and understandable to everyone, regardless of their socio-cultural identity. These educated urban community participants were recruited through convenience sampling. The booklet finalized by the multidisciplinary expert panel was re-evaluated by them using the PEMAT to assess understandability and actionability. Additionally, open-ended feedback was recorded on clarity, usability, and overall impression of the booklet [26–28]. This phase ensured the material's effectiveness in communicating complex concepts in a relatable format to the community.

Data analysis

Descriptive analysis of PEMAT scores was done to evaluate the understandability and actionability of the booklet. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Fleiss' kappa, which measures the degree of agreement among multiple raters beyond chance for categorical ratings. For each PEMAT item, ratings (Agree = 1, Disagree = 0) were aggregated across raters, and kappa was computed by comparing the observed proportion of agreement to the expected agreement by chance. Items marked as “Not Applicable” were excluded from the analysis. Kappa values were interpreted using standard benchmarks (≤ 0: poor, 0.01–0.20: slight, 0.21–0.40: fair, 0.41–0.60: moderate, 0.61–0.80: substantial, 0.81–1.00: almost perfect agreement). Analysis was performed using R software version 4.5.3.

Qualitative data from open-ended responses were analyzed using an inductive thematic analysis approach. An initial coding framework was developed through familiarisation with the data, followed by open coding of a subset of responses to identify recurrent patterns. Codes were iteratively refined and grouped into higher-order categories through constant comparison. Each response was coded across one or more domains as applicable. Coding was conducted iteratively, with ongoing refinement of code definitions to ensure conceptual clarity and consistency. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and the framework was finalized once thematic saturation was achieved. Representative quotations were selected to illustrate each theme in the results. Findings from both quantitative and qualitative analyses were used to refine the booklet and enhance its effectiveness for the target population.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 10 ACP experts and 16 educated urban community participants were included in the study. The ACP expert panel consisted of 4 public health professionals, 3 palliative care specialists, 1 palliative care counsellor, 1 neurologist, and 1 patient advocacy representative. Their ages ranged from 27 to 66 years (47.8 ± 12.80 years). Among the educated urban community participants, 25% were male, and 75% were female, with ages ranging from 38 to 87 years (56.06 ± 13.61 years). Half of the educated urban community participants were graduates, and the other half were post-graduates. 56.2% of them self-reported themselves to be proficient in the English language, and 44.8% self-reported to have an advanced level of proficiency in the English language.

ACP experts’ feedback

Thematic analysis of this set of feedback highlighted domains of terminology consistency and legal accuracy, language clarity and inclusivity, content completeness, formatting and visual design, and overall length and usability. The experts frequently pointed out the need for consistent and standardized terminology across the document, particularly in legally sensitive sections (“There are some words which have been used synonymously—agent, health care power of attorney, etc. Use a word consistently that is used by Supreme Court”; “the man is addressing the lady as ‘Naani’, whereas through the document the word ‘aunty’ is being used”).

Suggestions also emphasized improving linguistic clarity and neutrality, including simplification and gender-neutral phrasing (“‘talk with him or her’ can be replaced with ‘them’”). The experts also identified gaps in clinical completeness, suggesting inclusion of additional relevant information (“Tube feeding only mentions tube through the nose… tube in the stomach (PEG) can be added”). Concerns regarding visual clarity and layout were recurrent, including difficulty interpreting graphical elements and the need for better formatting (“Illustration/photos… may be helpful”). Finally, the length and density of the document were again highlighted as barriers to usability (“This is a very long document… it would be better to cut short the contents and give it as a supplementary document”). Collectively, these inputs reinforced the need for greater standardization, simplification, visual coherence, and content optimization to improve usability and acceptability. Stakeholder feedback and corresponding design changes

Educated urban community participants’ feedback

Thematic analysis of open-ended stakeholder feedback identified key domains including clarity and usability, length and redundancy, visual design and structure, procedural clarity, and emotional framing. Overall, participants perceived the document as accessible and informative, with comments such as “I found it very comprehensive and easy to understand” and “Interesting, fresh information on something I had no knowledge about.” However, concerns were raised regarding excessive length and repetition, which could limit engagement (“I only felt it was too long and hence people may not read through to the end”). Visual inconsistency and layout complexity were also frequently highlighted, with participants noting that “too many text boxes, each of different design – distracting and confusing” and expressing a preference for more structured presentation (“Visually, I would like the content to be more structured to make clear distinctions between segments”). Suggestions regarding document flow included bringing more actionable, practical sections earlier in the document (“Page 15 should come sooner… this is where it gets more tactical and therefore more useful”). Feedback also pointed to gaps in procedural inclusivity, particularly for individuals lacking social support (“There may be people who do not have anyone with whom they can have such a conversation”). Finally, respondents emphasized the importance of emotional framing when addressing sensitive content, noting discomfort with certain sections (“The treatment choices worksheet made even me very uncomfortable… it needs something prior that strips off the tension”) and suggesting a more gradual, values-based introduction (“It might be useful to have questions about ‘what an ideal life/good day looks like’ initially…”). Collectively, these findings informed iterative refinements to enhance the readability, usability, inclusivity, and acceptability of the tool. Final booklet integration

Based on combined insights from ACP experts and the educated urban community participants, the booklet was refined to improve readability, simplify instructions, and ensure cultural resonance. The final version is a 20-page flipbook covering six key areas: (i) the importance of ACP, (ii) reflections on personal values and treatment preferences, (iii) appointment of healthcare proxies and guidance for choosing a suitable person, (iv) life-support treatment options, (v) communicating wishes with family and healthcare providers, and (vi) renewal of the directives. Discussion

ACP educational initiatives have shifted focus from template-based advance directives to the development of decision aids in recent years that take into account an individual’s values and priorities beyond medical decisions [29–31]. The complexity of ACP conversations necessitates tools to facilitate discussions, enhance comprehension, and guide decision-making, especially in settings characterized by strong family/community involvement and pervasive social norms in healthcare [10].

A self-directed ACP workbook distributed to long-term care residents was found to effectively elicit a range of end-of-life care preferences and reflections. In a mixed-methods study involving 44 residents, most participants expressed a desire to remain involved in care decisions (70%) and prioritized quality of life over quantity (55%), while 20% voiced concerns about receiving insufficient care at the end of life. Open responses further captured preferences related to spiritual care and post-mortem planning [32].

A randomized controlled trial conducted in South Korea examined the effect of a video-assisted advance care planning (ACP) decision aid (comprising a video and booklet) on end-of-life care preferences among patients with advanced cancer. In this study of 204 participants, the intervention group showed a significantly greater shift toward hospice care preferences compared to the control group, particularly under assumptions of shorter life expectancy. This study added to the limited evidence from non-Western settings that structured ACP decision aids can influence patient preferences for end-of-life care in the short term [33]. Extensive evidence supporting the role of video decision support systems in ACP highlights the role of culturally tailored decision support tools in ACP [33–36].

Evidence from various health domains shows that culturally tailored educational materials improve comprehension, engagement, and acceptability among diverse populations. In fields like neurocognitive disorders, reproductive health, and preventive health behaviours, using culturally familiar language and visuals has helped enhance comprehension and address differences in health literacy [4, 15, 16].

Against this backdrop, the present study contributes to the development and validation of a culturally appropriate ACP educational booklet designed for public use in India. The phased, stakeholder-driven approach used in this study is consistent with established best practices for developing culturally responsive patient-facing resources [37]. Effective patient-reported initiatives emphasize co-design, iterative refinement based on qualitative feedback, and the use of plain-language to enhance accessibility and comprehension [37]. Our methodology reflects these principles through systematic incorporation of expert and end-user feedback, simplification of terminology, and iterative modification to improve clarity and usability.

The high understandability and actionability scores observed among ACP experts and the educated urban community participants suggest that the booklet effectively communicates complex concepts. The slightly lower scores among the educated urban community participants than ACP experts may reflect differences in familiarity with medical decision-making processes and/or the frequency of engagement. The ACP expert validation phase of this study provided crucial feedback affirming the booklet's cultural appropriateness and content accuracy, establishing its readiness for application with the educated urban community participants. Their validation reinforced the booklet's high level of engagement and comprehensibility. The inclusion of qualitative feedback allowed for contextual interpretation of quantitative PEMAT scores and facilitated improvements to the booklet.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers a framework that can be adapted for developing and validating an ACP awareness tool for the Indian public, with promising implications in policy and practice. Healthcare providers, patient advocacy groups, and policymakers can leverage the findings of the study to promote discussions around ACP. A standardized and validated assessment tool (PEMAT) was employed to assess the understandability and actionability of the booklet. Combining inputs from multidisciplinary ACP experts and educated urban community participants provided dual validation to the booklet.

However, the study's sample in the community validation phase was limited to educated urban community participants who were predominantly urban, English-speaking, and ACP-aware, restricting the generalizability of the findings to rural, non-English-speaking, and/or ACP-naïve populations. Additionally, convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, and self-reported understanding could have overestimated retention or behavioural change. Lastly, this study did not assess behavioural outcomes, conduct formal readability or psychometric reliability testing (including test–retest reliability), or evaluate implementation feasibility in real-world settings.

Future research should aim to translate and validate the booklet into regional languages to expand its reach and assess the scope of its geographical viability. In addition to this, mapping the impact of this resource on the actual completion of an advance medical directive and family discussions will provide further insights into its effectiveness. Integration into primary care, public health programs, or hospital settings, coupled with longitudinal studies assessing behavioural outcomes, will optimize its contribution to the domain of public health.

Conclusion

The development of a culturally adapted ACP booklet represents a pioneering initiative within India's complex cultural landscape. Its high acceptability and potential for improving ACP awareness underscore its significance as a model for similar interventions globally. By fostering informed healthcare decisions, this resource contributes to the broader goal of advancing patient-centred care in culturally diverse populations.
