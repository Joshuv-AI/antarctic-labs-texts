Physiological and molecular mechanism analysis of Cyclocodon lancifolius seedlings in response to varying degrees of drought stress
Authors: Jian Wang, Xiaoxia Wan, Qingxia Liu, Yan Zhang, Bei Tian, Chunling Chen, Ronghui Gu, Bo Wang, Jingzhong Chen, Liang Chen, Qingwen Sun, Yuan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCyclocodon lancifolius is an increasingly valued dual-purpose medicinal-edible plant resource in contemporary society. Owing to the coincidence of its rapid seedling growth phase with the season characterized by frequent droughts, water-deficit stress severely compromises seedling development and biomass accumulation under field cultivation. Nevertheless, the Physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying drought stress perception, signal transduction and adaptive responses in C. lancifolius seedlings remain virtually uncharacterized. Consequently, an integrated dual-omics approach combing transcriptomics and metabolomics is imperative to initially dissect the drought-responsive regulatory networks, thereby providing a foundational framework for elucidating the complete signal transduction cascades that govern drought adaptation in this species.ResultsSeven-month-old C. lancifolius seedlings were subjected to three treatments: control (C), moderately drought (MD), and severely drought (SD). Drought stress inhibited the growth and development, and photosynthesis of C. lancifolius seedlings, significantly reducing Tr, PN, Ci, Gs, LCP, and CE in photosynthetic characteristics. Drought stress also modulated other physiological and biochemical characteristics, including reduced relative water content (RWC), leaf biomass, and chlorophyll content, which affect membrane lipid peroxidation and osmotic adjustment by increasing electrolyte permeability, malondialdehyde (MDA), and proline content. Concurrently, antioxidant enzymes such as peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) were activated to scavenge excess reactive oxygen species (ROS). Transcriptome analysis showed that drought stress induced more downregulated genes than upregulated genes in C. lancifolius seedlings. Metabolomics analysis revealed that there were more upregulated metabolites than downregulated metabolites. Combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses highlighted the crucial roles of starch and sucrose metabolism, glutathione metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and flavonoid biosynthesis in drought tolerance of C. lancifolius seedlings, and explore the synthetic pathway of luteolin and caffeoylquinic acid. It was discovered that certain drought conditions promoted the caffeoylquinic acid accumulation and reduced the content of luteolin.ConclusionsThe study provides the first expressed genome resource for C. lancifolius, a novel medicinal and edible plant. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic data collectively reveal the critical molecular regulatory network underlying drought stress response in C. lancifolius. Thus, these findings lay the foundation for molecular breeding of drought-resistant varieties for C. lancifolius and other medicinal plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07373-1.

Background

In recent years, the frequency of drought events has increased globally owing to climate variability, with extreme droughts expected to intensify [1–3]. Plants, being highly susceptible to drought stress, experience severe effects on their growth and development, leading to death and reduction in plant productivity. Therefore, drought stress represents a critical environmental factor that limits the distribution, diversity, growth, and development of plant species [4]. Under drought stress, plants adapt through various physiological, biochemical, molecular, and metabolic adjustments [5, 6]. Soybean tolerates drought stress by increasing endogenous hormone levels, inhibiting photosynthetic pathways, reducing water loss, and enhancing cellular protection mechanisms [7, 8]. Similarly, wheat adapts to drought stress through osmotic adjustment, antioxidant enzyme activities, ABA signaling, modulation of photosynthesis, altered metabolic pathways, and accumulation or reduction of secondary metabolites [9, 10].

Multiple genes related to signaling pathways participate in the response to drought stress. In the ABA signaling pathway, ABA binds to PYR/PYL receptors, causing PP2C to become inactive. As PP2C is inhibits SnRK2, inactivation of PP2C activates SnRK2. The activated SnRK2 induces stomatal closure and osmotic adjustment by regulating downstream ion channels and transcription factors (AREB) [11]. The Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway act as a core pathway for plant responses to drought stress, which generally functions by responding to ABA and regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Furthermore, several WRKY transcription factors act as substrates of the MAPK cascade under drought stress [12]. Genes related to anabolic pathways also play an important role in plant drought stress, including starch and sucrose metabolism (e.g., INVs, TPS, SUS, SPS, ISA), glutathione metabolism (e.g., GPX, GR, 6PGD, GS), phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (e.g., PAL, 4CL, HCT, C4H), and flavonoid biosynthesis (e.g., CHS, CHI, F3H, DFR), and these genes were identified as significant influencing plant resistant to drought stress [13–18]. Moreover, transcription factors (e.g., HSF, bZIP, MYB, NAC, ERF) act as molecular switches, regulate the transcription of downstream genes, and respond to various upstream regulatory factors [19].

In recent times, with advancement of research has revealed that the accumulation of numerous metabolites is regulated by drought stress. Phenylpropanoid metabolites, including flavonoids (e.g., luteolin, naringenin chalcone, naringenin) and phenolic acids (e.g., caffeoylquinic acid), play a critical role in plant adaptation to drought stress [20, 21]. For instance, In Zea mays, Hippophae rhamnoides and Triticum aestivum, flavonoids enhance drought tolerance by mitigating oxidative stress and modulating stress responsive pathways [22–24]. The flavonoids luteolin, vitexin, genistin, and hyperoside were significantly increased in sour orange of citrus species (drought-tolerant) under drought stress, but these flavonoids were significantly decreased in lemon (drought-sensitive) in response to drought stress [25]. In addition, caffeoylquinic acid was significantly decreased in Ligularia fischeri under drought stress [26].

Cyclocodon lancifolius (Roxb.) Merr, previously named Campanumoea lancifolia (Roxb.) Merr is a perennial herb of the Campanulaceae family that grows in thickets, forests, and grasslands at elevations below 1,500 m, primarily distributed in the Yunnan, Guizhou, and Hunan provinces of China [27]. Its young shoot tips are a delicious vegetable consumed in daily life. Modern research has validated that its leaves are rich in flavonoids, which prevent and treat neuropathic diseases, delay aging, strengthen DNA protection, treat coronary heart disease, and exhibit anticancer and antiviral effects [28]. Its fruits, called Hong Guo ginseng Guo (HGGG), are rich in mineral elements, polysaccharides, dietary fiber, and anthocyanins and have long been consumed as fresh fruits or juices in traditional practices. Owing to its appealing taste and health benefits, HGGG has become a valuable resource with medicinal, edible, and health-related benefits [29]. Therefore, the medicinal and edible plant C. lancifolius has been cultivated in Guizhou, Yunnan, Guangxi, Sichuan, and Zhejiang provinces of China, with a current cultivation area exceeding 670 hectares. Field investigation in cultivation base of C. lancifolius’s revealed that the rapid seedling growth stage coincides with the hot and arid season. Adequate soil moisture is a critical factor influencing seedling growth and development, and prolonged water deficit for one week may lead to seedling mortality. Currently, researchers have only preliminarily investigated partial metabolites and pharmacological activities of C. lancifolius, while its molecular mechanisms in response to drought stress remain unexplored [30, 31].

Advancements in high-throughput sequencing and Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS) technologies have enabled broader application of transcriptomics and metabolomics to study the molecular response mechanisms of plants to drought stress [32–35]. For example, an integrative analysis of the metabolome and transcriptome of Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim. leaves under drought stress indicated that flavonoid components of Zanthoxylum bungeanum Maxim. leaves and document changes in flavonoid metabolism under drought stress [18]. In potatoes, a combined analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic demonstrated that the flavonoids as modulators of drought resistance, and MYB-WD40-bHLH transcription factor complex play a pivotal regulatory function via the flavonoid pathway [36]. In addition, the key candidate genes (such as PRR7, PRR5, GI, NCED, PP2C, PYL, ABF, WRKY33, and P5CS, etc.) and three metabolites (oxidised glutathione, abscisic acid, and phenylalanine) were found to be related to drought tolerance [37]. Taken together, the multi-omics approaches have advanced our comprehension of the molecular regulatory networks in plant drought tolerance.

In this study, to elucidate the molecular regulatory network underlying the response of C. lancifolius seedlings to drought stress, we conducted phenotypic, physiological, biochemical, transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses on C. lancifolius seedlings subjected to varying degrees drought stress. Through integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic data, we reconstructed the molecular regulatory network and identified several key drought-responsive genes and metabolites of C. lancifolius. These findings not only uncover genetic mechanisms of drought adaptation but also provide a theoretical basis for breeding highly drought-ressitant C. lancifolius varieties.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and drought stress treatment

C. lancifolius seeds were obtained from the Germplasm Resource Nursery of the College of Pharmacy, Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (GUTCM). Seed were sown in humus medium with pot (13 cm height, 15 cm diameter). Nine seedlings per pot were transplanted, with each treatment replicated in nine pots. All plants were cultivated in a growth chamber at 28 °C under a 16 h/8 h (light/dark) photoperiod and 60% relative humidity. C. lancifolius seedlings that are seven months old and have grown to 10 leaves are then subjected to drought treatment. Measurement of soil moisture content (SMC) was carried out using the weighing method: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{SMC}\;(\%)\;=\frac{\mathrm{Weight}\;\mathrm{of}\;\mathrm{original}\;\mathrm{soil}\;-\;\mathrm{Weight}\;\mathrm{of}\;\mathrm{dried}\;\mathrm{soil}}{\mathrm{Weight}\;\mathrm{of}\;\mathrm{dried}\;\mathrm{soil}}\times100\%$$\end{document}; a steady soil moisture content was maintained by uniform spraying on the soil surface. The seedlings were classified three groups: the control group were subjected to well-watered condition with 75% SMC (C, control), the second group were withhold watering for 5 days with 45% SMC (MD, moderate drought), and the third group were withhold watering for 8 days with 25% SMC (SD, severe drought) [38, 39]. Seedlings were collected, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for later experiments.

Determination of physiological and biochemical indicators

The relative water content (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{RWC}=\frac{\left(\mathrm{FW}-\mathrm{DW}\right)}{\left(\mathrm{TW}-\mathrm{DW}\right)}\times100\%$$\end{document}) of leaves was determined by the saturated water weight method [42]. Leaf biomass was determined by placing leaf samples in a 70 °C oven and drying them to a constant weight.

The relative electrolyte leakage measurements were collected as previously described with slight modification [43]. After thoroughly washing the plant tissues with sterile water, cut them into pieces, and soak the pieces in 20 mL of sterile water for 3 h, shaking constantly during this period. Use the P902 Conductivity Meter (Shanghai Youke Instrument and Meter Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) to measure the conductivity of the above suspension as V1. Boil the above suspension at 90 °C for 30 min, then cool it at room temperature and measure the conductivity again as V2. The relative electrolyte leakage is calculated by the following equation: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Electrolyte}\;\mathrm{permeability}\;(\%)\;=\frac{\mathrm V1}{\mathrm V2}\times100\%$$\end{document}.

The contents of malondialdehyde (MDA) and proline were determined using MDA Content Assay Kit and Proline Content Assay Kit (Boxbio, AKFA013M and AKAM003M, Beijing, China), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Chlorophyll content was determined using a multifunction microplate reader (MEL, Synergy 2, USA). Leaf samples from each treatment group were homogenized with a mixed solution (anhydrous ethanol: acetone = 1:2), and the absorbance values at 645 nm and 663 nm were measured using the MEL reader. For detailed procedures were described in the BOXBIO manual (AKPL003M).

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) levels in leaf samples from each treatment group were detected using DAB staining. Leaves were incubated in DAB staining solution in the dark for 10 h. Chlorophyll was removed by washing the leaves 3–5 times with ethanol, and then observed. Detailed procedures are described in the Beyotime manual (P0203).

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined using the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) method, with the content of SOD determined by measuring changes in absorbance at 560 nm. For detailed steps, refer to the BOXBIO manual (AKAO001M-50 S). Peroxidase (POD) activity was determined using the guaiacol method, with POD content detected by measuring changes in absorbance at 470 nm. For detailed steps, refer to the BOXBIO manual (AKAO005M). Catalase (CAT) activity was determined using the ammonium molybdate colorimetric method, with changes in absorbance at 405 nm used to detect CAT content. For detailed steps, refer to the BOXBIO manual (AKAO003-2 M).

RNA extraction and RNA-seq

Total RNA was isolated using the Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The total RNA quantity and purity were analysis using the Bioanalyzer 2,100 and RNA 1,000 Nano LabChip Kit (Agilent, CA, USA) with RIN number > 7.0 [44]. Poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads were extracted to mRNA from total RNA. The sensitivity of mRNA to bivalent cations is used to fragment mRNA at elevated temperature [45]. Used to generate RNA-seq Libraries with insert sizes ranging from 300 (± 50) base pairs (bp) using mRNA-Seq sample preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA). And then performed the paired-end sequencing on an Illumina Novaseq™ 6,000 platform.

Transcriptome data analysis

The raw reads that contained adaptor contamination, low quality bases and undetermined of the bases were removed to obtain high quality clean read [46]. De novo assembly of the transcriptome was performed with Trinity (2.4.0) [47]. All assembled Unigenes were aligned against the non-redundant (Nr) protein database, Gene ontology (GO), Swiss-Prot, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and eggNOG databases using DIAMOND (0.7.12)(E-value < 0.00001) [48–50]. Transcripts Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped reads (TPM) method was used to perform expression level for unigenes [51]. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified according to the criteria of |log2FC| >1 and FDR ≤ 0.05. Enrichment analyses of GO and KEGG pathways based on the differentially expressed genes were performed by using the ClueGO plugin in Cytoscape [52].

Metabolic sample preparation and mass spectrometric data acquiring

Weigh 100 mg of C. lancifolius seedlings samples (six biological replicates from each treatment: C, MD, SD), ground them with Liquid nitrogen, added 120 µL of 50% methanol, and mixed thoroughly. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min, and then stored overnight at −20 °C for extraction. After centrifugation at 4,000 rpm for 20 min, the supernatants were transferred into 96-well plates and stored at −80 °C until LC-MS analysis. In addition, quality control (QC) samples were prepared by combining 10 µL of each extraction mixture from C. lancifolius seedlings under different treatments [53].

The mass spectrometric (MS) data of all prepared extracts were acquired by the LC-MS system following the protocols [54]. Firstly, the chromatographic separations were performed on Thermo Scientific UltiMate 3000 HPLC equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (100 mm*2.1 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters, UK). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water, 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (Acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid). Elution conditions: 5% B, 0.5 min; 5-100% B, 6 min; 100% B, 8 min; 100-5% B, 8 min; 5% B, 10 min. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, the column oven was Maintained at 35 °C, and the injection volume for each sample was 4 µL.

The high-resolution tandem mass spectrometer Q-Exactive (Thermo Scientific) was used to detect metabolites and obtain their mass data [55]. Q-Exactive assays eluents in positive and negative ion modes. The analyzer scanned over a mass range of m/z 70 − 1,050 for full scan at a resolution of 70,000. Fragmentation modes were employed in data-dependent acquisition and three ions with the highest abundance detected in each scan were selected for the determination of their fragmentation with a resolution of 17,500. Dynamic exclusion was implemented to remove some unnecessary information in spectra. In the pool of all samples, a quality control sample is collected after 10 samples are measured.

Metabolite identification and multivariate analysis

The raw data file was analyzed using XCMS software of R software, were converted into mzXML format, and were subsequently processed for peak picking, peak grouping, retention time correction, second peak grouping, and annotation of isotopes and adducts [56, 57]. Internal standards and any known pseudo positive peaks, were removed from the data set, and normalization of the data set. Analytical identification of metabolites, quantitative analysis and screening of differential expressed metabolites (DEMs) using metaX software [58]. For processed data, comment to HMDB and KEGG. LC-MS spectral data of each single sample were collected, the data will be preprocessed before the principal component analysis.

For the difference in metabolite concentration between two groups detected by the student t-tests, the p-value was calculated using the t-tests and then corrected by the FDR (Benjamini-Hochberg) [59]. Partial least squares discrimination analysis (PLS-DA) was used to determine the relationship between metabolite expression level and sample type. The VIP value was calculated, and a VIP ≥ 1.0 was used to select important features.

Joint analysis of the transcriptome-metabolome

The basic synthetic pathways of starch and sucrose metabolism, glutathione metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis in C. lancifolius seedlings were mapped according to transcriptomic data through KEGG analysis. Further analysis of the relationship between DEGs and DEMs screened from these pathways was preformed using Pearson correlation analysis. Correlation analysis was conducted using the OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool/62). DEGs-DEMs pairs with pearson’s correlation (|r| >0.8, p < 0.05) were selected for analysis, and the co-expression network was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape software.

RT-qPCR validation

Total RNA was extracted from C. lancifolius samples according to the instructions of the TransZol Up kit (TransGen). Reverse transcription was performed to synthesize cDNA with the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The RT-qPCR reaction system was prepared using SYBR Green I reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and samples were loaded onto the CG-05 fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (Heal Force, Shanghai, China) for amplification. A two-step method was employed, and the amplification protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 ℃ for 30 s. Three biological and three technical replicates were performed for RT-qPCR. Primers for selected genes were designed using Primer Premier 6.0, with Actin as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression was analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method (Primer listed in Table S1).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three independent biological replicates for each treatment. All data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 software. Data were presented as the mean ± SD of three independent experiments. Statistical significance (P < 0.05) was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s test.

Results

Phenotypic and photosynthetic responses under drought stress

C. lancifolius seedlings under varying drought stress conditions were investigated in this study. The results revealed that seedlings in the C group maintained robust growth, with plump leaves without wilting, and erect stems. In the MD group, seedlings exhibited wilting leaves and stem curvature. In the SD group, seedlings exhibited whole-plant wilting, severely wilted leaves, and pronounced stems curved (Fig. 1A).

An analysis of the photosynthetic characteristics of three groups of C. lancifolius seedlings revealed that in the light response curve, the three groups of seedlings exhibited an increase in PN with increasing PPFD. When PPFD reached a certain value (light saturation), the curve tended to stabilize and then showed a downward trend. Among them, C group reached the maximum net photosynthetic rate (9.5403 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹) at a Light saturation point of 1497.07 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, while the MD and SD groups reached Maximum net photosynthetic rate of 3.71132 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ and 2.33477 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ at Light saturation point of 862.543 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹ and 388.739 µmol·m⁻²·s⁻¹, respectively (Fig. 1B-D). At the same time, Tr, PN, Ci, Gs, LCP, and CE in the MD and SD groups were significantly decreased compared with the C group, and the lowest was the SD group. Physiological and biochemical responses under drought stress

Under progressive drought stress, the leaves of C. lancifolius seedlings suffered varying degrees of damage (Fig. 2A). Compared with C group, the RWC and leaf biomass were significantly reduced in the MD and SD groups, showing a greater reduction in the SD group than in the MD group (Fig. 2B and C). Drought stress reduced the chlorophyll content in the leaves of C. lancifolius seedlings, with the chlorophyll a, chlorophyl b, and total chlorophyll contents in the MD and SD groups significantly lower than in the C group (Fig. 2D-F).

Further analysis revealed that drought stress enhances electrolyte permeability, particularly in the SD group, where it reached 82.05% compared to 21.58% in the C group (Fig. 2G). Concurrently, drought stress also promoted MDA and proline accumulation, with the highest levels in the SD group (Fig. 2H and I). Thus, drought stress resulted to severe membrane lipid peroxidation and osmotic adjustment in the leaves of C. lancifolius seedlings.

To mitigate oxidative stress caused by drought stress, C. lancifolius seedlings activated their antioxidant system. SOD, POD, and CAT activities significantly increased in the MD and SD groups, but the accumulation levels in the MD group were higher than in the SD group (Fig. 2J-L). Illumina sequencing and assembly

Using Illumina Novaseq™ 6,000 on C. lancifolius seedlings, raw sequences bipartite yielded 55.90 Gb of clean data for nine samples, evenly distributed across three groups, with Q20 > 97.55%, Q30 > 93.12%, and a GC content of approximately 46% (Table S2). The clean read assembly yielded 63,337 genes, with a total length of 53,989,471 bp and an average length of 852 bp. N50, a quality control index for transcript assembly was 1,714 bp, indicating a high-quality assembly, as lengths over 1,000 bp are generally preferred (Table S3).

Gene annotation and functional classification

The 63,337 genes obtained were annotated using the following mainstream databases: GO (24,998 genes), KEGG (20,093 genes), Pfam (22,601 genes), Swiss-Prot (20,074 genes), eggNOG (28,714 genes), and NR (30,402 genes) (Table S4).

In the unigene’s NR database annotation, 48% of the sequences matched known plant genes (E-value < 10−5), and most closely matched with Cynara cardunculus (12.65%), followed by Actinidia chinensis (12.04%) and Vitis vinifera (9.14%) (Fig S1A). The eggNOG annotation classified 15,604 unigenes into 23 taxa, including signal transduction mechanisms, defense mechanisms, and carbohydrate transport and metabolism (CMTM), which are related to the drought stress response (Fig S1B). In the GO annotation analysis, 24,998 unigenes were categorized into biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF) (Fig S1C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis classified 20,093 unigenes into six major categories: organismal systems, metabolism, human diseases, genetic information processing, environmental information processing, and cellular processes (Fig S1D). Most of these were assigned to the metabolic category, with carbohydrates being the most abundant in metabolism, represented by 1,644 genes.

GO classification and KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs

To determine the functional differences in differential genes of C. lancifolius seedlings samples under varying levels of drought stress, GO classification and enrichment analyses were performed. The results showed that the DEGs in each group were annotated into three classifications-biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. Among the three comparison groups, the most enriched DEGs were molecular function, integral component of membrane, and chloroplast. Among the three comparison groups, the most enriched DEGs were molecular functions, membrane components, and chloroplasts. Among these three GO terms, the SD/C comparison group had more enriched DEGs than the MD/C comparison group. These GO terms may play a role in responding to drought stress (Fig. 4A).

Discussion

Effects of drought stress on the physiological and biochemical characteristics of C. lancifolius seedlings

Drought stress is a key environmental factor limiting plant growth and development, and the water deficit it induces significantly disrupts physiological metabolism in plants [60]. Our results showed that drought stress significantly suppressed the growth and development of C. lancifolius seedlings. This growth inhibition resulted from drought-induced physiological responses in C. lancifolius. Drought stress inhibits photosynthesis by triggering stomatal closure, which reduces mesophyll CO2 concentration and thereby suppresses both photosynthesis and carbon metabolism. Thus, alterations in several photosynthetic parameters are strongly linearly correlated with water deficit [61]. The results indicated that drought stress significantly reduced Tr, PN, Ci, LCP, CE and Gs in C. lancifolius seedlings, while Ls and WUEi were significantly increased. These changes were highly consistent with drought stress severity. In bambara groundnut, Gs, Tr, and Ci were significantly reduced, and WUEi was significantly increased under drought-stressed conditions [62]. PN, Tr, Gs, and WUEi differed significantly between drought stress treatments in Sargent’s cherry [63]. Therefore, with intensifying drought stress, the photosynthetic efficiency of C. lancifolius seedlings significantly decreased.

Alterations in plant physiological and biochemical provides an intuitive understanding of how plants respond to drought stress. RWC and biomass reflect the water status and growth response of plants [64]. In this study, drought stress caused a decrease in the relative water content and biomass of C. lancifolius seedlings, with the SD group showing a greater reduction than the MD group. These results are highly consistent with the changes in relative water content and biomass observed in corn subjected to drought treatment [65]. Under drought stress, the growth and development of C. lancifolius seedlings was inhibited, and their ability to maintain water content was weakened. Drought stress is an important factor in chlorophyll degradation, and its content affects plant photosynthesis [66]. Under drought stress, the chlorophyll content in the leaves of C. lancifolius seedlings decreased, and the SD group exhibited the greatest decline in chlorophyll content under drought stress, which is consistent with its lower photosynthetic metabolism.

Oxidative damage caused by ROS was exaggerated under water stress condition. Electrolyte permeability and MDA are important indicators for evaluating the degree of oxidative damage in plant suffering [67]. Our results showed that H₂O₂, electrolyte permeability and MDA accumulated in C. lancifolius seedlings under drought stress, with more severe accumulation occurring as drought severity increased. Similar results have been reported in many plants subjected to drought stress (e.g., maize, soybean, wheat) [68–70]. Furthermore, ROS-scavenging system involving antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, POD, and CAT were activated to counteract excess ROS in plant [71]. SOD, POD, and CAT activation of C. lancifolius seedlings increased significantly, consistent with the enzyme activity changes in Chinese cabbage under drought stress [72]. Additionally, the osmolyte proline content increased significantly, which helps maintain cellular water balance in C. lancifolius seedlings under varying drought stress.

DEGs response to drought stress

Drought is a crucial environmental factor affecting plant growth and development. Plants adapt to drought stress by forming a complex gene regulatory network, which induces differential gene expressions in response to varying environmental conditions [73, 74]. In this study, natural drought treatments were employed to identify DEGs under varying degrees of drought stress, aiming to preliminarily elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the drought stress response in C. lancifolius seedlings. Results revealed a great number of downregulated genes than upregulated genes in C. lancifolius seedlings under MD and SD treatments, suggesting that gene downregulation is a primarily respond to drought stress in C. lancifolius seedlings.

Combined analysis of GO and KEGG annotations could identified drought-responsive metabolic networks. The DEGs were most enriched in molecular function, integral component of membrane, and chloroplast in C. lancifolius seedlings under drought stress. These enrichments correlated with a decrease in chlorophyll content and photosynthetic efficiency, concomitant with an increase in MDA, electrolyte permeability, H2O2, proline and antioxidation enzymes. Plant hormone signal transduction, Starch and sucrose metabolism, and Plant-pathogen interaction pathway were identified to play critical roles collectively in enabling plants to resist drought stress [37]. Consistent with our findings, these pathways were significantly enriched in C. lancifolius seedlings after drought stress. Drought stress induced the accumulation of endogenous hormones (e.g., ABA, JA, and ET), while activated downstream signaling cascades and enhanced defense responses [75]. Starch and sucrose metabolism serves as a core pathway by regulating osmolyte content (e.g., Glucose, fructose, and sucrose), energy homeostasis, and signal transduction, thereby improving plant drought stress [76]. Pathogen-triggered defense system and hormones signal, indirectly modulate plant response to drought stress [77]. Moreover, new DEGs were identified under moderate and severe drought stress in C. lancifolius seedlings, respectively, which are associated with distinct pathways. These unique pathways likely play critical roles in enhancing the capacity of C. lancifolius seedlings to cope with differential drought intensities. These results align with reports in Dinanath grass under drought stress [78].

TFs are crucial upstream regulatory proteins in plant responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [79]. Under drought stress conditions, most members of the six TF families (ERF, GRAS, WRKY, bZIP, C3H, and HSF) were upregulated in C. lancifolius seedlings, suggesting their potential role in positively regulating drought response. Studies confirm the positive regulatory roles of HSF, WRKY, bZIP, and ERF in drought resistance [80–84]. Brassica napus transcription profile results demonstrated that most HSF family genes are upregulated by both drought and heat conditions, while some are responded to a high CO2 treatment; WRKY gene family members were upregulated significantly under drought stress conditions in Haloxylon, playing a crucial role in mitigating abiotic stress damage, which was higher in TF; Similarly, CgbZIPs were strongly upregulated by salt and drought stress, as well as ABA treatments; Transgenic tobacco plants overexpressing CgbZIPs exhibited enhanced tolerance to salt and drought, which was marked by improved morphological and physiological indexes. In this study, the upregulation of ERF, WRKY, and HSF TFs indicates their role in drought tolerance mechanism regulation in C. lancifolius seedlings.

DEMs response to drought stress

Drought stress not only affects plant life cycle and reproduction but also causes greater osmotic stress and metabolite alterations in plants [85, 86]. Research on plant metabolites under drought stress has focused on osmotic substances, with plants accumulating proline as a defense mechanism. Amino acids, essential osmotic regulators in plants, respond to water deprivation [87]. Studies on wheat and grape rootstocks show that plants resist drought damage by regulating metabolic substances, thereby developing drought tolerance mechanisms [10, 88]. Metabolomic analyses of C. lancifolius seedlings under varying drought conditions revealed an upward trend in proline levels of C. lancifolius seedlings, with increased amino acid expression and their derivatives, possible a response to drought stress. Additionally, biofilm integrity is crucial for plant drought resistance, with lipids and lipid-like molecules playing key roles in maintaining biofilm stability [89]. In C. lancifolius drought stress, these molecules were mostly upregulated and partially downregulated, with seven lysolecithin DEMs upregulated in MD/C while 36 were upregulated in SD/C and only one downregulated. This indicates that C. lancifolius seedlings resist varying drought stresses by regulating lipids and lipid-like molecules. Studies show that drought stress influences flavonoid content in phytomedicinal herbs [90, 91]. In metabolomics analysis, multiple flavonoids were identified as differentially expressed metabolites from three comparison groups, indicating flavonoid importance in the C. lancifolius drought response. Metabolites were altered under different drought conditions, mostly increases with increasing aridity, suggesting that C. lancifolius seedlings resist drought stress through multiple metabolic pathways.

Drought stress activates various metabolic pathways

Starch, the primary carbohydrate in plants, is hydrolyzed to glucose under drought stress, providing energy through respiration and synthesizing sucrose precursors, which are essential for drought resistance [92, 93]. As a stress-protective disaccharide, trehalose plays an important role in plant responses to drought stress. In wheat, it participates in osmotic regulation, cell stability, and energy provision to resist drought stress [94]. ROS acts as a signaling molecule in response to drought stress. Maintaining ROS homeostasis is crucial for plants to alleviate oxidative stress and enhance drought tolerance. Increased glutathione levels in Populus trees help reduce ROS accumulation and enhance drought tolerance [95]. This study found that drought stress upregulated otsB and TREH gene expression, whose synergistic effect promoted trehalose accumulation, supplying energy to combat drought stress. The glutathione metabolic pathway is vital in counteracting oxidative damage induced by drought stress. Protection against oxidative damage is closely linked to the gene expression of key enzymes regulating oxidative state, such as APX, GRC1, DHAR, MDHAR, GPX, and GST [96]. In this study, drought treatment led to the upregulation of GPX, SRM, and GCLC genes, along with the accumulation of glutathione, spermine, and 5-oxoproline.

Plants respond to oxidative damage caused by osmotic stress by accumulating active oxygen scavengers such as phenols and flavonoids, and the accumulation of phenols and flavonoids can improve the drought tolerance of plants [97, 98]. Among them, luteolin and caffeoylquinic acid, as important representative compounds of flavonoids and phenolic acids, play a significant role under drought stress [99]. The results of this study indicate that appropriate drought stress promotes changes in the content of caffeoylquinic acid in C. lancifolius seedlings. HCT, as the key enzyme in the synthesis of caffeoylquinic acid, plays an important role in the synthesis of caffeoylquinic acid. As the content of caffeoylquinic acid increases, the expression level of HCT also increases. Furthermore, the overexpression of HCT enhances the resistance of Arabidopsis and tobacco to abiotic stress [100, 101]. This study found that drought stress induced the accumulation of caffeoylquinic acid, and the amount of caffeoylquinic acid accumulated in the SD group was higher than that in the MD group. At the same time, the expression levels of HCT4, HCT6, HCT10, and HCT12 were highly consistent with caffeoylquinic acid, and the quantitative relationship was positively correlated. This result is highly similar to those reported in previous studies on drought stress in wheat [10]. PAL is a key initial enzyme in the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, and its activity is closely related to the synthesis of phenolic and flavonoid compounds [102]. CHS is the first rate-limiting enzyme in the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway, which influences flavonoid accumulation in plants [103]. Chalcone, the fundamental backbone for various flavonoids, is synthesized upstream of this pathway. Therefore, the CHS-catalyzed reaction is a crucial rate-limiting step in the overall flavonoid synthesis [104]. Studies on Ginkgo biloba and Fagopyrum dibotrys have shown that flavonoid content correlates with CHS gene expression levels [105, 106]. In drought-treated C. lancifolius seedlings, two PAL and four CHS genes were identified, all of which showed downregulation, and a decrease in luteolin content was also observed. Previous studies have also shown that drought stress induces a decrease in luteolin content in globe artichokes [107]. Therefore, C. lancifolius seedlings respond to drought stress by inducing the expression of certain differential genes and metabolites.

Conclusions

In summary, this study used a combined analysis of photosynthetic characteristics, physiological and biochemical indicators, transcriptomics, and metabolomics to reveal for the first time the molecular mechanisms underlying the response of C. lancifolius seedlings to drought stress. Drought stress inhibited the growth of C. lancifolius seedlings and reduced their photosynthetic characteristics, including Tr, PN, Ci, Gs, LCP, and CE. It affects various physiological and biochemical indicators, such as a decrease in RWC, leaf biomass, and chlorophyll content, an increase in electrolyte permeability, MDA content, and H2O2 content. It eliminates ROS by activating antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, POD, and CAT, and Maintains osmotic balance by accumulating proline. Transcriptome and metabolome analyses identified 19,847 DEGs and 549 DEMs, which were significantly enriched in pathways such as starch and sucrose metabolism, plant hormone signal transduction, and Plant-pathogen interaction. Transcriptome-metabolome joint analysis revealed that starch and sucrose metabolism, glutathione metabolism, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and flavonoid biosynthesis play important roles in responding to drought stress in C. lancifolius seedlings. HCT, PAL, and CHS regulate the synthesis of luteolin and caffeoylquinic acid. This study reveals critical molecular regulatory network underlying drought tolerance in C. lancifolius seedlings, which are tightly linked to drought stress intensity. Furthermore, these findings offer valuable genetic resources for drought-resistant breeding in C. lancifolius and other medicinal plants.
