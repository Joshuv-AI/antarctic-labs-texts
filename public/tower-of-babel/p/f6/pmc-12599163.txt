Discriminating childhood traumatic experiences by molecular profiles: the case of neglect and sexual abuse
Authors: Chiara Pesca, Luisa Lo Iacono, Silvia Bussone, Sergio Comincini, Cristina Trentini, Valeria Carola
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Child maltreatment (CM) covers various forms of physical, emotional, and sexual abuse and neglect. Although the scientific literature has extensively documented that exposure to sexual abuse and/or neglect during childhood can cause long-term harm to an individual's overall well-being, the psycho-biological impact of these specific forms of CM requires further exploration. This pilot study tested the hypothesis that experiencing childhood neglect and sexual abuse are associated with psychological alterations as well as biological alterations, namely blood gene expression changes.Methods: This study encompasses a group of volunteer university students, who completed a battery of questionnaires to evaluate the presence of neglect and sexual abuse experience during childhood (CTQ-SF) and psychological distress (SCL-90-R; BDI-II). Both subsets were compared with control groups. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells were collected from all groups to extract RNA and perform genome wide expression analyses.Results: Neglected and sexually abused individuals showed evidence of biological alterations. Through a genome wide transcriptomic analysis, combined with multivariate nomogram analysis, we identified two groups of 5 genes, the changes in expression of each group identified a subject who experienced either neglect or sexual abuse, with a probability of 95%. Among the first group of genes, the expression of ARMC1 correlated significantly with depressive scores in neglected individuals. Among the second group of genes, the expression of ABTB1 correlated significantly with general psychological distress in sexually abused individuals.Conclusion: These results support that childhood neglect and sexual abuse are associated with gene expression changes and psychological outcomes, underscoring the importance of refining the diagnostic process with more objective screening and assessment tools.

Introduction

Child maltreatment (CM) refers to a broad spectrum of adverse experiences, including physical, emotional, and sexual abuse, as well as physical and emotional neglect, that occur before the age of 18 (WHO, 2022). A substantial body of contemporary developmental and clinical research consistently highlighted the complexity of CM experiences and their cumulative impact on developmental outcomes (Barnett et al., 1993; Cicchetti & Toth, 2005). Felitti et al. (1998), together with subsequent research on complex trauma (Briere & Spinazzola, 2005; van der Kolk, 2005), demonstrated that CM constitutes a pervasive risk factor for maladaptive trajectories across physical, cognitive, socioemotional, and psychological domains.

Although many studies distinguish between neglect, sexual abuse, and other forms of CM, a critical challenge in this field is the classification of CM subtypes. This difficulty arises from the fact that such experiences often co-occur, complicating the attribution of specific outcomes to a single category (Barnett et al., 1993; Manly et al., 2001). Despite these challenges, examining specific subtypes remains essential to better understand both shared and unique developmental consequences (Cicchetti & Valentino, 2006).

Child sexual abuse involves coercive sexual acts imposed on a child (Putnam, 2003; Trickett & Putnam, 1993) and is consistently associated with profound effects on attachment, self-concept, interpersonal functioning, and heightened risk for a range of psychopathologies, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and dissociative symptoms (Parker & Nemeroff, 2021; a review of the literature: Boumpa et al., 2024; Browne & Finkelhor, 1986). Conversely, neglect predominantly involves omissions in the caregiver's duty to meet the child's basic needs (Dubowitz et al., 2005; Hildyard & Wolfe, 2002). Neglect has been associated with disruptions in cognitive and language development, deficits in socioemotional functioning, and long-term impairments in stress regulation (Simon et al., 2024; Spratt et al., 2012).

Evidence indicates that both forms of CM significantly disrupt normative developmental processes critical to socioemotional, relational, cognitive, and psychological functioning (Blanchflower & Bryson, 2024; Parker & Nemeroff, 2021). At the psychological level, CM has been strongly linked to internalising and externalising symptoms, conversion disorders, eating disorders, and an increased risk for substance use, suicidal ideation, and reduced life opportunities in adulthood (Currie & Widom, 2010; Hailes et al., 2019). Although some developmental consequences are subtype-specific – for instance, sexually transmitted infections in cases of sexual abuse (Hammerschlag, 2011) or impaired physical growth in cases of neglect (Flaherty et al., 2006) – a substantial body of research documents cross-cutting alterations across physiological and psychological systems. These include dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis (Reilly & Gunnar, 2019; Schalinski et al., 2015), compromised immune function (D'Elia et al., 2018), and epigenetic changes such as altered DNA methylation and accelerated cellular aging (Lang et al., 2020; Warner et al., 2020). Moreover, recent literature indicates that different forms of CM can lead to alterations in gene expression profiles in peripheral blood, including lymphocytes. For instance, Apsley et al. (2023) reported significant changes in monocyte gene expression in individuals with a history of CM, providing evidence for a direct molecular impact of early adverse experiences on the peripheral immune system.

However, despite these advances, current research on peripheral biomarkers in the context of CM still faces substantial limitations. To date, no molecular indicators have been validated for reliable use in clinical practice to objectively signal long-term risk. In other words, while the study of peripheral correlates of CM exposure has considerably expanded in recent years, currently available biomarkers remain at a putative and exploratory stage and have not yet achieved the specificity, reliability, and reproducibility required for diagnostic or prognostic applications.

Building on the existing literature, the general objective of this study was to investigate the long-term biological consequences of CM and to explore potential molecular markers associated with its psychopathological outcomes. Specifically, our aim was to determine whether different CM subtypes – namely childhood neglect and sexual abuse – might differentially impact physiological functions, leading to distinct alterations in gene expression profiles in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (AIM 1).

Once these biological alterations were identified, a further objective was to examine whether certain gene expression changes might be associated with specific psychological outcomes, tentatively considered as candidate markers of psychopathology (AIM 2). In other words, we aimed to assess whether these alterations may act as preliminary, subtype-sensitive signals, potentially contributing to a more precise psychobiological characterisation. This approach also allows us to begin cautiously exploring the possible utility of such molecular correlates in clinical assessment, while acknowledging that their relevance for understanding the relationship between CM subtypes and long-term mental health outcomes remains to be validated.

To achieve these aims, we profiled PBMC transcriptomic patterns in young adults with histories of physical and/or emotional neglect or sexual abuse during childhood, as well as in healthy controls. By analysing these data using multivariate and integrative approaches, we sought to identify candidate peripheral biomarkers capable of discriminating between specific trauma experiences and quantifying associated risks for developing psychopathology.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

This sample included a group of 219 volunteers University students (44 men and 175 women; mean age ± SD = 24 ± 3.9 years). The inclusion criteria were being between 18 and 28 years old and native Italian speakers. Exclusion criteria were the presence of major disorders of the central nervous system (e.g. epilepsy), self-reported history of major psychiatric disorders, and any condition affecting the ability to participate, including denial of informed consent.

First, the participants were asked to fill out the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form (CTQ-SF; Bernstein et al., 2003) to assess the presence of childhood neglect and/or abuse. In accordance with the scores obtained on the various scales comprising the CTQ-SF, we classified individuals as follows: subjects with abuse (who exceeded the cut-off for one or more scales of emotional, physical, or sexual abuse), subjects with neglect (who exceeded the cut-off on one or both scales of emotional or physical neglect), subjects with complex trauma (who exceeded the cut-off in one or more abuse scales as well as in one or more neglect scales), and control subjects. Then, for our study, three subgroups were selected among the 219 subjects, consisting of 7 individuals with Emotional and/or Physical Neglect (3 men and 4 women; mean age ± SD = 21.7 ± 2.1 years), 10 individuals with Sexual Abuse (3 men and 7 women; mean age ± SD = 25.6 ± 2.75 years) and 10 control subjects (10 women; mean age ± SD = 24.3 ± 2.6 years).

In these three groups the psychological symptoms and psychological distress were measured by the Symptom Check-List-90 item revised (SCL-90-R; Derogatis, 1994), and the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II; Beck et al., 1996; Supplementary Table 1). Following the psychological assessment, these individuals underwent a blood draw for subsequent biological analysis. Personnel responsible for collecting biological and psychometric data, assigned a numeric code to each individual. Such collected psychometric and biological data were then provided anonymously to the researchers performing statistics.

The experimental procedure occurred in the Department of Dynamic and Clinical Psychology, and Health Studies (‘Sapienza’, University of Rome) during the 2019–2021 period. Each participant gave written informed consent before participation after receiving a complete description of the study and having an opportunity to ask questions. This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Department of Dynamic and Clinical Psychology, Sapienza, University of Rome (Prot. n. 0000453 and Prot. n. 0000112).

Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form

The Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form (CTQ-SF; Bernstein et al., 2003) is a self-reported questionnaire consisting of 28 items on traumatic events experienced in the first 15 years of life. Specifically, the test comprises an overall score that reflects the severity of the maltreatment that was suffered and five subscales that assess the presence of Emotional Abuse, Physical Abuse, Sexual Abuse, Emotional Neglect, and Physical Neglect.

In detail, the Emotional Neglect subscale investigates the caregiver's failure to satisfy the basic psychological needs of the child. Instead, the Physical Neglect subscale assesses the caregiver's failure to meet the child's basic physical needs.

All 28 items are constructed as statements that begin with the phrase ‘When I was growing up … ’ and evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale, from ‘never true’ = 1 to ‘very often true’ = 5. For each dimension, the score varies from 5 (absence of traumatic events) to 25 (history of severe maltreatment). All scales provide a single cutoff score that identifies levels of severity that belong to each CM dimension (absence of CM, low CM, moderate CM, severe CM). For the Emotional Neglect Scale, a score greater than or equal to 15 is considered positive. For the Physical Neglect Scale, a score greater than or equal to 10 is considered positive. For the Sexual Abuse Scale, a score greater than or equal to 8 is considered positive. In this study, the sample was divided into healthy controls and neglected and sexually abused individuals. Those who reported a positive score on the emotional or physical neglect scale were classified as neglected. Those who reported a positive score on the scale of sexual abuse were classified as sexually abused.

The CTQ-SF has excellent psychometric qualities, characterised by good reliability and validity (Bernstein et al., 2003). In the present study, the Italian CTQ–SF was administered, translated, and validated by Sacchi et al. (2018).

Symptom Check-List-90 items-Revised (SCL-90-R)

The Symptom Check-List-90 items-Revised (SCL-90-R; Derogatis, 1994) is a self-report questionnaire that evaluates psychopathological symptoms and psychological distress in adult and adolescent clinical and nonclinical subjects.

It consists of 90 items, which are scored on a 5-point Likert scale from ‘0 = Not at all’ to ‘4 = Very much’, and asks participants to report if, during the last seven days, they have suffered from symptoms of Somatization (SOM: perception of dysfunctions within one's body, e.g. headaches); Obsessive-Compulsivity (O–C: characteristic symptoms of obsessive-compulsive syndrome, e.g. having to check and double-check what you do); Interpersonal Sensitivity (I-S: feelings of inadequacy and inferiority with respect to comparison with other people, e.g. feeling that people are unfriendly or dislike you); Depression (DEP: clinical manifestations of depressive syndrome); Anxiety (ANX: general signs of anxiety, e.g. feeling fearful without an apparent reason); Hostility (HOS: characteristic thoughts, feelings and behaviours of anger, e.g. having urges to beat, injure, or harm someone); Phobic Anxiety (PHOB: fear reaction to a person, place or specific situation perceived as irrational by the subject and disproportionate to the stimulus, e.g. feeling afraid to go out of your house alone); Paranoid Ideation (PAR: characteristic manifestations of paranoid thinking, e.g. the idea that you should be punished for your sins); Psychoticism (PSY or: withdrawal, isolation and other typical symptoms of schizophrenia, e.g. having thoughts that are not your own).

In addition to the nine scales described above, the questionnaire provides a Global Severity Index (GSI) scale, a global indicator of the intensity of discomfort used to determine the severity of psychological distress. For the present study, the Italian-validated version of the SCL-90-R was employed (Prunas et al., 2012).

Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II)

The Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II; Beck et al., 1996) is a self-report questionnaire developed to assess the presence, intensity, and severity of depressive symptomatology in adolescent and adult people. Respondents are asked to select the most appropriate statement to describe their mood in the last 2 weeks on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3. BDI-II provides a total score obtained by adding the scores for the 21 items and two domain scores (the Somatic-Affective domain and the Cognitive domain). Scores between 0 and 13 are not considered clinically significant levels of depression; scores between 14 and 18 show mild to moderate levels of depression; moderate-severe depression is indicated by scores from 19 to 29; and higher scores from 30 to 60 are indicative of greater severity of depressive symptoms. This study employed the BDI-II Italian-validated version by Montano and Flebus (2006).

Blood composition analysis, PBMC isolation and RNA extraction

Blood (6 ml) was drawn from 27 participants and collected in two EDTA vacutainers (BD, Toronto, Canada) at the end of the clinical assessment. Within two hours from collection, one vial was used for total and differential blood cells count and complete haematocrit analysis (i.e. Neutrophils, Eosinophils, Basophils, Monocytes, and Lymphocytes percentages; Haemoglobin, White Cells, and Platelets concentrations), while from the second vial PBMCs were isolated by density gradient over Ficoll-Hypaque (GE Healthcare BioSciences, Little Chalfont, United Kingdom), and RNA was purified using Total RNA purification Kit (Norgen Biotek, Thorold, Canada). RNA quantity was determined using a NanoDrop UV–VIS spectro-photometer by evaluating the absorbance at 260 and 280 nm, respectively.

mRNA library preparation, sequencing, and data analysis

RNA seq (mRNA 3′DGE) was performed at NGD technology service (Pozzuoli) on PBMC samples of Neglected (n = 7), Sexually abused (n = 10) and Control (n = 10) groups. Total RNA from individual samples was quantified using the Qubit 2.0 fluorimetric Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quality tested by Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer RNA assay (Agilent technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Only RNA samples with RIN value > 7.0 were included in the study. Libraries were prepared from 100ng of total RNA using the QuantSeq 3′ mRNA-Seq Library Prep Kit FWD for Illumina (Lexogen GmbH). Quality of libraries was assessed by using screen tape High sensitivity DNA D1000 (Agilent Technologies). Libraries were sequenced in a single-end 100 bp mode on a NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system using an S1, 100 cycles flow cell (Illumina Inc.). Illumina novaSeq base call (BCL) files are converted in fastq file through bcl2fastq (version v2.20.0.422). Sequence reads were trimmed using bbduk software (bbmap suite 37.31) to remove adapter sequences, poly-A tails, and low-quality end bases (regions with average quality below 6). Alignment was performed with STAR 2.6.0a (Dobin et al., 2013). Sequence reads were mapped against the most recent version of the mouse reference genome (GRCm38/mm10) from UCSC, using RefSeq as the gene structure and annotation database. The expression levels of genes were determined with htseq-count 0.9.1 (Anders et al., 2015) by using Ensembl genes annotations (Version 6.0). Individual sample counts were normalised via Relative Log Expression (RLE) using DESeq2 R library (Love et al., 2014). Principal Component Analysis and differential expression analysis were performed with the DeSeq2 software comparing in parallel Neglected (n = 7) versus Control (n = 10) and Sexually abused (n = 10) versus Control individuals.

Bioinformatics gene expression analysis

The Orange data mining software (Demsar et al., 2013) was fed with data regarding gender, age, differential blood cell count, and individual normalised read count values of gene expression from RNA-Seq. The last data were previously filtered to exclude unknown (predicted) genes and genes with very low levels of expression (avg reads count less than 2). The software was used to compare these values toward the different categories of neglect and control samples in one case, and of Sexually abused and control samples in the second analysis.

Two supervised Nomograms analyses were performed by means of a Naive Bayes learner/classifier, identifying 5 of the best rated gene expression profiles with positive influence on neglect and control categories and on sexual abuse and control categories. A confidence interval of 95% was used to correlate gene expression values to neglect versus control samples, to sexual abuse versus control categories.

Statistics

The data were checked for normal distribution with the Shapiro–Wilk test. One-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were performed to evaluate the impact of Neglect and Sexual abuse on psychological symptoms. Pearson correlation analyses evaluated the correlation between ARMC1 and ABTB1 expression levels and psychological symptoms. Statistical analyses were carried out using Statistica, version 12.0 (StatSoft, Tulsa, OK, USA).

Results

Gene expression profiles differ in the PBMCs of individuals who were neglected and individuals who were sexually abused during childhood

Among the 219 subjects in the initial sample, 18/219 exhibited forms of abuse, 18/219 exhibited forms of neglect, 42/219 exhibited forms of both abuse and neglect, and 141/219 served as controls. To evaluate the biological impact of childhood experience of neglect and of sexual abuse (AIM 1), we selected subjects who only experienced physical and/or emotional neglect (n = 7) and subjects who experienced sexual abuse (n = 10). There was no overlap between the two groups. The transcriptional alterations that were induced by each type of child experience were analysed by genomewide Quant-Seq mRNA sequencing of RNA extracts from the PBMCs of the neglect and sexual abuse groups, and the gene expression profile of each group was compared to a group of control (n = 10). By principal component analysis (PCA; Supplementary Figure 1), which visualises individual distances in transcriptional profiles, overall transcriptional levels had a total variance of 37% and 32% in the neglect and sexual abuse analysis, respectively. We performed a differential expression analysis that resulted in 355 genes modified significantly (p < .01) by the neglect experience and 163 by the sexual abuse, versus control. The most significant genes (p < .05) from both analyses are shown in red toward the top of the volcano plots in Figure 1A–B. However, when the ‘False discovery rate’ statistical correction was applied to the database, only 35 coding genes survived for the neglect analysis while none remained for the sexual abuse as reported in Table 1. In a different approach, to select gene candidates whose expression profiles could discriminate between the experimental and control groups, gene expression measures (normalised mRNA-seq reads count) of controls and neglected participants, as well of controls and sexual abuse were subjected to multivariate bioinformatics analysis using the Orange Data Mining tool (https://orangedatamining.com/). Correlation of the profiles of the top genes with psychological variables in neglected individuals

Pearson correlation was then performed to analyse the association between psychological symptoms and gene expression levels identified in the multivariate bioinformatics analysis in neglected individuals (AIM 2). Since ARMC1 expression correlated positively only with the SCL-90-R Depression subscale (r = 0.62; Cohen's d = 1.58), we analysed the BDI-II scores, a specific tool used to investigate the presence of depression. Notably, ARMC1 expression also correlated positively with BDI-II scores (r = 0.62; Cohen's d = 1.58; Figure 3). The other psychological parameters did not correlate with ARMC1 expression. The expression profiles of the remaining genes were not significantly associated with psychological symptoms. Discussion

Our study supports the hypothesis that experiences of childhood neglect or sexual abuse are associated with significant psychological and biological alterations.

We first examined whether exposure to childhood neglect and sexual abuse was associated with changes in PBMC mRNA expression in early adulthood using a genome-wide approach. Previous work has shown that traumatic childhood experiences can induce long-term transcriptional changes in peripheral blood cells, both in preclinical and clinical contexts (Lo Iacono et al., 2015, 2017, 2018, 2020). Recent literature further indicates that different forms of CM can specifically alter gene expression profiles in lymphocytes, providing a direct molecular link between early adversity and peripheral immune system function (Apsley et al., 2023; Heim et al., 2019; Kumsta et al., 2017). Moreover, neglect and sexual abuse have been shown to exert differential effects on neurodevelopment and immune functioning, with neglect particularly impacting socioemotional and cognitive systems and sexual abuse more strongly associated with neuroendocrine dysregulation and psychopathology (Dannlowski et al., 2012; McCrory et al., 2012; Teicher et al., 2016).

Out of the 219 recruited participants, only 7 experienced exclusively emotional or physical neglect, and 10 experienced sexual abuse. These limited numbers highlight the challenges of studying specific trauma subtypes in humans, given the frequent co-occurrence of multiple adversities. Furthermore, PCA analysis revealed heterogeneity within the sexual abuse group, preventing discrimination between this group and controls and explaining the lack of differentially expressed genes in standard analyses.

To identify gene expression signatures associated with neglect or sexual abuse, all PBMC transcriptomic data were subjected to multivariate analysis combined with blood composition analysis. This novel approach identified five candidate genes for the neglect group (AGPS, AOAH, APLP2, ARMC1, CD300LF) and five for the sexual abuse group (ABTB1, ACSS1, ANKRD11, ANKRD46, APBB1). These genes collectively discriminated participants exposed to neglect or sexual abuse from controls with at least 95% probability. Notably, none of these genes met the stringent criteria for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with adjusted p-values <.05, although all were significant at p <.05, suggesting that multivariate approaches may enhance the identification of putative phenotype-associated markers compared to standard whole-transcriptome analyses. Similar multivariate strategies have been successfully applied to assess gene and protein expression profiles in tumour progression (Comincini et al., 2007; Rognoni et al., 2010) and in identifying autophagy-related gene and miRNA signatures in paediatric coeliac disease (Comincini et al., 2017).

For the neglect group, most candidate genes are linked to inflammatory functions and have been associated with depressive disorders. These findings align with studies demonstrating that inflammation-related gene expression mediates the effects of CM on long-term mental health (Miller & Cole, 2012; Nusslock & Miller, 2016). AGPS is involved in chronic inflammatory processes and is upregulated in post-mortem brain tissue from MDD patients (Bozelli et al., 2021; Chandley et al., 2022). AOAH regulates CRF release and has been implicated in depressive behaviours (Aguiniga et al., 2020). APLP2, a member of the amyloid precursor protein family, has been proposed as a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker distinguishing Alzheimer-type dementia from depression (Post et al., 2006). CD300LF modulates brain immune responses and has been linked to MDD and age-related processes (Bennett et al., 2016; Evans et al., 2023; Lago et al., 2020). ARMC1, a mitochondrial gene, influences mitochondrial dynamics and has been associated with depressive symptoms in GWAS studies (Luciano et al., 2012; Wagner et al., 2019).

For the sexual abuse group, most identified genes are related to aging and cognitive function. Three genes (ABTB1, ANKRD11, ANKRD46) share ankyrin-repeat domains involved in protein interactions. ABTB1 has been implicated in aging and cognitive performance (de Lucia et al., 2020), while ANKRD11 and ANKRD46 have been associated with cognitive impairment and depression, respectively (Shyn et al., 2011; Sirmaci et al., 2011). These results are consistent with literature demonstrating that sexual abuse specifically affects neurocognitive development and socioemotional processing, potentially via transcriptional and epigenetic alterations (Parker & Nemeroff, 2021; Putnam, 2003; Trickett & Putnam, 1993).

Considering that participants were young adults without formal psychiatric diagnoses, these transcripts likely do not indicate overt inflammatory or psychological pathology but may reflect a prodromal state of general psychological distress. This profile could serve as a preclinical signal for potential development of psychopathology associated with neglect or sexual abuse. Correlations between ARMC1 and depressive symptoms in the neglect group, and between ABTB1 and multiple SCL-90-R subscales, support the hypothesis that these peripheral biomarkers reflect subtype-specific psychological vulnerabilities.

Overall, our findings provide preliminary evidence that PBMC gene expression profiles, when integrated with clinical and psychosocial evaluations, may represent a complementary research tool for elucidating individual histories of childhood neglect and sexual abuse, with potential translational implications. This is especially relevant in clinical settings, where current assessments primarily rely on interviews and self-report questionnaires. The identification of peripheral biomarkers could improve the objectivity and accuracy of exposure assessment, inform early detection of individuals at risk, and ultimately guide the development of targeted preventive or therapeutic interventions. Nevertheless, these results must be considered exploratory and cannot yet be regarded as diagnostic or clinically applicable.

This study has several limitations. Although the groups were homogeneous in terms of psychological and demographic variables, they were small, which, due to methodological reasons, might have limited the identification of associations between biological and psychological parameters. In particular, the sample sizes of the neglect and sexual abuse subgroups were very limited. Therefore, the findings reported here should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. It is also important to note that the small sample size, while reducing statistical power, reflects the intrinsic difficulty of recruiting individuals with isolated trauma subtypes, given the frequent co-occurrence of multiple adversities.

We also recognise that the control group, which consisted of 10 female participants, introduces potential concerns regarding both statistical validity and sex-related biases. This limitation further underscores the preliminary nature of our findings and the need for replication in larger and more balanced samples.

Moreover, the study did not include an independent replication cohort, which further limits the generalisability of the findings. Additionally, sex-specific analyses were not feasible due to the small and unbalanced distribution of males and females across groups. This represents an important limitation, as sex-related differences may influence both biological and psychological outcomes of CM.

It should also be highlighted that, unlike preclinical models in which different types of trauma can be more easily categorised and controlled, in humans such categorisation is inherently challenging due to the frequent overlap of adverse experiences. Nevertheless, our findings support the notion that different subtypes of childhood maltreatment may have distinct long-term biological consequences. In this sense, although categorising individual types of CM in humans is difficult, the present study represents a first attempt to do so and provides preliminary evidence of biologically distinct outcomes associated with childhood neglect versus sexual abuse. Yet, these results remain tentative and may not at this stage be considered as providing validated biomarkers or diagnostic tools.

Future research should build upon the present findings in several complementary directions. First, replication in larger and independent cohorts is essential to validate the candidate peripheral biomarkers identified and to increase the statistical power and generalisability of the results. Longitudinal studies are also warranted to follow participants over time and assess the stability of gene expression profiles as well as their predictive value for the later emergence of psychological and psychopathological outcomes.

Second, preclinical modelling should be integrated to elucidate causal mechanisms linking CM subtypes to specific transcriptional changes. Future studies could evaluate the functional implications of these genes in depression and stress-induced behavioural alterations using pharmacological and molecular approaches in established preclinical models. Such approaches would allow for causal testing of the effects of specific genes on psychological and physiological outcomes and could inform the development of targeted interventions.

Finally, the genes identified through the multivariate approach (e.g. ABTB1, ACSS1, AGPS, ANKRD11, ANKRD46, APBB1, AOAH, APLP2, ARMC1, and CD300LF) should be further examined to determine their associations with other psychological, physiological, and biological phenotypes commonly observed in cases of childhood neglect and sexual abuse, which were not addressed in the present study. Overall, combining replication, longitudinal follow-up, and preclinical modelling represents a critical pathway toward establishing robust, clinically relevant molecular markers of CM.
