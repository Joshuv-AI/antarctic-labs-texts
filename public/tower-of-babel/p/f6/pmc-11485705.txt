Interference from anti-drug antibodies on the quantification of insulin: a comparison of an LC-MS/MS assay and immunoassays
Authors: Georgina Marta Varga, Manca Spendal, Jens Sigh, Tue Søeborg, Nikoline Juul Nielsen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim: This study aims to compare the anti-drug antibody (ADA) interference in four pharmacokinetic (PK) assays across different platforms (AlphaLISA, Gyrolab, LC-MS/MS) and to devise a strategy for ADA interference mitigation to improve the accuracy of measured drug in total PK assays.Materials & methods: Spiked test samples, created to achieve different ADA concentrations in human serum also containing an insulin analogue, were analyzed alongside pooled clinical samples using four assays.Results & conclusion: Interference was observed in all platforms. A novel approach using the Gyrolab mixing CD, including acid dissociation in the PK assay, significantly reduced interference and thereby improved relative error from >99% to ≤20% yielding measurements well within the acceptance criteria. Clinical sample results reinforced findings from the test samples.

Introduction

Insulin is probably one of the most important and best-studied peptide hormones in the field of biochemistry. By altering the amino acid sequence of human insulin, scientists have changed its pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles and subsequently developed insulin analogs with improved therapeutic efficacy [1,2]. Although the use of advanced purification and recombinant protein therapeutics significantly decreases the immune response in patients, it cannot eliminate the immunogenicity of the therapeutic insulin [3]. Human recombinant insulin and insulin analogs have been reported to cause anti-drug antibody (ADA) development [4]. Individuals typically develop polyclonal antibodies (pAb) which may have harmless effects or cause adverse effects by inhibiting the drug and/or by neutralizing endogenous proteins [5]. The development of these ADAs can also have negative effects during the sample analysis by interfering with detection, and thus quantification of the drug. In our study, the term ‘ADA interference’ is specifically used to describe a phenomenon that affects the accuracy of total drug detection during analytical quantification, with the bioavailability of the drug not being within the scope of this definition/paper. Ligand-binding assays, the most commonly used form of immunoassays, rely on the interaction between assay antibodies (Abs) and drugs. Therefore, any factor that hinders this antibody-drug binding can disrupt the method. For instance, an ADA binding to an antigen on an overlapping epitope with the assay Ab can cause an interfering effect on immunoassays. There are several ways to identify the presence of interfering ADAs during immunoassay-based sample analysis. These include encountering atypical PK profiles, observing non-parallelism in diluted samples, obtaining pre-dose positive results for competitive assays, or unsuccessful reanalysis due to ADA-drug immunocomplex dissociation during sample pre-treatment [6].

Several methods are available for insulin detection and quantification, and these can be divided into three main groups: immunoassays, chromatographic assays and electrochemical biosensor assays [7,8]. Immunoassays are a category of bioanalytical methods that are widely utilized in various fields of biomedical and pharmaceutical analysis, including the diagnosis and monitoring of various diseases. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA), radioimmunoassay (RIA), on-chip immunoassay, homogeneous time-resolved fluorescence assay (HTRF), luminescent oxygen channeling immunoassay (LOCI™, AlphaLISA) and Gyrolab® are among the various techniques encompassed by immunoassays. Peptides and smaller proteins may exhibit reduced selectivity in the immunoassays mentioned above due to a lack of Ab specificity, resulting in cross-reactivity between the assay and matrix proteins. It has been shown that chromatography and mass spectrometry-based assays could have an advantage over immunoassays, and these are often used as a complementary bioanalytical platform [9,10]. Therefore, LC-MS/MS as an alternative method, is often recommended. We are focusing on three platforms in the framework of this paper, AlphaLISA, Gyrolab and LC-MS/MS.

AlphaLISA® (LOCI™) assay is a luminescent oxygen channeling homogenous bead-based proximity assay [11,12]. The validated format has an incubation time of 1.5 h, requires no wash steps, is read in microplates, and uses acceptor and donor beads. The technique utilizes donor beads that, upon exposure to 680 nm laser, activate a cascade of reactions by exciting ambient oxygen into singlet state.

Automated immunoassay platforms incorporating microfluidic CD technology, such as Gyrolab™, offer a relevant alternative in the field of immunoassay platforms, combining microfluidic technology with fluorescence detection to process samples efficiently. The technique relies on a network of microchannels, comprising eight primary structures on a nanoliter scale [13]. The flow of liquid in the channels is regulated by capillary forces, hydrophobic barriers and the adjustment of revolution speed, which applies varying levels of centrifugal force to the entire CD, depending on the desired effect [14].

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) coupled to tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) is frequently used for insulin analysis due to its high selectivity, sensitivity and ability to chromatographically separate complex mixtures [8,9,15,16]. However, the presence of lipids, proteins and other biological molecules in blood and serum can cause matrix effects, which can negatively affect the accuracy and precision. Therefore, sample extraction procedures and purification methods are used to achieve improved selectivity and sensitivity of drug determination [17]. LC-MS/MS is the gold standard for bioanalysis of small molecules, but recent advances have made it a promising technique for also large peptides, such as insulins [18,19]. The presence of ADA interference is not suspected to cause issues for the accurate analysis of biotherapeutics by chromatography-based assays compared with antibody-based bioassays [20–22]. Furthermore, when the necessary high quality reagent antibodies are not available, LC-MS/MS method is a viable alternative. This study aims to compare three platforms for bioanalysis of serum samples containing an experimental long-lasting insulin analogue (LI1) and potential ADA interference and to suggest a mitigation strategy for an accurate PK measurement in total PK assays when ADAs are present.

Methods & materials

Preparation of negative control, test samples & clinical samples

To imitate the ADA interference, pAb were obtained from immunized guinea pigs (GP) from specimen A and B containing an estimated 5 and 2.3% LI2 specific immunoglobulin (IgG), respectively. LI2 is an insulin analog, sharing two out of three amino acid mutations (relative to human insulin) with the targeted analyte (from now on referred as drug or LI1, MW 6378.4 g/mol). Ten test samples (TS) (five from specimen A (TS-A) and five from specimen B (TS-B)) were created by spiking a fixed concentration (20 nM) of drug and 10.5–60.6 nM (1.6–9.1 μg/ml) of pAb into pooled mixed-gender human serum (BioIVT, from now on referred to as serum) to obtain an estimated ADA/drug ratio ranging from 0.5 to 3.0 based on molar concentrations. The negative control was prepared by spiking serum with the same concentration of drug as in TS without any addition of ADAs. The two tested samples from a previously conducted clinical trial (CS-I and CS-II) were prepared by pooling samples that contained ADAs based on previous radioimmunoassay analysis results. Calibration standards and quality control samples (QCs) were prepared by spiking drug stock solution (29860 nM) into serum and were stored at -20°C (Table 1).

Conjugation of antibodies

Monoclonal antibody (mAb), specific for the drug, was conjugated (biotinylated) with Biotin-X-NHS (Cat. no. B2643, Sigma), and was used both in AlphaLISA and Gyrolab assays. The detailed preparation procedure can be found in the supporting information of this paper. The mixture was purified using a NAP-5 columns (cat. no. 17-0853-01) according to the manufacturer's instructions (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) using PBSx1 buffer. The concentration of the two batches was measured by NanoDrop™ (Thermo Scientific) (0.327 and 0.331 mg/ml). Pooled solution of biotinylated mAb was stored in 50% glycerol at a final (stock) concentration of 904 nM at -20°C.

For conjugation of acceptor beads, the mAb specific for human insulin was directly coupled to the AlphaLISA acceptor beads (Cat. no. 6772001, Perkin Elmer) in a coupling ratio of 5:3 (6 mg antibodies per 10 mg beads). The detailed preparation procedure can be found in the supporting information of this paper. The suspension of conjugated acceptor beads in a final concentration of 5 mg/ml was then transferred to 1.5 ml Eppendorf Safe-Lock tubes (PCR clean, amber–light-protected) and was stored at 4°C.

For Gyrolab assays, labelling of mAb was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions, using Alexa Fluor-647 (Alexa Fluor™ 647 NHS Ester, cat. no. A37573, Thermo Scientific). The detailed preparation procedure can be found in the supporting information of this paper. The labelled detection mAb was diluted to 1000 nM in PBSx1 Tween buffer (0.010 M Phosphate, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.01% Tween-20, 0.2% BSA, 0.02% Sodium Azide) and stored protected from light at 4°C. Before every use, the detection mAb stock solution was centrifuged for 15 min at 4000 RPM to pellet possible aggregates.

AlphaLISA

The biotinylated Ab and conjugated acceptor beads were initially diluted in assay buffer (LOCI buffer) (25 mM HEPES, 50 mM NaCl, 12 mM Na-EDTA, 0.2% dextran, 0.5% BSA, 0.1% BGG, 0.02% HBR1, 0.1% Tween-20, 0.01% proclin 300, 0.01% gentamycin pH 7.4) and mixed (Ab mix). Batch calibrators, QCs and samples were diluted ten-times (MRD: minimum required dilution) in assay buffer, vortexed and transferred to the 96-well plate (Half Area White Flat Bottom Polystyrene Not Treated Microplate, Corning). Thirty microliters of the Ab mix was added to each well, gently mixed and incubated in the dark for 60 min at RT. Thereafter, 60 μl of the pre-diluted (75-times) donor bead solution was added, and the plate was sealed with a Perkin Elmer seal, incubated for 30 min in the dark at RT and was read on the EnVision™ 2103 Plate Reader (Perkin Elmer). Watson LIMS (Thermo Fischer Scientific) was used to fit the calibrators with a four-parameter logistic curve, weighted by the response (1/Y2) and was used to obtain back-calculated concentration of the samples. The QC standard samples were both added twice in the wells. Calibrators, QCs and samples were analyzed in duplicates.

Gyrolab

Two Gyrolab assays were used in this study, one utilizing Gyrolab® Bioaffy 1000 CD (later referred as Gyrolab 1 or G1) and Gyrolab® Mixing CD 96 (later referred as Gyrolab Mix or GM). The instrument used for all the experiments was a Gyrolab® xPand automated immunoassay platform from Gyros Protein Technologies AB. The Gyrolab instrument was controlled using Gyrolab Control software and the experiments were set up using the Gyrolab Manager software. Gyrolab Evaluator software, together with the add-on module Gyrolab Viewer was used for analysis of the data and evaluation of the column profiles (CP). Calibrators, QCs and samples were analyzed in duplicates.

Gyrolab 1

Alexa Fluor mAb was diluted with Rexxip™ F buffer (Gyros Protein Technologies AB) and biotinylated mAb was diluted in PBSx1 buffer to obtain the concentration of 15 nM and 0.1 mg/ml (666 nM), respectively. All calibrators, QCs and samples were diluted with the MRD of 10 in assay buffer (same as with AlphaLISA) and were together with Alexa Fluor mAb, biotinylated mAb and wash buffer PBSx1 Tween transferred to the plate (Skirted 96-well PCR Plate, cat. no. AB0800, Thermo Scientific). The plate was sealed with foil and centrifuged at 1000 rpm (2 min) to remove air bubbles, then transferred to the Gyrolab instrument and analyzed using Bioaffy 1000 CD. The instrument was operated with the method provided by the manufacturer (1000-3W-006-A) with a PMT (photo multiplier tube) 1% setting.

Gyrolab Mix

For reagent optimization, the acidification reagent used was acetic acid (0.6–1.5 M). To 200 nl of sample, an acid reagent (200 nl) was added, followed by the addition of the basic neutralizing reagent (200 nl). For the Mixing CD the assay antibodies were added to the sample in a mixture together with the neutralizing reagent (2 M Tris-HCl buffer, pH 8-9). The combinations in Table 2 were assessed during optimization (Figure 1). Dilution factors (DF) in terms of MRD: 3.3 and 10 in LOCI buffer were tested. For the evaluation of the reagents, calibrators and QCs were analyzed in duplicate, and the %RE, %CV, the capacity to remove the interference, the noise and the signal intensity were evaluated. The instrument was operated with a PMT 5% setting.

LC-MS/MS

Equipment

The chromatographic system used was a Shimadzu Nexera-X2 UHPLC. Autosampler temperature: 10°C, column: C18 XSelect Peptide column (2.1 × 50 mm, 2.5 μm, 130 Å) from Waters (Milford USA). Mobile phase: eluent A (95% water and 5% ACN) and eluent B (95% ACN and 5% water) with additional 1% formic acid in both solvents. A SCIEX Triple Quad™ 6500 mass spectrometer with IonDrive™ Turbo V ion source (AB Sciex, Canada) was used for detection. Analyst software (version 1.7.2.) was used to control the instruments and to acquire and process data.

Sample preparation

Serum samples (20 μl) were precipitated with MeOH 1:5 (v:v) containing 1.5 nM of insulin degludec (internal standard). The solution obtained was centrifuged at 6446 g for 15 min at 11°C. Prior to injection, the supernatant underwent an additional 1:1 dilution with water (v:v).

Chromatography

After sample preparation, 20 μl was injected into the LC-MS/MS system with a flow rate of 0.6 ml/min. The eluent B content of the gradient changed linearly between the following timepoints: 18% at 0 min, 27.3% at 0.2 min, 34.5% at 1.3 min, 95% from 1.4 min to 1.5 min and 18% from 1.6 min until 2.4 min. The column temperature was set to 60°C.

MS conditions

The detection was performed in positive ionization mode with 500°C source temperature, 35 psi source gas pressure, 5 kV ion spray voltage, 70 psi gas 1 pressure and 80 psi gas 2 pressure. The precursor and product ion (in Da) for the drug were 1276.5→1204.8 with declustering potential at 180 V, collision energy at 52 V and cell exit potential at 13 V. The precursor and product ion (in Da) for the internal standard were 1221.7→1366.7 with declustering potential at 140 V, collision energy at 40 V and cell exit potential at 16 V. The collision gas pressure was 12 psi, and the entrance potential was set to 10 V for both MRM transitions. The concentration of the drug was determined by calculating the peak area ratio of the drug to the internal standard, and then interpolating it with the calibration curve (weighted linear regression (1/x)). The calibration and QC standard samples were both injected once before and once after the samples.

Test sample acceptance criteria

The acceptance criteria (AC) for test samples were evaluated in terms of precision and accuracy. Precision was expressed using coefficient of variation (CV) and was assessed only for LBA. Accuracy was expressed using relative error (%RE = 100*(nominal value – measured value)/nominal value). The current guidelines for bioanalytical method validation specify that the %RE AC is set at 20% for precision and ± 20% for accuracy on LBA, ± 15% for accuracy on LC-MS/MS as outlined in Bioanalytical Method Validation and Study Sample Analysis M10, ICH Harmonised Guideline, May 2022.

Validation of PK assays

Fit-for-purpose validation of the assays (Gyrolab 1, LC-MS/MS, Gyrolab Mix) was carried out according to the Bioanalytical Method Validation and Study Sample Analysis M10, ICH Harmonised Guideline, May 2022 regarding precision (intra-run and inter-run precision), accuracy (intra-run and inter-run accuracy), dilutional linearity and carry-over (only applicable for LC-MS/MS). AlphaLISA was previously fully validated within the department according to FDA and EMA [FDA 2001, EMA 2012] guideline recommendation, thus only validation parameters assessed in the other three assays are presented in this paper for comparison.

The qualification results show that all three methods developed are both precise and accurate, with data well within the validation AC. The Gyrolab 1, Gyrolab Mix and AlphaLISA assays exhibited very comparable CVs in terms of inter-assay precision.

AlphaLISA

To compare the different platforms in terms of their ADA tolerance, various concentrations of GP pAb from two specimens (A and B) with an expected affinity toward the insulin analogue were spiked to samples containing a fixed amount (20 nM) of drug (test samples: TS-A and TS-B). Samples were first analyzed with the reference method AlphaLISA to show that the desired level of interference was reached Figure 2. shows that a decrease in accuracy was achieved (i.e., accuracy outside the TS acceptance criteria, ± 20% RE) and that the degree of interference is dependent on the spiked amount of pAb. Despite biological variations among the selected GP pAb, it was possible to create a range of samples (5 test samples from pAb A and 5 test samples from pAb B were prepared, TS-A with estimated 0.5, 0.9, 1.2, 1.5 and 1.9 ADA/drug and TS-B with estimated 1.2, 1.7, 2.1, 2.6, 3 ADA/drug) that showed a very similar dependency between %RE and ADA/drug ratio, that resulted in the overlap of both curves (Figure 2).

AlphaLISA was set up as a homogenous sandwich assay, meaning that the presence of ADAs can prevent the formation of the sandwich complex because the ADAs may bind to the same epitope as one of the assay antibodies, or it may cause steric hindrance that prevents the assay Ab from accessing the drug epitopes. This can lead to an underestimation of the drug concentration as shown in Figure 2, where the 1.2 ADA/drug ratio diminished the accuracy to above 55.0% RE and the 3.0 ADA/drug ratio diminished the accuracy above 99.0% RE, which was below the assay's quantification limit.

Gyrolab 1

Test samples analyzed with the Gyrolab 1 assay, as depicted in Figure 2, demonstrated that ADA interference was not eliminated in any of the samples as the recoveries failed to meet the TS acceptance criteria (± 20% RE). In a heterogeneous sandwich assay, like the Gyrolab 1, ADAs can also interfere with sandwich complex formation leading to an underestimation of the drug concentration. However, if the ADA-drug complex gets captured by the assay reagents (capture antibodies) and immobilized on a solid phase, the subsequent wash step may disrupt the weaker ADA-drug interactions of low affinity ADAs and remove the interference. Thus, the expectation was that the Gyrolab 1 assay would lower these interferences. Nevertheless, the unsuccessful mitigation of interference could be attributed to several factors: due to unavailable epitopes the drug can be eluted instead of being captured or ADAs may bind to the drug with high avidity, suggesting that the wash step is insufficient in eliminating this interference. As the assay Ab is monoclonal, an optimized wash step could be developed that exploit solely the polyclonal nature of the ADAs that bind the drug on several epitopes with varied affinity. Such optimization of the assay protocol could include utilization of multiple wash steps and modification of wash buffer.

LC-MS/MS

Figure 2 shows that the LC-MS/MS, as described by Xu et al. [23], Bults et al. [24] and Wang et al. [25] is less susceptible to ADA interference compared with AlphaLISA and Gyrolab 1. It is often described that LC-MS based methods are insensitive to ADA interference [21–23]. However, the outcomes of the analysis of the TSs unambiguously demonstrate that this is not a universal truth. Persistent interference in the LC-MS/MS assay may result from co-precipitation of the ADA-drug complex with other matrix constituents during protein precipitation, however the hypothesis presented herein is speculative and was not further investigated in this paper.

The LC-MS/MS platform demonstrated distinct behavior for samples from specimens A and B (TS-A, TS-B) compared with the AlphaLISA and Gyrolab platforms (Figure 2). Testing of the TS using both the AlphaLISA and Gyrolab 1 assay resulted in overlapping of their respective %RE vs. ADA/drug ratio curves. However, analysis on LC-MS/MS revealed that TS-A and TS-B diverged from this pattern, producing two distinct curves. The accuracies for TS-A and TS-B samples obtained with the LC-MS/MS platform were 20–29% and 3.2–15% higher than the accuracies obtained by AlphaLISA or Gyrolab 1, respectively. The divergence can be attributed to the polyclonal nature of these ADAs, because of their unique affinities and avidities. Consequently, this results in varying rates of interference reduction across the sample groups, which reflects the individual biological variation. The results indicate that the antibodies from samples TS-B have a higher avidity than those from TS-A.

Gyrolab Mix

The Gyrolab Mixing CD, which was originally developed for the Gyrolab platform solely for ADA analysis, was utilized in a PK assay format for detection of the drug. The setup was used to perform acid dissociation assisted immunoassay, with acid dissociation integrated directly into the original assay format itself, eliminating the need for any additional sample pre-treatment steps. Since the antibody-antigen bond is pH-dependent, determining the optimal conditions for the assay reagents' is important [26]. While varying the volumes of reagents during acid dissociation is common practice, this is not feasible with the Gyrolab system's arrangement of the volume definition chambers in the instrument (defined at 200 nl). However, Gyrolab`s automated system makes it possible to test different reagents for acidification or subsequent neutralization without the labor-intensive and less precise manual work.

To investigate the pH dependence of ADA interference mitigation, four concentrations (0.6, 0.9, 1.2 and 1.5 M) of acetic acid and three pH levels (8.0, 8.5, 9.0) of tris-HCl buffer were tested on TS with the highest ADA/drug ratio from specimen A and B, respectively (TS-A: 1.9 and TS-B: 3.0 estimated ADA/drug ratio) (Supplementary Table S1). During acidification, the pH is only influenced by the sample and the acid itself. In contrast, the pH following neutralization depends on both the acidic mixture and the neutralizer. As a result, during the neutralization step, pH categories are determined by the average pH (e.g. 7.3 and 7.0 is displayed as average 7.1 pH, Table 2) (Figure 1). Similar observations were made when employing the same acetic conditions with different neutralizers (Figure 1A) or using similar neutralizing conditions (Figure 1B). Notably, a distinct trend was found, showing that accuracy improved as the acidity of the conditions increased. Nevertheless, the lowest pH levels resulted in measurements outside of the TS acceptance criteria, potentially introducing positive bias in the detected concentrations.

The signal-to-noise ratio displayed an inverse relationship with increased acidity, as also described by Mikulskis et al. [27]. This could be due to a pH variation induced conformational change in the protein structure of either the antibodies or the drug itself. These alterations can reduce the formation of the immunoassay sandwich, resulting in a diminished signal. Both acidification and neutralization pH play a crucial role in the removal of interference and subsequent sandwich-formation in the assay. Acidification is required to break the bond between the drug and the interfering ADAs, while the neutralization is necessary for forming the immunoassay sandwich.

The experiments imply that the optimal pH among the tested conditions for neutralization was ∼5.2, regardless of the acid dissociation pH (3.5–3.2), to achieve effective interference mitigation of TS. The use of 0.9 M acetic acid to acidify and a neutralizing reagent with a pH of 8.5 were chosen as optimal. Moreover, a tenfold MRD in LOCI buffer prior to analysis was shown to be favorable. Physiological pH (∼7) is typically preferred for neutralization [27,28], however in this study, the more acidic conditions were found to be more effective. Commonly, lower acid concentrations (0.04–0.30 M) and higher dilution factors (5–10) are employed [27,29,30]. However, due to the volume limitation of Gyrolab, permitting only to a 1:1 dilution with acid, samples exhibiting significant interference necessitated higher acid concentrations to meet the TS acceptance criteria.

To evaluate the impact of additional dilution on interference mitigation, the TS were subjected to three additional DF in serum: 10, 20 and 40. It was observed that the undiluted samples were not within the TS acceptance criteria for %RE, with a maximum value of 32.8% RE. However, samples up to 1.5 ADA/drug ratio showed acceptable results with %RE within 12.8. Our findings indicate that there was no significant improvement in accuracy beyond a tenfold dilution (ANOVA: two-factor with replication: F (2,30) = 3.32, p = 0.154). By the assay with the additional tenfold dilution in serum even the TS with the highest level of interference (up to 3.0 ADA/drug) were within the TS acceptance criteria. Nevertheless, a noticeable slight difference is still present between the TS-A and TS-B samples. There is a minor increase in the average CV percentages from undiluted (3.2% CV), trough tenfold (3.7% CV), 20-fold (4.5% CV) to a 40-fold (5.6% CV) diluted samples in serum. However, even the highest CV observed (13.2%) was well within the TS acceptance criteria. The Gyrolab instrument is a fully automated immunoassay platform that enables precise sample handling. The automated nature of the system minimizes the potential for human error and enables highly repeatable results, as demonstrated by the good precision experienced during validation. The developed PK assay utilizing the Gyrolab Mixing CD was able to reduce the interference from >99.0% RE on AlphaLISA assay to 15.9% RE for the highest 3.0 ADA/drug test sample.

The findings suggest that there may be room for improvement in the method's MRD (10), since an additional tenfold dilution in serum led to improved accuracy. However, this could mean that an optimal method would lose sensitivity, potentially by a factor of 10 (from 0.2 to 2 nM). On the other hand, if the additional dilution would decrease the noise more than the signal, then lower concentrations could still provide accurate and precise data. It is important to mention that the potential better combinations of acid/base reagents in terms of concentrations and pH or using different reagents for sample treatment could also lead to improvements. Drug concentration in the sample may be a big factor in relation to which samples suffer from interference, as lower drug levels are typically more susceptible to interference from ADAs. Consequently, inaccurate detection of the drug may result from some degree of interference, although the extent of interference may vary among different assays; measured drug levels may depend on the assay platform utilized, as illustrated by the results described in this paper. It is hypothesized that the interfering ADAs are denatured upon acid addition and therefore leave the drug free for immunoassay sandwich formation. The same approach could be easily integrated into an LC-MS/MS assay, after which we would expect similar results without the neutralizing step. Several papers have reported the use of acid pre-treatment as a means of dissociating the ADA-antigen bond in both LC-MS- and LBA-based assays [21,27,31]. Diverse alternative strategies for sample pre-treatment are available, including heat treatment, the use of solid-phase extraction (SPE) columns, or precipitation with salt or metal ions, depending on the specific characteristics of the drug and the sample matrix [17,32–34]. While the neutralization is often needed for LBA, for LC-MS assays the acid compatibility with the MS needs to be considered.

Clinical samples

The test samples were able to effectively mimic the interference effects, although they may not have been completely equivalent to real-life conditions. This is partly because GP antibodies differ from human antibodies, and because it is challenging to anticipate the specimen specific concentration or avidity of ADA interference that may arise in actual clinical samples (CS). Human serum samples from previously dosed type II diabetics were pooled into two groups (CS-I and CS-II) according to their levels of LI1-specific and cross-reacting anti-human insulin antibodies as measured by RIA (Figure 3). To simplify the comparison between assays, the data from clinical samples were normalized to the Gyrolab Mix method and therefore expressed as relative recoveries. This approach was based on the hypothesis derived from TS that the most accurate results would be obtained using the Gyrolab Mix assay. The CS were tested for parallelism by dilution in serum (DF: 2, 5, 10, 20, 40). Due to varying insulin concentrations in the CS, different DFs produced the results in the range of each assay. Both sample pools showed considerable interference on LC-MS/MS, Gyrolab 1, and AlphaLISA compared with the Gyrolab Mix method. Relative recoveries for the dilutions of CS-II were: LC-MS/MS: 82–94%, AlphaLISA: 65–90% and Gyrolab 1: 59–85%. For CS-I, the corresponding relative recoveries were: LC-MS/MS: 86–96%, AlphaLISA: 52–76% and Gyrolab 1: 55–68%. It was observed that the dilution had the most prominent improving effect for the Gyrolab 1 and AlphaLISA compared with the LC-MS/MS assay, which showed higher relative recoveries across the entire dilution range but showed no observable effect of dilution. The AlphaLISA assay showed an improvement in % relative recovery upon dilution for each level, with a maximum improvement of 24% for CS-I and 25% for CS-II. On the other hand, the Gyrolab 1 assay exhibited an even greater improvement in % recovery with increasing dilution, with a maximum improvement of 26% for CS-I and 13% for CS-II. Based on the ICH M10 recommendation for parallelism, the results obtained in this study would be considered acceptable, as the maximum CV was within 30%. However, the AlphaLISA and Gyrolab 1 methods produced results that deviated more than 30% from the hypothesized true value. These findings suggest that without knowledge of the presence of interference, there is a risk of misinterpreting the measured drug concentration. As predicted by the TS, the additional wash step did not provide an increased benefit for the Gyrolab 1 assay over the AlphaLISA. These results are consistent with the initial findings based on the TS. The results from both the clinical and the test samples demonstrate that the Gyrolab Mix method yielded the highest measured LI1 concentrations among the four platforms tested, while the dilution-corrected concentration remained stable with further dilution, in contrast to the results obtained by either the Gyrolab 1 or the AlphaLISA assay. This indicates that the results from the Gyrolab Mix method do not suffer from ADA interference. In general, the LI1 concentration levels measured by the LC-MS/MS assay fell between those detected by the Gyrolab Mix method, which were higher, and the AlphaLISA and Gyrolab 1 assays, which were lower. These findings confirm the original hypothesis that the Gyrolab Mix method delivers sufficiently accurate data.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the TS created using GP pAb accurately reflect the results observed in clinical samples, which were susceptible to ADA interference. These findings suggest that the Gyrolab 1 assay, compared with AlphaLISA assay, offer no improvement in accuracy, likely due to bound ADAs preventing drug binding to the capture antibodies or the additional wash step being unable to disrupt the drug-ADA bounds. While the LC-MS/MS assay shows better accuracy, the results are still unsatisfactory, indicating the need for further investigation into sample preparation methods for samples affected by ADAs. This study also highlights the novelty, that as of today no literature was found describing the utilization of the Gyrolab Mixing CD (developed for ADA assays) in a PK assay format. By diluting samples for Gyrolab Mix assay an additional ten-times in serum, we achieved interference elimination for up to 3 ADA/drug ratio. This indicates that the developed PK assay on Gyrolab Mixing CD is sufficient. Nevertheless, additional refinement has the potential to further optimize its performance, particularly with respect to the optimization of assay dilution factors (MRD), assay reagents and incubation time. The results of this study emphasize the importance of considering the potential presence of interfering ADAs when developing and validating quantitative bioanalytical assays. Therefore, it is recommended to carefully evaluate the potential for ADA interference and if required implement strategies to mitigate its effects to ensure reliable and accurate results. Additionally, due to the experienced ADA interference on the tested platforms it is suggested to explore alternative sample pre-treatments beyond acid dissociation, such as salt or heat treatment, and evaluate their advantages and disadvantages.
