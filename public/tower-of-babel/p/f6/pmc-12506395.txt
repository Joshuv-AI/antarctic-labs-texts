A LjBAK1-associated E3 ubiquitin ligase, LjPUB7, negatively regulates early symbiosis by targeting NFRs
Authors: Yong Feng, Zhongming Zou, Chao Liu, Dandan Zhang, Yanan Wang, Yi Ma, Yangrong Cao, Ping Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Nod factor receptors (NFRs) are essential for initiating symbiotic signaling in legumes, mediating rhizobial infection and nodule development. Tight regulation of NFR levels is crucial to prevent inappropriate immune responses and maintain cellular homeostasis. Co-expression of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 triggers cell death in Nicotiana benthamiana, which is specifically inhibited by LjBAK1-mediated ubiquitination and subsequent degradation, suggesting the existence of a LjBAK1-E3 ligase complex for NFR protein turnover. Further analysis identified LjPUB7, a plant U-box E3 ubiquitin ligase in Lotus japonicus, as a regulator of early symbiotic interactions. LjPUB7 interacts with both LjBAK1 and NFRs, and directly ubiquitinates NFRs. Loss-of-function Ljpub7 mutants display increased infection thread formation, enhanced nodule development, and elevated expression of early nodulation genes. These findings reveal that LjPUB7 negatively regulates early rhizobial infection by targeting NFR1 and NFR5 for ubiquitination and degradation, thereby providing insights into the fine-tuned control of symbiotic signaling in legumes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07265-4.

Introduction

Receptors are essential for orchestrating a wide range of physiological processes, particularly in response to specific ligands and environmental stimuli [1]. Pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), which recognize pathogen or microbe-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs/MAMPs) such as bacterial flagellin or fungal chitin, are key components of innate immune responses, effectively limiting pathogen growth [2]. However, the activation of these receptors can also negatively impact plant growth. Consequently, precise regulation of receptor protein levels is crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis and enabling host fitness benefits [3]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, the bacterial flagellin was primary recognized by the PRR FLAGELLIN-SENSING 2 (AtFLS2) [4]. Upon flagellin perception, AtFLS2 rapidly interacts with its co-receptor, AtBAK1 [5], leading to the direct phosphorylation of the Plant Ubox (PUB) E3 ubiquitin ligase PUB12 and PUB13. This phosphorylation event promotes the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of AtFLS2, effectively preventing excessive or prolonged activation of immune responses [6]. Beyond flagellin, other MAMPs can also be recognized by plasma membrane localized PRRs. For example, the lysin motif (LysM)-containing receptor-like kinase (LYKs) AtCERK1 (Chitin elicitor receptor kinase) and AtLYK5 mediate the perception of chitin, the second most abundant polysaccharide in nature, triggering plant immunity against fungal pathogens [7, 8].

In contrast to the immune-activating role of PRRs in Arabidopsis thaliana, the legume L. japonicus utilizes structurally similar receptors, Nod Factor Receptor 1 (LjNFR1) and Nod Factor Receptor 5 (LjNFR5), to recognize the rhizobial lipo-chitooligosaccharide Nod Factor (NF) [9]. This recognition initiates symbiotic signaling, a process that requires precise modulation of the host immune responses to enable the establishment of a beneficial interaction with nitrogen-fixing bacteria [3, 10]. Interestingly, co-overexpression of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves triggered hypersensitive cell death [11], suggesting a potential for these receptors to elicit immune responses. Similarly, co-overexpression of MtNFP (Nod Factor Perception, the Medicago truncatula ortholog of NFR5) and MtLYK3 in N. benthamiana resulted in defense-like responses, including defense gene expression, phenolic compound accumulation, and cell death [12]. These findings indicate a conserved role for plant LYKs in defense and highlight the versatility of NFRs in mediating both immune and symbiotic responses. Consequently, the abundance of NFRs must be tightly controlled to prevent elicitation of defense responses and facilitate rhizobial infection.

Previous studies identified the NFR-interacting RING-type E3 Ligase 1 (NIRE1) as a crucial role in NFR1/NFR5 homeostasis, optimizing rhizobial infection and nodule development [13]. Prior to rhizobial inoculation, NIRE1 associates with both NFR1 and NFR5, mediating their degradation via K48-linked polyubiquitination. However, upon rhizobial inoculation, NFR1 phosphorylates NIRE1 at a conserved residue, Tyr-109, leading to a functional switch. This switch enables NIRE1 to mediate K63-linked polyubiquitination, resulting in the stabilization of NFR1/NFR5 within infected root cells [13]. Several other E3 ubiquitin ligases have been identified as interactors of NFRs that are essential in the initial stages of the rhizobia-legume interaction [14]. In L. japonicus, LjPUB13 encodes a Plant U-box (PUB) E3 ubiquitin ligase, characterized by a U-box motif and a C-terminal ARMADILLO (ARM) repeat domain. Its expression is spatially regulated during symbiosis with Mesorhizobium loti. Ljpub13 mutant plants display delayed and reduced nodulation, accompanied by retarded growth. In vitro ubiquitination assays confirm that LjPUB13 is a functional E3 ligase, specifically interacting with and ubiquitinating NFR5, but not NFR1. These results suggest a mechanism in which LjPUB13 post-translationally regulates NFR5, potentially leading to its altered activity, or relocalization [15]. In Medicago truncatula, Plant U-box protein 1 (MtPUB1) was identified as an interactor of LYK3, the putative Medicago ortholog of NFR1. MtPUB1 co-localizes and interacts with LYK3 at the plasma membrane. MtPUB1 is expressed during symbiosis and its expression is induced by Nod factors (NFs). Overexpression of MtPUB1 resulted in decreased nodulation efficiency, while MtPUB1-RNAi plants exhibited increased nodulation efficiency in wild-type plants inoculated with Sinorhizobium meliloti strains carrying altered NF structures, suggesting that MtPUB1 negatively regulates symbiotic nodulation [16].

In this study, we found the co-receptor LjBAK1 can specifically inhibits the cell death response triggered by co-overexpression of LjNFR1/LjNFR5 in a ubiquitination-dependent manner. A plant U-box type E3 ligase, LjPUB7, was subsequently identified as the specific interactor of LjNFR1 in L. japonicus. Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis revealed the presence of LjPUB7 homologs in model plants, and exhibit high sequence similarity Medicago truncatula MtPUB1. LjPUB7 expression is predominantly localized in roots at the early stage of symbiosis, with a significantly upregulation inoculation with both compatible and non-adapted rhizobia but not pathogenic bacteria, suggesting a specific role in the legume-rhizobia symbiosis. LjPUB7 interacts with and ubiquitinate both NFR1 and NFR5, and negatively regulates symbiosis. These findings uncover a fine-tuned mechanism that a plant U-box type E3 ligase dynamically regulate NFRs protein levels.

Methods

Plant material and strains

Wild-type (WT) L. japonicus ‘Miyakojima MG-20’ and N. benthamiana seedings were kindly provided by Dr. Zhongming Zhang [17, 18] from the Huazhong Agricultural University in Wuhan, China. Homozygous Ljpub7 loss-of-function mutants used for phenotypic analysis were generated in this study. Mesorhizobium loti MAFF303099 was used for inoculation experiments. Agrobacterium rhizogenes strain LBA1334 was employed for hairy root transformation, and Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain EHA105 was used for transient protein expression in N. benthamiana leaves and for stable transformation of L. japonicus. All bacterial strains above were provided and maintained by Yangrong Cao [13]. The Escherichia coli used for in vitro ubiquitination assay were kindly provided by Prof. Dongping Lü [19] from the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, China.

Plasmid construction

In this study, all constructed plasmids were obtained by Gibson assembly [20]. To generate the constructs for yeast two-hybrid assays, the full-length LjPUB7 coding sequence (CDS) and the protein kinase domains of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 were cloned into BamHI/EcoRI sites of pGADT7 or pGBKT7 vectors. For bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays, full-length CDS of LjPUB7 was cloned into the SpeI/KpnI site of pSCYCE(R), which contains a HA-tag [21] to obtain PUB7::SCC fusions. The full-length cDNA of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 were cloned into the BamHI/XhoI site of pSCYNE, which contains a FLAG-tag [21] to obtain LjNFR1::SCN and LjNFR5::SCN. For CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, sgRNA primers were designed using CRISPR-P v2.0 [22] and commercially synthesized by GenScript. Vectors for hairy root transformation and stable transformation of L. japonicus were constructed according to the methods detailed [18]. For in vitro ubiquitination, the LjPUB7-CDS-(G4S)2-strep tag fusion fragment was amplified from the pEASY-Blunt-LjPUB7 vector. A point mutation (W318A) was introduced into LjPUB7-CDS using site-directed mutagenesis based on the pEASY-LjPUB7-Blunt vector. Both wild-type and mutant LjPUB7 constructs were then cloned into the pACYCDuet or pACYCDuet-AtUBC8 vectors using BamHI/NotI restriction sites. pCDFDuet-AtUBA1, pACYCDuet-AtUBC8, pCDFDuet-LjNFR1-CDS, pCDFDuet-LjNFR5-CDS, pCDFDuet-LjNFR1-CDS-MYC-AtUBA1, pCDFDuet-LjNFR5-CDS-MYC-AtUBA1, and pET28a-FLAG-AtUBQ10 were previously generated [13].

Transient expression and immunoblot analysis in N. benthamiana

Transient expression in N. benthamiana was performed as described in [23]. Briefly, N. benthamiana plants were cultivated in a growth chamber under controlled conditions (22 °C, 40–70% relative humidity, 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod) for approximately 4–6 weeks. Separate A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 cultures, each carrying a different construct, were combined and then mixed with an Agrobacterium culture harboring the P19 suppressor in an infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES-KOH pH 5.8, and 200 µM acetosyringone). After incubating for 2 h at room temperature, the Agrobacterium cultures were infiltrated into the leaves of N. benthamiana plants. Leaves were photographed, stained with trypan blue as described in [12], or harvested three days post-infiltration. Proteins were extracted from N. benthamiana leaves expressing recombinant proteins using native extraction buffer 1 (NB1) and denaturing buffer (DB) for immunoblot analysis as described in [24].

Trypan blue staining

Trypan blue staining was modified from [25]. Briefly, transient expression in N. benthamiana was performed as described in [23]. Leaves were collected at various days after infiltration and heated in Trypan Blue solution to 90 °C for 3 min. Remove the Trypan Blue solution and discard as phenol waste. Immediately add chloral hydrate solution for approximately 48 h. Discard chloral hydrate solution and add 50% glycerol to cover the leaves.

Yeast two-hybrid screening and analysis of pairwise interactions

To identify proteins interacting with the C-terminal domain of NFR1 (NFR1-CD), a yeast two-hybrid screen was conducted using a L. japonicus root cDNA library as described previously [26]. The bait plasmid, pGBKT7-NFR1-CD, was transformed into the yeast strain Y187 using the lithium acetate (LiAc) method according to the Yeast Handbook (Clontech). For mating experiments, yeast cells were spread onto SD/−2 synthetic dropout medium (-Trp/-Leu) and SD/−4 selective medium (-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade) and incubated at 28 °C for 4 to 5 days. Each experiment was performed with at least three biological replicates.

Phylogenetic analysis of E3 ligases

A command line version (v2.1.3) of diamond blastp [27] search (E-value threshold of 1e-5) was performed to identify LjPUB7 homologs in different species, using the M. truncatula, L. japonicus, A. thaliana, and N. tabacum proteome sequences as the database and the LjPUB7 protein sequence as the query. Candidate E3 Ligase protein sequences were aligned using the ClustalX 2.0 program [28]. Phylogenetic analysis was then performed using MEGA-6 software [29], employing the Jones-Taylor-Thornton (JTT) matrix-based model with a discrete Gamma distribution (+ G, five categories). Maximum likelihood phylogeny estimation was conducted with one thousand bootstrap replications to assess the robustness of the tree topology.

Hairy root transformation

Seeds were scarified with concentrated H₂SO₄ for 8 min, then surface-sterilized in 2% sodium hypochlorite solution and stratified at 4 °C for 24 h. Sterilized seeds were germinated on half-strength Murashige and Skoog (1/2 MS) agar plates at 23 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod. A. rhizogenes strain LBA1334, harboring the binary vector containing the gene of interest, was cultured in LB medium to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.6–0.8 and resuspended in sterile water with 20–40 µg/mL acetosyringone (AS). Seedling hypocotyls were excised and immersed in the Agrobacterium suspension for 30 min. Seedlings were then transferred to MS medium (without sucrose) for 3 days in the dark followed by 1–2 days under Light. Following co-cultivation, seedlings were transferred to HRE rooting medium supplemented with 1% sucrose and 300 µg/mL cefotaxime, and incubated at 23 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod for 10–14 days. Transformed roots were confirmed by GUS staining, and non-transformed or excessively stained roots were excised. Healthy, transformed roots were acclimatized in sterile water before transplanting to vermiculite/perlite (1:1, v/v) substrate.

Stable transformation

Stable transformation was achieved using A. tumefaciens strain EHA105 carrying the desired binary vector. Seeds were sterilized and germinated as described in the hairy root transformation section. Hypocotyl segments (approximately 0.3 cm) were excised and inoculated with Agrobacterium for 20 min, then co-cultivated on 1/10 B5 medium supplemented with 5 µM 6-BA, 0.5 µM NAA, 100 µM AS and 1x B5 vitamins, for 7 days in the dark. Callus induction and selection were performed on B5 medium supplemented with 20 g/L sucrose, 4 g/L GelRite, 5 µM 6-BA, 0.5 µM NAA, 1 x B5 vitamins, 15 µg/mL hygromycin, and 300 µg/mL Timentin. Explants were subcultured every week for 5–10 weeks until green callus emerged. Shoots were induced from the selected callus on B5 medium supplemented with 20 g/L sucrose, 4 g/L GelRite, 5 µM 6-BA, 1.25 mM (NH₄)₂SO₄, 1 x B5 vitamins, 7.5 µg/mL hygromycin and 300 µg/mL Timentin, subculturing every week until shoots formed. Elongated shoots were then transferred to shoot growth medium (similar to shoot induction medium, with 20–40 µL/L 6-BA) with 7-10 day subculturing intervals. Rooting was induced by transferring shoots to B5 medium supplemented with 20 g/L sucrose, 4 g/L GelRite, 0.5-1 µM NAA, 1 x B5 vitamins and 300 µg/mL Timentin. Rooted plantlets were transferred to soil for further growth. Transgenic seed selection was performed on MS plates containing 50 mg/L hygromycin.

Rhizobial infection and nodulation assays

WT plants and Ljpub7 mutant plants were employed for phenotypic analysis, following established protocols [18]. Seeds were sterilized and germinated as described in the hairy root transformation section. Germination was conducted at 28 °C in the dark for 2 days. Subsequently, seedlings were transferred to 32-well pit discs filled with a sterilized mixture of vermiculite and perlite (2:1, v/v). The seedlings were maintained under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark cycle at 23 °C. For inoculation, a liquid culture of M. loti (GFP-expressing strain) grown in Tryptone Yeast (TY) medium, reaching an optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) of 1.0, was centrifuged to pellet the bacteria. The bacterial pellet was then resuspended in a half-strength Broughton and Dilworth (B&D) nitrogen-free medium supplemented with 0.5 mM KNO₃. Five days post-transplantation into the pit discs, plant seedlings were inoculated with 50 mL of rhizobia suspension at OD₆₀₀ = 0.02. Infection thread analysis was performed at 5 dpi, while nodule number analysis was performed at 7 and 14 dpi.

In vitro ubiquitination assays

In vitro ubiquitination assays were performed using a bacterial reconstituted system as previously described [13, 19]. To generate the LjPUB7 substrate, the coding sequence of LjPUB7 fused with a (G4S)2-strep tag was amplified from the pEASY-Blunt-LjPUB7 vector. A point mutation, LjPUB7-W318A, was generated by site-directed mutagenesis using the pEASY-LjPUB7-Blunt vector as a template and subsequently cloned into pACYCDuet or pACYCDuet-AtUBC8 vectors using BamHI/NotI restriction sites. Vectors including pCDFDuet-AtUBA1, pACYCDuet-AtUBC8, pCDFDuet-LjNFR1-CDS, pCDFDuet-LjNFR5-CDS, pCDFDuet-LjNFR1-CDS-MYC-AtUBA1, pCDFDuet-LjNFR5-CDS-MYC-AtUBA1, and PET28a-FLAG-AtUBQ10, all utilized in this study, were previously generated and are available in our laboratory [13].

Real-time PCR.

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Genomic DNA was removed prior to first-strand cDNA synthesis using an oligo(dT) primer and the PrimeScript RT Reagent kit (Takara Biotechnology). Real-Time PCR was performed using SYBR Select Master Mix reagent (ABI, Waltham, MA) on an QuantStudio Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gene expression levels were normalized to the constitutively expressed housekeeping genes Actin and Ubiquitin. All experiments were conducted with at least three biological replicates and three technical replicates.

All primer sequences used in this study are Listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Results

LjBAK1 specifically inhibits the cell death response triggered by co-overexpression of LjNFR1/LjNFR5 in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves.

To investigate the degradation mechanisms of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5, these two receptors were co-overexpressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter. Cell death was observed 2–3 days post-infiltration (Fig. 1A), consistent with previous reports [11, 23]. In Arabidopsis, AtBAK1 enhances the interaction between AtPUB12/AtPUB13 and AtFLS2 through phosphorylation, promoting FLS2 ubiquitination and degradation, thus preventing excessive activation of the plant immune response [6, 30]. To determine whether a similar relationship exists between LjBAK1 [18] and LjNFR1/LjNFR5, we co-expressed LjBAK1 with LjNFR1/LjNFR5 in Nicotiana leaves. A decrease in cell death was observed (Fig. 1B, C) when LjBAK1 was co-expressed with LjNFR1/LjNFR5, compared to the expression of LjNFR1/LjNFR5 alone or with the empty vector, both of which resulted in significant cell death. These findings suggest that LjBAK1 effectively suppresses cell death induced by co-expression of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5.

In a similar experiment, overexpression of AtCERK1 in N. benthamiana leaves induced a comparable cell death phenotype (Fig. 1D) [12]. To determine whether the inhibition of cell death by LjBAK1 is specific to LjNFR1/LjNFR5 or a more general phenomenon, we co-expressed LjBAK1 with AtCERK1 in N. benthamiana leaves. The results showed that LjBAK1 did not suppress AtCERK1-induced cell death (Fig. 1E, F). These findings suggest that LjBAK1 specifically inhibits cell death induced by co-expression of LjNFR1/LjNFR5.

LjBAK1 promotes LjNFR1/LjNFR5 protein degradation through a ubiquitination-dependent pathway

In Arabidopsis, AtBAK1 interacts with AtPUB12/AtPUB13 and enhances their interaction with FLS2, leading to FLS2 ubiquitination and degradation, a process that regulates immune signaling [6, 31]. Given that cell death induced by co-expression of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 is specifically suppressed by LjBAK1, we hypothesized that a similar ubiquitination-mediated degradation mechanism might exist between LjBAK1 and LjNFR1/LjNFR5. To test this hypothesis, HA-tagged LjNFR1 or Myc-tagged LjNFR5 were transiently co-expressed with or without FLAG-tagged LjBAK1 in N. benthamiana leaves. Immunoblotting analysis of leaf samples collected at different time points post-infiltration revealed a significant reduction in the protein abundance of both LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 when co-expressed with LjBAK1, compared to the empty vector control, particularly at 2- and 3-days post-infiltration (Fig. 2A, B). These findings suggest that LjBAK1 promotes the degradation of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 proteins.

To further investigate the role of ubiquitination in this degradation process, we treated N. benthamiana leaves with MG132, a known inhibitor of protein ubiquitination and degradation [17]. Leaves were infiltrated with MG132 3 h prior to sampling, after co-expressing LjNFR1 or LjNFR5 with LjBAK1 for 2 days. Immunoblotting analysis showed that protein levels of both LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 were significantly increased in MG132-treated samples compared to the control group (Fig. 2C, D). These results strongly suggest that the degradation of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 proteins mediated by LjBAK1 relies on the ubiquitination pathway.

Since any direct interaction between these proteins is likely very weak or nonexistent [15, 18] (Figure S1), we propose that LjBAK1 interacts with a specific E3 ubiquitin ligase in Nicotiana, which then ubiquitinates and degrades LjNFR1 and LjNFR5. Previous studies have identified several E3 ubiquitin ligases in N. benthamiana, many of which are involved in immune responses [32, 33]. Genome-wide functional annotation has revealed the presence of at least 600 E3 ubiquitin ligases in N. benthamiana, including 172 annotated as U-box domain-containing proteins, indicating a robust ubiquitination system in this model plant [34, 35]. This lays the foundation for LjBAK1-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 in N. benthamiana.

Identification and cloning of LjPUB7

To investigate the ubiquitination regulatory mechanisms of LjNFR1/LjNFR5 mediated by LjBAK1, we employed a yeast two-hybrid screen. The intracellular kinase domain of LjNFR1 was used as bait against a cDNA library derived from root tissue. Yeast colonies that grew on SD-dropout medium were selected, and positive clones were confirmed by PCR and sequencing. Sequence analysis, in conjunction with data from the Lotus Base [36], identified the seventh positive clone as a plant U-box type E3 ligase, which was subsequently designated as LjPUB7. LjPUB7 is located on chromosome 2, spans a single exon, and encodes a protein with an estimated molecular weight of 75 kDa. This protein consists of 687 amino acid residues and contains two predicted domains: a C-terminal ARMADILLO (ARM) domain and a central U-box domain (Fig. 3A, B). This structural organization classifies LjPUB7 as a member of the plant U-box E3 ligase family. Notably, previous studies have shown that PUB-type E3 ubiquitin ligases in Arabidopsis are involved in the ubiquitination and degradation of receptor proteins [6, 8].

To explore the evolutionary relationship between LjPUB7 and known plant U-box E3 ubiquitin ligases, we conducted a BLAST [27] search using the LjPUB7 protein sequence as a query. Homologous proteins identified in various species were then aligned with known plant U-box E3 ligases for a comprehensive analysis. Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis revealed LjPUB7 homologs in model plants such as A. thaliana, N. benthamiana, and Medicago truncatula. LjPUB7-like proteins from different species exhibit high sequence similarity, with LjPUB7 sharing 79.5% identity with the M. truncatula protein MtPUB1, clustering on the same branch of the phylogenetic tree. In contrast, LjPUB7 shares only 27.1% sequence similarity with Arabidopsis AtPUB13, clustering on different branches (Fig. 2C), suggesting a closer evolutionary relationship between LjPUB7 and MtPUB1.

A potential role of Ljpub7 early rhizobia-host interaction

To investigate the expression pattern of LjPUB7 in L. japonicus, we analyzed RNA-seq data from public databases [36]. Transcriptome analysis revealed that LjPUB7 is predominantly expressed in roots, with significantly lower expression levels in other tissues (Figure S2A). During symbiosis, LjPUB7 transcript levels were significantly higher in roots than in nodules, and were significantly upregulated during the early stages of symbiosis (1–3 days post-inoculation (dpi) with rhizobia) (Figure S2B). These findings were verified by qRT-PCR (Fig. 4A-C) and further supported by histochemical analysis of LjPUB7pro expression in transgenic hairy roots (Figure S3). This analysis revealed strong GUS activity in roots 3 dpi with rhizobia (Fig. 4D-I). Furthermore, inoculation with the NodC mutant rhizobia severely inhibited LjPUB7 expression compared to inoculation with wild-type rhizobia (Figure S2B), suggesting that successful host recognition of rhizobia, and subsequent nodulation factor signaling, is required for LjPUB7 expression. These findings strongly suggest that LjPUB7 plays a critical role in the early stages of rhizobia-host interaction.

LjPUB7 associates with NFR1 and NFR5

To further validate the interaction between LjPUB7 and LjNFR1, the full-length LjPUB7 coding sequence (CDS) was cloned into the pGADT7 vector and transformed into the AH109 yeast strain. The kinase domains of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 were cloned into the pGBKT7 vector, resulting in pGBKT7-LjNFR1-KD and pGBKT7-LjNFR5-KD, which were then transformed into the Y187 yeast strain. After mating, yeast cells were grown on selective media. The results indicated that LjPUB7 interacted with both LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 in yeast (Fig. 5A).

Analysis of the LjPUB7 protein structure using the HMMSmart database and relevant literature revealed the presence of one U-box domain and one ARM domain. These domains are conserved within the U-box E3 ligase family and are known to mediate protein-protein interactions. To identify the key domain(s) responsible for the interaction of LjPUB7 with LjNFR1 and LjNFR5, we constructed several LjPUB7 variants containing different domain combinations: a truncated version lacking the N-terminal sequence (UND), a version containing both the U-box and ARM domains (UboxARM), a version containing only the ARM domain (PUB1ARM), and a version lacking the ARM domain (UNDUbox). These variants were subjected to yeast two-hybrid assays with LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 (Fig. 5B). The results revealed that only the LjPUB7 variant lacking the ARM domain failed to interact with either receptor, while all other variants exhibited interactions. This finding suggests that the ARM domain is crucial for the interaction between LjPUB7 and the receptors LjNFR1 and LjNFR5.

To further validate this interaction in vivo, LjPUB7 was cloned into the pSCYCE(R) vector, and LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 were cloned into the pSCYNE vector. These constructs were transformed into Agrobacterium and co-expressed in tobacco leaves. Two days after infiltration, tobacco leaves were examined under a confocal microscope. To minimize protein degradation, MG132 was applied to the tobacco leaves prior to the experiment. The results revealed strong fluorescence in tobacco leaves co-expressing LjPUB7 with either LjNFR1 or LjNFR5 (Fig. 5C), confirming the in vivo interaction of LjPUB7 with LjNFR1 and LjNFR5. The interaction between LjPUB7 and LjBAK1 was also confirmed using BiFC and Co-IP assay (Figure S1, S4). Since our results showed that LjBAK1 does not directly interact with LjNFR1 or LjNFR5 [18] (Figure S1), we suggest that the degradation effect of LjBAK1 on NFR1/NFR5 is indirect, with LjPUB7 acting as a “bridge” connecting LjBAK1 and NFR1/NFR5.

LjPUB7 mediates the ubiquitination of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5

Domain analysis and functional prediction indicated that LjPUB7 possesses the characteristics of a functional E3 ubiquitin ligase. To experimentally validate this prediction, we employed an in vitro ubiquitination assay system established in Escherichia coli [19]. The full-length LjPUB7 CDS was cloned into the pACYCDuet-Strep vector and transformed into BL21 (DE3) competent cells for protein expression, along with FLAG-tagged Arabidopsis ubiquitin, S-tagged AtUBA1 (E1 ubiquitin-activating enzyme), and S-tagged AtUBC8 (E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme) (Fig. 6A). Western blot analysis using an anti-FLAG antibody detected polyubiquitinated protein bands of PUB7, confirming the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of LjPUB7 (Fig. 6B).

Given that LjPUB7 associates with NFR1 and NFR5, we hypothesized that LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 might be substrates of LjPUB7. To test this, we constructed the kinase domains of LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 into the expression vectors pACYDuet-Myc-AtUBA1. Following induction of protein expression, Western blot analysis revealed the presence of multiple bands of varying sizes above LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 in the experimental lane, compared to control lanes (Fig. 6C, D). This observation suggests that LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 undergo ubiquitination, supporting their role as substrates of LjPUB7.

LjPUB7 negatively regulates symbiosis in L. japonicus

To elucidate the role of LjPUB7 in the symbiotic signaling pathway, we employed CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing using three guide RNAs (gRNAs) targeting the 5’ end of the LjPUB7 gene (Figures S5, S6). This resulted in the generation of two independent mutant lines, designated Ljpub7-1 and Ljpub7-2 (Fig. 7A, B). The Ljpub7-1 mutant exhibited a guanine insertion at the sgRNA1 site, while the Ljpub7-2 mutant harbored a 48-bp deletion and a 2-bp insertion at the sgRNA3 site. Both mutations introduced premature stop codons, leading to truncated LjPUB7 protein products (Fig. 7A, B, C).

In response to rhizobial treatment, the Ljpub7 mutants exhibited a significant increase in both the number of infection points (IPs) and infection threads (ITs) at 5 days post-inoculation (Fig. 7D, E). Nodulation kinetics following inoculation with M. loti revealed the Ljpub7 mutant exhibited a significant increase in both the number and density of nodules at 7 dpi with slightly increased shoot (Figure S8), but no significant difference was observed at 14 dpi (Fig. 7F, H). These findings suggest that LjPUB7 may act as a negative regulator during the early stages of rhizobial symbiosis. Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis was conducted to evaluate the expression levels of key early nodulation genes in the roots of both the MG20 wild-type and Ljpub7 mutant lines and the results indicated significantly elevated mRNA levels of these genes in the roots of Ljpub7 mutants compared to MG20 (Fig. 7H-J, S9). These findings further support the notion that the absence of LjPUB7 enhances early infection signals from rhizobia, suggesting a potential role for LjPUB7 in regulating the initiation of rhizobial symbiosis.

Discussion

NFRs act as gatekeepers, initiating symbiotic signaling that leads to rhizobial infection and nodule development in legumes. Overexpression of either LjNFR1 or LjNFR5 triggers spontaneous nodule organogenesis and upregulation of nodulation-related genes in the roots of transgenic L. japonicus in the absence of rhizobia [37]. Interestingly, co-overexpression of either NFRs or their homologs in N. benthamiana leaves triggers defense-related responses, such as defense gene expression and hypersensitive cell death [11, 12], suggesting that NFRs may have the potential to elicit immune responses. These findings are consistent with the roles of other members of the LysM receptor kinase (LYK) family, which are key to recognizing diverse polysaccharides [38]. For instance, in Arabidopsis, AtCERK1 and AtLYK5 mediate chitin perception, triggering plant immunity against fungal pathogens [7, 8].

Sustained activation of defense-related receptors can negatively impact plant growth; therefore, precise regulation of receptor protein levels is crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis and ensuring optimal host fitness [3]. In Arabidopsis, recognition of bacterial flagellin by AtFLS2 triggers AtBAK1-mediated phosphorylation of PUB12 and PUB13 [5]. This phosphorylation event promotes AtFLS2 ubiquitination and subsequent degradation, thereby preventing excessive or prolonged immune activation [6]. Given the importance of precisely regulating NFR levels to prevent nodulation in the absence of rhizobia and to avoid excessive immune responses, it is intriguing to investigate whether a similar regulatory mechanism operates in L. japonicus, involving BAK1 mediating E3-dependent ubiquitination and degradation of NFRs. In this study, we show that co-expression of NFRs triggered cell death can be specifically inhibited by LjBAK1 in N. benthamiana leaves (Fig. 1). Notably, previous findings suggest that LjBAK1 exhibits very weak or no direct interaction with NFRs [15, 18] (Figure S1). Thus, we propose that LjBAK1 interacts with a specific E3 ubiquitin ligase, acting as a “bridge” connecting LjBAK1 and LjNFR1/LjNFR5, which then ubiquitinates and degrades NFRs. This hypothesis is further supported by the observation that MG132, a known inhibitor of protein ubiquitination and degradation, effectively suppresses this degradation (Fig. 2).

LjPUB7 was identified as an interacting protein of LjNFR1, and was shown to interact with both NFRs (Fig. 3). LjPUB7 was classified as a member of the plant U-box E3 ligase family and encodes a protein consists of two domains: a C-terminal ARMADILLO (ARM) domain and a central U-box domain (Fig. 3A, B). Further analysis revealed LjPUB7 negatively regulates symbiosis at the early stages. Several E3 ligase have been identified playing a significant role during nodule symbiosis and nitrogen fixation [14]. The SEVEN IN ABSENTIA (SINA) E3 ubiquitin ligase SINA4 specifically reduced SYMRK protein levels, and ectopic expression of SINA4 in rhizobia-inoculated roots impaired infection thread development [39]. In contrast, another SymRK-interacting E3 ubiquitin ligase, SIE3, exhibits a different mode of action [17]. Overexpression of SIE3 promoted nodulation, while conversely, downregulation of SIE3 transcripts impaired infection thread development and nodule organogenesis, indicating a positive regulatory function in symbiotic interactions [17]. Two plant U-box-type E3 ligases have been previously reported to regulate nodule symbiosis. M. truncatula Plant U-box protein 1 (MtPUB1) acts as a bona fide E3 ligase and negatively regulates infection and nodulation [16]. MtPUB1 interacts with both LYK3 and DMI2, but it has not been shown to directly ubiquitinate these receptors [16, 40]. Conversely, the L. japonicus LjPUB13 encodes positively regulate symbiosis and directly ubiquitinate LjNFR5 [15]. These findings suggest that E3 ligases employ diverse mechanisms in the regulation of nodule symbiosis.

Sequence alignment of LjPUB7 and LjPUB13 revealed low sequence similarity between the two, with nucleotide and protein sequence identities of 39.85% and 31.94% (Figure S10), respectively. Transcription analysis revealed that LjPUB13 exhibits high expression levels in various L. japonicus tissues (Figure S11A), implying a potential role in tissues beyond the root. This contrasts with LjPUB7, which is primarily expressed in the root, with only minor expression in the leaves (Figure S2A). Furthermore, phenotypic analysis showed that the loss of LjPUB7 has a minor impact on plant growth and development (Figure S8), with only a slight enhancement in shoot development in the mutant at the early stages of rhizobium inoculation (7 dpi). This suggests that LjPUB7 may be involved in negatively regulating early development, which contrasts significantly with the proposed mode of action of LjPUB13. Previous reports have indicated that Ljpub13 mutants exhibit a reduced plant growth phenotype [15]. Coupled with their differing interactions with NFRs (Figure S12), these data strongly suggest that they may perform distinct biological functions. Nevertheless, a shared characteristic is that both LjPUB7 and LjPUB13 exhibit the highest expression levels in the root relative to other organs (Figure S11A). During rhizobium symbiosis, their expression patterns are relatively similar, with both showing higher expression in the root during the early stages of inoculation (1–3 dpi) and lower expression in nodules at later stages (Figure S2B, S11B). Future research will focus on the crosstalk between LjPUB7 and other receptor kinases involved in symbiosis and defense, as well as the detailed role of LjBAK1-LjPUB7 complex in flg22- and rhizobia-triggered defense responses. These investigations will be crucial for further elucidating the interaction mechanisms between symbiosis and immunity.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings reveal a novel mechanism by which LjPUB7, a U-box E3 ligase, negatively regulates early symbiotic interactions in L. japonicus through ubiquitination of NFR1 and NFR5. LjBAK1 mediated cell death suppression induced by LjNFR1 and LjNFR5 co-expression in N. benthamiana, suggesting the existence of a LjBAK1-LjPUB7 E3 ligase complex to regulate NFR protein levels. This regulatory pathway ensures precise control of NFR abundance, preventing excessive immune responses and optimizing rhizobial infection. The identification of LjPUB7 as a key component in this process provides valuable insights into the complex interplay between immunity and symbiosis in legumes.
