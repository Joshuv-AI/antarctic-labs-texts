Introduction

Neuroendocrine tumors of the uterine cervix account for 0.9 % to 1.5 % of cervical tumors [1]. They are rare, aggressive tumors with a poor prognosis, and their therapeutic management remains a dilemma for many medical teams. It is a type of neuroendocrine cancer that most often involves the gastroenteropancreatic tract and the lungs. Treatment is based on surgery and concomitant radio-chemotherapy. The work has been reported concerning the SCARE 2020 criteria [2].

Presentation of case

The patient was 55 years old, married, mother of 3 living children, with no particular pathological history, menopausal for 4 years. She presented with abnormal uterine bleeding of low intensity with leucorrhoea for 6 months prior to admission, with no associated digestive or urinary signs. On gynecological examination, a 4 cm ulcer-burgeoning process was noted on the cervix, infiltrating the vaginal cul-de-sacs and extending to the upper 2/3 of the vagina; the right and left parametrium were fixed. Biopsies taken at this site showed a proliferation of carcinomatous cells arranged in compact patches, outlining tubulo-glandular formations, leaning against each other to form a cribriform appearance. Cyto-nuclear atypia was high, with numerous mitoses. These cells were arranged in diffuse masses or sheets, often centered by necrosis, with no significant glandular or squamous differentiation. These carcinomatous masses are arranged within a moderately abundant fibrocollagenous stroma. A complementary immunohistochemical study of the various fragments revealed labeling of the carcinomatous cells with the following antibodies: antisynaptophysin, p16, CD56, ACE. This makes the tumor compatible with high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma or invasive large-cell carcinoma of the uterine cervix.

Ultrasound revealed a voluminous, well-limited, finely echogenic uterine retention with a liquid-liquid level measuring 165 × 110 mm, with a few endometrial buds. Abdominal-pelvic CT revealed a globular uterus with regular contours, the site of endocavitary fluid retention, and irregular circumferential parietal thickening of the cervical-isthmic portion, heterogeneously enhanced after injection of contrast medium, measuring 30 × 45 mm. Associated pelvic fat infiltration, with bilateral iliac osteolytic lesions, osteocondansent lesions of the anterior arch of the 7th right rib, and primary iliac adenopathies (the largest measuring 20 mm), the examination was classified as FIGO IIIC1 (Fig. 1). A bone scan was carried out, returning without any particularities.

Scannographic appearance of a globular uterus, the site of uterine retention.

The decision taken at the multidisciplinary consultation meeting was concomitant radiochemotherapy. The patient underwent 6 courses of chemotherapy 1 week apart (Cisplatin 80 mg/m2 and Etoposide 75 mg/m2) concomitant with radiotherapy at a dose of 46 Gray to the pelvis in 30 sessions, 10 Gray to the parametrium and 6 Gray to the adenopathies. Radiotherapy was not supplemented by brachytherapy because the cervix was not accessible. The post-therapeutic radio-clinical evaluation showed the persistence of a tumor measuring almost 28 mm still infiltrating the vaginal cul-de-sacs, hence the indication for surgical treatment, which consisted of an enlarged closure colpohysterectomy with pelvic courage (Fig. 2). Post-operative management was straightforward.

Intraoperative appearance of the tumor infiltrating the uterine serosa and adnexa.

The anatomopathological result of the surgical specimen showed a large-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma infiltrating the uterine wall, cervix, parametrium, and adnexa, with lymph node metastases in the primitive iliac chain.

The patient was seen in consultation 4 months after the surgical procedure, the clinical examination was unremarkable, the vaginal section was clean, there was no bleeding, and an abdominopelvic scanner was requested.

Discussion

Neuroendocrine carcinoma is a rare and aggressive malignancy, arising mainly in the respiratory and digestive tracts. Its prevalence represents only 3 % of cervical tumors, which are predominantly squamous cell carcinomas [3]. Clinically, it manifests as abnormal uterine bleeding and leukorrhea, and exceptionally as a paraneoplastic syndrome (Cushing's syndrome, carcinoid syndrome, hypoglycemia, syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, hypercalcemia) [4]. First identified in 1957, its true incidence is probably underestimated, as it is described under different terminologies such as carcinoid tumor, argyrophil cell carcinoma, apudoma, oat cell carcinoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma, atypical small cell carcinoma, intermediate cell carcinoma [5]. In 1997, Albores-Saavedra et al. proposed a standardized classification of neuroendocrine tumors into four subtypes: small-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, the most common, characterized by high mitotic activity, extensive necrosis, and vascular invasion, frequently associated with human papillomaviruses. The other subtypes are large-cell neuroendocrine carcinoma, classified as high-grade, and typical and atypical carcinoid tumors, classified as differentiated neuroendocrine tumors [6]. In contrast to squamous cell carcinomas of the uterine cervix, there has been an increase in the incidence of large cell neuroendocrine carcinomas over the last two decades, reflecting the use of this common terminology, which has also, thanks to the publication of retrospective series, highlighted several features specific to these tumors. These tumors occur at a median age of 42 years, which seems younger than that of squamous cell carcinomas of the uterine cervix. Clinical symptomatology is non-specific, and patients most often present with induced uterine bleeding, recurrent leucorrhoea, and/or a pelvic mass discovered on examination. Rarely, patients present with clinical and biochemical features of hormonal secretion [7]. They are distinguished from squamous cell carcinomas by their higher recurrence rate and late diagnosis, due to the ineffectiveness of cervical-uterine smear screening for this type of tumor. On the other hand, association with human papillomaviruses (HPV) 16 and 18 is a common risk factor for both types of carcinoma [1,7]. Furthermore, according to a 2018 study on the contribution of HPVs to the formation of neuroendocrine tumors of the cervix, based on a series of 1575 cases of invasive cervical tumors, HPV DNA was detected in 85.7 % of neuroendocrine tumor cases (HPV16 in 54.8 % and HPV18 in 40.5 %) [8].

Diagnosis is based on histological and, above all, immunohistochemical studies demonstrating the presence of at least one neuroendocrine marker (synaptophysin, chromogranin A, specific neuronal enolase), enabling a positive diagnosis [9]. Macroscopically, the tumor is usually endocervical. Two main forms are observed: the massive nodular form with little adherence to the columnar mucosa, and the multicorronodular basaloid tumor. Mannion et al. compared the microscopic characteristics and survival rates of the four categories; small-cell carcinomas had a poorer prognosis, with similarities to small-cell carcinomas of the lung. They are characterized by a high mitotic index, extensive necrosis, massive lymphatic and vascular invasion, and a strong association with HPV18 [10]. Treatment of neuroendocrine carcinomas of the uterine cervix is based on surgery, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy. The treatment decision depends essentially on the FIGO stage, tumor size, lymph node staging, and the presence or absence of distant metastases. Staging follows that of all cervical tumors. However, it is important to recognize the increased risk of lymphatic and vascular invasion, and the high rate of extrapelvic recurrence. For example, early lymphatic invasion of locoregional adenopathies has been observed in 40 % of stage IB small cell tumors less than 3 cm in diameter. In 60 % of these tumors, vascular and lymphatic invasion was observed at the time of diagnosis. Metastases are most frequently bone, supraclavicular, and pulmonary [1]. According to a recent study published in 2017 on prognostic factors and optimal treatment of cervical neuroendocrine tumors classified I and II, approximately 198 cases in the Department of Gynecology-Obstetrics, Japan: 88 patients (94.6 %) underwent radical hysterectomy with pelvic dissection, of whom 18 received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, 14 patients received adjuvant radiotherapy, 48 adjuvant chemotherapy, and 11 patients adjuvant radiochemotherapy. They concluded that first-line radical surgery is more effective than radiotherapy alone, with a lower locoregional recurrence rate. Adjuvant chemotherapy with EP (etoposide + platinum)/CPT-P (irinotecan + platinum) gave very good results in this series, with a high survival rate compared with adjuvant radiotherapy [1]. On the other hand, two authors have reported disappointing results for local treatment (surgery with or without radiotherapy) of tumors classified as stage I-IIA: Sheet et al., the first authors, found an overall survival rate at three years of 16 % and a progression-free survival rate at five years of 0 % [11]. For Sevin et al., the five-year disease-free survival rate was 36 %. Mainly hematogenous (67–90 % of cases) and lymph node relapses (34 % of cases), a high incidence of adenopathy at diagnosis (40–60 %), and frequent vascular invasion have prompted most authors to combine systemic with local treatment [12]. Zivanovic et al. retrospectively compared local treatment alone (surgery) with local treatment combined with adjuvant chemotherapy. They found a three-year recurrence-free survival rate of 83 % for patients receiving cisplatin and etoposide-based chemotherapy versus 0 % for local treatment alone [13]. Because of the rate of early metastatic spread, some authors have preferred to use neoadjuvant chemotherapy; Wang demonstrated a complete response in 6 of 7 patients who received CAV/PE before hysterectomy [14]. On the other hand, Lee et al. found no benefit in the 11 patients who received neoadjuvant therapy (2 of the 5 patients with TNT graded IB1 and the other 6 patients with stage IB2-IIA tumors who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy died within 2 years) [15]. For locally advanced tumors (stages IIb-IV) and inoperable patients, a combination of radiotherapy and chemotherapy is recommended, according to the protocol of Hoskins et al. [16].

In these stages, chemotherapy with at least five cycles of cisplatin and etoposide is associated with a better probability of recurrence-free survival. Indeed, this was the protocol used in our patient with a poor clinical response after neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with radiotherapy. Advanced tumors are treated with platinum-based chemotherapy. In case of recurrence or chemoresistance, a second line of therapy is initiated with vincristine/doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide and topotecan. In the case of metastatic disease or recurrence, chemotherapy with either cisplatin and etoposide alone, or alternating with VAC chemotherapy (vincristine, adriamycin, and cyclophosphamide) is indicated [17].In general, according to recommendations published by Chan et al. in 2003, surgical treatment is primarily indicated for NETs classified I-IIA, measuring less than 4 cm, sometimes followed by adjuvant chemotherapy or radiochemotherapy, while I-IIA tumors measuring more than 4 cm require neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgery [18]. For cervical neuroendocrine tumors classified as IIb-IV, radiochemotherapy according to the Hoskins protocol is recommended [16].

Conclusion

Neuroendocrine tumors of the uterine cervix are very aggressive and rare tumors, which explains the lack of randomized trials and makes their management increasingly difficult. Multicenter clinical trials are needed to try to determine an unambiguous and effective treatment for large-cell neuroendocrine carcinomas of the uterine cervix, to improve patient survival.
