Comparison of adverse perinatal outcomes in late-onset fetal growth restriction with Delphi procedure consensus criteria
Authors: Min Yuan, Yang Zhang, Jianqiao Luo, Xirong Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe problem of the management of fetal growth restriction (FGR) is in lack of gold standard. Several experts established a consensus definition of FGR through Delphi procedure, while few studies focus on the perinatal outcomes among sub-types of late-onset FGR diagnosed by Delphi procedure consensus criteria. ObjectiveTo compare the adverse perinatal outcomes in different subgroups of late-onset FGR according to the Delphi procedure consensus criteria.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted between 2013 and 2020 including late-onset FGR diagnosed by Delphi consensus: Group 1, estimated fetal weight (EFW) or abdominal circumference (AC)<3rd centile; Group 2, EFW or AC<10th centile and umbilical artery pulsatility index (UA-PI) > 95th centile; Group 3, EFW or AC<10th centile and EFW or AC crossing centiles > 2 quartiles on growth centiles; Group 4, EFW or AC crossing centiles > 2 quartiles on growth centiles and UA-PI > 95th centile. Adverse perinatal outcomes (APOs) of 1071 late-onset FGR, including cesarean or instrumental delivery for non-reassuring fetal status, acidosis, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, respiratory problems, and any adverse perinatal outcome, were compared with appropriate gestational age (AGA) group (Group 5, n = 515) as adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).Results1071 subjects with late-onset FGR were included, 371(34.6%), 67(6.3%), 516(48.2%), and 117(10.9%) were classified into four groups respectively according to Delphi standard. All mortalities occurred in fetuses with an EFW < 3rd percentile. Compared with Group 5, Group 1 showed the highest risk concerning any APOs (aOR = 4.91, 95% CI: 3.53–6.84), acidosis (aOR = 5.81, 95% CI: 2.96–11.37), neonatal hyperbilirubinemia (aOR = 4.71, 95% CI: 3.30–6.71) and respiratory problems (aOR = 3.65, 95% CI: 2.31–5.77) in the multivariable-adjusted model. As the risk of APOs in Group 2 (aOR = 2.60, 95% CI: 1.44–4.73) and Group 3 (aOR = 1.55, 95% CI = 1.11–2.16) followed, we did not find an increasing APOs risk in Group 4 (aOR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.49–1.58).ConclusionsAccording to the Delphi consensus, the morbidity was inconsistent among different types of late-onset FGR. EFW or AC < 3rd percentile presented the highest risk of APOs. Compared with AGAs, Group 4 was not associated with an increasing risk of APOs. Further study is needed to develop protocols for the risk-stratification management of late-onset FGR.

Introduction

FGR is defined as a failure of the fetus to reach its genetically determined growth potential due to maternal, fetal, or placental factors [1]. Fetuses with FGR have increased perinatal morbidity and mortality, primarily attributed to suboptimal fetal nutrition and placental insufficiency. FGR accounts for approximately 32% of late iatrogenic preterm births [2], and is a significant risk factor for severe hypoxic events, including fetal distress and neonatal acidosis. Studies have shown that improving the prenatal detection of FGR can substantially reduce the fetal mortality rate from 18.9% to 9.7% [3]. The diagnosis of fetal growth restriction (FGR) lacks a gold standard, primarily due to the inherent challenge of defining an individual fetus’s optimal growth potential. While fetal biometric parameters serve as the clinical mainstay for diagnosis, significant controversy persists regarding the choice of specific measures (e.g., EFW vs. AC), cutoff thresholds (e.g., 3rd vs. 10th percentile), and reference standards [1].

To address this, Gordijn et al. convened a Delphi panel of 106 experts to establish consensus [4]. Different combinations of these parameters generate different subtypes of FGR. Several studies revealed the substantial heterogeneity in the risk of adverse perinatal outcomes across the distinct subtypes of FGR [5, 6]. The Delphi consensus framework, while providing diagnostic standardization, could potentially limit risk stratification in FGR, thereby impacting clinical management.

Late-onset FGR, diagnosed at or after 32 weeks of gestation, accounts for approximately 70%-80% of FGR cases [7, 8]. The clinical challenge of late-onset FGR lies in its detection and diagnosis, due to its high incidence and frequent overlap with constitutionally small fetuses. Late‑onset FGR typically presents with normal or mildly abnormal umbilical and cerebral Doppler indices, yet it remains associated with unpredicted intrauterine fetal death [9]. The Delphi panel defined diagnostic criteria for late-onset FGR comprising two solitary parameters (EFW or AC < 3rd percentile) and four contributory parameters (EFW or AC < 10th percentile, EFW or AC crossing > 2 quartiles, CPR < 5th percentile, or UA-PI > 95th percentile).

Materials and methods

Study groups

A retrospective cohort study analyzed late-onset FGR cases delivered at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University between Jan 2013 and Aug 2020. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (2021-57). Inclusion criteria required: (i) singleton pregnancies managed in our hospital from the first trimester, and (ii) at least three fetal ultrasounds at 11–13⁺⁶, 20–23⁺⁶, and > 32 week. We excluded pregnancies with chromosomal or structural abnormalities, incomplete records, or severe maternal systemic diseases (e.g., congenital heart or immune disorders).

Gestational age was determined by last menstrual period, adjusted to first-trimester crown-rump length if the discrepancy exceeded 7 days. The EFW was calculated according to Hadlock BPD/HC/AC/FL formula [10]. EFW and AC centiles were derived from the Buck Louis gestational-age specific fetal-growth standards [11]. EFW or AC crossing centiles > 2 quartiles was defined as a decline of > 50% between the 20–23⁺⁶ week and > 32 week ultrasound assessments [12]. Gestational age-specific UA-PI centiles referred to the study of INTERGROWTH-21st Project [13]. All measurements were obtained by three experienced sonologists in accordance with the ISUOG practice guidelines, using Voluson-E6 or Voluson-E8 ultrasound equipment (GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria) [14]. To minimize operator-dependent variability, standardized protocols were strictly adhered to throughout the study period.

The maternal complications and perinatal outcomes were compared with those of 515 randomly selected fetuses with an EFW appropriate for gestational age (AGA, Group 5) during the corresponding time period.

Study design

Maternal and fetal characteristics, pregnancy complications, and APOs were analyzed across the five groups. Maternal and fetal characteristics included age, pre-pregnancy BMI, method of conception, gestational age and birth weight. Pregnancy complications included gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), gestational hypertensive disorders (GHD), and NRFS. The primary perinatal outcomes for the five categories were APOs, defined as the presence of one or more of the following conditions: neonatal metabolic acidosis, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, respiratory disorders, low Apgar score, and stillbirth. All data were retrieved from the electronic patient record system.

Definitions of maternal complications and perinatal outcomes

Diagnoses were re-verified against clinical records and laboratory examinations. Chronic hypertension was defined as hypertension diagnosed before pregnancy or before 20 weeks of gestation; or hypertension first diagnosed during pregnancy that persists beyond the postpartum period [15]. GHD was defined according to the ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 202 [16]. GDM was diagnosed at 24–28 weeks using a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test with thresholds of FPG ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-h ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-h ≥ 8.5 mmol/L [17]. NRFS required abnormal fetal heart rate tracing during antepartum and intrapartum monitoring [18]. Delivery mode followed the Chinese consensus guideline with informed consent. Neonatal outcomes were defined as: low Apgar score (5-min ≤ 7); [19] metabolic acidosis (UA pH < 7.2 and base excess < -5 mmol/L); [20] hyperbilirubinemia per the 2014 Chinese expert consensus; [21] and respiratory disorders (including RDS, apnea, and wet lung). Stillbirth was defined as the delivery of a fetus showing no signs of life, indicated by the absence of breathing, heartbeats, umbilical cord pulsation, or voluntary muscle movements [22].

Statistical analysis

Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test. Continuous and categorical variables are presented as mean ± SD and n (%), respectively. Group comparisons employed one-way ANOVA or the Jonckheere-Terpstra test for continuous variables, and the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, with post hoc Bonferroni adjustments. Univariate logistic regression was modeled to assess the relationship among groups and APOs, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariate analysis was also conducted. A sensitivity analysis re-examined these associations after excluding pregnancies complicated by preterm birth. All analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). A p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the subjects

A total of 1116 late-onset FGR cases were delivered at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University between Jan 2013 and Aug 2020. After excluding 14 with chromosomal abnormalities or structural malformations and 31 cases with incomplete data, 1071 remained for analysis (Fig. 1). Of the 1071 subjects, 371(34.6%), 67(6.3%), 516(48.2%) and 117(10.9%) were classified into the four groups based on Delphi criteria. The baseline characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. No significant differences were observed across the five groups in maternal age (p = 0.062), mode of conception (p = 0.544) or the prevalence of chronic hypertension (p = 0.138). However, significant differences were found in pre-pregnancy BMI (p < 0.001), gestational age at delivery (p < 0.001), and birth weight (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences in BMI were observed among Group 1–4. However, Group 5 had a significantly higher mean BMI (22.0 ± 3.3 kg/m²) compared to Group 1 (21.2 ± 3.2 kg/m²), 2 (20.6 ± 3.0 kg/m²), 3 (20.9 ± 2.9 kg/m²), and 4 (21.5 ± 2.9 kg/m²), although all values remained within the normal range. Gestational age at delivery was significantly lower in Group 1 (38.0 ± 2.1 weeks) compared to Group 2 (39.1 ± 1.9 weeks), 3 (39.2 ± 1.5 weeks), 4 (39.4 ± 1.1 weeks), and 5 (39.3 ± 1.5 weeks) (p < 0.001). Birth weight followed a similar pattern: Group 1 had the lowest mean birth weight (2365.6 ± 471.3 g), followed by Group 2 (2731.2 ± 442.5 g) and 3 (2881.1 ± 402.7 g), while Group 4 (3156.5 ± 381.5 g) and 5 (3305.5 ± 429.2 g) had the highest (all p < 0.05 for pairwise comparisons with Group 1).

Adverse pregnancy outcomes across the three groups are presented and compared in Table 3. APOs occurred in 49.6% (181/371) of fetuses in Group 1, compared to 29.9% (20/67) in Group 2, 21.3% (110/516) in Group 3, 13.7% (16/117) in Group 4, and 15.5% (80/515) in Group 5 (p < 0.001). Specifically, the rate of neonatal metabolic acidosis was highest in Group 1 (13.6%, 50/371) and Group 2 (9.0%, 6/67), followed by Group 3 (2.5%, 13/516), Group 4 (0.0.0%, 0/117) and Group 5 (2.3%, 12/515) (p < 0.001). Similarly, neonatal hyperbilirubinemia was most frequent in Group 1 (41.5%, 154/371), with progressively lower rates in Group 2 (19.4%, 13/67), 3 (16.1%, 83/516), 4 (12.8%, 15/117), and 5 (12.2%, 63/515) (p < 0.001). Respiratory disorders followed a comparable pattern: 21.7% (80/371) in Group 1, 13.4% (9/67) in Group 2, 7.4% (38/516) in Group 3, 0.9% (1/117) in Group 4, and 6.0% (31/515) in Group 5 (p < 0.001). Stillbirth and low Apgar score were most frequently observed in Group 1. Comparisons of low Apgar score and stillbirth across groups were not feasible because of small sample sizes. Therefore, these outcomes were combined into a composite adverse perinatal outcome measure for analysis.

After multivariable adjustment, Group 1 remained at significantly higher risk for the composite adverse perinatal outcome (aOR = 4.91, 95% CI: 3.53–6.84) and for each individual component, including acidosis (aOR = 5.81, 95% CI: 2.96–11.37), neonatal hyperbilirubinemia (aOR = 4.71, 95% CI: 3.30–6.71), and respiratory disorders (aOR = 3.65, 95% CI: 2.31–5.77). Group 2 and 3 also demonstrated elevated risks for selected outcomes. Group 2 had a significantly higher risk of composite adverse perinatal outcome (aOR = 2.60, 95% CI: 1.44–4.73), acidosis (aOR = 4.81, 95% CI: 1.70–13.58) and respiratory disorders (aOR = 2.53, 95% CI: 1.13–5.67), while Group 3 showed modestly increased risks for the composite outcome (aOR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.11–1.58). In contrast, Group 4 did not show significantly increased risks for any adverse perinatal outcome compared to the AGA reference group.

In a sensitivity analysis excluding preterm cases, the associations between FGR subtypes and adverse perinatal outcomes remained consistent, as shown in Table 5. For Group 1, the adjusted odds ratio for the composite outcome was 3.49 (95% CI: 2.40–5.08), compared to 4.91 (95% CI: 3.53–6.84) in the full cohort. Similarly, the risk estimates for Group 2 and 3 were minimally attenuated: aOR = 2.22 (95% CI: 1.12–4.40) vs. 2.60 (95% CI: 1.44–4.73) for Group 2, and aOR = 1.44 (95% CI: 1.00–2.08) vs. 1.55 (95% CI: 1.11–2.16) for Group 3. Group 4 continued to show no significantly elevated risk (aOR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.53–1.79). These findings suggest that the observed associations were not substantially confounded by preterm delivery.

Discussions

A major clinical challenge in the management of late-onset FGR lies in accurately distinguishing it from constitutional smallness. This diagnostic ambiguity directly informs the primary objective of the present study: to compare perinatal outcomes across distinct subtypes of late-onset FGR defined by the Delphi consensus criteria, thereby clarifying the clinical significance of each subtype.

In our cohort, severe fetal growth restriction accounted for approximately 40% of late‑onset FGR cases, a proportion slightly lower than that reported by E. Meler et al. [23] EFW or AC < 3rd percentile, previously classified as severe FGR, is associated with a significantly increased risk of morbidity and mortality regardless of Doppler results [24]. Consistent with this, E. Meler et al. reported a pronounced risk of APOs in fetuses below the 3rd percentile (adjusted OR = 6.3). [23] Our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating that even after adjustment for potential confounders, fetuses with EFW or AC < 3rd percentile (Group 1) remained at substantially elevated risk (aOR = 4.91, 95% CI: 3.53–6.84)), reinforcing the notion that severe small size alone may serve as a sufficient criterion for identifying high‑risk FGR.

For pregnancies with EFW < 3rd percentile or EFW < 10th percentile combined with UA-PI > 95th percentile, the ISUOG guideline recommends delivery no later than 37⁺⁶ weeks [25], while the SMFM suggests delivery at 37 weeks [8]. In our cohort, the mean gestational age at delivery was 38.0 ± 2.1 weeks for Group 1 (EFW or AC < 3rd percentile) and 39.1 ± 1.9 weeks for Group 2 (3rd centile ≤ EFW or AC < 10th percentile with UA-PI > 95th percentile), both exceeding the recommended delivery windows. This discrepancy may be attributable to the retrospective nature of our study. And further prospective studies are needed to optimize delivery timing for late-onset FGR.

A recent prospective study by Athauda et al. proposed a risk stratification protocol for late-onset FGR, recommending delivery at 40–41 weeks for low-risk cases, defined as: EFW between the 3rd and 10th percentiles with normal Doppler findings and no decline in AC, or EFW > 10th percentile with an AC drop ≥ 50 centiles from previous scans and/or a cerebroplacental ratio (CPR) < 5th percentile [26]. In our cohort, the mean gestational age at delivery for Group 3 and 4 was 39.2 ± 1.5 weeks and 39.4 ± 1.1 weeks, respectively, aligning closely with the recommended delivery window for low-risk phenotypes. These observations may provide additional real-world evidence supporting the feasibility of term delivery in appropriately selected late-onset FGR cases. Currently, no international consensus exists regarding the optimal timing of delivery for late‑onset FGR. The decision to deliver in FGR‑affected pregnancies requires comprehensive consideration of both fetal and maternal factors, and should incorporate local experience and resource availability.

FGR has also been defined using a combination of EFW < 10th percentile and abnormal Doppler parameters in several studies, with the Society of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists of Canada endorsing UA Doppler as a valuable tool for refining the diagnosis [27]. In a large cohort (n = 1,116), Unterscheider et al. demonstrated that fetuses with EFW < 3rd percentile or EFW < 10th percentile plus abnormal UA Doppler were at increased risk of adverse perinatal outcomes and NICU admission [6]. Our findings extend this evidence by showing that, within a well‑characterized late‑onset FGR population, fetuses meeting the criteria of AC or EFW < 10th percentile with UA‑PI > 95th percentile (Group 2) exhibited a significantly higher risk of both composite adverse perinatal outcomes and neonatal acidosis. Importantly, while previous studies have emphasized the prognostic value of Doppler abnormalities, our results further delineate the specific contribution of this combined phenotype in a late‑onset cohort, reinforcing the need for targeted intensive monitoring in this subgroup.

Abnormal UA Doppler reflects the severity of placental insufficiency, which may lead to severe fetal growth restriction or perinatal death. A decline in growth velocity has been proposed as an early indicator of placental dysfunction [28], and multiple studies have examined the association between reduced fetal growth and adverse perinatal outcomes [5, 29]. Various metrics have been used to quantify longitudinal growth, including conditional centiles, growth rate, and growth velocity [30–32].

However, the utility of growth velocity for assessing adverse perinatal outcomes remains controversial. Studies in SGA or low-risk populations have reported inconsistent results. A prospective cohort study by Sovio et al. found that neonatal morbidity was significantly associated with slow AC growth velocity in second-to-third trimester in SGA (RR = 2.5, 95%CI: 1.7–3.6) [33]. In contrast, a secondary analysis of the IRIS study reported no significant increase in adverse perinatal outcomes associated with an isolated decline in fetal growth trajectory among low-risk pregnancies [34]. Studies that examined growth velocity in conjunction with Doppler parameters have also yielded conflicting findings. Caradeux et al. assessed the relationship between EFW z-velocity and adverse perinatal outcomes, and found that only Doppler abnormalities—not growth velocity alone—were significantly associated with adverse outcomes [35]. Similarly, Galán Arévalo et al. reported that a reduction of 20 centiles was not associated with adverse perinatal outcomes, with cesarean delivery for NRFS being the only exception; this risk was significantly increased only in AGA fetuses with a drop exceeding 40 centiles (RR = 2.41, 95% CI: 1.11–5.21) [36]. Together, these discrepancies underscore the substantial heterogeneity in the literature, which may be attributable to inconsistent definitions of fetal growth potential and variations in the measurement of fetal growth trajectories. Our study showed a increased risk of any APOs in fetuses with EFW or AC<10th centile and AC or EFW crossing centiles > 2 quartiles on growth centiles (aOR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.11–2.16). This may be because our criteria incorporated both a significant deviation in growth percentile and an absolute size below the 10th percentile. However, alternative explanations cannot be excluded, including inherent variability in ultrasound measurements, and differences in the timing or frequency of growth assessments. In addition, we did not stratify the analysis by maternal age or pre‑pregnancy BMI, which may have influenced the observed associations given the established relationship between advanced maternal age, obesity, and adverse perinatal outcomes. These factors should be considered when interpreting our findings.

We also explored the combination of growth centiles with Doppler parameters for predicting neonatal morbidity. In our study, AGA fetuses with a reduction of ≥ 50 EFW centiles between the second and third trimester and a UA Doppler > 95th percentile did not have a significantly increased risk of APOs (aOR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.49–1.58). Although UA Doppler and fetal growth velocity are established predictors for distinguishing late‑onset FGR from constitutionally small fetuses [37], and Schreiber et al. demonstrated that reduced growth velocity correlates with higher UA‑PI and lower cerebroplacental ratio (CPR) [38], the clinical utility of combining these markers in AGA fetuses with normal EFW remains uncertain. Indeed, Sovio et al. reported that neither Doppler parameters nor growth velocity were independently associated with the occurrence of the primary adverse outcomes when EFW exceeded the 10th percentile [33]. A key negative finding from our study is that, even when both a substantial decline in growth trajectory and abnormal UA Doppler were present, AGA fetuses did not show an increased risk of adverse outcomes. A potential explanation for this null finding is the evaluation of the growth velocity and Doppler parameters at diagnosis rather than close to delivery, which may not capture acute hemodynamic deterioration immediately preceding adverse events. Clinicians should interpret the findings of the present study with caution and remain attentive to AGA fetuses presenting with Doppler abnormalities or declining growth trajectories.

The main limitation is the retrospective study design, and heterogeneity in clinical management may have arisen from the retrospective identification of some FGR cases during data analysis. And a methodological limitation of this study is the absence of routine middle cerebral artery (MCA) and cerebroplacental ratio (CPR) Doppler measurements, as these parameters were not systematically assessed in our retrospective cohort. This may partially account for the relatively low proportion of cases classified into Group 2. Additionally, the mean BMI in this regional cohort ranged between 20 and 22 kg/m², which may limit the generalizability of our results to populations with a higher prevalence of obesity. Owing to the small number of participants with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m² (n = 11), we were unable to perform subgroup analyses stratified by obesity. Therefore, our findings may not be directly applicable to obese populations. Furthermore, our study did not include certain neonatal morbidity indicators, such as NICU admission rates and length of hospital stay. During part of the study period, the NICU also served as a regular newborn nursery, which limited the ability of these parameters to accurately reflect neonatal morbidity severity.

Conclusions

This real-world study reports the risk of APOs in different subgroups of late-onset FGR, thereby providing further evidence-based guidance for its diagnosis and management. The risk of adverse perinatal outcomes varied substantially across Group 1–4, underscoring the need for further investigation into risk-stratified management protocols. In particular, future studies should focus on refining the threshold for intervention in Group 1 (EFW or AC < 3rd percentile). Further high‑quality studies are needed to better understand the implications of Doppler abnormalities and declining growth percentiles in AGA fetuses. Our results are highly relevant for antenatal counseling. Moreover, further studies are needed to develop optimized management protocols for the different types of late-onset FGR.
