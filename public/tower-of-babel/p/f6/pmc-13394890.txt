Predictive Value of Composite Inflammatory Markers for Stroke Prognosis: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Bing Wu, Jie‐Jie Li, Jing Jing, Jin‐Xi Lin, Xia Meng, Yong Jiang, Yong‐Jun Wang, Hong‐Qiu Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNovel composite inflammatory markers' role in stroke prognosis is understudied, and the best predictor is unclear, requiring further exploration.ObjectivesThis study aimed to systematically evaluate the associations of 6 novel composite inflammatory markers on stroke prognosis.MethodsBased on the Third China National Stroke Registry (CNSR‐III), we analyzed 14,476 participants aged ≥ 18 years with ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack. Novel composite inflammatory markers were calculated, including neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), and pan‐immune inflammation value (PIV). Outcomes at 1 year included stroke recurrence (primary outcome) and ischemic stroke, composite vascular events, mortality, and poor functional outcome (mRS 2–6) as secondary outcomes. Multivariable Cox or logistic regression analyses were performed to estimate the association of composite inflammatory markers with these outcomes. XGBoost, IDI, and NRI were utilized to evaluate predictive performance. Additionally, subgroup analyses and sensitivity tests were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings.ResultsThere were 1403 (9.7%), 1300 (9.0%), 1484 (10.3), 483 (3.3%) and 2865 (21.7%) patients with recurrent stroke, ischemic stroke, composite vascular events, mortality, and poor functional outcome within 1 year. SII, PIV, PLR, NLR, SIRI, and MLR were positively associated with stroke recurrence, with hazard ratios (95% CIs) of highest‐quartile vs. lowest‐quartile 1.39 (1.20, 1.61), 1.40 (1.21, 1.62), 1.16 (1.00, 1.34), 1.42 (1.22, 1.66), 1.38 (1.18, 1.61) and 1.18 (1.02, 1.37), respectively. A graded increase in stroke risk across quartiles was observed (Log‐rank p < 0.05). All six markers were positively associated with ischemic stroke, composite vascular events, mortality, and poor functional outcome. SIRI demonstrated better predictive performance [IDI: 0.004 (0.002, 0.007); NRI: 0.082 (0.052, 0.107)] than the other five composite inflammatory markers for stroke recurrence. Among eight XGBoost models, the AUC increased from 0.598 (base) to 0.610 (adding NIHSS/mRS), and further to 0.624 with the addition of SIRI, which was the highest.ConclusionsNovel composite inflammatory markers were significantly associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes in stroke patients. When accounting for the influence of inflammatory markers, SIRI demonstrated stronger predictive ability for stroke recurrence than the other markers.

Introduction

The 2021 Global Burden of Disease Study reports that stroke is the second leading cause of death worldwide and the primary cause of death and disability among adults in China; in 2021, China recorded 26.3 million stroke cases, 2.59 million deaths, and 53.19 million disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) [1, 2]. Studies have shown that immune status and inflammatory mechanisms are involved throughout the pathophysiological process of ischemic stroke, not only driving the progression of brain injury but also being closely associated with stroke recurrence and poor prognosis [3, 4, 5].

The immune system plays a dual role in the pathogenesis of stroke: during the acute phase, inflammatory responses can increase blood–brain barrier permeability, exacerbate oxidative stress, and lead to neuronal damage [6, 7, 8]; whereas in the subacute and chronic phases, appropriate immune regulation contributes to neural repair, angiogenesis, and clearance of necrotic tissue. Given this dual role, inflammatory mediators are not only involved in the disease process but also hold potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets [9, 10].

Commonly used inflammation‐related indicators in clinical settings include interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hs‐CRP), neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, which can, to some extent, reflect the body's inflammatory and immune status [11]. To enhance the predictive efficacy of inflammatory markers, recent studies have attempted to integrate multiple indicators, proposing a series of composite inflammatory markers such as the neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune‐inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), and pan‐immune inflammation value (PIV) [12]. These novel indices offer the advantages of being easily obtainable and cost‐effective, enabling a comprehensive assessment of the body's immune balance and inflammatory response intensity [13, 14].

Previous studies had explored the associations of composite inflammatory markers with diabetes, cardiovascular disease, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, malignancies, and all‐cause mortality [15, 16, 17, 18]. However, research specifically focusing on stroke outcomes such as recurrence remains relatively limited. One study on acute ischemic stroke patients treated with intravenous thrombolysis found that a higher PIV was independently associated with poor 3‐month outcomes, with predictive ability comparable to traditional markers such as PLR, NLR, and SII [19]. Another study on the same patient population demonstrated that higher SIRI and SII were also independently associated with poor 90‐day outcomes [20].

Although previous studies have explored the associations of composite inflammatory indices with stroke recurrence, several gaps remain. First, most studies had focused on only a few composite inflammatory markers, lacking a systematic comparison of various novel markers, including PIV and SIRI. Second, regarding outcome measures, previous research had primarily concentrated on short‐term outcomes (e.g., 90‐day events), with less attention paid to long‐term outcomes such as 1‐year stroke recurrence, composite vascular events, and mortality. Third, few studies had investigated the predictive value of six composite inflammatory indices for stroke recurrence. To address these limitations, the present study uses a large, national, prospective cohort (CNSR‐III) to systematically evaluate the associations of six novel composite inflammatory markers (SII, PIV, PLR, NLR, SIRI, MLR) with 1‐year stroke recurrence, composite vascular events, mortality, and poor functional outcomes. Furthermore, we will systematically compare the predictive value of these inflammatory markers for 1‐year stroke recurrence, aiming to provide evidence for identifying the optimal composite inflammatory biomarker for predicting stroke recurrence.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The design of the Third China National Stroke Registry (CNSR‐III) has been previously described in detail [21]. Briefly, the CNSR‐III was a nationwide prospective registry for patients with acute ischemic stroke or TIA presented to hospitals between August 2015 and March 2018 in China. Participants were consecutively enrolled if meeting the following criteria: (1) > 18 years old, (2) diagnosis of ischemic stroke or TIA within 7 days, and (3) informed consent from participant or legally authorized representative. Patients were included from 201 study sites. Patients were followed up at 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year.

After excluding participants with missing baseline data on absolute values of neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets, and monocytes (n = 331), as well as those lacking 1‐year follow‐up prognostic outcomes (n = 359), a total of 14,476 participants were ultimately included in the study (Figure S1). The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (Approval number: KY2015‐001‐09). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Composite Inflammatory Markers Definition

In this study, inflammatory biomarkers were calculated from complete blood count (CBC) testing. Participants fasted for 9 h before venous blood collection. Samples were analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer (Sysmex XN‐1000) to obtain absolute counts (×109 cells/L) of neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, and platelets. Based on these counts, the following indices were calculated: systemic immune‐inflammation index (SIRI) [22], systemic immune‐inflammatory index (SII) [23], pan‐immune inflammation value (PIV) [24], neutrophil‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (NLR) [25], platelet‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (PLR) [26], and monocyte‐to‐lymphocyte ratio (MLR) [27], using the formulas below: SIRI=neutrophil count×monocyte countlymphocyte count SII=neutrophil count×platelet countlymphocyte count PIV=neutrophil count×monocyte count×platelet countlymphocyte count NLR=neutrophil countlymphocyte count PLR=platelet countlymphocyte count MLR=monocyte countlymphocyte count

Follow‐Up and Prognosis Assessment

The primary outcome was the recurrence of stroke within 1 year, which was determined as a new‐onset focal neurological deficit resulting from a cerebral ischemic or hemorrhagic event, with confirmation via computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging. The secondary outcomes included ischemic stroke, composite vascular events (encompassing recurrent stroke, myocardial infarction, and vascular death), all‐cause mortality within 1 year, and a poor functional outcome, defined as a modified Rankin Scale score of 2 to 6 at the 12‐month follow‐up [28].

Covariates

Covariates were collected by trained research staff through face‐to‐face interviews or extraction from electronic medical records. Age and admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score were included as continuous variables. The following variables were included as categorical variables: sex (male/female); smoking history (yes/no); prior history of stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, myocardial infarction, peripheral arterial disease, and other heart diseases (yes/no for each); TOAST classification (1 = large‐artery atherosclerosis, 2 = cardioembolism, 3 = small‐vessel occlusion, 4 = other determined etiology, 5 = undetermined etiology) [29]; and pre‐stroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score (ranging from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater disability).

Statistical Analysis

We compared data from patients with and without stroke within 1 year. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, depending on data distribution, and were compared using the t‐test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and compared using the Chi‐square test.

The composite inflammatory markers were categorized into quartiles based on their 25th (P25), 50th (P50), and 75th (P75) percentiles, with the first quartile (Q1) designated as the reference group. Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to estimate the associations of these markers with the risks of stroke events, ischemic stroke, composite vascular events, and all‐cause mortality within 1 year after onset. We excluded participants with pre‐stroke mRS ≥ 2 and applied multiple logistic regression analyses to explore the associations of six inflammatory markers with poor functional outcome at 1 year. To evaluate the robustness of these associations, three adjustment models were constructed: Model 1 included no covariate adjustment. Model 2 adjusted for eight variables recommended by the Essen Stroke Risk Score (ESRS), namely age, history of hypertension, history of diabetes mellitus, history of myocardial infarction, history of other cardiovascular diseases, history of peripheral arterial disease, history of stroke/TIA, and smoking status [30]. Model 3 further adjusted for four additional strongly associated variables on top of Model 2: admission NIHSS score, pre‐stroke mRS score, gender, and TOAST classification. Results are presented as adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for Cox models, odds ratios (ORs) for logistic models, and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We employed Kaplan–Meier curves with the log‐rank test to compare the 1‐year cumulative risk of stroke recurrence across quartile groups of the inflammatory markers. We fitted restricted cubic splines (RCS) with knots at the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles to model the dose–response relationship with stroke risk.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and Concordance index (C‐index) were used to quantify the discriminative accuracy of the 6 novel inflammatory biomarkers for incident stroke during follow‐up. Net reclassification index (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) index were also employed to further assess the incremental predictive utility compared to the basic models. To evaluate the predictive value of inflammatory markers for 1‐year stroke recurrence in patients with stroke and TIA, we constructed eight predictive models using the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) machine learning algorithm. The dataset was randomly split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). All models shared a set of core predictors, including age, history of hypertension, history of diabetes mellitus, history of myocardial infarction, history of other cardiovascular diseases, history of peripheral arterial disease, history of stroke/TIA, smoking status, gender, and TOAST classification. Subsequently, admission NIHSS and pre‐stroke mRS score were added, and then six composite inflammatory markers (PIV, MLR, NLR, PLR, SII, SIRI) were individually incorporated to form the following eight models: Model 1 (core predictors only), Model 2 (core predictors + admission NIHSS + pre‐stroke mRS score), and Models 3–8 (Model 2 plus one inflammatory marker each). The XGBoost models were trained on the training set, with hyperparameters tuned via cross‐validation, and their predictive performance.

To evaluate potential effect modifiers, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), sex, history of stroke, admission NIHSS score (≤ 5 vs. > 5), and TOAST classification (large artery atherosclerosis, cardioembolism, small vessel occlusion, other determined or undetermined etiology). A sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding patients with TIA. Cox proportional hazards regression or logistic regression models were used to assess the associations between the composite inflammatory markers and outcomes within 1 year after onset, including stroke events, ischemic stroke, composite vascular events, all‐cause mortality, and poor functional outcome. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC), with a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Study Participants

This study included 14,476 participants (mean age 62.23 ± 11.27 years). Median levels (IQR) of inflammatory markers were: SII 536.58 (366.93–814.54), PIV 224.29 (136.98–376.77), PLR 123.13 (94.82–161.86), NLR 2.53 (1.83–3.71), SIRI 1.07 (0.69–1.72), and MLR 0.24 (0.18–0.33). Participants with stroke recurrence were older and had significantly higher SII, PIV, NLR, SIRI, and MLR levels versus those without recurrence. Large artery atherosclerosis and cardioembolism were more prevalent in the recurrent stroke group by TOAST classification (Table 1).

A consistent pattern of associations was observed for 1‐year ischemic stroke, composite cardiovascular events, and all‐cause mortality. All six inflammatory markers (SII, PIV, PLR, NLR, SIRI, and MLR) showed positive associations with the risk of 1‐year ischemic stroke and 1‐year all‐cause mortality, while five of these (SII, PIV, NLR, SIRI, and MLR) were positively associated with the risk of 1‐year composite cardiovascular events (Table S1).

All six composite inflammatory markers were positively associated with the risk of poor functional outcome at 1 year in patients with pre‐stroke mRS score < 2. Compared with the Q1 group, the adjusted odd ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals for the Q4 group were as follows: SII 1.71 (1.50, 1.94), PIV 1.79 (1.57, 2.04), PLR 1.43 (1.25, 1.62), NLR 1.81 (1.58, 2.07), SIRI 1.95 (1.70, 2.23), and MLR 1.70 (1.49, 1.95) (Table S2).

The Log‐rank test revealed a significant difference in stroke incidence rates among the quartiles (p < 0.05), indicating a graded increase in stroke risk with higher inflammatory marker levels.

Incremental Predictive Value of Novel Composite Inflammatory Markers

Figure 2 presents the ROC curves and AUC values for eight XGBoost models. The base model, which included only core predictors, achieved an AUC of 0.598. After adding admission NIHSS and pre‐stroke mRS score to the base model, the AUC increased to 0.610. Further incorporation of different inflammatory markers (MLR, NLR, PIV, PLR, SII, SIRI) resulted in AUCs ranging from 0.608 to 0.624. Among these, the model incorporating SIRI (Base + NIHSS + mRS + SIRI) yielded the highest AUC of 0.624.

The six inflammatory models were each constructed by adding one inflammatory marker to the enhanced model: PIV, MLR, NLR, PLR, SII, or SIRI.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analysis

Subgroup analysis indicated that the associations between the six inflammatory markers and 1‐year stroke recurrence were numerically stronger in older patients (≥ 65 years), women, and the cardioembolism subgroup. However, none of the interaction p‐values reached statistical significance. Therefore, these observed subgroup differences should be interpreted with caution, and further validation in future studies with larger sample sizes is warranted (Figure 3). Sensitivity analysis excluding TIA patients confirmed the robustness of the primary results, showing slightly strengthened associations for all indicators (Table S3).

Discussion

This large‐scale prospective cohort study demonstrates that elevated levels of novel composite inflammatory markers are robustly associated with an increased risk of 1‐year adverse outcomes in ischemic stroke patients, including stroke recurrence, ischemic stroke, composite vascular events, all‐cause mortality, and poor functional outcomes. Among the six inflammatory markers evaluated, SIRI demonstrated superior performance in predicting stroke prognosis, suggesting its utility as a key prognostic biomarker and potential targets for refining risk stratification and guiding targeted preventive strategies.

Previous studies have mainly focused on the association between stroke and basic inflammatory markers such as NLR, MLR, and PLR, while research on SIRI, PIV, and SII has been relatively limited. Especially, there is a lack of research that includes both SIRI and PIV and directly compares their predictive performance. This study systematically evaluated and compared the association and predictive efficacy of six inflammatory biomarkers with stroke prognosis in a large Chinese population cohort, revealing that SIRI has a higher predictive value compared to other biomarkers.

Multiple studies support the importance of SIRI in stroke prognosis assessment. For example, a prospective cohort study of acute ischemic stroke patients receiving intravenous thrombolysis reported that SIRI and SII were associated with 90‐day poor outcomes [20]; however, that study did not include PIV. Another retrospective study also suggested that SIRI outperformed NLR, PLR, and the lymphocyte‐to‐monocyte ratio (LMR) in predictive ability [31], yet again did not include PIV. Furthermore, a prospective study based on the general U.S. population found that dynamic changes in SII and SIRI were significantly associated with cardiovascular disease risk [32], but PIV was not included in the analysis. PIV, as an emerging comprehensive inflammatory marker, has gradually gained attention in recent years and has demonstrated predictive value in various diseases; however, research in the field of ischemic stroke remains relatively limited [33, 34]. Wang et al. compared the prognostic value of PIV, SII, NLR, and PLR in stroke patients receiving thrombolysis and found that all these inflammatory parameters were associated with poor outcomes [19]. Huang et al. reported that in patients with non‐traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage, PIV ≥ 1362.45 was independently associated with 90‐day all‐cause mortality [35]. A single‐center retrospective study of ischemic stroke patients in the intensive care unit further found that PIV excelled in predicting in‐hospital mortality, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) as high as 0.921, significantly outperforming SII [36]. In addition, a large prospective cohort study based on the general U.S. adult population suggested that elevated PIV was independently associated with risks of all‐cause mortality and mortality from cardiovascular disease, cancer, and diabetes [37].

Notably, a single‐center retrospective study of acute ischemic stroke patients undergoing mechanical thrombectomy reported that elevated PIV was independently associated with 3‐month poor functional outcomes, and its predictive performance surpassed that of other inflammatory markers including SIRI—a finding that differs from the results of the present study [38]. However, a retrospective study of stroke patients receiving intravenous thrombolysis indicated that SII and NLR had the strongest predictive value, while PIV had the weakest predictive power [39]. These inconsistent results suggest that the predictive performance of inflammatory markers may vary depending on the study population, intervention type, and follow‐up duration, warranting further in‐depth exploration in future studies.

From a pathophysiological perspective, the role of systemic inflammatory markers in cerebrovascular events is well‐supported and can be understood through three main mechanisms: First, following acute ischemic cerebral injury, molecules released by necrotic cells activate immune cells within the central nervous system. Second, neutrophils, as the first responders, rapidly infiltrate the ischemic brain, releasing various pro‐inflammatory mediators including matrix metalloproteinases, which exacerbate the intracerebral inflammatory response; these cells aggregate extensively in the infarct core and penumbra, contributing to blood–brain barrier disruption, brain edema, and further damage to endothelial cell membranes and the basement membrane via released inflammatory factors [3, 40]. Third, activated microglia and infiltrating circulating leukocytes jointly regulate the inflammatory response in the perilesional area, releasing a large number of inflammatory mediators that collectively influence neural damage and repair processes [4, 41]. Previous studies have confirmed the close association between neutrophils and stroke severity as well as functional outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke [3, 6].

Regarding SIRI, this index combines neutrophils, monocytes, and lymphocytes, which can more comprehensively represent the inflammatory response and immune status in the body. Monocytes migrate and infiltrate early after stroke onset, differentiating into macrophages under the influence of factors such as OX‐LDL, phagocytosing low‐density lipoproteins to form foam cells, and releasing various inflammatory substances [42]. Furthermore, activated monocytes secrete vascular endothelial growth factor, increasing vascular permeability and disrupting the blood–brain barrier [43]. The findings of this study further confirm that SIRI, in reflecting systemic inflammatory status and predicting prognosis, outperforms peripheral blood cell ratios such as NLR, LMR, and PLR, and serves as an independent predictor of 1‐year adverse outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke.

In this study, we found that the base model including only core covariates achieved an AUC of 0.598 for predicting stroke recurrence. After adding NIHSS and mRS scores, the AUC increased slightly to 0.610. Further incorporation of SIRI raised the AUC to 0.624, yielding a more pronounced performance gain than adding clinical functional scores alone. This finding suggests that, within a prediction framework already containing traditional core covariates and clinical functional indicators, SIRI still provides additional discriminative information, thereby further enhancing the predictive ability of traditional markers for stroke recurrence and offering a new supplementary dimension for risk stratification. The clinical significance of these inflammatory markers lies primarily in two aspects: first, at the mechanistic level, they deepen our understanding of the inflammatory immune regulatory mechanisms underlying stroke recurrence, providing clinical evidence for the potential role of inflammatory pathways in secondary stroke prevention; second, at the application level, they can serve as auxiliary complementary indicators to existing risk assessment tools, helping to optimize risk stratification and identify potentially high‐risk populations, rather than replacing current well‐established risk assessment systems or serving as independent clinical decision‐making criteria.

The strengths of this study are as follows. First, as a national, representative prospective cohort study based on the Chinese stroke population, its findings have good generalizability to Chinese stroke patients. Second, this study is among the largest in the field to date, ensuring high reliability of the results. Moreover, this study is the first to systematically assess the associations of six composite inflammatory markers with stroke recurrence, ischemic stroke, composite vascular events, mortality, and poor functional outcome within 1 year. Third, through predictive value analysis, we found that SIRI outperforms the other five inflammatory markers in predicting stroke recurrence, providing evidence for future prediction of stroke recurrence and the selection of inflammatory markers.

This study also has several limitations. Firstly, this is a prospective cohort analysis based on stroke registration data, rather than a multicenter clinical trial. Second, some potential confounding factors (such as genetic background) were not included in the statistical model, which might have influenced the results. Most importantly, composite inflammation markers may change dynamically during hospitalization; future research should aim to dynamically monitor these indicators throughout the clinical course rather than relying solely on a single measurement at admission.

Conclusion

This study, conducted in a Chinese stroke population, demonstrated that composite inflammatory markers were significantly and positively associated with 1‐year stroke recurrence, ischemic stroke, all‐cause mortality, composite vascular events, and poor functional outcomes. Predictive analyses further revealed that SIRI exhibited superior predictive performance compared with other inflammatory markers. This study provides robust evidence for the relevance of novel composite inflammatory markers to stroke prognosis in the Chinese population, suggesting that SIRI may serve as a potential biomarker for assessing stroke prognosis. Future studies are warranted to validate these findings and to explore the practical value of these inflammatory markers in different clinical settings.
