A Novel Decomposition‐Based Growth Model for Simulating Stress‐Modulated Spinal Growth
Authors: Serhat Onur Çakmak, Ercan Gürses
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnderstanding the mechanobiological mechanisms of spinal growth is essential for modeling progressive deformities, particularly adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS). In this study, we present a novel finite element methodology for simulating mechanobiological growth governed by the Hueter‐Volkmann law. The approach is developed in the large deformation framework and uses the Hueter‐Volkmann law for the first time in a decomposition‐based framework in which the growth is incorporated into the constitutive model via multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient. For comparison purposes, we also implement the sequential method widely used in the literature. Although both approaches are based on the Hueter‐Volkmann law, which relates the normal compressive stress on the growth plate to local growth inhibition, they differ significantly in how this principle is integrated into the finite element framework. Unlike the sequential method, which requires successive growth computation and mechanical solution steps, along with repeated updates of the growth direction, the proposed decomposition‐based approach solves stress‐induced deformations and growth simultaneously. It is formulated in the Lagrangian configuration, eliminating the need to update the growth direction throughout the simulation. The proposed formulation not only offers superior numerical stability but also exhibits lower computational complexity compared to the sequential method, particularly under large deformations. The method is evaluated using a finite element model of a simplified Functional Spinal Unit (FSU) under symmetric and asymmetric compressive loading over a two‐year growth period. While both approaches produce comparable wedge angle progression, the decomposition‐based formulation, which more realistically resembles the actual growth process, results in smoother deformations, less element distortion, and substantially reduced computational cost. Additionally, a robust wedge angle calculation technique is introduced using least plane fitting to full endplate nodes of the vertebral bodies, improving geometric accuracy and reducing mesh sensitivity. Together, these features establish a reliable and efficient framework for long‐term spinal growth simulations and offer a promising foundation for future patient‐specific modeling and clinical applications.Keywords: finite element method (FEM), functional spinal unit (FSU), Hueter–Volkmann law, mechanobiological growth, wedge angle

Introduction

Scoliosis is fundamentally defined as a spinal deformity characterized by lateral curvature. One of the most prevalent forms of scoliosis in adolescents is known as adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS), which affects more than 2.5% of the general population [1], and there is still no clear explanation for the etiology of the condition [2, 3]. However, it is widely acknowledged that a key factor that contributes to the progression of spinal deformity is the Hueter–Volkmann principle [4, 5, 6]. This principle explains how deviations from normal physiological loading, such as excessive or asymmetric mechanical stresses on epiphyseal growth plates, can disrupt normal growth patterns. Specifically, it states that increased compressive stress inhibits local growth, while reduced stress promotes it. Applied to the pathomechanism of scoliosis, the Hueter–Volkmann principle suggests that asymmetric loading of the vertebral bodies contributes to the progression of abnormal spinal curvature. This concept is also supported by frequent clinical observations of localized vertebral deformities, often appearing as wedging in scoliotic spines [7, 8, 9]. These clinical and biomechanical findings point to the crucial role of growth modulation in the development and progression of scoliosis. Asymmetric mechanical loading not only deforms vertebral geometry but also alters local growth dynamics. This interaction between mechanical forces and bone growth forms the basis of mechanobiological growth.

In recent years, several studies have employed finite element (FE) modeling to simulate mechanobiological growth processes in the functional spinal unit (FSU) and spine, aiming to better understand the biomechanical basis of AIS progression. In the literature, the growth process is integrated into finite element models using the Hueter–Volkmann law, and its simulation is carried out through a stepwise incremental procedure. This procedure generally includes two main phases: the loading step and the growth step. In the loading step, stresses are computed and converted to thermal loads while growth is temporarily frozen. In the subsequent growth step, external mechanical loads are removed, and growth occurs in the form of thermal strains. This sequential strategy allows the growth process to be modeled in a relatively simple manner. This approach has been adopted in several studies that simulate mechanobiological growth in the spine. For instance, Driscoll et al. [10] employed this method to investigate the effect of concave–convex stress asymmetry on vertebral deformation. Lin et al. [11] compared two mechanobiological growth models using a similar stepwise framework. Aubin et al. [12] utilized this strategy to simulate the biomechanical effects of anterior and costo‐vertebral tethering techniques for fusionless scoliosis treatment. Villemure et al. [13] applied the procedure to test different pathogenesis hypotheses in AIS. In a related context, Guy and Aubin [14] investigated growth modulation in the spine during brace treatment of AIS using a finite element simulation. D'Andrea et al. [15] created patient‐specific osteo‐ligamentous finite element models of the spine, ribcage, and pelvis and considered region‐specific, stress‐modulated growth to validate simulated curve progression with patient clinical angle measurements. Across all of these studies, the sequential application of loading and growth steps, which is based on the Hueter–Volkmann principle, has provided a relatively simple framework for simulating asymmetric vertebral development in AIS using commercial finite element software. However, this procedure requires significant computational resources and extensive preprocessing efforts, as it involves node redefinition and restarting the analysis at each growth cycle. More importantly, it does not properly reflect the physical growth process, which happens simultaneously, since it models loading and growth in a successive manner. The implementation details of this method are described in Section 2.1.

The main objective of this paper is to propose a more robust, general, and physical methodology for implementing the Hueter–Volkmann principle within a finite strain framework based on the concept of incompatible growth configurations. This method utilizes the well‐established concept of multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient into elastic and growth components and is referred to herein as the decomposition‐based method. It has been used in the literature as a reliable approach for modeling growth in various biomechanical systems. For example, Göktepe et al. [16] applied this approach to simulate cardiac growth, cardiac dilation, and ventricular wall thickening. Tepole et al. [17] employed it in the context of skin tissue expansion, modeling soft tissue growth during reconstructive surgery. Similarly, Himpel et al. [18] developed a computational framework for isotropic multiplicative growth, demonstrating its utility in general biological growth processes. Xue et al. [19] applied the decomposition‐based method to model avascular tumor growth, incorporating biochemical and poroelastic effects into a coupled mechanobiological framework. These studies underscore the versatility and robustness of the decomposition‐based method in capturing complex growth phenomena across different biological tissues. However, to the best of our knowledge, there have been no studies in which pediatric spine growth is simulated using the concept of incompatible growth configurations. All previous computational works on pediatric spine growth, which are based on the Hueter–Volkmann law, are variants of the sequential method and use thermal strains with anisotropic thermal expansion coefficients to simulate growth. In this study, we propose a novel methodology that applies the decomposition‐based approach to simulate mechanobiological growth in the adolescent spine, offering a new computational framework to evaluate the biomechanical progression of growth in vertebral growth plates governed by the Hueter–Volkmann law.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we first present the sequential mechanobiological growth methodology commonly used in the literature to implement the Hueter‐Volkmann principle, followed by our proposed novel decomposition‐based methodology, which is also based on the Hueter‐Volkmann principle but implemented through a different modeling strategy. Furthermore, the details of the finite element model and the proposed wedge angle calculation method are described in this section. In Section 3, the results of both methods are compared, and their respective advantages are discussed. Finally, conclusions are provided in Section 4.

Methodology

Mechanical modulation of bone growth refers to the phenomenon where abnormal mechanical loading alters growth patterns, leading to deformities. This phenomenon has important implications for the progression of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis.

Mechanical modulation of bone growth, including longitudinal growth of vertebral bodies, is commonly explained by the Hueter–Volkmann law (e.g., [20, 21, 22, 23]). This law, originally proposed by Hueter and later expanded by Volkmann [24], states that sustained compressive loading inhibits growth in immature bone, whereas reduced loading promotes growth acceleration. However, this ‘law’ reflects complex physiological processes whose underlying mechanisms remain only partially understood. This is particularly evident in the case of the Hueter‐Volkmann principle, which specifically addresses load‐induced changes in the longitudinal growth of bone. Sequential Method

In the sequential method, the growth process is simulated using a stepwise incremental procedure, with each cycle representing 1 month, similar to approaches developed in the literature (e.g., [11, 13, 25]). This approach, illustrated in Figure 1a, is here referred to as the sequential method and consists of four main steps. First, boundary conditions are defined and external loads are applied to the FSU model. Second, growth modulation rates induced by mechanical loading are calculated based on the stress distribution within the load sensitive area, in accordance with the Hueter‐Volkmann law. Third, the external loads previously applied to the FSU model are removed, and the growth modulations calculated for each element within the load sensitive region are directly mapped onto the corresponding underlying growth area elements shown in Figure 1b as equivalent unidirectional thermal loads. For each growth area, the local direction of these loads is individually determined by fitting an average plane to the bottom surface nodes using a least squares method, and the normal vector of this plane is defined as the local growth direction. These thermal loads are then applied along their respective directions to simulate geometric changes, resulting in the expansion of the solid elements in the growth direction within the growth region and consequently altering the overall geometry of the model. Finally, the wedge angle is calculated, and the FSU geometry is updated to reflect the growth through node redefinition. In this way, the mesh is updated without introducing any residual growth‐induced stresses, providing a stress‐free configuration for the first step of the next cycle in the FSU model.

The implementation of this method is carried out as follows:

Stresses and deformations within the FSU are evaluated using ABAQUS, while the magnitude and direction of growth are calculated in MATLAB. The direction of growth is determined using the coordinates of all nodes on the bottom surface of each growth region. The normal vector of the best fit plane passing through these points is obtained using a least squares procedure, which is described in detail in Section 2.4. Notably, the same least squares‐based plane fitting is also used in the post‐processing step to compute the coronal wedge angle from the superior and inferior endplate surfaces, where the wedge angle is evaluated in MATLAB.

The computed growth modulation and growth direction are incorporated into ABAQUS by applying anisotropic thermal expansion to the elements within the growth areas. In this framework, the temperature field is defined based on the computed growth modulation depending on the normal stress in the load‐sensitive area.

The updated (grown) model is then used as the new mesh for the subsequent iteration, ensuring that no residual growth‐induced stresses accumulated. In the sequential workflow, each cycle consists of a loading analysis followed by a separate growth analysis. The structure is first loaded to obtain the stress measure required by the growth law, after which the analysis is terminated and the corresponding unloaded configuration is taken as the starting point for the growth step. The growth step is then applied in a separate analysis without transferring stresses from the loading step, so the growth step begins from a stress‐free state by construction. The resulting nodal coordinates of the grown configuration are used to generate a new input file for the updated geometry, on which the next loading analysis is performed. Since the model is not remeshed between iterations, the growth representation was inherently constrained by the original number of elements in the growth plates and throughout the FSU region.

Decomposition‐Based Method

The load‐sensitive area is highly responsive to mechanical influences, as it encompasses the reserve, proliferative, and part of the hypertrophic zones [27, 28]. In contrast, the growth area physiologically corresponds to the remaining portion of the hypertrophic zone and the degenerated cartilage regions, where chondrocyte apoptosis and matrix calcification occur.

In the sequential method, stress values from the load‐sensitive area are used to compute modulated growth rates. Although this physiological distinction is meaningful, no significant difference is observed between the stress distributions in the growth area and the load‐sensitive areas, as discussed in detail and shown in Appendix A. Therefore, in the decomposition‐based method, we specifically propose using stress values extracted directly from the growth region, rather than from the load‐sensitive area in the Hueter‐Volkmann equations. In this framework, stress is defined as a spatially local field variable, evaluated at the integration points of each element, thereby enabling a pointwise local coupling between mechanical stimulus and growth response.

Building on this framework, a mechanobiological growth algorithm is developed to establish the mathematical relationship between mechanical stimuli and growth. To clarify the time integration strategy, the proposed decomposition‐based approach does not advance growth through restart‐based cycles with explicit geometry updates, as commonly done in sequential growth simulations (e.g., monthly cycles). Instead, the full two‐year growth process is computed in a single continuous FE analysis using incremental time stepping. At each time increment, the growth part of the deformation gradient, Fg in (5), is updated using the stress state from the previous increment, so growth evolves within the same simulation without intermediate restarts or geometric reconstruction. Based on a time increment sensitivity study, the two‐year period is discretized into 200 uniform time increments (i.e., 100 increments per year), which yields converged predictions and is used in all reported simulations.

Recall that in the proposed model, the growth direction is defined in the Lagrangian (reference) configuration. In the proposed decomposition‐based formulation, the growth direction is defined once in the reference configuration as the unit normal to the initial growth plate surface, denoted by Ng. The growth law is embedded directly in the growth deformation tensor Fg in (5), so no explicit directional update is required. When quantities are interpreted in the current configuration, the effective orientation associated with Ng follows automatically from the deformation mapping (i.e., it is obtained by a kinematic push forward operation using the deformation gradient), rather than by re‐estimating the growth plate plane. This differs from the sequential method, where explicit geometry updates between cycles necessitate recomputing the growth plate plane and its normal (via a best‐fit plane) at the beginning of each growth step to define the updated growth direction in the new configuration.

As a result, fixing the growth direction throughout the analysis provides significant advantages in terms of computational efficiency and ease of implementation. In addition to this advantage, the decomposition‐based method also simplifies the post‐processing steps. In the sequential method, wedge angle evaluations require continuous data exchange between MATLAB and ABAQUS during each iteration, significantly increasing the computational cost. In contrast, in the decomposition‐based method, the wedge angle is calculated only after the entire simulation is completed, eliminating the need for iterative data transfer and thus further improving computational efficiency. This contributes to a more streamlined and computationally efficient simulation workflow.

Finite Element Models

Simplified and idealized geometries play a crucial role in developing mechanobiological algorithms and in exploring how specific stimuli or factors influence growth within a theoretical framework. For instance, geometric simplifications such as a cylinder for blood vessels [33], a sphere for tumors [34], or idealized bone morphologies [35, 36] are commonly employed. Although such geometries do not accurately represent real anatomical structures, they are valuable for parametric studies, enabling researchers to assess sensitivity to different factors and investigate generalized mechanobiological behavior. A similar modeling strategy is frequently observed in scoliosis studies, where simplified geometries are employed to examine complex spinal growth mechanisms (e.g., [10, 25, 37]).

In this study, a simplified cylindrical functional spinal unit (FSU) is used for modeling. The FSU is the smallest motion segment of the spine that retains the biomechanical characteristics of the entire spinal column. It consists of two adjacent vertebrae, the intervertebral disc, and the ligaments connecting them, but excludes other tissues such as muscles [38]. A finite element model (FEM) of FSU is developed, comprising two vertebral bodies and an intervertebral disc, as illustrated in Figure 3. A Cartesian coordinate system is defined with its origin located at the centroid of the inferior growth plate of the bottom vertebral body. The x‐axis aligns with the anterior–posterior direction, the y‐axis represents the lateral direction, and the z‐axis corresponds to the cranial–caudal anatomical direction (i.e., the longitudinal axis of the spine). To provide anatomical context and facilitate interpretation, the model in Figure 3 is color‐coded to distinguish different anatomical regions of the FSU, each assigned distinct geometric properties. According to Gilad and Nissan [40], the L4 vertebral body diameter in adult males (aged 20–38) is approximately 35 mm. However, to better reflect the skeletally immature spine, we additionally considered pediatric MRI morphometric measurements reported by Zhang et al. [41], who quantified vertebral body width and depth from true transverse MRI images. In the lumbar region (L1–L5), Zhang et al. [41] reported mean values on the order of approximately 33–37 mm for width and 23–25 mm for depth across juvenile groups. Since the present work uses a simplified vertebral body representation with a cylindrical geometry, we modeled the vertebral body diameter as a representative value of 30 mm, which provides a reasonable intermediate approximation between the reported width and depth measures while maintaining geometric simplicity. The cream‐colored area corresponds to cancellous bone, while the red area indicates the cortical bone, modeled with a thickness of 0.5 mm.

The nonlinear elastic properties, highlighted in bold in Table 1, are applied to all regions of the FSU in the decomposition‐based method, except for the cancellous and the cortical bone regions of the vertebral body. These two regions are modeled using linear elastic properties, as their deformations are expected to remain small relative to the soft tissue structures (e.g., the intervertebral disc and the growth plate), and this simplification reduces computational cost. In the sequential method, nonlinear elastic properties are similarly assigned to the same regions as in the decomposition‐based approach, except for the growth area. Linear elastic properties are assigned to the growth area due to a software limitation, as ABAQUS does not permit the assignment of anisotropic thermal expansion coefficients in combination with Neo‐Hookean hyperelastic materials. In other words, hyperelastic material properties are assigned to the load‐sensitive area, mineralized area, nucleus pulposus, and annulus fibrosus regions, consistent with the FSU model used in the decomposition‐based growth simulation, as these regions are capable of undergoing large deformations due to their material characteristics.

The FSU is primarily meshed using eight‐node linear brick elements (C3D8), except for the nucleus pulposus region. Since the nucleus pulposus is modeled with a Poisson's ratio of 0.49, indicating near incompressibility, standard elements in this region could lead to volumetric locking. To mitigate this issue, hybrid elements (C3D8H) are employed in the nucleus pulposus to ensure numerical stability and accurate deformation behavior. Reduced integration linear brick elements (C3D8R) are utilized for the cancellous and cortical bone regions to enhance computational efficiency. In addition, fully integrated elements (C3D8) are assigned to the growth plate and annulus fibrosus regions to ensure accurate stress distribution, as shown in Figure 4.

Wedge Angle Calculation

The wedge angle formed in the vertebral body is a critical factor in the onset and progression of scoliosis. Wedge deformities can occur in both the coronal and sagittal planes, as shown in Figure 5a. The coronal plane deformity is typically characterized by lateral wedging of the vertebral bodies. The coronal wedge angle α of the vertebral body is calculated based on the difference between the convex and concave vertebral heights in the Y–Z (coronal) plane, as illustrated in Figure 5b. If the convex and concave vertebral heights are equal, then the corresponding wedge angle is 0°. In contrast, the wedge angle in the sagittal plane is calculated in the X–Z (sagittal) plane, and it is relevant for evaluating the angles of spinal kyphosis and lordosis. However, in this study, evaluation of the sagittal wedge angle is not necessary, as the applied asymmetric loading varies linearly only in the Y‐direction. As a result, no asymmetric growth occurs, and consequently no wedge formation develops in the sagittal plane. Note that due to simplifed geometry of FSU, where the cross section is circular and the model is axisymmetric, the distinction of sagittal and coronal planes is not really important, and the only thing that breaks the axisymmetry is the asymmetric loading.

Wedge angle of the grown vertebral body under asymmetric loading.

A proper calculation of the wedge angle is critical in finite element models, as the surface geometry of the vertebral body may exhibit waviness due to stress concentrations resulting from significant variations in material properties across different regions of the FSU. Therefore, wedge angle measurements based on a limited number of nodes of the finite element model may yield inaccurate results. To address this, a new measurement approach is proposed, which, to the best of the authors' knowledge, has not been previously implemented or explicitly documented in finite element studies. The proposed method utilizes all top and bottom surface nodes to construct two distinct average planes for the superior and inferior surfaces. These average planes are computed using a least‐squares fitting procedure, where the surface nodal coordinates are used to determine the best‐fit plane that minimizes geometric deviation, as shown in Figure 6. The normal vectors of the resulting planes, shown in yellow and blue in Figure 6, are then calculated and projected onto the Z–Y plane to determine the wedge angle in the coronal plane, which is subsequently obtained using trigonometric relationships. This formulation provides a more systematic and robust framework for the quantification of wedge angle in computational spine models.

Results and Discussion

This section compares the sequential and proposed decomposition‐based growth methods under symmetric and asymmetric loading conditions in terms of morphology, stress distribution, wedge angle progression, and computational efficiency.

Symmetric Loading

The FSU model is analyzed over a 2‐year growth period, which is consistent with the duration commonly used in previous studies, for example, [12, 13]. In the simulation, the bottom surface of the FSU is fully constrained, while a uniform compressive load of 0.52 MPa is applied to the superior surface of the FSU. This magnitude, as used in the study by Fok et al. [25], corresponds to the physiological load acting on the L3 vertebra in an adolescent weighing approximately 45 kg. To model the growth response under these loading conditions, two previously discussed mechanobiological growth methodologies are employed.

As illustrated in 7, the FSU configurations obtained at the end of the 2‐year growth simulations using the two growth methods show noticeable differences, particularly near the interface between the intervertebral disc and the load sensitive region highlighted by the red boxes. In the sequential method (Figure 7b), visible fluctuations develop along the geometry of the loading plate growth area, and these become increasingly pronounced over successive growth cycles. These irregularities are primarily related to the numerical characteristics of the sequential implementation. Specifically, the cortical bone generates a high stress gradient across the growth plate located on the intervertebral disc, as evidenced in Figure 8. The sequential method has stronger stress gradients in the growth area, as shown in Figure 8b. Since the adopted growth law directly links compressive normal stress to growth inhibition, these local stress differences produce nonuniform growth, which progressively amplifies the observed waviness. Over successive cycles, this accumulated effect artificially modifies the vertebral body morphology through localized growth irregularities.

On the other hand, the decomposition‐based method (Figure 7a) maintains a much smoother structural response throughout the simulation, without observable distortion‐related artifacts. As evidenced by Figure 8a, the stress distribution in the growth area is also markedly smoother, owing to the coupled nature of the formulation. Since growth and mechanical deformation are solved simultaneously within a single continuous analysis, local stress gradients are handled more consistently and are not repeatedly reestablished through separate cycle‐based updates. This reduces the accumulation of local numerical irregularities and suppresses the development of artificial element distortion. Consequently, the decomposition‐based framework preserves a smoother final geometry and avoids the artificial fluctuations observed in the sequential method.

The wedge angles are calculated using the method described in Section 2.4. As shown in the wedge angle–time plot in Figure 9, no wedge angle formation is observed over time for either method, which is consistent with expectations under uniform compressive load. This result is obtained by calculating the wedge angle from the full set of nodes on the vertebral end‐plates by fitting the least squares of the average planes. This strategy provides a more comprehensive geometric representation and reduces the influence of local mesh irregularities or numerical fluctuations. Consequently, although the end‐plates in the sequential method exhibit local waviness, as shown in Figure 7b, the fitted average planes remain such that no wedge angle develops.

In addition to morphological differences, the two methods also perform significantly differently in terms of computational efficiency. The sequential growth simulation with nonlinear material behavior requires approximately 8347 s to complete under symmetric loading conditions, whereas the decomposition‐based method completes the same simulation in only 972 s. This notable reduction in computation time demonstrates that the decomposition‐based method provides a clear computational advantage, even under symmetric loading conditions.

Asymmetric Loading

To facilitate the comparison of numerical results for AIS progression, the FSU model is analyzed over a two‐year growth period under a prescribed asymmetric compressive loading condition. The bottom surface of the FSU is fully constrained, and a pressure distribution is applied on the superior surface of the FSU such that it varies linearly along the Y direction, ranging from 0.15 to 0.78 MPa. This loading range is consistent with the concave–convex stress levels reported by Driscoll et al. [10] at the curve apex. Muscle forces are not explicitly modeled; instead, the loading is intentionally idealized to enable a controlled and consistent comparison between the sequential and the decomposition‐based growth implementations under identical geometry and loading conditions.

Figure 10 compares the initial configuration of the FSU model with the final configurations obtained after 2 years of growth simulation under asymmetric loading. In Figure 10a, the blue‐colored layers denote the growth areas subjected to mechanobiological growth modulation in the growth plates. Figures 10b and 10c show the final configurations obtained using decomposition‐based and sequential methods, respectively. Notably, the sequential growth method (Figure 10c) results in an irregular deformation pattern in growth plates around the intervertebral disc region, characterized by localized fluctuations in the endplate geometry. This behavior is likely due to the cumulative effects of element distortion caused by the repeated update of geometry over successive growth cycles. In contrast, the decomposition‐based growth method (Figure 10b) yields a much smoother deformation profile, particularly along the disc‐endplate interface. The observed differences highlight the differences of the two growth methods and suggest that the decomposition‐based approach offers a smoother deformation response and potentially greater numerical stability in long‐term growth simulations.

The quantitative progression of wedge angle over the two‐year simulation period is presented in Figure 11 for the upper and lower vertebral bodies. In the decomposition‐based method, wedge angle is evaluated in post‐processing at a fixed frequency of every 10 time increments during a single continuous simulation. Therefore, there are 20 data points for the decomposition‐based curve. In contrast, the sequential method provides one value per monthly growth cycle, resulting in 24 data points over the same period. As shown in Figure 11, both methods exhibit a similar overall trend of increasing wedge angle over 2 years. However, the sequential method consistently predicts larger wedge angles for both the upper and lower vertebral bodies. This difference is consistent with the cycle‐based nature of the sequential procedure, in which repeated geometry updates can progressively generate localized growth irregularities near the disc and growth plate areas (see Figure 10), thereby affecting the computed wedge angle progression. Since wedge angle is a key indicator of vertebral wedging, which contributes to overall curve magnitude commonly quantified clinically by the Cobb angle [43], the higher values predicted by the sequential workflow imply a stronger progression tendency under the same loading scenario. In contrast, the decomposition‐based method yields a smoother progression by integrating growth within a single continuous finite element analysis.

Comparison of wedge angle progression over a two‐year time for both growth methods under asymmetric loading.

In addition to the differences in growth response, the two methods also differ markedly in computational efficiency. The sequential growth simulation took approximately 8979 s to complete, whereas the decomposition‐based method achieved the same simulation in only 992 s. This substantial reduction in computation time demonstrates the clear efficiency advantage of the decomposition‐based framework. Beyond the savings during the solution phase, the decomposition‐based method also offers important advantages in preprocessing. The sequential workflow requires continuous data exchange between MATLAB and ABAQUS throughout the simulation process, which substantially increases the overall preprocessing time and adds implementation complexity. In contrast, the decomposition‐based method handles growth within a single incremental finite element analysis, eliminating the need for repeated external data transfer. This simplifies the overall workflow and reduces both preprocessing effort and implementation burden. Taken together, these advantages make the decomposition‐based approach a more suitable choice for large scale or long term spinal growth simulations.

Limitations

The present study has several limitations arising from the simplifying assumptions adopted in the finite element model. The FSU was represented using an idealized axisymmetric cylindrical geometry; therefore, no distinction was made between the coronal and sagittal planes. Also, to simplify the FSU model, posterior elements, ligaments, and muscle forces were not included. Moreover, the geometric representation was not intended to replicate a patient‐specific anatomy or an exact MRI‐derived geometry. Rather, the model was intentionally simplified to enable a controlled comparison between the sequential and decomposition‐based growth implementations under identical finite element model and loading conditions.

The intervertebral disc was modeled as hyperelastic; therefore, time‐dependent viscoelastic behavior was not considered, and the anisotropic response of the annulus fibrosus was neglected. Consequently, the results should be interpreted primarily as a methodological comparison between two growth implementation strategies rather than as a patient‐specific or fully physiological prediction of spinal growth.

Conclusion

In this study, we proposed a novel decomposition‐based method to simulate mechanobiological growth and evaluated its performance by comparing it with a widely used sequential method. Although both approaches are based on the same mechanobiological growth principle (e.g., the Hueter‐Volkmann law), they differ fundamentally in how the growth process is integrated into the finite element framework. In the proposed method, growth is embedded directly into the constitutive model through a multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradient, and hence, growth occurs simultaneously with loading and deformation. Unlike the sequential framework, which uses thermal strains with anisotropic thermal expansion coefficients to simulate growth and requires repeated updating of the growth direction due to its Eulerian nature, the decomposition‐based approach is formulated in the Lagrangian configuration. As a result, the growth direction remains fixed throughout the simulation, eliminating the need for directional updates. This reformulation simplifies implementation, enhances computational efficiency, and maintains numerical robustness under large deformation conditions. Furthermore, in the sequential method, the growth always occurs sequentially in a stress‐free state after a mechanical loading step, which does not properly represent the physical growth process.

To assess the performance of the method, a finite element model of a simplified Functional Spinal Unit (FSU) was analyzed under both symmetric and asymmetric loading conditions. The results showed an overall trend in wedge angle progression for both approaches. In addition to its numerical stability and physical soundness, the decomposition‐based method demonstrated substantial improvements in computational performance. Both total runtime and preprocessing time were significantly reduced compared to the sequential implementation, primarily due to the fixed growth direction and the elimination of iterative data exchange between external scripts and the finite element solver.

Another key contribution of this study lies in the proposed wedge angle calculation method. Unlike previous approaches that rely on a small number of nodal measurements or simplified geometric assumptions, our method utilizes full endplate coordinate data to construct average planes using the least squares method. This enables a more accurate and reliable quantification of wedge angle computation while also reducing the impact of local mesh irregularities or numerical fluctuations.

Overall, the proposed framework addresses multiple methodological gaps in the existing literature by offering a robust, generalizable, and computationally efficient approach to modeling growth‐induced spinal deformities. These characteristics make it particularly well suited for large‐scale, long‐term, or patient‐specific growth simulations and provide a promising foundation for future applications in clinical decision‐making and biomechanical analysis of spinal development.

Supplementary Validation of Local Stress Measures

In the sequential growth model, stress values in the loading‐sensitive area are used to calculate growth modulation, as described in Section 2.1. The loading‐sensitive area is highly responsive to mechanical stimuli, as it includes the reserve, proliferative, and part of the hypertrophic zones [27, 28, 44]. In contrast, the growth area physiologically corresponds to the remaining portion of the hypertrophic zone and the degenerated cartilage regions, where chondrocytes undergo apoptosis and the surrounding matrix becomes calcified. Although the growth area does not actively respond to mechanical stimuli to initiate mechanobiological growth, it continues to expand axially due to growth.

Although using stress values from the loading‐sensitive area is physiologically reasonable and a commonly employed procedure in the sequential method [10, 11, 37], as previously discussed, the finite element analysis indicates that the stress levels in the growth area and the loading‐sensitive area are very similar. Therefore, in the decomposition‐based method, the Hueter–Volkmann law for finite growth is implemented using stress values from the growth area instead of the loading‐sensitive region. This modeling choice simplifies the numerical implementation without compromising mechanical accuracy.
