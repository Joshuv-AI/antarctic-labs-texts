Gut Instincts, Machine Decisions: Evaluating AI Accuracy in the Diagnosis and Treatment of Disorders of Gut‐Brain Interaction
Authors: Matthew Ahn, Colleen H. Parker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDisorders of Gut‐Brain Interaction (DGBIs) are common yet challenging diagnoses for gastroenterologists and primary care clinicians. Large language models (LLMs) may support clinical management, but their accuracy remains poorly defined. This study evaluated the diagnostic and treatment accuracy of five LLMs using a convenience sample of clinical scenarios from the Rome IV Multidimensional Clinical Profile (MDCP).MethodsSixty eight cases representing the DGBIs were entered into commonly accessible, untrained LLMs: ChatGPT 4.0, Google Gemini 2.5 Pro, Microsoft Copilot, OpenEvidence, and Perplexity. A standardized prompt elicited a diagnosis, treatment options, and confidence ratings for each diagnosis. Outputs were assessed against the MDCP recommendations and expert opinion.Key ResultsDiagnostic accuracy across models demonstrated Perplexity at 74%, ChatGPT and Google Gemini at 72% respectively, and Microsoft Copilot and OpenEvidence at 65% respectively. Treatment accuracy across models demonstrated ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Microsoft Copilot generating accurate treatment options between 53% and 54% of cases each, compared with 41% for OpenEvidence and Perplexity each. No statistically significant differences were observed between AI models for diagnostic or treatment accuracy. Confidence ratings were uniformly high regardless of accuracy, with the mean scores ranging from 93% to 96% and standard deviation ranging from 1% to 4%.Conclusions and InferencesUntrained LLMs demonstrated promising but imperfect diagnostic and treatment performance in this study of a convenience sample of DGBI cases. High confidence despite diagnostic errors and inconsistent treatment recommendations highlights the need for cautious clinical integration and validation.

Introduction

Accurately identifying disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBIs) remains a critical but often elusive goal in clinical care. DGBIs, formerly known as functional gastrointestinal disorders, are conditions related to any combination of motility disruption, visceral hypersensitivity, altered mucosal and immune function, altered gut microbiota and altered central nervous system processing [1]. These conditions are defined by their chronic gastrointestinal symptoms that occur in the absence of inflammatory, metabolic, or structural abnormalities [2]. These conditions include irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), functional dyspepsia, functional constipation, functional bloating, and functional diarrhea, among others [3]. Collectively, DGBIs have been estimated to affect more than 40% of the worldwide population. The DGBIs, which are commonly encountered in both primary care and in the gastroenterologist's office, have a significant impact on quality of life and health care utilization [4]. Since the COVID‐19 pandemic, evidence suggests that the prevalence and impact of DGBIs have continued to increase [5].

The Rome IV criteria was developed as a framework for clinicians to recognize and diagnose DGBIs based on symptoms rather than physiological criteria or biomarkers [1]. Along with the Rome IV criteria, the Multidimensional Clinical Profile (MDCP) was developed as a standardized collection of clinical vignettes, symptom profiles, psychosocial modifiers, and methods of treatment based on the Rome IV categorization of DGBIs. Since its introduction, the Rome IV criteria has been widely disseminated to improve awareness and competence in recognizing DGBIs [6]. Despite widespread dissemination of the Rome IV criteria, both general practitioners and gastroenterologists often encounter difficulties in confidently diagnosing DGBIs [7]. Clinicians have described the diagnostic process for conditions like IBS to be time‐consuming, complex, and challenging compared to other conditions with clear biomarkers such as inflammatory bowel disease. Given the stigma that many patients living with DGBIs experience due to the lack of a clear metabolic or structural basis for their symptoms, arriving at the correct diagnosis is a critical step in maintaining the patient‐provider relationship and in providing appropriate patient care [8].

Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly large language models (LLMs), have emerged as a potential tool to support clinical decision making including in the field of DGBIs [9]. LLMs are AI programs trained on vast text datasets with the capacity to understand, generate, process, and navigate complex associations between words [10]. Prior studies in gastroenterology have predominantly evaluated LLM performance in structured simulated settings with a particular focus on patient education and decision support [11]. To date, LLMs are continuing to evolve to become more accurate in clinical diagnosis, approaching the accuracy of gastrointestinal specialists [12, 13]. For example, models such as ChatGPT have demonstrated high accuracy, reliability and fair readability in response to common patient questions regarding endoscopic procedures [14]. When provided with structured contextualized prompts, Lin et al. demonstrated that ChatGPT can identify high‐risk features in colorectal cancer screening with relatively low rates of hallucination compared to non‐contextualized inputs [15]. Similarly, Lahat et al. found that LLMs can provide generally accurate and comprehensive responses to general gastrointestinal‐related patient questions although performance varies widely based on question complexity [16]. However, comparatively fewer studies have examined LLM performance in the diagnosis and management of DGBIs, which is characterized by symptom‐based criteria [11]. Furthermore, although studies have compared the diagnostic performance of different LLMs across internal medicine questions, there have been limited comparative studies of different LLMs in gastroenterology [17].

Given the time and resource constraints faced by clinicians, LLMs may help streamline diagnoses in clinical care. However, reports of AI hallucinations, in which AI tools generate fabricated but plausible outputs not supported by underlying data, are a concerning risk involved in relying on these models [18]. A recent Delphi consensus on AI use in gastroenterology highlights limited diagnostic consensus statements given the lack of large‐scale validation studies in different populations [19]. Furthermore, the majority of existing literature is based on simulated scenarios rather than real‐world patient inputs, which limits external validity [20]. Additionally, concerns have been raised regarding bias, such as the amplification of health disparities and inaccuracies in disease representation using LLMs in gastroenterology [21]. At present, there is a lack of literature investigating the accuracy of AI models in diagnosing DGBIs compared to validated consensus‐based guidelines such as the Rome IV criteria. This exploratory study evaluates the accuracy of untrained, commonly available AI models in generating DGBI diagnoses based on clinical vignettes from the Rome IV MDCP.

Materials and Methods

AI Models

LLMs were selected for evaluation based on public accessibility and availability for clinical use. The five models selected for the study include ChatGPT 4.0 (ChatGPT), Google Gemini 2.5 Pro (Google Gemini), Microsoft Copilot, OpenEvidence, and Perplexity. These models were selected based on their reported popularity among clinicians, integration into existing healthcare systems, and claims of being evidence‐based or informed by peer‐reviewed literature. ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, and Perplexity are general‐purpose LLMs, whereas OpenEvidence is a retrieval‐augmented generation (RAG) LLM sourcing from high‐impact journals, designed to prioritize evidence citations rather than general‐purpose dialog [22].

Data Collection Procedure

Permission for use of the MDCP case scenarios in this study was obtained from the Rome Foundation. Case scenarios from the MDCP are generated and rigorously reviewed by a panel of experts in DGBIs. Each case scenario was input into the AI models without the identifying diagnosis, diagnostic category, psychosocial modifiers, or treatment options. The case scenarios were randomized to minimize pattern recognition based on MDCP categorizations. Each case scenario was entered under a standardized input format for consistency. A case scenario was first provided, followed by a specific prompt: “You are presented with a case scenario above. Please provide the most likely diagnosis in detail, your confidence score in your diagnosis, and treatment options.” [23] Assigning a specific role for role‐based prompting was not used, as previous studies have shown no consistent improvement in model performance with role assignment [24]. No domain‐specific training or fine‐tuning was applied to any of the evaluated LLMs prior to testing, in order to assess baseline performance of these commonly accessible and untrained LLMs. Case scenarios identified as “Multicultural Disorders” in the MDCP were excluded given the significant variability in diagnostic strategies and treatment options that exist among clinicians.

All data were collected within a 24‐h period on June 21, 2025 to reduce potential variation from model retraining or updates. Searches were conducted using new premium accounts to minimize influence from prior user history. Outputs from each AI model were recorded and independently reviewed. Given that the case scenarios contained no patient identifying information, no ethics approval was required for this study.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of accurate diagnostic matches between AI generated and MDCP reference diagnoses. The secondary outcomes included the reported confidence from the LLM in its diagnoses and the accuracy of treatment recommendations compared with the MDCP reference and expert opinion.

Model Evaluation

The scoring system for model evaluation was adapted from a previous study that used an ordinal scale to assess AI model accuracy compared with established clinical guidelines [23, 25]. To assess the diagnostic accuracy of each model, the performance of the model was categorized as accurate, partially accurate, and inaccurate. The accurate label was assigned to diagnoses that were identical to the MDCP diagnosis or the MDCP diagnosis in addition to the clinical/psychological modifier. If the historic names for a diagnosis were used, it was also accepted as accurate. Partially accurate was assigned to (i) diagnoses that identified a possible clinical/psychosocial modifier but inaccurate primary MDCP diagnosis or (ii) only identified a portion of the MDCP diagnosis. Inaccurate was assigned to (i) diagnoses that did not match with the MDCP diagnosis, (ii) identified the accurate category of diagnosis but inaccurate diagnosis subtype in the MDCP diagnosis, or (iii) provided the accurate MDCP diagnosis but included additional irrelevant diagnoses.

To assess for the accuracy of each model identifying the treatment options, the performance of the model was categorized as accurate, partially accurate, and inaccurate. An accurate response for treatment options included all core treatment options identified in the MDCP for a given diagnosis and based on expert opinion. A partially accurate response identified at least one appropriate therapy but added at least one treatment option that was not identified in the MDCP or omitted at least one treatment option identified by the MDCP or by expert opinion. An inaccurate response did not identify any treatment options listed by the MDCP or based on expert opinion.

All scoring was conducted independently by two reviewers with clinical expertise in the diagnosis and management of DGBIs. In cases of disagreement, the reviewers met to discuss the case and reached final consensus.

Statistical Analysis

For each model, the proportions of accurate, partially accurate, and inaccurate outputs were calculated for diagnoses and treatment recommendations. Overall model performance in the accuracy of diagnoses and treatment options was compared using the Friedman test for dependent samples [26]. Concordance across all cases for diagnostic and treatment option accuracy was assessed using Fleiss' kappa statistic for multi‐rater agreement. A planned subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate diagnostic performance across MDCP diagnostic categories, including esophageal disorders, gastroduodenal disorders, bowel disorders, centrally mediated disorders of gastrointestinal pain, gallbladder and sphincter of Oddi disorders, anorectal disorders, and functional gastrointestinal disorders in neonates/toddlers and in children/adolescents [1].

Results

A total of 68 eligible MDCP case scenarios were included, representing eight diagnostic categories: anorectal disorders (n = 4), bowel disorders (n = 16), centrally mediated disorders of gastrointestinal pain (n = 5), child/adolescent (n = 11), esophageal disorders (n = 7), gallbladder and sphincter of Oddi disorders (n = 5), gastroduodenal disorders (n = 9), and neonate/toddler (n = 11).

Confidence Scores

Overall, all models consistently reported high confidence in their diagnoses with the mean scores ranging from 93% to 96% and standard deviation ranging from 1% to 4% (Table 3). For all generated diagnoses, the highest mean confidence score was reported by Google Gemini at 96%, followed by ChatGPT at 95%, OpenEvidence at 94%, and Microsoft Copilot and Perplexity at 93% each. When the confidence scores were stratified according to the accuracy of the diagnoses, all models reported high mean confidence scores regardless of whether the diagnoses were accurate, partially accurate, or inaccurate. For accurate diagnoses, the highest mean confidence score was reported by Google Gemini at 96%, followed by ChatGPT at 95%, Microsoft Copilot and OpenEvidence at 94% each, and Perplexity at 93%. For partially accurate diagnoses, the highest mean confidence score was reported by Google Gemini at 95%, followed by ChatGPT and Perplexity at 94% each, then Microsoft Copilot at 93% and OpenEvidence at 92%. Subgroup Analysis by MDCP Diagnostic Category

When a subgroup analysis was conducted based on the MDCP diagnostic category, there were wide variations in the performance of the AI models (Figure 3). All models performed poorly in diagnosing anorectal disorders but were more accurate in diagnosing child/adolescent conditions or esophageal disorders. In comparing the performance of each AI model to one another, there was modest variation in most diagnostic categories except for the gallbladder and sphincter of Oddi disorders, which reported greater variation in diagnostic accuracy among each model.

Discussion

With the growing impact of AI models such as LLMs in gastroenterology, a fundamental question is whether these tools are accurate for diagnosing DGBIs. This exploratory study illustrates both the potential and limitations of LLMs when applied to simulated case scenarios from the Rome IV MDCP. Overall, the models demonstrated reasonable performance in the accuracy of diagnoses and treatment options [27, 28, 29]. However, performance varied substantially when stratified according to MDCP diagnostic category. All models demonstrated high confidence in their diagnoses, irrespective of whether the diagnosis was accurate, partially accurate, or inaccurate. With the increasing availability and advancement of artificial intelligence, LLMs are becoming an increasingly common source of health information for patients, regardless of whether this use is disclosed to their physicians [30]. As such, understanding their performance and limitations is important knowledge that will affect the care of patients.

For diagnostic accuracy and treatment recommendations, our findings suggest that the selected models perform at a similar level of accuracy for all DGBI cases. We observed non‐significant differences in performance from the Friedman test and moderate concordance among models as measured by Fleiss' kappa in their classification of diagnostic and treatment accuracy. Although there were no significant differences in overall performance among the LLMs, there may be some degree of consistency in how models categorized case complexity and responded to clinical prompts due to possible exposure to overlap in training data. Nevertheless, concordance was not strong, indicating heterogeneity in overall classification capability of the various LLMs.

Generalized LLMs, such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini, and Perplexity, tended to perform better than more specialized RAG LLMs, such as OpenEvidence, that are designed specifically for clinician use and source data from medical journal publications. Although some studies have demonstrated that special‐purpose LLMs have outperformed general‐purpose LLMs in answering clinical questions, our findings align with emerging research to suggest that this advantage may not extend to DGBIs [22, 31, 32]. OpenEvidence, for example, frequently provided evidence‐based pharmacologic or procedural recommendations for many of the cases presented but often omitted supportive care practices such as patient validation, education, and reassurance. In the context of DGBIs, these supportive care practices are foundational for building trust and establishing a therapeutic relationship [33]. In the era of evidence‐based medicine and the rising role for research evidence in clinical care, our findings underscore the enduring importance of supportive care practices alongside pharmacologic or procedural treatments.

Subgroup analyses revealed variability in the accuracy of AI models across the different DGBI categories. Higher diagnostic accuracy was observed in esophageal disorders and child/adolescent disorders compared to poorer accuracy with anorectal disorders. These patterns likely reflect the quality of available data to train or inform LLMs: esophageal conditions are well‐represented in available literature for AI models, whereas anorectal disorders are less studied and more inconsistently described. Literature evaluating the impact of AI in neurogastroenterology and motility has primarily focused on esophageal techniques such as esophageal manometry with few studies on anorectal manometry [34]. The markedly reduced diagnostic performance in anorectal disorders appeared to be driven not only by limited exposure to relevant data but also inconsistent terminology used in this domain. Semantic discrepancies between terminology such as anterior resection syndrome, levator ani syndrome, and Rome‐IV defined terminology like fecal incontinence and chronic proctalgia respectively are often nuanced and inconsistently applied in clinical practice. Furthermore, anorectal disorders are less prevalent than other DGBIs, such as IBS and functional dyspepsia, and there is a relative paucity of international guidelines beyond specific society guidelines for anorectal disorders [4, 35]. The sensitive nature of these conditions may also contribute to delayed presentation and under‐documentation, further limiting data availability [36]. At present, there are limited large‐scale studies on the application of AI in clinical care of benign anorectal disorders, with even fewer studies on anorectal disorders classified as DGBIs, reflecting the challenges and gaps underlying the poor diagnostic performance of LLMs in this diagnostic category [37].

Interestingly, historical terminology for accurate diagnoses, particularly in gallbladder and sphincter of Oddi disorders, was frequently reproduced, despite updated Rome IV criteria and evidence from the EPISOD trial that has displaced previous classifications [38]. Our findings suggest that the persistence of older terminology in the literature continues to influence model outputs. Similarly, in centrally mediated disorders of gastrointestinal pain, responses often included partial or combined diagnoses. In these cases, there were often combinations of multiple diagnoses and clinical modifiers, reflecting the complexity of this diagnostic category and limitations of models in handling multi‐label imbalances in biomedical data [39].

Treatment recommendations generated by AI models also varied across MDCP diagnostic categories. Most models performed well in esophageal, neonate/toddler, and gallbladder and sphincter of Oddi disorders where there were well‐established and defined treatment recommendations such as sphincterotomy for certain sphincter of Oddi conditions and reassurance for infantile colic. In contrast, performance was poorer for bowel and gastroduodenal disorders, where treatment options are numerous, evolving, and individualized according to each patient's presentation. These findings suggest that LLMs may be most helpful in clinical questions for conditions with standardized management pathways, but less reliable when clinical decision‐making is multifactorial [39].

An interesting finding was that all models expressed high levels of confidence in their diagnosis, irrespective of the accuracy of their diagnosis. LLMs can struggle in appraising the relevance, quality, and trustworthiness of their source information to differentiate truth from fiction [22]. This pattern suggests potential overconfidence, where models present information persuasively even when inaccurate. In contrast, clinicians often exercise diagnostic caution, particularly when there is high uncertainty [40]. For patients with DGBIs who frequently face stigma and ambiguity, this distinction is critical. If adopted into practice, LLM outputs must therefore be evaluated critically rather than at face value, regardless of how confidently they are framed by the model itself.

Strengths of our study include the comprehensive selection of cases representing a wide diversity of DGBI presentations. Our study used a standardized comparative design to evaluate several commercially available and easily accessible, at low to no cost, LLMs under identical testing conditions. The MDCP provides a rigorous and expert validated reference standard that is aligned with the Rome IV criteria. This ensures diagnostic and treatment benchmarks that reflect current consensus rather than clinical opinion. Standardized prompts were applied uniformly across all models to minimize variability to isolate differences attributable to model performance. Clinical vignette‐based evaluation of LLMs, while unable to fully replicate the complexity of actual clinical encounters, is a well‐established methodology in AI and LLM clinical research [41]. This allows for controlled and reproducible assessment against a gold standard. Finally, scoring was performed by two reviewers with expertise in DGBIs, with discrepancies resolved by consensus, to strengthen the reliability of the outcome assessments.

Our study is also the first to date to compare and evaluate the performance of various available LLMs with regards to DGBIs in several domains such as diagnosis, treatment options, and confidence in diagnosis. However, limitations must also be acknowledged. Given the exploratory design of the study, these findings highlight the potential benefits and limitations of LLMs in certain aspects of diagnosis of DGBIs but do not establish their utility in actual patient care. Additionally, a key limitation is that general purpose LLMs were evaluated without domain‐specific training for DGBIs. This decision was made to assess commonly used, untrained LLMs without extensive task‐specific adaptations before their use in clinical practice [42]. However, prior studies have demonstrated that training can improve diagnostic performance, suggesting that our findings may underestimate the diagnostic accuracy achievable with optimized models. LLM selection was based on availability and reported uptake, thus it may not represent the full range of LLMs available for clinical use. Furthermore, LLM outputs are non‐deterministic and can change rapidly with system updates. Our findings reflect model performance at a single time point and may not be generalizable to future versions.

It should be noted that the relatively small number of case scenarios used in this study is a key limitation. The number of case scenarios included in this study was limited by the finite set of cases available within the MDCP, which represents a curated collection of expert‐developed cases created by a panel of neurogastroenterologists [39]. Consideration of additional scenarios would require similar expert validation to ensure clinical accuracy and consistency. As such, our study reflects a convenience sample of all eligible cases rather than a sample determined by an a priori power calculation [15]. This limited number of cases may reduce statistical power, increase the risk for type II error, and limit the precision of effect estimates. Although all LLMs were evaluated on the same cases, enabling consistent comparisons and reduced variability, this design improves precision of between‐model comparisons but does not overcome the limited sample size. Subgroup analyses by diagnostic category were a secondary exploratory analysis due to the small sample sizes and unequal case distribution from the MDCP.

Another limitation is the novel scoring system utilized in this study, as no validated scoring systems currently exist for assessing the diagnostic accuracy of AI tools and LLMs [23, 25]. Despite this limitation, the ordinal scoring system adapted from prior literature reflects clinical reality in which there are rarely nominal outcomes for diagnoses and treatments [25, 43]. Specialists in neurogastroenterology often receive referrals that contain partially accurate diagnostic elements but ultimately require revisions when the investigations or additional clinical details do not fully support the initial diagnosis [44, 45]. Although previous studies have assessed AI accuracy by evaluating differential diagnoses, our study focused on the single most likely diagnosis consistent with the Rome IV MDCP, which provides one diagnosis per case [1, 43]. Accordingly, this ordinal scoring system reflects the reality of partially accurate clinical decisions and reinforces the role for strong clinical reasoning and physician judgment to be embedded in AI use. We did not apply predefined thresholds for acceptable accuracy, as validated benchmarks for evaluating LLM diagnostic and therapeutic accuracy have not been established to date. However, the development of standardized thresholds would be an important step toward more meaningful clinical evaluation of LLMs in the future.

Despite the limitation noted above, our findings provide an early benchmark for understanding the opportunities and limitations of the use of LLMs in DGBIs. Future investigations that would be of interest include evaluating the impact of AI models directly in patient care for DGBIs and assessment of other AI models. This would provide greater insights into the potential application of LLMs in clinical care.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings demonstrate that untrained LLMs showed promising but imperfect accuracy in diagnosing and recommending treatments for a convenience sample of DGBI cases from the Rome IV MDCP. While they performed best in well described conditions with standardized treatments, accuracy was lower for more complex cases. The frequent expression of high confidence, regardless of accuracy, highlights the need for cautious clinical interpretation. As these tools continue to evolve, their role should be viewed as supportive but not replacing clinician expertise, patient‐centered care, and evidence‐based judgment. Ultimately, their value will not replace the clinician, but rather empower clinicians to deliver more accurate, nuanced, and compassionate care.
