Persistent hypervascularity of the corpus luteum in the setting of acute ovarian torsion
Authors: Douglas Rogers, Ragheed Al-Dulaimi, Reham Ellessy, Maryam Rezvani, Anne Kennedy, Akram Shaaban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe corpus luteum with associated stromal edema (CLOE) is 1 of the most common mimics of acute ovarian torsion without a lead-point mass, both on imaging and clinically. Peripheral hypervascularity of the corpus luteum in the setting of ovarian stromal edema has been described as potentially having negative predictive value for ovarian torsion. The presented cases demonstrate that corpus luteum hypervascularity may persist in the setting of acute torsion. When considering CLOE as an alternative diagnosis to ovarian torsion, it is critical that the finding of corpus luteum hypervascularity does not supersede more specific or concerning findings for ovarian torsion, such as a twisted vascular pedicle or a surgical abdomen/peritoneal signs. Hypervascularity of the corpus luteum may also be present in the setting of intermittent ovarian torsion. When there is suspicion for adnexal torsion, early gynecological consultation increases the chance for preservation of ovarian function and fertility.Keywords: Ovarian torsion, Adnexal torsion, Corpus luteum

Introduction

Adnexal torsion accounts for ∼3% of gynecologic emergencies, requiring urgent surgical intervention to avoid ovarian stromal hemorrhage/necrosis and to preserve ovarian function [1,2]. Patients typically present acutely with severe, localized pelvic pain which may be accompanied by nausea and vomiting [3]. The most common associated imaging findings on ultrasound and CT are due to ovarian edema, including stromal enlargement with peripheral displacement of follicles, which is seen in >95% of cases [4,5]. Other associated imaging findings include adjacent free fluid, stromal hemorrhage, abnormal ovarian location, uterine tilting, and the “follicular ring sign” [2,6,7]. The most specific feature is the “whirlpool sign,” in which the twisted vascular pedicle is directly visualized [6].

In the absence of a lead-point adnexal mass (such as a mature teratoma), the diagnosis of ovarian torsion becomes more challenging because there are other causes of ovarian edema that can cause pelvic pain. Ovaries containing a corpus luteum may idiosyncratically become edematous, leading to pelvic pain and overlapping imaging features with ovarian torsion [[8], [9], [10]]. This is known as corpus luteum with ovarian edema (“CLOE”), and is likely the most common mimicker of acute ovarian torsion without a lead point mass [9]. A previously published series of 69 consecutive cases that were concerning for ovarian torsion on imaging (39 CLOE and 30 ovarian torsion) evaluated for differences between cases of CLOE and ovarian torsion without a lead point mass. This series demonstrated peripheral hypervascularity of the corpus luteum in 67.7 % of CLOE cases, and no corpus luteum enhancement in cases of ovarian torsion (of which 64% contained a corpus luteum; n = 16). This series defined corpus luteum hypervascularity as enhancement of the corpus luteum wall on CT, or blood flow within the corpus luteum wall that was greater than adjacent ovarian stroma on color Doppler ultrasound. All 16 torsed ovaries in this series that contained a corpus luteum had a devascularized/nonenhancing corpus luteum wall, suggesting that if a “ring-of-fire” was present associated with a corpus luteum in an edematous ovary, the alternative diagnosis of CLOE should be considered [9].

The potential use for ovarian blood flow evaluation in the setting of acute pelvic pain flies in the face of conventional wisdom, which is that the presence or absence of blood flow is of dubious significance for the diagnosis of ovarian torsion, as up to 60% of torsed ovaries demonstrate persistent blood flow, likely due to the dual blood supply of the ovary [2,5,[11], [12], [13]]. As it turns out, no test is perfect; we present 2 cases of proven ovarian torsion that had maintained hypervascular perfusion of the corpus luteum wall. Although evaluation of the corpus luteum blood flow may have clinical utility, this imaging finding is insignificant compared to other more specific findings, such as a clearly visualized twisted pedicle, or the presence of a surgical abdomen and peritoneal signs.

Case 1

A 21-year-old female with history of irregular menses presented to the emergency department with right lower quadrant pain. This pain awoke her from sleep with 9/10 sharp pain that would not subside. This was associated with nausea and a headache. She denied fever, chills, vomiting, bloating, constipation, urinary symptoms, or abnormal vaginal bleeding/discharge.

Vital signs were normal, and physical examination revealed tenderness to palpation in the right lower quadrant with guarding. Laboratory evaluation was negative, including CBC, CMP, serum lipase, urinalysis, and pregnancy test.

A contrast enhanced CT (CECT) of the abdomen and pelvis was performed, demonstrating significant enlargement of the right ovary to 12.8 × 9.4 × 6.6 cm. The ovary was relatively hypoattenuating, had an adjacent twisted pedicle, and a persistently enhancing corpus luteum in the periphery (Fig. 1).

(A) Coronal oblique multiplanar reformat CECT shows an enlarged, hypoattenuating right ovary containing an enhancing corpus luteum within the periphery (black arrow). A small vessel extends from the ovarian hilum to supply the corpus luteum wall (blue arrow) and (B) Coronal oblique multiplanar reformat CECT shows the enlarged right ovary with a twisted vascular pedicle (arrow).

The gynecology team determined that the patient had a surgical abdomen, and proceeded to laparotomy. The right ovary was significantly enlarged and twisted 1 and a half times around the utero-ovarian ligament with a small amount of serous free fluid. They performed cystectomy and detorsion of the ovary, which appeared viable. Surgical pathology of the resected cyst was compatible with a follicular cyst. The patient had a smooth recovery and was discharged later that day without further complication.

Case 2

A 51-year-old female with history of vaginal hysterectomy presented to the emergency department with right lower quadrant pain. This pain was initially mistaken by the patient for constipation the day before, but upon presentation had progressed to severe, sharp localized pain associated with nausea and vomiting. She denied fever, chills, urinary symptoms, or abnormal vaginal bleeding/discharge.

Vital signs were normal, and physical examination revealed tenderness to palpation in the right lower quadrant. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated mildly elevated WBC (13.1), and was otherwise negative, including CMP, serum lipase, urinalysis, and pregnancy test.

A CECT of the abdomen and pelvis was performed, demonstrating enlargement of the right ovary to 7.0 × 3.7 × 4.9 cm. The ovary was relatively hypoattenuating, had an adjacent twisted pedicle, and a persistently enhancing corpus luteum on the periphery (Fig. 2). MRI was then obtained, which showed T2 hyperintense stromal edema along with the persistently enhancing corpus luteum (Fig. 3).

(A) Axial CECT shows an enlarged, hypoattenuating right ovary (blue arrow) containing an enhancing corpus luteum within the periphery (black arrow) and (B) Axial oblique multiplanar reformat CECT shows the enlarged right ovary with a small vessel extending from the ovarian hilum to supply the corpus luteum wall (black arrow), and a twisted vascular pedicle (blue arrow).

(A) Sagittal T2 MRI shows an enlarged right ovary with T2 hyperintense edematous stroma (black arrow), and a circumscribed T2 intermediate corpus luteum in the periphery (blue arrow) and (B) Sagittal T1 postcontrast with fat saturation MRI shows the enlarged right ovary with persistently enhancing corpus luteum wall (arrow).

The gynecology team determined that the patient had a surgical abdomen, and proceeded to laparotomy. The right ovary was enlarged and twisted twice around the infundibulopelvic ligament, so oophorectomy was performed. The patient had a smooth recovery and was discharged the next day without further complication.

Discussion

The corpus luteum with associated ovarian edema (CLOE) is likely the most common mimicker of acute ovarian torsion without a lead point mass, due to overlapping clinical symptoms and imaging findings [8]. Both entities demonstrate findings of ovarian edema on imaging, such as stromal enlargement and peripheral displacement of follicles, and both may present with localized pelvic pain [8,9]. On average, torsed ovaries are significantly larger compared to CLOE, and patients with acute torsion are more likely to present within 24 hours of symptom onset [9].

It is well-known that the presence of vascular flow (arterial and/or venous) demonstrated with color and pulse-wave Doppler ultrasound cannot exclude acute ovarian torsion, as more than half of acute ovarian torsion cases have preserved blood flow [2,5,[11], [12], [13]]. In fact, the mechanism for the development of ovarian edema is due to diminished venolymphatic outflow which is exceeded by persistent arterial inflow, which may be from ovarian and/or uterine artery branches. Despite this, a previously published series demonstrated that the presence of peripheral hypervascularity of the corpus luteum with an edematous ovary may be a useful imaging feature with negative predictive value for ovarian torsion [9]. However, as the presented cases show, uncommonly an acute adnexal torsion may coexist with persistently increased blood flow in a corpus luteum wall.

When approaching a case of pelvic pain where imaging shows an edematous ovary with an associated mass, it is important to recognize that the imaging findings may be less important than the clinical context and physical examination, particularly in regards to the decision to pursue laparoscopy. There are instances when gynecologists perform laparoscopy in the setting of a surgical abdomen, and adnexal torsion is not found. However, often in these cases, an alternative cause for the patient’s presentation is identified and treated (e.g. a persistently bleeding ruptured ovarian cyst) [14]. The most specific imaging finding for adnexal torsion in the setting of pelvic pain and an enlarged ovary is the “whirlpool sign.” If twisting of an adjacent pedicle is clearly seen, ovarian torsion can be confidently diagnosed on imaging [15,16]. In the absence of this highly specific imaging feature, the radiologist should acknowledge that there are other potential causes of ovarian edema beyond acute torsion.

It is reasonable to consider CLOE in the differential diagnosis for an edematous ovary when there is peripheral hypervascularity of the corpus luteum on ultrasound or CECT. Although a corpus luteum with a “ring-of-fire” is more indicative of CLOE than ovarian torsion, it is of utmost importance that this finding does not supersede more specific findings for ovarian torsion (such as a twisted pedicle), or the presence of a surgical abdomen, peritoneal signs, or a high level of clinical concern by the consulting surgeon.

An additional confounding clinical entity is the diagnostically challenging intermittent adnexal torsion and detorsion. It is self-evident that the presence or absence of corpus luteum hypervascularity may be unreliable in this context, particularly during detorsion. When intermittent symptoms are present over a more extended period of time, a higher index of suspicion is required to make the diagnosis of adnexal torsion [2,17].

Conclusion

Despite the tremendous efforts invested into optimizing the imaging diagnosis of adnexal torsion, it ultimately remains a clinical diagnosis in many cases, especially when no twisted pedicle is visualized on imaging. Acute adnexal torsion may coexist with persistent peripheral hypervascularity of the corpus luteum. When considering the corpus luteum with ovarian edema (CLOE) as an alternative diagnosis to ovarian torsion, it is critical that the presence of a persistently vascularized corpus luteum as a negative predictive factor for ovarian torsion does not supersede more specific or concerning findings for ovarian torsion, including a twisted pedicle or a surgical abdomen. When there is suspicion for adnexal torsion, early gynecological consultation increases the chance for preservation of ovarian function and fertility.
