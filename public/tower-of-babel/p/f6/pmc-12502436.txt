Severe combined immunodeficiency with BCG-osis by salvage therapy with allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation: cases report and literature review
Authors: Yuqing Jiao, Quan Li, Zhi Chen, Li Yang, Zhuo Wang, Fang Tao, Yu Du, Xiuwen Xu, Zuofeng Li, Hao Xiong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSevere combined immunodeficiency(SCID) combined with Bacillus Calmette-Guérin(BCG) disease(BCG-osis) is a rare but life-threatening complication in pediatric patients who received allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). Early recognition and intervention are essential to prevent severe complications.Case presentationWe have described two pediatric cases of SCID combined with BCG-osis in which the patients received salvage therapy with allo-HSCT. Data were collected from two male infants who underwent allo-HSCT for SCID with BCG-osis at Wuhan Children’s Hospital between January 2017 and December 2022. The data of the two patients were retrospectively collected and analyzed to summarize their clinical characteristics, treatment, and prognosis. Patient 1 presented with SCID combined with disseminated BCG-osis and underwent HSCT after only 30 days of anti-tuberculosis therapy. The tuberculosis infection recurred at 83 days after transplantation, and the patient eventually died of multi-organ failure and disseminated intravascular coagulation. Patient 2 presented with SCID combined with disseminated BCG-osis after HSCT. The patient responded well to antituberculosis drugs and successfully completed a year and a half of anti-tuberculosis treatment. On day 591 post-transplant, the anti-tuberculosis regimen was discontinued in this patient.ConclusionsPatients with SCID have severe defects in cellular and humoral immunity. Genetic screening at birth would help identify such patients, and the BCG vaccination should be delayed in such cases. The sole curative option for SCID is HSCT, which can achieve immune reconstitution when performed early on and can improve the survival rate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06127-0.Keywords: Disseminated bacillus Calmette-Guérin disease, Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, Severe combined immunodeficiency, Pediatric tuberculosis, Mycobacterium tuberculosis

Introduction

Allogeneic Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is a curative treatment modality that is critical for the treatment of many hematologic malignancies, some nonmalignant hematological disorders, and a variety of autoimmune diseases (such as leukemia, severe aplastic anemia, severe immunodeficiency disease (SCID), and myelodysplastic syndrome) [1]. SCID is characterized by severe deficiency of T lymphocytes that leads to severe impairment of cellular and humoral immune function. Typically, patients develop recurrent infections, chronic diarrhea, and growth retardation within the first 3 to 6 months of life and die early [2]. HSCT can correct the genetic defects of SCID patients, achieve immune reconstitution, and cure the disease. HSCT was first performed in an SCID patient in 1968 [3], and since then, HSCT has seen continuous improvements and has become the main curative treatment in certain SCID cases. In this study, we have retrospectively analyzed the records of two patients with SCID who developed disseminated BCG-osis and were treated with HSCT at Wuhan Children’s Hospital between January 2017 and December 2022.

Method

Patients

To explore the clinical characteristics of SCID combined with disseminated BCG-osis in the peri-transplantation period, we retrospectively collected data from all pediatric SCID patients (n = 2) who underwent allo-HSCT between January 2017 and December 2022 at Wuhan Children’s Hospital. Donor selection and conditioning regimens

Studies by the European Organization for Immunodeficiency Stem Cell Transplantation (EOISCT) have shown that matched sibling donor (MSD) is the first choice, with the best donor outcome and an overall survival (OS) close to 90%. Matched family donor (MFD) is the second choice, with an OS comparable to that of MSD. As a last resort, cord blood or a 1-locus-mismatched unrelated donors are considered [4, 5]. Both patients in this study had allo-HSCT: patient 1 had an unrelated donor fully compatible peripheral blood stem cell transplant and patient 2 received a HSCT from a sibling hemizygous haploidentical. The regimen for clear marrow preconditioning and graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis was similar in both patients. Before the procedure, both children received busulfan, 1.2 mg/kg per dose, Q6H, IV (−9 d to −7 d); thiotepa, 5 mg/kg per dose, −6 d, Q12H; cyclophosphamide, 25 mg/kg per day, IV, 4 d (−5 d to −2 d) and anti-thymocyte globulin (4.5 mg/kg) for 3 consecutive days (−3 d to −1d). After the procedure, on day + 3, they received cyclophosphamide (10 mg/kg daily). The GVHD prophylaxis regimen consisted of cyclosporine with mycophenolate mofetil and low-dose methotrexate. Cyclosporine was started at 1.5 mg/kg per day IV (−9 d to −2 d) and was changed to 3 mg/kg day on day 1 prior to HSCT. The cyclosporine levels were monitored twice a week (therapeutic range, 100–250 ng/ml). Mycophenolate mofetil was started at 30 mg/kg daily (−9 d to + 30 d), and then the dose was reduced to half from + 31 d to + 45 d. Methotrexate was administered on day 1 (15 mg/m2), day 3 (10 mg/m2), and day 6 (10 mg/m2) after the transplant. In the case of patient 2, basiliximab for GVHD prophylaxis was added on day 4 post-transplant, and the remaining medications were the same for both patients. (Table 2 and Table 3).

Case presentation

Case 1

Patient 1 was a 3-month-old male infant diagnosed with X-linked SCID (SCID; IL2RG mutation). The patient’s brother has passed away due to severe combined immunodeficiency disease and has not undergone genetic testing. The specific details are unknown. The pediatric patient 1 received the BCG vaccine at birth but experienced recurrent fever at 30 days before transplantation, and had no history of exposure to tuberculosis patients, and erythema at the site of BCG vaccination, with no significant abnormalities on lung CT and no significant decrease in temperature even after administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics. Blood mNGS was used to detect M. tuberculosis(TB), and the result of the Xpert test was negative. These findings were indicative of acute disseminated tuberculosis. The patient was immediately started on antituberculosis treatment with rifampicin (20 mg/kg, QD, i.v.), isoniazid (15 mg/kg, QD, i.v.). However, isoniazid and rifampicin were discontinued after one week due to a significant increase in bilirubin levels and yellowish discoloration of the skin. It was determined that the anti-tuberculosis drugs had caused hepatic injury, which was relieved with the addition of a hepatoprotective drug. On post-transplantation day 13, the patient developed acute GVHD (intestinal, grade I). Grading criteria for acute GVHD were based on the Mount Sinai International Consortium for Acute Graft-versus-Host Disease (Magic) criteria [6, 7]. Neutrophil recovery was not delayed, and the absolute neutrophil count was > 0.5 × 109/L from day 16 of allo-HSCT. However, poor platelet implantation was noted. On post-transplantation day 2, the patient developed recurrent fever. An anti-infection therapeutic regimen with isoniazid (80 mg per dose, QD, i.v.), linezolid, levofloxacin, posaconazole, and voriconazole (9 mg/kg, Q12H on the first day, i.v.) was administered. However, the patient continued to have recurrent fever, next-generation sequencing (NGS) of a blood sample on day 12 after transplantation indicated the presence of M. tuberculosis (sequence number 10). In response, levofloxacin was discontinued and replaced with amikacin (7.5 mg/kg, Q12H, i.v.). On day 16 after transplantation, a blood metagenomic NGS (mNGS) pathogen test was conducted again and revealed the presence of the M. tuberculosis complex group (sequence number 229) as well as M. tuberculosis (sequence number 17). These results were indicative of an ongoing tuberculosis infection. By day 29 after transplantation, the body temperature had become more stable, so amikacin was discontinued and anti-tuberculosis treatment was continued with oral levofloxacin. The patient’s liver function returned to normal, so oral isoniazid was added to the regimen, in addition to rifampicin, for anti-tuberculosis treatment. A lung CT scan showed that there were no significant abnormalities. On day 44 after transplantation, a repeat high-throughput blood routine test revealed that M. tuberculosis had been largely eliminated. Subsequently, tuberculosis treatment was continued with oral administration of isoniazid (60 mg/dose, QD) and rifampicin (75 mg/dose, QD). However, the patient still had recurrent fever, which was considered to be caused by viral infection. Gammaglobulin 2.5 g infusion and ganciclovir antiviral therapy were added, and the interval between the patient’s fever was gradually prolonged. On the 47th day after transplantation, a red rash appeared on the trunk of the child and gradually increased. Acute GVHD (skin, I°) was considered, and the antirejection drug was adjusted to methylprednisolone 2 mg/kg/d, oral ruxolitinib and mycophenolate mofetil. On day 71 after transplantation, the patient was discharged from the hospital for observation with improvement of the rash and body temperature.

On post-transplantation day 83, the patient was admitted to the hospital again with recurrent fever, scleral yellow staining, edema of both eyelids and feet, and decreased heart rate, and the cerebrospinal fluid DNA sequencing monitoring was completed, suggesting that the number of sequences of Enterococcus faecalis was 410, the number of sequences of type A fine cyclovirus type 2 was 26, and the number of sequences of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex group was 22. Anti-infection treatment was started. In addition, cardiopulmonary resuscitation and endotracheal intubation were performed, and the patient was placed on respiratory-assisted ventilation. However, the patient’s condition continued to deteriorate, and he eventually died of septic shock, multiple organ failure, and disseminated intravascular coagulation on post-transplantation day 95.

Case 2

Patient 2 was a 2-month-old male infant with SCID due to adenosine deaminase deficiency(ADA), with TBNK lymphocyte subsets suggestive of T- and B-lymphocyte deficiency and NK-cell reduction. The child had already been vaccinated with the BCG vaccine at birth and had no history of exposure to tuberculosis patients. On the 6th day after transplantation, the patient experienced recurrent fever, after full coverage anti-infective treatment was given, there was no significant improvement in the temperature of the patient. Blood mNGS for pathogens detected adenovirus type A (sequence number 11067) and M. tuberculosis (sequence number 3), due to the small number of tuberculosis sequences, no special treatment was given, and the patient was considered to have implantation reaction, and the temperature returned to normal after administration of gamma globulin and methylprednisolone treatment. However, on the 21 st day after transplantation, he continued to have recurrent high fever. Subsequent blood mNGS results revealed adenovirus type A (sequence number 35628) and M. tuberculosis (sequence number 4). We immediately performed TB-related blood tests, which were negative for anti-Mycobacterium tuberculosis antibody and T-SPOT. At + 31 days post-transplantation, patient 2’s cells were successfully implanted. Due to the possibility of M. tuberculosis bacteremia, a lung CT scan was taken. The scan showed mild inflammatory changes in the left lung and swelling of the left axillary lymph node. Based on these findings, the patient was administered a combination of isoniazid (3/5 tablets/day), pyrazinamide (125 mg/day), and linezolid (60 mg, 3 times a day) for anti-tuberculosis treatment. On day 32 after transplantation, the NGS results indicated that the patient was negative for M. tuberculosis. As the patient’s condition had stabilized, he was discharged from the hospital and asked to continue with the oral treatment regimen for tuberculosis.

The patient’s left axillary lymph node gradually enlarged without fever, there was redness and inflammation at the BCG vaccination site, and on post-transplantation day 111, a color ultrasound examination revealed a mixed hypoechoic area measuring approximately 3.2 cm × 2.3 cm × 2.5 cm in the left axillary area, with unclear borders. It was suspected to be a post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder, but it was not considered at this time because the patient was negative for EBV antibodies as well as DNA. Subsequent biopsy of the left shoulder and axillary lymph nodes confirmed left axillary lymph node tuberculosis. Due to a decrease in hemoglobin in the blood routine examination, the patient’s treatment regimen was modified. Linezolid and pyrazinamide were discontinued and replaced with levofloxacin and ethambutol for anti-tuberculosis treatment. As the time of completion of this case report, the patient had successfully completed a year and a half of anti-tuberculosis treatment and was off the anti-tuberculosis regimen.

Discussion and conclusions

In the present report, we have described two pediatric cases of SCID combined with BCG-associated complications in which the patients received salvage therapy with allogeneic HSCT. BCG-osis is a significant complication of BCG vaccination in patients with SCID, and children with SCID are more susceptible to BCG-associated complications [12], particularly those receiving HSCT, and its early diagnosis and treatment are crucial to ensuring survival. However, there is a paucity of clinical studies and reports on the occurrence of BCG complications in pediatric patients with SCID treated with salvage HSCT. Therefore, the findings from the cases described here would be useful for clinicians in the future and may provide guidance on clinical decision making in such cases.

The diagnosis of tuberculosis is also complicated by the findings of radiological examinations. For example, in Patient 1, lung CT did not reveal any abnormalities. Although CT imaging can help detect TB, it is not uncommon to find atypical imaging patterns, which may require differentiation from other bacterial infections and cannot provide a definitive diagnosis. Additionally, CT cannot be used to detect latent infection and should only be used as supporting evidence for TB diagnosis. Therefore, imaging can only be used as supporting evidence for the diagnosis of TB. In addition, the radiological findings could be misleading in cases where other infections are also present. The diagnosis of tuberculosis is further complicated by the limitations of other methods that are typically used for its diagnosis. However, recent studies have shown that metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) has high specificity in confirming the presence of tuberculosis [16], especially in cases of extrapulmonary tuberculosis. This was also evident in patients 1 and 2 in our study, as both patients initially presented with unknown fever, and despite receiving broad-spectrum antibiotics and immunosupportive therapy, their temperatures did not significantly decrease, and mNGS analysis of their blood samples was indicative of M. tuberculosis infection. Therefore, it is recommended that mNGS be considered in cases where there is a clinical suspicion of tuberculosis as traditional culture and molecular biological methods may yield inconclusive results.

Managing tuberculosis in the long term is challenging because of the development of drug resistance and drug-induced hepatotoxicity and drug interactions. For example, Patient 1 developed cutaneous jaundice, progressive elevation of total bilirubin levels, and impaired liver function, as a result of which isoniazid was prematurely discontinued. Thus, the toxicity of anti-tuberculosis drugs, their interaction with pretreatment drugs, the development of GVHD, and the use of immunosuppressants make the prevention and treatment of allo-HSCT combined with tuberculosis infection complicated and difficult.

Among the two patients in our report, only Patient 2 is currently alive and has successfully completed the course of anti-tuberculosis drugs. Thus, in patients who have SCID and have been vaccinated with BCG, there is a predisposition to BCG reactivation during HSCT therapy and mortality appears to be high. Consistent with our findings, the results of Marciano et al. confirmed that patients with SCID have a higher risk of comorbid disseminated BCG-osis that leads to increased mortality [17]. Therefore, timely screening for tuberculosis is crucial in such cases. Additionally, as patients with primary immunodeficiency conditions are susceptible to tuberculosis, they should receive antituberculosis medications before transplantation, and tuberculosis should be controlled as much as possible before transplantation, if the primary condition allows. Particularly in patients with SCID, the BCG vaccination is associated with a high incidence of complications, which increases the risk of morbidity and mortality. In a study reported by Mazzuchelli [18] et al.data were collected on 70 pediatric patients with SCID from 23 centers: 60 of the 70 patients received BCG vaccination before disease diagnosis, and 12 of 30 (42%) HSCT recipients died, mainly from infection and BCG-related complications. Therefore, in patients with SCID who have received this vaccination, it is necessary to carefully screen for BCG-osis and administer anti-tuberculosis drugs both before and after transplantation.

Literature review of combined BCG-related complications in patients with SCID

Countries that are still encouraging early BCG vaccination need to start implementing neonatal SCID screening. Until a safer and more effective anti-tuberculosis vaccine is available, it is advisable to consider delaying BCG vaccination in order to protect highly vulnerable individuals from preventable complications. Moreover, it is important to delay BCG vaccination and implement neonatal SCID screening to ensure vaccination is not administered in cases where it is contraindicated [17]. In some developed countries, newborn screening for SCID has been conducted with the T cell receptor excision ring test, which allows for the diagnosis and timely intervention of SCID patients before symptoms appear [19]. In the future, this test could help identify individuals in whom the BCG vaccination is counterindicated. As the recovery of T cell count and function after transplantation is a slow process that typically takes at least one year [20], there is still a high risk of opportunistic infections, including BCG reactivation, within the first year after transplantation. Therefore, it is important for clinicians to remain vigilant throughout this period and regularly screen for this infection. Finally, in all recipients who develop tuberculosis infection and receive treatment, gradual discontinuation of medication can be considered once immune reconstruction has occurred.
