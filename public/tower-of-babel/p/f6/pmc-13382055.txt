Interaction mechanisms of oat starch and three types of oat lipid during simulated Sanshu processing
Authors: Pai Peng, Xiaolong Wang, Liang Zou, Haelee Fenton, Vicky Solah, Xinzhong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe large amount of lipids in oats have an impact on the structure of oat starch during thermal processing. To further elucidate effect of lipids on starch during traditional oat processing, this study examined starch–lipid interactions during Sanshu processing by combining oat starch (S) with oleic acid, monoolein, and triglyceride to form starch–lipid complexes (SO, SM, ST). The structure of the complex was characterized and the results revealed that the interaction between monoolein and starch was the strongest (CI 71.88–79.63%), followed by oleic acid (CI 38.82–54.83%), whereas the interaction with triglyceride was relatively weak (CI 2.66–9.00%). Both monoolein and oleic acid bind to starch to form V-type complex, whereas triglyceride predominantly adheres to the starch surface as a protective layer. Molecular dynamics simulations also verified this mechanism. Notably, the interaction in SM significantly enhances the rheological properties, while triglycerides' inhibition may enhance food digestive resistance.Keywords: Oat starch, Oat starch-lipid interaction, Oleic acid, Monoolein, Triglyceride

Introduction

Chinese traditional oat flour products processing comprises three continuous thermal treatments (Sanshu processing, Supplementary Fig. 1): Yishu (roasting thermal treatment, RTT), Ershu (boiling thermal treatment, BTT), and Sanshu (steaming thermal treatment, STT). Sanshu processing can improve the processing quality of oats, extend the shelf life of oats products, improve the sensory and texture quality of oat noodles, and promote the formation of unique flavor substances of oats (Gu et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2010).

Starch is the primary major component of oats (approximately 60%), which structure has significant effects on the processing quality of oat flour (Shen et al., 2024). Sanshu processing can play a pivotal role in its effect on starch structure and rheological properties. For roasting thermal treatment, oats are adjusted to 20–25% moisture and belong to low humidity thermal treatment (Ruge et al., 2011). In the low moisture system, high temperature roasting causes significant changes in oat components, especially starch (Gu et al., 2022; Hu et al., 2010). Previous study indicated that roasting could significantly destroy the crystallinity and lower the gelatinization enthalpy of starch (González et al., 2007). For boiling thermal treatment, a significant amount of boiling water (>60%) is added to oat flours, which will have greater impacts on starch, including starch gelatinization, disintegration, and molecular rearrangement (Gu et al., 2023; Pinto et al., 2012). The application of this type of thermo-mechanical treatment is found to facilitate the interaction between starch and other small molecules (Peng, Wang, Zou, et al., 2022). Furthermore, the moderate gelatinization and boiling water kneading could modify the rheological characteristics and improve the texture and processing adaptability of oat dough (Gu et al., 2023). For steaming thermal treatment, oat noodles are heated by high temperature steam from bottom to top, the starch is further gelatinized and able to easily interact with other components (Zhang et al., 2018). It also has been studied that the pre-baking-steaming treatment could improve the digestion resistance of starch by starch-lipid/starch-protein interaction (Zou et al., 2023).

Oats also contain a large amount of lipids (2–12%), which significantly influence oat product properties (Shen et al., 2024). The lipid is broken down into fatty acids or aromatic hydrocarbons during roasting, which creates rich flavor and inhibits off-flavor development for oats (Hu et al., 2010; McGorrin, 2019; Yang et al., 2023). Roasting also promotes the release of lipids, leading to the increase of lipid content (Gu et al., 2022). Meanwhile, lipids are small molecules that are thought to have great potential to interact with starch during cooling stage after thermal treatment (Zhao et al., 2024). In high-moisture conditions (such as boiling, steaming), thermal treatment causes starch to transform from a double helix into a single-helical structure through and then form hydrophobic cavities (Chen et al., 2025). This transformation is facilitated by an array of intermolecular forces, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, electrostatic interactions, van der Waals forces, and CH-π interactions (Zhou, Chen, et al., 2025). Then during the recrystallization of α-starch, the nonpolar portion of lipids can enter the hydrophobic cavity of the starch single helix and form V-type inclusive complexes, which is also the traditional type 5 resistant starch (RS5) (Gutiérrez & Tovar, 2021). Although amylopectin could also bind to lipids (Reddy et al., 2019), amylose remains the primary component of the starch-lipid complexes (Gutiérrez & Bello-Pérez, 2022). V-type structures are divided into two categories based on the thickness of the crystal layer and crystal size, amorphous or small ordered structures referred to as type I or VI (melting point 95–105 °C) and lamellar crystal structures known as type II or VII (melting point 110–130 °C) (Zhou et al., 2022; Zhou, Wang, et al., 2025). Numerous studies have revealed that the starch-lipid complexes can alter the properties such as digestibility, thermal stability, or texture in food and pharmaceutical applications (Feng et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2020; Seo et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2025). Starch-lipid complexes are also studied for their potential health benefits (Wang et al., 2024).

At present, studies on starch and lipids mainly focus on starch-fatty acid interactions, which lipids usually have small molecular size and simple molecular structure (Feng et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024; Reddy et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2022). Also, starch could not complex with oils or triglyceride, and oils were mainly distributed on the starch granule surface, which can inhibit the water absorption and gelatinization of starch (Li et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2022). The interaction intensity between fatty acids of varying chain lengths and starch differs. Notably, the 12‑carbon fatty acid exhibits a higher propensity to interact with starch, resulting in the formation of RS5 (Wu et al., 2022). Most of these starch-lipid studies are based on a binary model system, which mainly based on gel or paste systems with a very high moisture content (such as 20 g starch in 300 mL water) (Sun et al., 2025), with samples prepared using RVA or Mixolab (Cai et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2025). However, these conditions do not correspond to traditional food processing methods, which not conducive to reflecting the really situation in food processing.

In oats, lipids primarily consist of polyunsaturated fatty acid triglycerides—mainly oleic (about 38%), linoleic (about 35%), and palmitic acid (about 16.5%), which are mostly composed of a mixture of various triglycerides (Kouřimská et al., 2018; Pokhrel et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024). Owing to their large molecular size and steric hindrance, these lipids may be unable to penetrate the hydrophobic core of the amylose helix (Chen et al., 2025). Furthermore, traditional oat-food processing involves complex procedures, including roasting, boiling, and steaming, each of which may differentially affect starch–lipid interactions. Despite the importance of these interactions, both the overall interactions during oat-food processing and the specific forms of interactions between starch and different lipids remain inadequately understood. Therefore, in this study, three representative types of lipids in oats (oleic acid, monoolein, triglyceride) were selected and combined with oat starch to perform a simulated Sanshu processing to make starch-lipid complex, and their impact on the structure and rheological properties of oat starch were analyzed. The purpose is to elucidate the interactions between starch and lipids during Sanshu processing of traditional Chinese oat flour products.

Materials and methods

Materials

Oat grains of Bayou-18 variety were planted and harvested in Zhangjiakou city, Hebei province in 2021. The collected grains were stored at 4 °C for starch isolation.

Oleic acid (O815202) and monoolein (M813316) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Triglyceride (S31045) was purchased from Shanghai Shyuanye Biosystem Co., Ltd.

Isolation of oat starch

Refer to the previous method (Tang et al., 2019) with minor modification. Crushed oat grains were dissolved in NaOH solution (0.01 mol/L) at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v), and then the pH was adjusted to 10.0 by HCl. The solution was stirred for 2 h at 30 °C and then filtered at 200 mesh. The filtrate was centrifuged at 4000g for 15 min. A small amount of protein from the upper layer of the precipitate was removed, followed by dispersing with petroleum ether and stirring for 6 h to remove lipids. The extract was centrifuged again, washed three times with 95% ethanol and three times with distilled water, then finally freeze-dried to obtain native oat starch (S0).

The starch content of the isolated oat starch was 91.43%, and the amylose content was 22.58% (determined by Total Starch/ K-AMYL test kid, Megazyme, Ireland). Apart from starch, it also contained small amounts of other components, mainly about 1.89% protein and 1.06% lipid.

Preparation of starch-lipid complexes by simulated Sanshu processing

Native oat starch (S0) was mixed with the three lipids (oleic acid, monoolein, and triglyceride) at 10︰1 (w/w) by a KM336 Stand Mixer (Kenwood, Guangdong, China) for 5 min and stable at 40 °C for 10 h. To simulate the traditional oat-food processing of different complexes, three mixtures were regulated to 25% moisture content and roasted at 150 °C for 20 min to obtain the RTT samples, respectively named SO1, SM1, and ST1. The RTT samples (100 g) were then mixed with boiled water (90 g) and squeezed into oat noodle-slices (BTT sample, respectively named SO2, SM2, and ST2). Finally, the BTT samples were steamed for 10 min to obtain the STT samples, respectively named SO3, SM3, and ST3. Furthermore, pure oat starch was also treated with simulated processing as control, respectively named S1, S2, and S3. With a natural cooling process (about 2 h), all samples were freeze-dried and powdered for further experiments.

Determination of complexation index (CI)

The CI values of the different starch-lipid complexes were determined by the previously proposed method (Chen et al., 2025). The starch samples (1.0 g) were mixed with distilled water (10 mL) and incubated at 95 °C for 3 h. The prepared starch solution was cooled to 25 °C and centrifuged at 4000 g for 10 min. The supernatant (0.5 mL) was added to distilled water (8.0 mL) and 1 mL of iodine solution (containing 2.0% KI and 1.3% I2), and then stabilized 15 min before measuring the absorbance at 690 nm. Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) analysis

Oat starch and starch-lipid complexes (50 mg) were suspended in 1 mL of distilled water, and sequentially stained with fluorescein-5-isothiocyanate solution (FITC, 50 μL, 2 mg/mL in acetone) and Nile Red solution (50 μL, 2 mg/mL in acetone) for 12 h. The upper layer of the dye and free lipids were subsequently removed by centrifugation at 8000 g and more than 5 times washing was applied to make the supernatant clear. The stained samples (precipitate) were suspended in 1 mL aqueous glycerol (50%, v/v). An appropriate amount of the suspension was aspirated and dropped onto a slide, and CLSM (FV1200, Olympus, Japan) was used to observe the microstructure of the starch and starch-lipid complexes at 60×. The excitation/emission wavelengths for FITC and Nile Red were operated at 488/518 nm and 568/625 nm, respectively.

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analysis

The gel permeation chromatography system (GPC, Wyatt, USA) was used to detect oat starch and the complexes as reference to the previous method (Zou et al., 2023). The mobile phase, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) containing LiBr (100 mmol/L). Starch (25 mg) was dissolved in 5 mL of mobile phase and heated at 80 °C for 16 h with continuous shaking to ensure complete dissolution. All solutions were filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE filter film before being injected into the GPC system.

Thermal characteristics analysis

Oat starch and starch-lipid complexes (3 mg) was added with 9 mg of deionized water and placed in a pan for 10 h. The thermodynamic characteristics were determined by a Q600 differential scanning calorimeter (DSC, Mettle Toledo, USA). Each pan was heated from 20 °C to 150 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, with an empty pan as the control (Zhou et al., 2025).

Another 6 mg of each sample were determined by a STA449F5 thermal gravimetric analyzer (TGA, Netzsch, Germany). Each sample was heated from 25 °C to 600 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, and the derivative thermos-gravimetry (DTG) curve were calculated based on the TG curve recorded by TGA.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

The crystal characteristics of oat starch and starch-lipid complexes were analyzed using a D8 Advance XRD (Bruker, Germany). The sample was flattened on the slide, and the scanning ranged from 4° to 40° at a rate of 10°/min and 40 KV, 30 mA operation conditions (Peng et al., 2025). The relative crystallinity was calculated using the MDI Jade 6.0 (Materials Data Inc., Livermore, CA).

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy analysis

The oat starch and starch-lipid complexes were mixed with dried pure KBr at a ratio of 1︰100 to form a slice under pressure of 11 LBS, and the FTIR spectrum obtained by a Vertex 70 FTIR spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) at the range of 400–4000 cm−1.

Solid state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (13C NMR) analysis

The solid-state 13C CP/MAS spectra of oat starch and starch-lipid complexes were recorded on a JNM-ECZ400R/S1 13C NMR (Japan Electron Optics Laboratory, Japan). The double helix proportion was defined as the ratio of fitting peak area of C2,3,5 (68–78 ppm) to the total fitting peak area, the single helix proportion was defined as the ratio of the fitting peak area of V-type characteristic peak of C1 (94–105 ppm) to the total fitting peak area, and the proportion of the amorphous phase (PPA) was defined as the ratio of the fitting peak area of C4 (80–84 ppm) to the total area (Yin et al., 2018).

Liquid 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) analysis

Oat starch and starch-lipid complexes (Pre-treat with petroleum ether for removing the excess lipids) (20 mg) were mixed with 1 mL of deuterated DMSO, continuously shaken for 10 h and centrifuged at 10,000 g. The supernatant was transferred to a liquid NMR tube, and an AVANCE III 600 NMR spectrometer (1H NMR) was used to record the 1H NMR spectra at 600 M Hz. Further analysis was conducted using MestReNova software.

Molecular dynamics simulation

The linear amylose molecule (polymerization degree 30) was constructed using the Carbohydrate Builder of GLYCAM-Web. The monoolein and triglyceride were constructed in Chem3D, optimized using the MM2 module, and visualized and analyzed using Pymol 2.1. The oleic acid structure was obtained from the PubChem database. Molecular docking of the linear amylose and three lipid molecules was performed using the MOE2019.01 molecular operation environment.

To study the binding mechanism, molecular dynamics simulations were carried out on selected complexes using Gromacs 2020. The small molecule topology was constructed for lipid ligands using sobtop, charges fitted using RESP, and TIP3P explicit water model selected. Sodium or chloride ions were used to neutralize system charges. The workflow included energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm for a maximum of 50,000 steps. The minimized systems were then gradually heated from 0 to 300 K under the NVT ensemble for 500 ps. Subsequently, NPT equilibration was performed for 1 ns at 300 K and 1 bar to stabilize the system density and pressure. After equilibration, 200 ns production molecular dynamics simulations were performed for each amylose-lipid complex under the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 bar. Constraints were applied to small molecule and heavy atom positions, with 10,000 steps of energy minimization on water molecules. The system was heated to 300 K and equilibrated before the 200 ns simulation in the NPT ensemble. Trajectory data was saved every 20 ps and analyzed with trjconv. The structural stability and binding behavior of each complex was analyzed by calculating the root mean square deviation, root mean square fluctuation, radius of gyration, solvent-accessible surface area, intermolecular hydrogen bonds, and center-of-mass distance.

Contact angle tester analysis

The hydrophilicity of oat starch and starch-lipid complexes was determined by referring to our previous method (Wang, Liu, Sun, et al., 2025). Oat starch and starch-lipid complexes were pressed into thin flakes and the contact angle of pure water between every flake was determined using an OCA20 dynamic optical contact angle tester (KRÜSS GmbH., Hamburg, Germany).

Rheological behavior analysis

According to the procedure described previously (Sun et al., 2023), the oat starch and starch-lipid complexes were mixed with water (10%, w/v) at 95 °C for 30 min to prepare a gel. Frequency sweep at an oscillation mode was conducted on gel samples in a frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz at 25 °C using a dynamic rheometer (MCR 302, Anton Par, Germany).

In vitro starch digestibility analysis

Referring to our previous method (Peng, Wang, Liao, et al., 2022), the digestive characteristics of starch and starch-lipid complexes (100 mg) were simulated using glucosidase (from Aspergillus Niger, 2500 U/mL, A107823, Aladdin) and 4 mL pancreas solution (from Pig Pancreas, 3000 U/mL, P1750, Sigma) in a centrifuge tube under constant temperature oscillation at 37 °C. The starch digestion curves were obtained by plotting the starch digestion ratio (%) as a function of time (min). The relative contents of rapidly digestible starch (RDS, starch hydrolysis at 20 min), SDS (starch digested between 20 and 120 min), and RS (starch hydrolysis over 120 min) were calculated based on the hydrolysis situation.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., USA). The data were subjected to a one-way analysis of variance and significant differences test (p < 0.05) among the parameters that were tested using the multiple range test by Duncan.

Results and discussion

CI changes

Table 1 presents the CI values, ranging from 9.00% to 79.63%, for various starch–lipid complexes. Pure starch served as the blank control for calculating the complexation index in each starch–lipid group. After roasting, the SO1 (CI: 38.82%) and SM1 (CI: 71.88%) samples exhibited significant interactions, forming stable complexes, whereas the ST group displayed a considerably lower CI (2.66%). Subsequently, during the boiling stage, all lipids further interacted with starch, leading to a marked increase in the CI, which reached its maximum values (SO2: 54.83%; SM2: 79.63%; ST2: 9.00%). This enhancement can be attributed to the thermo-mechanical treatment, which promotes starch gelatinization and disintegration, thereby facilitating molecular rearrangement and the formation of inclusive complexes (Peng, Wang, Zou, et al., 2022). Although a large number of complexes formation occurred during the boiling process, the CI value remained lower than that of the suspension system, which made in ultra-high moisture conditions (5%, w/w, dry basis) (Sun et al., 2021). This discrepancy is attributed to the reduced molecular mobility in the simulated food processing system, which restricts further interactions between starch and lipids. Finally, during the steaming stage, CI values decreased in all samples (SO3, 48.34%; SM3, 75.06%; ST3, 5.24%), likely because the excessive thermal treatment promoted the unfolding of helical structures that subsequently formed stable gel networks in oat-based food. Concurrently, the unfolding of the helical structure disrupted a little of complexes with some lipids released, which further contributed to the reduction in CI values.

Different lipids also showed different CI values, with SM > SO > ST, which is consistent with previous studies (Chen et al., 2025). Monoolein exhibited the highest compatibility with starch (CI: 71.88–79.63%) because the glycerol moiety enhanced hydrophobicity, which increased the polarity of the single-chain molecule and facilitated its entry into the amylose hydrophobic cavity. In contrast, the branched structure and significant steric hindrance of triglyceride impede their ability to penetrate the helical cavity, thereby limiting the formation of inclusive complexes with starch (Chen et al., 2025). Additionally, similar to oils, the multi-branched configuration of triglyceride tended to form an unstable protective layer on the starch surface through hydrogen bonds, which could partially inhibit starch hydration and gelatinization (Chen et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2020; Zhong et al., 2024).

Micromorphology

Fig. 1 presents the morphology of starch and complexes as recorded by CLSM. The starch exhibited a green color with staining, while the lipids displayed in red. As can be seen in Fig. 1, the natural starch (S0) exhibited uniformly spherical granules with smooth surfaces, although a small number of clustered starch forms (the typical of oat starch), were also observed (Peng et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023). Following roasting, the starch surface became damaged and turned into polygonal structures, where the destruction of starch granules was particularly pronounced in the S1. In the ST group, some starch retained its smooth spherical morphology due to the wrapping and protective effects of triglyceride, which was similar to oils (Chen et al., 2020). After steaming and boiling, the S, SO, and SM groups exhibited irregular granules as the starch underwent further gelatinization and disintegration, thereby enhancing interactions between starch and lipids in the SO and SM groups. Notably, only the ST group demonstrated additional aggregation, likely because triglyceride is less prone to forming complexes with starch and tends to promote aggregation by adhering to the starch surface.

With added lipids to starch, the red regions in the images facilitate visualization of the binding state between lipids and starch (Chen et al., 2025). As shown in Fig. 1, both the SO and SM groups exhibited pronounced starch–lipid complexes, whereas the ST group showed non lipid presence. This discrepancy could be attributed to the formation of V-type inclusive complexes in the SO and SM groups, while lipids in the ST group did not readily interact with starch and were entirely removed during repeated water washing. Moreover, the lipid components (red) were more evenly distributed in the SO2 and SM2 images, likely due to the formation of more stable complexes after steaming. Additionally, the SM group displayed a higher abundance of these complexes compared to the SO group, as evidenced by the greater extent of red areas, which was also consistent with the calculated CI value.

Molecular weight distribution

As can be seen in Table 1, The average molecular weight (Mw) of natural oat starch was 1.205 × 107 g/mol. During roasting, steaming, and boiling, the Mw of all groups progressively decreased due to starch disintegration and degradation, which always accompanied by the gelatinization of starch and the dissociating of a small amount of short-chain starch (Lu et al., 2023; Peng, Wang, Zou, et al., 2022). This transformation also facilitates the further molecular rearrangement of the starch chain, ultimately leading to the formation of new ordered structures and starch-lipid complexes (Zou et al., 2023).

Notably, the S group exhibited the most pronounced decline in Mw (S1–S3, ranging from 9.848 × 106 to 4.883 × 106 g/mol), which could be attributed to the higher molecular freedom in the single starch system. As without the constraints imposed by other components, the starch molecules were more susceptible to thermal degradation. Additionally, the polydispersity index (Mw/Mn) of the S group increased significantly (from 2.101 to 5.537), further indicating that partial gelatinization resulted in a broader molecular weight distribution and reduced uniformity. In contrast to S group, the starch disintegration of SO and SM groups were lower. This reduction was mainly attributed to the formation of a protective lipid layer on the starch surface, with additional lipids binding to the overflowing starch chains. The protected starch molecules and the complexes maintain relatively high Mw, thereby widening the molecular weight distribution and reducing uniformity. Notably, the disintegration degree of SM group was higher than that of SO group, which promoted more interactions between starch and lipids, a finding that aligns with the calculated CI values.

Furthermore, the Mw/Mn in SM group was higher than that in SO group, indicating a greater extent of starch gelatinization and, consequently, the formation of more complexes. In contrast, in group ST, triglyceride had limited interactions with starch and remain wrapped on its surface, which impede both the gelatinization and disintegration of starch. As a result, the Mw of starch remained relatively high (from 1.062 × 107 to 9.551 × 106 g/mol), but localized gelatinization without lipid interaction contributed to a broader molecular weight distribution and an increased Mw/Mn ratio.

Thermal properties and crystal structure

The DSC curves of starch and complexes are presented in Fig. 2A. After thermal treatment, the starch exhibited a phase transition peak near 110 °C, indicating the formation of complexes within the system. Similar complex peaks were observed across all starch-lipid groups, suggesting that binding occurred to produce stable type II complexes (∼110 °C) (Zhou et al., 2025). The emergence of multiple peak patterns implied that these complexes were polymorphs, with varying degrees of binding that yield subtle differences in the decomposition temperature (Seo et al., 2015). The lower DSC peak temperature, the poorer the thermal stability of the substance. In SM1, the earlier peak suggests a higher presence of unstable polycrystalline complexes, possibly including a portion of type I complexes. In contrast, the increased peak temperatures observed in SM2 and SM3 indicate that boiling and steaming promote the transformation of these complexes into forms with greater thermal stability.

For ST group, the CI value indicated no significant interaction between starch and triglycerides, although the DSC melting peak of the group was notably distinct. This phenomenon may be attributed to the stable crystalline structure of the triglycerides, as supported by the XRD spectrum (Fig. 2B, ST1), with a relative higher melting point (The melting point of solid triglyceride mixtures is usually above 80 °C) (Zéberg-Mikkelsen & Stenby, 1999). As depicted in the DSC curve, after three cycles of thermal treatment, the melting temperature of the ST group decreased. This reduction in melting temperature might result from the successive disruption of certain triglyceride structures by the simulated thermal treatments. In the S group, S0 and S1 did not display any complex peaks, whereas significant complex peaks were evident in S2 and S3. This discrepancy may be attributed to the relatively minor damage incurred by roasting, which limits starch overflow and hinders the formation of intermolecular interactions. Conversely, steaming induced greater damage to the starch, potentially converting some starch chains into short-chain oligosaccharides. The interaction among both large and small free molecules consequently formed a complex that resembles the starch-lipid interaction, primarily driven by the rearrangement of starch molecules.

The DTG curve (Fig. 2B) exhibits a point of greatest rate of change at ∼307 °C (Tmax), indicating the maximum weight loss rate. All complex groups display a broader range of weight loss compared to the control group (S) and exhibit multiple weight loss peaks, mirroring those observed in the DSC curve, indicating complex and diverse structure. This behavior is consistent with progressive loss of crystallinity and molecular rearrangement of the starches after Sanshu processing (Boldrini, 2025). Furthermore, the addition of new substances and formation of stable starch-lipid complex are key factors contributing to the widening of the temperature range for starch weight loss and the increase in Tmax of the SO and SM groups.

Natural oat starch (S0) exhibited an A-type crystal structure, as evidenced by XRD peaks at 15.1°, 17.0°, 18.0°, and 23.1° (Fig. 2C, S0). Following thermal treatment, all oat starch samples (S1, S2, S3) transformed into a V-type structure, characterized by an amorphous configuration and a distinct diffraction peak at 20.0° (Peng, Wang, Zou, et al., 2022). However, when lipids were incorporated, the starch formed pronounced V-type inclusive complexes (typical diffraction peaks at 13° and 20°) in SO and SM groups, suggesting the formation of RS5 in the oat starch-lipid mixture during thermal treatment (Shen et al., 2024), which also presented a typical V6—I type complex consisting of six anhydro-glucose units per turn (Seo et al., 2015). Luo et al. (2020) found it that type I complex developed a V-type pattern with wide diffraction peaks, while type II complex had an obvious V-type pattern and very sharp peaks. In Fig. 2C, the broad diffraction peaks observed at 13° and 20° in SO and SM groups are characteristic of the type I complex (Luo et al., 2020), which contrasts with the type II complex identified via DSC. This difference may be attributed to the simultaneous formation of both type I and type II complexes in the SO and SM systems. Our previous study demonstrated that pre-baking-steaming increased the release of residual starch and lipid fragments, thereby enhancing macromolecular interactions during cooking and promoting the formation of V-type crystals in oat–buckwheat noodles (Zou et al., 2023). As shown in the Fig. 2C, the V-type diffraction peaks corresponding to SO3 and SM3 are more distinct than those observed for the other samples. This phenomenon may be attributed to the formation of V-type complexes during the initial two thermal treatments, which subsequently aggregated in the third treatment, resulting in smaller and more stable crystals.

Furthermore, ST groups exhibited notable triglyceride crystals while sustaining less starch damage during processing. Triglycerides can crystallize into three different polymorphic forms which are α (hexagonal), β' (orthorhombic) and β (triclinic) (Zéberg-Mikkelsen & Stenby, 1999). This distinct lipid crystal structure may contribute to the inhibition of starch degradation during the thermal treatments. Theoretical calculations by previous study further revealed that each single chains of lipids required at least 18 anhydro-glucose units of starch chain in the formation of V-type inclusive complex, whilst one triglyceride molecule requires approximately 35 glucose residues for adsorption (Chen et al., 2025). Thus, Triglycerides exhibit difficulty forming inclusive complexes with starch. Instead, they predominantly adsorb onto the starch surface, where they engage in relatively weak non-covalent interactions mediated by peripheral hydrogen bonds.

Short-ordered structure

Fig. 2D shows the FTIR spectra, including the typical starch region around 1000 cm−1. The degree of order (DO; Fig. 2E) and the extent of double helix formation (DD; Fig. 2F) in starch is usually quantified by the absorbance ratios of 1047/1022 and 995/1022, respectively (Shen et al., 2024). S0 exhibited the highest DD (1.2240) and DO (1.2428) values, which could be attributed to its natural crystalline starch forming a stable structure through orderly arranged double helix. Furthermore, the S group showed a gradual decrease in both DD and DO values during the simulated Sanshu processing, which reduction was primarily due to disruption of natural crystallization by the excessive gelatinization of starch (Peng et al., 2025).

Similarly, in the SO and SM groups, roasting induced a significant decrease in DD and DO values. This observation is attributed to the partial disintegration of the crystalline structure during the transition from the native state to a preliminary pre-gelatinization phase, resulting in the loosening and disordering of the double helix orientation. Subsequent boiling induced a slight increase in the DD and DO values of SO2 and SM2, likely due to short-term high-temperature and high-humidity treatment exposing more starch fragments to the polar environment, which opened the helical structure and damaged some starch chains, and enhanced the potential for cross-linking into additional double helix. Conversely, after extended steaming, SO3 and SM3 experienced a marked decrease in DD and DO values, a change attributed to further sufficient gelatinization of starch fragments under prolonged high-temperature and high-humidity conditions, which produced more free starch chains that rearrange molecularly to form uniform gels during cooling. Concurrently, the fully gelatinized starch molecules exhibited increased mobility, rendering them more prone to interact with lipids through molecular rearrangement, and ultimately forming single-helix inclusive complexes. For the ST group, both the DD and DO values continuously decreased throughout the Sanshu processing; however, the final DD (1.0091) and DO (1.0170) values of ST3 remained higher than those in SO3 and SM3. This phenomenon could be attributed to the presence of a Triglyceride barrier that impeded the complete gelatinization of the starch. Although a certain gelatinization occurred in the ST samples, the spatial arrangement of starch fragments and chains was constrained by the triglycerides, thereby hindering the formation of new ordered molecular structures through rearrangement.

Compared to S group, the addition of lipids led to the appearance of two distinct absorption peaks at 1714/1750 cm−1 and 2854 cm−1. Unlike the S group, the FTIR spectra of the starch–lipid groups (S0, SM, ST) exhibited prominent lipid peaks (approximately 1745 and 2854 cm−1), indicating the presence of uniformly distributed lipids or corresponding starch–lipid complexes, as numerous studies have attributed this phenomenon to the interaction between starch and lipids (Peng et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2018; Zou et al., 2023). In the SO and SM groups, starch gelatinization induced the further molecular rearrangement, leading to the formation of stable V-type complexes with single-chain lipids, a finding corroborated by XRD results. Notably, the SM group showed a significant blue shift in the peak near 1745 cm−1 (to 1714 cm−1), suggesting a change in molecular conformation that may result from a higher complexation degree between starch and SM, which also corresponded to the CI value. In contrast, although the ST group also displayed characteristic starch–lipid complex peaks (1745 and 2854 cm−1), XRD analysis of the V-type peak and variations in short-range order (DD/DO) indicated that starch did not readily form inclusive complexes with Triglyceride, resulting in a very low CI value. This behavior may be attributed to the adsorption of Triglyceride onto the starch surface, where peripheral hydrogen-bond interactions lead to the formation of starch–lipid complexes that differ structurally from V-type complexes.

13C NMR spectra (Fig. 3A) showed a typical starch structure with the characteristic peak pattern of hexose structure from C1 to C6 (Supplementary Table 1) (Mutungi et al., 2012). The broad C1 peak observed at 94–105 ppm is associated with the typical single-helix structure present in the V-type crystalline region or dispersed within the amorphous region, whereas the signal near 76 ppm is positively correlated with the double-helix content in starch (Yin et al., 2018). Typically, the C1 resonance of starch A-type crystals exhibited a triple peak; however, following thermal treatment, the starch adopted a V-type conformation characterized by a single peak (Ma et al., 2024). Notably, among all the samples, only S0 consists of natural starch, while the other treated starches and their complexes predominantly exhibit a V-type structure, in agreement with the corresponding XRD spectra. The calculated proportion of double helix and single helix through spectral fitting, are shown in Fig. 3B and C. Unlike XRD which could only measure the repeating units of helix packed into long-range crystalline structure in starch granule, the 13C NMR is also able to detect the total short-range ordered structure of starch molecule including sub-crystal regions, the overlapped crystal and amorphous region, as well as double helix that are arranged in amorphous regions (Yin et al., 2018).

In the Sanshu processing of S group, the proportion of the double helix continuously decreased (from 57.04% to 53.03%), whereas the single helix increased significantly. This phenomenon is mainly attributed to the ongoing gelatinization and degradation of starch induced by sustained thermal treatment; then upon cooling, numerous starch chains undergo rearrangement to form new single helix structures (from 1.57% to 6.41%). The trend in helix content changes observed in the SM group was similar to that of the S group, although the increase in single helix was less pronounced. This difference likely stemmed from a change in system polarity upon lipid inclusion, which partially inhibited starch molecular rearrangement. Similarly, the SO group followed a comparable trend, where the influence of roasting was relatively minor, with 56.43% double helix and 1.79% single helix in SO1, consistent with the lower CI value calculated for SO1 (38.82%). After boiling, the single helix increased markedly in SO2 (3.36%), primarily due to extensive formation of single helix complexes during high-humidity thermal treatment. However, differences between SO2 and SO3 were not significant, possibly due to a balancing effect between the increase in starch single helix and the decrease in complex single helix during the transition from steaming to boiling, resulting in minimal overall changes. In contrast, the ST group showed little variation in single helix, which remained at a low level (1.27–1.91%), likely due to triglyceride inhibited starch gelatinization and the gel formation, which then consequently hinder the formation of single helix complexes. Concurrently, the double helix content in the ST group remained relatively high (56.47–58.55%), with only a slight decrease after steaming, indicating minor damage to the crystalline region.

Changes of 1H NMR spectra

Fig. 4 shows the 1H NMR spectra of the starch and complexes. The 1H NMR spectra display characteristic signals of starch molecules within the range of 3.3 to 6.0 ppm (Supplementary Table 1), which are related to the hydrogen protons of glucose units (3.30 to 3.70 ppm) and 1-β-H protons (5.10 ppm) in the starch (Chi et al., 2008; Ma et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2021). Protons at 4.60 ppm, 5.s0 ppm, and 5.50 ppm in the spectra can be attributed to the 6-OH, 2-OH, and 3-OH of the glucose units in the starch (Dona et al., 2007). As the Sanshu processing conducted, the H signals in the S group intensified (Fig. 4A). This enhancement could be attributed to the gradual gelatinization and disintegration of the starch, which increased the solubility, on account of starch dissolution occurring through the peeling of the granule in anhydrous DMSO (Dona et al., 2007). Concurrently, the DB value in the S group decreased after roasting, primarily due to the partial debranching of the macromolecular amylopectin in the crystalline region.

The 1H NMR spectrum shows characteristic signals of lipid molecules within 0.50 to 3.00 ppm (Barison et al., 2022). The bands at 2.25 and 2.00 ppm are attributed to the 1-H on the methyl group and the H on the adjacent methylene group of the –C=C– moiety, respectively, which relates to unsaturated fatty acids in the lipid. What's more, the signals at 1.23 and 1.47 ppm are ascribed to the protons on the methylene group and another methylene group located one –CH2– unit away from the –C=O group; additionally, the proton peak of trace water in the lipid appears in the same region. All three complex groups exhibited significant signals from both starch and lipid protons, but the differences in these signals indicated varying degrees of complexation between the lipid and starch. The signal changes in the SO and SM groups were particularly similar, with starch proton intensities exceeding those observed in natural oat starch without thermal treatment, although the overall signal magnitude was reduced and the DB value was lower compared to the S group. This phenomenon may be attributed to two factors. First, the formation of a lipid film during thermal treatment diminished the proton signals in the starch region. Second, the interaction between a portion of the lipid and the starch led to the formation of a complex that enhances the DB value.

What is worth noting was the emergence of new proton peaks around 4.00 ppm in SM group, which may suggest that the greatest complexing strength, which correlated with the significant CI value. Although the proton peaks for the SO and SM groups were similar, the DB value for the SM group (8.67–12.01%) was notably lower than that of the SO group (14.78–15.22%). This phenomenon can be attributed to the addition of monoolein, which enhanced starch dispersion and debranching, promoting lipid-starch interactions and inclusive complex formation. Similarly, the hydrogen proton signals in the ST group starch increased with Sanshu processing, comparable to the S group trend. The triglyceride's limited starch interaction introduced steric hindrance facilitating starch decomposition and debranching upon thermal treatment, leading to smaller DB values (Lu et al., 2023). Additionally, the poor solubility of triglycerides resulted in weak lipid signals in the 1H NMR spectrum.

The dynamic self-assembly mechanism

To gain an in-depth understanding of the self-assembly behavior and interaction mechanisms between amylose and oleic acid, monoolein, and triglyceride at the atomic level (Fig. 5A), we performed 200 ns all-atom molecular dynamics simulations on the selected optimal complex conformations. Consistent with previous studies, the complex conformations revealed that the formation of the SO and SM complexes primarily involves single-helix embedding, while the ST group exhibits peripheral hydrogen bonding interactions (Chen et al., 2025).

A is the molecular docking situation; B is the root mean square deviation; C is the root mean square fluctuation; D is the radius of gyration; E is the solvent-accessible surface area; F is the the center-of-mass distance between the receptor and ligand; G is the hydrogen bond numbers. SO, SM, ST represent the interaction groups of oleic acid, monoolein, triglyceride and starch, respectively.

The root mean square deviation (RMSD, Fig. 5B) of the system is a key indicator reflecting dynamic stability. Trajectory analysis shows that during the simulation (200 ns), the three complexes exhibit distinct kinetic equilibrium characteristics. Among them, the backbone RMSD of the ST system rapidly equilibrates and stabilizes around ∼1.0 nm (Fig. 5), with minimal fluctuation, which may due to the special initial conformation. The starch and lipids in the ST group cannot tightly bind to form a single-helix embedded complex, merely adsorbing onto the surface of the starch molecule. In contrast, the SM system shows significant RMSD fluctuations in the early simulation stage (0–100 ns), reaching up to 2.0 nm, but decreases markedly after 100 ns and stabilizes around ∼1.2 nm. This suggests that the system undergoes a certain degree of conformational rearrangement before reaching a stable binding state, which could be due to the molecular polarity of monoolein, and also explains the process by which oleic acid autonomously enters the helical cavity of starch to form a V-type complex. The RMSD of the SO group remains stable at a relatively high level (approximately 1.8–1.9 nm), suggesting that the molecular conformation of oleic acid is more likely to form a stable complex with amylose rapidly.

The root mean square fluctuation (RMSF, Fig. 5C) analysis further revealed that, except for localized high-flexibility fluctuations in specific atomic regions (Index 600–700) in the SM system, the fluctuations of most residues were effectively constrained (< 1.0 nm). This indicates that the complexes in the SM group resulted from molecular rearrangement, with the structures gradually stabilizing. Additionally, changes in the radius of gyration (Rg, Fig. 5D) and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA, Fig. 5E) visually reflected the compaction process of the complexes. As the simulation progressed, the Rg values of all three systems significantly decreased from an initial value of approximately 2.0 nm and eventually converged within the range of 1.0–1.2 nm. Simultaneously, the SASA values gradually reduced from an initial value of around 55 nm2 and stabilized at approximately 35–40 nm2. The synchronous downward trends in Rg and SASA strongly demonstrate that the amylose molecules undergo coiling and reorganization in aqueous solution, effectively encapsulating the lipid small molecules within their internal hydrophobic cavities (SO and SM) or tightly binding them on the periphery (ST), thereby forming highly compact complex conformations.

To further elucidate the dynamic interaction patterns between lipid molecules and amylose, we monitored the evolution of the center-of-mass distance between the receptor and ligand (Fig. 5F) as well as the hydrogen bond network. The center-of-mass distance results indicate that the ST complex consistently maintained an extremely close spatial distance (< 1.0 nm), demonstrating a highly tight binding state. In the SM group, the center-of-mass distance exhibited significant fluctuations (2–6 nm) before 100 ns, but rapidly decreased and stabilized within 1.0 nm after 100 ns, which aligns closely with the “conformational rearrangement followed by stabilization” phenomenon observed in the RMSD analysis. Regarding hydrogen bonding, the number of polar groups in the ligand significantly influenced the density of the hydrogen bond network. Monoolein, which contains more polar functional groups, formed the highest number of relatively stable hydrogen bonds with the starch backbone during the later stage of the simulation (100–200 ns), dynamically maintained at 2–4 bonds (Fig. 5G). In contrast, triglyceride, as a representative nonpolar lipid, formed the fewest hydrogen bonds with starch (typically 1–2 bonds), while oleic acid exhibited an intermediate number. This observation suggests that the binding strengths of the three complexes follow the order SM > SO > ST, which is consistent with the differences in their CI values.

Hydrophilicity

Fig. 6 depicts the contact angle, reflecting differences in the surface hydrophilicity (water absorption properties) of starch and the complexes. Notably, a larger contact angle indicates a reduced hydrophilicity of the sample (Wang, Liu, Sun, et al., 2025). Fig. 6 revealed that the hydrophilicity of pure starch increased following roasting, which was attributed to the partial disintegration of native starch, which result in the formation of free starch chains that expose additional hydrophilic groups (Chi et al., 2008). However, subsequent boiling and steaming treatment reduced starch hydrophilicity, which was likely due to the formation of a dense gel during high-moisture thermal treatment and a corresponding molecular rearrangement (re-crystallization) during cooling stage that hide some of the hydrophilic groups. This thermal decomposition of starch was also similar to the enzymatic hydrolyzed starch by Zhang et al. (2021).

For the three groups of starch–lipid complexes examined, lipids significantly influence the hydrophilicity of the samples. The incorporation of lipids followed by roasting markedly reduced the hydrophilicity, primarily due to the inherent hydrophobicity of the lipids. In addition, roasting result in minimal interaction between starch and lipids, with most lipids remaining on the surface and preventing water molecules from accessing the starch. Notably, in the ST1 sample, Triglyceride adhered to the starch surface to form a protective layer, severely inhibiting water absorption and yielding the largest contact angle (118.5°). Conversely, steaming substantially enhanced the hydrophilicity of the complexes. This improvement was attributed to increased interactions between lipids and starch, as the hydrophobic end of the lipids integrate into the interior of the starch helix and expose additional hydrophilic end on the surface. Although earlier discussions suggested that steaming might disrupt the complexes and release bound lipids, the consequent unfolding of the starch helix further exposed hydrophilic groups, thereby reducing the contact angle even more.

Significant differences persist among the three type lipids. The SM group, which exhibited the strongest interaction, also demonstrated superior hydrophilicity, followed by the SO group. This suggested that both lipid embedding and starch chain unfolding contribute to enhancing the hydrophilicity. Notably, throughout the Sanshu processing, the ST group consistently maintained a lower hydrophilicity. This observation implied that Triglyceride have difficulty entering the starch helix and instead primarily adhered to the surface, forming a hydrophobic layer that promoted starch particle aggregation and inhibited starch gelation.

Dynamic rheological and digestive properties

The interaction between starch and lipids leads to the formation of robust network structures known as starch-lipid complexes (Shen et al., 2024). Fig. 7A and B illustrate the G' (storage modulus) and G" (loss modulus) in rheological properties of the starch and complexes. As can be seen in Fig. 7, SM2 exhibited the highest G' and G" values, correlating with its highest concentration of the complex, which enhanced the rheological performance of the starch gel. With regard to the G' values, the response curves for SM2 and S0 were relatively similar, indicating that the SM2 complex gel and natural starch exhibited comparable viscosity. SM1 and SM3 followed it, suggesting that the interaction between monoolein and starch effectively maintained the viscosity of the gel system. A similar phenomenon was observed for ST2, likely due to a minor interaction between triglyceride and starch that still confers a capacity to sustain gel viscosity. The remaining samples exhibited significantly low G' values, attributed either to starch degradation or insufficient starch–lipid interactions, ultimately leading to a diminished gel network (Shen et al., 2024). In terms of G" values, aside from SM2, which exhibited a markedly high elastic modulus, SM1, SM3, S0, and ST2 present similar levels that were superior to others. Collectively, these rheological properties imply that the SM2 complex could substantially enhance the processing quality of oat starch. This type of complex is anticipated to be developed into a premium food additive or employed to enhance the processing characteristics of oat flour through incorporation techniques.

In vitro digestion analysis is shown in Fig. 7C (digestive curve) and Fig. 7D (starch component), which revealed that rapidly digestible starch (RDS) levels continuously increased in all groups during the Sanshu processing, suggesting that traditional oat processing accelerates starch hydrolysis and promotes a rapid glycemic response. In the S, SO, and SM groups, starch components exhibited similar trends during treatment, with resistant starch (RS) decreasing and both RDS and slowly digestible starch (SDS) increasing. Notably, after roasting and boiling, the SO2 and SM2 maintained a relatively high RS content (respectively 44.13%, 44.50%) compared to the S2 (26.95%). In contrast, steaming led to a significant conversion of RS to SDS, resulting in higher SDS levels in SO3 and SM3 (respectively 48.56%, 50.63%). Furthermore, SDS levels in SM2 (31.31%) and SM3 (50.63%) were slightly higher than those in SO2 (30.19%) and SO3 (48.56%), indicating that the increased number of complexes in the SM group promoted greater SDS formation. It was also noteworthy that in the ST group during steaming, SDS was significantly converted to RS (37.75% RS in ST3). This phenomenon may be attributed to insufficient starch gelatinization due to the protection of triglyceride and molecular spatial hindrance, which induced pronounced molecular aggregation and enhanced resistance to digestion—a structural change that was corroborated by CLSM. The processing system for oats exhibits similar characteristics. Oat flour tends to agglomerate as a result of lipid adsorption, which also alters its digestibility. By modifying the lipid composition in oat flour, it may be possible to develop oat-based products with enhanced digestive resistance, thereby significantly improving their nutritional value.

As shown in Fig. 8, a simplified model is presented to further illustrate the interaction patterns between the three lipids and oat starch after Sanshu processing. The roasting process causes relatively little damage to the starch (Fig. 8 SO1, SM1, ST1). Some of the starch granules do not disintegrate; only a low degree of interaction occurred in SO1 and SM1. After boiling and steaming, both the SM and SO groups produced a large amount of type I and type II starch-lipid complexes, which was verified by the broad distribution of complex diffraction peaks in XRD and the complex transformation peaks (>110°) in DSC. In the SM group, due to the stronger polarity of monoolein, the strength of hydrophobic interactions was greater, resulting in the formation of more complexes, with the highest CI value (71.88–79.63%). In the SO group, the interaction strength was relatively lower (CI value 38.82–54.83%), but it had more free starch chains due to complete gelatinization. Specially, in the ST group, triglyceride have a stable crystal structure and maintain it well after thermal treatment, which also easy to adhere to the surface of starch. And the starch is not prone to gelatinize and overflow under the protection of triglycerides, but maintaining a certain particle morphology and easily undergoing particle aggregation.

Conclusion

In simulated thermal treatments for traditional oat-food processing, the interaction between monoolein and starch was the most pronounced, followed by that with oleic acid, while the interaction with triglyceride was relatively weak. Notably, both roasting and boiling treatments gradually enhanced the lipid-starch interactions, reaching a peak complexation index during the boiling stage. Subsequently, excessive thermal treatment during steaming resulted in a slight disruption of the complex.

Both monoolein and oleic acid bind to starch via similar mechanisms, primarily involving the self-assembly of lipids and starch through hydrogen bonding. Specifically, the hydrophobic segments of the lipids spontaneously enter the cavity of the starch single helix, forming a V-type inclusion complex. In contrast, due to its distinct crystalline structure and the influence of molecular steric hindrance, triglyceride is incapable of forming inclusion complexes with starch; instead, it predominantly adsorbs onto the surface of starch, forming a protective layer that inhibits starch gelatinization and promotes starch aggregation, thereby enhancing its resistance to digestion. Changes in the short-range order further indicate that starch undergoes molecular rearrangement upon interaction with lipids, leading to the formation of single-helix complexes. Molecular dynamics simulations further confirmed the differences in the interactions between starch and the three lipids.

Moreover, the strong interaction between monoolein and starch markedly improves the rheological properties of starch, suggesting that this complex holds promise for enhancing the processing quality of oats, while triglycerides are anticipated to mitigate the anti-digestive properties of oat-based foods. In the future, by intentionally modifying the lipid composition of oat flour, it may be possible to enhance both the processing quality and the nutritional value of oat-based products.
