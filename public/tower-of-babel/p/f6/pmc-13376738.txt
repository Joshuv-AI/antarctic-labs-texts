A parallel convolutional neural network with background removal and lesion segmentation for field plant disease severity classification
Authors: Mounes Astani, Mohammad Hasheminejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractToday, the intelligent automation of agriculture has received much attention from researchers. One of the important factors for the success of this automation is the timely diagnosis of plant disease and making a decision appropriate to the existing conditions of the plant. Since the progress of the disease is a determining factor in the type of treatment method, the diagnosis of the severity of the disease is of particular importance. However, accurate diagnosis of plant disease progression depends on various factors, including the availability of appropriate and well-annotated training datasets for designing an efficient diagnostic system. On the other hand, the similarity of the complications of different diseases has made this work challenging. In this study, two tomato diseases, namely Bacterial Spot and Mosaic Virus, are investigated using images collected from the PlantVillage, Taiwan tomato leaves, Field-PlantVillage, and Syn-PlantVillage datasets. The disease severity levels are divided into six stages for Bacterial Spot and four stages for Mosaic Virus, and a specifically designed deep convolutional neural network is proposed for severity classification. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves high accuracy under challenging field conditions and outperforms several state-of-the-art methods.Keywords: Deep learning, Disease severity detection, Parallel feature extraction, Sparse feature selection, Image segmentationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Plant sciences

Introduction

Plant diseases cause substantial yield losses and represent a major threat to global food security. Early detection and accurate assessment of disease severity are therefore essential for effective crop management and sustainable agriculture1. However, diagnosing disease severity remains challenging due to the large diversity of plant diseases, the strong visual variability across infection stages, and the limited availability of well-annotated severity datasets2. Traditional approaches based on expert visual inspection or hyperspectral sensing are often expensive, time-consuming, and impractical for large-scale field deployment3,4.

With the rapid development of artificial intelligence, machine learning and deep learning techniques have been widely adopted for automated plant disease analysis. Recent review studies have comprehensively summarized the evolution of plant disease detection systems from classical image processing to deep learning-based frameworks. Vishnoi et al.5 reviewed machine vision and soft computing techniques and highlighted the superiority of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in learning discriminative features directly from raw images, while a subsequent survey emphasized the importance of robust feature representations for disease classification and severity estimation6. These studies confirm the growing interest in deep learning as a powerful tool for plant disease diagnosis.

Early efforts on disease severity assessment relied mainly on image preprocessing and handcrafted feature extraction. For example, Patil and Bodhe7 segmented diseased regions using thresholding and estimated severity based on lesion shape and area. Sethy et al.8 combined GLCM features, fuzzy logic, and support vector machines to grade disease severity, while Owomugisha and Mwebaze9 used ORB and color features with conventional classifiers for multi-level severity estimation. Hyperspectral imaging has also been explored to capture subtle physiological changes related to disease progression10,11 but such systems often require complex and costly hardware that limits their applicability in real field conditions3.

The emergence of deep convolutional neural networks has significantly improved the performance of plant disease classification and severity estimation. Ferentinos12 demonstrated the effectiveness of deep CNNs for large-scale plant disease recognition, and Wang et al.13 employed transfer learning with VGG and ResNet architectures for multi-stage severity grading. Xiang et al.14 proposed a lightweight parallel CNN architecture (L-CSMS) based on channel shuffle and multi-scale convolutions, achieving superior performance compared with several state-of-the-art models under laboratory conditions. More recent studies have explored multitask learning frameworks15,16, capsule networks17, U-Net-based segmentation combined with attention mechanisms18, and hybrid deep learning and watershed approaches for fine-grained severity grading19.

Despite these advances, several limitations remain. First, many methods are evaluated mainly on controlled laboratory datasets such as PlantVillage, and their performance often degrades in real field environments with complex backgrounds, illumination variations, and occlusions20,21. Second, reliable segmentation of diseased regions remains a critical preprocessing step for accurate severity estimation. Classical segmentation methods and shallow neural networks such as radial basis function neural networks have been proposed to isolate lesion regions22, yet their robustness in unconstrained field images is still limited. Third, although multi-scale and parallel CNN architectures have shown promising results14,23, the integration of background removal, lesion segmentation, and parallel feature extraction within a unified framework has not been sufficiently explored for multi-class disease severity classification under realistic field conditions. In contrast to existing approaches, our method explicitly addresses the variability of field images and investigates the robustness of severity classification across different database conditions. The proposed method

Diseases of agricultural products cause different symptoms on the leaves of plants over time. Accurate and timely diagnosis of plant disease prevents disease progression and further damage to the product. Engineering science has contributed greatly to the economy of countries in the field of agriculture and human health with various processes on images and the suggestion of machine learning and expert deep learning classifications. Despite the research, it is still challenging to diagnose the severity of plant disease in field conditions. Also, creating databases for plant disease stages is an important issue that can be investigated. In this article, Bacterial Spot disease and Mosaic Virus related to the tomato plant in Plant Village, Taiwan tomato leaves, Field-PlantVillage, and Syn-PlantVillage databases are divided into 6 and 4 disease progression stages. The goal is to diagnose the tomato plant disease stage in different conditions of the dataset in the training and testing phase with high accuracy. Therefore, to check the performance of deep learning classifiers in laboratory and field conditions, 16 tests have been performed on six classifiers in four datasets. Then the effect of different processes in these conditions has been investigated. According to the obtained results, a convolutional neural network is proposed. This deep neural network leads to removing misleading information from the image, increasing accuracy, and reducing processing time. It also extracts different effective features from the image by removing the background and segmenting the diseased area of the leaf and the leaf itself. In addition to having layers for background removal and lesion segmentation, the proposed convolutional neural network (BaSPaC) has parallel convolutional layers having kernels of different sizes. The task of these kernels is to extract various features from the segmented image. Along with the segmented images, the original images are also used for feature extraction and classification. A sparse feature selection layer was added after the feature extraction layer to increase the accuracy and reduce the calculation time. Finally, a fully connected layer is proposed to classify disease stages. Therefore, the steps of the proposed method are as follows:

Creating the dataset

One of the crucial issues in the diagnosis and classification of plant leaf disease stages is to have a database that includes different periods of the disease. For this purpose, in this article, the images related to the Mosaic Virus and Bacterial Spot disease of tomato plants in the Plant Village database, Taiwan tomato leaves (https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/ngdgg79rzb/1), Field-PlantVillage, and Syn-PlantVillage (https://drive.g.oogle.com/file/d/15aEoop-GT4OJsm94T91tHy-1SYsWv1Lp/view?usp=sharing) were divided into different stages of the disease. Plant Village database and Syn-PlantVillage have fewer challenges, and the Taiwan database and Field-PlantVillage have field challenges. There are various quantitative and qualitative criteria for dividing disease stages2. To ensure the reliability of these stages, three plant‑pathology experts (each with > 10 years of field experience and > 25 peer‑reviewed publications) independently annotated all images. Disagreements of one severity level or more triggered a consensus meeting in which a multi‑criteria decision framework (lesion area, chlorosis intensity, leaf deformation) was applied until 100% agreement was reached. Inter‑annotator consistency was quantified using Cohen’s κ, yielding κ = 0.87 for bacterial spot and κ = 0.83 for mosaic virus (excellent agreement). Moreover, χ² tests confirmed that the label distribution across severity classes is statistically homogeneous (p > 0.05). All annotation procedures, κ values per batch, and version‑controlled label tables are provided in the Supplementary Material. In this article, Quantitative Ordinal Scale is used to split the phases of the disease. Therefore, four steps are considered for Mosaic Virus and six severity stages for Bacterial Spot25.

In the first stage of the Mosaic Virus disease, the leaf image shows chlorosis and mild mosaic (light green and dark green). In the second stage, a severe mosaic pattern appears together with a change in leaf shape. In the third stage, severe leaf deformation with color change, yellowness, and dentate or crenate margins is observed, and in the fourth stage the deformation becomes more pronounced, leaves become smaller and increasingly yellow.

The first stage of Bacterial Spot disease is a series of chlorosis and yellow spots. In the second stage, the spots are enlarged and a yellow color is formed around them. In the third stage, the spots become larger and cover the sides of the leaf, and the center of the spots becomes necrotic. In the fourth stage, the spots become wider and several spots are connected and take part of the leaf. In the fifth stage, a large part of the leaf becomes necrotic (dead tissue). In the sixth stage, most of the leaves (3/4) become necrotic.

The proposed classifier

We used challenging and laboratory dataset conditions to diagnose the second stage of tomato plant disease. If a classifier has acceptable performance for data with a specific condition, it cannot be guaranteed that it will perform successfully on data with other circumstances. Sometimes, the image that the farmer uses in the field to identify the stage of the disease may have challenges that are not considered by the diagnostic system. Therefore, it affects the classification accuracy. In this article, the aim is to propose a novel CNN structure that has acceptable accuracy if the images of the training stage and testing with different conditions. Therefore, image segmentation is done first with Cascaded MRCNN (Garg et al., 2021) and AISA31. Then the features are extracted from the processed images in parallel. Since the size of the complication changes in different severities of the disease, kernels with different sizes have been used in this structure. Feature extraction has also been performed on the original images of the database without processing. After the feature extraction layer, a sparse feature selection layer is used in the proposed neural network to increase the accuracy and reduce the calculation time. Finally, with a fully connected layer, the classification of the stages of the disease has been done. Figure 3 shows the proposed classification structure. The proposed classification framework.

Image processing

Various challenges, such as noise in the image, low image quality, stray backgrounds, few leaves in the image, etc., in the conditions of the agricultural image database, reduce the accuracy of deep learning classifications. In addition, one of the disadvantages of conventional deep learning classifications is their dependence on the images of the training stage. If the conditions of the training and testing stages are not the same, the classification accuracy will decrease. Therefore, to remove misleading information from the image, extract different features from the image, increase the classification accuracy, and reduce the training time, inspired by two Cascaded MRCNN and AISA networks, similar processes are performed on the input images. In the Cascaded MRCNN framework, the leaf and the diseased part of the leaf are segmented simultaneously. Therefore, in the training phase, two loss functions are evaluated. AISA automatically removes leaf background in laboratory and field conditions. The AISA algorithm uses a hyper-green factor to select some background and target pixels. The GrabCut algorithm is used for image segmentation. Then by comparing the original image, the disease lesion is recovered. The steps of each algorithm are shown in Fig. 4.

Flowchart of the Cascaded MRCNN and AISA algorithms.

Feature extraction

In the aforementioned section, three images, including the removed background leaf, the segmentation of each leaf, and the diseased segment have been obtained. In the feature extraction section of the BaSPaC network, inspired by the parallel strategy of feature extraction in the Googlenet, a network is proposed that can increase classification accuracy with fewer parameters and effective feature extraction. Also, recent studies have shown that parallel processing in the feature extraction stage reduces overfitting and plays a crucial role in classification accuracy2. The output of the Cascaded MRCNN and AISA network is three images, including leaves without background, segmentation of each leaf, and the diseased area of the leaf. The size of these images is 256 × 256. These images are entered as input to the proposed feature extraction model. The size of the images is changed to 224 × 224. As shown in Fig. 5, in the proposed feature extraction stage, three parallel convolutional networks containing kernels with sizes of 9 × 9, 3 × 3, 5 × 5, 7 × 7, and 11 × 11 have been implemented. The reason for using different sizes of kernels in the proposed classification is to extract different characteristics of disease spots and leaves that change in size over time. In each convolutional layer, several kernels are first applied to the image, then a non-linear ReLU function is applied to it. The non-linear function does not change the size of the convolutional layer. Only values less than zero are passed to the output as zero and values greater than zero are passed to the output unchanged. The next step is Max pooling with a specified Stride and Padding applied to the image. In CNN1,2 three convolutional layers and CNN3 two convolutional layers with different numbers and sizes of kernels have been implemented.

Results

The purpose of this article is to diagnose the disease stage of tomato plants in different conditions of the dataset. In this research, first, a data set with the stages of two diseases of tomato plants, namely Bacterial Spot disease and Mosaic Virus, has been created. The images of these two diseases are taken from four data sets: Plant Village database, Taiwan tomato leaves, Field-PlantVillage, and Syn-PlantVillage. According to the dataset’s images, four disease classes are considered for Mosaic Virus and 6 disease classes for Bacterial Spot. All experiments have been run in MATLAB 2018 with 16 Gb RAM and Intel Core i7 CPU @ 2.8 GHz hardware. The size of all images is 256 × 256. The processed images are resized to 224 × 224. The images of the four datasets have been augmented with data augmentation. The ratio of training to test images is considered different in the conditions of different databases. If the conditions of the data set are the same, the ratio of the training images to the test is considered to be 70 − 30. Also, To avoid overfitting K-fold cross-validation method with k = 5 was used. In fact, in this research, we seek to propose a precise classification to detect the disease stage of tomato plants in field conditions. At first, 16 experiments have been conducted to check the performance of deep learning classifiers in the same and different states of the data set, and the effect of different processing in these conditions on the accuracy of the classifiers has been investigated. Then the performance results of the proposed classifier are shown in Sects. 2–4.

Reliability and reproducibility analysis

To evaluate the stability and reproducibility of the reported results, all experiments were conducted twice under identical experimental settings with different random initializations. The performance metrics obtained in both runs were highly consistent across all experiments.

The proposed method consistently outperformed the L-CSMS4 baseline in terms of accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score in all experimental settings. The observed improvements remain stable across repeated runs, indicating that the reported performance gains are not caused by random fluctuations in model initialization or training dynamics.

These results confirm the reliability of the proposed framework and demonstrate that its superior performance is consistently maintained across repeated experiments.

Computational complexity and deployment feasibility

To evaluate the suitability of the proposed framework for real-world field deployment, we analyzed its computational complexity, inference time, memory footprint, and scalability under the same experimental settings reported in the Results section.

All experiments were conducted in MATLAB 2018 on a system equipped with an Intel Core i7 CPU @ 2.8 GHz and 16 GB RAM. The input images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels. The proposed architecture is composed of a ResNet50 backbone with a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN), followed by three convolutional layers with 256 filters and one transposed convolution layer with 256 filters.

The total number of trainable parameters in the proposed model is approximately 14 million, resulting in a model size of about 56 MB. The average inference time for a single image on CPU is 52 ms, corresponding to a processing throughput of approximately 19 images per second. The peak memory usage during inference is approximately 1.3 GB. Since the proposed framework is fully convolutional and supports batch processing, its computational complexity scales linearly with the number of input images. This enables efficient processing of large image batches without significant degradation in performance.

These results indicate that the proposed method achieves a favorable trade-off between classification accuracy and computational efficiency, and that it is suitable for real-time or near real-time deployment in practical agricultural field conditions using standard computing hardware.

Reproducibility and fairness of baseline comparisons

To ensure fair and reproducible comparisons with the baseline and state-of-the-art methods, all competing models were implemented and trained under identical experimental conditions whenever possible. Specifically, all deep learning models were trained using the same training and testing splits, identical data augmentation strategies, identical input image resolution (224 × 224), and the same optimization settings, including learning rate, batch size, number of epochs, and early stopping criteria.

For pre-trained backbone networks, the official implementations and default parameter settings provided by the original authors were adopted. Hyperparameter tuning for all baseline models was performed using the same validation protocol to avoid biased optimization toward the proposed method. In addition, all experiments were repeated under identical hardware and software environments (MATLAB 2018, Intel Core i7 CPU, 16 GB RAM) to guarantee reproducibility. These measures ensure that the reported performance comparisons are fair, standardized, and fully reproducible.

Robustness analysis and generalization ability

To analyze the robustness of the proposed framework under real-world perturbations, we evaluated its performance on challenging field datasets (Taiwan tomato leaves and Field-PlantVillage) that naturally include illumination variation, occlusion, complex backgrounds, shadows, partial leaf visibility, and motion blur. The experimental results in cross-dataset evaluation (laboratory training – field testing and vice versa) demonstrate that the proposed method maintains stable performance under mismatched and noisy acquisition conditions, indicating a strong robustness to real-world visual perturbations. Furthermore, the background removal and lesion segmentation modules help reduce the influence of irrelevant background information and illumination changes, thereby improving generalization performance in field environments. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that the current experiments are limited to tomato leaf diseases. Although the proposed framework is generic and fully data-driven, further validation on additional crops and disease types will be considered in future work to further assess its generalization capability.

Conclusion

This article aims to diagnose the severity of two tomato plant diseases in the same and different data conditions for the training and testing phase. For this purpose, the severity of Mosaic Virus and Bacterial Spot disease of tomato plants in Plant Village, Taiwan tomato leaves, Field-PlantVillage, and Syn-PlantVillage datasets divided into 4 and 6 stages. Then, we evaluated the performance of six classifiers in matching and non-matching conditions of the dataset to detect the severity of the disease in 16 experiments. From these 16 experiments, we conclude that deep learning and RF classifiers are highly dependent on the images of the training stage. If the conditions of the dataset are not the same, in the training and testing phases, the classification accuracy will decrease. If the laboratory images are used in the training phase and field images in the test phase, the classification accuracy percentage is between 33 and 38. If the dataset conditions are the same (both training and field test datasets), the conditions of the training and test images still affect the recognition accuracy. The next concern is to investigate the effect of background removal, Middle filter, Image Contrast enhancement, Image segmentation, and Shadow removal on the accuracy of detecting the disease stage of the tomato plant. In all the experiments, various image processing techniques increased the detection accuracy. The effect of the shadow removal process is less than other processes, but the background removal, the Middle filter, image contrast enhancement, and image segmentation increased the accuracy of the classification. Among the Random Forrest, EfficientNet B3, EfficientNet B4, EfficientNet B5, EfficientNet B7, and AlexNetOWTBn classifiers, the highest accuracy is attributed to EfficientNet B4, EfficientNet B5, EfficientNet B7. Therefore, according to the results obtained from 16 experiments, a classifier was proposed to diagnose the stage of the disease with high accuracy in different conditions of the dataset. At first, the proposed classifier removed the misleading challenges of the image and segmented the leaf and the disease spot by using two parts of Cascaded MRCNN and AISA. Then, in the feature extraction phase, three parallel structures with different kernel sizes were used. Also, feature extraction on the main dataset images with the backbone strategy of Resnet-50-FPN has been done. Then useful features were selected with a neural network and entered into a fully connected layer. The proposed method compared to base methods in matching and non-matching conditions for training and testing. The results show the superiority of the proposed method compared to other methods.

To conduct further studies in the field of plant disease diagnosis in the future, one may create a dataset with different classes of disease severity for different plants. Then, a feature selection layer can be used in the classifier structure after the image processing stage. It also created more parallel networks in the feature extraction stage, with the same and different kernels. It is also possible to change the size of the input images according to the size of the kernels to extract extra features from small and large spots.
