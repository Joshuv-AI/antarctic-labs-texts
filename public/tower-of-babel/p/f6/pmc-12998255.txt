Successful Removal of a Screw Nail From the Right Lower Lobe in a 1‐Year‐Old: New Hybrid Technique: Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Abdullah Aldraihem, Baraa Awad, Mohammad Halawani, Fahad Alsaab, Tamer Abusido
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInstantly, in the operating room, multidisciplinary critical thinking and collaboration between pediatric ENT and pulmonology teams enabled the development of an innovative hybrid technique for distal airway foreign body removal in a challenging infant case. The approach combined flexible bronchoscopy for visualization with malleable forceps through a rigid scope, providing a safe and minimally invasive intervention in comparison to surgery.Keywords: flexible bronchoscopy, foreign body aspiration, pediatrics, rigid bronchoscopy

Introduction

Foreign body aspiration (FB) is a serious medical emergency in children. It occurs when a foreign object enters the respiratory tract. It accounts for up to 7% of accidental deaths in children under 4 years and affects approximately 3.2 to 18.0 per 100,000 children annually, which can lead to significant illness and even death [1].

The most frequently aspirated materials vary from organic to non‐organic, with food materials known to be the most common. This situation occurs when a child inhales an object into the airway, leading to a range of clinical presentations, from mild respiratory distress to acute asphyxiation [1].

Diagnosing foreign body aspiration (FBA) can be challenging due to the wide range of symptoms, which can resemble other respiratory conditions. Children may experience sudden coughing, wheezing, or stridor or show more subtle symptoms that worsen gradually. Early recognition is essential for effective management. While radiographic imaging can assist, it is only sometimes conclusive as many aspirated objects are radiolucent [2].

Rigid bronchoscopy is considered the gold standard for removing inhaled foreign bodies in pediatric patients. However, there are instances where it may not be feasible, and alternative techniques have not been widely reported, especially in younger age groups. In our report, we will discuss a unique utilization of a flexible bronchoscope in cooperation with rigid bronchoscopy tools, which successfully helped to retrieve a small FB that landed at a distal bronchial segment. We will also explain the technique and highlight the importance of collaboration between otolaryngology specialists and pulmonologists to combine the experiences, enhance the chances of success, and avoid more invasive interventions [3, 4].

Case Report

A 1‐year‐old girl with an unremarkable past medical history presented to the emergency room with symptoms of a viral illness. She had a mild cough and decreased oral intake. While the history was unremarkable for other associated symptoms, and her mom denied significant fever, it is worth saying she has 3 older siblings aged 3, 5, and 9 who all play with her all the time. During the examination, she had stable vital signs; her pulse and respiratory rate were within normal limits, and she was well‐oxygenated while breathing room air. She appeared alert and well, without signs of respiratory distress with a blocked nose. However, the physician noted decreased air entry in the right subscapular area during auscultation, which warranted further attention. After that, while clarifying with her mother, she disclosed that the child's earring screw had been missing for the last 24 h, but she didn't witness her swallowing it, choking, or gasping with it. A comprehensive evaluation of other systems was conducted, but it revealed no significant abnormalities or concerning findings. We further evaluated for possible child neglect, but it really seems a very low possibility.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

At this stage, foreign body aspiration was suspected; other differentials included an upper respiratory tract infection, and viral pneumonia as well were considered. Since the foreign body ingestion was unwitnessed, further work was done, including complete blood count and electrolyte testing, all showing no remarkable findings. An incidental finding on the chest X‐ray revealed the presence of what seems like a small nail screw in the right lower lobe (Figure 1). The decision was made for admission to organize with the support of the Aerodigestive service for a combined rigid and flexible bronchoscopy procedure. After 24 h, she was shifted to the operating room for the combined procedure.

Chest X‐ray, both frontal and lateral views, confirms the presence of a metallic foreign body in the right lower lobe, as indicated by the arrow.

Operative Preparation

To begin with, meticulous preparation of the instruments that might be used, either from the Pediatric otolaryngology or Pediatric Pulmonology side, was prepared. These instruments included Parison laryngoscopy, rigid bronchoscopies of different sizes, telescopes, optical forceps, foreign body baskets, malleable biopsy forceps, flexible bronchoscopies sizes 2.2 and 2.8 mm, and standby tracheostomy tubes. The suitable sizes and lengths of all instruments that fit the child's age were ready in the operating room.

Initial Procedure Attempt

The airway was prepared by applying 4 mg/kg Lidocaine all over the larynx and into the airway to avoid any unwanted laryngeal or bronchospasm.

The first assessment was done with a rigid bronchoscopy. While the patient was anesthetized with spontaneous ventilation throughout the ventilation port, a size 3.7 rigid bronchoscope was inserted carefully until reaching the right main bronchus to the maximum level for rigid scopes, during which we continuously observed the oxygen saturation. At this level, we could see the light reflection on the FB but couldn't visualize it fully, indicating an even deeper location. A guided attempt was made using the telescope and optical forceps, which was unsuccessful and confirmed the need for a flexible scope to visualize deeper into the segment. After that, the telescope was removed, and the Pediatric pulmonologist embedded a size 2.2 flexible bronchoscope through the rigid bronchoscope, bypassing its terminal toward the secondary bronchi at the nearest level at which the screw nail was identified at the orifice of the lateral segment of the right lower lobe (Figure 2).

Bronchoscopy view of the metallic foreign body.

We decided to intubate the child to use the flexible bronchoscopy and its tools in a secure airway. Through the ETT size 4, we introduced the flexible scope size of 2.8 with a clear view of the small screw, but we failed to retrieve it by graspers forceps and retrieval baskets due to granulation tissue around the object and the very small size of the retrieving tools that can fit through the 1.2 mm working channel.

Hybrid Technique in Removing the Lower Airway Foreign Body

At that point, we carefully analyzed all available options to avoid more aggressive procedures such as thoracotomy in this young girl. The team adopted a novel hybrid approach as we decided to remove the ETT and ventilate the patient through the rigid bronchoscopy ventilation port and utilize the suction port to insert a grasping malleable forceps (size 7 French) while inserting the flexible scope as a guiding and adjusting tool to support the forceps and move side by side with the camera, giving us visual support till reaching the FB. While the otolaryngologist inserted the malleable forceps, the pulmonologist handled the flexible scope size 2.2 to guide and push the forceps when reaching deeper segments (Figure 3; please see Video 1). Careful attention was paid while advancing to avoid moving the FB deeper. FB was clearly seen at the lateral segment of the right lower lobe bronchus. With the forceps, we managed to grasp the FB. After that, we started pulling both the forceps and the scope slowly, bringing the FB out of the airway (Figure 4).

Post Operative

The child was admitted initially to the Pediatric intensive care unit for observation; she had mild stridor post operative, which resolved in the first 48 h. Consequently, she was discharged in good health without any complications after 72 h from the surgery. She received comprehensive follow‐up care from specialists in Pediatric Pulmonology and ENT. During her scheduled visits with these services post‐procedure, she was doing well with normalized CXR (Figure 5).

Discussion

We presented this case because foreign body aspiration in this age group is quite challenging, especially when it lodges in one of the lower airway segments. The challenging aspect, other than the location of the object and the narrow airway surrounding it, is the technical difficulties, given the limitation of available retrieving tools in this age group.

The harmony and multidisciplinary thinking between Airway teams “Pulmonology and Otolaryngology” was fruitful in creating a combined new technique utilizing the malleable forceps and flexible scope to guide its movement through the rigid bronchoscopy port as described. This innovation helped us spare the kid from going into more invasive surgery such as thoracotomy.

Foreign body aspiration in children can be a terrible experience for pediatric patients and their families and can have serious consequences. A retrospective cohort published in 2020 reported that preschool‐aged children are at risk for foreign body aspiration, with the risk decreasing significantly as children age [1].

The clinical presentation of foreign body aspirations can vary widely, from showing no symptoms to life‐threatening events. Due to this variability and the need for more sensitive or specific investigations, diagnosing foreign body aspiration can be challenging. The most common foreign bodies are organic materials, which are not visible on X‐rays, making diagnosing them difficult for physicians [1, 5].

Several techniques for removing foreign bodies in children have been described in the literature, particularly when standard rigid bronchoscopy is insufficient. These include balloon catheter retrieval (e.g., Fogarty catheter), cryoprobe‐assisted extraction, magnet‐based removal for metallic objects, and even bronchotomy in rare cases [2]. While some of these methods are effective, they often require specialized equipment, carry a higher risk of complications, or involve surgical intervention. In contrast, our hybrid approach using a flexible bronchoscope for visualization, guidance, and pushing, and malleable forceps through the rigid scope's suction port provided a safe, minimally invasive, and cost‐effective alternative, especially suitable for challenging anatomical locations in younger age groups [6, 7].

Although rigid bronchoscopy remains the primary modality for managing foreign airway bodies in children, recent studies have shown increasing utilization of flexible bronchoscopy, especially in cases involving distally located or radiolucent foreign bodies. One review reported rigid bronchoscopy being used in approximately 85%–90% of pediatric FB aspiration cases, with flexible bronchoscopy utilized in 10%–15% either for diagnostic or retrieval purposes [6]. Each method has distinct advantages depending on the case characteristics, patient age, and object location. While rigid bronchoscopy provides excellent visualization of the proximal airways and superior control of the airway, it also allows the use of larger forceps and instruments, which enhances retrieval success for most foreign bodies. However, it has limited reach in distal bronchial segments. In contrast, flexible bronchoscopy offers better access to distal airways. Its smaller working channel, however, restricts the size and strength of retrieval tools, limiting its effectiveness in certain cases. As such, flexible bronchoscopy is often used as an adjunct or second‐line approach when rigid methods are unsuccessful or anatomically constrained. But with current advances in flexible bronchoscopy production, and new retrieving smaller tools and new combined techniques such as in our case, we believe the future would add more roles to flexible scopes than what is happening now.

Complications during foreign body removal from the pediatric airway can be significant and must be anticipated. These include hypoxemia, laryngospasm or bronchospasm triggered by airway manipulation, mucosal trauma or bleeding, and rarely pneumothorax from barotrauma or accidental perforation. Moreover, retained foreign bodies pose additional risks such as chronic cough, localized wheezing, recurrent pneumonia, bronchiectasis, abscess formation, and persistent granulation tissue. These considerations underscore the importance of early intervention, proper technique, and a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach in challenging cases like ours [8].

The rigid bronchoscope is considered the gold standard for diagnosing and managing foreign body aspiration in children, but it has limitations, especially when dealing with distal foreign bodies. However, flexible scopes, which can now reach distal foreign bodies, are recently showing promising results and work as effectively as rigid ones with a great success rate.
