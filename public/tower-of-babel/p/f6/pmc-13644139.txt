Neuritogenic effects of Ulva lactuca aqueous extract on Neuro-2a cells
Authors: Chien-Hsing Wang, Zih-Ting Huang, Kuo-Feng Tai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

In particular, there is a lack of well-characterized neuritogenic agents, highlighting the need for novel candidates. Marine-derived natural products have garnered attention owing to their structural diversity and biological activities. Background

Neurodegenerative diseases are highly prevalent, posing a global health challenge. In 2019, approximately 57.4 million people were living with dementia, and this figure is projected to reach 152 million by 2050 [1]. Alzheimer’s disease, the most common cause of dementia, accounts for 60%–70% of cases and affected an estimated 34–40 million individuals worldwide in 2019 [1]. Parkinson’s disease, the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, affected about 8.5 million people during the same year [2]. Both disorders are characterized by progressive neuronal loss, resulting in profound impairments in memory, cognition, and motor control.

Neuritogenic activity—defined as the ability of a compound to stimulate neurite initiation and elongation—is essential for neuronal differentiation, maturation, and repair [3, 4]. Enhancing neurite outgrowth has been explored as a therapeutic strategy to mitigate neuronal loss and synaptic dysfunction in neurodegenerative diseases [5, 6]. In vitro, neurite extension is regarded as a reliable morphological biomarker of neuroregenerative potential [7, 8]. The mouse neuroblastoma cell line Neuro-2a (N2a) is a widely used model for assessing neuritogenic activity because it responds consistently to differentiation cues, such as serum withdrawal and nerve growth factor (NGF) stimulation [9–11]. Under these conditions, N2a cells adopt neuron-like morphology characterized by elongated neurites, making them ideal for examining mechanisms underlying neurite formation [12, 13]. This model allows for reproducible quantification of neuritogenic responses, typically expressed as the proportion of neurite-bearing cells [7, 11].

Several naturally derived compounds, including hericenones, erinacines, and curcumin, and extracts of Ganoderma, Grifola, and the red alga Porphyra yezoensis have demonstrated the ability to stimulate neurite outgrowth in cultured neuronal systems [8, 9, 14, 15]. However, despite these promising findings, the number of well-characterized neuritogenic compounds remains limited, warranting further identification of effective agents. Notably, studies on neuritogenic activity of green macroalgae are relatively scant. Ulva lactuca (sea lettuce), a green macroalga rich in polysaccharides, flavonoids, and phenolic compounds, possesses diverse biological properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and wound-healing properties [16–20]. Recent studies have indicated that marine algae, including U. lactuca, may exert neuroprotective effects through modulation of oxidative stress and polysaccharide-mediated neural regeneration [16]. Nonetheless, the ability of U. lactuca to promote neurite outgrowth has not yet been systematically evaluated.

Despite the growing body of research on neuroprotective and neuritogenic natural products, several important limitations remain. Neurotrophic factors, such as NGF, are known to promote neurite outgrowth; however, their clinical application is restricted by poor stability, limited blood–brain barrier permeability, and potential adverse effects [21]. In addition, many synthetic neuritogenic compounds have been reported, but their use is often limited by cytotoxicity, low biocompatibility, and narrow therapeutic windows [10, 12, 13]. Marine-derived natural products, particularly algae, have garnered attention as they contain abundant bioactive compounds, including sulfated polysaccharides, phenolics, and flavonoids, which exhibit antioxidant, neuroprotective, and neuritogenic activities [16, 22–24]. Several studies have demonstrated that extracts or compounds from marine algae can enhance neuronal survival, reduce oxidative stress, and promote neurite outgrowth in neuronal models [16, 23]. However, despite these advances, the number of well-characterized neuritogenic agents derived from marine resources remains limited, and the neuritogenic potential of many algae species, including U. lactuca, has not been systematically investigated. In the present study, aqueous extract of U. lactuca was selected in order to provide a simple and reproducible preparation suitable for initial biological screening. Accordingly, this study was designed as a preliminary in vitro investigation to evaluate the neuritogenic potential of aqueous extract of U. lactuca in N2a cells and to determine whether co-treatment with NGF could enhance neurite extension.

Methods

Preparation of U. lactuca aqueous extract

Dried U. lactuca powder was obtained from Kung-Long Ocean Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hualien, Taiwan). An aqueous extract was prepared following the procedure described by Wang et al. [19]. Briefly, 100 g of dried powder was suspended in 1,000 mL of sterile distilled water, stirred at 300 rpm for 1 h at 60 °C, and then extracted at room temperature (22–25 °C) for 72 h. The extract was filtered (Whatman No. 1), and the filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure using a centrifugal evaporator (CES-8080, Panchum, Taipei, Taiwan) and lyophilized. The dried extract was stored at − 20 °C until use [20]. The extraction yield was approximately 13.21% (w/w), consistent with previously reported values (~ 10%–15%) [25]. Aqueous extraction was selected as a simple and reproducible method to recover water-soluble bioactive constituents, particularly polysaccharides and phenolic compounds [25], and to ensure compatibility with cell-based assays. Accordingly, the extract was used as a crude preparation for preliminary biological screening. All experiments were performed using the same batch of extract to ensure consistency.

Cell culture

Mouse neuroblastoma N2a cells were obtained from the Taiwan Cell Line Bank (Bioresource Collection and Research Center, Hsinchu, Taiwan). The cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM; Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with Earle’s Balanced Salt Solution, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1.5 g/L sodium bicarbonate, 0.1 mM non-essential amino acids, 1.0 mM sodium pyruvate, 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide (CO₂) (Panasonic Healthcare, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay [26]. Briefly, N2a cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10³ cells per well in 96-well plates and cultured in MEM for 16 h to allow adherence. The cells were then treated with serial concentrations of U. lactuca aqueous extract (1, 5, 15, 20, 30, and 50 mg/mL) for 24 and 48 h. Untreated N2a cells cultured in MEM served as the control group. Each treatment was performed in triplicate. After incubation, the culture medium was replaced with MTT solution (5 mg/mL), and the plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The resulting formazan crystals were solubilized in isopropanol, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a spectrophotometer (DR900; Hach, Loveland, CO, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were obtained from three independent experiments, each conducted in triplicate.

Neurite outgrowth assay

Neurite induction was evaluated following a modified protocol described by Eik et al. [27]. N2a cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 1 mL of complete growth medium and incubated overnight at 37 °C. To identify the optimal concentration for neurite induction, the cells were treated with NGF (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at concentrations of 5–100 ng/mL. The concentration that resulted in the greatest neurite extension was selected as the positive control for subsequent assays. The aqueous extract of U. lactuca was prepared at a stock concentration of 100 mg/mL and diluted with sterile distilled water to the desired working concentrations. The concentration range (mg/mL) was selected based on preliminary screening experiments and is consistent with that used in studies on crude natural extracts, where higher concentrations are often required owing to the low abundance of active constituents in crude extracts. Before treatment, the complete medium was replaced with medium containing 5% FBS to induce differentiation. Cells cultured in 5% FBS medium alone served as the negative control. For neurite outgrowth experiments, N2a cells were treated with aqueous U. lactuca extract at concentrations of 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 mg/mL. All groups of cells were incubated for 24–48 h under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂) before analysis. Following incubation, the cells were visualized under a phase-contrast microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantification of neurite-bearing cells and morphometric analysis

Cells were defined as neurite-bearing when at least one neuritic process extended to a length greater than twice the cell-body diameter. Undifferentiated N2a cells displayed polygonal morphology. Cells showing irregular spreading, sheet-like extensions, or shrinkage-related artifacts, as well as clusters containing > 5 cells, were excluded from analysis [27, 28]. Five random fields (approximately 100–200 cells/well) were examined per well using an Olympus microscope and analyzed with cellSens imaging software. For quantitative morphometric analysis, neurite length was measured using Fiji/ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Images were calibrated using the microscope scale bar prior to analysis. Neurites were manually traced using the segmented line tool, and neurite length was measured in µm using the Measure function in ImageJ. For each neurite-bearing cell, only the longest neurite was measured and used as an indicator of neurite extension; total neurite length per cell was not quantified. The mean neurite length was then calculated from at least 30 neurite-bearing cells per treatment group in each independent experiment.

Immunofluorescence staining

To confirm neuronal differentiation, α-tubulin immunostaining was performed as described by Shaimerdenova et al. [29], with minor modifications. N2a cells were seeded in 12-well plates and treated for 48 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde containing 0.1% Triton X-100 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.0) for 10 min. After two PBS washes, the cells were blocked with 0.2% bovine serum albumin for 1 h at room temperature (approximately 22–25 °C). Subsequently, the cells were incubated with mouse anti-α-tubulin Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated antibody (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA; #322588; 1:1,000 in blocking buffer) for 3 h at 37 °C in the dark under 5% CO₂. After three additional PBS washes, the nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Fluorescence images were captured using an Olympus fluorescence microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analyses

All experiments were independently repeated three times, with each experiment performed in triplicate. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Data normality was assessed using standard methods prior to statistical analysis. For comparisons involving more than two groups, statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test. Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Exact p-values are reported where appropriate.

Results

Effects of U. lactuca extract on N2a cell viability

As shown in Fig. 1, treatment with aqueous U. lactuca extract at concentrations between 1 and 50 mg/mL increased N2a cell viability at both 24 and 48 h compared with that in the control. However, a concentration-dependent decrease in cell viability was observed at 30–50 mg/mL after 48 h relative to that at 24 h. Differential effects of U. lactuca extract on cellular metabolic activity were observed across the tested concentration range, with increased cell viability at lower concentrations and reduced cell viability at higher concentrations. Consequently, concentrations up to 30 mg/mL were selected for subsequent neurite-outgrowth assays to ensure optimal cell viability.

Effect of Ulva lactuca extract on the viability of N2a cells. N2a cells were treated with various concentrations of aqueous U. lactuca extract for 24 and 48 h, and cell viability was determined using the MTT assay. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test. Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Abbreviations: ANOVA, analysis of variance; N2a, Neuro-2a cells; MTT, 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide; SD, standard deviation

Effects of NGF on neurite outgrowth activity in N2a cells

NGF was used as a positive control in the neurite-outgrowth assay owing to its well-established role in neuronal differentiation and maintenance, as previously reported by Sofroniew et al. [21]. Cells were classified as neurite-bearing if they possessed at least one process extending to a length equal to or greater than twice the diameter of the cell body [27]. As shown in Fig. 2, NGF treatment at concentrations ranging from 5 to 70 ng/mL induced neurite extension in a dose-dependent manner after 24 and 48 h. Notably, neurite outgrowth was significantly enhanced after 48 h of exposure compared with that after 24 h (p < 0.05). Following 48 h of stimulation with 5 ng/mL NGF, the percentage of neurite-bearing cells significantly increased to 7.8% ± 0.58% compared with that in the negative control group (4.7% ± 0.38%) (p < 0.05). At 50 ng/mL, neurite-bearing cells reached 9.1% ± 0.89% (p < 0.05), representing the peak of neuritogenic activity. However, at higher doses (70 ng/mL), neurite outgrowth declined slightly to 7.4% ± 0.62% (p < 0.05), which may reflect reduced cellular responsiveness at higher concentrations or experimental variability. Consequently, 50 ng/mL NGF was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments. At 80 and 90 ng/mL, NGF did not significantly alter neurite formation relative to that in control cells. At 100 ng/mL, neurite formation appeared to decrease (0.9% ± 0.04%); however, this observation should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect reduced responsiveness at supraphysiological concentrations or experimental variability. No significant cytotoxicity was observed under NGF treatment conditions based on morphological assessment.

Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test. Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Effects of U. lactuca extract on neurite outgrowth activity in N2a cells

As shown in Fig. 3a, control cells remained small and rounded with minimal neurite extension, whereas NGF-treated cells (Fig. 3b) and U. lactuca-treated cells (Fig. 3c–f) exhibited enlarged somata and elongated neuritic processes after 48 h of treatment. Extract-treated cells also displayed clearly defined neurite projections measuring at least twice the cell-body diameter. These morphological changes confirmed that U. lactuca extract promoted neurite elongation and neuronal differentiation in N2a cells.

Arrows indicate neurite extensions. Scale bar = 50 μm. Abbreviations: N2a, Neuro-2a cells; NGF, nerve growth factor; FBS, fetal bovine serum

Quantitative analysis (Fig. 4) showed that treatment for 24 and 48 h increased the proportion of neurite-bearing cells in a concentration-dependent manner. The strongest response occurred at 20 mg/mL, where 16.9% ± 1.4% of cells displayed neurite-bearing morphology—significantly greater (p < 0.05) than that in both the negative control (3.03% ± 0.28%) and NGF-treated positive control groups (7.8% ± 1.2%). At higher concentrations (25–30 mg/mL), neuritogenic activity declined relative to that under 20 mg/mL, indicating a biphasic dose–response pattern. After 48 h, neurite formation was more pronounced at lower extract concentrations (5–15 mg/mL) than that observed after 24 h, whereas at higher concentrations (20–30 mg/mL) it decreased over time. Nevertheless, all concentrations between 5 and 30 mg/mL significantly (p < 0.05) increased neurite-bearing percentages compared with controls, confirming that U. lactuca extract enhances neurite outgrowth in N2a cells in both concentration- and time-dependent manner. The concentration of 20 mg/mL was selected for morphometric analysis as a representative concentration that induced substantial neurite outgrowth while remaining within the effective concentration range identified in the neurite-bearing cell assay. Quantitative morphometric analysis based on the longest neurite per cell (Fig. 5) demonstrated that both NGF (50 ng/mL) and aqueous U. lactuca extract (20 mg/mL) significantly increased neurite length compared with that in the negative control group (p < 0.01). The mean neurite length increased from approximately 49 μm in the negative control group to 95 μm following NGF treatment and 132 μm following treatment with U. lactuca extract. Moreover, neurite length in the U. lactuca-treated group was significantly greater than that in the NGF-treated group (p < 0.01). These findings were consistent with the qualitative morphological observations and supported the neuritogenic activity of U. lactuca extract.

Combined effects of aqueous U. lactuca extract and NGF on neurite outgrowth in N2a cells

To determine whether U. lactuca extract interacts synergistically with NGF, neurite outgrowth was examined under combined treatment conditions. Co-administration of extract at a low concentration (5 mg/mL) with NGF at 10, 30, or 60 ng/mL markedly increased (p < 0.05) the proportion of neurite-bearing cells compared to treatment with NGF or the extract alone (Fig. 6). However, combining extract at a higher concentration (10 mg/mL) with NGF at the same doses significantly reduced (p < 0.05) neurite formation relative to extract-only treatment. Moreover, the 10 mg/mL + NGF combination yielded fewer neurite-bearing cells than did the 5 mg/mL + NGF combination, suggesting concentration-dependent modulation of NGF signaling. These findings indicate that U. lactuca extract at low concentrations potentiates NGF-induced neuritogenesis, whereas higher concentrations attenuate this response in a concentration-dependent manner.

Immunofluorescence staining of α-tubulin

In addition to morphological observation under the light microscope, indirect immunofluorescence staining provides a reliable approach for evaluating neuronal differentiation through the detection of neuron-specific cytoskeletal markers. α-Tubulin, a fundamental component of microtubules, forms the structural backbone of axons and dendrites and plays a central role in neurite elongation. Figure 7 illustrates immunocytochemical labeling of N2a cells, with α-tubulin (green) outlining neuritic networks and cell nuclei counterstained with DAPI (blue). As shown in Fig. 7a–d, control cells exhibited limited neurite formation, whereas cells treated with 20 mg/mL aqueous U. lactuca extract (Fig. 7e–h) for 48 h showed more pronounced neuritic structures and visibly elongated neurites compared with those in control cells. α-Tubulin immunofluorescence staining qualitatively confirmed the presence of neuritic processes in extract-treated cells. These changes were more evident at concentrations associated with increased neurite length in the morphological analysis. Neuritic branching was observed in extract-treated cells; however, branching complexity was not quantitatively evaluated in the present study.

Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrated that the aqueous extract of U. lactuca promotes neurite outgrowth in N2a cells in a concentration-dependent manner, with maximal effects observed at moderate concentrations and reduced activity at higher concentrations, indicating a biphasic response. In addition, low concentrations of the extract enhanced NGF-induced neuritogenesis, suggesting a potential synergistic effect. These findings indicate that the U. lactuca extract possesses neuritogenic activity and may be a promising natural source of neuroactive compounds.

Seaweeds are rich sources of bioactive compounds owing to their phytochemical diversity [30–32]. Marine algal polysaccharides and phenolic compounds exhibit antioxidant and neuroprotective activities that may contribute to neurite initiation and elongation [3, 16, 33, 34]. Although chemical characterization was not performed in this study, previous studies indicate that aqueous extracts of U. lactuca contain sulfated polysaccharides, phenolics, and flavonoids [30]. Therefore, the specific active constituents have not yet been identified. The biphasic response of the aqueous U. lactuca extract is consistent with findings from previous studies on marine-derived natural products [23], including algal polysaccharides that enhance neuronal survival and reduce oxidative stress [16]. These findings suggest that U. lactuca extract exhibits neuritogenic activity and may represent a potential source of neuroactive compounds among relatively underexplored green macroalgae. However, the underlying mechanisms were not investigated in this study and remain to be clarified. The MTT assay reflects cellular metabolic activity rather than direct proliferation. During neuronal differentiation, increased metabolic activity may accompany cytoskeletal remodeling, neurite extension, membrane synthesis, and protein production. Therefore, the observed increase in the MTT signal likely reflects the elevated metabolic demands associated with differentiation rather than increased cell proliferation.

Natural molecules, such as hericenones from Hericium erinaceus and curcumin from Curcuma longa, are recognized inducers of neurite outgrowth [8, 9, 14]. Our data position U. lactuca among these neuritogenic natural sources, further supporting the neuroactive potential of marine macroalgae. The biphasic dose response observed here mirrors findings from previous studies, where excessive concentrations of bioactive phytochemicals may induce oxidative or metabolic stress, thereby suppressing neurite elongation [35].

Previous studies have shown that neurite outgrowth induced by small molecules is associated with activation of the ERK/MAPK pathway [10, 12, 13, 21]. In this study, the enhanced neuritogenic effect observed under combined treatment with U. lactuca extract and NGF may reflect modulation of similar signaling responses; however, the underlying mechanisms were not investigated and warrant further investigation. Reduced neurite formation at higher extract or NGF concentrations may reflect decreased cellular responsiveness or experimental variability, highlighting the importance of dose optimization when evaluating crude natural extracts. Possible explanations may include receptor desensitization or feedback regulatory mechanisms, although these possibilities were not investigated in the present study. Similar non-linear NGF dose–response patterns have previously been reported in differentiating N2a cells [9], suggesting that reduced neuritogenic responses at higher NGF concentrations may not be unprecedented in this model. Neuronal differentiation was assessed morphologically based on established criteria [8], and α-tubulin immunofluorescence provided qualitative confirmation of neurite formation. α-Tubulin plays a key role in neurite extension and neuronal structure [35–38]. Our fluorescence observations were qualitative, and quantitative analysis of neurite branching complexity and fluorescence intensity was not performed. However, neurite length was quantitatively analyzed using Fiji/ImageJ and was significantly increased in extract-treated cells compared with that in control cells. Neurite outgrowth was quantified based on the proportion of neurite-bearing cells, a widely used indicator in N2a models. Although additional morphometric parameters may provide further insight, this endpoint is commonly applied in preliminary screening studies, and further quantitative analyses are required to better characterize the effects of U. lactuca extract.

This study was conducted using an in vitro model, and although N2a cells serve as a reliable system for evaluating neuronal differentiation, they do not fully replicate the complex cellular interactions present in vivo. In addition, detailed phytochemical characterization of the aqueous extract was not performed; therefore, the extract should be regarded as a crude preparation, and the specific bioactive constituents responsible for the observed effects remain to be identified. Although neurite length was quantitatively analyzed using Fiji/ImageJ, additional morphometric parameters, including neurite branching complexity and fluorescence intensity, were not quantitatively evaluated. Moreover, osmolarity measurements and inert macromolecule controls were not included, and the effects of high concentrations of NGF on cell viability were not directly assessed. Accordingly, this study should be considered a preliminary in vitro screening of neuritogenic activity. Therefore, further studies, including bioassay-guided fractionation, chemical characterization, mechanistic investigations, and in vivo validation, are required to identify the active constituents of the U. lactuca extract and elucidate their underlying mechanisms of action.

Conclusions

The aqueous extract of U. lactuca demonstrated neuritogenic activity in differentiating N2a cells, increasing the proportion of neurite-bearing cells and promoting neurite elongation. These effects may be associated with the presence of multiple bioactive constituents within the extract, although the specific active components were not identified in this study. However, the observed inhibition at higher concentrations highlights the importance of dose optimization when using crude extracts. Further studies, including bioassay-guided fractionation, chemical characterization, mechanistic analysis, and in vivo validation, are required to identify the active compounds and confirm their biological effects. Overall, our findings support further investigation of U. lactuca as a potential source of neuritogenic compounds.
