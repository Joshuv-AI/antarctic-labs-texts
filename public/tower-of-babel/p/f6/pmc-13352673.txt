Comparative evaluation of implant stability using three osteotomy techniques- a randomised clinical trial
Authors: Nandita Venkatesh, Manoj Shetty, Chethan Hegde, Nandita Sorte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe aim of this clinical trial was to compare the implant stability in terms of primary and secondary stability with three different osteotomy techniques, to establish a protocol that can consistently help achieve increased implant stability in the maxillary posterior region.Materials and methodsA total of 30 patients (19 males, 11 females; mean age 46.3 ± 8.7 years) requiring single implant placement in the maxillary posterior region were divided into three groups. Endosseous implants (Straumann Bone Level Tapered, SLActive surface, 4 mm × 10 mm) were placed in Group I using conventional osteotomy, Group II with Summers’ osteotome technique, and Group III with the osseodensification technique. All implants were placed with an emerged (non-submerged) technique and immediately restored with provisional prostheses adjusted to eliminate centric and eccentric contacts (immediate restoration without functional loading). The stability was assessed at the time of implant placement, at three months and at six months using resonance frequency analysis (Osstell). One-way ANOVA and Tukey post-hoc test were used to compare the ISQ values between groups, and repeated measures ANOVA for intragroup comparisons.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed in the ISQ values at the time of placement, three months, and six months after implant placement between Group I (Conventional osteotomy), Group II (Summers’ osteotome technique), and Group III (Osseodensification). However, numerical trends indicated slightly lower initial primary stability in Group II and slightly higher values in Group III throughout the observation period. All implants achieved 100% survival at six months.ConclusionWithin the limitations of this exploratory study, all three osteotomy techniques achieved adequate primary stability for immediate restoration in the maxillary posterior region. While no statistically significant differences were detected between techniques, numerical trends suggest that osseodensification may offer marginally better primary stability and Summers’ osteotome technique showed slightly lower initial values. These findings should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size and relatively short follow-up period. Further large-scale, adequately powered studies are needed to definitively establish clinical superiority of any technique.Clinical trial registrationCLINICAL TRIAL REGISTRY OF INDIA (CTRIICMR) REGISTRATION NO. Introduction

Since the discovery of the phenomenon of osseointegration by Branemark in the mid-1960s, prosthetic rehabilitation of completely and partially edentulous patients using implants has become a predictable alternative treatment modality with improved treatment outcomes [1, 2]. Osseointegration is the direct structural and functional connection between ordered, living bone and the surface of a load-carrying implant, which involves a complex cascade of cellular and molecular interactions that promote new bone formation and remodeling [3, 4]. Recent studies highlight that osseointegration is not merely a passive process but involves complex biological and mechanical interactions at the bone-implant interface, making it critical for implant stability and thereby the success of implant therapy. Bavetta et al. [5] demonstrated that insertion torque and ISQ can be used as stability parameters for immediate loading of implants placed in fresh extraction sockets. Similarly, Gehrke et al. [6] compared insertion torque and implant stability of two different implant macrogeometries in the initial periods of osseointegration, highlighting the importance of implant design for achieving optimal primary stability.

Factors such as bone density, implant type, and surgical technique play a role in the stability of an implant [7–10]. Studies have shown that the implant stability can be negatively impacted by low bone density, which is frequently seen in the posterior maxillary region where a thin layer of cortical bone encases trabecular bone [11, 12].

Surgical technique plays a major role in implant stability [10, 13]. The stability of implants has been shown to be influenced by conventional drilling techniques, which use sequentially larger burs that cut or remove bone at the site of placement [14–16]. Multiple bone condensation techniques have been proposed to increase the quality of bone at sites with lower density, thereby improving the primary stability of the implant. These include the use of osteotomes, piezoelectricity, lasers, specially designed burs, or undersized preparations to improve bone-implant contact [10, 17–19].

A commonly employed technique to preserve bone while condensing it at the osteotomy site was introduced by Summers in 1994 [20], in which the site is prepared by tapping expansion osteotomes using a surgical mallet [21, 22]. The greatest drawback of this technique is the trauma associated with the use of the mallet and the possible microfractures that could occur at the osteotomy site [23, 24].

A newer osteotomy technique introduced by Huwais and Meyer [25] uses osseodensification burs that are fluted and allow for bone preservation and condensation by compaction autografting. The non-cutting, condensing action of this technique results in higher density bone. A strong and dense thick layer of bone tissue is created along the walls and base of the osteotomy, improving the quality and quantity of bone around the implants, which has been shown to correlate with increased implant stability [26–28]. Recent systematic reviews confirm that osseodensification demonstrates promising results in enhancing primary stability, particularly in low-density bone [29, 30]. Mohammadi et al. [29] conducted a meta-analysis showing that osseodensification technique resulted in significantly higher primary and secondary stability compared to conventional drilling in low-density bone. Similarly, Abdelraouf et al. [31] in their randomized clinical trial reported significantly higher ISQ values with osseodensification compared to conventional drilling in the posterior maxilla at all time points.

Various methods exist to assess implant stability, including assessment of insertion and removal torque measurements, Periotest, and Resonance Frequency Analysis [32, 33]. Resonance Frequency Analysis (RFA) has been found to be a non-invasive and accurate method to quantitatively measure implant stability [34–36]. RFA uses the concept of resonance frequency of an object in response to the application of a magnetic pulse, which correlates with the micromotion of the implant and the stiffness of the bone-implant interface. The measurement is expressed as the Implant Stability Quotient (ISQ) values on a scale from 1 to 100. High stability is considered at values > 70 ISQ, medium between 60 and 69 ISQ, and low stability < 60 ISQ [37, 38].

The purpose of this study was to compare the implant stability—both primary and secondary—using three different osteotomy techniques at the time of placement, and at three and six months postoperatively, to establish a protocol that can consistently help achieve increased implant stability in the maxillary posterior region. The null hypothesis was that there is no significant difference in implant stability achieved with conventional drilling technique, Summers’ osteotome technique, and osseodensification technique.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study was a prospective randomized, parallel-group, active-controlled in vivo study conducted in compliance with the CONSORT guidelines for clinical trials. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee, AB Shetty Memorial Institute of Dental Sciences, Mangaluru, Karnataka, India (Approval number: ABSM/EC29/2019, dated 10-10-2019). The trial was registered with the Clinical Trial Registry of India (CTRI-ICMR) with registration number CTRI/2021/09/036513 (registered retrospectively on 15/09/2021; enrollment began September 2020). All patients provided written informed consent to participate in the study and for their data to be published.

Study population and sample size

Patients reporting to the OPD, Department of Prosthodontics and Implantology, between September 2020 and September 2021 and requiring single implant placement in the maxillary posterior region were considered for the study.

Sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1) at a 95% confidence interval and 90% power, based on the effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.9) reported in a similar study by Markovic et al. [39] This yielded a minimum sample size of 6 participants per group. To strengthen validity and account for potential variability, 30 patients (10 per group) were enrolled in the current study.

A total of 84 patients were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 39 did not meet inclusion criteria and 15 declined to participate, resulting in 30 patients being randomized into three groups of 10 each. No patients discontinued or were lost to follow-up.

The study population consisted of 19 males and 11 females with a mean age of 46.3 ± 8.7 years (range: 32–62 years). The demographic distribution across groups was as follows:

Randomization and Blinding

Patients were randomly allocated into their corresponding group using the lottery method of randomization. The random allocation sequence was generated and implemented by a faculty member (not involved in surgical procedures or outcome assessment), ensuring allocation concealment. The participants were assigned to the study by this faculty member and were blinded to the intervention group.

Blinding of the surgeon was not feasible due to the distinct nature of the surgical techniques. However, the outcome assessor who recorded implant stability measurements using RFA was blinded to the intervention group to minimize detection bias.

Study groups

Participants were divided into three groups:

Group I (n = 10): Conventional osteotomy using sequential osseous extraction drilling.Group II (n = 10): Summers’ osteotome technique using expansion osteotomes (Xive) of progressively increasing diameter to condense bone.Group III (n = 10): Osseodensification technique using multi-fluted, tapered Densah™ burs (Versah).

Surgical protocol

All surgeries were performed by the same experienced surgeon under local anesthesia. A preoperative radiographic examination (orthopantomography or cone beam computed tomography) was conducted to assess bone dimensions and quality.

Conventional osteotomy (group I)

Standard drilling technique was performed using Straumann drills for bone level tapered implants by sequential drilling at 650–800 rpm with copious irrigation.

Summers’ osteotome technique (group II)

Initially, a pilot drill was used to create the osteotomy to the final depth, followed by the use of expansion osteotomes (Xive) of increasing diameter along with a mallet to expand the osteotomy and condense bone laterally.

Osseodensification technique (group III)

Osteotomy preparation was performed using Densah™ burs (Versah) in a counterclockwise direction at 800–1200 rpm with copious irrigation to condense and preserve bone.

The length and diameter of all implants (Straumann Bone Level Tapered, SLActive surface, 4 mm diameter × 10 mm length) were selected based on preoperative CBCT evaluation of bone height and width. Implants were placed at an insertion torque of 25 Ncm using a physiodispenser.

Stability assessment

Immediately following implant placement, the primary stability was evaluated using the Osstell Mentor equipment (Osstell AB, Gothenburg, Sweden). ISQ values were recorded from both buccal and palatal aspects by the blinded outcome assessor.

All implants were placed using an emerged (non-submerged) technique with temporary titanium cylinders connected. Sutures were placed to approximate the flaps and allow for transmucosal healing. Immediate restoration was carried out with provisional prostheses fabricated chairside. The provisional prostheses were carefully adjusted to eliminate all centric and eccentric occlusal contacts, ensuring immediate restoration without functional loading (i.e., immediate rehabilitation protocol).

The primary outcome measure of implant stability was assessed again at three months and six months post-placement using RFA by the same blinded outcome assessor. At six months, after confirming adequate secondary stability, the provisional prostheses were replaced with definitive prostheses in physiologic occlusion (functional loading).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean, standard deviation) for ISQ values were calculated for both buccal and palatal aspects at each time point. One-way ANOVA was used for intergroup comparisons at each time point, with post-hoc Tukey’s multiple comparison test where applicable. Repeated measures ANOVA was performed for intragroup longitudinal comparisons. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical software (SPSS ver.23) was used to analyze the collected data.

A total of 84 patients were assessed for eligibility. Fifty-four patients were excluded (39 not meeting inclusion criteria, 15 declined to participate), resulting in 30 patients being randomized (10 per group). All 30 patients completed the study with no dropouts or loss to follow-up. All 30 implants (100%) survived at the end of the six-month observation period.

Statistical analysis

The mean Implant Stability Quotient (ISQ) values showed no statistically significant differences between the three osteotomy techniques at the time of placement, three months, or six months (p > 0.05 for all comparisons).

Although numerical differences were observed (e.g., Group II showed slightly lower mean ISQ values at baseline: buccal 67.30 ± 14.44, palatal 66.10 ± 14.44; while Group III showed slightly higher values throughout: baseline buccal 71.30 ± 11.34, 3 months buccal 73.20 ± 6.35), these did not reach statistical significance. Since no significant differences were found in the one-way ANOVA, post-hoc testing was not required.

Repeated measures ANOVA within individual groups showed no statistically significant changes in ISQ over the 6-month period, indicating relatively stable implant conditions throughout the healing period.

Given the small sample size (n = 10 per group) and relatively large standard deviations, particularly in Group II at baseline, these non-significant findings should be interpreted with caution. The study may have been underpowered to detect clinically meaningful differences, and the absence of statistical significance does not necessarily imply clinical equivalence.

Discussion

With the advent of implant dentistry, it has become a mainstay for the rehabilitation of missing teeth. The replacement of teeth in the posterior maxillary region has been a challenging situation to manage owing to the decreased bone density at the site. This has especially been problematic in patients desiring immediate replacement of teeth. Conventionally, a period of 3–6 months has been advised before prosthetic loading to allow for osseointegration of implants in the posterior maxilla, termed as late loading [40].

The concept of immediate restoration has revolutionized treatment protocols as it allows for prosthetic rehabilitation immediately after implant placement. For immediate restoration protocols to be followed, it is imperative to have high primary stability of the implant, and an ISQ value above 65 is recommended [37, 41]. Studies have shown that implant micromotion lower than 100 microns stimulates osteoblastic activity and improves osseointegration, while micromotion above 150 microns impairs osseointegration [42, 43]. Decreased micromotion can be achieved by a higher bone-implant contact ratio and adequate primary stability.

In the posterior maxilla especially, owing to the decreased density of bone, various techniques of bone condensation have been proposed to improve primary stability, bone-implant contact, and bone density or bone volume percentage. These techniques include the osteotome technique, use of piezoelectric or laser devices, underpreparation of the site, and osseodensification techniques [10].

Summers’ osteotome technique

The osteotome technique proposed by Summers, based on the description by Tatum, involves the use of a special set of hand instruments to prepare the implant bed with lateral and apical condensation of bone whilst avoiding the removal of bone at the site. A set of osteotomes with gradually increasing diameters are used in which the base of the smaller instrument corresponds to the active portion of the next size instrument [20].

Studies by multiple authors (Summers 1994, Hahn 1999, Nkenke et al. 2002, Slete et al. 2018) indicate that the osteotome approach to bone condensation has been shown to increase bone formation around the implant with increased bone-to-implant contact and minimal marginal bone loss, though insertion torque values may be lower [20, 44–47]. Another advantage associated with the osteotome technique has been found to be decreased heat generation during osteotomy compared to other techniques, which could decrease the detrimental effects of heat on bone cell activity, thereby improving the success of osseointegration [39, 48]. Successful outcomes of immediate restoration of implants placed following the condensing technique have been published in several clinical reports [22, 47][68].

The disadvantages postulated with this technique include the trauma to patients associated with the tapping procedure and use of a mallet, and the formation of microfractures at the osteotomy site. Studies by Stavropoulos et al. (2007) and Buchter et al. (2004) suggested that preparation of the implant bed by osteotomes could have a deleterious effect on implant stability and osseointegration. Buchter et al. suggested that this could be due to microfractures created by the osteotome in peri-implant bone which may lead to delayed bone recovery, impaired bone-to-implant contact, and decreased implant stability [49, 50].

Osseodensification technique

A novel technique introduced by Huwais and Meyer in 2013 [25] is the osseodensification technique which uses specialized burs (Densah™, Versah) that have both cutting and densifying actions. The burs when run in a clockwise direction lead to a cutting action, and when run in an anticlockwise direction cause condensation of bone in an outward direction by a mechanism termed “compaction autografting“ [14]. The reverse-cutting bur has a larger number of lands coupled with copious irrigation and is run at a speed between 800 and 1200 rpm, allowing for lateral and apical condensation of bone within the osteotomy with decreased chatter [51].

Apart from autografting of bone chips along the walls of the osteotomy, the burs also cause burnishing of bone through the process of plastic deformation with application of compressive stresses below the ultimate tensile strength of bone. This allows for a certain amount of viscoelastic recoil of bone at the osteotomy by preserving the fluid content and collagen integrity, which is lost due to conventional drilling into bone. The opposing axial force generated by the bur-to-implant contact also improves the haptic feedback received by the surgeon, leading to a more controlled drilling procedure based on the bone density available at the site [32].

Recent studies have shown increased implant stability values, increased removal torque values, better bone-to-implant contact, and bone volume percentage with the osseodensification technique when compared to conventional osteotomy [25, 29–31, 36, 52–58]. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Mohammadi et al. [29] demonstrated significantly higher primary and secondary stability in implants placed using osseodensification compared to conventional drilling in low-density bone. Similarly, Abdelraouf et al. [31] reported significantly higher ISQ values with osseodensification at all time points in a randomized clinical trial in the posterior maxilla.

Assessment of implant stability

Since bone-implant contact and bone volume cannot be quantified clinically, implant stability serves as a reliable measure to assess the prognosis of implants. Implant stability can be measured by methods such as removal and insertion torque values, Periotest, and Resonance Frequency Analysis. Removal and insertion torque value assessments are invasive methods, and Periotest (Schulte et al. 1983) was found to have decreased sensitivity and reliability [33].

In light of these disadvantages, Resonance Frequency Analysis is an easy, quick, and reliable non-invasive method to assess implant stability. This technique uses a small sensor attached to the implant that acts as a small tuning fork in response to magnetic pulse vibrations of frequency between 5 and 15 kHz induced by a piezoceramic transducer. The resonance frequency of this sensor-implant unit reflects the micromobility or stiffness of the unit within bone; with an increase in implant stability (decreased micromobility), the vibration frequency of the sensor increases. For ease of communication, the resonance frequency is expressed as the Implant Stability Quotient (ISQ) value. The scale runs from 1 to 100, in which 1 corresponds approximately to 1000 Hz and 100 to 10,000 Hz frequency. A higher ISQ value therefore implies better implant stability [59].

Immediate restoration protocol

The concept of immediate restoration of implants helps improve patient comfort and esthetics. The principle behind this concept is based on Frost’s mechanostat theory, by which bone undergoes adaptive remodeling when controlled load is applied to the implant in line with the direction and magnitude of the load. This reduces the risk of overloading the implant, leading to a more controlled rate of bone remodeling and better implant osseointegration [60]. In the current study, immediate restoration without functional loading was employed—provisional prostheses were placed with all centric and eccentric contacts eliminated to minimize early mechanical stress while providing esthetic benefit.

Study findings

While multiple studies have compared implant stability achieved using either osseodensification or Summers’ osteotome technique with conventional osteotomy, few studies in literature compare the stability of implants between all three techniques in human models with immediate restoration protocols.

The current study included a total of 30 patients divided into three groups of 10 each. Group I received endosseous implants (Straumann Bone Level Tapered, SLActive surface) using a conventional osteotomy technique, Group II received implants using Summers’ osteotome technique with osteotomes (Xive) and a mallet, and Group III received implants through osseodensification protocol via Densah™ burs. Implant stability was assessed at baseline, three months, and six months using resonance frequency analysis with Osstell equipment.

The mean ISQ value noted in Group I was 70.50 ± 5.28 buccally and 70.30 ± 4.97 palatally at the time of placement, which allowed for immediate restoration as discussed previously. Three months after implant placement and restoration, the mean ISQ value obtained was 68.40 ± 5.73 buccally and 68.20 ± 5.71 palatally in Group I. This slight decrease in ISQ values at 3 months, though not significant, could be due to extensive bone remodeling that takes place during this period. The mean ISQ values after 6 months were 73.70 ± 4.44 buccally and 73.10 ± 4.77 palatally, suggesting stabilization of remodeling [39].

In Group II, the mean ISQ values of 67.30 ± 14.44 buccally and 66.10 ± 14.44 palatally were recorded at the time of placement. These values, though not statistically significantly different, were numerically lower than those of the conventional osteotomy and osseodensification groups. A possible explanation for this could be the formation of microfractures in the peri-implant bone [61, 62]. Another possible reason, as stated by Blanco et al. [41], could be that use of osteotomes could lead to condensation of bone at the apex of the osteotomy as opposed to condensation along the lateral walls. The mean ISQ values obtained in Group II buccally and palatally at three months were 71.00 ± 3.62 and 69.60 ± 4.85, and at six months were 74.50 ± 4.47 and 74.00 ± 4.27 respectively. There was no statistically significant difference between the ISQ values at different time intervals, but there was a continuous numerical increase in implant stability from the time of placement to 3 months and 6 months after placement, which is in concordance with the study by Buchter et al. [49]

In Group III, the mean ISQ values buccally and palatally were 71.30 ± 11.34 & 70.70 ± 11.99 at the time of placement, 73.20 ± 6.35 & 72.30 ± 6.09 at three months, and 73.60 ± 8.05 & 73.10 ± 7.81 at six months. The mean ISQ values, though not statistically significantly different, were numerically greater when compared to the other two techniques at time of implant placement and at three months after restoration, and also remained relatively constant between the third and sixth month. A previous study using microcomputed tomography imaging of osseodensification osteotomies has shown a “spring-back” effect in which the diameter of the osteotomy was found to be smaller compared to that of conventional osteotomy. This is explained by the viscoelastic recovery of bone when prepared using osseodensification technique, which creates residual strains within the osteotomy. This in turn compresses the bone against the implant soon after placement, leading to increased bone-to-implant contact with improved bone healing. This technique has also been found to increase bone density both along the lateral walls and apex of the osteotomy.

The mean ISQ values between Groups I, II, and III at the time of placement, three months, and six months follow-up showed no statistically significant differences. This suggests that within the limitations of this exploratory study, the use of bone condensation techniques does not demonstrate statistically significant superiority on implant stability compared to conventional osteotomy. There was also no statistically significant difference between the two bone condensation techniques, though numerical trends suggest marginally better primary stability with osseodensification. All techniques used in this study provided adequate primary stability to allow for immediate restoration of implants with 100% survival at the end of 6 months despite the relatively low density of bone in the posterior maxilla [32].

Contextualization with recent literature

While osseodensification showed numerically higher ISQ values, the differences were not statistically significant in the current study. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, especially in light of the small sample size and variability. Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses [29, 30], along with recent randomized clinical trials [31, 63], indicate that osseodensification may enhance primary stability, particularly in low-density bone. Abdelraouf et al. [31] reported statistically significant differences favoring osseodensification in the posterior maxilla, while other studies with similar sample sizes reported non-significant trends similar to the current findings. The lack of statistical significance in our study may reflect a Type II error due to insufficient power rather than true equivalence between techniques.

An important consideration in interpreting these non-significant differences is the relatively small cohort size with fairly wide standard deviations—partially the result of inter-individual variability inherent to in vivo clinical research in the maxillary posterior region. The lack of operator blinding and reliance on a single implant system are additional factors limiting generalizability. Nonetheless, reproducible ISQ trends and stable outcomes at six months support the overall reliability of all examined techniques under the tested conditions.

Recent in vitro and experimental investigations have highlighted the emerging role of magnetodynamic osteotomy techniques as an alternative bone compaction approach [64]. These techniques, along with osseodensification and manual bone condensation, aim to enhance peri-implant bone density and improve primary stability, particularly in low-density bone. However, contemporary evidence suggests that implant macro-design may exert a stronger influence on primary stability than the osteotomy technique alone [64, 65].

Studies evaluating different osteotomy approaches have demonstrated that tapered implant designs, such as those used in the present study, can significantly increase insertion torque and implant stability irrespective of the preparation technique [65]. This interaction between implant geometry and surgical technique indicates that biomechanical outcomes cannot be attributed solely to osteotomy protocols.

Therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences observed in the present study may be partially explained by the stabilizing effect of the tapered implant macro-design used, which could have masked subtle differences between osteotomy techniques. These findings emphasize the need to interpret results within the broader context of implant design–technique interaction rather than considering surgical technique in isolation [64–66].

Study limitations

The present study has several important limitations that should be acknowledged:

Small sample size and statistical power: The study included only 10 patients per group. Although sample size calculation was performed, the study was likely underpowered to detect clinically meaningful differences between techniques, especially given the variability of ISQ values. Future research should address these limitations through adequately powered multicenter studies with larger sample populations, objective bone density stratification, longer follow-up periods, and prospective trial registration. Conclusion

The present study evaluated the stability of immediately restored implants in the maxillary posterior region using three different osteotomy techniques: conventional osteotomy, Summers’ osteotome technique, and osseodensification. The assessment of implant stability in the three groups was performed using resonance frequency analysis (Osstell™). ISQ values were measured at the time of implant insertion, at three months, and at six months following immediate restoration.

Within the limitations of this exploratory study, the results showed no statistically significant differences in the stability of implants from baseline to three months and six months within groups or between groups. The implants placed using conventional osteotomy showed a slight decrease in ISQ at three months, while implants placed using Summers’ osteotome technique showed a slightly lower initial ISQ. The stability achieved using osseodensification technique was maintained with numerically higher values, but without statistically significant differences.

This study supports the view that primary stability suitable for immediate restoration can be consistently achieved in the maxillary posterior region using any of these three osteotomy protocols, provided patient- and site-related factors are carefully controlled. While numerical trends suggest that osseodensification may offer modest advantages in primary stability and Summers’ osteotome technique may result in slightly lower initial stability, these differences did not reach statistical significance and should be interpreted cautiously given the small sample size and exploratory nature of the study.

The study does not provide evidence for equivalence or superiority of any technique. The absence of statistically significant differences may reflect Type II error due to insufficient statistical power rather than true clinical equivalence. Condensation techniques may offer modest advantages in selected cases, and clinical decisions should incorporate patient comfort, surgical skill, cost considerations, and individualized treatment planning.

Future research with larger sample sizes, objective bone density stratification, longer follow-up periods, and adequate statistical power is needed to definitively establish whether clinically meaningful differences exist between these osteotomy techniques in the posterior maxilla.
