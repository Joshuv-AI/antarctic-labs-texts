MiR-24-3p delivered by adipose derived stem cells exosomes alleviate macrophage pyroptosis via NLRP3/Caspase1/GSDMD in sepsis ALI
Authors: Kuo Shen, Peng Wang, Hao Zhang, Xianqi Wang, Heliang Fu, Jin Li, Jijun Chen, Peng Zhao, Wenjie Cheng, Yuhang Wang, Ruizhi Wang, Rui Zheng, Dahai Hu, Junjie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis is a life-threatening condition associated with high morbidity and mortality, frequently resulting in acute lung injury (ALI) as one of its most severe complications. The pathogenesis of sepsis-induced ALI is significantly influenced by excessive inflammation, particularly that mediated by macrophages, plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of sepsis-induced ALI. This study sought to explore the protective effects and underlying mechanisms of adipose-derived stem cell exosomes (ADSCs-exo) on macrophage-mediated inflammation and pyroptosis.MethodsADSCs-exo were isolated and characterized using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), Western blotting, and transmission electron microscopy. A macrophage pyroptosis model was established through lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation. The effects of ADSCs-exo on the secretion of inflammatory cytokine and pyroptosis-related markers (NLRP3, Caspase1, GSDMD) was evaluated. Mechanistic studies included dual-luciferase reporter assays and NLRP3 inhibition experiments. In vivo, a cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) sepsis model was utilized to evaluate the impact of ADSCs-exo on pulmonary inflammation, histopathological damage, survival rates, macrophage polarization (M1/M2), and components of the pyroptosis pathway (NLRP3, Caspase1).ResultsIsolated ADSCs-exo displayed characteristic features of exosomes. The administration of ADSCs-exo mitigated LPS-induced hyperinflammation in macrophages, which was associated with a decreased expression of pyroptosis executors, namely Caspase1, GSDMD and NLRP3. Mechanistically, ADSCs-exo facilitated the delivery of miR-24-3p, which specifically targeted NLRP3 to inhibit pyroptosis. In mice subjected to CLP-challenge, the administration of ADSCs-exo improved lung pathology, enhanced survival rates, and reduced markers of pyroptosis in pulmonary tissues.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that ADSCs-exo attenuates ALI by modulating macrophage pyroptosis through the miR-24-3p/NLRP3/Caspase1/GSDMD axis, thereby presenting a promising therapeutic approach for sepsis-associated ALI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04722-7.Keywords: ADSCs exosome, Sepsis, ALI, Pyroptosis, NLRP3

Background

Sepsis constitutes a significant global healthcare challenge, marked by a dysregulated host response to infection that leads to life-threatening organ dysfunction [1]. Epidemiological studies reveal that over 48.9 million individuals worldwide are affected by sepsis annually, with a mortality rate surpassing 25%, resulting in approximately 11 million deaths [2]. This scenario imposes a substantial economic burden on both the families of patients and the healthcare system. Despite advancements in medical care and increased attention to sepsis, its mortality rate remains elevated [3], although its incidence has stabilized in recent years. Sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI) is a common and severe complication among sepsis patients [4]. The management of septic ALI is particularly challenging due to its high mortality rate and significant potential to progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), which has a mortality rate of 30–40%. Research indicates that approximately 68.2% of sepsis patients develop ALI, with a 90-day mortality rate reaching 35.5% [5]. Therefore, exploring the pathogenesis and treatment of sepsis-induced ALI is a crucial priority in contemporary sepsis research.

Despite extensive research on sepsis and ALI, the precise pathogenesis of sepsis-induced ALI remains inadequately understood. Studies have identified macrophage-induced inflammatory cascade reactions as critical in the development of sepsis [6]. However, the initiation and progression of these inflammatory cascades are not yet fully elucidated. Macrophages are central to mediating the inflammatory response during sepsis [7]. Upon pathogen invasion, macrophages become activated and adopt the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, resulting in the release of significant quantities of inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [8]. While this response is essential for pathogen clearance, it also contributes to tissue damage, particularly in the lungs [9]. The disruption of the pulmonary vascular barrier allows inflammatory cells, including neutrophils and macrophages, to infiltrate lung tissue [8]. This infiltration perpetuates a cycle of ongoing inflammation and tissue damage, which can lead to ALI and potentially progress to ARDS [10, 11]. Consequently, targeting the inflammatory response of macrophages has emerged as a primary therapeutic strategy for ALI. Despite advancements in therapeutic strategies, current approaches remain inadequate. Therefore, it is crucial to conduct a comprehensive investigation into the complex mechanisms underlying macrophage-mediated hyperinflammatory responses, and to develop of innovative, precisely targeted therapeutic interventions.

Pyroptosis, a distinct form of programmed cell death, is triggered by inflammasome activation [12]. This process is marked by progressive cellular swelling, culminating in membrane lysis, release of cellular contents, and a potent inflammatory response [13]. The molecular pathways regulating pyroptosis involve two primary mechanisms: the canonical pathway, which is driven by Caspase-1 activation, and the non-canonical pathway mediated by Caspase-4/5/11, each initiates distinct downstream inflammatory cascades [14]. Of particular note is the NLRP3 inflammasome, a pyroptotic vesicle that serves as a critical regulator of pyroptosis [15]. Upon activation of NLRP3, Caspase-1 is typically cleaved, facilitating pyroptosis and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β and IL-18 [16]. Recent studies have identified macrophage pyroptosis as a crucial mechanism in the pathophysiology of various diseases. Specifically, NLRP3 activation has been implicated in the development of atherosclerosis, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and a range of central nervous system disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), multiple sclerosis (MS), and stroke [17]. Metformin has been shown to mitigate sepsis-induced ALI by inhibiting NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis via an AMPK-dependent pathway, with a pronounced effect observed in the lungs. Therefore, targeting NLRP3 to limit macrophage pyroptosis and its associated inflammation presents significant therapeutic potential for sepsis-induced ALI.

Adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), located within the stromal vascular fraction of adipose tissue, have gained recognition as a therapeutically significant population of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) due to their multipotent differentiation potential and robust paracrine activity [18]. A critical mechanism of action for ADSCs is the secretion of exosomes, which facilitate the transfer of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids to target cells [19]. Among the various components transported by exosomes, microRNAs (miRNAs) are particularly prominent, playing a crucial role in regulating numerous biological processes. Research has demonstrated that exosomes derived from ADSCs possess dual therapeutic capabilities, by promoting tissue regeneration while mitigating inflammatory responses. This dual functionality underscores their protective roles in various inflammation-related disorders such as diabetes, sepsis, and acute respiratory distress [20]. Our previous studies have shown that exosomes derived from ADSCs, which contain miR-125b-5p, can reduce ferroptosis in pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (PMVECs), activating the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 pathway, thereby offering therapeutic benefits in sepsis-induced ALI [21]. Furthermore, our experimental validation has demonstrated that ADSC-EVs inhibit the Notch signaling pathway by transporting miR-148a-3p, thereby effectively reprogramming macrophage polarization towards anti-inflammatory phenotypes during septic pathogenesis [22]. Previous studies have indicated that exosomes derived from human ADSCs can attenuate macrophage inflammation and reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels by upregulating heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) expression [23]. However, the mechanism by which ADSCs-exo mitigate inflammation and pyroptosis remain to be fully elucidated.

This study sought to clarify the anti-inflammatory and anti-pyroptotic effects of ADSCs-exo, along with the underlying mechanisms. The results revealed that ADSCs-exo significantly reduced inflammation associated with macrophage pyroptosis. Pretreatment with ADSCs-exo substantially suppressed the expression of NLRP3, GSDMD and Caspase1.NLRP3 inhibition assays confirmed that the anti-pyroptotic effect of ADSCs-exo is dependent on downregulation of NLRP3. MiRNA profiling has identified exosomal miR-24-3p as a pivotal regulator of the NLRP3/Caspase1/GSDMD signaling axis. In vivo experiments have shown that ADSCs-exo significantly improve survival rates in mice, mitigate lung injury, and decrease the expression levels of NLRP3 and Caspase-1. This study elucidates that ADSCs-exo attenuate ALI induced by sepsis by inhibiting macrophage pyroptosis via the NLRP3/Caspase-1/GSDMD pathway through the delivery of miR-24-3p. These findings offer novel insights into ALI treatment and highlight the therapeutic potential of ADSCs-exo.

Methods

Ethical approval

Adipose tissue samples were obtained from five healthy donors (aged 28–42 years; with a body mass index (BMI) ranging from 22 to 26 kg/m²; all of whom tested negative for metabolic diseases). These donors underwent abdominal liposuction at Department of Burns and Skin Surgery, Xijing Hospital, in compliance with the approved guidelines (BSH [2015] D-15). Prior to the surgical procedure, all participants were informed of the study’s objectives and methodologies and provided consent for the donation of tissues excised during the operation. The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University approved the study, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Serum and PBMC collection

Peripheral blood samples (5 mL) were collected from both sepsis patients and healthy volunteers at the emergency department and intensive care unit of Xijing Hospital. Serum and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were extracted from buffy coat samples using Lymphoprep density gradient centrifugation, in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The criteria for inclusion and exclusion of sepsis patients were as follows: Inclusion criteria required (a) a diagnosis of sepsis and admission to the emergency department; (b) adherence to the Sepsis 3.0 diagnostic criteria for sepsis (infection plus a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of ≥ 2), as established by the Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) and the European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM) in 2016; (c) an age range of 18 to 60 years; and (d) consent to participate in the study, with informed consent obtained from the subjects or their legal representatives. Exclusion criteria encompassed (a) a hospital stay of less than one day; (b) the presence of acute or chronic infectious diseases; (c) receipt of chemoradiotherapy within the preceding three months; and (d) the occurrence of fatal trauma. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xijing Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and analysis

PBMCs were collected from three patients with sepsis and 3 healthy volunteers. Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed using a Chromium sequencing instrument (10X Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. The gene expression library was sequenced using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system. The methodology for scRNA-seq has been previously described in our earlier research [24]. The analysis of scRNA-seq data was conducted using the Omicshare Cloud Platform (https://www.omicshare.com/).

Isolation and characterization of human ADSCs

Subcutaneous adipose tissue from humans was meticulously dissected to remove fascia and blood vessels, subsequently minced into small fragments, and subjected to enzymatic digestion using a 0.1% type I collagenase solution (Gibco, Cat. No. 17100-017, USA) at 37 °C with continuous agitation. The resultant mixture underwent filtration and centrifugation, after which the cell pellet resuspended in a medium specifically formulated for ADSCs (OriCell Medium, Cyagen, China). ADSCs were then incubated with fluorescently labeled antibodies targeting cell surface markers (anti-CD29-FITC, anti-CD44-PE, anti-CD73-FITC, anti-CD90-FITC, anti-CD34-PE, and anti-CD45-FITC) and subsequently analyzed via flow cytometry using a BD FACSAria™ III System (BD Pharmingen). Six-well plates were pre-coated with a 0.1% gelatin solution. Adipogenic differentiation was initiated when cell confluence reached 90–100%, while osteogenic differentiation commenced at 60–70% confluence, utilizing commercial differentiation kits from Cyagen Bioscience (Guangzhou, China). Following 2 weeks of adipogenic induction and 3 weeks of osteogenic induction, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with Oil Red O or Alizarin Red. Imaging was performed using an Olympus IX71 optical microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

Isolation, characterization, and labeling of ADSCs-exo

ADSCs were cultured in 175 cm² flasks with a medium supplemented with exosome-depleted serum. To eliminate bovine exosomes, fetal bovine serum (FBS) underwent ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 18 h. Exosomes were isolated from the conditioned medium using differential ultracentrifugation, adhering to established protocols [21, 22]. The centrifugation steps were conducted at 4 °C using a Ti70 rotor (Optima XPN-100 Ultracentrifuge, Beckman Coulter, USA) as follows: 300 × g for 10 min to remove cellular debris, 2,000 × g for 10 min to eliminate cellular fragments and apoptotic bodies, 10,000 × g for 30 min, and 100,000 × g for 90 min. Subsequently, exosomes were then resuspended in 100 µL PBS and characterized for morphology and size by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; ZetaView® System). Western blotting was used to confirm the expression of exosomal markers CD9 and CD63. Exosomal protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit, resulting in an average concentration of 2 µg/µL. Prior to experimental application, exosomes were diluted in complete medium and sterilized through filtration with a 0.22-µm membrane.

Macrophage culture and grouping

RAW264.7 macrophages sourced from ATCC, were consistently routinely cultured in RPMI 1640 complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin antibiotics. The experimental setup utilized a 6-well plate system, with cells seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL and divided into three groups: a blank control group (treated with PBS), a LPS stimulation group (treated with 1 µg/mL LPS), and a LPS + ADSCs-exo intervention group (treated with 1 µg/mL LPS in conjunction 50 µg ADSCs-exo). Following a 12 h treatment period, both cells and culture supernatants were collected. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method for subsequent real-time quantitative PCR analysis of cytokine mRNA expression; the concentration of inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and TNF-α in the supernatant were quantitatively assessed using LEGENDplex™ multiplex factor detection technology combined with ELISA; Furthermore, the expression levels of key proteins involved in signaling pathways, such as NF-κB were validated through Western blot technology.

Cytokine protein array

RAW264.7 cells, at a concentration of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL, were subjected to a 12-hours treatment. Subsequently, and the culture supernatants were analyzed utilizing a Raybiotech cytokine array kit, in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

ELISA

Supernatants collected from RAW264.7 cells subjected to a 12-hour treatment were utilized to quantify IL-1β and TNF-α protein concentrations, employing enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits, in accordance with established protocols.

Western blot

After washing with pre-cooled PBS, macrophages were lysed using RIPA buffer (AccuRef Scientific, China) supplemented with protease inhibitors (PMSF) for a duration of 15 min in an ice bath. The protein supernatant was subsequently collected by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 5 min. Protein quantification was conducted using the BCA method (AccuRef Scientific, China), after which 50 µg of protein samples were resolved via 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membrane (Millipore) at a constant voltage of 100 V for 40 to 100 min. Membrane blocking was achieved through incubation with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 h The following steps were then performed: (1) Incubation with primary antibodies targeting NLRP3, Caspase-1, GSDMD, exosome markers CD9/CD63/TSG101, M1/M2 phenotypic markers iNOS/Arg1 and β-actin, (1:1000, sourced from Abcam, CST, Proteintech and Johnson & Johnson Biological) 2 at 4℃ overnight; (2) Incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:3000, Basted) at 37 °C for 1 h. Detection was performed using ECL chemiluminescence (Promega), and images were captured using a fluorescence imaging system (FluorChem FC). Gray-scale analysis was conducted using ImageJ software, while β-actin serving as the internal reference.

RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted utilizing the TRIzol reagent (Takara, Japan) and subsequently quantified. For the quantification of mRNA, 500 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Kit (Takara). RT-PCR was conducted employing the Ultra SYBR mix (CWBIO, Beijing, China) alongside gene-specific primers on a Bio-Rad IQ5 system (Bio-Rad, USA). The amplification protocol initiated with a denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles comprising denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 60 s. Relative gene expression levels were determined using the 2⁻ΔΔCT method, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. For miRNA analysis, 600 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed using the Mir-X™ miRNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit (Clontech), and miRNA expression levels were subsequently quantified via q-PCR using the miScript SYBR Green PCR Kit, with U6 snRNA employed for normalization.

Flow cytometry

RAW264.7 cells, at a concentration of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL, underwent a 12-hour treatment, followed by washing with sterile PBS, and resuspension in 100 µL of PBS. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with 5 µL of anti-CD86 or anti-CD206 antibody at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min, with gentle agitation every 15 min. After three additional PBS washes, the cells were resuspended in 300 µL of PBS. Fluorescence intensity was then assessed using a BD flow cytometer. Experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Macrophages, at a concentration of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL, were subjected to a 12-hours treatment, followed by washing with sterile PBS, detachment using a cell scraper, and fixation in an electron microscopy fixative. The samples underwent a series of preparatory steps including dehydration, critical-point drying, and gold/carbon sputter-coating prior to imaging with a scanning electron microscope at various magnifications to assess both overall morphology and detailed structural features.

Immunofluorescence staining

RAW264.7 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at ambient temperature, subsequently washed with PBS, permeabilized for 20 min with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked for 1 h with 2% BSA. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NLRP3, GSDMD, or Caspase1, each at a dilution of 1:200 (Cell Signaling Technology). This was followed by a 1-hour incubation at 37 °C with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies at a 1:200 dilution. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and visualized using an Olympus FSX100 microscope.

Cell transfection with hsa-miR-24-3p mimics/inhibitors

RAW264.7 cells were cultured in 6-well plates and transfected with 100 nM hsa-miR-24-3p mimics, inhibitors, or a negative control utilizing Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). Following a 24-hour incubation period, the cells were harvested for quantitative qPCR analysis to evaluate miRNA levels and Western blotting to determine the expression of target protein. Pyroptosis-related markers, including NLRP3, GSDMD, and Caspase1, were analyzed in triplicate.

Luciferase reporter assay

To determine whether NLRP3 is a direct target of miR-24-3p, RAW264.7 cells were co-transfected with 50 nM miR-24-3p mimics and either wild-type or mutant NLRP3 3′-UTR luciferase reporter constructs (HANBIO, Shanghai, China) using Lipofectamine 2000. Luciferase activity was assessed 24 h post-transfection employing the Promega Luciferase Assay System in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

CLP-induced sepsis mouse model

The animal experiments received approval from the Animal Care Committee of Fourth Military Medical University (Xi’an, China) and were conducted in strict accordance with their established guidelines. A total of 54 male BALB/c mice, aged 6–8 weeks, were purchased from the university’s animal center and maintained under standard conditions. Of these, 30 mice were randomly assigned into three experimental groups (n = 10 per group): sham control, CLP model, and CLP + adipose-derived stem cell-derived exosomes (ADSCs-exo; 100 µg in 100 µl PBS). These 30 mice were utilized to monitor survival over a 48-hour period, during which survival curves were generated. Another 24 mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 8 per group): sham control, CLP model, and CLP + ADSCs-exo. Sepsis was induced by CLP surgery, and treatments were administered immediately following the procedure. Lung tissues, Serum, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were collected 24 h post-CLP to evaluate therapeutic effects.

Isolation and detection of alveolar macrophages

Alveolar macrophages were isolated from the collected BALF, which was centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min. Subsequently, half of the cells were resuspended and incubated with F4/80 and CD86 antibodies for flow cytometry analysis, while the remaining cells were lysed for western blot analysis of NLRP3, Caspase-1, and GSDMD.

Multiplex cytokine assay

Cytokine concentrations in mouse samples were quantified for thirteen different cytokines utilizing the LEGENDplex™ multiplex assay kit, adhering to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histopathological analysis

Tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Histological changes were assessed using H&E staining. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), deparaffinized sections were subjected to endogenous peroxidase blocking with 3% H₂O₂ and non-specific site blocking with 5% BSA. The sections were subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting MPO, NLRP3, or Caspase-1, followed by staining with DAB chromogen using the PV6000 Histostain™ Kit (ZSGB, China). For immunofluorescence (IF), sections were incubated with primary antibodies (PKH26, TUNEL, 8OHdG, iNOS, CD206, NLRP3, Caspase-1) and corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI in anti-fade mounting medium. Confocal microscopy images were captured using the following filter parameters: DAPI (λex 330–380 nm / λem 420 nm), CY3 (λex 510–560 nm / λem 590 nm), FITC (λex 465–495 nm / λem 515–555 nm).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed utilizing GraphPad Prism 8.0 and presented as mean ± SD. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate. For single-factor comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, followed by LSD post-hoc testing. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Results

ScRNA- sequencing revealed macrophage reduction and pyroptosis in patients with sepsis

In this study, our primary focus was on macrophage within peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients with sepsis. As is shown in Fig. 1A, the number of macrophages decreased by nearly half in sepsis patients. Concurrently, a total of 1,779 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (847 upregulation and 932 downregulation. Figure 1B and C). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were significantly associated with infection related pathways (Fig. 1D). Interestingly, the DEGs were enriched in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway. Besides, pathways associated with programmed cell death, including pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and apoptosis, are also identified. These findings suggest that macrophages in sepsis patients underwent more cell death, contributing to their reduced numbers. Taking the characteristic of macrophages and the pathophysiology of sepsis into consideration, we further analyzed pyroptosis and the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway in sepsis-affected macrophages. As illustrated in Fig. 1E, the upregulated DEGs in sepsis macrophages were significantly enriched in the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway. Furthermore, key molecules involved in pyroptosis, such as NLRP3, GSDMD, Casp1 are more widely expressed in sepsis macrophages than that of healthy volunteer (Fig. 1F). Diffusion pseudotime analysis indicated that macrophages in sepsis patients exhibited notable differentiation dynamics compared to those in healthy individuals, who maintained an original state. In the sepsis group, there was a marked transformation, with key pyroptosis-related molecules being highly expressed (Fig. 1G). These results together indicated that macrophages underwent significant pyroptosis during sepsis, contributing to the excessive inflammatory response and the decrease of macrophages.

Activation of pyroptosis in PBMCs from sepsis patients

To verify the scRNA sequencing results, we collected PBMCs from sepsis patients and healthy volunteers and conducted pyroptosis-related test. LEGENDplexTM multiplex assay kit was used to detect cytokine concentrations in serum. Figure 2A illustrated a significant increase in cytokine concentrations, such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL8 and IL-18 in the sepsis group compared to the control group. ROS detection revealed an increased proportion of ROS-positive cells and ROS accumulation in PBMCs from sepsis patients (Fig. 2B and C). The 8OHdG staining results indicated that ratio of 8OHdG positive cell was significantly increased (Fig. 2D). PI and Calcein AM staining indicated that PBMCs in sepsis patients showed more cell injury (Fig. 2E). These results suggested that PBMCs in sepsis patients underwent significant cell injury. Furthermore, we detected pyroptosis of PBMCs by scanning electron microscope and flow cytometry. Figure 2F illustrated that PBMCs in the sepsis groups exhibited notable cell swelling and membrane pore formation, indicative of pyroptosis. Besides, flow cytometry results showed that ratio of Cleaved GSDMD positive macrophages was significantly increased in sepsis patients (Fig. 2G), which meant more pyroptosis. Collectively, these results in all indicated that pyroptosis was activated in macrophages from patients with sepsis.

Characterization of ADSCs and exosomes

Primary ADSCs displayed the characteristic spindle morphology with vortex-like growth patterns under phase-contrast microscopy (Fig. 3A). To evaluate their multilineage differentiation potential, passage 3 (P3) ADSCs underwent adipogenic and osteogenic induction, confirmed by Oil Red O-stained lipid droplets and Alizarin Red S-detected calcium nodules, respectively. Flow cytometry verified characteristic MSC surface markers: CD44 (98.90%), CD29 (98.20%), CD90 (96.40%), and CD73 (75.90%) were positive, whereas hematopoietic markers CD34 (0.86%) and CD45 (0.20%) were negative (Fig. 3B). Exosomes were isolated from ADSC-conditioned medium and characterized by TEM, NTA, and Western blotting. TEM revealed cup-shaped vesicles with bilayered membranes (Fig. 3C). NTA indicated exosome diameters ranging 55–155 nm (mean 105.7 nm; Fig. 3D). Western blotting confirmed the enrichment of exosomal markers (CD63/CD9/TSG101) and the absence of β-actin (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these results validated the successful isolation of ADSCs-derived exosomes (ADSCs-exo).

ADSCs-exo modulate the inflammatory phenotype of macrophages

RAW264.7 cells were exposed to 1 µg/mL LPS to induce inflammation and 50 µg/mL ADSCs-exo to evaluate its anti-inflammatory effects. Cytokine protein array analysis revealed that LPS stimulation markedly elevated the expression of inflammatory cytokines, including INF-gamma, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-4, IL-6, CXCL8, and MCP1. In contrast, ADSCs-exo markedly reduced the expression of these cytokines (Fig. 4A). ELISA confirmed that ADSCs-exo reduced IL-1β and TNF-α protein expression, consistent with protein array findings (Fig. 4C). Overall, ADSCs-exo attenuated LPS-induced macrophage inflammation. Specific macrophage markers were further examined using flow cytometry and Western blotting. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that LPS stimulation markedly elevated the expression of the M1 marker CD86. Conversely, ADSCs-exo decreased CD86 expression while upregulating the M2 macrophage marker CD206 (Fig. 4B). Western blotting revealed that LPS stimulation significantly elevated iNOS expression, an M1-specific marker, whereas ADSCs-exo treatment reduced iNOS levels and enhanced Arg-1 expression, indicative of M2 polarization (Fig. 4D). Additionally, ROS is essential for macrophage activation. Therefore, we further detected the expression of ROS in macrophages of different treatment groups. ROS within the cells was labeled with FITC, and its fluorescence intensity was measured using flow cytometry to assess ROS expression levels. The study found that LPS stimulation notably elevated both the proportion of ROS and the mean fluorescence intensity in RAW264.7 macrophages compared to the control group. After treatment with ADSCs-exo, the proportion of ROS cells and the mean fluorescence intensity in RAW264.7 macrophages induced by LPS could be decreased (Figure S1). Therefore, ADSCs-exo attenuated LPS-induced inflammation and promoted M2 macrophage polarization.

ADSCs-exo alleviate LPS-induced pyroptosis by regulating NLRP3

To illustrate the mechanism of ADSCs-exo on macrophage inflammatory responses, we further detected expression of pyroptosis-related molecules. Western blotting demonstrated that LPS stimulation markedly increased the expression of pyroptosis-associated molecules including NLRP3, GSDMD, and Caspase1 in RAW264.7 cells, whereas ADSCs-exo notably decreased their expression (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence staining was conducted to assess NLRP3 and Casp1 expression in macrophages. The findings demonstrated that LPS stimulation markedly elevated the fluorescence intensity of pyroptosis-associated molecules NLRP3 and Caspase1. Conversely, ADSCs-exo treatment significantly diminished the fluorescence intensity of these molecules (Fig. 5D). Subsequently, we detected the expression of inflammatory factors with RT-PCR. LPS stimulation led to a significant upregulation of mRNA levels of NLRP3, Caspase1, GSDMD, IL-18 and IL-1β, whereas ADSCs-exo treatment markedly reduced their expression (Fig. 5B). Subsequently, SEM was used to observe the morphological changes in RAW264.7 macrophages. LPS stimulation resulted in cellular swelling and the formation of pores in the membrane. In contrast, the treatment of ADSCs-exo ameliorated cellular injury and restored the morphology of the cell membrane (Fig. 5C). These findings demonstrated that ADSCs-exo significantly decreased the expression of pyroptosis-related molecules. To further elucidate the relationship between ADSCs-exo and pyroptosis, we used NLRP3-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) to inhibit NLRP3 expression and determine whether the regulatory effects of ADSCs-exo on pyroptosis are dependent on NLRP3 activity. Following the inhibition of NLRP3 expression, the effects of ADSCs-exo on pyroptosis-related molecules were re-evaluated. Western blot analysis indicated that the downregulation of NLRP3 expression counteracted the inhibitory effects of ADSCs-exo on pyroptosis-related molecules, thereby suggesting that the regulation of these molecules by ADSCs-exo is contingent upon NLRP3 expression (Fig. 5E). In summary, these results demonstrated that ADSCs-exo alleviate LPS-induced pyroptosis by the regulation of NLRP3.

ADSCs-exo regulate NLRP3 via miR-24-3p

To further elucidate the regulatory role of ADSCs-exo on NLRP3, we conducted non-coding RNA sequencing on ADSCs-exo to identify potential regulatory molecules. The sequencing identified 45 miRNAs in ADSCs-exo, with miR-24-3p, miR-23a-3p, miR-12136, and miR-125b-5p exhibiting high expression levels. Subsequent PCR validation confirmed elevated expression of miR-24-3p, miR-12136, miR-23a-3p, and miR-125b-5p in ADSCs-exo, consistent with miRNA sequencing data. Target gene prediction analysis identified NLRP3 as a potential target of miR-24-3p. PCR analysis demonstrated a significant upregulation of miR-24-3p expression in RAW264.7 cells following ADSCs-exo treatment (Fig. 6A). A dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed to confirm the regulatory impact of miR-24-3p on NLRP3. The dual-luciferase reporter assay demonstrated that miR-24-3p binds to the 3’ UTR of NLRP3, thereby inhibiting downstream gene transcription (Fig. 6B). To confirm the suppression of miR-24-3p on NLRP3, we further designed and synthesized miR-24-3p mimics and inhibitors and transfected them into RAW264.7 cells. PCR detection results indicated that miR-24-3p mimics significantly elevated miR-24-3p expression, whereas the inhibitors did not produce a significant change (Fig. 6C). The introduction of miR-24-3p mimics resulted in a notable reduction in the expression of pyroptosis-associated molecules, including NLRP3, GSDMD, and Caspase1, while the miR-24-3p inhibitors markedly elevated their expression (Fig. 6D).These results indicate that ADSCs-exo inhibit NLRP3 through miR-24-3p, thereby alleviating pyroptosis and inflammation in macrophages.

ADSCs-exo alleviate CLP-induced sepsis-associated lung injury in mice

To explore the comprehensive effects of ADSCs-exo on sepsis-associated lung injury in mice, we designed and conducted animal experiment (Fig. 7A). Initially, mice were disposed of CLP, followed by intravenous injection of 100 µg (100 µL) of human ADSCs-exo to observe their effects. The results demonstrated that CLP treatment successfully induced sepsis in mice, with a mortality rate of approximately 50% at 24 h and 90% at 48 h. In contrast, treatment with human ADSCs-exo reduced the mortality rate to 10% at 24 h and approximately 40% at 48 h (Fig. 7B). Subsequently, using a multi-cytokine detection kit, we analyzed the expression of 11 inflammatory factors in the serum of mice from different treatment groups. The results indicated that expressions of various inflammatory factors in the mouse serum, including IL-6, TNF-α, CCL2, CCL3, and CCL4, were significantly elevated in CLP-induced septic mice. Notably, the group treated with ADSCs-exo exhibited a substantial reduction in the expression of these inflammatory factors (Fig. 7C).

Discussion

Sepsis, a life-threatening condition characterized by significant global incidence and mortality, represents a critical challenge to global health [25]. Among its complications, sepsis-induced ALI emerges as one of the earliest and most severe outcomes, often resulting in devastating clinical consequences. Sepsis-induced ALI primarily arises from a hyperinflammatory response due to excessive macrophage activation [26]. ADSCs-exo have shown promising therapeutic potential in mitigating sepsis-induced ALI [27], although the precise molecular mechanisms underlying their protective effects remain incompletely understood. To address knowledge gap, we conducted a systematic investigation into the therapeutic potential and mechanisms of ADSCs-exo in the context of sepsis-induced ALI. Our study confirmed that ADSCs-exo can alleviate sepsis-induced ALI. Treatment with ADSCs-exo effectively reduced the macrophage inflammatory response and oxidative stress by inhibiting pyroptosis and promoting the polarization of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype. Research indicates that exosomes from human ADSCs can mitigate macrophage pyroptosis by transferring abundant miRNAs, thereby providing protection against sepsis-induced lung injury.

Macrophage hyperinflammation plays a critical role in sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI) [28]. Upon activation by invading pathogens, macrophages undergo polarization to the inflammatory M1 phenotype, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, as well as reactive oxygen species (ROS). These activated macrophages serve as key mediators in ALI by initiating and amplifying the inflammatory cascade, which compromises the integrity of the pulmonary vascular endothelial barrier, damages alveolar epithelial cells, and results in pulmonary edema [29]. In the present study, we demonstrated that ADSCs-derived exosomes (ADSCs-exo) can suppress M1 macrophage polarization and increase the M2 macrophage ratio both in vivo and in vitro. Our previous research also suggested that ADSCs-exo modulate macrophage polarization through the regulation of the Nrf2/HO-1 axis [23]. However, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) results indicated that the number of macrophages in sepsis patients is reduced compared to healthy individuals. This reduction in macrophage numbers may represent a novel therapeutic target. Macrophages contribute to inflammation and tissue injury via NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent pyroptosis, which releases pro-inflammatory mediators and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) [30]. In advanced stages of disease, persistent macrophage activation can result in immunosuppression, thereby increasing the susceptibility to secondary infections. Addressing macrophage-mediated inflammation through strategies such as modulating macrophage polarization, inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome, or employing MSC-Exos to regulate macrophage function is essential for the treatment of sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI) [31]. Both previous research and the findings of this study demonstrate that exosomes derived from ADSCs-exo effectively mitigate excessive inflammation and confer protective effects. Earlier studies suggest that extracellular vesicles from ADSCs influence the Notch-miR-148a-3p axis to modulate macrophage polarization, thereby reducing sepsis in murine models [22]. Furthermore, research indicates that exosomes from adipose-derived stem cells alleviate inflammation and oxidative stress by modulating the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway in macrophages [23]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms remain complex and subject to debated. A comprehensive understanding of the role of macrophages in sepsis-induced lung injury augments our understanding of the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms and offers a theoretical foundation for the development macrophage-targeted therapeutic interventions.

Pyroptosis, a form of programmed cell death driven by GSDMD, is characterized by the formation of pores in the cell membrane, leading to cellular swelling and membrane rupture. This process facilitates the release of cellular contents, thereby eliciting a potent inflammatory and immune response [14]. The NLRP3 protein, a cytoplasmic immune sensor, is activated by cellular stress signals. As a member of the NOD-like receptor family, NLRP3 becomes active during infection or inflammation, leading to the formation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, which plays a pivotal role in macrophage pyroptosis [32]. Studies have demonstrated that activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is observed in patients with ALI and sepsis, and its inhibition has been shown to confer protective effects in septic animal models [33].Cinnamaldehyde has been reported to reduce the expression of NLRP3 and IL-1β expressions both in vitro and in the lungs of mice injected with LPS [34]. Additionally, research suggests that dihydromyricetin confers protection against ALI by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation [35]. The inhibition of the abnormal activation and assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome is essential to prevent the excessive inflammatory response associated with the progression of sepsis [16]. The development of drugs and therapeutic strategies targeting NLRP3 regulation for sepsis treatment remains an ongoing area of investigation. Animal experiments conducted in this study indicate that ADSCs-exo significantly reduces inflammatory cell infiltration and macrophage pyroptosis in lung tissues affected by sepsis-associated ALI. Subsequently, we investigated specific mechanisms involved through cellular experiments. In vitro, a pyroptosis model was established using LPS-stimulated macrophages. The results demonstrated that ADSCs-exo markedly inhibited macrophage pyroptosis and mitigated LPS-induced inflammatory responses. Mechanistic analysis revealed suppression of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Expression levels of key components in the classical pyroptosis pathway, including NLRP3, GSDMD, and Caspase-1, were significantly reduced. Further experiments on NLRP3 inhibition confirmed that the regulation of the pyroptosis pathway by ADSCs-exo is dependent on its downregulation of NLRP3.

In recent years, there has been a significant increase in research focusing on ADSCs and their exosomes in the context of tissue injury and repairg. Studies have demonstrated that exosomes derived from ADSCs-exo facilitate wound healing, reduce scarring, and modulate macrophages activity, thereby providing protection against various inflammatory diseases. Nonetheless, the precise mechanisms underlying these effects remain to be fully elucidated [36]. Exosomes primarily exert their influence by delivering their molecular contents to target cells. miRNAs, which play a crucial role in post-transcriptional regulation, have been found to be enriched within exosomes. The phospholipid bilayer structure of exosomes effectively shields miRNAs from degradation by RNA enzymes [37]. Through their rich miRNA content, exosomes exert a wide range of regulatory effects on recipient cells. Notably, MSC-derived exosomes deliver miR-22-3p, which suppresses NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated inflammation, thereby mitigating podocyte injury and promoting wound healing in diabetic models [38]. Furthermore, in the context of ferroptosis in PMVECs induced by excessive inflammatory responses, ADSCs-exo can deliver highly expressed miR125b-5p to modulate the Keap1/Nrf2/GPX4 pathway, thereby reducing ferroptosis associated with the excessive inflammatory response [21]. In this study, we conducted sequencing and analysis of the miRNAs present in ADSCs-exo, and predicted the miRNA molecules within these exosomes that may regulate NLRP3 through target genes. Mechanistic investigations demonstrated that miR24-3p, which is abundantly present in ADSCs-exo, targets the 3’ untranslated region of NLRP3, resulting in decreased its expression and elucidating the regulatory impact of ADSCs-exo on NLRP3.Our integrated studies revealed that ADSCs-exo attenuated macrophage pyroptosis by delivering miR-24-3p, which inhibited the NLRP3/Caspase1/GSDMD signaling pathway in macrophages. Furthermore, our sequencing data identified over 40 miRNAs, with miR-24-3p being the most prominent. However, it is not concluded that miR-24-3p is the sole effective miRNA in ADSC exosomes; miR-125b-5p and miR-148a-3p have also been previously reported to have therapeutic potential in the treatment of sepsis [21, 22]. Therefore, the therapeutic effect of ADSC exosomes on sepsis is likely a cumulative result of the collective action of these miRNAs. Further mechanistic exploration is warranted to achieve a comprehensive understanding.

Nonetheless, this study is subject to certain limitations. Specifically, the research was confined to cellular and animal models, lacking clinical validation. Additionally, the methods employed for the extraction and preparation of exosomes may influence their therapeutic efficacy. Future clinical investigations should prioritize optimizing the preparation and application processes of exosomes to improve the clinical utility of ADSCs-exo in the treatment of sepsis-induced lung injury.

Conclusions

In this study, ADSCs-exo were found to mitigate the inflammatory response in macrophages stimulated by LPS, promote macrophages polarization towards the M2 phenotype, and decreased the expressions levels of NLRP3, Caspase1, and GSDMD involved in macrophage pyroptosis. Subsequent experiments involving NLRP3 inhibition and dual-luciferase reporter gene assays revealed that ADSCs-exo modulate the NLRP3 signaling pathway in macrophages by delivering miR-24b-3p, thereby reducing macrophage pyroptosis. In a model of CLP-induced sepsis in mice, ADSCs-exo were shown to attenuate pulmonary inflammation and oxidative stress, with a concomitant significant downregulation of NLRP3 and Caspase1 expression. Collectively, these findings elucidate a potential therapeutic mechanism whereby the delivery of miR-24-3p by ADSCs-exo alleviates the ALI induced by the inflammatory response associated with macrophage pyroptosis through the regulation of NLRP3/Caspase1/GSDMD pathway, thereby ameliorating sepsis-induced lung injury.
