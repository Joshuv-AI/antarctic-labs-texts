Eco-engineered bio-imprinted polymer for selective aluminum sequestration in wastewater
Authors: Huda Sharef, Mohammed S Almoiqli, Aveen Jalal, Wafa Mazi, Bnar Ibrahim, Ayman Alrehaili, Maha Ali Aljowni, Ali Hamzah Alessa, Nabil Fakhre, Obaid A Alharbi, Raed A Alsulami, Mubarak M Albarqi, Muneer Baata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAluminum (Al) poisoning in wastewater poses a significant environmental and public health threat, with potential association to neurological diseases such as Alzheimer’s. Conventional remediation methods often rely on synthetic materials with environmental drawbacks. This study presents a sustainable alternative by synthesizing ion-imprinted polymers (IIPs) from chia seed extract, a biobased resource rich in functional groups. Using Al (III) ions as a template and epichlorohydrin as a crosslinker, IIPs with specific sites for capturing aluminum. These IIPs demonstrated performance superior to non-imprinted polymers (NIPs), achieving a maximum adsorption capacity of 188.7 mg/g and a removal efficiency of 96.02%. Optimal conditions were found to be a pH of 5.5, a temperature of 30 °C, and a 30-minute contact time. Statistical analysis through ANOVA confirmed the significance of the findings (p < 0.001). The adsorption process conformed to the Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, and Sips isotherm models, as well as the pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, intraparticle diffusion, and Elovich kinetic models, suggesting a monolayer, endothermic, and spontaneous characteristic. The IIPs also proved highly reusable, maintaining their effectiveness over 10 regeneration cycles. These results demonstrate that chia seed-based IIPs provide a biobased, selective, and reusable approach for aluminium removal from wastewater, offering a balance between sustainability and adsorption efficiency.Keywords: Al (III)-IIP, Chia seed, Selective adsorption, Kinetic, IsothermSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Aluminum contamination in industrial wastewater represents a serious environmental issue with far-reaching implications for ecosystem health and human safety1,2. Despite aluminum’s natural abundance in Earth’s crust, industrial activities such as aluminum smelting, metal finishing, paper production, and water treatment using aluminum-based coagulants have significantly elevated Al (III) concentrations in wastewater, often exceeding the WHO guideline of 0.2 mg/L3,4. Aluminum’s neurotoxic effects, including its established links to Alzheimer’s disease and other neurological disorders, underscore the urgent need for effective remediation technologies5,6.

Current aluminum removal technologies face significant limitations hindering their widespread implementation. Conventional methods such as chemical precipitation, coagulation-flocculation, and membrane filtration often suffer from high operational costs, secondary pollution generation, and limited selectivity in complex matrices7,8. While ion-imprinted polymers (IIPs) have emerged as promising materials for selective metal ion removal, their synthesis typically relies on environmentally harmful crosslinkers and energy-intensive processes that contradict sustainability principles9. Recent reviews have highlighted many IIP approaches consuming large amounts of synthetic crosslinkers and demonstrateing variable performance, with some Al-IIP studies reporting capacities as low as 0.15–0.24 µmol/g10.

Bio-based adsorbents derived from agricultural wastes and natural polymers offer an attractive alternative due to their low cost, renewabiltity, and biodegradability11,12. However, current bioadsorbents face critical limitations, including poor mechanical stability, limited selectivity in multi-ion systems, and inconsistent regeneration performance13. Most importantly biosorption research remains confined to laboratory-scale batch studies, with limited demonstration of real wastewater treatment applications14.

A critical research gap exists in developing materials that combine the high selectivity of IIPs with the sustainability advantages of bio-based materials. While chia seeds (Salvia hispanica L.) represent an underexplored yet promising feedstock due to their rich polysaccharide content and abundant functional groups (hydroxyl, carboxyl, and amino groups), as antioxidant compounds (Fig. 1), no studies have investigated their potential as precursors for aluminum-selective ion-imprinted polymers15,16. This gap is particularly significant given the growing demand for sustainable water treatment technologies with both high performance and environmental compatibility.

Antioxidant compounds extracted from chia seed

Response Surface Methodology (RSM), a mathematical and statistical optimization approach, has demonstrated significant effectiveness in process development, enhancement, and optimization studies. RSM offers rich analytical data while minimizing experimental expenses through the gradual reduction of necessary experiments. This work examines the optimization of aluminum ion adsorption from wastewater utilizing Response Surface Methodology (RSM), across various operational parameters intended to replicate genuine environmental aqueous matrices. The Box-Behnken design, the most widely adopted experimental design in RSM applications, is employed to systematically examine interactions among multiple process parameters17–21.

The novelty of this work lies in pioneering the use of chia seed extract as a bio-based precursor for synthesizing aluminum-selective ion-imprinted polymers, addressing the fundamental trade-off between selectivity and sustainability that limits current technologies. By utilizing the inherent functional groups in chia seed polysaccharides and employing a green crosslinking approach with epichlorohydrin, this study introduces a new paradigm for sustainable selective adsorption that could revolutionize aluminum removal from industrial wastewater.

Therefore. this research primarly aims to:, (1) synthesize and characterize novel Al, (III)-imprinted polymers using chia seed extract as a sustainable bio-based precursor;, (2) evaluate their adsorption performance, selectivity, and reusability compared to conventional non-imprinted polymers;, (3) optimize operational parameters for maximum aluminum removal efficiency; and, (4) elucidate the adsorption mechanisms through comprehensive kinetic and isotherm modeling. This work represents the first systematic investigation of chia seed-based ion-imprinted polymers for aluminum removal, potentially establishing a new class of sustainable, high-performance adsorbents for wastewater treatment.

Experimental

Materials

Chia seeds were sourced from a local market. The following chemicals were used in this study: Epichlorohydrin (ECH) (CAS No. 106-89-8) obtained from TCI; Ethanol (CAS No. 64-17-5) from Sigma-Aldrich; Sodium tripolyphosphate (STPP) (CAS No. 7758-29-4) supplied by ACMEC Biochemical; Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (CAS No. 1310-73-2) from Scharlau; Hydrochloric acid (HCl) (CAS No. 7647-01-0) from BDH; Aluminum nitrate nonahydrate (CAS No. 7784-27-2) from Sigma-Aldrich; Sodium fluoride (CAS No. 7681-49-4) also from Sigma-Aldrich; and Nitric acid (CAS No. 7697-37-2), which was likewise procured from Sigma-Aldrich.

Monomer extracted from chia seed

Chia seeds were processed to extract functional phytochemicals using a modified procedure described by Rahman et al22., with minor modifications. Briefly, chia seeds were thoroughly washed with deionized water, oven-dried at 60 °C for 24 h, and subsequently ground into a fine powder using a laboratory mill. Approximately 10 g of the powdered material was subjected to extraction in 100 mL of ethanol using an ultrasonic water bath for 4 h at 40 °C to enhance solvent penetration and mass transfer. The resulting mixture was filtered to remove insoluble residues, and the filtrate containing the ethanol-extracted phytochemicals was employed as the functional monomer source for the synthesis of the Al (III) ion imprinted polymer (IIP), as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Schematic representation of chia seed extraction, ion-imprinted polymer (IIP) preparation, and the adsorption–desorption process.

Preparing Al (III)-IIPs and Al (III)-NIPs

The Al-IIP was synthesized via bulk polymerization using the chia seed extract as the monomer23. Briefly, 20 mL of monomer solution was mixed with 5mL of hydrated metal salt (1000 mg/L) solution, then adjusted pH to 5.5 and swirled for one hour at 25 ℃. Subsequently, incorporate 0.3 mL of epichlorohydrin, used as the crosslinking reagent, together with 10 mL of 2.5% STTP (g/L), and allow the mixture to reflux for 18 h. The synthesized polymer underwent filtration and was subjected to multiple washing processes. To remove template ions, the polymer was treated with 0.5 M NaF (60 mL) under stirring for 3 h, repeated three times, followed by regeneration in 0.01 M HNO3 for 1 h24. A non-imprinted polymer (NIP) was synthesized using the same procedure but without Al (III). This ensured proper control for evaluating the selectivity and adsorption performance of the IIPs, as shown in Fig. 2.

RSM and experiment design

Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was employed to systematically optimize adsorption conditions and evaluate interactions among variables, providing predictive insight into the adsorption mechanism. Experiments were designed using Minitab software, considering solution pH (2–10), initial Al (III) concentration (10–160 mg/L), and temperature (10–50 °C). A fixed polymer dose of 0.04 g and a contact time of 30 min were used.

A second-order polynomial model was employed to illustrate the interaction effects and nonlinear relationships among the variables in the experimental domain. This model enabled the forecasting of optimal adsorption conditions throughout the process optimization phase. A linear regression model was employed to elucidate the impact of each parameter on Al(III) uptake. Overall, RSM offered a systematic and effective approach for assessing and enhancing the adsorption process, facilitating a more profound comprehension of the system’s dynamics across different conditions.

Results and discussion

Adsorbent characterization

The chia seed–derived Al(III) ion-imprinted polymer shows enhanced adsorption due to the synergy between its bio-based chemical functionality and the structural specificity introduced by imprinting. Unlike non-imprinted polymers (NIPs), its adsorption is primarily governed by template-driven recognition rather than nonspecific surface sorption, as confirmed by spectroscopic and microscopic characterization.

FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 3) showed distinctive bands that correspond to functional groups that are important for metal coordination. The wide absorption band at 3282 cm-1 is due to –OH stretching vibrations. The peaks at 2861 cm-1 and 1647 cm⁻¹ are due to C–H stretching and C = O vibrations, respectively. The 1169 cm⁻¹ band is for C–O stretching vibrations. As these functional groups are also observed in NIPs, the imprinting process does not introduce new functional groups but rather creates spatially complementary cavities, consistent with previous studies on ion-imprinted polymer mechanisms29.

FTIR of Chia seed, IIP, and NIP.

FESEM analysis provided direct morphological evidence for the imprinting effect (Fig. 4). The Al-IIP before adsorption exhibited a highly porous, interconnected surface architecture, which is characteristic of template-guided polymerization leading to the formation of binding cavities (Fig. 4a,b)30. After Al (III) adsorption, a visible reduction in pore definition was observed, indicating occupation of these cavities by metal ions (Fig. 4c)31. In contrast, the NIP showed no distinct cavity structures either before or after adsorption, displaying only nonspecific surface textures as depicted in Fig. 4d,e,f. These observations confirm that the IIP contains structurally encoded selective binding domains that govern adsorption through shape and size complementarity rather than simple surface attachment.

FESEM images of adsorbents in different scales: Al-IIP (a, b) before and (c) after Al(III) adsorption; NIP (d, e) before and (f) after Al(III) adsorption.

EDX and elemental mapping further validated the adsorption mechanism (Fig. 5). Before adsorption, the IIP matrix was composed primarily of C, O, N, and Cl (Fig. 5a), while after adsorption, a clear Al signal appeared throughout the polymer network (Fig. 5b). This confirms that aluminum ions were successfully captured and immobilized within the functional binding sites of the polymer rather than weakly accumulated on the surface. consistent with the behavior of other reported metal-IIP systems30,31. Because EDX provides qualitative elemental information, these findings align with the FTIR, FESEM, and kinetic evidence confirming specific binding within imprinted cavities rather than nonspecific surface deposition.

EDX and elemental mapping of Al-IIP before and after Al(III) ion adsorption.

Concerning ion-imprinted polymers, TEM can be employed to visualize the polymer’s surface and internal structure, providing insights into the presence and distribution of the imprinted cavities. The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis has been performed at three specific scales: 100 nm, 150 nm, and 350 nm, as depicted in Fig. 6. The polymer sphere displays distinct imprinted layers on its surface, along with a specific distribution of cavities, selectivity, and binding capacity32. These cavities are expected to facilitate high binding specificity for Al (III) ions, contributing to the superior adsorption capacity observed in batch experiments.

TEM morphology in different scales (100, 150, 350 nm) of the prepared Al-IIP using extracted chia seed.

The AFM images in Fig. 7 indicate how the surface of the IIPs changes before and after Al (III) is adsorbed. The surface initially displays increased roughness owing to the existence of open, imprinted cavities intended for particular ion binding. These cavities enhance the surface’s porous characteristics. Upon the adsorption of Al (III), the cavities are filled, leading to a smoother surface as the ions occupy the imprinted voids. This observation validates the efficient ion sequestration by the IIP. The change in surface texture indicates the selectivity and binding capacity of the IIPs33.

Surface characterization of Al-IIP (a) and Al-NIP (b) by AFM before and after Al (III) uptake.

Overall, these combined structural and compositional analyses validate the design principle of Al (III)-IIPs: the selective cavities created via template imprinting, supported by inherent functional groups, are responsible for the enhanced adsorption performance compared to non-imprinted polymers.

Investigation of adsorption phenomena

Effect of contact time

The impact of contact time on Al (III) adsorption by Al-IIP and NIP was studied under controlled conditions (60 mg/L Al (III), pH 5.5, 30 °C, and 0.04 g adsorbent), as shown in Fig. 8. Both materials exhibited a rapid initial uptake of Al (III) ions, reflecting the abundance of active sites at the start of the process. However, the Al-IIP reached equilibrium in about 30 min, while the NIP required nearly 50 min, demonstrating the superior adsorption kinetics of the imprinted polymer. The Al-IIP achieved a higher removal efficiency (≈ 99%) compared to the NIP (≈ 87%), primarily due to the presence of ion-imprinted cavities tailored to Al (III) ions in terms of size, coordination geometry, and functional group arrangement. These specific binding sites enable fast and selective complexation during the early adsorption stage. In contrast, the NIP lacks such recognition sites, relying instead on non-specific surface interactions, which results in slower adsorption and lower capacity34. As the process continues, the imprinted cavities gradually become occupied, reducing the adsorption rate due to site saturation and increased diffusion resistance toward the inner polymer regions. This trend aligns with FESEM and AFM observations, which confirmed the presence of well-defined cavities in Al-IIP that are filled after adsorption. The relatively short equilibrium time further indicates that adsorption is driven by strong interactions between Al (III) ions and functional groups within the imprinted sites, supporting a chemisorption-dominated mechanism35.

The time duration influence of uptake of Al-IIPs and Al-NIPs.

Effect of pH

Solution pH is a key factor influencing Al(III) adsorption, as it affects both the surface properties of the adsorbent and the speciation of Al(III) in solution36. The effect of pH on Al(III) uptake by Al-IIP and NIP was examined across a pH range of 2–10 under fixed conditions (initial concentration: 60 mg L⁻¹, adsorbent dose: 0.04 g, temperature: 30 °C), as shown in Fig. 9. At low pH values (2–4), adsorption efficiency for both materials was significantly reduced. This is mainly due to protonation of functional groups such as –OH and C = O within the polymer matrix, which limits their ability to coordinate with Al(III) ions. Additionally, the high concentration of H⁺ ions competes with Al(III) for available binding sites, further suppressing adsorption34.

The pH influence on Al-IIP and Al-NIP.

As pH increased to 5–6, adsorption improved markedly, reaching its maximum at pH 5.5. In this range, partial deprotonation of functional groups enhances coordination between Al(III) ions and the pre-organized binding sites within the imprinted cavities. The superior performance of Al-IIP compared to NIP under these conditions underscores the role of molecular imprinting in creating selective sites with optimal geometry and functional group orientation for Al(III) binding. This observation is consistent with the chemisorption-driven mechanism suggested by kinetic studies and supported by FTIR analysis34. Beyond pH 7, adsorption efficiency declined, primarily due to hydrolysis and precipitation of Al(III) as aluminum hydroxide species, reducing the concentration of free ions available for adsorption. Similar pH-dependent trends have been reported for other ion-imprinted systems, with optimal uptake typically occurring under mildly acidic conditions. Based on these findings, pH 5.5 was chosen as the optimum for subsequent experiments37,38.

Effect of dosage

The impact of adsorbent dosage on Al(III) removal by Al-IIP and NIP was evaluated across a range of 0.01–0.06 g under constant conditions (60 mg L⁻¹ Al(III), pH 5.5, and 30 °C), as shown in Fig. 10. Initially, increasing the adsorbent dose led to a noticeable improvement in removal efficiency, primarily due to the greater availability of active binding sites and increased surface area for adsorption. The maximum removal efficiency for both materials was achieved at a dosage of 0.04 g39. At this point, an optimal balance exists between the number of available adsorption sites and the concentration of Al(III) ions in solution, ensuring efficient utilization of the imprinted cavities in Al-IIP. The superior performance of Al-IIP compared to NIP at the same dosage further confirms the role of molecular imprinting in creating selective, high-affinity sites for Al(III) binding40.

The adsorbent dose influence on Al-IIPs and Al-NIPs.

Beyond this optimal dosage, further increases in adsorbent mass did not significantly enhance removal efficiency. This plateau effect can be explained by the limited availability of Al(III) ions relative to the excess adsorption sites, resulting in underutilization. Additionally, higher dosages may lead to particle aggregation and overlapping of adsorption sites, reducing effective surface area and hindering mass transfer. Such behavior is typical of systems governed by a finite number of active sites, consistent with Langmuir-type adsorption and the site-specific kinetics observed in this study41,42. Based on these findings, 0.04 g was selected as the optimal adsorbent dosage for all subsequent experiments.

Effect of concentration

The impact of initial Al(III) concentration on adsorption performance was studied for Al-IIP and NIP across a range of 10–150 mg L⁻¹ under constant conditions (pH 5.5, adsorbent dose: 0.04 g, temperature: 30 °C), as shown in Fig. 11. The results indicate that the percentage removal of Al(III) decreases as the initial concentration increases for both adsorbents41. At lower concentrations, removal efficiency is high because the number of available active sites greatly exceeds the amount of Al(III) ions in solution. Under these conditions, Al(III) ions can easily access the imprinted cavities in Al-IIP, enabling efficient and selective adsorption. However, as the concentration rises, the number of Al(III) ions surpasses the available binding sites, leading to progressive saturation of the cavities and a decline in removal percentage43. Effect of temperature

Temperature plays a crucial role in Al (III) adsorption as it influences ion mobility, mass transfer, and the strength of adsorbent–adsorbate interactions. The effect of temperature on Al (III) uptake by Al-IIP and NIP was studied over the range of 10–50 °C under constant conditions, as shown in Fig. 1245.

The temperature influence of Al (III) ion onto IIPs and NIPs.

Adsorption efficiency increased as the temperature rose from 10 °C to 30 °C for both materials. This improvement is attributed to the higher kinetic energy of Al (III) ions at elevated temperatures, which enhances their diffusion toward active sites and promotes effective coordination with functional groups in the polymer matrix. The maximum adsorption occurred at 30 °C, reaching about 99% for Al-IIP and 87% for NIP. The superior performance of Al-IIP reflects the presence of selective imprinted cavities that enable strong and efficient binding under optimal thermal conditions46. Beyond 30 °C, adsorption efficiency declined noticeably. This reduction can be explained by the weakening of Al (III)–binding site interactions at higher temperatures, where increased thermal agitation disrupts coordination bonds and promotes desorption. Excessive heat may also compromise the structural integrity of imprinted cavities, reducing site specificity and overall adsorption capacity47.

The existence of an optimum temperature suggests that Al (III) adsorption onto Al-IIP is controlled by a balance between improved mass transfer and the thermodynamic stability of metal–polymer interactions. This temperature-dependent behavior supports the chemisorption-driven mechanism inferred from kinetic and isotherm analyses, confirming that adsorption occurs through selective interactions within well-defined imprinted sites rather than simple physical adsorption.

Isotherm studies

Adsorption isotherms describe the relationship between the concentration of Al (III) ions in solution and the amount adsorbed onto the polymer surface at equilibrium, providing insight into surface properties and adsorption mechanisms56. To interpret the adsorption data collected, four distinct models were employed: Langmuir, Freundlich, Temkin, Dubinin-Radushkevich, and Sips (Table 1)57,58.

The Langmuir model describes monolayer adsorption on a uniform surface with no interaction between adsorbed ions, while the Freundlich model accounts for multilayer adsorption on a heterogeneous surface with increasing ion uptake as concentration rises1,59. The Temkin model considers how ion–adsorbent interactions affect adsorption efficiency in a limited concentration range, while the Sips model blends Langmuir and Freundlich principles to describe adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces influenced by pH, concentration, and temperature60,61.

The adsorption data for Al (III) ions onto ion-imprinted polymers (IIPs) and non-imprinted polymers (NIPs) reveal the IIPs, significantly higher adsorption capacities and stronger binding affinities across all isotherm models, as shown in Table 3; Fig. 14. The Langmuir model provided the best fit, with IIPs showing a higher maximum adsorption capacity (qmax, cal. = 188.7 mg/g) and a much greater Langmuir constant (KL = 5.88) than NIPs (qmax = 149.25 mg/g, KL = 0.1089), along with lower error values, indicating favourable monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface. The Freundlich model suggested surface heterogeneity, but it showed poor fit, especially for NIPs, as reflected in high error metrics. The Temkin model indicated stronger adsorbent–adsorbate interactions for IIPs (at = 134.14) but had a better statistical fit for NIPs. The (D-R) model predicted the highest capacity for IIPs (qmax = 201.36 mg/g) and revealed chemisorption as the dominant mechanism (E > 10 kJ/mol). The Sips model, which blends Langmuir and the Freundlich behaviours, showed that the IIP surface was nearly homogeneous (n≈ 1), but its fit was inferior to Langmuir and D-R. Overall, the Langmuir and D-R models best described the adsorption of Al (III) onto IIPs, confirming their superior performance and specificity compared to NIPs. The strong fit to the Langmuir model aligns with numerous studies reporting monolayer adsorption on homogeneous IIP binding sites presented by He et al.62 and40.

The comparison of adsorption capacity between the synthesized Al-IIP and previously reported biosorbents shows that the new biosorbent achieved an outstanding maximum adsorption capacity of 188.7 mg g⁻¹, far exceeding those of chitosan-based IIPs (48 mg g⁻¹)63, natural pumice (0.056 mg g⁻¹)64, and immobilized sewage sludge (27 mg g⁻¹)65. It also outperformed synthetic systems such as IIP–PEI/SiO₂ (53.7 mg g⁻¹)66, and semi-interpenetrating polymer cryogel (1.47 mg g⁻¹)67. This superior performance arises from the abundance of chelating groups and well-defined imprinted cavities formed during polymerization. Negative ΔG° values for both adsorbents across all temperatures confirm that Al(III) adsorption is spontaneous. Furthermore, the increasing negativity of ΔG° with rising temperature indicates that adsorption becomes more thermodynamically favorable at higher temperatures, consistent with the observed temperature-dependent behavior. The positive enthalpy change (ΔH° = +160.5 kJ mol⁻¹ for Al-IIP and + 88.5 kJ mol⁻¹ for NIP) demonstrates that the process is endothermic and dominated by chemical interactions rather than physical forces. These values far exceed the typical threshold for physisorption (< 40 kJ mol⁻¹), confirming strong coordination between Al(III) ions and functional groups in the polymer matrix. This is characteristic of ion-imprinted systems, where adsorption involves dehydration of hydrated Al(III) ions followed by site-specific complexation within imprinted cavities31.

The large positive entropy change (ΔS° = +581.9 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹) indicates a significant increase in disorder at the solid–solution interface during adsorption. This entropy gain is attributed to the release of water molecules from the hydration shell of Al (III) and structural rearrangement of polymer chains upon metal binding54. The high entropy compensates for the endothermic nature of the process, resulting in negative ΔG° values and driving spontaneous adsorption according to ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°.

These thermodynamic results align with kinetic and isotherm analyses, confirming a chemisorption-controlled mechanism involving selective binding within imprinted cavities. The magnitude of ΔH° is comparable to or greater than values reported for chitosan–Cu(II) (ΔH ≈ 98 kJ mol⁻¹) and alginate–Pb(II) systems (ΔH ≈ 115 kJ mol⁻¹), highlighting the superior binding strength and thermodynamic favorability of the chia seed–derived Al-IIP47. Overall, the findings confirm that Al(III) adsorption onto Al-IIP is spontaneous, endothermic, entropy-driven, and fully consistent with the molecular imprinting strategy employed in this study.

Selectivity

The selectivity of the synthesized Al-IIP toward Al(III) ions was assessed through competitive adsorption experiments using chemically similar metal ions, including Ca(II), Cd(II), Mg(II), Mn(II), Ni(II), and Zn(II). These ions were chosen because they have comparable ionic radii and coordination properties, making them suitable for evaluating the specificity of the imprinted polymer. Binary solutions containing equal concentrations (60 mg L⁻¹) of Al(III) and each competing ion were treated with 0.04 g of adsorbent at 30 °C for 2 h, as shown in Fig. 16.

Selectivity of Al (III) ion in the presence of other ions using IIPs and NIPs.

The Al-IIP demonstrated a strong preference for Al(III) over all competing divalent ions, confirming its high selectivity. This behavior cannot be explained solely by similarities in ionic size, as several competing ions have radii close to that of Al(III). Instead, the selectivity arises from a combination of factors: charge density, coordination geometry, and the site-specific interactions created during molecular imprinting. Al(III) ions, with their higher charge (+ 3) and stronger coordination tendency, form more stable complexes with oxygen- and nitrogen-containing functional groups in the polymer matrix. During imprinting, Al(III) templates induce the formation of cavities with an optimal spatial arrangement and coordination environment tailored to the geometry and binding requirements of Al(III). In contrast, divalent ions exhibit mismatched coordination geometry and weaker electrostatic attraction, resulting in less favorable interactions with the imprinted sites74.

This pronounced selectivity is consistent with the thermodynamic and kinetic findings of this study. The high enthalpy change and chemisorption-driven kinetics indicate that Al(III) adsorption involves strong, site-specific interactions that competing ions cannot replicate. Consequently, even in the presence of chemically similar species, Al-IIP preferentially recognizes and binds Al(III), validating the effectiveness of molecular imprinting for selective metal ion recognition. Noting that future work will evaluate Al (III) uptake in the presence of competing ions to fully establish selectivity in realistic multicomponent systems.

Regeneration and reusability

Regeneration and reusability are essential for assessing the practical and economic viability of adsorbent materials. To evaluate these properties, the synthesized Al-IIP and NIP were subjected to ten consecutive adsorption–desorption cycles under identical conditions (Al(III) concentration: 60 mg L⁻¹, adsorbent dose: 0.04 g, pH 5.5, and temperature: 30 °C). Removal efficiency was monitored after each cycle, as shown in Fig. 17. Desorption of Al(III) was achieved using NaF followed by HNO₃24.

Reusability of Al (III) onto IIPs and NIPs.

Fluoride ions form strong soluble complexes with Al(III), enabling efficient elution from the imprinted cavities, while nitric acid restores the protonation state of functional groups in the polymer matrix. This regeneration approach effectively removes bound metal ions without damaging the polymer structure. The Al-IIP demonstrated excellent stability, maintaining about 95% removal efficiency even after eight cycles. This minimal decline confirms the robustness of the imprinted cavities and indicates that repeated adsorption–desorption does not significantly alter the polymer’s chemical functionality or structural integrity40.

In contrast, the NIP exhibited a gradual decrease in performance, dropping from roughly 75% to 50% after seven cycles, likely due to the loss of weakly bound, non-specific sites during regeneration. The superior reusability of Al-IIP supports the chemisorption-driven, site-specific mechanism identified in kinetic, isotherm, and thermodynamic studies. Its ability to retain high efficiency after multiple cycles highlights the structural stability and practical potential of the chia seed–derived Al-IIP for long-term aluminum removal from aqueous systems75.

Chemical mechanism

Figure 20 illustrates the proposed chemical mechanism by which ion-imprinted polymers interact with metal ions through pre-organized cavities formed around the template ion during polymerization. In the Al-IIP, these cavities contain densely packed O-donor functional groups (hydroxyl, carbonyl/carboxyl), whose positions and orientations are fixed by the cross-linked network9,76. After template removal, they remain as binding pockets that are geometrically and chemically complementary to Al (III). At the working pH, Al (III) (a hard Lewis acid) is selectively coordinated by these hard O donors, forming inner surface complexes. FTIR changes (shift/broadening of O–H and C = O/C–O bands, and intensity variations after Al(III) loading and elution) indicate direct participation of these groups in Al–O bond formation rather than simple electrostatic attraction77. Within an imprinted cavity, several neighboring O atoms can chelate a single Al (III), likely yielding an octahedral coordination environment, whereas in the non-imprinted polymer, the same groups are randomly distributed and less able to provide multidentate, geometry-matched coordination78.

Possible adsorption mechanism of Al-IIPs.

Electrostatic attraction between deprotonated O-containing groups and Al (III), hydrogen bonding, and host–guest size complementarity assist pre-concentration and orientation of Al (III) at the cavity entrance, but selectivity and strength are dominated by chelate complexation at these inner-sphere sites79,80. The Langmuir isotherm and the Dubinin–Radushkevich model, together with the calculated mean adsorption energy, confirm monolayer chemisorption on a finite number of high-affinity, partially heterogeneous imprinted sites9,81. The pseudo-second-order kinetics further support that the rate-limiting step is electron sharing/exchange during Al–O bond formation, not mass transfer, consistent with complexation-controlled uptake reported for other metal-IIPs and chitosan-based sorbents9,77,81.

Application of a real sample

To assess the practical applicability of the synthesized Al-IIP, adsorption experiments were carried out using real dairy wastewater collected from a local processing facility in Erbil, Kurdistan Region, Iraq. Before testing, the samples were filtered to remove suspended solids and particulates, ensuring accurate measurement of dissolved Al (III) ions. Batch adsorption was performed under optimized conditions (adsorbent dose: 0.04 g, pH 5.5, temperature: 30 °C, and contact time: 30 min), and residual Al(III) concentrations were determined using ICP-OES.

The untreated wastewater contained an initial Al (III) concentration of 0.98 mg L⁻¹, indicating trace-level contamination within a complex matrix of competing ions and organic matter. To validate the method’s reliability and selectivity, spiked recovery tests were conducted by adding known Al(III) concentrations (30, 60, and 90 mg L⁻¹) to the samples. Recovery rates of 99.8%, 97.1%, and 90.04%, respectively, confirmed excellent accuracy across a wide concentration range. Additionally, relative standard deviation (RSD) values below 1.09% demonstrated high precision and reproducibility. The strong recovery performance, even in the presence of interfering ions such as Ca(II) and Mg(II), highlights the superior selectivity of Al-IIP. This can be attributed to the imprinted cavities that enable site-specific chemisorption of Al(III), consistent with the selectivity, kinetic, isotherm, and thermodynamic findings discussed earlier. Overall, the successful application of Al-IIP to real dairy wastewater confirms its robustness, selectivity, and practical feasibility for aluminum removal and preconcentration in complex environmental matrices, underscoring its potential for real-world water treatment and monitoring applications.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed a novel, sustainable, and cost-effective Al (III) ion-imprinted polymer using chia seed extract as a natural monomer. The Al-IIP demonstrated excellent selectivity and adsorption efficiency toward Al (III) ions in both simulated aqueous solutions and real environmental samples. Under optimized conditions (pH 5.5, temperature 30 °C, and adsorbent dosage 0.04 g), the polymer achieved a maximum adsorption capacity of 188.45 mg/g, significantly surpassing that of the non-imprinted polymer (NIP), which reached 150.01 mg/g under the same conditions. Isotherm studies demonstrated a strong fit to the Langmuir model, indicating monolayer adsorption at homogeneous sites, while kinetic analyses followed a pseudo-second-order model, confirming chemisorption as the dominant mechanism. Remarkably, the Al-IIP retained 95.72% removal efficiency after eight adsorption–desorption cycles, highlighting its structural stability and practical applicability, whereas the NIP showed a significant decline. Statistical validation via ANOVA (p = 0.005) confirmed the reliability of the enhanced adsorption performance. Overall, this work establishes bio-derived ion-imprinted polymers as highly promising, reusable, and eco-friendly adsorbents for metal remediation in complex aqueous systems, offering a scalable approach for sustainable water treatment technologies.
