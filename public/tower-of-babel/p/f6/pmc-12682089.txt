Response of accommodation and vergence systems to low dose atropine
Authors: Emily C Woodman‐Pieterse, Rohan P J Hughes, Shelley Hopkins, Alice F Cunningham, Jana Niemand, Bianca Romeo, Natasha L Westcott
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFew studies have comprehensively investigated the effect of low dose atropine on the binocular vision system beyond accommodative amplitude. This study examined the effect of 0.05% atropine eye drops on a range of accommodation and vergence parameters across a 10‐day period.MethodsTwenty myopic, adult participants (mean age [SD] 22.3 [1.0] years, mean spherical equivalent refraction [SD] −1.9 [1.0] D) were randomised to receive either atropine (0.05% atropine sulphate, n = 10) or control (0.9% sodium chloride, n = 10) eye drops for nightly use for nine consecutive nights. Accommodative posture, monocular and binocular accommodative amplitude (AA), positive and negative relative accommodation (PRA/NRA), binocular accommodative facility (BAF), distance and near heterophoria, positive and negative fusional vergences (PFV/NFV), near point of convergence (NPC) and gradient accommodative convergence to accommodation ratios (AC/A) were measured at baseline, day‐3 and day‐10.ResultsBy day‐10, accommodative lag had increased significantly and AA, PRA and BAF had all decreased significantly in the atropine group, while there were no significant changes in any of the control group measurements. Near heterophoria shifted significantly towards esophoria and the NFV break and recovery points were decreased significantly in the atropine group by day‐10. Additionally, NPC reduced by a clinically significant amount (that bordered on statistical significance), with no changes found in the control group. There was no significant change in distance heterophoria, AC/A, NRA or PFV (all p > 0.05) at day‐3 or day‐10 in either treatment group.ConclusionsAtropine (0.05%) use disrupted accommodation significantly over 10 days and increased convergence at near. While near heterophoria shifted towards esophoria, participants' ability to counteract this was also diminished by a reduction in NFV. This suggests that the near heterophoria and fusional reserves of children using 0.05% atropine for myopia control should be monitored closely.Keywords: accommodation, atropine, binocular vision, myopia

Key points

Low dose atropine (0.05%) caused a significant decrease in accommodation range, an increase in near esophoria and a reduction in near negative fusional reserves after nine nights in young adult myopes.

INTRODUCTION

As the prevalence of myopia rises in children and adolescents, parents and practitioners are increasingly seeking strategies to delay onset or slow progression. 1 , 2 Across numerous clinical trials, low dose atropine eye drops of varying concentrations (typically 0.01%, 0.025% and 0.05%) have demonstrated efficacy in slowing myopic shifts and axial elongation in children. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Atropine is an anticholinergic agent that non‐selectively binds to muscarinic receptors within the cornea, iris, ciliary body, lens, amacrine cells, retinal pigment epithelium, choroid and sclera. 11 The mechanism through which atropine protects against myopia development and progression is not fully understood, but is thought to involve binding to receptors within the retina and choroid to disrupt eye growth signalling to the sclera via increased dopamine release. 12 Myopia progression appears to reduce in a dose‐dependent manner with increasing atropine concentration, but greater myopia control comes at the expense of increasing anticholinergic side effects such as mydriasis and cycloplegia. 13 The optimum concentration for myopia control appears to vary with age and ethnicity, with higher concentrations being required for adequate control in younger children and more pigmented eyes. 8 , 10 , 14

Myopia is associated with more unstable binocular vision compared to other refractive groups in both children and adults. Myopes have higher and more variable accommodative lags, less stable accommodative responses, 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 lower accommodative facility, 20 shorter working distances, 21 , 22 higher accommodative‐convergence to accommodation (AC/A) ratios 23 , 24 and are more likely to have a near heterophoria outside of the 1 ∆ esophoria – 3 ∆ exophoria range. 25 Given this, consideration must be given to how myopia control treatments affect the binocular vision system in a population already predisposed to instability.

While low dose atropine is widely reported to diminish the amplitude of accommodation, 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 relatively few studies have investigated how this may also affect the binocular vision system. Most previous studies investigating the effects of 0.01% atropine on accommodation and vergence have reported little to no effect on accommodative lag, accommodative facility, AC/A ratios, horizontal heterophorias or fusional reserves. 10 , 30 , 31 , 32 However, clinical trial data suggest that a 0.01% concentration may not control axial elongation sufficiently and that 0.05% atropine has approximately twice the efficacy, while maintaining an acceptable side effect profile, making it the concentration of choice for progressing myopia. 29 While one recent study examined the effects of 0.05% atropine on accommodation and vergence and reported no significant changes, the medication was only instilled once and parameters remeasured 24 h later. 33

The objective of this study was to investigate the effect of 0.05% atropine eye drops on a range of accommodation and vergence parameters over a 10‐day period, with the aim of providing clinicians with evidence‐based guidance relating to binocular vision function associated with low dose atropine use.

METHODS

This was a randomised, double‐masked, 3‐visit clinical trial with a parallel groups design. The test group received 0.05% atropine sulphate, while a control group received 0.9% sodium chloride. Approval was received from the Queensland University of Technology (QUT) Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) and the trial was registered on the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12621000075886) prior to commencing recruitment. All participants provided written informed consent prior to study enrolment.

Young adults aged 18–25 years with myopia, who were habitually corrected with single vision distance spectacles or contact lenses (myopic refractive error within −0.75 to −6.00 D with ≤1.50 DC of astigmatism or ≤2 D of anisometropia), were recruited from the QUT Optometry and Vision Science student population. Participants were initially pre‐screened for eligibility via an online survey to exclude anyone who was currently or had previously used myopia control treatment; had any history of significant ocular injury, infection or surgery; was pregnant or breastfeeding; had any known allergy to anticholinergic agents or the benzalkonium chloride (BAK) preservative or had any ocular or systemic medical condition or medication that may interact with atropine.

Potential participants attended a baseline screening visit at the university campus. Distance and near logarithm of minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) visual acuity (VA) was measured at 6 m (using an Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study [ETDRS] chart) and 40 cm (with a Bailey‐Lovie logMAR reading chart), using the participants' habitual refractive correction. Those with habitual VA measures of >0.10 logMAR or >0.10 logMAR acuity difference between their two eyes were excluded from the study. Distance and near cover test (both unilateral and alternating) and the TNO stereo test (Laméris Ootech, ootech.nl) were performed to exclude any individuals with strabismus and amblyopia. The mean photopic pupil diameter of each eye was measured using the VIP‐300 pupillometer (Neuroptics, neuroptics.com) in full room lighting (mean [SD] illuminance 279 [110] lux) using a light meter (Protech, electusdistribution.com.au) while participants fixated on a target at 6 m with their non‐measured eye. Distance and near interpupillary distance were measured for each participant using a pen torch and ruler.

Intraocular pressure (IOP) was measured using the TAO1i rebound tonometer (iCare, icare‐world.com). Slit lamp biomicroscopy was used to evaluate the health of the anterior segment, including van Herick grading of the anterior chamber angles and sodium fluorescein instillation to examine tear film stability and ocular surface staining of the cornea or conjunctiva. Any significant ocular health findings were graded and recorded according to the Efron Grading Scale for Contact Lens Complications. 34 Participants with a history of pharmacological angle closure, van Herick ratios <0.3 or IOP >21 mmHg were excluded from the study.

Once eligibility was confirmed, baseline measures of accommodation and binocular vision function were established through each participant's habitual correction. Distance and near heterophorias (D/N phoria) were measured using Howell‐Dwyer (Cyclopean Design, Victoria, Australia) distance (3 m) and near (33 cm) cards, with esophoria and exophoria recorded as positive and negative values, respectively. Near point of convergence (NPC) was measured using a near target (0.20 logMAR) placed initially at a 40 cm working distance, and then slowly moved towards the participant, with the first point of sustained diplopia of the letters recorded as the NPC ‘break and the NPC recovery’ as the point where target singularity was regained while the target was slowly moved away from the participant. The NPC break and recovery were measured as the distance from the target to the participant's spectacle or corneal plane (cm). Positive and negative fusional vergences (PFV/NFV) were measured at near only, while participants fixated a 0.20 logMAR target at 40 cm in primary gaze and a horizontal prism bar was used to introduce increasing prism. NFV was measured first using base‐in prism followed by PFV with base‐out prism, and the magnitude of horizontal prism required to produce break (diplopia) and recovery (single vision) was recorded.

Monocular and binocular amplitude of accommodation (AA) was determined using the push‐up method, measured to the spectacle or corneal plane in spectacle and contact lens wearers, respectively. Binocular accommodative facility (BAF) was performed with a 0.20 logMAR target at 40 cm using ±2.00 D binocular lens flippers, with the number of cycles completed in 1 min being recorded as cycles per minute (cpm). Positive and negative relative accommodation (PRA/NRA) were measured behind a phoropter with the participant's habitual distance correction in place, with a 0.20 logMAR letter target held by a near point rod at 40 cm. Positive lenses (NRA) and then negative lenses (PRA) were added binocularly in 0.25 D increments until participants reported sustained blur. Accommodative convergence to accommodation ratio (AC/A) was measured via the gradient method to plus and minus lenses, using a Howell‐Dwyer near card and recording the heterophoria values with habitual correction and with +2.00 D followed by −2.00 D lenses added binocularly. The AC/A ratio to plus and minus lenses was calculated as the change in near heterophoria divided by the absolute magnitude of the lens powers. Accommodative posture was measured monocularly from each eye using the WAM‐5500 open field autorefractor (Grand Seiko, grandseiko.com), where participants fixated a distance ETDRS chart target (logMAR 0.20) at 6 m and near target (logMAR 0.20) at 40 cm, with the fellow eye being occluded, while wearing their habitual correction. Five measures were taken at distance and at near for each eye and averaged. At the baseline visit only, one drop of Tropicamide 1% was instilled into each eye and a cycloplegic autorefraction (WAM‐5500) was performed 30 min later. The habitual distance correction that participants intended to wear for the duration of the 10‐day trial was all within ±0.50 D (mean spherical equivalent [SER]) of their cycloplegic SER autorefraction result.

Participants were then allocated to the treatment or control group (1:1 ratio) via a block randomisation scheme by the chief investigator (EWP) and the prescription and signed consent forms were sent to a sterile compounding pharmacy for dispensing and delivery (Your Solution Compounding Pharmacy, yoursolutioncompounding.com). The test (0.05% atropine sulphate preserved with 0.1 mg/mL BAK) and control eye drops (0.9% sodium chloride preserved with 0.1 mg/mL BAK) were labelled as ‘Solution A’ and ‘Solution B’, respectively, and packaged in identical 5 mL multi‐dose preparation bottles, having the same volume (2.5 mL) and appearance (colourless). Eye drops were couriered via a cold chain delivery service to the participants within 24–48 h, stored in a foil bag, in which the participants kept their drops in the refrigerator when not in use to protect the product from sunlight. Participants were given verbal and written instructions on how to safely handle and store their eye drops at home, and instructed to return the remaining unused eye drops at the study conclusion. To minimise observational bias, both the participants and members of the research team who performed data collection were masked to the intervention, with the de‐identification code of the solution contents (A or B) known only to the compounding pharmacy and the chief investigator (EWP). Participants were instructed to instil one drop in each eye at night before bed (between 21:00 and 00:00 hr) and perform punctal occlusion for 60 s afterwards to minimise systemic absorption. Eye drop use was to start the evening of day‐1 and conclude the evening of day‐9 (the night before the final study visit) and text message reminders were sent by the research team each night (1–9) at 21:00 hr to remind participants to use their drops. Participant compliance with eye drop use was confirmed by weighing the bottles before and after the 10‐day trial period.

After eye drop use commenced, a second visit was scheduled for day‐3 (after two nights of eye drop use) and a third visit on day‐10 (after nine nights of use). All visits were scheduled between 09:00 and 12:00 hr to minimise variation in the period between drop instillation the evening before and repeated measures of binocular vision parameters across the trial period. The repeated data collection procedures on both days 3 and 10 included: pupil diameter, distance and near VA, IOP, anterior segment examination, distance and near heterophorias, NPC, PFV/NFV, AA, BAF, PRA/NRA and accommodative posture.

Prior to study commencement, estimates of effect size were calculated using published normative means and standard deviations and clinically meaningful differences for each of the study's outcome measures. 35 The calculated effect size was smallest for BAF at 0.4 m, and to detect a difference in BAF between treatment groups with 95% confidence using two‐sided type I error and a minimum power of 80%, a minimum sample size of 18 participants was determined.

Twenty‐one participants (13 females, 8 males) with a mean age (SD) of 22.4 (1.0) years and mean SER of −2.22 (1.54) D were enrolled in the trial. Participants were predominantly of East/Southeast Asian ethnicity (n = 11, 52%), followed by Caucasian (n = 8, 38%), Indian (n = 1, 5%) and mixed East Asian/Caucasian (n = 1, 5%). One female participant withdrew from the trial due to a serious adverse event—the sudden onset of a right‐sided Bell's palsy that occurred after the initial screening visit and trial enrolment, but prior to the commencement of eye drop use. The University Human Research Ethics Committee and Clinical Trial Sponsor (QUT) were notified and the event was deemed to be unrelated to the interventional eye drops. The remaining 20 participants were allocated equally into the atropine (n = 10) and control (n = 10) groups, with no significant differences in mean age, ethnicity, sex distribution, iris colour or SER between groups (all p > 0.05) (Table 1).

The mean of each parameter at each time point was calculated for all participants and the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to ensure data normality prior to further statistical analysis. A repeated measures analysis of variance (RM‐ANOVA) was performed to examine the change in the primary (D and N phoria, PFV/NFV, AA, BAF and PRA/NRA) and secondary outcome measures (IOP, pupil diameter and D and N VA), with within‐subject factors of time (baseline, day‐3 and day‐10) and between‐subject factors of treatment group (atropine and control). If the sphericity assumption was violated, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction to the degrees of freedom was applied. If significant differences (p < 0.05) were identified in the RM‐ANOVA, post hoc testing with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was performed.

The findings were also depicted using a graphical analysis method established by Goss and Jackson 36 and modified by Gifford et al., 37 constructed with group mean (SD) values from baseline and day‐10 being plotted on the same graph, with separate graphs for each treatment group. In this method, the zone of clear single binocular vision (ZCSBV) represents the mean accommodation and vergence range over which participants maintain clear single vision and beyond which binocular vision breaks down and diplopia and/or blur occur. Vergence and accommodation were plotted on the x‐ (∆) and y‐axes (D), respectively, with the accommodative and vergence demand reference point (near tests performed at 40 cm = 2.5 D; accommodative demand × mean participant interpupillary diameter [2.5 × 6.2 cm] = 15.5 ∆) marked. 38 The maximum and minimum limits of the accommodation range were calculated by adding mean absolute PRA to and subtracting mean absolute NRA from the accommodative demand (maximum = accommodative demand reference + |PRA|; minimum = accommodative demand reference − |NRA|) and these were plotted along the vertical vergence demand line. Similarly, the maximum and minimum limits of the vergence range were calculated using the mean absolute PFV break (maximum = vergence demand reference + |PFV break|) and NFV break (minimum = vergence demand reference − |NFV break|), which were plotted along the horizontal vergence demand line. The habitual accommodation and convergence posture was then plotted to illustrate where the posture sat with respect to the limits of the ZCSBV. This was calculated by adding the group mean near phoria to the 15.5 ∆ vergence demand reference line along the x‐axis (negative value for exophoria, positive value for esophoria) and subtracting the accommodative posture from the 2.5 D accommodation demand reference line along the y‐axis (positive value for lag, negative value for lead of accommodation).

RESULTS

Accommodation

Monocular and binocular AA were significantly affected by time (monocular AA F 2,34 = 7.63, p = 0.02; binocular AA F 2,34 = 12.98, p < 0.001) and treatment group (monocular AA F 1,17 = 30.41, p < 0.001; binocular AA F 1,17 = 25.29, p < 0.001) and there was a significant time by treatment interaction (monocular AA F 2,34 = 11.71, p < 0.001; binocular AA F 2,34 = 14.64, p < 0.001). At baseline in the atropine group, the mean monocular and binocular AA was 9.8 (0.9) D and 10.0 (1.4) D, respectively; in the control group, the mean monocular and binocular AA was 10.2 (2.0) D and 10.8 (2.0) D, respectively. The mean (SD) change in monocular AA at both day‐3 (−2.7 [2.5] D, p = 0.01) and day‐10 (−6.1 [1.0] D, p < 0.001) was significant for the atropine group, but not for the control group at any time (all p > 0.05) (Table 2). In the atropine group, monocular AA was 7.1 (2.1) D at day‐3 and 3.7 (0.8) D at day‐10. Binocular AA was reduced by a similar magnitude in the atropine group at day‐3 (−2.7 [2.7] D, p = 0.02) and day‐10 (−6.1 [1.6] D, p < 0.001), with no significant change at day‐3 or day‐10 in the control group (all p > 0.05) (Figure 1a). In the atropine group, binocular AA was 7.3 (2.6) D at day‐3 and 3.9 (1.1) D by day‐10.

PRA was significantly affected by time (F 2,36 = 28.33, p < 0.001), treatment (F 1,18 = 34.00, p < 0.001) and there was a significant time‐by‐treatment interaction (F 2,36 = 21.07, p < 0.001), with participants in the atropine group clearing a mean (SD) of 1.9 (1.1) D less minus by day‐3 (p < 0.001) and 3.7 (1.6) D less minus by day‐10 (p < 0.001). There were no significant changes in PRA in the control group at any time point during the study (all p > 0.05), and there was a significant between‐group difference in the change in PRA at day‐3 and day‐10 (day‐3 atropine group −2.48 (1.80) D, control group −4.5 (1.67) D; day‐10 atropine group −0.68 (0.47) D, control group −4.28 (1.63) D) (both p < 0.001) (Figure 1b and Table 2). NRA remained unchanged by time (F 2,36 = 1.33, p = 0.28) and treatment (F 1,18 = 0.42, p = 0.53) and there was no time‐by‐treatment interaction (F 2,36 = 0.56, p = 0.58).

BAF was significantly affected by time (F 1.52,27.36 = 24.65, p < 0.001) and treatment group (F 1,18 = 96.89, p < 0.001) and there was a significant time by treatment interaction (F 1.52,27.36 = 33.91, p < 0.001). In the atropine group, BAF decreased by a mean of −7.3 (4.9) cpm by day‐3 (p < 0.001) and by −15.0 (3.4) cpm by day‐10 (p < 0.001), with all 10 participants in the atropine group unable to complete a single cycle with the ±2.00 D flippers due to an inability to clear the −2.00 D lenses (Figure 1c). BAF did not change in the control group at day‐3 or 10 (both p > 0.05), with significant between‐group differences in BAF at both days 3 and 10 (both p < 0.001).

Accommodative posture changed significantly with time (F 2,36 = 12.56, p < 0.001) and treatment group (F 1,18 = 17.41, p < 0.001) and there was a significant time by treatment interaction (F 2,36 = 9.79, p < 0.001), with a mean (SD) increase in accommodative lag at day‐3 (+0.41 [0.32] D, p < 0.001) and day‐10 (+0.73 [0.50] D, p < 0.001) in the atropine group (Figure 1d and Table 2). There were significant between‐group differences at days 3 (atropine group +0.85 (0.44) D, control group +0.51 (0.44) D, p = 0.002) and 10 (atropine group +1.17 (0.68) D, control group +0.62 (0.38) D, p = 0.002). The changes in accommodative posture in the control group were small and not statistically significant (all p > 0.05).

Vergence

Distance heterophoria was not significantly affected by time (F 2,36 = 0.82, p = 0.45) or treatment (F 1,18 = 0.90, p = 0.36) and there was no significant time‐by‐treatment interaction (F 2,36 = 1.34, p = 0.27). However, for the near heterophoria, there was a significant effect of time (F 1.24,22.33 = 6.27, p = 0.02), treatment (F 1,18 = 13.41, p = 0.002) and time‐by‐treatment interaction (F 1.24,22.33 = 6.56, p = 0.01). Near heterophoria in the atropine group shifted towards esophoria by day‐3 (change: +1.4 [2.0] ∆, p = 0.05) and further still by day‐10 (change: +5.0 [4.5] ∆, p < 0.001). There was no significant change in near heterophoria from baseline in the control group (all p > 0.05) and the change in near heterophoria was significantly different between the treatment groups by day‐10 (p = 0.005) (Table 3 and Figure 2).

Secondary outcomes

Photopic pupil diameter changed significantly with time (F 2,36 = 14.70, p < 0.001) and treatment group (F 1,18 = 38.98, p < 0.001) and there was a significant time‐by‐treatment interaction (F 2,36 = 24.77, p < 0.001). Both monocular and binocular distance VA were unaffected by time (monocular VA: F 2,36 = 0.13, p = 0.88; binocular VA: F 2,36 = 0.06, p = 0.94) and treatment (monocular VA: F 1,18 = 1.17, p = 0.30; binocular VA: F 1,18 = 0.22, p = 0.65) and there was no time‐by‐treatment interaction (monocular VA: F 2,36 = 0.22, p = 0.22; binocular VA: F 2,36 = 1.68, p = 0.20) (Table 4). DISCUSSION

In this randomised, placebo‐controlled trial, young adult myopes showed clinically and statistically significant changes to their binocular vision over a 10‐day period when using 0.05% atropine eye drops. Accommodative amplitudes reduced significantly in the atropine group, on average by approximately 3 D at day‐3 and 6 D at day‐10. Given the mechanism of action of atropine, this finding is not surprising; however, the amount of cycloplegia observed in the current study was significantly greater than that found in the Low‐concentration Atropine for Myopia Progression (LAMP) study, which reported a mean loss of 2.3 D of accommodation after 2 weeks of nightly 0.05% atropine use, which remained stable thereafter in Chinese children aged 4–12 years. 5 Additionally, a short‐term study within a racially diverse sample of children observed no significant difference in accommodative amplitude 24 h after a single administration of 0.05% atropine. 33 Taken together, these results might suggest that the accommodative amplitude declines over a period of time before stabilising, and that a single dose of atropine is not adequate to evaluate the impact on binocular vision. However, a short‐term study of Caucasian school children with light irides found that one drop of 0.05% atropine reduced the monocular accommodative amplitude by 4 D compared with the fellow untreated eye, a magnitude similar to the present study, which supports a greater influence of ocular pigmentation on the observed change. 39 While less cycloplegia was reported in the LAMP cohort compared to the present study, this may have been due to the younger mean age of the participants (8.4 years) and the higher overall ocular pigmentation may have been more influential. More heavily pigmented irides have the capacity to bind significantly more atropine and attenuate mydriatic and cycloplegic effects compared to an identical concentration in lighter, less pigmented irides. 40

The reduced ability to stimulate accommodation in the atropine group was seen in the marked reduction in PRA (ability to clear minus lenses binocularly) at day‐3 which deteriorated further by day‐10. The loss of PRA by day‐10 was sufficient to be clinically significant, with a minimum normal value of PRA expected to be −2.37 D, whereas the atropine group was only able to clear an average of −0.68 (0.47) D. 35 There was no significant change in either group's ability to relax accommodation (measured via NRA), although both groups exhibited an NRA that was slightly higher than the accommodative demand, suggesting that the habitual distance refractions may have been slightly over‐minused (<0.25 D). Comparatively, in a group of 8–12‐year‐old Chinese children who were randomised to receive either 0.01% atropine alone or in combination with orthokeratology, there was no significant change in PRA or NRA in the 0.01% atropine‐only group at 3 months. 31 However, the discrepancy was presumably due to the significantly lower concentration of atropine used compared with the present study. The PRA reduction in the atropine group was consistent with the significant decline in BAF, with a reduction from a mean of 15 cpm to approximately 8 cpm, with four of the 10 participants receiving atropine being unable to complete even one cycle of ±2.00 D lenses. By day‐10, 100% of the atropine group were unable to complete a single cycle due to their inability to clear the −2.00 D lenses. Previously published studies have shown no significant effect of 0.01% atropine on monocular or binocular accommodative facility in children over the long term. 10 , 31

Accommodative lag increased by a statistically and clinically significant amount with atropine use, increasing further between day‐3 and day‐10. While the control group showed no significant change in their posture from baseline (group mean +0.57 D), the atropine group's increase in lag at day‐3 and day‐10 put their group mean outside of the normal range of +0.50 (0.25) D at both time points. 35 This result is at odds with previous reports of no significant change in accommodative posture 24 h following the instillation of 0.05% atropine, 33 and after longer treatment periods (3–24 months) with 0.01% atropine. 10 , 31 It is suspected, as with accommodative amplitude, that a single dose of 0.05% atropine will not produce changes equivalent to the impact of ongoing daily treatment.

The NPC break and recovery points were not significantly affected by atropine in this study, although the atropine groups' mean break and recovery points were more reduced than the normal expected range for NPC, and this difference bordered on significance. 35 In a study of young Caucasian adults with light irides using 0.01% atropine over five nights, the NPC break point increased slightly overall, but this did not reach statistical significance. 30 Breliant et al. examined multiple concentrations of atropine, noting that NPC recovery was increased significantly 24 h after instillation of 0.01% atropine, but not following the instillation of 0.03% or 0.05% atropine. 33

While distance heterophoria did not change significantly in the atropine group, the near heterophoria underwent a clinically and statistically significant 5 ∆ esophoric shift by day‐10, which was significantly more esophoric than the control group's near heterophoria. Of the few published studies that measured heterophorias following atropine use, most reported no significant changes in distance or near heterophoria during short‐ or long‐term treatment. 31 , 33 In a 12‐month placebo‐controlled trial, myopic children with intermittent exotropia who used 0.01% atropine underwent a statistically significant decrease in near exotropia (i.e., a near esophoric shift) of ~1 ∆; however, this change was considered too small to be clinically significant. 32 The shift towards esophoria at near was accompanied simultaneously by a mean increase in near PFV in the atropine group (albeit one that was not statistically significant) and a clinically and statistically significant reduction in near NFV. At baseline, both the atropine and control groups had fusional vergence ranges that were slightly greater than the normal expected range for adults, 35 and the NFV (break and recovery) was significantly higher at baseline in the atropine than the placebo group. This meant that despite the statistically significant decrease in NFV in the atropine group by day‐10, NFV reserves remained within the normal range. However, given the progressive nature of the magnitude of loss across this investigation, it is possible that over a longer treatment period in an individual with lower divergence reserves, NFV may reduce to a level low enough to produce asthenopia. Previous studies have reported no significant change in the horizontal fusional reserves with 0.01% atropine in children at 3 and 12 months. 31 , 32 However, a significant reduction in the NFV break and recovery 24 h after 0.03% atropine instillation has also been reported, with the placebo group showing a similar change, but no significant change was observed in those receiving 0.05% atropine. 33

The concern these findings raise is that in the long term, an increased tendency to converge at near, coupled with decreased divergence capacity, may lead to an increase in medial rectus muscle tone and the eventual development of esotropia in susceptible individuals. The differences between the findings of the present study using 0.05% atropine in older participants compared to those using lower concentrations in younger cohorts may suggest that 0.01% is a ‘safe’ concentration that is less detrimental to binocular vision than 0.05%. However, multiple case reports in recent years have suggested that 0.01% atropine does have the potential to affect binocular vision significantly. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 Several cases have been published since 2020 that describe a sudden onset, large angle esotropia (larger at near than distance) in children and adolescents (aged 4–15 years) using low dose atropine eye drops for myopia control. 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 The development of esotropia in these cases has been attributed to the residual accommodation retained with lower concentrations of atropine. The increased innervational stimulation of accommodation required to overcome the partial cycloplegia could cause excessive reflex convergence during near tasks, as participants struggled to accommodate to the stimuli presented. 45

Consistent with previous studies, photopic pupil diameter significantly increased at both day‐3 (mean + 1.4 mm) and day‐10 (mean + 1.9 mm) in the atropine group by an amount that was approximately midway between reports from participant groups with heavily pigmented irides (~1 mm) 5 and those with light irides (~3 mm). 39 Distance VA was unaffected by atropine use in the present study, which is in agreement with others investigating 0.05% atropine. 5 , 33 , 39 However, with regard to near VA, the atropine group here decreased by nearly 2 full lines of logMAR VA by day‐10, which has not been reported in other investigations of 0.05% atropine in younger participants. 5 , 33 , 39 The mean age of participants included in the current study was 22 years, whereas clinical trials investigating atropine efficacy and safety typically use populations aged 6–12 years, who will have much higher baseline accommodative amplitudes. The fact that older participants will have lower accommodative reserves, when compared to paediatric cohorts, may explain why the partial cycloplegic effect of 0.05% atropine produced a decrease in near VA in this investigation that has not been reported by others. 5 , 33 , 39 Further, atropine did not affect IOP significantly in this study, consistent with previous work. 46

The conclusions that can be drawn from the results of this research are limited by several factors. First and foremost is the age range of the participants, which was at least 10 years older than a typical patient being treated with low dose atropine. The accommodative amplitude of a child within the optimal treatment range for 0.05% atropine ranges from approximately 16 D at 3–5 years to 11 D at 10 years of age, 47 , 48 compared with an average of approximately 10 D (range 7.4 to 14.3 D) in all participants in this study. PRA also decreases with age, but not significantly until early in the fourth decade of life. 49 Additionally, although this study was designed as a placebo‐controlled trial, due to the mydriasis and cycloplegia associated with 0.05% atropine use, true masking of both examiner and participant was not always possible, with the changes in accommodative amplitudes and pupil diameter being apparent in some participants from as early as the day‐3 visit. While the parameters being measured using objective techniques were presumably unaffected, it is possible that the parameters requiring some subjective input from participants (such as relative accommodation, accommodative facility, heterophoria and fusional reserves) may have been influenced by assumptions regarding their eye drop allocation. An additional limitation of this study is that 75% of participants had brown irides, meaning these results may not be reflective of a typical response in those with less heavily pigmented eyes. The duration of the study could also be seen as a limitation, since it is possible that the binocular vision system could stabilise or change further with ongoing treatment. Although ideally the study would be longer, it is the longest duration that has been investigated thus far with respect to binocular vision, and the results show all affected parameters trending towards further decline between days 3 and 10. Furthermore, the sample size was significantly smaller than participant numbers included in large, randomised controlled trials that have investigated the safety and efficacy of low dose atropine in paediatric populations. While we believe the statistical analysis demonstrates that the study was adequately powered to detect these changes in binocular vision, it should be acknowledged that such a small study population limits the generalisability of these results.

This study should be replicated in paediatric populations and over a period more reflective of typical treatment duration. Future clinical trials involving long‐term low dose atropine use should incorporate additional binocular vision testing at baseline and follow‐up visits, beyond those that evaluate accommodation in isolation, particularly near fusional vergence reserves and near heterophoria measurements. The effects of low dose atropine should also be investigated in participants with less robust baseline binocular vision (such as large baseline heterophoria at near or lower negative fusional reserves) than those of the participants included in this study, who overall had measurements that fell within normal reference ranges. Given that this study was not statistically powered to examine the potential effect that iris colour or intraocular pigmentation may have on the response of the binocular vision and accommodation systems, this should be considered when designing future studies.

This study is the first to report that low doses of atropine, frequently prescribed to the paediatric population for daily use over a period of years, can cause significant alteration of the binocular vision system. The use of 0.05% atropine across nine nights caused a significant decline in the accommodation range and an increase in near esophoria, combined with a diminished capacity to counteract this increasing convergence due to a loss of negative fusional reserves. These findings may indicate that ongoing atropine use, particularly in children with high near work demands, shorter working distances or existing pre‐treatment esodeviation at near, may place undue stress on an already fragile binocular vision system. Baseline binocular vision status should be considered carefully prior to prescribing low dose atropine eye drops for myopia control, and monitored from as early as 1–2 weeks after commencing treatment. Practitioners should ensure that near heterophoria and fusional reserves are tested routinely in patients treated with 0.05% atropine, in addition to tests of accommodative function.
