Characteristics, all-cause healthcare resource utilisation, and costs among high-risk adults with long COVID during Omicron predominance, 2022–2023: a retrospective cohort study using UK primary care data
Authors: Jingyan Yang, Kiran K Rai, Hannah Gowman, Cale Harrison, David Gruben, Rebecca Butfield, Charlie Reynard, Rosie Hulme, Isabel Jimenez, Hannah R Volkman, Jennifer L Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong COVID, a diverse set of symptoms that persist after a minimum of 4 weeks from the initial SARS-CoV-2 infection, has posed substantial burden to the UK healthcare system. However, little is known about the clinical and economic burden during Omicron predominance, and further, the burden on social care.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted using UK primary care electronic records of adults with confirmed and/or probable acute COVID-19 (index) from The Health Improvement Network between 09/2022 and 05/2023 and eligible for COVID-19 vaccination. Long COVID was identified if patients had ≥ 1 long COVID signs/symptoms or long COVID diagnostic or referral code ≥ 4 weeks post-infection. Baseline characteristics, healthcare resource utilisation (HCRU) and costs, and care home admission rates and costs (rates per 100 patient-months (PM) and costs per-patient-per-month (PPPM)), were described in those with long COVID. Outcomes were also stratified by age and COVID-19 vaccine eligibility.ResultsOf 5,661 eligible patients, 2,772 (49.0%) had long COVID; of these, very few (0.8%; n = 23) had a long COVID diagnostic or referral code. Patients with long COVID had high primary and secondary care HCRU: primary care consultation rate of 160.6 per 100 PM; mean primary care consultation cost of £82 PPPM; and hospitalisation rate of 7.1 per 100 PM. In stratified analyses, HCRU rates and primary care costs were higher in older age groups than the younger age groups, and separately, among those eligible for two booster doses than those eligible for only one booster dose during the respiratory virus season.ConclusionThis study highlights the healthcare burden of long COVID during the UK Omicron period, including higher primary care use, hospitalisations, and costs, particularly in older adults and those at high-risk eligible for two boosters, underscoring the need for targeted strategies to address long COVID.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14852-0.Keywords: COVID-19, Long COVID, Primary care, HCRU, Care home, United Kingdom

Background

Five years after the start of the COVID-19 pandemic and over 4 years since the United Kingdom (UK) began its vaccination roll-out, many COVID-19 patients continue to experience long-term symptoms. The long-term sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection is most often referred to as “long COVID” but is also known as “post COVID-19 condition”, “chronic COVID-19” and “post-acute COVID-19”. In December 2020, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) published guidance on the management of long COVID in the UK, in which the long-term effects were defined as new or ongoing symptoms ≥ 4 weeks after the start of acute COVID-19, and therefore encompassing both the ongoing symptomatic (from 4 weeks to up to 12 weeks) and post-COVID-19 syndrome (> 12 weeks) phases [1]. The symptomology is heterogeneous, including fatigue, fever/chills, brain fog, and shortness of breath, where symptoms may be a result of multiple distinct pathologic pathways [2], may vary in type and severity [3], experienced for varying time periods and can change over time [4]. Due to its diverse manifestation and lack of a gold standard approach to defining long COVID, it often remains underdiagnosed [5].

Data from the UK’s Office for National Statistics (ONS) from February and March 2024 showed long COVID affects 3.3% of the population in England and Scotland, equating to around 2 million people [6]. Although previous studies in the UK have primarily focused on the incidence of long COVID among those with severe COVID-19 infection (i.e., hospitalised patients) [7–9], long COVID is not confined to individuals who experienced severe acute COVID-19. Substantial burden also exists among those with mild-to-moderate infections, in which individuals can recover quickly from the acute phase of the illness, while long-term symptoms can significantly impair daily functioning and quality of life [10, 11]. Evidence from a UK cross-sectional survey between January and April 2022 indicated that approximately 69% of patients with mild-to-moderate acute COVID-19 developed long COVID symptoms [12], challenging the assumption that mild disease equates to a lower long-term impact. Furthermore, there is a paucity of evidence on the prevalence of long COVID in those at increased risk of severe COVID-19. In efforts to maintain the protection of those most at risk, guidance published by the Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunisation (JCVI) has informed eligibility for seasonal COVID-19 vaccinations, whereby those considered at high risk of severe COVID-19 are offered one seasonal vaccination dose, and those at highest risk are offered two doses. Individuals considered at high risk include: residents and staff of care homes for older adults, frontline health and social workers, those aged 50 years and older, those aged 5 to 49 years and in a clinical risk group, household contacts or carers of people with immunosuppression [13], with those considered at the highest risk as individuals aged 75 years and older, residents of care homes for older adults, and patients aged 5 years and older with immunosuppression [14].

More importantly, while prior studies provided some insights on long-COVID associated costs in COVID-19 patients requiring hospitalization [15–17], the economic burden of community-managed long COVID in the UK remains unclear. Two studies using the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) database have indicated a median primary care consultation cost of £39 and £31 in the unvaccinated group and vaccinated group with primary series, respectively [18], and long COVID resulted in on average, an additional £2.44 per patient for primary care consultations [19]. However, those findings are based on data from the early phase of the pandemic (up to 2021), an important consideration as Omicron is associated with less severe outcomes compared to earlier variants [20–22], including a reduced risk of long COVID compared to the preceding variants [23]. The present study therefore aims to quantify all-cause HCRU, direct medical costs and transition of care in adult patients with long COVID in the primary care settings in the UK.

Methods

Data source

We used UK primary care electronic healthcare record (EHR) data from patients in England, Northern Ireland, Scotland and Wales, who were registered with general practices contributing to The Health Improvement Network (THIN) [24]. THIN includes health records from 20 million patients from > 790 UK general practitioner (GP) practices, representing 5.0% of the UK population. The distribution of age, sex, prevalence of major medical conditions and mortality rates within THIN is generalisable to the UK population [24]. THIN data include patient demographics, diagnoses and symptoms, clinical measurements, laboratory test results and primary care prescribed medications [25]. Clinical terms within THIN, such as diagnoses and symptoms, are recorded using Read Codes [26].

Study design and population

To address the aim of describing the characteristics, HCRU and costs in patients at risk of severe acute COVID-19 and develop long COVID, a retrospective cohort study was conducted. High-risk adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) who were eligible for either the autumn 2022 or spring 2023 COVID-19 vaccination [27], defined by the UK’s COVID-19 vaccination prioritisation criteria, the Green Book Chap. 14a (see Supplementary Table 1 for operational definitions) [28], with a confirmed or suspected COVID-19 diagnosis code (where the date of the COVID-19 clinical code was defined as the index date) between 1st September 2022 and 31st May 2023 were included. Individuals with a COVID diagnosis < 3 months prior to the index date, or those whose death date occurred prior to the recorded COVID-19 diagnosis date were excluded. The index period was chosen to align with Omicron predominance [27, 29] and the UK’s respiratory virus season [30]. Patients were required to be registered at their GP practice for a minimum of 12-months prior to and 4 weeks after the index date to enable assessment for long COVID (defined as symptoms persisting ≥ 4 weeks post-index). Patients were followed until the earliest of: one year follow-up (i.e., post COVID-19 diagnosis); date of transfer out of GP practice; death; or 31st December 2023.

Study measures

Baseline characteristics

Sociodemographic characteristics at index included: age (categorised as 18–49; 50–64; 65–74; 75–84; and ≥ 85 years), sex, country of GP practice (England; Northern Ireland; Scotland; and Wales), ethnicity (White or Black and minority ethnic [BAME] groups) and social deprivation (measured using quintiles of the 2019 Index of Multiple Deprivation [IMD] score based on the patient’s GP practice postcode). Care home residence status was observed in the 2 years prior to index. Vaccine eligibility groups (eligible for one dose, and eligible for two doses) were defined according to status at index, and were based on guidance for the 2022/2023 respiratory virus season. Those eligible for one vaccine dose (autumn 2022) included patients aged 50–74 years or those aged 5–49 years meeting criteria for high risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes, as defined in the Green Book: Chap. 14a [13, 28]. Those eligible for two vaccine doses (autumn 2022 and spring 2023) included: patients aged ≥ 75 years, immunosuppressed individuals aged ≥ 5 years, or care home residents [14]. Immunosuppressed individuals in the former group were included the two dose group.

Clinical characteristics during the baseline period included: the most recent smoking status (current, former and non-smoker), recent body mass index (BMI) (assessed as a categorical variable: underweight [< 18.5 kg/m2], normal [18.5–24.9], overweight [25.0-29.9]; obese [≥ 30.0]; and unknown) in the five years prior to index, Quan-Charlson Comorbidity Index (Quan-CCI) 2011 [31] within two years prior to index (categorical variable: 0; 1–2; and ≥ 3), frailty as per the electronic frailty index (eFI) in the five years prior to index [32] (fit and frail, with the latter defined as any level of frailty), index quarter (i.e., COVID-19 diagnosis quarter: Quarter 3 [Q3] 2022: September; Quarter 4 [Q4] 2022: October-December; Quarter 1 [Q1] 2023: January-March; and Quarter 2 [Q2] 2023: April and May 2023) and number of previous COVID-19 infections (continuous). Vaccinations during baseline included: influenza vaccination within 12 months prior to index (yes/no), pneumococcal vaccination at any time in patient’s medical history (yes/no), and a COVID-19 seasonal vaccination during the indexing period prior to index (yes/no). Primary care resource use in the 5 years prior to index was assessed and defined as two separate variables: pre-pandemic (prior to 1st February 2020) and during pandemic GP practice consultations (1st February and onwards).

Post-infection vaccination was identified by assessing COVID-19 vaccination after the index date and during follow-up.

Long COVID

Patients with long COVID were identified during the follow-up period. Long COVID was defined as having ≥ 1 primary care clinical code for long COVID signs or symptoms as identified by Subramanian et al. [33] (see Supplementary Table 2), or a long COVID primary care diagnostic or referral clinical code ≥ 4 weeks after index (i.e., COVID-19 diagnosis), as per the NICE definition of long COVID [1] up until the end of follow-up. To account for long COVID’s heterogeneous presentation, we included symptoms that may overlap with other conditions.

All-cause HCRU, primary care costs and admission to a care home

Outcomes (i.e., time-zero) were assessed starting ≥ 4 weeks after index, up until the end of follow-up in patients with long COVID. For HCRU, all-cause primary care consultations (where all clinical consultations were considered), all-cause hospital admissions and outpatient specialist referrals (specifically, cardiology, psychiatry, neurology, pulmonology/respiratory and ear, nose and throat specialist referrals) were reported. Primary care clinical consultations were costed using information from the Personal Social Services Research Unit (PSSRU) [34] and were based on the cost (GBP) of a GP visit. Changes in residence to a care home setting during follow-up were also captured, i.e., admission to a care home among those who did not have a care home clinical code at baseline. Patients who were not observed to have a care home clinical code during follow-up were assumed not to have experienced an admission. Care home admissions may have been either respite or long-term residential care, and therefore, care home type was not differentiated in this study.

Statistical analysis

Means (standard deviation, SD), or medians (quartiles, Q1, Q3) were calculated for numeric variables, with frequency counts and percentages presented for categorical variables. HCRU rates (e.g., outpatient specialist referral rate) were reported per 100 person-months (PM), where the sum of the total number of healthcare interactions observed across all patients was divided by the sum of the time at risk across all patients, and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated using a univariate Poisson regression.

Among those identified with long COVID, HCRU, care home admission post-index and primary care cost outcomes were stratified by age (18–49 years; 50–64 years; 65–74 years; 75–84 years; and ≥ 85 years) and COVID-19 vaccine eligibility (1 dose: eligible for one COVID-19 vaccine dose during the respiratory virus season, i.e., eligible for autumn 2022 booster only; 2 doses: eligible for two COVID-19 vaccine doses during the respiratory virus season i.e., both autumn 2022 and spring 2023 COVID-19 booster). HCRU was compared across stratifications using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables (i.e., primary care consultation cost) and Wald test, from a Poisson regression, for rates.

Results based on data from < 10 patients were suppressed to comply with Cegedim’s reporting rules. The initial cohort was defined, and the data were extracted, using the data extraction for epidemiological research (DExTER) tool for automated clinical epidemiological studies [35], with all analyses conducted in STATA version 18.0.

Discussion

In this UK population-based retrospective cohort study, we observed among those with long COVID, costs and HCRU and care home admissions were primarily driven by older adults and the high-risk who were eligible for two COVID-19 vaccine doses during the 2022/2023 respiratory virus season.

Our findings on the sociodemographic characteristics of those with long COVID are consistent with previous studies, which have shown the risk for developing long COVID was greater in those of female sex, non-white ethnicity [36] and with increasing age [37], as well as recent ONS long COVID prevalence estimates which were higher in females (3.5%) and ages 35–64 years (4.7%-5.0%) [6]. We also observed a high proportion of ever smokers (ex- and current smokers) with long COVID, which aligns with evidence that smoking is associated with increased risk of developing long-term sequelae of COVID-19 [38]. That said, smoking is likely to act as an effect modifier, whereby smokers are at increased risk of comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease, which is both a symptom and a risk factor for developing long COVID [39]. As such, health system interventions could be tailored based on risk factors associated with long COVID. Additionally, whilst the geographic preponderance for long COVID in Scotland and Wales may reflect unmeasured confounders, it enables geographically focused public health policies with practical implementation benefits.

Data from a population-based retrospective study, conducted between January 2020 and January 2023 in England, reported median GP consultations 0.82 (95% CI: 0.46, 1.42) PPPM and 0.59 (95% CI 0.31, 1.09) PPPM in those with and without long COVID, respectively [40]. The study also reported median HCRU related costs of £59 (95% CI 25, 130) PPPM and £37 (15, 89) in those with and without long COVID, respectively, which encompassed primary and secondary care (outpatient, inpatient and emergency department) costs. Our study demonstrated similar patterns; primary care resource use was high in those with long COVID. However, notably, during our Omicron predominance study period we found overall greater primary care resource use in those with long COVIDCOVID (median GP consultations [95% CI] PPPM: 1.4 [0.8, 2.2]. Data not reported in tables).

Further, whilst the costs in the present study only focused on GP consultations the estimates in each subgroup were higher (median GP consultation cost [95% CI] PPPM: long COVID: £66 (39, 106); without long COVID: £35 (14, 67). A second study population-based English study estimated primary care consultation rate as 1.59 and 2.76 in those with a long COVID diagnosis and those without a diagnosis but symptoms of long COVID, with a mean cost (per person year) as £94 and £130, respectively [19]. In comparison with our present study, these estimates are also lower, however it should be noted that Tufts et al.’s study defined long COVID using a differing definition, occurring ≥ 12 weeks after index (COVID-19 diagnosis), and the 2020/2021 study period covered an earlier stage of the pandemic (i.e., Alpha variant predominance period). Separately, among those with long COVID, we observed higher HCRU and costs in older adults and those at highest risk of outcomes of SARS-CoV-2 infection (i.e., eligible for two COVID-19 vaccine doses); these findings align with a population-based study using English data assessing HCRU and costs during the acute phase of COVID-19 [41], suggesting the impact of these drivers of higher resource use persist beyond the initial infection.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first UK-based study to assess care home admission rates during Omicron predominance. As such, incident care home admission may be attributed to the increased frailty, worsening comorbidities, increased risk of falls and poorer quality of life associated with long COVID among older adults [42, 43]. Further, due to delays in clinical coding (i.e., recording of care home admission), it is difficult to determine whether the admission to a care home setting was attributable to outcomes experienced during the acute phase of COVID-19, long COVID, or other non-COVID-19 related factors, such as the effects of comorbidities.

Given the high incidence of COVID-19 in care homes [44], increased risk of long COVID among older patients [37], and the paucity of evidence on long COVID burden in this setting, further research is warranted particularly given the potential implications for social care systems and vulnerable populations in care home settings.

This study had several limitationsBy. Most notably, this study is descriptive, and analyses did not account for confounding, such as baseline health status and prior HCRU. Therefore, causal inferences cannot be drawn, and observed differences in HCRU, costs and care home admission by age and COVID-19 vaccine eligibility should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, by restricting the cohort to individuals eligible for seasonal COVID-19 vaccination, we examined a population at high risk of severe COVID-19. This selection criterion likely resulted in overestimation of long COVID prevalence, HCRU and associated costs, with limited generalisability to the broader population. Noting that eligibility asvaccine eligibility was only assessed at the time of COVID-19 diagnosis, and as per study design, we would have excluded persons who met the criteria during follow-up (e.g., aged < 75 years at COVID-19 diagnosis but turned 75 years during follow-up). Further, the end of routine COVID-19 testing within the UK (1st April 2022 in England and Wales, 22nd April 2022 in Northern Ireland and 1st May 2022 in Scotland [45–47]) may have led to undertesting and underreporting of acute COVID-19 infections in the general population during the study period. Those with milder symptoms are less likely to interact with the healthcare system, and in contrast those with symptoms requiring intervention are more likely to present at their GP practice, which may have introduced selection bias in this study. Although note, a systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2024 reported a similar prevalence estimate as observed in this study; the estimated pooled long COVID prevalence in Europe was 46.3% (95% CI: 39.5% − 53.0%) [xxx] [48]. Second, currently there is no universally agreed definition for long COVID, and thus the definition adopted within this study may not reflect other definitions of long COVID. The lack of a clear definition may lead to GPs misdiagnosing long COVID, and others may refrain from including a diagnosis (i.e., misclassification). As a result, we adopted an inclusive approach to identifying patients based on long COVID diagnostic or referral codes and the presence of long COVID signs and symptoms. However, due to database limitations, those with worsening of pre-existing signs/symptoms subsequent to COVID-19 could not be fully captured in this study and therefore, our long COVID prevalence estimate may not reflect such persons. Secondary care HCRU and costs were identified using diagnostic codes in primary care records. However, the information regarding hospitalisations available in the THIN database is limited, and thus the secondary care HCRU and costs observed in this study may be underestimated. The impact of long COVID has been dynamic with each COVID-19 variant [49, 50]; as such, patients with long COVID infected by the wildtype virus or preceding virus variants are likely to have experienced differing symptoms and severity of symptoms than those developing long COVID during Omicron predominance [2, 50]. Due to the extremely low uptake of long COVID diagnostic codes, we were unable to isolate healthcare resource use and costs specifically attributable to long COVID from those related to comorbidities, potentially inflating estimates of long COVID-specific burden. Lastly, although the patient composition of the THIN database is nationally representative of the UK population, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, there is an overrepresentation of patients from more affluent geographical areas than the general population; this may limit the generalizability of HCRU estimates to those living in areas of deprivation. Secondly, participation in the THIN database is voluntary for GP practices; this introduces possible selection bias, which may impact on the representativeness of the population and generalizability of the results. Finally, the use of Read codes within primary care records introduces possible diagnostic misclassification, as the accuracy and consistency of coding may differ between practices.

As a descriptive study, our analysis is subject to confounding by indication. Future research incorporating adjustment for baseline patient characteristics, assessing the effects of COVID-19 vaccination in high-risk patients and exploring alternative long COVID definitions would complement these findings and provide deeper insight into the healthcare burden of long COVID. Our study suggests that long COVID imposes a considerable burden on UK health and social care system even during the Omicron predominance period, where incidence rates were relatively lower compared to earlier variants. These findings provide evidence for the need of long-term resource allocation and prevention of long COVID.
