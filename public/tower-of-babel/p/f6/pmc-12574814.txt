Operative Time as a Universal Risk Factor for Infectious Complications after Colorectal Cancer Surgery: A Multicenter Stratified Analysis by Surgical Site
Authors: Takao Shimizu, Takeshi Yamada, Takuya Nishino, Kay Uehara, Akihisa Matsuda, Keisuke Minamimura, Yasuyuki Yokoyama, Ryo Ohta, Nobuhiko Taniai, Hiroshi Yoshida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives: Postoperative infectious complications in colorectal cancer prolong hospital stays and increase the risk of recurrence. Given that risk factors may vary depending on the surgical procedure―namely, right-sided colon, left-sided colon, and rectal cancer―we aimed to identify both common and procedure-specific risk factors． Methods: This retrospective multicenter study included 2,636 patients who underwent elective colorectal cancer surgery between April 2015 and March 2023 at three affiliated institutions. The primary endpoint was the incidence of Clavien-Dindo grade ≥2 infectious complications by surgical site. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify common and procedure-specific risk factors across the three anatomical groups． Results: Infectious complications occurred in 18.5% of patients. The incidence significantly differed by surgical site: 11.7% in right-sided colon, 14.6% in left-sided colon, and 22.0% in rectal cancer (P < 0.001). Operative time was the only risk factor common to all procedures. Right-sided colon cancer was associated with additional risks including advanced age, low BMI, male sex, and open surgery. Rectal cancer, in contrast, showed younger age and male sex as contributing factors. Both male sex and higher BMI correlated significantly with longer operative times in all groups． Conclusions: Prolonged operative time appears to be a consistent and independent risk factor for postoperative infectious complications across various colorectal cancer procedures. In contrast, other factors such as age, body mass index, sex, and surgical approach showed associations that varied depending on the type of procedure．

Introduction

Colorectal cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide[1]. Although surgical resection is the cornerstone of curative treatment, postoperative complications remain a significant clinical concern. Data from the Japanese National Clinical Database (NCD) report complication rates of 6.8% for sigmoid or partial colon resection, 7.4% for right hemicolectomy, and 11.9% for low anterior resection[2]. Among these, infectious complications are particularly frequent and clinically impactful. They not only prolong hospitalization but also increase the risk of recurrence and worsen long-term survival outcomes[3,4]. Thus, identifying modifiable perioperative risk factors for infectious complications is essential to improving surgical outcomes. Various factors―such as advanced age, frailty, male sex, comorbidities, smoking, alcohol consumption, anemia[5], weight loss, and obesity―have been reported as potential contributors to postoperative complications[6]. However, findings across studies remain inconsistent．

While previous studies have investigated risk factors for postoperative infections in colorectal cancer, most have treated the disease as a single entity or broadly categorized it as colon versus rectal cancer. Few have conducted stratified analyses based on anatomical location―namely, right-sided colon, left-sided colon, and rectum―despite well-documented differences in patient demographics, comorbidities, and surgical complexity. For example, right-sided colon cancer is more common in older patients and those with cardiovascular disease[7], whereas rectal cancer carries a higher risk of anastomotic leakage[8,9], particularly in males with narrow pelvic anatomy[10], though this association remains debated[11]．

To address this critical gap in the literature, the present study analyzes infectious complications separately for right-sided colon, left-sided colon, and rectal cancer surgeries, each of which has distinct anatomical and biological characteristics. Importantly, it remains unclear whether known risk factors―such as open surgery, obesity, operative time, age, and sex-are universally applicable across these surgical subtypes or are procedure-specific. This study seeks to clarify that distinction by identifying both shared and procedure-specific risk factors, thereby enabling more individualized risk stratification and targeted perioperative management strategies in clinical practice．

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective multicenter study. Data from patients who underwent surgery for colorectal cancer at Nippon Medical School Musashi Kosugi Hospital, Nippon Medical School Hospital or Nippon Medical School Chiba Hokusoh Hospital from April 2015 to March 2023 were reviewed. The inclusion criteria were patients with pathologically confirmed colorectal cancer who underwent elective complete resection of the primary tumor, including those with R1 resection. The presence of distant metastasis was allowed. Exclusion criteria were emergency admission and complicated resection (e.g. urinary bladder resection or hepatectomy). This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Nippon Medical School (Tokyo, Japan, approval number: B-2020-144). Informed consent was obtained by providing a means to opt out．

The primary endpoint was the incidence of Clavien-Dindo (CD) grade 2 or higher infectious complications. Infectious complications included superficial or deep incisional surgical site infection (SSI) and organ space SSI. Superficial and deep incisional SSI involved onset within 30 days after surgery, and (i) purulent drainage from the incision, (ii) bacteria isolated from fluid or tissue collected from the incision, or (iii) intentional re-opening of the incision due to continuing manifestation of at least one of the following symptoms of infection: pain, tenderness, localized swelling, redness, or warmth, or (iv) diagnosis of incisional SSI by a physician. Organ/space SSI included onset within 30 days after surgery and infection extending to any part of the body that was opened or manipulated during the surgical procedure, and (i) purulent drainage from a drain implanted in an organ/body cavity or (ii) bacteria isolated from aseptically collected fluid or tissue specimens from organs/body cavities, or (iii) evidence of abscesses or other infections involving organs/body cavities found by direct search, during reoperation, tissue pathology, or radiology, or (iv) diagnosis of organ/space SSI by a physician. Remote infections included urinary tract infection and pneumonia．

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted using the following 11 variables: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status classification (ASA-PS), open surgery, operative time, perioperative blood transfusion (defined as transfusion during or up to discharge after surgery), use of anticoagulants, use of antiplatelet agents, iron supplementation, and diabetes mellitus. In accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, the selection of variables was based on established evidence from previous literature prior to conducting the analysis. Additionally, since the right-sided colon cancer group had the fewest events (106 cases with infectious complications), the number of variables in the model was limited to 11 to avoid overfitting．

Age, sex, BMI, ASA-PS, and diabetes are well-established risk factors for infectious complications, as consistently reported across multiple studies[6,8,9]. Open surgery and longer operative time are also recognized as significant risk factors with strong supporting evidence[12,13]. Perioperative blood transfusion has been associated with immunosuppression and increased risk of infection, including anastomotic leakage[14,15]. The use of anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents was considered a surrogate for underlying cardiovascular comorbidities, which may elevate infection risk[16,17]. Iron supplementation was included as a surrogate marker for preoperative anemia, a known contributor to infectious complications[5]．

Although the presence of stoma and abdominoperineal resection (APR) are well-known risk factors for infectious complications, they were not included in the present analysis. This is because the primary objective was to identify risk factors that are applicable across all three types of colorectal surgical procedures. Consequently, certain potential risk factors, such as tumor T stage, tumor size, and smoking status, were not included in the multivariate model. Similarly, data on chemical or mechanical bowel preparation were excluded due to lack of standardization across institutions and surgical procedures. Omission of these factors represents a limitation of this study, as they may influence the risk of postoperative infectious complications．

Operative time ≥180 minutes has previously been associated with a nearly threefold increase in the risk of surgical site infections[12,13]. However, operative time varies by procedure type, and completion of rectal cancer surgery, particularly for low rectal lesions, within 180 minutes is generally unrealistic. Therefore, operative time was analyzed as a continuous variable, as were age and BMI．

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as numerical values and percentages and compared using chi-squared tests. Continuous values were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Multivariate logistic regression was performed using the forced input method to identify independent factors associated with outcomes. A two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using R software version 4.2.2 Patched (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria)．

Results

Patient characteristics

During the study period, 3,015 patients underwent colorectal cancer surgery. Of those, 172 were admitted as emergency cases, and 207 received complicated resections. Both of these groups were excluded from further analyses; thus, this study included 2,636 patients (right-sided colon 907, left-sided colon 800, rectum 929). Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. Rectal cancer was more common in males, was associated with lengthier operations, and had a high proportion of laparoscopic surgeries. The proportion of female patients was higher in right-sided colon cancer compared to left-sided colon and rectal cancer. Right-sided colon cancer involved older patients with a higher prevalence of open surgeries, and required blood transfusions or iron supplementation. The median operation time for rectal cancer surgery was 263 minutes, longer than that of the other surgical procedures, with the first quartile also exceeding 180 min．

Incidence of infectious complications and their risk factors

A total of 488 patients (18.5%) developed infectious complications classified as Clavien-Dindo grade ≥2. The overall incidence of infectious complications differed significantly by surgical procedure: 11.7% for right-sided colon, 14.6% for left-sided colon, and 22.0% for rectal cancer (P < 0.001; Table 2). Similarly, the incidence of surgical site infections (SSI) showed significant variation among the groups (right-sided 5.1%, left-sided 7.8%, rectum 12.9%, P < 0.001). In contrast, no significant differences were observed in the incidence of remote infections. Although SSI varied by surgical site and remote infections did not, this study focused on total infectious complications as the primary outcome due to their potential prognostic impact. Accordingly, we proceeded with the analysis of risk factors for overall infectious complications, as originally planned．

Determinants of operative time

Because operative time was identified as a common risk factor for infectious complications, we further analyzed the factors associated with longer operative durations using 10 of the 11 predefined variables, excluding operative time itself. As shown in Figure 1, median operative time was significantly longer in male patients across all three procedures (right-sided P<0.001, left-sided P<0.001, rectum P<0.001). Factors influencing operative time are shown in Table 4. In all procedures, male gender (right-sided P<0.001, left-sided P<0.001, rectum P<0.001), BMI (right-sided P<0.001, left-sided P<0.001, rectum P<0.001), and blood transfusions (right-sided P=0.004, left-sided P<0.001, rectum P<0.001) showed a significant correlation with longer operative time. Conversely, open surgery showed a significant correlation with shorter operative time in all three surgical procedures (right-sided P<0.001, left-sided P=0.015, rectum P<0.001). Use of anticoagulants was significantly correlated with shorter operative time exclusively in the right-sided (P=0.003), whereas antiplatelet agents showed a significant association with shorter operative time only in the rectum (P=0.014)．

Discussion

This study identified prolonged operative time as the only risk factor for postoperative infectious complications common to all three colorectal procedures―right-sided colon, left-sided colon, and rectal cancer surgeries―despite the distinct clinical characteristics and complication rates associated with each. In contrast, other factors such as age, sex, BMI, and surgical approach varied in their associations depending on the surgical site, highlighting the importance of procedure-specific risk assessments．

Despite differences in patient backgrounds, prolonged operative time is the only risk factor common to all three colorectal procedures. Increases in operative time of 15, 30, and 60 minutes have been shown to raise the risk of SSI by 13%, 17%, and 37%, respectively[18]. Even in short-duration surgeries, such as laparoscopic cholecystectomy, prolonged operations lead to an increase in infectious complications[19]. This may be attributable to prolonged exposure to micro-organisms and higher risk of intraoperative hypothermia[20]. Interestingly, the types of bacteria detected differ between short and long surgeries. In spine surgery, for example, intestinal bacteria such as Enterococcus have been detected only in procedures with longer operative times[21]．

Among the three procedures, rectal cancer surgery had the highest incidence of postoperative infectious complication, which coincided with the longest operative times. However, other conventional risk factors―such as older age, high ASA score, open surgery, and low BMI―were less prevalent in this group. Aside from a higher proportion of male patients, rectal cancer cases did not exhibit an increased frequency of these risk factors, suggesting that operative time may play a particularly dominant role in infection risk in rectal surgery. Interestingly, younger age was associated with a higher risk of infectious complications in rectal cancer, which contrasts with findings from previous studies. In recent years, the incidence of early-onset colorectal cancer has been rising, particularly in rectal cancer cases[22-24]. Early-onset colorectal cancer is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage[25], which may be associated with longer operative time. However, in the present study, no correlation was found between patient age and operative time in rectal cancer surgery. Therefore, further research is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms of this association．

In contrast, right-sided colon cancer exhibited several procedure-specific risk factors. Older age, male sex, low BMI, and open surgery were all independently associated with increased infectious complications. Right-sided colon cancer was more common in female patients and in older individuals, with a higher prevalence of open surgery, cases requiring blood transfusions or iron supplementation, and use of anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents in the present study. Many of these factors are characteristic of frailty, and in patients considered unable to tolerate prolonged surgery due to frailty, open surgery was often selected in an effort to shorten operative time. It is well known that patients with right-sided colon cancer tend to be older, have more comorbidities, and are more likely to be clinically frail compared to those with left-sided colon or rectal cancer[7,26,27]. Numerous studies have reported an increased risk of infectious complications in elderly patients[28,29]; however, in this study, older age was a procedure-specific risk factor only for right-sided colon cancer．

While open surgery have been widely recognized as risk factors for postoperative infections[30,31], our findings indicate that these associations may be procedure-specific. Open surgery was not significant in left-sided or rectal procedures. These discrepancies may be attributable to differences in surgical exposure, patient physiology, or institutional practices. Previous randomized controlled trials have yielded conflicting results regarding the risk of infection in open versus laparoscopic colorectal surgery; however, most did not stratify patients by tumor location, limiting their interpretability in this context[32,33]．

Several studies have suggested that males may have a biologically increased vulnerability to infections. The X chromosome, of which women possess two copies, harbors around 1,100 genes, including several that regulate immune functions, such as interleukin-1 (IL-1) receptor-associated kinase 1 (IRAK1), IL-2 receptor-γ chain, IL-3 receptor-α chain, IL-9 receptor, Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) and 8, and FOXP3[34]. Females have stronger innate and adaptive immune responses than males, leading to more effective elimination of pathogens. Males have higher proportions of natural killer cell counts[35], whereas females have higher neutrophil counts in peripheral blood[36]. However, given that male sex was a procedure-specific risk factor only for right-sided colon cancer in this study, it is unlikely that biological sex differences alone play a major role in the development of infectious complications．

Although male sex and high BMI were not directly associated with infectious complications in the overall analysis, they were significantly correlated with longer operative times across all surgical sites. Prior studies have linked these factors to technical difficulty, particularly in pelvic procedures[37,38]. High BMI has been considered a risk factor for infectious complications due to factors such as the role of adipose tissue in modulating inflammation and immunity[39], as well as significantly lower subcutaneous oxygen tension during surgery in obese patients compared to non-obese individuals[40]. However, the findings of the present study suggest that male sex and obesity may indirectly increase infection risk by prolonging operative duration, rather than through biological mechanisms alone．

ASA-PS ≥ 3 has previously been reported as a risk factor for infectious complications[41,42]; however, in the present study, ASA-PS ≥ 3 was associated with shorter operative time. This inverse association may explain why it was not identified as a risk factor in this analysis. This inverse relationship may reflect a tendency among surgeons to perform less invasive or shorter procedures in patients with poor general health, possibly due to concerns over perioperative risk. As a result, the shorter operative time may have mitigated the expected impact of high ASA-PS on postoperative infectious complications. This suggests that ASA-PS alone may not accurately reflect risk in contexts where surgical strategies are tailored based on preoperative performance status. Of course, the potential influence of confounding with other covariates, selection bias, procedure-specific risk dominance, and limited statistical power cannot be ruled out. In particular, the possibility of selection bias must be acknowledged in a retrospective study. Since this study included only patients who underwent elective colorectal cancer surgery, those with severely impaired general condition (e.g., ASA class IV or V) may have been excluded from surgery altogether or treated with less invasive alternatives．

This study has several limitations. First, although this is a multicenter study, it is a retrospective in nature. Second, while all participating institutions followed clinical pathways to standardize the type and duration of postoperative prophylactic antibiotics, different antibiotic agents were used in some cases. Third, although laparoscopic surgery is generally associated with a lower incidence of infectious complications compared to open surgery, this study did not analyze laparoscopic and open procedures separately due to the relatively small number of open surgeries. In Japan, the frequency of open surgery is steadily decreasing, which may make it increasingly difficult to compare open and laparoscopic surgeries in the future[43]. In addition, mechanical and chemical bowel preparations were not standardized across institutions. Finally, data on smoking status, which is an established risk factor for infectious complications, were not available in this study．

In conclusion, this study suggests that prolonged operative time may be a common risk factor associated with postoperative infectious complications across all three colorectal cancer procedures: right sided colon, left sided colon, and rectal cancer surgeries. In contrast, other potential risk factors, including age, body mass index, male sex, and open surgery, showed procedure specific associations. These findings highlight the potential importance of operative time as a broadly relevant factor and support the need for procedure specific risk assessment strategies．
