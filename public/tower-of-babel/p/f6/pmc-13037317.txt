Surgical Techniques: Robotic versus conventional Laparoscopic cholecystectomy IN benign Gallbladder disease: a randomized controlled, open, parallel, non-inferiority, single-center trial (STaRLING trial)
Authors: Jennifer Straatman, Cezanne D Kooij, Stuart J Mercer, Philip H Pucher, Michael A Glaysher, Nicholas Jenkins, Vanessa Tucker, Nicholas C Carter, Gijsbert I van Boxel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobotic surgery is increasingly being adopted for gallbladder removal, despite the absence of randomized trials comparing its safety and patient outcomes to the current gold standard, laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC). Existing literature reviews have found several case series showing no significant differences in postoperative complications or recovery between robotic cholecystectomy (RC) and LC. This trial aims to evaluate whether RC is non-inferior to conventional LC.MethodsThis is a prospective single-center, randomized controlled, parallel, non-inferiority trial. All adult patients with benign gallbladder disease with an indication for cholecystectomy will be assessed for eligibility and included after obtaining informed consent. A total of 276 patients will be randomized to undergo either RC or LC. The primary endpoint will be the incidence and severity of postoperative complications within 30 days after surgery. Secondary endpoints include perioperative outcomes, total health care utilization, health-related quality of life (HR-QoL) as assessed by patient-reported outcome measures, and cost-effectiveness. Follow-up assessments will be conducted at 7 and 30 days postoperatively.DiscussionThis is the first randomized controlled trial designed to compare RC with LC in the treatment of benign gallbladder disease. The trial aims to evaluate the safety and non-inferiority of RC relative to LC, providing important evidence to guide the progress and adoption of robotic surgery in clinical practice.Trial registration This trial was registered on 14 July 2025 at ClinicalTrials.gov with the identifier NCT07119203, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07119203.Keywords: Robotic cholecystectomy (RC), Laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC), Benign gallbladder disease, Randomized controlled trial (RCT), Cost-effectiveness

Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Gallbladder disease is among the most common indications for gastrointestinal-related hospital admissions, with the vast majority of cases involving benign gallbladder disease [1, 2]. Consequently, cholecystectomy is one of the most frequently performed procedures by general surgeons, with over 75,000 carried out annually in the UK [3]. Initially performed as an open procedure, laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) has, since its introduction in 1985, become the gold standard for gallbladder removal, offering shorter hospital stay and quicker return to work compared to open cholecystectomy [4], along with low conversion rates to open surgery and a low incidence of bile duct injury [5].

In the early 2000 s, robotic surgery was introduced to overcome the challenges of laparoscopy, offering enhanced precision through features such as wristed instrumentation, tremor filtration, three-dimensional visualization, and motion scaling [6]. Initially, robotic surgery was primarily used for technically complex procedures [1]. However, as more centers expand their robotic surgery capabilities, its use in the treatment of benign gallbladder disease is increasing [7, 8]. Furthermore, beyond ‘standard’ cholecystectomy, robot-assisted techniques have the potential to facilitate more complex biliary procedures, such as common bile duct (CBD) exploration [9]. Nevertheless, it is imperative to establish whether robotic cholecystectomy (RC) is safe, and at least non-inferior to LC, before implementing this technique or exploring more advanced options.

A systematic review comparing RC with LC found that the existing evidence is limited and generally of low quality [10]. The available studies suggest that robotic procedures are associated with longer operating times, but may offer a lower conversion rate compared to LC. Postoperative recovery and complication rates appear comparable between the two approaches [10]. Despite the limited evidence, the growing body of data supporting robotic techniques in complex procedures, such as robotic esophagectomy and pancreaticoduodenectomy, highlights the potential value of routine RC [11, 12]. In addition, RC may serve as a suitable level 1 procedure for training surgeons and surgical teams in robotic systems and workflows [13]. This could potentially help shorten the learning curve for more advanced procedures, following a pattern similar to laparoscopic training, where procedures like appendectomy and cholecystectomy are used as foundational steps before progressing to advanced laparoscopic procedures [14]. Therefore, in order to progress the use of robotics, adequate assessment of RC versus LC is important.

Objectives {7}

The primary objective of this study is to determine whether robotic multiport cholecystectomy is non-inferior to conventional laparoscopic cholecystectomy in terms of morbidity and complications occurring within 30 days postoperatively. Secondary objectives are to compare both procedures with respect to perioperative outcomes and patient recovery, patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) such as health-related quality of life and patient satisfaction, as well as healthcare utilization and costs.

Trial design {8}

This single-center, multi-surgeon, randomized controlled, parallel, non-inferiority trial compares RC to LC in adult patients with benign gallbladder disease who have an indication for elective cholecystectomy.

Methods: participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

The STaRLING trial is designed as a single-center study. Patients are enrolled at the Queen Alexandra (QA) Hospital in Portsmouth, UK, which currently holds one of the largest series of both benign and malignant robotic upper gastrointestinal cases in the UK. It is also one of the few UK hospitals offering routine RC, with extensive experience in advanced robotic procedures including bile duct exploration, esophagectomy, and complex hiatal hernia surgery.

To minimize surgeon-related bias, all participating surgeons must have substantial experience with both LC and RC. Specifically, inclusion requires each surgeon to have performed a minimum of 200 LC and at least 32 RC procedures, ensuring they are beyond the learning curve [15, 16]. For quality assurance, anonymized, unedited videos of five RC procedures per participating surgeon will be reviewed by Mr. G.I. van Boxel and Mr. N.C. Carter as most senior robotic surgeons and proctors.

Interventions

Explanation for the choice of comparators {6b}

When adopting a novel technique, it should be as safe as the current gold standard. Therefore, RC is compared to, and should be non-inferior to, LC, which is the established gold standard for treating benign gallbladder disease.

Intervention description {11a}

Preoperative work-up

Preoperative work-up is the same for all patients, consisting of assessment of history and complaints, laboratory assessment of inflammation markers (white blood cell count, C-reactive protein) and liver function tests (total bilirubin, albumin, alkaline, phosphatase, alanine transaminase), abdominal ultrasound with assessment of gallbladder and bile ducts. If there is doubt about stones in the bile ducts, and additional MRCP may be performed. All patients are reviewed by the preoperative assessment team to assess them for fitness for surgery. Patients with a BMI over 35 kg/m2 will have a 2-week liver shrinkage diet prescribed preoperatively, as is standard protocol in the QA Hospital.

Anesthesia

Patients will have a general anesthetic. Anesthesia is the same for patients undergoing RC or LC, and the same anesthetics will be involved in both RC and LC cases. Patients will receive standard pre-medication as per local protocol in the QA Hospital, consisting of paracetamol 1000 mg tablets, ibuprofen 400 mg tablet and omeprazole 20 mg tablet. Patients will receive perioperative dexamethasone. Antibiotic prophylaxis will not be administered routinely, as per the findings of a recent meta-analysis these are not routinely indicated in patients operated electively for benign gallbladder disease [17]. Based on peri-operative findings, such as ongoing infection and bile leak, perioperative antibiotics may be administered. Use of antibiotics will be documented and monitored in this trial.

Surgery

The patient is placed in supine position with reverse Trendelenburg position and the procedure will be performed as described below. During the procedure local anesthetics can be administered, a total of 60 ml of bupivacaine 0.25% will be available. This can be used at the discretion of the surgeon and can be applied locally to the port sites and/or as a transverse abdominis plane (TAP) block. Data will be collected on use of local anesthetics.

Conventional laparoscopic cholecystectomy

Intra-abdominal entry is achieved by means of an open cut-down at the umbilicus and placement of 12 mm trocar. Where there is a contra-indication/difficulty of entry, the surgeon may opt to use a Veress needle or direct optical entry, mode of entry will be documented in the operation note. A further 12 mm trocar in the epigastric area, and 2 × 5 mm trocar in the right flank. End of the procedure

The gallbladder is removed. The abdominal wall fascia is to be closed at the site of retrieval of the specimen. The skin will be closed with an intracutaneous absorbable suture. Either plasters or wound glue may be applied as wound protection at the end of the procedure. All removed gallbladders will be sent for histopathological assessment as per local protocol.

Postoperative management

Patients in both investigated groups will receive the same postoperative treatment and recommendations, unless a deviation from this is warranted on clinical grounds (in which case this will be documented). Postoperative patients will be monitored on our recovery ward, and information on administered pain medication will be collected. All patients will go home with the same pain medication, consisting of co-codamol 30 mg/500 mg tablets, tramadol 50 mg tablets and prochlorperazine 3 mg buccal tablets. Alterations can be made based on patient requirements, such as allergies and comorbidities.

Participants can withdraw from the study at any time for any reason if they wish to do so without any consequences. The investigator can decide to withdraw a participant from the study for urgent medical reasons. Follow-up in terms of primary outcome of these participants will be performed as usual. Included patients will be analyzed according to the intention to treat.

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Not applicable, as patients undergo either RC or LC and do not need to take any further action. The study team will proactively contact patients for follow-up on postoperative day 7 and 30.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

Patients are allowed to use their own prescription drugs, unless the anesthetist advices differently. The majority of the patients included in the study will be day-case procedures – the rate of elective day-case cholecystectomy in the QA Hospital in 80% over the past 2 years. If patients are admitted following cholecystectomy (i.e., not day case), these patients will receive antithrombotic prophylaxis according to local protocol, consisting of enoxaparin 4000 IU (40 mg) subcutaneously, once daily for the duration of the admission. This dosage may be adapted according to the patient’s needs (i.e., patient weight, renal protection). Perioperative antibiotics are given at discretion of the surgeon and use of antibiotics will be recorded in the trial.

Outcomes {12}

The primary outcome is the comparison in total complications within 30 days between patients with benign gallbladder disease that are treated with RC and LC, considering al complications as classified by the Clavien-Dindo classification [20]. Data on complications will be collected to enable comparisons of complication frequency, the severity of individual complications, and, in cases of multiple complications, the comprehensive complication index score for each patient [21].

Other outcomes of interest are intraoperative outcomes, perioperative recovery, morbidity, patient satisfaction and cost-effectiveness. Intraoperative outcomes include the duration of the procedure (time between arrival and exit of the operating theater, and time from knife to skin to closure of the skin), perioperative blood loss, perforation of the gallbladder (i.e., bile spillage), intraoperative complications, difficulty of the procedure (grade 1–4 [22]), assessment of liver parenchyma, assessment (and if applicable, treatment) of CBD stones, performance of cholangiogram, placement of drain(s), analgesia used in theater, use of perioperative antibiotics, and the Surgical Task Load Index (SURG TLX) score. Data on perioperative outcomes will be compared as a marker for postoperative recovery, involving analgesia needed in recovery, opioids prescribed upon discharge, frequency of same day discharge, and time from departing theater to discharge. When assessing overall morbidity, not only complications should be taken into account, but also added morbidity because of necessary additional procedures and interventions, and possible complications from these added procedures. RC and LC will be compared for readmission rates, needed interventions after cholecystectomy and overall health care utilization. Patient’s wishes, satisfaction and quality of life should be taken into account, as this will help in deciding which procedure is preferred from a patient perspective. The patient journey may provide important insights for the role of RC in clinical settings. The following patient-reported outcome measures (questionnaires) will be used: EuroQol 5D-5L (EQ-5D), Quality of Recovery 15 (QoR-15), preoperative assessment of Otago Gallstones condition-specific questionnaire (O-CSQ). Lastly, to assess the impact of implementing RC on hospital costs, case data will be collected and compared between the RC and LC groups. Operative costs, admission costs, and additional treatment costs (i.e., total healthcare utilization) will be evaluated from the index operation up to 30 days postoperatively.

Sample size {14}

Postoperative complications within 30 days were used as the primary outcome measure for the sample size calculation, based on demonstrating non-inferiority of RC compared to LC, using a frequentist approach. The systematic review comparing RC and LC showed no statistically significant differences between RC and LC, with a weighted odds ratio of 1.21 (95% CI = −0.80–1.84), with P = 0.86. Complications were reported in 42 out of 526 patients who underwent RC (7.9%) and in 62 out of 790 patients who underwent LC (7.8%) [10]. If there is truly no difference between RC (7.9%) and LC (7.8%), then 250 patients in total are required to be 90% sure that the upper limit of a one-sided 95% confidence interval (or equivalently a 90% two-sided confidence interval) will exclude a difference in favor of the standard group of more than 10%. Taking into account a 10% loss to follow-up, a total of 276 patients will be included [23].

Recruitment {15}

Patients will be recruited at the outpatient clinic of the department of upper gastrointestinal surgery or at the surgical ambulatory unit of the QA Hospital, Portsmouth, UK.

Assignment of interventions: allocation

Sequence generation {16a}

Patients will be randomized by the research team using the built-in module within the project’s REDCap electronic case report form (eCRF) database, which is managed by Portsmouth Hospitals University NHS Trust. A 1:1 allocation ratio will be applied using block randomization with a fixed block size of 10, along with stratification for intraoperative imaging (yes/no), to ensure balanced group sized throughout the enrolment period.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

Allocation is not concealed and will be revealed to participant and researcher upon randomization.

Implementation {16c}

To allow for adequate preparation of the surgical teams and materials, patients will be randomized by the research team following their verbal agreement to participate. The patient can then be scheduled for surgery on a robotic list or conventional laparoscopy list according to their allocation.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {17a}

Patients, researchers, and surgeons will not be blinded.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {17b}

The trial is open label, therefore there is no unblinding procedure.

Data collection and management

Plans for assessment and collection of outcomes {18a}

All data will be collected on a specialized, online, secure database (REDCap). In this trial, relevant data will be collected from hospital records (e.g., baseline characteristics, comorbidities, and preoperative assessments), laboratory and radiology systems (for blood results and imaging), and operation notes. A standardized surgical checklist will be implemented to ensure complete and consistent intraoperative data collection by participating surgeons. Postoperatively, patients will be monitored in the recovery ward, and data on administered pain medication will be collected. Any required hospital admission following surgery will be documented. If discharge is delayed despite meeting all clinical discharge criteria, this will be noted in the eCRF. To monitor for postoperative complications, all patients will be contacted by phone on postoperative days 7 and 30. During these follow-ups, data will be collected on complications, patient recovery, quality of life, and return to work or normal daily activities. PROMs, including the EQ-5D and O-CSQ, will be collected preoperatively; EQ-5D and QOR at discharge and day 7; and EQ-5D, QOR, and O-CSQ at day 30. A detailed timeline of data collections points is provided in Table 1.

Plans to promote participant retention and complete follow-up {18b}

During recruitment, patients will receive detailed information about the study design and requirements, with an emphasis on the importance of completing follow-up. Participation is voluntary, and patients may withdraw at any time without providing a reason; withdrawn participant will not be included in follow-up assessments. All enrolled patients will be contacted by phone on postoperative days 7 and 30 to collect data on complications, completion of PRIOM questionnaires and return to work or daily activities. Prior consultation with patients in the outpatient surgical clinical indicated a preference for follow-up via telephone.

Data management {19}

All data will be handled within the conditions of the General Data Protection Regulations (GDPR). Required data will be entered and stored on a specialized, online, secure database (REDCap), administered by Portsmouth Technologies trials Unit (PTTU) and hosted by AIMES, and ISO 2700 Information Security Management System. The REDCap system is fully validated in accordance with industry and regulatory standards and incorporates controlled access security. Data is backed up on-site nightly with a 7-day retention period. Regular data quality control checks, reporting and statistics will be performed using tools built into the REDCap platform. Access to the database will only be provided to members of the study team with approval of the Chief Investigator (CI) of the study. The CI and the trial coordinator will have access to all research data collected in this study in order to perform the data analyses required.

Confidentiality {27}

The study will adhere to the GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018, which require that personal data be de-identified as soon as practically possible. To achieve this, a randomly generated subject identification code, created by REDCap, will be used to link study data to individual participants. The key linking the code to personal identifiers will be securely maintained by the study coordinators. All handling of personal data will be in full compliance with GDPR, and study staff will ensure the confidentiality and protection of participants’ personal information.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {20a}

Data will be analyzed using IBM SPSS. The primary outcome, complications within 30 days post-surgery, will be compared between groups using chi-square or McNemar tests, as appropriate. Differences will be considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. Multivariate analysis will be conducted using logistic regression.

Secondary outcomes will be assessed as follows: normally distributed continuous data will be presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using Student’s t-test; non-normally distributed data as median and interquartile range (IQR) and analyzed using Mann–Whitney U test. Linear or logistic regression models will adjust for confounders and effect modifiers. Categorical variables will be expressed as percentages and compared using Chi-square or McNemar tests, as applicable.

Interim analyses {21b}

There are no interim analyses planned.

Methods for additional analyses (e.g., subgroup analyses) {20b}

There are no subgroup analyses planned.

Methods in analysis to handle protocol non-adherence and any statistical methods to handle missing data {20c}

The primary outcome will be assessed using an intention-to-treat analysis. Missing data will be reduced to a minimum by using the appropriate measures described above.

The intensity of verification is in relation to the risk associated with the intervention investigated. Data that will be checked by all means are the informed consent form, in- and exclusion criteria and the primary outcome. The monitor will also verify if all (serious) adverse events ((S)AE) are reported adequately and within the time determined by the sponsors standard operating procedures, and regulations.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

Adverse events (AE) will be captured from the time of a participant’s enrolment into the study until the end of their participation. All AEs will be recorded in the participant’s medical notes and reported in the eCRF. Information that will be provided as a record include description, start time, end time, duration, severity, treatment and outcome. Cholecystectomies can lead up to certain postoperative complications, such as wound infections, bile duct stone requiring postoperative ERCP, fluid collection requiring drainage, bile leak requiring treatment, pneumonia, and pain management requiring prolonged admission or readmission. Theses complications will not be separately mentioned as AEs as these are complications that are generally known complications of a cholecystectomy and therefore exempt from safety reporting. All patients undergoing gallbladder surgery in the QA Hospital are informed on the above risks and possible complications prior to giving informed consent for the procedure. A serious adverse event (SAE) must be reported to the Research Ethics Committee (REC) if, in the CI’s opinion, it is both related to the study procedures and unexpected. Such events should be reported within 15 working days of awareness using the sponsor’s SAE form.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

A comprehensive audit trail will be maintained throughout the study detailing data changes, reasons for changes, dates that the changes were made, and who made the changes. This audit trail will be exported and stored in the trial master file (TMF) at study close. For data points that are incomplete or data that violates the validation rules, a query will be generated, which can then be assigned to a user to resolve. The database cannot be locked while queries remain open. Reports for missing data will be scheduled monthly.

Plans for communicating important protocol amendments to relevant parties (e.g., trial participants, ethical committees) {25}

A substantial amendment is defined as an amendment to the terms of the ethical review application or the protocol or any other supporting documentation, that is likely to affect to a significant degree the safety or physical or mental integrity of participants of the trial, scientific value of the trial, conduct or management of the trial, quality or safety of any intervention used in the trial. All substantial amendments will be notified to the REC and Health Research Authority (HRA). Non-substantial amendments will not be notified and actioned accordingly via the Research Office.

Dissemination plans {31a}

The study results will be published in a peer-reviewed journal and shared with key stakeholders to promote awareness and adoption. Dissemination efforts will target hospital colleagues through presentation of results at local clinical governance meetings, healthcare professionals elsewhere through publication and conference presentations, and the public (especially patients) via newsletters, updated information leaflets, and engagement with the Patient Public Involvement (PPI) team to explore further outreach strategies.

Discussion

Robotic surgery was initially introduced for technically demanding procedures due to its enhanced precision to laparoscopy, but its use has increasingly expanded to include less complex interventions, such as cholecystectomy [6–8]. As the adoption of RC grows, it is crucial to determine whether RC is safe and at least non-inferior to the gold standard (LC) in terms of short-term clinical outcomes before broader implementation. To our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial specifically designed to compare RC and LC in the treatment of benign gallbladder disease. The findings may provide the evidence needed to support broader adoption of RC in clinical practice and help guide future guideline development and robotic training pathways.

A major strength of this trial is its randomized design, which minimizes bias and enhances the validity of the findings. Additionally, the inclusion of PROMs at multiple time points allows for a comprehensive assessment of quality of life and supports the planned cost-effectiveness analysis. Although the follow-up period is relatively short at 30 days, this timeframe is well-defined and clinically meaningful. It is expected to minimize missing data while still capturing key outcomes, including occurrence of complications and return to work or normal daily activities.

Several limitations of this trial should be acknowledged. First, as this is a single-center study, there is a potential risk of center-specific bias related to surgical expertise, patient population, or institutional protocols, which may affect the external validity of the findings. However, this limitation is partially mitigated by involving multiple surgeons with substantial experience in both procedures. Second, the broad inclusion criteria may result in a more heterogeneous study population, but this also improves the external validity and generalizability of the findings to clinical practice in (other) Western healthcare settings. Third, one could argue that the sample size calculation should have been based on bile duct injury, given its clinical relevance and associated morbidity. However, the incidence of bile duct injury is very low, and considerable heterogeneity exists in its definition across the literature, making it an impractical primary endpoint for powering this trial [10].
