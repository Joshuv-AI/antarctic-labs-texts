2025 Update of the Taiwan Expert Consensus on Hormone Receptor-Positive Metastatic Breast Cancer Management
Authors: Jiun-I Lai, Chih-Chiang Hung, Chin-Yao Lin, Chung-Liang Li, Wei-Pang Chung, Ching-Hung Lin, Cheng-Ping Yu, Ming-Yang Wang, Sung-Chao Chu, Chen-Teng Wu, Kuo-Ting Lee, Shyr-Ming Sheen-Chen, Jyh-Cherng Yu, Zhi-Jie Hong, Chao-Ming Hung, Chin-Sheng Hung, King-Jen Chang, Chen-Hsiang Chang, Yuan-Ching Chang, Hsien-Kun Chang, Dwan-Ying Chang, Yao-Jen Chang, Wen-Hung Kuo, Wen-Ling Kuo, Shou-Tung Chen, I-Chun Chen, Fang-Ming Chen, Shin-Cheh Chen, Wei-Wu Chen, Dar-Ren Chen, Ling-Ming Tseng, Yen-Dun Tzeng, Chi-Cheng Huang, Chiun-Sheng Huang, Hsien-Tang Yeh, Guo-Shiou Liao, Ta-Chung Chao, Tsu-Yi Chao, Liang-Chih Liu, Chun-Yu Liu

ABSTRACT
Hormone receptor-positive metastatic breast cancer remains a major clinical burden and is characterized by sequential endocrine resistance over the disease course. In 2023, the Taiwan Breast Cancer Society (TBCS) published an expert consensus to support real-world decision-making in Taiwan. Subsequently, rapid advances in biomarker-informed strategies (e.g., estrogen receptor 1 and PI3K/AKT pathway alterations), endocrine partners, estrogen receptor (ER)-targeting agents (including oral selective ER degraders and emerging protein degraders), and the expanding role of antibody-drug conjugates based on human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 expression categories have materially increased the complexity of treatment selection and sequencing. Therefore, TBCS convened an expert panel to update the previous consensus while preserving its foundational framework and eight-chapter structure: risk evaluation and biomarker testing (I/II), first-line treatment selection (I/II), second-line treatment selection (I/II), and treatment options other than endocrine-based approaches (I/II). A multidisciplinary panel conducted a systematic literature review and iterative discussions using a modified Delphi process to identify the key clinical questions, update and refine statements where new evidence was practice-changing, and maintain continuity with the 2023 Consensus. The panel formulated updated recommendations, assigned levels of evidence and strengths of recommendations, and the key updates were integrated into practical treatment algorithms reflecting Taiwan’s practice considerations. This updated consensus provides a continuity-based, yet evidence-responsive framework to facilitate the integration of emerging therapies into clinical practice in Taiwan and the Asia-Pacific region.

INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer remains one of the most commonly diagnosed malignancies in women, and hormone receptor-positive (HR+) disease represents a major proportion of patients encountered in routine practice [1]. Metastatic breast cancer (MBC) remains therapeutically challenging because it is generally incurable, and resistance ultimately emerges over the course [1]. Over the past decade, the management of HR+ MBC has shifted from sequential single-agent endocrine therapy to combination approaches that pair endocrine backbones with targeted agents [234].

In 2023, the Taiwan Breast Cancer Society (TBCS) convened a multidisciplinary expert panel to develop a consensus framework intended to be practical for real-world care in Taiwan [5]. The present document is a focused update built on the 2023 consensus framework, retaining the original architecture, terminology, and clinical decision logic. It revises only those components for which the evidence base, therapeutic availability, or implementation considerations have meaningfully changed since the previous consensus. To maintain continuity and usability, the updated recommendations were organized within the same eight-chapter structure: risk evaluation and biomarker testing (I/II), first-line treatment selection (I/II), second-line treatment selection (I/II), and treatment options other than endocrine-based approaches (I/II).

The rationale for updating the 2023 consensus was two-fold. First, the therapeutic landscape has expanded quickly, creating more plausible options at each decision point with multiple effective endocrine backbones and cyclin-dependent kinase 4 and 6 (CDK4/6) inhibitor strategies [24] and increasing the risk of inconsistent sequencing across institutions [6]. In particular, biomarker-driven escalation strategies have become more actionable in routine workflows, including the earlier and more systematic use of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) in selected settings [1], whereas new endocrine partners [78] and estrogen receptor (ER)-targeting agents have further diversified endocrine-based approaches [910]. Second, the non-endocrine armamentarium has evolved, with additional targeted options beyond endocrine therapy shaping later-line choices [1112] and with antibody-drug conjugate (ADC) eligibility and positioning increasingly guided by human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression categories rather than a single binary label [1314]. As Trop-2–directed and other ADCs move into routine practice [1516], there is growing attention on safety trade-offs and practical sequencing in heavily pretreated patients [1718]. Together, these developments warranted a structured update that kept the 2023 consensus “core” intact while explicitly documenting where and why recommendations were revised [1].

Accordingly, this 2025 update aims to: (1) preserve the consensus backbone that clinicians are already using, (2) integrate practice-changing evidence in a disciplined, transparent manner, and (3) provide a Taiwan-adapted, implementable approach to testing, treatment selection, and sequencing across the full treatment journey for HR+ MBC.

METHODS AND MATERIALS

TBCS assembled a multidisciplinary expert panel comprising medical and surgical oncologists with extensive clinical and research experience in breast cancer management. The panel members were selected based on their expertise in breast cancer therapeutics, familiarity with emerging treatment paradigms, and representation of diverse healthcare settings across Taiwan.

Literature review and evidence assessment

As the foundational step for this update, the panel performed a comprehensive review of the full eight-chapter 2023 TBCS consensus framework [5], including the original statements, supporting rationale, and associated treatment algorithms. Using this structured review, the panel identified chapters and statements requiring updates based on: (1) new practice-changing clinical evidence, (2) evolving biomarker testing strategies and real-world feasibility, and (3) changes in the treatment landscape that could alter sequencing and positioning in routine practice. Chapters and statements without substantive evidence changes were retained to preserve continuity in the 2023 consensus.

Subsequently, the panel subgroups conducted structured reviews of the published literature using PubMed, Google Scholar, and major oncology society databases. The search terms included combinations of “hormone receptor-positive breast cancer,” “estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer,” “metastatic breast cancer,” “CDK4/6 inhibitors,” “PI3K inhibitors,” “AKT inhibitors,” “SERD,” “estrogen receptor degrader,” “protein degrader,” “antibody-drug conjugates,” “HER2-low,” and specific drug names. Evidence was gathered from clinical trial publications, conference abstracts, existing guidelines (e.g., the European Society for Medical Oncology [ESMO]), and relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Consensus development process

Following a comprehensive review of the original eight-chapter 2023 consensus, the expert panel identified the priority areas requiring updates. These selected areas included: (1) risk evaluation and biomarker testing (part II), (2) selection of first-line treatment (part I), (3) selection of second-line treatment (parts I and II), and (4) treatment options beyond endocrine-based approaches (part I) [5]. Based on these topics, a treatment algorithm was developed to support clinical decision-making. Building on the 2023 consensus framework, draft updates were developed specifically for the identified statements requiring revision, followed by iterative discussions to ensure that the new recommendations remained internally consistent across chapters and aligned with Taiwan’s practice considerations. The panel used a modified Delphi process comprising two voting rounds. The first round included 10 experts, and the second round included 17 experts. During the first voting round, the panelists provided detailed opinions and specific recommendations for revision, and the draft statements were edited accordingly. A second voting round was subsequently conducted to incorporate further feedback and refinement, culminating in a final set of updated consensus statements.

Evidence grading system

The quality of evidence supporting each statement was graded using the criteria adapted from the US Preventive Services Task Force and the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation approach [19]: Level I (evidence from at least one well-conducted randomized controlled trial (RCT) or a meta-analysis of RCTs), Level II (evidence from well-designed non-randomized studies, including cohort or case-control studies, or from large case series), and Level III (evidence based on expert opinion, case reports, or descriptive studies). The strength of recommendation was categorized as follows: A (strong recommendation based on high-quality evidence), B (moderate recommendation), C (weak recommendation based on limited evidence), or D (recommendation against use).

Updated consensus statements

Only the sections that underwent substantive revision in the 2025 update are restated below; the unchanged components of the 2023 consensus framework were retained without full restatement.

Section 1: Risk evaluation and biomarker testing (II)

The risk evaluation of HR+ MBC increasingly depends on timely biomarker testing to match patients to endocrine, targeted, and ADC strategies [1]. Given the clonal evolution under endocrine pressure, reassessment at progression, often using minimally invasive approaches such as liquid biopsy, can refine sequencing decisions across later lines of therapy [1]. This section outlines a pragmatic approach to testing, emphasizing that the phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PIK3CA) status may be determined using tumor tissue- or plasma-based assays [12].

Somatic alterations in the PIK3CA/AKT serine/threonine kinase 1 (AKT1)/phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) pathway can guide the selection of pathway-targeted therapies: PI3K-directed strategies for PIK3CA-mutant diseases [12] and AKT inhibition for tumors with relevant pathway alterations [20]. Estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1) mutations represent an important mechanism of acquired endocrine resistance and inform the use of ER-targeting strategies [9]. Patients with pathogenic germline breast cancer susceptibility gene 1/2 (BRCA1/2) variants are more likely to benefit from poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors [1121].

For ADC, a HER2-low status (immunohistochemistry (IHC) 1+ or IHC 2+ with negative in situ hybridization [ISH]) predicts the benefits from trastuzumab deruxtecan [13], and emerging data also support its activity in selected HER2-ultralow tumors (IHC 0 with membrane staining) [22]. In contrast, trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP2) expression testing is not required to select TROP2-directed ADCs, such as sacituzumab govitecan [1517] or datopotamab deruxtecan [16].

Beyond baseline selection, longitudinal ctDNA monitoring for emerging ESR1 mutations can provide prognostic and predictive information and support switching from an aromatase inhibitor (AI) to an oral selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD, e.g., camizestrant) while continuing first-line CDK4/6 inhibition in selected patients before overt clinical progression [23]. When feasible and clinically indicated, a metastatic site biopsy may also be considered to clarify the tumor biology and guide subsequent treatment options (Table 1) [191316222324252627].

Section 2: Selection of first-line treatment (I)

This section explores the selection of the first-line treatment. The first-line management of HR+ MBC aims for sustained control while delaying chemotherapy, with endocrine therapy as the backbone [1]. For most patients, combining endocrine therapy with a CDK4/6 inhibitor is supported by randomized evidence [234], including patients with clinically aggressive disease [28]. In premenopausal patients, the suppression of ovarian function is recommended when endocrine-based regimens are used [29].

Ribociclib has demonstrated overall survival benefits in phase III trials [729], and abemaciclib has also shown overall survival benefits in combination with fulvestrant [30]. Selection of the endocrine partner should consider prior adjuvant exposure: for patients without relapse on an AI, or with recurrence ≥ 12 months after stopping adjuvant AI, a CDK4/6 inhibitor plus an AI is an appropriate first-line option [234]. An earlier relapse on or shortly after AI makes a SERD-based partner (e.g., fulvestrant) [18] a rational choice to avoid cross-resistance and may support the consideration of next-generation ER-targeting options in selected settings (e.g., imlunestrant) [10].

For relapse on adjuvant endocrine therapy or within 12 months of completing adjuvant treatment, biomarker-directed intensification is appropriate; for tumors with a PIK3CA mutation, inavolisib plus palbociclib plus fulvestrant is supported by phase III evidence [12]. Emerging data also support adapting therapy before overt progression: after ≥ 6 months of AI plus CDK4/6 inhibition, detection of an ESR1 mutation by liquid biopsy may justify substituting the AI with camizestrant while continuing CDK4/6 inhibition in selected patients [23].

In selected patients with substantial comorbidities or poor performance status, endocrine therapy with CDK4/6 initiation as a second-line regimen may be acceptable [1]. There is limited direct evidence to guide CDK4/6 inhibitor re-introduction, specifically after relapse following adjuvant CDK4/6 inhibitor therapy; however, rechallenge strategies have been explored in the metastatic setting and may be considered on an individualized basis (Table 2) [23468101223242829313233343536].

Section 3: Selection of second-line treatment (I)

Second-line treatment for HR+ MBC aims to preserve endocrine sensitivity, delay chemotherapy when appropriate, and tailor therapy to the resistance mechanisms that emerge after first-line endocrine therapy with or without CDK4/6 inhibition [1]. In routine practice, the key determinants include prior endocrine exposure and tolerability, tempo of progression, duration of benefit from CDK4/6 inhibition, and actionable genomic alterations [1].

Evidence-based options typically retain an endocrine backbone, most commonly a fulvestrant or an AI, and add a targeted agent to address pathway-driven resistance. For patients who have progressed on prior endocrine therapy without prior CDK4/6 inhibition, fulvestrant plus a CDK4/6 inhibitor is supported by phase III evidence [30]. After progression on a CDK4/6-based regimen, selected patients may be considered for CDK4/6 continuation/rechallenge strategies with a switch in the endocrine partner based on metastatic rechallenge data [3132].

In tumors with PIK3CA mutations, alpelisib plus fulvestrant provides a biomarker-directed strategy after endocrine progression [37], including populations with prior CDK4/6 exposure [38]. For broader endocrine resistance without targetable alteration (or when PI3K/AKT-directed therapy is not suitable), everolimus-based combinations remain an established option [394041]. Finally, for tumors harboring PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN alterations, capivasertib plus fulvestrant offers another targeted approach after at least one metastatic endocrine line, or for recurrence within 12 months of completing adjuvant endocrine therapy (Table 3) [7202530313237383940414243444546].

Section 4: Selection of second-line treatment (II)

This section focuses on novel endocrine-based strategies for estrogen receptor-positive (ER+)/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2−) advanced or MBC, with particular attention to tumors harboring ESR1 mutations as a clinically relevant mechanism of acquired endocrine resistance [9]. Across these statements, the overarching goal is to extend endocrine-based disease control after progression on prior endocrine therapy, while avoiding a premature transition to chemotherapy when an endocrine option remains appropriate.

For patients with ESR1-mutated disease who progress after at least one line of endocrine therapy, oral SERDs have been emphasized as key options. Elacestrant provides a benchmark oral SERD strategy after endocrine progression [9], and next-generation agents such as camizestrant further support ER-targeting approaches in selected settings [23]. In addition, imlunestrant, either as monotherapy or in combination with abemaciclib, represents another ER-directed strategy under evaluation following endocrine therapy [10].

Beyond SERDs, alternative endocrine-based regimens are also considered, including tucidinostat plus exemestane for ER+/HER2− disease [47]. For patients previously treated with CDK4/6 inhibitors, endocrine combinations that incorporate pathway targeting may be relevant; the statements include giredestrant plus everolimus as a strategy [48]. Finally, this section introduces next-generation protein degradation approaches, highlighting proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) ER degraders (e.g., vepdegestrant) as an emerging endocrine option for ESR1-mutated diseases after endocrine progression (Table 4) [910264748].

Section 5: Treatment options other than endocrine-based approaches (I)

This section addresses the treatment options beyond endocrine-based approaches for patients with HR+ MBC who have progressed on endocrine therapy or are inappropriate candidates for further endocrine treatment [1]. These statements emphasize the importance of biomarker- and line-of-therapy-guided strategies.

For patients with deleterious germline BRCA1/2 mutations, PARP inhibitors represent important non-endocrine options, particularly when endocrine therapy is ineffective or unsuitable and chemotherapy has been administered in the early or metastatic setting [1121]. For patients without actionable BRCA mutations, ADCs are key later-line therapies, with agent selection guided by HER2 expression and prior treatment exposure [1417].

Trastuzumab deruxtecan is considered for treating HER2-low disease after substantial endocrine exposure [13], and emerging data support its activity in selected HER2-ultralow tumors [22]. In HER2− disease after endocrine failure, datopotamab deruxtecan may be used after at least one line of chemotherapy [16], while sacituzumab govitecan is generally positioned after endocrine therapy and multiple chemotherapy lines [1517]. Finally, abemaciclib monotherapy is a potential option after endocrine progression and previous chemotherapy in a metastatic setting, with tolerability (notably diarrhea) and prior treatment intensity guiding patient selection (Table 5) [11131415161718212249].

Treatment algorithm

Based on the consensus statements across the five sections, the panel integrated key recommendations into practical treatment algorithms for HR+ MBC. The first flowchart (Figure 1) emphasizes first-line stratification by endocrine sensitivity, distinguishing de novo or late relapse from early recurrence or progression on adjuvant endocrine therapy, and aligns the initial treatment selection accordingly. During radiographic progression, subsequent choices are organized around biomarker-guided escalation (e.g., ESR1 mutations supporting oral ER-targeting strategies; PIK3CA/AKT/PTEN pathway alterations supporting targeted combinations), while also incorporating clinically used options such as mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibition and selected CDK4/6 inhibitor continuation/rechallenge approaches, where appropriate.

DISCUSSION

This 2025 update was developed as an extension of the 2023 TBCS consensus and is intended to preserve the original framework while incorporating the practice-changing advances that have emerged since the previous publication [5]. The panel prioritized updates in areas where new evidence, evolving testing strategies, and expanding therapeutic options materially affected real-world decision-making and treatment sequencing in Taiwan. Developed through a structured, multidisciplinary voting process in Taiwan, the updated consensus provides practical, evidence-based recommendations that emphasize precise biomarker testing, covering the timing and approach of PIK3CA assessment to inform PI3K/AKT-pathway–directed strategies [112], ESR1 mutation detection using ctDNA to guide endocrine switching in endocrine-resistant settings [923], and germline BRCA1/2 testing to identify candidates for PARP inhibition [1121]. This update also clarifies the sequencing considerations for endocrine-sensitive diseases, including the positioning of CDK4/6 inhibitors across common first-line scenarios and key management principles that reduce the variability in real-world practice [2346]. Finally, it incorporates emerging targeted options that are increasingly relevant to clinical workflows, including inavolisib-based regimens for PIK3CA-mutated disease [1224], camizestrant for emerging ESR1-mutated disease [23], and trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd) for patients with HER2 expression categories, such as HER2-low and HER2-ultralow [131422].

Several key themes have emerged from this update. First, biomarker-guided treatment selection is central to the treatment journey [1]. The actionable alterations include PIK3CA pathway abnormalities that inform PI3K-targeted strategies [37] and newer PI3Kα approaches in selected settings [12]. Alterations in AKT1/PTEN and related pathway components support AKT inhibition [20]. ESR1 mutations are clinically relevant mechanisms of acquired endocrine resistance and inform ER-targeted approaches [9]. Patients with germline BRCA1/2 variants are more likely to benefit from PARP inhibitors [1121]. In addition, HER2 expression categories, including HER2-low and HER2-ultralow, provide predictive information for ADC selection [13] and eligibility considerations in the selected populations [14].

Second, the first-line treatment has become more nuanced. A CDK4/6 inhibitor plus endocrine therapy remains the foundational approach for most patients, as supported by pivotal phase III studies of palbociclib [2], ribociclib [3], and abemaciclib [4]. Overall survival benefits have been demonstrated with ribociclib-based programs [729] and abemaciclib-based therapies in endocrine-resistant settings [30]. In premenopausal patients, ovarian function suppression is integral to endocrine-based approaches [29]. The choice of an endocrine partner should reflect previous endocrine exposure and resistance biology, including ESR1 mutation status when relevant [23]. For patients with early endocrine resistance and PIK3CA-mutant disease, the triplet strategy of inavolisib, palbociclib, and fulvestrant represents a new intensification option, supported by phase III evidence [12]. Real-time detection of emergent ESR1 mutations, followed by substitution of an AI with camizestrant while continuing CDK4/6 inhibition, offers a proactive strategy before overt clinical progression in selected settings [23].

Third, second-line and endocrine-based options have broadened substantially. For patients who progressed on prior endocrine therapy without prior CDK4/6 inhibition, fulvestrant combined with CDK4/6 inhibition is supported by phase III evidence [30]. After progression on CDK4/6-based therapy, selected patients may be considered for CDK4/6 continuation or rechallenge strategies with a switch in endocrine partners, based on metastatic rechallenge data [3132]. For endocrine resistance, everolimus-based combinations remain established options [394041], supported by foundational evidence for mTOR inhibition with endocrine therapy [45]. The biomarker-directed strategies include alpelisib plus fulvestrant for PIK3CA-mutant disease [37] and data supporting its activity after prior CDK4/6 exposure [38]. Additional PI3Kα-directed approaches with inavolisib-based regimens have further expanded treatment options in selected patients [1224]. For tumors harboring PIK3CA/AKT1/PTEN alterations, capivasertib plus fulvestrant provides another targeted approach after metastatic endocrine therapy or for early recurrence following adjuvant endocrine therapy [20]. ER-directed options after endocrine progression include elacestrant [9] as well as next-generation agents such as imlunestrant (± abemaciclib) [10] and giredestrant-based strategies combined with pathway targeting [48]. The emerging endocrine approaches include PROTAC ER degradation with vepdegestrant [26] and endocrine combinations incorporating tucidinostat plus exemestane [47].

Fourth, ADCs play a clear role in patients who have exhausted endocrine-based approaches. Trastuzumab deruxtecan has demonstrated significant efficacy in HER2-low disease [13] and activity in selected HER2-ultralow tumors [22]. Additional later-line options include sacituzumab govitecan after appropriate prior therapy [1517] and datopotamab deruxtecan in later-line settings after endocrine failure and chemotherapy [16]. For patients with advanced disease after substantial prior therapy, additional evidence supports ADC activity in endocrine-pretreated populations [22]. However, the optimal sequencing of ADCs relative to each other and targeted endocrine strategies remains an area of active investigation [14].

In relation to contemporary international guidance, the present consensus is broadly concordant with National Comprehensive Cancer Network, American Society of Clinical Oncology, and ESMO recommendations as well as the Pan-Asian adapted ESMO guidance already used as a core reference in this update, in prioritizing endocrine therapy plus CDK4/6 inhibition for most patients with HR+/HER2− MBC, ovarian function suppression for premenopausal patients receiving endocrine-based therapy, biomarker-directed use of PI3K/AKT-pathway therapies, ER-targeting agents for ESR1-mutated disease, PARP inhibitors for germline BRCA1/2-mutated disease, and ADCs after exhaustion of endocrine-based options [151525354]. Therefore, the differences between this consensus and international guidelines are mainly related to positioning, timing, and implementation, rather than opposing therapeutic principles. First, the panel did not impose a rigid hierarchy among CDK4/6 inhibitors, despite noting differences in the overall survival data, because no direct randomized comparison has established a universally preferred agent, and treatment selection in Taiwan must also consider prior exposure, toxicity profile, comorbidities, availability, and patient preference [51]. Second, CDK4/6 inhibitor continuation or rechallenge after progression is framed as a selected, individualized strategy rather than a routine standard for all patients, reflecting emerging but limited evidence [313251]. Third, ctDNA-guided ESR1 monitoring and pre-radiographic switching from an AI to camizestrant while continuing CDK4/6 inhibition are included as emerging strategies for carefully selected patients; however, the panel recognizes that this approach remains dependent on testing feasibility, access, regulatory status, and maturation of long-term outcome data [235154]. Finally, the inclusion of regionally relevant or emerging endocrine-based options, including tucidinostat-containing therapy and next-generation ER-targeting or protein-degradation strategies, reflects the Asia-Pacific evidence, local availability considerations, and the rapidly changing treatment landscape, which should not be interpreted as replacing globally established standards where stronger or more mature evidence-based options are available [4751525354].

This study had several limitations. Regulatory approval, reimbursement status, and local availability may evolve after publication, and access to comprehensive biomarker testing varies across practice settings, which may affect the feasibility of biomarker-driven recommendations in resource-constrained environments. These factors may also explain the differences in the timing or prioritization between the Taiwan consensus and international guidelines, particularly for newly approved, regionally available, or still-emerging treatment strategies. In addition, long-term follow-up remains limited for some newer agents and prospective evidence guiding optimal sequencing across multiple post-CDK4/6 lines and ADCs is still emerging.

CONCLUSION

Overall, this update emphasizes a pragmatic, biomarker-informed approach to HR+ MBC care, integrating contemporary endocrine and targeted strategies and the expanding role of ADCs while maintaining a clear decision logic that can be implemented across diverse practice settings. As evidence, approval, and access continue to evolve, these recommendations should be applied in the context of individual patient factors and local feasibility, and periodic updates will remain necessary to sustain the alignment between emerging data and routine clinical care.
