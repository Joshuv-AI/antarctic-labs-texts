Periprocedural Assessment of Coronary Microcirculatory Function to Predict Clinical Outcomes After Elective Percutaneous Coronary Intervention
Authors: ChenGuang Li, You Zhou, Danbo Lu, Jiaqi Ma, Chunfeng Dai, Jianying Ma, Yumeng Hu, Jianyi Xu, Jianping Xiang, Zhangwei Chen, Juying Qian, Junbo Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) could restore epicardial blood flow through the stent‐based angioplasty. However, coronary microcirculatory function also affects clinical outcomes and can be quantified by the angiography‐derived index of microcirculatory resistance (angio‐IMR). The prognostic significance of periprocedural angio‐IMR in patients undergoing elective PCI remains unclear. This study aimed to assess angio‐IMR before and after PCI, as well as their association with long‐term prognosis.MethodsData from 1768 patients with stable coronary artery disease undergoing elective PCI were analyzed. The primary end point was major adverse cardiac events (MACE) comprising death and nonfatal myocardial infarction.ResultsThe median angio‐IMR was 11.3 (interquartile range, 7.7–15.0) and 17.0 (interquartile range, 14.1–20.4) before and after PCI (P<0.001), respectively. During a median follow‐up period of 20 months, MACE occurred in 53 patients (3.0%). Periprocedural angio‐IMR changes (Δangio‐IMR) exhibited superior predictive power for MACE compared with post‐PCI angio‐IMR alone. Specifically, Δangio‐IMR>10 was independently associated with an increased risk of MACE (adjusted hazard ratio, 2.43 [95% CI, 1.32–4.49]; P=0.004). In comparison with periprocedural myocardial infarction, Δangio‐IMR>10 could provide additional prognostic value in predicting MACE (area under the curve, 0.681 versus 0.635, P<0.05).ConclusionsΔangio‐IMR was a predictor of MACE in patients with stable coronary artery disease undergoing elective PCI. Incorporating Δangio‐IMR could enhance the identification of patients at high risk of MACE.Keywords: angiography‐derived index of microcirculatory resistance, coronary microvascular function, percutaneous coronary intervention, prognosisSubject Categories: Quality and Outcomes, Percutaneous Coronary Intervention, Prognosis

Over the past few decades, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has become a primary therapeutic modality for coronary artery disease (CAD). Multiple intravascular imaging and functional evaluation methods are currently available enabling precise assessment of stenosis and optimization of stent implantation. 1 However, a controversial issue persists regarding whether PCI could improve the prognosis of patients with stable CAD beyond symptomatic relief. 2 , 3 One possible explanation is that although PCI could alleviate obstruction within epicardial vessels, the coronary microvasculature (CMV) is inaccessible to conventional angioplasty procedures. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Nevertheless, data regarding the periprocedural status of CMV are limited.

Microvascular function could be assessed by the index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR) through an invasive guidewire‐based measurement. 8 It has been reported that IMR obtained following primary PCI was an independent risk factor of mortality in patients with ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). 9 In recent years, the coronary angiography–derived IMR (angio‐IMR) emerges as a noninvasive wireless method to conveniently assess microcirculatory function, which has been validated by several studies. 10 , 11 Notably, the post‐PCI angio‐IMR had a strong association with an elevated risk of adverse cardiovascular events in patients with non‐STEMI. 12

Yet, prior studies mainly focused on postprocedural evaluation of CMV. The dynamic changes of CMV, taking preprocedural CMV status into account, has not been comprehensively investigated. Moreover, evidence is lacking elucidating the prognostic implications of angio‐IMR in patients with stable CAD undergoing elective PCI. Therefore, we conducted this study aiming to assess angio‐IMR preceding and following PCI and explore their association with long‐term prognosis.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

Patients with stable CAD referred for elective PCI at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University between 2016 and 2019 were prospectively enrolled. Informed consent was obtained from each patient during their index hospitalization. Patients with elevated hs‐cTnT (high‐sensitivity cardiac troponin T) on admission were excluded. As for patients who underwent multiple PCI procedures during the study period, only data from their first procedure were retained for the final analysis. Patients with suitable angiographic views for angio‐IMR measurement were ultimately included in the present study for retrospective analysis (Figures 1 and 2). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (B2021‐726R) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

PCI Procedures

Coronary angiography and intervention were performed according to routine clinical practice. Coronary angiograms were obtained following intracoronary nitroglycerin injection (100–200 mg) from a standard series of 6 to 8 projections for the left coronary artery and 2 to 3 projections for the right coronary artery. All patients were on dual antiplatelet therapy at the time of PCI. Procedural anticoagulation was achieved by weight‐adjusted unfractionated heparin or bivalirudin with dose adjustment to maintain an activated clotting time of about 300 seconds. Decisions about stenting strategies, glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor adoption and vasodilatory therapy (such as calcium channel blockers and nitroprusside) were left to the operator's discretion. Procedural success of PCI (thrombolysis in MI flow grade 3 and <30% residual stenosis in the target vessel) was achieved in all patients. After the procedure, all patients received dual antiplatelet therapy for at least 12 months unless bleeding events occurred. Pre‐PCI and post‐PCI angiograms were analyzed by personnel blinded to the patients' clinical data (including their major adverse cardiac events [MACE] status), at the central core laboratory through a 3‐dimensional quantitative coronary angiography approach using a commercial software (AccuIMR, Version 1.0, Artery Flow Technology).

Clinical Outcomes and Follow‐Up

The primary end point of the study was MACE after successful PCI, defined as a composite of all‐cause mortality and nonfatal type I MI according to the fourth universal definition of MI. The secondary end points included unplanned revascularization and readmission for unstable angina. Follow‐up was conducted through outpatient clinic data or telephone interview.

Angio‐IMR Measurement

Angio‐IMR was measured in a blinded fashion by an independent core laboratory, using a commercial software (AccuIMR, Version 1.0, Artery Flow Technology) as previously described (Figure 3). 13 First, a 3‐dimensional model was built from the ostium to the distal segment of the target coronary artery, based on the anatomic information from 2 angiographic views with a minimum angular separation of 30°. Second, Pahyp (the mean aortic pressure at maximal hyperemia), ∆Phyp (the pressure drop along the reconstructed coronary artery), and Vhyp (the maximal hyperemic flow velocity) were estimated by the computational fluid dynamic method. Finally, angio‐IMR was calculated using the following equation:

Pdhyp is the mean arterial pressure at the distal segment during simulated maximal hyperemia. Pdhyp=Pahyp−∆Phyp. L represents the vessel length between the ostium and the distal segment of the target vessel. Of note, as preprocedural angio‐IMR was calculated in the presence of epicardial stenosis, the formula was adjusted as proposed by Yong et al. 14 The difference between post‐ and pre‐PCI angio‐IMR was calculated and presented as Δangio‐IMR.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as either the median and interquartile range (IQR) or the mean±SD, as appropriate. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and corresponding percentages. For the comparison of continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test or Student's t test was employed, as appropriate. Frequencies were compared with the chi‐square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Maximally selected log‐rank statistics and restricted cubic spline analysis were used to explore the optimal cutoff point. Kaplan–Meier curves and log‐rank test were carried out for survival analyses. Univariable and multivariable cox regression analyses were performed to identify the prognostic value of angio‐IMR on top of conventional predictors. Variables recognized to be correlated with clinical outcomes from prior literature were included, namely age, hypertension, diabetes, male sex, history of smoking, prior MI, history of PCI, stent length, Synergy Between Percutaneous Coronary Intervention With Taxus and Cardiac Surgery score, baseline diameter stenosis and angiography‐derived fractional flow reserve. The time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis were exploited to demonstrate the performance of angio‐IMR in predicting the primary end point. For intermodel comparisons, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was calculated. Statistical analysis was conducted with R software (version 4.2.2) and SPSS (version 25.0, IBM, Armonk, NY). A 2‐tailed P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The angio‐IMR was 11.3 (IQR, 7.7–15.0) before PCI and 17.0 (IQR, 14.1–20.4) following PCI (P<0.001). In comparison with pre‐PCI values, 89.1% of the patients had an increase in angio‐IMR after PCI. Preprocedural angio‐IMR was positively correlated with postprocedural angio‐IMR (Spearman r = 0.581, P<0.001). Their relationship with Δangio‐IMR is displayed in Figure 4.

Association of Angio‐IMR With Clinical End Points

The outcome data were documented during the follow‐up with a median period of 20 (IQR, 17–34) months. The primary end point occurred in 53 (3.0%) patients, including 18 deaths and 35 nonfatal MI. As for the secondary end points, 138 (7.8%) patients had unplanned revascularization, and 174 (9.8%) patients were readmitted for unstable angina.

The incidence of MACE was not necessarily high in patients with high post‐PCI angio‐IMR alone (Figure 4). The prognosis‐relevant threshold was 24.9 for post‐PCI angio‐IMR and 10.4 for Δangio‐IMR determined by maximally selected log‐rank statistics (Figure 5A and 5C). Restricted cubic spline curves showed a nonlinear relationship between the log‐transformed hazard ratio of MACE and angio‐IMR values (Figure 5B and 5D). Notably, Δangio‐IMR had a more profound influence on the risk of MACE because the 95% CI of hazard ratio (HR) was consistently >1 when Δangio‐IMR was over the threshold.

For clinical applicability, the cutoff values of post‐PCI angio‐IMR and Δangio‐IMR were set at 25 and 10, respectively. Patients were grouped accordingly, and their baseline characteristics are presented in Tables 1 and 2. There were 343 patients (19.4%) with Δangio‐IMR>10, and these patients had a higher prevalence of male sex, hypertension, and smoking history (all P<0.05). The diameter stenosis was higher in patients with Δangio‐IMR>10 (71.7±9.4 versus 67.5±8.9, P<0.001), whereas the residual stenosis was comparable between the groups (13.2±4.1 versus 13.1±4.0, P=0.066). The difference in baseline characteristics between patients with post‐PCI angio‐IMR>25 and those ≤25 was minor.

Patients with Δangio‐IMR>10 had a significantly increased incidence of MACE (5.5% versus 2.4%, log‐rank P=0.004) (Figures 2 and 6A), whereas patients with post‐PCI angio‐IMR>25 did not (4.8% versus 2.8%, log‐rank P=0.160) (Figure 6B). Considering the relatively small population size of patients with post‐PCI angio‐IMR>25, patients were further stratified based on the thresholds directly derived from maximally selected log‐rank statistics and the 75th percentile of angio‐IMR distribution (Figure S1). The results were consistent, that is, Δangio‐IMR was significantly associated with MACE, whereas post‐PCI angio‐IMR was not. Based on maximally selected log‐rank statistics determined cutoff values of pre‐ and post‐PCI angio‐IMR (9.2 and 24.9, respectively), patients were divided into 4 groups. The incidence of MACE was the highest among patients with lower pre‐PCI and higher post‐PCI angio‐IMR (Figure S1). However, the incidence of secondary end points was not significantly different between patients divided by either Δangio‐IMR or post‐PCI angio‐IMR (Table 3 and Figure S2). To be specific, the incidence of unplanned revascularization was not significantly elevated in patients with either Δangio‐IMR>10 (7.4% versus 9.3%, P=0.241) or post‐PCI angio‐IMR>25 (8.0% versus 6.1%, P=0.427). Besides, patients showed similar rates of readmission for unstable angina divided by either Δangio‐IMR (9.5% versus 11.4%, P=0.290) or post‐PCI angio‐IMR (10.0% versus 8.2%, P=0.476).

As shown in Table 4, Δangio‐IMR>10 was independently associated with an elevated risk of MACE in the multivariable regression model (adjusted HR [adHR], 2.43 [95% CI, 1.32–4.49]; P=0.004). Consistent results were observed when Δangio‐IMR was further divided into 5 strata (Figure S3). Subgroup analysis implied Δangio‐IMR>10 was an independent risk factor of MACE in female patients without diabetes or smoking history (Figure S4). In contrast, post‐PCI angio‐IMR>25 was not a significant predictor of MACE (adHR, 2.27 [95% CI, 0.98–5.28]; P=0.057). Periprocedural MI (PMI), defined as post‐PCI hs‐cTnT >5×upper reference limit, was regarded as a conventional risk factor for MACE. Patients with PMI had significantly higher Δangio‐IMR values [5.0 (IQR, 2.0–8.6) versus 6.5 (IQR, 2.6–10.0); P<0.001]. However, PMI was not an independent risk factor of MACE either (adHR, 1.20 [95% CI, 0.63–2.31]; P=0.583). Clinical Value of Angio‐IMR in Predicting MACE

In comparison with the conventional model including PMI, the new model incorporating categorical Δangio‐IMR (>10 or not) had an improved performance in predicting MACE. The area under the curve was increased from 0.635 to 0.681 (P<0.05), which confirmed the addition of Δangio‐IMR enhanced the discriminatory power (Figure 7A). This finding was further supported by time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves and area under the curve (Figure 7B, 7C, and 7D). On the calibration plot (Figure S6A), predicted probabilities of the new model were closer to the observed probabilities, implying superior predictive accuracy. Decision curve analysis confirmed that the new model with Δangio‐IMR had greater net benefits for predicting prognosis (Figure S6B).

DISCUSSION

Our study revealed the association between angio‐IMR and long‐term prognosis in patients with stable CAD undergoing elective PCI. The change in microvascular function, as represented by Δangio‐IMR, emerged as a more powerful predictor of MACE compared with post‐PCI angio‐IMR or PMI.

It is acknowledged that CMV plays a crucial role in myocardial perfusion, and its dysfunction proves to be an important cause of angina and myocardial ischemia. 15 , 16 , 17 Coronary microcirculatory dysfunction (CMD) has been demonstrated to be associated with a notably higher risk of adverse outcomes in patients with STEMI 9 , 10 , 18 , 19 and non‐STEMI. 12 , 20 As for stable patients who underwent elective PCI, the prognostic impact of CMD is unclear. It was reported that post‐PCI IMR>25 was an indicator of CMD, which was associated with increased risks of MACE. 21 , 22 However, post‐PCI angio‐IMR>25 was not a significant predictor of MACE in the present study. It must be acknowledged that our results were meant to be hypothesis‐generating rather than against previous findings. Although the prognostic influence of angio‐IMR>25 post PCI did not reach statistical significance, the positive correlation between angio‐IMR and risks of MACE was in accordance with former publications. Besides, this study chose hard end points (mortality and MI), and angio‐IMR was calculated based on angiograms through a wireless method. In this case, perhaps there is a different cutoff IMR value of prognostic significance, which needs to be investigated in future studies. In addition, this may be limited by the single‐centered, retrospective nature of this study, and the median follow‐up period of 20 months was relatively short. Patients with stenosis >90% or thrombolysis in MI flow grade 0 to 2 before PCI were excluded due to the limitation of the angio‐IMR calculating method. These patients represented a high‐risk population, which, as a result, may lead to the discordance between our study and the prior research.

As a less invasive approach, angio‐IMR makes it more convenient to assess the microvascular function. The absolute values of angio‐IMR in this study were comparable to prior studies including patients with stable CAD 23 , 24 and lower than those in patients with non‐STEMI 12 or STEMI. 10 This was in accordance with general expectations, which, in a way, verified the credibility of our results.

The baseline status of coronary microcirculation before PCI was seldom investigated. Preexisting microcirculatory dysfunction could increase the risk of PMI. 25 It has been proposed that IMR is independent of epicardial stenosis severity and the periprocedural changes in IMR could precisely represent the alteration of CMV condition. 14 , 26 , 27 Patients with Δangio‐IMR >10 could not only have higher post‐PCI angio‐IMR but also have lower pre‐PCI angio‐IMR. It was likely that measuring postprocedural angio‐IMR alone would overlook patients with lower pre‐PCI angio‐IMR values, even if their absolute changes in angio‐IMR,that is, damage in CMV, was substantial. This suggested that evaluating CMV only once may not be able to fully characterize the coronary microcirculatory function, which may subsequently undermine the prognostic significance of CMV.

Before the wide use of functional evaluating approaches, the status of CMV used to be assessed by PMI. In the absence of side branch occlusion or flow‐limiting dissections, microembolization from plaque debris could impair the coronary microcirculation and cause myocardial injury, which could be detected through elevated cardiac troponin. 28 PMI is usually defined as a post‐PCI hs‐cTnT values >5‐fold upper reference limit, 29 although the prognostically significant threshold is still debated. 30 , 31 It has been reported that the dynamic changes of IMR was higher in patients with PMI and post‐PCI IMR was an independent predictor of PMI. 32 In this study, patients with PMI had significantly higher Δangio‐IMR values and vice versa. Notably, Δangio‐IMR >10 was also a significant risk factor of PMI (odds ratio, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.18–1.99]; P=0.001). The positive correlation between Δangio‐IMR and elevated hs‐cTnT implied that CMD could possibly lead to myocardial injury and MACE. Our research further explored the prognostic implications of periprocedural angio‐IMR and revealed the superior MACE predicting power of Δangio‐IMR over PMI. Nevertheless, it should be acknowledged that the cutoff point of Δangio‐IMR we chose in this study was proposed out of clinical applicability. Large‐scale studies are necessary to assess the true implications of our findings more adequately.

Patients with Δangio‐IMR>10 were more commonly to be male and have hypertension or smoking history, which were conventional risk factors of MACE. After adjustment for baseline diameter stenosis and postprocedural angiography‐derived fractional flow reserve, Δangio‐IMR>10 was still a significant prognostic indicator. Our findings indicated that Δangio‐IMR could add incremental prognostic value after accounting for post‐PCI angiography‐derived fractional flow reserve, that is, microcirculatory dysfunction had independent predictive value beyond residual epicardial ischemia. CMD after PCI could bring endothelial cell injury and abnormal blood flow conditions, which, as a result, could possibly activate platelets and promote thrombosis. 33 , 34 Patients prone to CMD may also be at a prothrombotic state originally. 35 However, the pathological mechanisms underlying CMD and acute cardiovascular events should be further investigated in the future.

Limitations

There are some limitations that must be acknowledged. First, this study was a single‐center and exploratory study, and only patients with stable CAD were included, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Multicenter studies are needed to confirm our results and further validate the role of Δangio‐IMR in different patient populations. Second, the median follow‐up period of 20 months may be relatively short for assessing long‐term prognosis. Third, although our study demonstrated the association between Δangio‐IMR and MACE, it did not provide insights into the underlying mechanisms. Future research should focus on elucidating the pathophysiological mechanisms linking CMD to adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Fourth, intravascular imaging data depicting the target lesions were missing. Because the plaque characteristics are also associated with the risk of CMD and prognosis, research incorporating intravascular imaging techniques would provide further evidence for prognostic implications of CMD. Fifth, for the accuracy of angio‐IMR calculation, patients with stenosis >90% or thrombolysis in MI flow grade 0 to 2 before PCI were excluded. These patients were theoretically at higher risk of MACE among patients with stable CAD. The application of periprocedural evaluation of CMV in this population needs to be validated in future studies. Finally, although the predicting power was improved with the addition of Δangio‐IMR, the performance of multivariable models was moderate. More evidence is needed to build a more efficient model for risk stratification.

CONCLUSIONS

Δangio‐IMR is independently associated with MACE among patients with stable CAD undergoing elective PCI. Periprocedural assessment of coronary microcirculatory function could potentially improve risk stratification and thereby guide precision medicine.
