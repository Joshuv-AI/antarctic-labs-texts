Determining the Minimal Clinically Important Difference of the 40‐Item Smell Identification Test in People With Cystic Fibrosis

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) and olfactory dysfunction (OD) are highly prevalent among people with cystic fibrosis (PwCF) and negatively impact quality of life. The 40‐item Smell Identification Test (SIT) is widely used to assess psychophysical olfaction, but a CF‐specific minimal clinically important difference (MCID) has not been established. This study aimed to determine the SIT MCID in PwCF treated with elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) and/or endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS).MethodsData from three prospective, multi‐institutional observational studies were pooled. Participants were ≥12 years old with confirmed CF and CRS who completed SIT at baseline and ≥1 follow‐up (3, 6, 9, 12, or 24 months). Distribution‐based MCIDs were calculated using four methods: standard error of measurement (SEM), minimal detectable change (MDC = 1.96 × SEM), 0.5 × baseline standard deviation (SD), and 0.5 × SD of change scores (ΔSD).ResultsA total of 122 participants were enrolled (mean age 32.9 years, 54% female). Of these, 99 contributed follow‐up SIT scores (79 ETI, 20 ESS). SIT scores remained stable with ETI, with a small but statistically significant decline at 6 months (−1.4, p = 0.02). ESS was associated with mean gains of 3.1–4.5 points at early follow‐up, though these did not reach significance. Pooled distribution‐based MCID estimates ranged from 2 to 4 points, with an overall threshold of 3.1 (95% CI: 2.1–4.1).ConclusionsThis CF‐specific SIT threshold provides a clinically interpretable cut‐off for assessing olfaction. These findings establish a foundation for future work and highlight the importance of developing disease‐specific MCIDs to guide clinical care and research.

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) and olfactory dysfunction (OD) are prevalent among people with cystic fibrosis (PwCF) and negatively impact one's quality of life (QoL) [1, 2, 3]. OD in particular affects up to 90% of adults with CF and, in a general population, is often associated with reduced appetite, changes in eating behavior, social withdrawal, and psychosocial distress [4, 5]. As advances in CF care continue to improve survival and respiratory outcomes, optimizing sinonasal health, including olfaction, has become an increasingly important focus [1].

The 40‐item Smell Identification Test (SIT) is a validated and widely used tool for assessing odor identification [6]. It is commonly applied in both sinonasal research to track symptom burden and evaluate treatment response. Interpreting meaningful changes in SIT scores is enhanced by using a minimal clinically important difference (MCID), which is the smallest change in score perceived as meaningful by patients [7]. While a SIT MCID of approximately 4.0 has been suggested in non‐CF populations with CRS [8], a CF‐specific threshold has not been established.

Prior studies have shown that MCID thresholds for olfactory outcomes in PwCF can differ from those in the general population. The Questionnaire of Olfactory Disorders‐Negative Statements (QOD‐NS), a patient‐reported outcome capturing subjective olfactory‐related QoL, is one example [9]. The MCID of the QOD‐NS has previously been determined to be 5.2 in people without CF, and 3.7 in PwCF [9, 10]. Unlike the QOD‐NS that subjectively addresses olfactory function, the SIT is a psychophysical test of olfaction, which requires its own validated MCID to guide interpretation. Establishing a CF‐specific MCID for the SIT is critical to ensure that observed score changes in PwCF are interpreted optimally.

This gap is particularly relevant when evaluating treatment response to elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI), a CF transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) modulator therapy, and endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS), two commonly used interventions to manage sinonasal disease in PwCF. Multiple studies have shown that ETI can restore CFTR function and improve pulmonary outcomes and sinonasal symptoms [1, 11, 12, 13]; however, olfactory function remained largely unchanged following ETI initiation in adults and children [2, 12, 14]. In the general CRS population, ESS has been associated with significant improvements in olfactory outcomes, including SIT scores [15]. However, in PwCF, the evidence for similar olfactory benefit after ESS is limited and less clear. This study aimed to determine the SIT MCID in PwCF using distribution‐based methods, based on longitudinal data from patients treated with ETI and/or ESS with pooled data from three prospective studies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

This study gathered data from three prospective observational studies conducted across eight US institutions. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval and informed consent/assent were obtained for all protocols and study participants.

Eligible participants were individuals ≥12 years old with a confirmed diagnosis of CF and CRS who completed the SIT at baseline and at least one follow‐up visit (3, 6, 9, 12, or 24 months). The 40‐Item SIT (Sensonics Inc.) was administered as a standardized, psychophysical test of olfactory identification (score range: 0–40). Each score corresponds to the number of correctly identified odorants, with higher scores indicating better olfactory function [16].

The three contributing studies are described below: Study 1 (eight centers): This prospective, observational study enrolled participants with CF and CRS (≥18 years old) from eight US institutions: University of California Los Angeles, Medical University of South Carolina, National Jewish Health (NJH), Oregon Health and Science University, University of Colorado, University of Utah, University of North Carolina, and Vanderbilt University Medical Center. Participants who underwent ESS were compared with medically managed controls. Longitudinal outcomes included SIT, QOD‐NS, 22‐item Sinonasal Outcome Test (SNOT‐22), CF Questionnaire‐Revised, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, and so on [17].Study 2 (two centers): This IRB‐approved, prospective pre–post study enrolled participants with CF at University of Pittsburgh and University of North Carolina. The study evaluated sinonasal outcomes before and after ETI initiation, including SIT scores and sinonasal symptom burden measured by SNOT‐22 [11].Study 3 (one center): This prospective, observational study enrolled participants with CF (≥18 years old) at NJH who were either homozygous for F508del or compound heterozygous with a minimal function mutation. Participants were assessed before and 6 months after ETI initiation using the SIT, sinus computed tomography, sinonasal QoL measures, health utility assessments, and productivity loss questionnaires [13].

MCID Calculation

MCIDs were calculated using four distribution‐based methods: Standard error of measurement (SEM): Derived from baseline SIT scores, the SEM reflects inherent measurement variability. Changes smaller than the SEM are likely to reflect measurement error rather than true changes in olfactory function [18].Minimal detectable change (MDC = 1.96 × SEM): This threshold represents the smallest detectable difference beyond measurement error at the 95% confidence interval (CI), providing a conservative benchmark for clinically meaningful change [19].0.5 × Baseline standard deviation (SD): Half of the baseline SD was used as a distribution‐based estimate of moderate effect size, a widely applied method across clinical research domains [20].0.5 × SD of change scores (ΔSD): For each follow‐up interval, the SD of change from baseline was calculated, and one‐half of this value was used as the MCID estimate. Unlike patient‐reported outcomes such as the QOD‐NS, where Cohen's d is well suited to detect small but noticeable shifts in subjective QoL, the SIT is a psychophysical test in which changes reflect correct odor identification rather than perceptual differences. Therefore, we replaced Cohen's d with 0.5 × SD of change scores, a validated alternative MCID method that is more appropriate for SIT. This approach captures the variability of actual score changes at each follow‐up and allows assessment of whether MCID estimates remain stable over time [20].

Other distribution‐based approaches, such as growth‐curve modeling and effect‐size conventions such as Cohen's d, were considered but excluded due to high variability and limited interpretability for SIT as a psychophysical test.

Exploratory Regression Analysis

Exploratory analysis of potential associations between patient characteristics and the change in SIT score over time was conducted using multivariable linear mixed effects models. The models used longitudinal SIT scores as the outcome and included covariates for time (years from baseline), baseline age, baseline body mass index (BMI), sex, treatment (ETI vs. ESS), an indicator for prior sinus surgery, and an indicator for prior CFTR modulation. For each covariate, the interaction with time was additionally assessed. Random subject intercepts were included to account for repeated observations within patients. Model coefficients and 95% CIs are reported for each covariate.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 122 individuals with CF were included in the pooled cohort (Table 1). The mean age was 32.9 ± 11.3 years. Of the 122 participants, 66 (54.1%) were female, 55 (45.1%) were male, and sex was not recorded for one individual (0.8%). Genotype distribution was as follows: 70 (57.4%) were F508del homozygous, 41 (33.6%) were compound heterozygous with one F508del allele, and nine (7.4%) carried other variants. The mean BMI was 23.6 ± 5.0 kg/m2. A total of 91 participants (75.0%) had undergone prior sinus surgery, 66 participants (54.1%) had previously received CFTR modulators, and seven (12%) had a history of lung transplantation. The mean baseline SIT score across the cohort was 27.9 ± 8.2. Baseline SIT scores did not differ significantly between the ETI and ESS groups (p = 0.41).

Follow‐Up Data

Out of the 122 participants with baseline SIT scores, 99 contributed at least one follow‐up SIT score and were included in longitudinal analyses (Table 2). The ETI subgroup included 79 participants who were treated with ETI, and the ESS subgroup included 20 participants who underwent ESS. Follow‐up completion rates for both were: 22 participants at 3 months, 57 at 6 months, 19 at 9 months, 25 at 12 months, and 26 at 24 months. The ESS group had no SIT data available at 24 months, as that study followed participants for 1 year. However, it should be noted that the contributing studies did not all have the same duration of follow‐up; some collected data for up to 1 year, while others extended longer and included more frequent follow‐up assessments.

Change in SIT Scores

Longitudinal evaluation of SIT scores are shown in Table 3. Among ETI‐treated participants, SIT scores remained largely stable across all follow‐up intervals, with mean changes ranging from −1.7 to +0.6. A statistically significant decline of modest magnitude was observed at 6 months (−1.4, p = 0.02). In the ESS group, average SIT improvements were observed at 3 months (+3.1), 6 months (+4.5), and 9 months (+3.4). However, none of these changes reached statistical significance, likely reflecting the small sample size in this subgroup. By 12 months, mean SIT scores in the ESS group had declined slightly (−0.7). When all 99 participants with follow‐up SIT scores were analyzed together, mean changes from baseline were small at every interval (range: −1.7 to +1.2) and did not reach statistical significance.

Exploratory Regression Analysis

Results of the exploratory multivariable linear mixed effects models evaluating longitudinal SIT scores are shown in Table S1. Prior CFTR modulator therapy was significantly associated with overall SIT score when averaged across all time points (p = 0.034). However, prior CFTR modulator therapy was not significantly associated with change in SIT score over time (p = 0.20). No other demographic or clinical variables, including prior sinus surgery, age, BMI, sex, or treatment group (ETI vs. ESS), were significantly associated with longitudinal change in SIT score.

Discussion

OD is common in PwCF and, in a general population, OD adversely impacts nutritional status, social functioning and QoL [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. The SIT is commonly employed in clinical and research practice as an assessment of olfaction. Interpretation of changes in SIT scores in PwCF has been limited by lack of a MCID specific to this disease. In this study that pooled data from three prospective studies, this gap was addressed by estimating the SIT MCID for PwCF. Using four distribution‐based approaches, the MCID estimates ranged from 2 to 4 points and the overall pooled estimate was 3.1. This threshold value could serve as a cut‐off to assess changes in olfaction among PwCF. Because this threshold was derived using distribution‐based methods, future anchor‐based validation will be helpful to determine whether this degree of change reflects a perceptible improvement for patients.

The finding that the SIT MCID in CF is slightly less than in non‐CF individuals (4.0) implies that relatively small changes in SIT scores could be clinically relevant for PwCF [8]. This may be because baseline olfaction in PwCF tends to be decreased due to longstanding sinonasal inflammation, so even modest improvements in olfaction may be clinically meaningful. PwCF may notice and experience benefits from smaller changes in olfaction than the general CRS population [21].

These reference estimates are also useful when used within other interventions. SIT scores were mostly unchanged during follow‐up in ETI‐treated PwCF, and a small decrease that was observed at 6 months reached statistical significance but was minimal (−1.4 points) and less than the newly estimated MCID. This is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that ETI improves sinonasal symptoms and radiographic findings, but does not have an appreciable impact on psychophysical olfaction measures [10, 11, 12]. In contrast, ESS was associated with mean SIT gains of 3.1–4.5 points in the early follow‐up periods, which approached or exceeded the MCID. Although these findings were not statistically significant, likely due to the sample size and limited follow‐up, these results suggest a possible benefit in those who undergo ESS, which warrant further exploration. It is also important to note that 75% of participants had undergone prior ESS. Revision surgery populations often have more refractory disease, which may limit the degree of olfactory improvement achievable with additional ESS or medical therapy. This likely contributed to both the lower baseline SIT scores and the modest gains observed.

Although ESS appeared to produce larger mean improvements in SIT scores than ETI, neither intervention group demonstrated statistically significant gains. ETI reduces sinonasal inflammation and improves radiographic findings of CRS, but these changes may not be sufficient to restore olfactory function [13]. In contrast, ESS directly removes obstructive tissue and secretions, potentially enabling the olfactory cleft to receive odor molecules easily, which may explain the nonsignificant SIT increases observed at early follow‐up [22].

Restoring olfactory function in PwCF is likely a multifactorial process. In a cohort of pediatric patients with CF treated with ETI, baseline olfactory function before treatment was below the 10th percentile in 60% of children aged 6–11 years old [11]. This cohort did not see an improvement in their olfactory scores after 6–9 months of ETI treatment. This suggests that early olfactory neuroepithelial function may be impaired by 6 years of age and a greater understanding of the neurodevelopmental window in children may help predict response to treatment as PwCF progress into adulthood and lead longer lives.

Exploratory multivariable regression with a random intercept did not identify demographic or clinical associations with the improvement in SIT over time. Although prior CFTR modulator therapy was associated with overall SIT scores when averaged across time points, this association appeared to be driven primarily by baseline differences rather than longitudinal change. No significant associations were observed between prior sinus surgery or other covariates and change in SIT score over time. These analyses were exploratory and likely underpowered due to limited longitudinal follow‐up, and therefore should be interpreted with caution. Further studies with larger cohorts and longer follow‐up will be needed to better evaluate predictors of olfactory change in PwCF. We also attempted to include anchor‐based MCIDs using patient‐reported outcomes, but anchor items were not employed in all of the three prospective studies. For this reason, distribution‐based strategies were favored, and future verification using anchor‐based techniques may be useful to corroborate these findings.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. Attrition and incomplete follow‐up data reduced the number of evaluable participants over time. Distribution‐based methods produced heterogeneous thresholds, which may influence the proportion of patients achieving MCID and highlight broader challenges in how MCIDs are operationalized in clinical research [23]. Future research should aim to validate these MCID estimates using anchor‐based approaches and incorporate patient‐reported measures to better align psychophysical testing with subjective perception. Another consideration when interpreting these results is the heterogeneity of the cohort, including variability in prior treatment exposure, ETI use, and baseline disease severity. This may limit the generalizability of the estimated MCID, and future studies in more homogeneous CF populations will be needed to validate and refine this threshold. Medication use including intranasal sprays was not consistently collected across studies and could not be incorporated into our analyses. Variation in these treatments may also influence sinonasal outcomes. Furthermore, repeated administrations of the SIT may introduce small familiarity‐related effects that could contribute to score variability over time. Future studies may benefit from the use of parallel or alternate SIT forms designed for longitudinal assessment [24]. SIT scores should also be interpreted with attention to how changes across the score range relate to clinical perception, particularly given that prior MCID validation applied a single threshold across heterogeneous baseline olfactory function [25]. Cultural differences in odor familiarity may also influence SIT performance, as prior work has shown that odor identification tests require cross‐cultural adaptation to account for variability in odor recognition across populations [26]. Multicenter studies with large cohorts are needed to evaluate the reproducibility of these thresholds and their predictive utility for treatment outcomes in PwCF.

Conclusion

In summary, these data suggest a CF‐specific SIT MCID of 3.1 points (95% CI: 2.1–4.1). This threshold has potential to serve as a clinically interpretable cut‐off for PwCF when assessing olfaction. These findings establish a foundation for future work, which can be further validated in larger cohorts incorporating anchor‐based techniques to guide both clinical care and research.
