Work-Related Quality of Life Among US Anesthesiologists: Burning Out from Burnout?
Authors: Michael A Smith, Steven B Porter
Published: 2026

Across the United States, physician burnout is an increasingly prevalent problem. The Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic worsened pre-existing trends in burnout.1 In this issue of Mayo Clinic Proceedings: Innovation, Quality, and Outcomes, Patel et al2 seek to shed more light on the topic by exploring an understudied aspect of burnout: sex-related concerns and medical comorbidities, which may contribute to reduced work-related quality of life (WRQoL).2

We commend the authors for taking a new approach to assess burnout. Despite all the work toward reducing burnout, there continues to be increasing rates of physicians at risk or qualifying for burnout syndrome based on Maslach Burnout Inventory criteria. Afonso et al1,3 produced 2 large-scale survey-based studies in 2021 and 2024 of US anesthesiologists, before and after the COVID-19 pandemic, respectively. Over this period, there was an increase in high risk for burnout (67.7% vs 59.2%) and those with burnout syndrome (18.9% vs 13.8%). Patel et al2 found that participants who were considered to have a moderate-to-high (positive) WRQoL had a burnout rate of 66% and as much as 92% in those with a low (negative) WRQoL, showing that this problem is still very much uncontrolled. One of the most commonly reported reasons for burnout was staffing shortages and long hours, dramatically worsened following COVID-19, based on data from Afonso et al.2,3

The rates of unhappiness at work are so alarmingly high that despite years of research, there seems to have been no major improvement in battling burnout. Patel et al2 state that although some data exists from other countries, there have been no anesthesiologist-based assessments of the interplay of sex-specific and comorbidity-specific concerns on WRQoL in the United States.4 This study provides some novel insight to the topic but has several limitations. For example, being stuck in an operating room without access to a bathroom is not unique to anesthesiologists. Similar concerns exist for surgical technologists, circulating nurses, and surgeons. Further, having to stand at work is not unique to anesthesiologists. In fact, depending on the work model, anesthesiologists rarely have to stand in 1 place for long periods; certainly, less than the providers huddled around the patient in the operating room. Additionally, the study differentiates between hospital and outpatient practices but does not comment on the differences between a care team model or anesthesiologist-only practice model. With a supervision model, for example, medical conditions such as benign prostate hypertrophy are unlikely to have much impact given the ability for a physician anesthesiologist to leave the operating room. Working odd hours, such as night shift work, are experienced by many different providers, including overnight nurses, overnight laboratory technicians, and a host of other hospital-based employees. Nor is working overnight in stressful environments unique to anesthesiology, as emergency medicine physicians, intensive care staff, and traumatologists can attest.

Patel et al2 focus heavily on health concerns as potential drivers of burnout. We question the notion that prostatic hypertrophy or menstruation should have any impact, specifically, on anesthesiologists’ happiness. That being said, comorbidities can bring on a variety of emotions to a person’s daily living and even the way they view the world outside of work. We must use caution when interpreting this in the setting of “clinical” burnout, where the job itself has no bearing on how someone perceives these issues.

Subspecialty type was also not assessed. Anesthesia can be an isolating specialty, and less social interaction can be connected to increased depression rates and an association with increased comorbidities.5 A critical care anesthesiologist, for example, may experience more social interaction and participate in team-based management, which could reduce feelings of isolation. However, potentially more sequential hours, severe patient comorbidities, and end-of-life discussions, may tip the scales toward burnout potential.

We must use caution when making too many assumptions based on any survey assessment of why clinicians are experiencing burnout. It is easy to point at workload as the culprit for experiencing unhappiness, and therefore, surveys are fraught with error from recall bias. Additionally, there is inherent bias in those that actually choose to answer the survey. With the advent of the internet, a clinician’s “to do” list never ends. The authors in fact use the term “eblast” to describe the substantial amount of emails that were sent in this study. The link was clicked <5% of the time per “blast.” The response rate was a modest 6.2%, a perfect example of how people have become disenchanted with all the information they receive daily, and may contribute to burnout. This information overload is not unique to anesthesiologists.

What are proposed solutions to burnout? There is a considerable amount of data to support a multilayered approach to managing employee wellness and burnout in the workplace.5,6 Workplace culture can play a role in fostering a benevolent work environment.3 Feedback is an integral part to this as well; being able to speak freely, feeling safe to both provide and receive feedback, may be able to reduce staff burnout. This applies to any job but particularly those in the medical field where stress of patient care is high and decisions can quite literally be a matter of life and death. More autonomy to be able to control daily and scheduling is another area that can be delegated to the personnel level and may improve morale.1 The issue with these solutions is that it is like putting a bandage on a wound that really needs a reconstructive surgeon. It is clear that we need a new approach, but how should these issues be addressed?

On the contrary, by assessing burnout repetitively, we are contributing to it. We cannot digest all the information we are exposed to every day, and the constant bombardment with alerts and new tasks worsens the problem. Patient messages going to an after-work “inbox” as for other specialties does not seem to be a problem for anesthesiologists given the focus on acute care1; this means anesthesiologists are often spared from out-of-office responsibilities, a positive WRQoL factor. This difference could be a point to press further into: eliminating as many after-hours communications as possible, including providing cross coverage for any patient follow-up concerns. An easy area to begin would be reducing “eblasts” by societies within our own specialty.

In summary, we thank Patel et al2 for shining more like on the interplay among sex, comorbidities, and WRQoL. We advocate for physicians to continue the search for actionable solutions to burnout in medicine.
