Predictive factors for the development of urethral stricture in patients undergoing holmium laser enucleation of the prostate: A retrospective study
Authors: Ferec Efendioğlu, Cihat Özcan, Zafer Tokatli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to determine the incidence and predictors of urethral stricture after holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP) and to assess the safety of the procedure. We retrospectively reviewed data from 202 patients who underwent HoLEP. Patients were divided into 2 groups: those with urethral stricture (group 2) and those without (group 1). Preoperative prostate-specific antigen (PSA), uroflowmetry with post-void residual (PVR), and International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) were evaluated. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of urethral stricture. Perioperative complications were classified according to the Clavien–Dindo system. The mean age was 67.0 ± 7.6 years in group 1 and 68.4 ± 7.3 years in group 2 (P = .335). Urethral stricture developed in 6.4% of patients. Multivariate analysis revealed that preoperative prostatitis (odds ratio [OR]: 116.81; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.32–10,330; P = .047) and the use of general anesthesia (OR: 269.07; 95% CI: 7.30–9912.74; P = .047) were independent predictors of stricture formation. Preoperative prostatitis and general anesthesia significantly increase the risk of urethral stricture after HoLEP.

1. Introduction

Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) due to prostate enlargement are common in elderly male patients.[1,2] Approximately 36% of patients under active surveillance for benign prostatic obstruction (BPO) require surgery within 5 years.[3,4] In 1996, the holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP) technique was introduced for the endoscopic surgical treatment of prostates weighing >80 g to reduce complications.[5,6] Currently, the HoLEP technique is widely used. The American and European Urology guidelines recommend that it be preferred for the surgical treatment of any BPO, regardless of prostate size.[7,8] Studies have shown that HoLEP is both effective and safe.[9,10] However, retrograde ejaculation, hematuria, urethral stricture, urinary incontinence, and other complications have also been reported.[10] Among these, urethral stricture is a complication that requires invasive re-intervention (such as internal urethrotomy), resulting in reduced patient comfort and increased financial costs. A review of the literature revealed many studies investigating the factors associated with the development of urethral strictures after HoLEP.[11-13] In these studies, we observed that factors such as previous prostate surgery, preoperative catheterization, preoperative urinary infection history, perioperative nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use, white blood cell (WBC) count in preoperative blood samples, and postoperative urinary infection were not evaluated. In this study, we evaluated the effects of these factors on the development of urethral strictures after HoLEP.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and population

This study included patients without a history of preoperative or intraoperative urethral strictures who underwent HoLEP between January 2021 and August 2023. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Medicana International Ankara Hospital (2023; BŞH 27/2). The procedures were performed by 2 urologists with 35 years (12 years for HoLEP) and 21 years (10 years for HoLEP) of professional experience. Patient data were collected retrospectively.

The total number of patients was 594, and we were able to determine whether these patients developed urethral strictures; however, complete data were available for only 202 patients. Therefore, 594 patients were used to calculate the incidence, and 202 patients were used to analyze the predictive factors.

A preoperative urine culture examination was performed to detect urinary tract infection (UTI). A bacterial growth of ≥105 colonies/mL in the urine culture was considered significant for UTI. Preoperative antibiotics from the third-generation cephalosporin group were administered to all patients for prophylaxis. Patients were discharged on the second or third postoperative day.

Postoperative follow-up evaluations were performed using uroflowmetry with post-void residual (PVR) measurement and the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) at 1 and 6 months postoperatively, and annually thereafter. Patients with a maximal urine flow rate (Qmax) < 15 mL/s on control uroflowmetry were suspected of having urethral stricture, and a definitive diagnosis was made using endoscopy.

Patients were divided into 2 groups: group 1 (no urethral stricture) and group 2 (urethral stricture). In this study, the relationship between the development of urethral stricture after HoLEP and several factors was investigated, including preoperative indwelling Foley catheter or Foley catheter insertion history (for any reason), preoperative white blood cell (WBC) count (103), history of diabetes mellitus (DM), postoperative NSAID use, history of preoperative prostatitis (acute bacterial prostatitis), history of secondary endoscopic prostate surgery, type of anesthesia (regional or general), presence of postoperative urinary tract infection (UTI), and development of postoperative urinary retention.

Preoperative prostatitis was defined based on the presence of clinical symptoms such as pelvic discomfort, perineal pain, dysuria, or urinary frequency. The diagnosis was confirmed by a positive urine or semen culture when available.

Postoperative UTI was defined as the presence of at least 1 urinary symptom (e.g., dysuria, urgency, frequency, or suprapubic pain) in combination with a positive urine culture showing ≥105 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL of a single uropathogen. Clinical judgment was also considered in cases presenting with fever or systemic symptoms.

Perioperative complications were evaluated using the Clavien Scoring System.

2.2. Surgical technique and equipment

HoLEP was performed with our previously described technique (low tension total retrograde HoLEP technique), as 2 or 3 lobes technique depending on prostate morphology (with/without huge median lob). A 21-French (Fr) continuous-flow laser resectoscope (Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany) combined with a mechanical tissue morcellator (Versacut, Lumenis Inc., San Jose) was used. The HoLEP procedure was performed using a 100-W holmium laser (Versapulse, Lumenis Inc., Santa Clara) at 42 W (1.2 J and 35 Hz). Following the operation, a 20-Fr or 22-Fr 3-way Foley (Rüsch, Germany) urethral catheter was inserted, and the catheter balloon was filled with 50 mL saline fluid and placed in traction. Traction duration was 1 hour.

2.4. Missing data and cohort definition

Although 594 patients underwent HoLEP during the study period, only 202 patients had complete clinical and laboratory data available for multivariate statistical analysis. The incidence of urethral stricture was calculated using the entire cohort (n = 594),whereas the analysis of predictive factors was conducted solely on the subset with complete data (n = 202).

Due to the retrospective nature of the study, missing data could not be assessed for randomness (e.g., MCAR, MAR, and MNAR), therefore, no multiple imputation was performed. Instead, a complete case analysis approach was used. This limitation was acknowledged, and the potential for selection bias was considered when interpreting the results.

3. Results

A total of 594 patients underwent HoLEP, 202 of whom had complete clinical data. Urethral strictures developed in 6.39% (38/594) of patients. The mean age was 67.03 ± 7.58 years in group 1 (no stricture) and 68.42 ± 7.27 years in group 2 (stricture), with no statistically significant difference between the groups (P = .335).

Preoperative prostatitis was observed in 34 (16.83%) patients. Fifteen (7.5%) patients underwent HoLEP as a secondary procedure. Preoperative catheterization was documented in 44 patients (21.8%) and permanent catheterization in 3 patients (1.5%).

A total of 38 strictures were identified, located in the meatus (n = 15), penile urethra (n = 12), membranous urethra (n = 7), and bladder neck (n = 4). Some patients had multiple strictures in different locations.

The mean preoperative white blood cell (WBC) count was 7.29 ± 1.98 × 103/μL. The mean operative time was 34.82 ± 14.8 minutes. The mean preoperative prostate volume was 92.31 ± 48.27 mL, the mean weight of removed tissue was 53.04 ± 36.23 g, and the mean duration of postoperative catheterization was 2.05 ± 0.22 days. The length of hospital stay ranged from 2 to 3 days.

In the postoperative period, urinary retention occurred in 4 patients (1.98%) and urinary tract infection (UTI) in 5 patients (2.5%; Table 1). For patients with urinary retention, a Foley catheter was reinserted and removed after 48 hours; all patients were able to void spontaneously thereafter.

4. Discussion

HoLEP is a well-established and effective method for the treatment of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). It is associated with certain complications, most notably urethral stricture.[14,15] In our study, 594 patients who underwent HoLEP were evaluated. However, detailed clinical variables that may influence urethral strictures (e.g., history of catheterization, infection, and type of anesthesia) were available for only 202 patients. Therefore, while the incidence of urethral stricture was calculated based on the entire cohort, a risk factor analysis was conducted on a subgroup of 202 patients.

In our study, the rate of urethral strictures following HoLEP was 6.39%. This rate is consistent with previous studies, which reported rates ranging from 0.8% to 11.7%.[14-17] Additionally, there was no statistically significant difference in the mean age between patients who developed strictures and those who did not (P = .335), consistent with prior studies indicating that age is not an independent risk factor for stricture formation.[14,15,17]

Our findings related to prostate volume show both agreement and inconsistency with existing literature. In our study, strictures were more frequently observed in patients with smaller prostate volumes (P = .003). This finding is in line with that of Elkoushy et al (P < .01),[15] whereas Morozov et al reported no such association.[18] These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in surgical technique or patient selection. In smaller prostates, the surgical capsule is often less distinct, making enucleation more challenging and potentially increasing the risk of tissue trauma, fibrosis, and stricture.

Preoperative catheterization was found to significantly increase the risk of urethral stricture (P = .001), which is consistent with the findings of Elkoushy et al (P = .01).[15] Prolonged mucosal irritation due to catheterization may lead to inflammation and subsequent scar tissue formation. Elkoushy et al also reported that longer operative time and prolonged postoperative catheterization increased the risk of stricture formation.[15] In our study, operative time was not significantly associated with stricture formation (P = .154). Moreover, since the catheters were uniformly removed on postoperative day 2, we could not evaluate the impact of catheter duration.

Another important finding was the association between anesthesia type and stricture development. Patients who underwent general anesthesia had a significantly higher risk of developing urethral strictures (P = .001). One possible explanation is that general anesthesia may cause intraoperative hypotension, potentially leading to ischemia in the periurethral tissues and urethra, which can promote fibrosis and stricture formation. Alternatively, this association may have been influenced by unmeasured confounding factors. For instance, patients receiving general anesthesia may differ in baseline characteristics, intraoperative management, or procedural complexity, which could independently affect stricture risk. Further studies are warranted to explore this association in greater depth.

No major complications, such as bleeding requiring transfusion, TUR syndrome, bladder perforation, ureteral orifice injury, thromboembolism, myocardial infarction, or mortality, were observed in our study. This contrasts with the findings of Morozov et al, who reported individual cases of transfusion and TUR syndrome.[18] We attribute our low complication rates to the fact that the included cases were performed between 2011 and 2021, during which the surgical team had surpassed the learning curve and gained considerable experience.

We were unable to evaluate the impact of resectoscope size on stricture development. Since 2021, all procedures have been performed using a standard 22-French resectoscope. Thai et al compared 26-Fr and 28-Fr resectoscopes and found no significant difference in stricture rates (P = .405).[19] Nevertheless, this does not eliminate the possibility that smaller instruments offer long-term benefits.

Regarding postoperative complications, none of our patients experienced a fever ≥38°C, which contrasts with Morozov et al, who reported such fever in 3% of cases.[18] Additionally, urinary retention (1.98%) and urinary tract infection (2.5%) were both significantly associated with an increased stricture risk in our study (P = .001). These complications may interfere with urethral healing by intensifying inflammatory responses. Although the incidence of these complications has been reported in the literature,[20] their direct impact on stricture formation remains underexplored.

Lastly, a history of preoperative prostatitis, secondary endoscopic surgery, diabetes mellitus, and absence of postoperative NSAID use were also associated with an increased risk of urethral stricture. These factors may impair wound healing and promote fibrosis by increasing fibroblast activity and inducing chronic inflammation. Patients with diabetes or a history of prostatitis may exhibit an exaggerated fibroproliferative response, predisposing them to urethral narrowing.

In conclusion, our study identified several risk factors associated with urethral strictures following HoLEP. These findings were compared with those of previous studies and interpreted using plausible clinical and biological mechanisms. Our results may contribute to optimizing patient selection and perioperative management strategies to minimize complications after HoLEP.

4.1. Study limitations

This study had several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as this was a retrospective study, it is inherently subject to selection bias. Although the initial cohort included 594 patients, complete data were available for only 202 patients, which may have affected the representativeness of the sample and the generalizability of the results. Second, the logistic regression analysis produced very high odds ratios with wide confidence intervals, suggesting potential issues related to the relatively small sample size or uneven distribution of key variables. These statistical limitations may have led to an overestimation of the strength of the observed associations. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further prospective studies with larger and more balanced populations are needed to confirm these results.

5. Conclusions

Therefore, HoLEP appears to be a safe procedure. In our study, a history of preoperative prostatitis and the use of general anesthesia were associated with an increased risk of developing urethral strictures. However, owing to the retrospective design and statistical limitations, these findings should be interpreted with caution.
