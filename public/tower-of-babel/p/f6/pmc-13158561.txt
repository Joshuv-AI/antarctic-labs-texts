A nine-month survival following rupture of a juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm without repair
Authors: James Donohue, Nathan Booth, Shyamal Pansuriya, Michael Trpkovski, Sachinder Hans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a case of 9-month survival following rupture of an abdominal aortic aneurysm that was managed nonoperatively with impulse control and reversal of anticoagulation. Open surgical repair was not offered because of significant medical comorbidities, and endovascular repair was not feasible because of anatomical considerations. The patient died 9 months later from respiratory failure.Keywords: Abdominal aortic diseases, Aortic aneurysm, Rupture aortic aneurysm, Ruptured aortic rupture aneurysm

Rupture of an abdominal aortic aneurysm remains a catastrophic medical emergency associated with high mortality rates. The mortality from ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (rAAA) with and without intervention can be as high as 81%.1,2 There has been an increasing use of endovascular repair for rAAAs during the past 2 decades. Despite the increasing use of endovascular repair for rAAA, the 30-day mortality ranges from 25% to 50%.3 Many studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of endovascular repair as compared with open repair for rAAAs and found survival advantages with a gain in quality-adjusted life years, similar levels of reintervention, and reduced costs.4,5

Despite technological advancements, not all patients with rAAAs are candidates for endovascular repair. Christenson et al in 1984 documented the first case of prolonged survival of an 8-cm rAAA without intervention.6 Tham et al also reported a 13-month survival of a rAAA without intervention.7 Another reported case series of 12 patients with acute rAAAs who did not undergo operative repair, had 11 deaths occurring in the first 48 hours, with the twelfth patient surviving for 15 months.4 We report a 9-month survival of an rAAA in an 85-year-old woman with multiple medical comorbidities who did not undergo either endovascular or open repair. The patient was managed with strict blood pressure control and reversal of anticoagulation. The patient’s husband agreed to publish her case report along with the images.

Case report

An 85-year-old woman with a past medical history notable for hypertension, peripheral arterial disease, recent non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation on warfarin, chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder, and a 60 pack-years history of smoking. She presented to the emergency department in July 2024 with an acute left side abdominal pain radiating through to her back. She presented with a blood pressure of 140/59 mmHg, a heart rate of 54, and oxygenating without difficulty. On physical exam, she had a tender pulsatile mass in the left paraumbilical area. She had absent dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses in both lower extremities. A computed tomography aortogram of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated a ruptured juxtarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm measuring 6.3 cm in diameter with an aneurysmal sac volume of 171.34 mL and extreme angulation of the aortic neck measuring 88° (Fig 1, A and B). The aortic neck length was 3 mm, and the aortic neck diameter was 24 mm. There was a small amount of hemoperitoneum and retroperitoneal hematoma with contrast extravasation on the right anterolateral aspect of the aneurysm.

(A and B) Transverse and coronal sections of computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis with intravenous contrast during the patient’s initial presentation to the emergency department. The CT scan demonstrates active extravasation and hematoma extending into the left retroperitoneum, causing left perinephric inflammation.

She was initiated on a nicardipine drip for blood pressure control to keep a mean arterial pressure 60 mmHg (80-100 mmHg systolic pressure). Warfarin was reversed with Kcentra. Hemoglobin in the emergency department was 11.3 g/dL with hematocrit 35.3%. Given the severe angulation of the aortic neck in a juxtarenal aortic aneurysm and bilateral small diameter and calcified external iliac arteries (less than 5.5 mm in diameter), the patient was not considered a suitable candidate for endovascular repair. This is demonstrated in Fig 2, Fig 3, a three-dimensional reconstruction from computed tomography angiography (CTA) taken on hospital day six. Open repair was not offered because of the patient’s prohibitive risk for perioperative complications and mortality. She was admitted to the intensive care unit for close monitoring and nonoperative management with permissive hypotension, impulse control, and anticoagulation reversal.

Three-dimensional reconstruction from computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the abdomen and pelvis taken 6 days after emergency department presentation. Aortic aneurysm neck measurement was 3 mm, and diameter was 24 mm. The distance between the aneurysm neck and the right renal artery takeoff is 3 mm and for the left renal artery takeoff is 2.5 mm.

(A) A transverse section of computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the abdomen and pelvis taken on hospital day 6, which demonstrates resolution of the extravasation and decreasing size of the left perinephric retroperitoneal hematoma. (B) A transverse section of CTA of the abdomen and pelvis taken outpatient 2 months following patients emergency department presentation, which demonstrates resolution of the retroperitoneal hematoma and left perinephric inflammatory changes.

She was transitioned to DNAR/DNI with discussions for hospice care. Serial hemoglobin checks continued to slowly drop from 9.7 g/dL to 7.8 g/dL, and hematocrit dropped from 29.7% to 24.5% on hospital day two. Her hemoglobin level was stable at 8 g/dL. A repeat CTA 6 days following her initial presentation to the emergency department demonstrated resolution of active contrast extravasation with decrease in the size of the retroperitoneal hematoma (Fig 3, A). She was discharged on the eighth hospital day.

A repeat CTA of abdomen and pelvis 2 months following her initial presentation demonstrated a slight increase in the size of the AAA, measuring 6.4 cm with intramural thrombus and resolution of periaortic hematomas (Fig 3, B). She was subsequently seen in vascular clinic for gangrenous changes to her right fifth toe. An abdominal aortogram and lower extremity arteriography 6 months following the rupture of AAA demonstrated right distal superficial femoral artery and proximal popliteal artery occlusion. Patient underwent successful femoral and popliteal angioplasty and stenting. She subsequently expired 9 months following rAAA at home with her family as a result of acute respiratory failure and failure to thrive.

Discussion

Despite advancements in the management of rAAAs, the morbidity and mortality following emergent repair is quite high. In contemporary vascular practice, endovascular repair is preferred as compared with open repair in patients with suitable anatomy for endovascular aortic repair (EVAR). Optimal aneurysmal features predicting EVAR success are aortic neck length >15 mm, aortic neck diameter <32 mm, aortic neck angulation <60°, and the amount of circumferential thrombus greater than 2 mm in thickness or >50% calcification increases the risk of poor proximal seal apposition. Neck characteristics outside of the instructions for use for all endografts are independently associated with completion type Ia endoleaks, perioperative mortality, 1-year sac expansion, and 1-year reinterventions among patients undergoing elective EVAR.8 The major factor impacting EVAR success is the neck morphology and angulation.5 Result of the IMPROVE trial suggests endovascular repair is not always feasible in some patients due to severe angulation of aortic neck and other contraindication for endovascular repair like small diameter iliac arteries.9 IMPROVE trial compared the 30-day mortality of EVAR vs open repair and found mortality following EVAR was 35.4% and 37.4% with open repair.10 A follow-up of IMPROVE trial data compared 3-year mortality between EVAR vs open repair and found 48% of patients in the EVAR group and 56% in the open group had died (P = .053). Mortality after 7 years was approximately 60% in both groups.

Recent data suggests that EVAR repair for rAAA, when feasible, has reduced perioperative mortality, shorter operative time, less blood loss, decreased need for intensive postoperative care, shorter hospital stay, and overall lower rates of major complications. Additionally, the minimally invasive nature of EVAR makes it an attractive option for high-risk elderly patients who may not tolerate open repair.

Occasional longer survival following a rAAA without repair has been reported, with the longest reported survival of 15 months. Our patient survived 9 months following her rupture and ultimately succumbed to her medical comorbidities. Permissive hypotension is a well-accepted treatment modality for patients presenting to the emergency department with rAAAs with normal or slightly elevated blood pressure to decrease blood loss prior to aortic clamping/occlusion. In this patient, strict blood pressure control with reversal of anticoagulation aided in achieving a seal at the site of rupture. Invariably, these patients do not have long survival without repair because the hematoma is contained in the retroperitoneal space, but after a short time, the hematoma continues to expand, with eventual rupture into the peritoneal cavity. In some patients with an rAAA with a small posterior leak, the lumbar spine can tamponade the site of rupture, and the patient may present with chronic contained rupture. Such patients usually do not require emergency repair.
