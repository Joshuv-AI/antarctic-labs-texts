Background

Influenza is an acute respiratory viral infection. The World Health Organization estimates that seasonal influenza, which affects approximately 1 billion individuals annually, is associated with 290,000 to 650,000 respiratory deaths, including 3 to 5 million cases of severe illness [1, 2]. Young children, particularly those under 5 years of age and those with chronic medical conditions, are among the most vulnerable populations and account for a substantial proportion of influenza-related hospitalizations [3]. Additionally, this group faces an elevated risk of ICU admission or development of bacterial pneumonia [4]. Severe influenza requiring ICU admission remains a significant clinical challenge due to its high mortality. Severe influenza cases are associated with intense pulmonary inflammation and injury [5], which may manifest as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) with potentially fatal outcomes [6]. Pneumonia is a major complication of influenza. The predominant clinical presentation involved specific pulmonary involvement that rapidly necessitated mechanical ventilation and frequently progressed to ARDS [7]. Studies have demonstrated that influenza-associated pneumonia is an independent predictor of mortality in influenza cases [8]. Therefore, early identification of risk factors for poor prognosis in severe influenza pneumonia is crucial. Currently, there is a paucity of studies examining the prognosis of severe influenza pneumonia. Consequently, this retrospective study investigated the clinical manifestations, laboratory data, and imaging findings of children with severe influenza pneumonia to identify risk factors associated with poor prognosis, with the aim of facilitating early identification and intervention to improve outcomes.

Methods

Study population

A retrospective study was conducted on ICU-admitted children diagnosed with severe influenza pneumonia from January 2020 to February 2025. Inclusion criteria required that all subjects meet the following conditions: (1) patients with acute respiratory symptoms, including fever, cough, or dyspnea; (2) age under 18 years; (3) a positive result for influenza virus antigen or nucleic acid PCR testing using nasopharyngeal aspirates or swabs collected before or within 24 h post-admission; (4) chest imaging performed before or within 24 h of admission showing pneumonia manifestations, including consolidation, linear densities, patchy densities, patchy opacities, parenchymal infiltration, or pleural effusion; (5) fulfillment of the diagnostic criteria for clinical severity assessment of severe pediatric pneumonia [9]. Exclusion criteria for the study encompassed patients meeting any of the following conditions: (1) a disease duration exceeding 7 days prior to ICU admission; (2) a history of severe chronic underlying respiratory diseases, such as pulmonary tuberculosis, lung cancer, interstitial lung disease, or pulmonary fibrosis; (3) patients already receiving long-term mechanical ventilation or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) before admission, which could confound the accurate assessment of the initial disease condition; (4) a long-term history of high-doses glucocorticoid or immunosuppressants use; (5) the clinical data’s is incomplete; and (6) severe and progressive underlying conditions, including disabling neurological disorders, severe chromosomal abnormalities, critical congenital cardiac diseases, and end-stage chronic respiratory diseases. Patients with mild and stable comorbidities that did not affect prognostic evaluation were included in the study. Ethical approval from the ethics committee of Henan Children’s Hospital Zhengzhou Children’s Hospital (2025-IITGC-008-002).

Study design

Data collection: Data on various parameters, including age, sex, height, weight, influenza virus type, clinical manifestations, underlying medical conditions, laboratory test results, imaging findings, treatment regimens, and clinical outcomes of pediatric patients diagnosed with severe influenza pneumonia were systematically collected.

Sample collection: All patients underwent sputum and blood bacterial cultures, as well as nucleic acid detection for multiple respiratory viral pathogens, upon admission. Within 24 h of ICU admission, patients also received auxiliary tests, including arterial blood gas analysis, complete blood count, and blood chemistry.

Treatment protocol: Antiviral therapy with peramivir was initiated in all patients upon admission. Concurrently, empirical or targeted antimicrobial therapy was administered based on the results of ancillary tests.

Prognosis assessment: Patients who died during hospitalization (10 cases), required ECMO support (5 cases), experienced multiple organ failure (9 cases), had treatment discontinued due to severe illness (10 cases), or developed severe neurological sequelae (11 cases) were classified into the poor prognosis group. In this study, clinical cure was defined as the complete resolution of clinical symptoms and signs, accompanied by normalization of chest imaging findings. Clinical improvement was characterized by significant amelioration of clinical symptoms and signs, along with substantial radiographic absorption of pulmonary lesions, without meeting the criteria for full recovery. Both outcomes were classified under the good prognosis group.

Statistical methods

SPSS 26.0 statistical software was used for statistical analysis. Quantitative data that conformed to a normal distribution were expressed as mean\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:$$\end{document}standard deviation and analyzed using an independent samples t-test; Non-normally distributed data were represented as median [M (P25, P75)] and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorial data were expressed as number (percentage) [n (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\%$$\end{document})] and analyzed using the chi-square test. Only variables significant in the univariate analysis (P <0.05) were included in the multivariate logistic regression. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify risk factors for poor prognosis in children with severe influenza pneumonia admitted to the ICU, and ROC analysis was used to determine the cutoff value for each index. All P values were two-tailed, and a P value of <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Laboratory data

No significant between-group differences were observed in white blood cell (WBC), neutrophil count (NEU), lymphocyte count (LYM), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), mean platelet volume (MPV), C-reactive protein (CRP), and C-reactive protein to albumin ratio (CAR) between the two groups (all P > 0.05). Discussion

Influenza is a contagious respiratory disease caused by the influenza virus. Despite advancements in medical technology, the global clinical burden of influenza continues to rise [10]. Influenza viruses infect bronchial epithelial cells, leading to epithelial damage, apoptosis, and desquamation [11]. Virus-induced cellular senescence can lead to pulmonary sequelae following influenza infection [12]. Severe lung infections may become fatal because of the extensive tissue damage that impairs organ function [13]. Therefore, early identification of risk factors associated with poor prognosis in severe influenza pneumonia is crucial for timely clinical intervention. In this study, we identified three independent prognosis factors associated with poor prognosis in severe influenza pneumonia: a fever peak \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\ge\:$$\end{document} 41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^\circ \mathrm{C}$$\end{document}, the need for IMV, and elevated IL-6 levels. We contextualize these findings within the pathophysiology of influenza virus infection, integrate them with recent evidence, and discuss their clinical implications.

Our study demonstrates that children with severe influenza pneumonia commonly present with high fever, with a peak temperature of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\ge\:\:$$\end{document}41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^\circ \mathrm{C}$$\end{document} identified as an independent risk factor for poor prognosis. Increased body temperature, both locally and systemically, is a key feature of the inflammatory response. Influenza viruses infect respiratory epithelial cells, innate immune cells and barrier tissues respond to pathogen- and damage-associated molecular patterns to induce inflammatory mediators, some of which are pyrogenic mediators [14]. The lung epithelium is the primary target of the influenza virus infection, and the resulting diffuse epithelial damage can impair gas exchange, resulting in severe pneumonia and the development of ARDS [15]. Fever can cause mitochondrial damage by boosting TH1-driven inflammatory responses [16], and high fever may increase mTOR activation in T cells [17, 18]; this enhanced T-cell activation may exacerbate host damage through amplified inflammation. Extreme hyperthermia further exacerbates alveolar collapse, hyaline membrane formation, pulmonary hemorrhage, and microvascular thrombosis, leading to complete disruption of gas exchange and irreversible refractory hypoxemia [19]. Consequently, the primary pathological features of severe influenza pneumonia include typical diffuse exudative alveolar injury accompanied by alveolar and interstitial edema, fibrinous exudate with hyaline membrane formation, inflammatory response cell infiltration, and, in severe cases, pulmonary hemorrhage [20].

For each 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^\circ \mathrm{C}$$\end{document} increase in body temperature, the metabolic rate rises by 13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\%$$\end{document}. At temperatures exceeding 41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^\circ \mathrm{C}$$\end{document}, oxygen consumption may surge to two to three times the physiological baseline, leading to rapid cellular hypoxia and activation of anaerobic glycolysis. Concurrently, hyperinflammation and impaired tissue perfusion [21] contribute to metabolic acidosis in patients with pneumonia, thereby exacerbating lung injury. Impaired pulmonary function and carbon dioxide retention can further drive respiratory acidosis, leading to higher rates of failure to avoid IMV and an increased risk of requiring IMV [22]. A study on the clinical characteristics and risk factors for death attributable to severe influenza in children identified mechanical ventilation as an independent risk factor for mortality among pediatric patients with severe influenza [23]. This study also found that IMV is significantly associated with poor prognosis in children with severe influenza pneumonia. Consequently, extreme hyperthermia in severe influenza pneumonia is not merely a symptom but also a key mediator of pulmonary injury and systemic failure.

IL-6 can be used to predict the severity of illness or death in influenza patients [24]. IL-6 is released by tissue macrophages and is an early and potent inflammatory mediator [25]. Influenza virus infection leads to marked weight loss, significantly increased viral load in lung tissue, and elevated serum levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 [26]. IL-6 plays a critical role in influenza pathogenesis in the lungs. Studies in IL-6−/− mice demonstrated reduced disease resistance, with alterations in protective mechanisms including imbalanced T-cell responses, impaired inflammatory resolution [27, 28], and prolonged neutrophil survival [29]. Additionally, elevated IL-6 levels during influenza infection are associated with hemophagocytic syndrome, which exacerbates pulmonary injury and an inferior clinical prognosis [30]. It has been found that IL-6 prevents apoptosis in virus-infected cells by increasing the expression of prosurvival molecules, such as Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, through the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 and nuclear factor kappa B signaling pathways [31]. Furthermore, IL-6 can induce vascular endothelial growth factor expression in epithelial cells, increase vascular permeability, and reduce myocardial contractility, ultimately contributing to multiple organ damage [32]. Our study identified elevated IL-6 as an independent risk factor for poor prognosis in patients with severe influenza pneumonia. This finding is consistent with the conclusion of Zhang Jiaying et al. [33], who reported that IL-6 is strongly and positively correlated with poor prognosis in children with influenza-associated pneumonia.

Furthermore, we performed ROC curve analysis and confirmed that fever peak \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\ge\:$$\end{document} 41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^\circ \mathrm{C}$$\end{document}, IMV, and IL-6 had moderate predictive value for poor prognosis in severe influenza pneumonia (all P < 0.05).

A study analyzing bronchoalveolar lavage fluid from patients with influenza A pneumonia [34] founds no statistically significant difference in viral and MP coinfection rates across disease severity subgroups. In contrast, bacterial coinfection rates differed significantly among these subgroups. Influenza virus infection disrupts the airway epithelium and compromises immune cell function, thereby creating a permissive environment for secondary bacterial infections that accelerate the progression of severe disease [35]. A systematic review and meta-analysis investigating the determinants of poor prognosis in patients with influenza pneumonia [36] demonstrated that bacterial coinfection markedly increases the risk of adverse clinical outcomes in this population. However, these results revealed that bacterial, viral, and MP coinfections exerted no significant impact on the prognosis of critically ill children with severe influenza pneumonia admitted to the ICU. This finding may be attributable to the baseline homogeneity of the enrolled participants and early standardized comprehensive ICU management, which effectively curbed the progression of coinfection.

This study has several limitations. As a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, its finding may lack sufficient generalizability. All risk factor analyses were based solely on baseline auxiliary laboratory parameters measured within the first 24 h of ICU-admission. Dynamic data on inflammatory markers and organ function indices were lacking; therefore, this study could not reflect the continuous trend of disease progression or the cumulative impact of subsequent changes in coinfection status on clinical prognosis. Moreover, the retrospective design is inherently prone to selection bias and limits the ability to predictive ability between risk factors and the prognosis of severe influenza pneumonia. Future research with larger sample sizes and standardized protocols in multicenter prospective studies is needed to validate these findings.

Conclusions

In summary, fever peak \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\ge\:$$\end{document} 41\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^\circ \mathrm{C}$$\end{document}, IMV, and elevated IL-6 levels are independent predictors of poor prognosis in children with severe influenza pneumonia. Clinically, physicians should pay close attention to critical baseline indicators at admission, including inflammatory stress status, magnitude of hyperthermia, and the need for respiratory. These parameters may facilitate the early identification of pediatric patients at high risk of adverse clinical outcomes and guide targeted interventions to reduce severe influenza pneumonia mortality.
