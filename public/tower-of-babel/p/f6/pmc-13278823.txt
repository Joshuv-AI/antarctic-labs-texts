Genomic Variation and Population Structure of Portuguese Native Dog Breeds
Authors: Ludmilla Blaschikoff, Octávio Serra, Fernanda Simões, Catarina Ginja, Ana Elisabete Pires
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe presence of dogs in Portugal is confirmed by zooarchaeological evidence dating to at least 7600 years ago. The 11 native dog breeds that exist in this territory are therefore the end‐product of a complex evolutionary process. This study characterises the genomic diversity and population structure of 10 native breeds based on whole‐genome sequencing (WGS) data obtained from 46 unrelated dogs (4–6 per breed, 9–16x coverage). These data were merged with public genomes and also downsampled to match the 170 K Illumina Canine HDBeadChip, including an additional 1770 dogs from 211 dog breeds for a worldwide comprehensive population genomics analysis. The results revealed multiple maternal haplotypes distributed across haplogroups A‐D, high autosomal diversity, low to moderate inbreeding, breed‐specific genomic signatures of evolutionary events, and shallow genetic differentiation ranging from greater homogeneity to intra‐breed sub‐structure. These results reflect the diverse history of Portuguese dog breeds, such as geographical isolation in the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog, recent admixture between the Algarve Barrocal and Portuguese Warren Hound, shared ancestry in the Alentejo Mastiff, Transmontano Mastiff and Estrela Mountain Dog breeds, and a severe demographic bottleneck in the Portuguese Water Dog. This research emphasises the importance of preserving these breeds for their contribution to the overall genomic diversity of dogs, functional utility and cultural relevance, providing the basis for outlining strategies for the conservation and genetic management of locally well‐adapted dog breeds.

Introduction

The oldest archaeological evidence of dogs (Canis lupus familiaris) in Portugal dates to ~7600 years ago through the excavation of two almost complete skeletons (Arias et al. 2017, 2015; Detry and Cardoso 2010; Pires et al. 2019), corresponding to the Late Mesolithic chronology in Western Europe. Since then, many more dog remains have been found in archaeological contexts throughout the Iberian Peninsula (e.g., Albizuri et al. 2019), suggesting human's interest in maintaining and using these animals as symbols in rituals (e.g., Daza‐Perea 2019; Valera et al. 2010), or as assistants. A study on Iberian dogs dated to the Chalcolithic period (ca. 5000–4000 BP) detected some degree of morphological variability based on height‐at‐shoulders estimations, with medium‐to‐large dogs likely, reflecting selection pressures for size differentiation and probably selected for tasks such as hunting or livestock guarding (Blaschikoff et al. 2022). Evidence of accentuated dog morphotypes in Portugal can be dated to the 10th century (Rodrigues 1993) and the 12th century (Braga 2000). In other regions of Europe, dogs dated from at least ca. 11 000 cal BP already show diversification at crania morphology (Evin et al. 2025), and also at mandibles morphology at least ca. 5000 cal BP (Brassard et al. 2022). During the Roman period (ca. 2000 BP), classes of dogs such as villaticus (property guarding), pastoralis (livestock guarding), venaticus (hunting) (Dansey 1831), lap dogs (Altuna Etxabe and Mariezkurrena Gastearena 1992), were already recognized and described in the literature, and in other sources, such as, iconography (e.g., in mosaics; Gómez 2023).

The Iberian dog populations have been subjected to multiple evolutionary processes such as natural and human‐mediated selection, genetic drift due to geographic isolation and population bottlenecks, which resulted in local differentiation and complex patterns of breed diversity. Presently there are 11 Portuguese native dog breeds of variable‐body size, shape and behavior internationally recognised by the Fédération Cynologique Internationale (FCI, https://www.fci.be/en/) (Figure 1). These breeds are maintained mainly in rural environments for functions such as livestock guarding/herding, property guarding, and hunting. The first dog show in Portugal took place in Oporto in 1902 (Alpoim 1999), while the first breed standards date to the beginning of the 20th century in the “Livro de Origens Português” (L.O.P.) book, founded in 1936 and first published in 1955 (Alpoim 1999; de Vasconcelos 1995).

Portuguese dogs are classified according to their morphology/function/behavior in five distinct groups, according to the FCI classification (see Supporting Information S1: Section S1 for additional information).

The Portuguese breeds are officially recognized, either nationally and/or internationally (exceptions are the Algarve Barrocal Dog and the Terceira Cattle Dog), and are in general characterized by small population sizes which are not subject to intensive artificial selection. Additionally, they are not closed breeding populations since new genetic input can still be recruited based on breed‐specific morphological and behavioural traits (related to their role) exhibited by individual dogs, mainly in rural areas, from where the breeds originated (a detailed description on the rules for a Registration Book's new entry can be found in Supporting Information S1: Section S2).

Genetic studies on Portuguese dog breeds based on uniparental molecular markers, revealed the presence of several mitogenomes distributed through clades A to D (Blaschikoff et al. 2022) confirming previous data on 8 breeds using a smaller mtDNA fragment (Pires et al. 2006), as well as Y chromosome haplotypes defined through Y‐Short Tandem Repeats (STRs) combined with Y‐Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (Pires et al. 2017). The analysis of autosomal genetic markers, such as STRs and Amplified Fragment Length Polymorphisms (AFLPs), revealed low levels of breed differentiation for a smaller dataset of Portuguese breeds (Pires et al. 2009). Expected heterozygosity levels showed that the least genetically diverse breeds were the Portuguese Pointer and the Portuguese Sheepdog, followed by the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog, while Transmontano Mastiff was the most diverse. A study by Van Asch et al. (2011) using autosomal STRs confirmed this shallow genetic structure for seven Portuguese dog breeds and relatively heterogeneous genetic compositions, preventing individual dogs´ genotypes from being easily assigned to their breed of origin.

For the majority of the Portuguese dog breeds, animals can be subjected to distinct selection strategies: in one group the show/urban dogs are bred with more intensive artificial selection for breed‐specific morphological/aesthetic traits, and in another group selected for performance according to specific function, for example, guarding, herding or hunting. According to Pedersen et al. (2013) in the first case it can lead to higher inbreeding levels and in the latter case to the maintenance of greater genetic variation. The Portuguese Water Dog is an exception, because due to new sanitary regulations, they are no longer allowed into the fishing boats and therefore are not used anymore for its original function, that is, aid local fishermen in their traditional work/tasks.

High‐throughput sequencing (HTS) technologies have enabled unprecedented opportunities to study dog genomes. Genomic information has become fundamental to describe patterns of genetic diversity and differentiation, and infer historical demographic processes, such as expansions/contractions, admixture, and the degree of gene flow. A recent genomic analysis including Portuguese Livestock Guarding Dogs (LGDs) identified two contemporary lineages, multiple ancestries, and extensive gene flow among several modern LGD breeds (Coutinho‐Lima et al. 2024). However, an in‐depth genomic analysis to understand the ancestral and phylogenetic relationships among all Portuguese dog breeds has not been carried out to date.

We aimed to conduct a large‐scale analysis of the genomic diversity and population structure of 10 Portuguese breeds, based on high‐throughput sequencing. These data were also downsampled to match the 170 K Illumina Canine HDBeadChip for a comprehensive population genomics analysis of worldwide breeds. Such study allowed for a better understanding of the origins, breed relationships, and the evolutionary processes underlying breed differentiation.

Materials and Methods

Biological Samples

For the Algarve Barrocal, Portuguese Pointing Dog, and Transmontano Mastiff breeds (n = 12), we collected five milliliters of peripheral blood from the cephalic vein by vacuum puncture into EDTA‐containing tubes and stored at 4°C. Blood collection was carried out by licensed veterinarians with the consent of dog owners. For the remaining breeds, we used DNA samples (n = 40) available from a biobank established by our team in 2004 at Instituto de Investigação Agrária e Veterinária (INIAV, IP), Portugal.

Genomic DNA was extracted from 0.2 mL of blood using the Jetflex genomic DNA purification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following manufacturer recommendations. DNA libraries were prepared from a minimum of 1.0 μg of genomic DNA using NEBNext DNA Library Prep Kit, following the manufacturer's recommendations. Briefly, genomic DNA was randomly fragmented to approximately 350 bp by shearing, end‐polished, A‐tailed, ligated to NEBNext adapters for Illumina sequencing, indexed, and PCR‐enriched using P5 and indexed P7 oligos. Paired‐end (2 × 150 bp) shotgun resequencing on NovaSeq 6000 instruments (Illumina) was outsourced to Novogene (UK). Details on the sampling and genomic data of 10 Portuguese (n = 46) and 4 foreign dog breeds (n = 11) generated in this study are provided in Table 1; Figure S1; and Tables S1 and S2.

Unless stated otherwise, the results were plotted with R Statistical Software v4.3.1 (R Core Team 2023) using the package ggplot2 v3.5.1 (Wickham 2016).

Data normality and variance homogeneity were assessed using Shapiro and Levene's tests, respectively. For analysis involving comparisons between two groups, Student's t‐test were used for normally distributed data, whereas non‐parametric Wilcoxon tests, including the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for paired data and the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for independent data, were applied when normality assumptions were not met. For comparisons involving more than two groups, a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used under normality assumptions; otherwise, the non‐parametric Kruskal‐Wallis test was used. When ANOVA results were statistically significant, a post hoc pairwise Tukey HSD test was conducted to identify specific group differences. In cases where the Kruskal‐Wallis test was significant, a post hoc pairwise Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was conducted. A significance level of α = 0.05 was applied for all statistical tests. These statistics were performed using R.

Pearson correlation coefficients and statistical significance values were estimated using the cor.test function in R. If the data were not normally distributed, Spearman correlation coefficients were used instead.

Whole‐Genome Sequencing and Variant Calling

The quality of the reads was checked using the FastQC v0.11.9 software (Andrews 2010). Cutadapt v1.18 (Martin 2011) was used to remove adapter sequences, discard reads < 35 bp and trim low‐quality bases (Q < 30) from read ends. Filtered reads were mapped to the dog mitochondrial and nuclear reference genome assemblies CanFam3.1 (GenBank accession numbers NC_002008.4 and GCA_000002285.2, respectively) using BWA MEM v0.7.17 (Li and Durbin 2009) with default settings. The alignments were indexed and sorted with SAMtools v1.7 (Li et al. 2009). Reads with mapping quality (MapQ) of 30 or greater were kept. AddOrReplaceReadGroups and MarkDuplicates tools from PICARD v2.18.14 (Broad Institute 2019) were used to add read groups and to remove duplicate reads, respectively. A set of known nuclear variants from dbSNP build 151 mapped to the Canfam3.1 reference genome (5 648 530 SNPs and Indels) was used to perform GATK v4.1.7 (McKenna et al. 2010) BaseRecalibrator with default arguments.

For the nuclear variants, individual GVCF files were generated using GATK HaplotypeCaller. GATK GenomicsDBImport and GenotypeGVCFs were used to consolidate the variants of all individuals and perform joint variant calling to produce a single multisample VCF, respectively. Variants with low quality (QD < 6 and GQ < 20), as well as low depth of coverage (DP < 5), were removed using GATK VariantFiltration. Only biallelic SNPs were selected using the GATK SelectVariants tool.

Missing data for each individual and SNPs, sample depth and minor allele frequency per SNP were accessed using a custom bash script. These results (Figure S3) were visualized using a custom R Markdown script. For population genetic analysis, a threshold to exclude low‐quality samples and SNPs was selected (see Figure S2 for more details).

SNPs annotation and additional statistics on the distribution of SNPs per breed are described in Supporting Information S1: Section S4.

For a comprehensive analysis of Portuguese and worldwide dog breeds, the 57 newly generated genomes (Table S2) and published whole‐genome sequence data for 96 dogs from 25 breeds and 2 Iberian wolves (Table S4) were downsampled to overlap the Illumina CanineHD 170 K SNP array—the data mapped to a genome assembly other than CanFam3.1 were converted using liftOver (Hinrichs 2006), prior to downsampling. The resulting dataset was merged with another 1674 dogs representing 195 worldwide breeds and 9 wild canids previously genotyped on the Illumina CanineHD SNP array (see Table S3) and allowed for a better representation of each breed. The datasets were combined with PLINK v1.9 (Purcell et al. 2007) (default version used, unless stated otherwise), addressing strand inconsistencies and triallelic positions by using ‐‐flip and ‐‐exclude, respectively, prior to merging. The raw merged dataset containing 1838 individuals (Table S5) and 163 886 autosomal SNPs was then filtered following the FAO guidelines (Ajmone‐Marsan et al. 2023) and using PLINK. SNPs with a call rate < 95% and individuals with a call rate < 90% were excluded, as well as SNPs with MAF < 1%. Pairwise kinship coefficients were estimated using the KING method (Manichaikul et al. 2010) implemented in PLINK v2.0 (Chang et al. 2015). Pairs with kinship coefficients > 0.177, indicative of first‐degree relationships (parent‐offspring/full siblings) were identified and one individual from each pair was removed. A total of 1767 individuals and 140 426 autosomal SNPs were retained for downstream analyses. Unless stated otherwise, all analyses with SNP array data used a minimum of 3 samples and a maximum of 10 randomly selected samples per study—to avoid size/source‐related bias affecting phylogenetic analysis, resulting in a dataset of 1669 samples. Details of excluded samples, including the criteria for removal, and an overview of samples used for each genomic analysis are summarized in Table S6.

Mitogenome Data

Mitogenomes from the 10 Portuguese (n = 45) and 4 international breeds (n = 10) previously described (Blaschikoff et al. 2022) were re‐processed in this study together with a new mitogenome from a Portuguese Pointing Dog breed, following the methodology described in Blaschikoff et al. (2022). These 56 mitogenomes were merged with 51 publicly available sequences from 27 worldwide dog breeds and crossbred dogs (not exhaustive list; Björnerfeldt et al. 2006; Duleba et al. 2015; Kim et al. 1998; Meadows et al. 2023; Pang et al. 2009; Verscheure et al. 2014), including a sub‐population of Portuguese Water Dog sampled in the US (n = 3; Meadows et al. 2023) and additional unpublished/publicly available sequences retrieved from GenBank (accession numbers AY656752.1, AY656744.1, AY656741.1, and AY656747.1), segregating within haplogroups A‐D. Additionally, 35 gray wolves ( Canis lupus lupus ) from Eurasia, one African wolf ( Canis lupaster ) and one coyote ( Canis latrans ), available from other studies (Arnason et al. 2007; Koblmüller et al. 2016; Koepfli et al. 2015; Björnerfeldt et al. 2006; Blaschikoff et al. 2022; Thalmann et al. 2013; Loog et al. 2020; Moliner et al. 2016), were included. The final dataset of 144 mitogenomes (Table S7) was aligned using Mafft v7 (Katoh and Standley 2013) with the options “UPPERCASE/lowercase: same as input” and “(nwildcard) N is treated like a wildcard” selected. Positions in the alignment with gaps in ≥ 90% of the samples were removed using trimAl (https://github.com/inab/trimal/tree/trimAl), unless this left < 60% of positions (in such case, the least gapped 60% were retained), resulting in 16 362 bases of the mitogenome.

Aligned sequences were collapsed into haplotypes using DNAsp v.6.12.03 (Rozas et al. 2017) and analyzed in PopArt v1.7 (Leigh and Bryant 2015) using a median‐joining network (Bandelt et al. 1999). The analysis, based on 15 856 bases (1390 variable sites, excluding gaps) explored maternal haplotype diversity following the nomenclature described by Duleba et al. (2015) and Pilot et al. (2010). Sub‐haplogroups were identified using MitoToolPy v1.0 (Peng et al. 2015).

Genetic diversity indices, including haplotype diversity (Hd) and nucleotide diversity (Pi), were calculated for each population using DNAsp. Additionally, an analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) was performed in Arlequin, excluding gaps and grouping samples by breed, except the Portuguese Water Dog, which were divided by geographic origin of the samples.

Phylogenetic Analyses of Autosomal Data

Following the FAO guidelines (Ajmone‐Marsan et al. 2023), PLINK was used for quality filtering of the initial dataset of 12 782 087 variants identified in 46 samples from 10 Portuguese breeds (n = 2–6 per breed). Non‐autosomal SNPs were removed, and autosomal SNPs were additionally filtered out when the missing genotype rate was > 10%. Individuals with call rates < 75% were removed, followed by filtering of SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 1%, as well as those deviating from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) (p < 0.001). SNPs in linkage disequilibrium were pruned using a sliding window of 50 markers, a step size of 5 markers and pairwise r2 threshold of 0.2 (−‐indep‐pairwise 50 5 0.2). Pairwise kinship coefficients were estimated using the KING method (Manichaikul et al. 2010) implemented in PLINK v2.0. Pairs with kinship coefficients > 0.177, indicative of first‐degree relationships (parent‐offspring/full siblings) were identified and one individual from each pair was removed.

Phylogenetic relationships among the Portuguese breeds were investigated using a NeighborNet graph inferred from pairwise Nei's genetic distances (Nei 1972), calculated for WGS data using the R package StAMPP v1.6.3 (Pembleton et al. 2013). Only breeds with a minimum of three samples (n = 42) were included, since smaller sample sizes decrease the precision of allelic frequencies estimates, which can inflate genetic distance measures in population‐level analysis (Ajmone‐Marsan et al. 2023). The resulting distance matrix was used to construct the network using Splitstree v4.14.2 (Huson and Bryant 2006).

For the phylogenetic analysis of SNP‐merged data, a maximum of 10 randomly selected samples per study per breed was established to avoid size/source‐related bias, resulting in a dataset of 1715 canids. A pairwise identity‐by‐state (IBS) genetic distance matrix for each pair of individuals based on the average proportion of shared alleles was computed using PLINK (−‐distance 1‐ibs square) and 100 bootstrap resampling with replacement. A rooted Neighbor Joining tree (NJ tree) was built with PHYLIP v3.698 software package (Felsenstein 1989) using the golden jackal (Canis aureus Linnaeus, 1758) as an outgroup. The phylogenetic tree was visualized and edited using FigTree v1.4.4 (Rambaut 2018).

The merging accuracy could be further confirmed by the phylogenetic clustering of these breeds consistently with what we obtained for WGS data. We detected some mislabelled samples (n = 11) in the public repositories which were removed from the analysis resulting in a dataset of 1704 samples. A new IBS Neighbor Joining tree was generated (using the same settings). Individuals were assigned to a breed if they clustered together in > 90% of resamplings. Breed groups were defined when two or more breeds shared the same branch in > 50% of resamplings.

Genomic Diversity

Quality control criteria used to analyse phylogenetic relationships were applied for genomic diversity, with the exception of linkage pruning.

Genomic diversity indices for the Portuguese dog breeds, such as nucleotide diversity (π), observed (H o) and expected heterozygosities (H e) were estimated from WGS data using VCFtools v0.1.16 (Danecek et al. 2011). Only breeds with ≥ 3 samples were included to avoid underestimating heterozygosity (Ajmone‐Marsan et al. 2023). Nucleotide diversity (π) was estimated per population (−‐keep) as the average number of nucleotide differences per site within 10Kb windows (−‐window‐pi 10 000) across the genome. Using the function ‐‐het, H o and H e were estimated for each population (−‐keep) (see Supporting Information S1: Section S5 for the formula used to calculate individual heterozygosity rates). Breed‐level values were obtained by averaging individual metrics within each breed. For the SNP array merged data, mean heterozygosity was calculated as described above.

Principal Components Analysis

Quality control criteria used for Phylogenetic analyses were applied for Principal Components Analysis (PCA). PCA was conducted using PLINK with ‐pca. The Tracy‐Widom test (Tracy and Widom 1993) was performed using the R package “AssocTests” v.1.0–1 (Wang et al. 2020), with significance level of 0.05, to calculate the most significant principal components. Only breeds with ≥ 3 samples were included. A dataset of nine Portuguese breeds for which WGS was obtained was used to investigate population structure.

A PCA on Portuguese breeds from SNP array merged data was also obtained as described above.

Breed Differentiation

Quality control criteria used for phylogenetic analyses were applied for genomic differentiation analysis.

Genome‐wide fixation index (F ST) values were calculated following Weir and Cockerham's method (Weir and Cockerham 1984), using VCFtools with a sliding window of 10 Kb and a step size of 10 Kb (−‐weir‐fst‐pop, −‐fst‐window‐size and ‐‐fst‐window‐step). Only breeds with a minimum of three samples were used. F ST values of Portuguese breeds were also calculated using the SNP array dataset.

To further investigate the genetic differences between LGDs and herding dogs—two dog types that work in the same environment (rural pastures/sheep flocks)—an F ST analysis was performed using WGS data. SNPs with an F ST > 0.65, representing potentially functional genomic variants, were retained, and candidate genes located within 1 Mb upstream or downstream of these SNPs were identified using GALLO v1.5 (Fonseca et al. 2020) with gene annotation information from the NCBI annotation release 95. The biological process, cell components, and molecular function of these candidate genes were inferred through Gene Ontology terms (Ashburner et al. 2000) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (Kanehisa and Goto 2000), and enrichment analyses were conducted using Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID, v.2023q4) (Huang et al. 2009; Sherman et al. 2022).

Runs of Homozygosity (ROH) Analysis

Quality control criteria used to estimate genomic diversity were applied for runs of homozygosity, with the exception of HWE filtering. Following Meyermans et al. (2020), neither MAF nor LD pruning was applied before ROH calculation. Estimates of ROH for each Portuguese dog breed based on WGS data were performed for breeds with ≥ 3 samples.

To detect ROH lengths and frequency, a sliding‐window approach using PLINK was performed with the following parameters: homozyg‐density 50, ‐‐homozyg‐gap 100, ‐‐homozyg‐kb 250, ‐‐homozyg‐snp 55, ‐‐homozyg‐window‐snp 55, ‐‐homozyg‐window‐het 1, ‐‐homozyg‐window‐missing 5. ROH lengths were grouped per breed into three classes, where the expected ROH length reflects the number of generations since the underlying IBD haplotypes last shared a common ancestor, using the formula from Stoffel et al. (2021): (100/(2 × g))cM/0.97 cM/Mb, where g is the number of generations and 0.97 is the sex‐averaged genome‐wide recombination rate in dogs (Campbell et al. 2016). Segments of ROH were classified as follows: small, < 1718.2 Kb (ancestry > 30 generations ago); medium, 1718.2–5154.6 Kb (~30–10 generations ago); and long, > 5154.6 Kb (< 10 generations ago).

ROH‐based inbreeding coefficients (F ROH) were calculated following McQuillan et al. (2008), where the total length of ROH per animal, above 250 Kb, was divided by the total length of the autosomal genome covered by SNPs, herein 2 203 764 000 bp. Breed‐level F ROH was calculated by averaging individual F ROH values within each breed.

For the SNP array data, F ROH values were calculated following the same approach as described above for WGS data with the following parameters: homozyg‐density 20, homozyg‐gap 1000, homozyg‐kb 250, homozyg‐snp 45, homozyg‐window‐het 1, homozyg‐window‐missing 5, and homozyg‐window‐snp 45. Inbreeding was also calculated for WGS and SNP array data using the method‐of‐moments F (F MOM)– see Supporting Information S1: Section S6 for more details.

A more detailed description on the detection of ROH segments can be found in the Supporting Information S1: Section S7.

Model‐Based Clustering Analysis

The SNP array dataset was used to carry out a model‐based clustering analysis with Admixture v.1.3.0 (Alexander and Lange 2011) and calculate individual ancestry proportions, with five replicates per K with a random seed number and default settings. For the Portuguese dataset, Admixture for K values from K = 2 to K = 15 was obtained (K = 15 for accommodating 13 populations plus two more, considering the potential intrabreed structure). For a global dataset, values of K = 2 to K = 33 were obtained by using all the Portuguese native breeds as well as those identified through IBD haplotype sharing analysis. The results were analyzed and visualized using the CLUMPAK software (Kopelman et al. 2015) to determine the best K based on the method of (Evanno et al. 2005). The Admixture output was plotted using the script AdmixturePlotter.R (https://github.com/TCLamnidis/AdmixturePlotter/blob/master/AdmixturePlotter.R).

Identity‐By‐Descent (IBD) Haplotype Sharing Analysis

The SNP‐merged dataset used to construct the phylogenetic tree described above was used for an IBD haplotype sharing analysis. Pairwise‐individual IBD segments were detected using Beagle v4.1 (Browning and Browning 2013) without imputation, on 1000 SNPs windows, considering 100 SNPs overlap and 10 SNPs end trimming. A minimum IBD segment length of 0.25 cM (~0.25 Mb) was used, assuming that 1 cM corresponds to ~1 Mb in dogs (Campbell et al. 2016).

Using custom scripts previously applied in published studies (e.g., Buckley et al. 2025; Meadows et al. 2023; Parker et al. 2017), total shared IBD segment lengths were calculated for all dog pairs from different breeds, assigning a length of 0 to pairs with no sharing. The median lengths of shared IBD were determined for each breed. To define a significance threshold for haplotype sharing across clades, the mean and standard deviation of total IBD segment lengths between pairs of dogs from different clades were calculated, excluding breeds which were not assigned to a clade. Then, the significance threshold was determined by adding the mean to twice the standard deviation. Breed‐level medians exceeding the 95th percentile (51.7 Mb) were considered significant; within‐breed IBD was also calculated. For breed pairs with median sharing above the 95th percentile, circle plots were generated using Circos v0.69–8 (Krzywinski et al. 2009).

TreeMix Analysis

The TreeMix v.1.1 (Pickrell and Pritchard 2012) software was used to investigate evidence of gene flow between Portuguese dog breeds. To accurately estimate allele frequencies, all sites with missing data were removed. A cluster‐stratified frequency for each marker was calculated using PLINK with the options ‐‐freq and ‐‐family.

To account for LD and considering that SNP density in the SNP array data is on average 1SNP/20Kb, SNPs were clustered into groups of 300 using the flag “‐k”, exceeding typical LD lengths in modern dogs (Yang et al. 2019). To assess confidence in the inferred tree, blocks of 300 SNPs were resampled with replacement using the flag ‐‐bootstrap.

Maximum likelihood (ML) trees allowing one to N migrations (m), where N is the total number of populations, were generated 10 times per edge count using random seeds (−seed). Standard errors of migration rates were calculated with the “‐se” option, and the “‐global” option was used to optimize the migration network. The Golden Jackal was used as an outgroup.

The OptM R package v0.1.8 (Fitak 2021) was used to estimate the optimal number of migration edges. Then, 1000 bootstrap replicates of the best m were run to ensure statistical power using a script from the R (v4.4.1) package BITEV2 v2.1.2 (Milanesi et al. 2017). Functions treemix.bootstrap, plot_resid and treemix.drift from the R package BITEV2 were used to plot the tree graph, residuals' error and drift for the optimal migration, respectively.

Results and Discussion

In this study, 46 genome‐wide sequence data (29 males, 17 females), from 10 Portuguese dog breeds were generated with an average depth of 12x (9–16x) (Tables S1 and S2, Figure S4a,b). A total set of 12 782 087 high‐quality autosomal SNPs—mostly intergenic (48.0%) and intronic (37.4%)—were selected for subsequent analyses. For more details on these SNPs genome distributions and their effects see Figure S5 and Table S8. The total number of SNPs per breed ranged from ~3.6 M in Portuguese Pointing Dog (mean coverage = 11x) to ~4.1 M in Estrela Mountain Dog (mean coverage = 13x) (Figure S6). While the ANOVA indicated a significant overall difference in breed averages (p = 0.02), only the pairwise comparison between these two breeds mentioned above was considered statistically significant (p = 0.04). The Portuguese Pointing Dog showed the lowest number of singleton SNPs (~1 M) and small‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound had the greatest value (~2.5 M) (Figure S7).

Within the Portuguese context, 26 mitotypes were described based on 15 956 nucleotide positions, from 49 dogs. From these, 21 mitotypes were breed‐specific (Figure S8, Table S9). Figure S9 shows the high density of polymorphic sites present within the D‐loop region.

Mitogenome Diversity Distributed Within Dog Haplogroups A to D

Mitogenomic variability was distributed among the dog haplogroups A, B, C and D (Figure S8). Haplogroup A was the most frequent (68.2%), followed by haplogroup B (13.6%). Haplogroups C and D were equally represented (9.1%). These frequencies were consistent with those reported for dogs worldwide (Meadows et al. 2023). Haplogroups E and F (described in Duleba et al. 2015; Quan Ke Thai et al. 2023) were not observed in the Portuguese breeds, confirming previous results (Pires et al. 2006).

Haplogroup A had representatives from all Portuguese breeds. The Portuguese Pointing dog was monomorphic (Figure S10) and shared its unique haplotype with two individuals of the Algarve Barrocal breed in Haplogroup A (Figure S8, Table S9). The Portuguese Water Dog formed an isolated branch within Haplogroup B, containing the only breed‐specific haplotype which is shared by the United States (US) and Portuguese (PT) populations.

The AMOVA tests revealed significant mitogenomic structure among Portuguese dog breeds (average F ST 26.75%, p < 0.01) due to the presence of breed‐specific haplotypes (Table S9), although some haplotypes were shared between breeds of distinct functional groups, for example, Portuguese Sheepdog, Saint Miguel Cattle Dog and Alentejo Mastiff within Haplogroup C, and Saint Miguel Cattle Dog and Portuguese Warren Hound within Haplogroup A (see Figure S8).

A more detailed description regarding the diversity of the mitogenomic composition of these breeds, including sub‐haplogroup identification, is available in the Supporting Information S1: Section S8.

Limited Inbreeding and Moderate Levels of Autosomal Genomic Diversity

The number and length of ROH segments were identified and analyzed to quantify the degree of inbreeding and interpret breed histories (Table S10). Genomic inbreeding (F ROH) values reflect the observed patterns of ROH fragments. F ROH was determined for each breed and independently for WGS and SNP array data (Table S11).

A total of 16 012 ROH fragments (> 250 Kb) across the autosomes of 42 dogs, with an average number of 381 ROH segments per individual and an average total ROH length per genome of 279.79 Mb (Figure 2a,b, Table S10) was identified. A detailed description of the number and total length of ROH segments is provided in the Supporting Information S1: Section S9.

The ROH fragments were classified into three length classes (X < 1.7 Mb, 1.7 < X < 5.2 Mb, X > 5.2 Mb) (Figure 2c) to understand how the ROH lengths could reflect distinct historical and biological processes. Most of the detected ROH (91.81%), were shorter than 1.7 Mb. Despite using different parameters to define a ROH, (Sams and Boyko 2019) observed that popular breeds tend to have a higher proportion of the genome with longer ROHs. In the Portuguese breeds, the skewed distribution of ROH towards shorter lengths could be explained by several factors: (a) the small population size of these breeds (rarity) and the associated low number of breeding females (risk status; see “Risk classification” in Supporting Information S1: Section S10; Table S12); (b) the occurrence of past demographic bottlenecks (Figure S11); and (c) possible matings between distant shared relatives, that is, deeper parental relatedness (Sams and Boyko 2019).

Figure 3a,b shows that approximately 5%–25% of the genomes of Portuguese breeds are covered by ROH, with the cumulative length in ROH ranging from 0.25 (all Portuguese breeds) to 21.6 Mb (Algarve Barrocal Dog, Castro Laboreiro Watchdog and Portuguese Water Dog). The almost total absence of long ROH (> 5.2 Mb) (Figure 2c) is indicative that recent inbreeding is of little or low concern in Portuguese dog breeds.

WGS‐based F ROH values indicated that the Portuguese breeds exhibit low to moderate inbreeding levels, with an average of 0.13 ± 0.10. These values ranged from 0.06 ± 0.05 for the Alentejo Mastiff to 0.26 ± 0.16 for the medium/large‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound (Figure 3c). Five breeds exhibited above‐average inbreeding values: the medium/large‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound dogs, the Portuguese Water Dog (F ROH = 0.20 ± 0.12), the Portuguese Pointing Dog (F ROH = 0.16 ± 0.10), the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog (F ROH = 0.16 ± 0.11), and the Algarve Barrocal Dog (F ROH = 0.13 ± 0.05), whereas the remaining breeds exhibited inbreeding values below the average for the Portuguese breeds (F ROH = 0.13) (Figure 3c). Despite these differences, no statistically significant differences were detected between any pair of breeds (Tukey's HSD test, p > 0.05 for all comparisons). A previous study (Meadows et al. 2023) presented higher F ROH averages than the observed here, although less Portuguese breeds were analyzed (e.g., Portuguese Water Dog_US population F ROH = 0.28 ± 0.08; Portuguese Sheepdog F ROH = 0.27 ± 0.04; Estrela Mountain Dog F ROH = 0.18 ± 0.023; Saint Miguel Cattle Dog F ROH = 0.19 ± 0.005; medium/large‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound F ROH = 0.30 ± 0.08; small‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound F ROH = 0.21 ± 0.02). These comparisons should be interpreted with caution since the use of different parameters may influence the results.

Genomic inbreeding estimated using WGS‐based F ROH and WGS‐based F MOM methods showed a positive and significant correlation (for more details on WGS‐based F MOM method and its correlation with the WGS‐based F ROH see Supporting Information S1: Section S11). The fact that these breeds are managed as not‐closed breeding populations explains the low to moderate inbreeding levels detected.

For the SNP array data and by including data from public sources, the sampling size for 7 Portuguese breeds could be increased (Table S5), including representatives of the Transmontano Mastiff (n = 3) and a sub‐population of the Portuguese Water Dog sampled in the US (n = 10). The F ROH values ranged from 0.02 ± 0.002 for the Transmontano Mastiff to 0.27 ± 0.06 for the Portuguese Water Dog US sub‐population, with an average F ROH across breeds of 0.14. Across breeds, SNP array‐based F R OH values were highly correlated with those from WGS‐based F ROH (R pearson = 0.84, p = 0.004, Figure S12). A worldwide breed comparison using the SNP array data confirmed the lower inbreeding values observed for the Portuguese dog populations (Figure S13).

The highest inbreeding levels (WGS‐based F ROH = 0.26 ± 0.16; SNP array‐based F ROH = 0.23 ± 0.15) and wide variance observed in the medium/large‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound breed are probably related to a severe population decline between the 1940s and 1980s due to the abandonment of big game hunting activities. An effort to recover this morphotype relied on the few individuals that remained (Veiga 2001).

Moderate inbreeding values were detected in the Portuguese Water Dogs sampled in Portugal (WGS‐based F ROH = 0.20 ± 0.12; SNP array‐based F ROH = 0.21 ± 0.12) which is likely due to a bottleneck that this population suffered from the 1950s onwards, being considered in 1970 the rarest dog breed in the world (Vasconcelos 1995). Based on SNP‐genotypes, the highest F ROH value was detected in the US population of Portuguese Water Dogs (SNP array F ROH mean 0.27 ± 0.06), whose first litter was born in 1971 (Molinari 1993). This breed became very popular in the US, its population is nowadays larger than the Portuguese one, but its morphotype diverged from the original working type (Molinari 1993; Vasconcelos 1995). The higher inbreeding of the US population of Portuguese Water Dogs elevates the risk of genetic drift, and thus the risk of losing breed‐specific genetic variants. Accordingly, SNP array‐based heterozygosity values were smaller for the US population (H o/H e = 0.35 ± 0.03/0.37 ± 0.0001) compared to the Portuguese population (H o/H e = 0.45 ± 0.08/0.47 ± 0.001) (Table S11).

The Portuguese Pointing Dog specimens also exhibited moderate levels of inbreeding (WGS‐based F ROH mean of 0.16 ± 0.10; SNP array F ROH mean of 0.21 ± 0.05). As shown in Figure 2c, this breed showed a high frequency of short ROH fragments and a high partition of short ROH fragments contributing to inbreeding (Figure S14). This suggests a deeper parental relatedness, that is, mating among related individuals several generations ago of the present‐day dogs, rather than recent inbreeding. The breed experienced different periods of decline, nearly to extinction in the early 20th century, followed by a recovery from the few individuals remaining. Another significant decline of its population size occurred in the 1950s when only one breeder maintained these animals, before being recovered again and turning into a very popular breed up to 1974 (Rodrigues 1993). This ROH pattern is compatible with such demographic fluctuations, dating to ~30 generations ago (or ~90 years ago) (Figure 2c). Recently, the L.O.P book shows instability and a decreasing trend in the number of registrations over the last ~20 years (Figure S11).

Castro Laboreiro Watchdog also showed moderate levels of inbreeding (WGS‐based F ROH = 0.16 ± 0.11; SNP array‐based F ROH = 0.15 ± 0.09) and a large variance, which can be explained by its population history—a breed known to have been maintained in a very isolated geographic region, the Minho region, in the Northwest of Portugal, with a small population size over the years. Additionally, the presence of distinct lineages—show dogs vs. rural dogs, can possibly contribute to the large variance of F ROH observed. A signal related to a within‐breed population structure was also detected in the Admixture analysis (see below), potentially related to the differentiation of these lineages.

The Algarve Barrocal Dog ROH‐based metrics were consistent with the average values found for other Portuguese dog breeds (Figures 2a,b and 3c). This breed has existed in the Algarve region and probably originated from Sighthounds and primitive type dogs (Clube Português de Canicultura 2014), nearly went extinct in the late 1990s when international hunting dog breeds became more appreciated, and were introduced in Portugal from the 1970s onwards. An effort to recover this breed was made from the remaining specimens which were used for subsistence hunting.

Observed and expected heterozygosity values based on WGS data were lowest for the medium/large‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound dogs (H o = 0.30 ± 0.08 and H e = 0.37 ± 0.04 × 10−3), whereas the highest values were observed for the Portuguese Pointing Dog (H o = 0.42 ± 0.04 and H e = 0.43 ± 1.55 × 10−3) and the Algarve Barrocal Dog (H o = 0.43 ± 0.02 and H e = 0.42 ± 0.83 × 10−3) (Figure 4, Table S11). The apparent paradox of the Portuguese Pointing Dog showing inbreeding and heterozygosity at a moderate level can be explained by its demographic history. As previously mentioned, a significant bottleneck occurred in the early 20th century, when the population was small and largely confined to the northern part of Portugal. This was likely followed by the breed recovery from a low‐numbered, but possibly genetically diverse, remaining population.

A higher frequency of homozygotes (lower H o) is expected in sub‐structured populations—the Wahlund effect (Wahlund 1928). In the case of the medium/large‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound, such deviation could be a sign of a sub‐structure caused by the presence of two different body size classes (Figure 4). For the Portuguese Water Dog—Portuguese population, the deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium may be due to the strong bottleneck that the breed suffered ~70 years ago. This may have severely affected the distribution of the alleles (Figure 4).

Based on SNP array data, for which information from more breeds is available, the Portuguese breeds showed similar average heterozygosity values (0.41 vs. 0.38 in the global dataset) and lower average inbreeding (0.14 vs. 0.26 in the global dataset) (Figure S13). As expected, in the global dataset, heterozygosity and inbreeding were significantly and negatively correlated (R Spearman = −0.48, p = 5.82 × 10−13), indicating that higher heterozygosity is associated with lower inbreeding. If considering only Portuguese breeds, this correlation was also negative, but not statistically significant (R Spearman = −0.50, p = 0.10).

Population pairwise F ST values based on WGS and SNP array data are described in Tables S13 and S14. Based on WGS data, shallow to moderate mean F ST values were observed among Portuguese breeds, ranging from 0.02 ± 0.01 in Alentejo Mastiff to 0.07 ± 0.02 in Portuguese Pointing Dog (average pairwise F ST across Portuguese breeds = 0.07) (Figure 4, Figure S15). SNP array data revealed similar patterns, ranging from 0.04 ± 0.03 in both Alentejo Mastiff and Transmontano Mastiff to 0.12 ± 0.02 in the Portuguese Water Dog_US population (average pairwise F ST across Portuguese breeds = 0.04) (Figure S16).

A collection of 13 potentially functional SNPs (F ST > 0.65) differentiating Herding and LGDs dogs was identified (Table S15). The genes that harboured these SNPs were associated with glycerolipid metabolism, including diacylglycerol and triacylglycerol biosynthesis. Such differences likely reflect adaptations in energy storage for LGDs—relatively sedentary, and lipid‐mediated signalling for Herding dogs—fast reactions, cognitive flexibility, and fine motor coordination, consistent with their different working functions (Figure S17, Table S16).

In summary, Portuguese native dog breeds—as functional breeds still used to perform specific tasks (with the exception of the Portuguese Water Dog)—showed low to moderate inbreeding and greater heterozygosity than other breeds, likely due to the current breeding management with recruitment of new breeding animals (new genetic inputs), beyond the registered population, due to a selection based on performance rather than aesthetic aspects, which according to Pedersen et al. (2013), tends to maintain genetic diversity. Some breeders also refer to attempts to cross bloodlines within breeds to retain genetic diversity. Chu et al. (2019) reported a reduction of approximately one puppy in litter size with every 10% increase in F ROH. Inbreeding can occur in small populations and is almost unavoidable in closed breeding populations, with insidious consequences.

Population Structure Reflects Breeds´ Classification

In the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) based on WGS (Figure 5a, Figure S18a), PC1 and PC2 explained 6.25% and 6.02% of the variance, respectively. These percentages are low and not statistically significant (p‐value 0.61 and 0.10031, respectively; Table S17), as expected in genome‐wide data with many loci contributing minimally. The explained variance is therefore limited. A separation of the most differentiated breeds is observed. PC1 allowed for the separation of the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog breed. PC2 allowed for the separation of the Portuguese Sheepdog, Castro Laboreiro Watchdog, and two hunting dog breeds—Portuguese Pointing Dog and Algarve Barrocal Dog—from the others. The segregation of these breeds was related to their higher mean F ST values based on WGS data (≥ 5%; Figure S15, Table S13), but also their moderate inbreeding (≥ 10%; Figure S18b). Additionally, PC2 captured the sub‐structure within the Portuguese Warren Hound dogs related to body size (height at withers), separating the large and medium‐sized dogs from the small ones. A central cluster of breeds was resolved after the removal of the most isolated breeds, allowing for the separation of the Portuguese Water Dog, Saint Miguel Cattle Dog, and small‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound (Figure S19a,b, Table S18).

A complementary analysis based on the 140 k SNPs (Figure 5b, Figure S20a) allowed for additional resolution. PC1 accounted for 12.9% of the genomic variation (p‐value < 0.050063, statistically significant; Table S19) displaying the isolation of the Portuguese Water Dog, especially the US population, while PC2 explained 9.22% of the variation (p = 0.0008, statistically significant; Table S19) confirming the isolation of the Portuguese Sheepdog breed. The isolation of these breeds in the PCA likely reflects their inbreeding (> 0.18; Figure S20b), as well as a founder effect and genetic drift (for the case of the Portuguese Water Dog US population). A second PCA, excluding these highly differentiated breeds, improved resolution among these centrally‐grouped breeds (Figure S21a,b, Table S20), allowing for the separation of the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog, small‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound and Estrela Mountain Dog.

Although the SNP array‐based PCA explained a higher percentage of genomic variation, the greater number of markers in the WGS‐based PCA provided a better resolution for separating the Portuguese breeds.

The PCA analysis is a non‐model, exploratory method with few assumptions, but its results can be confounded by the sampling size or demographic factors (McVean 2009; Novembre and Stephens 2008). These methods lack a genetic model with biologically meaningful parameters, relying on generic statistics (Patterson et al. 2006; Price et al. 2006), making their results difficult to interpret. To address these limitations, the PCA can be combined with model‐based methods, such as Admixture to investigate if the observed patterns can be explained by demographic processes.

Although the Portuguese breeds exhibited a shallow genetic differentiation (average F ST = 0.07 for WGS and F ST = 0.04 for SNP array data), breed‐specific genomic patterns, that is, genetic signatures related to ancestry patterns and selective breeding history were evident (Figure 5c). At K = 3, the optimal number of clusters inferred (Figure S22), the Portuguese Sheepdog and the Portuguese Water Dog showed early differentiation, consistent with the PCA based on SNP‐array data. These two breeds also exhibit some of the highest inbreeding and F ST values based on the SNP array data: Portuguese Sheepdog (F ROH = 0.18, F ST = 0.09); Portuguese Water Dog—Portuguese population (F ROH = 0.21, F ST = 0.07); and Portuguese Water Dog—US population (F ROH = 0.27, F ST = 0.12) (Table S11).

The Admixture analysis indicates the intra‐breed structure seen in the Portuguese Warren Hound breed (K = 4) (see PCA analysis above, the phylogenetic tree and haplotype sharing below). Veiga (2001) argued that the medium and large‐size types have derived slightly from an original hound‐type, while the small‐sized type has suffered more influences from exogenous breeds. It is also noteworthy the strict definition for the small‐sized upper limits for the height at withers, preventing crosses to occur (sexual isolation) with the medium and large‐sized (for these latter and for the withers height parameter, the breed standard describes two contiguous intervals), and therefore increasing its differentiation.

Among the Portuguese LGDs, the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog differentiated from the others at K = 5. With origins in a very remote region in the north‐west of Portugal, in the Minho region, this breed participated in more restricted and regional shepherding routes and has always been more isolated (Geraldes 1996), both geographically and genetically. Several analyses concurred in showing that within the LGDs, the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog shows a distinct genomic profile (see PCA analysis above and phylogenetic tree below).

At K = 7 the hunting breed Portuguese Pointing Dog formed its own cluster, followed by the Saint Miguel Cattle Dog (K = 8) (from the Azores Archipelago) and the Algarve Barrocal Dog (K = 9). The Portuguese Pointing Dog and Algarve Barrocal Dog maintained highly homogeneous admixture proportions up to K = 15.

Evidence for intra‐breed population structure was detected in the Portuguese Water Dog (K = 2), splitting the breed into two distinct clusters—the Portuguese and the US populations—which are geographically and genetically isolated populations. This differentiation is probably due to a founder effect and the high inbreeding that followed the establishment of the US population (see section Limited inbreeding and moderate levels of autosomal genomic diversity). This result was already evident by the PCA analysis (Figure 5b) and is corroborated by the Treemix (Figure S23).

At higher Ks, from K = 10 onwards, an intra‐breed stratification was observed and may reflect the existence of two breeding groups. It was possible to identify the cause for such signal: for the case of the Estrela Mountain Dog (K = 11), it could be an effect of a breeding lineage associated with the hair type (Table S21), and in the case of the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog (K = 12) to the origin of the specimens, that is, pedigree dogs (show dogs) versus working dogs (mainly rural dogs) (Table S2).

Noteworthy was the heterogeneous pattern shared by the Alentejo Mastiff and Transmontano Mastiff breeds evidencing an admixed ancestry and a close evolutionary affinity (SNP array mean F ST ~0; Table S14), in addition to their similarity in terms of morphology (conformation) and behaviour (function).

A global admixture (Figure S24) is provided in the Supporting Information S1: Section S12.

Patterns of Shared Haplotypes in Portuguese Dog Breeds

The IBS genetic distance estimated from 140 426 SNPs in 1693 dogs (213 breeds) and 11 wild canids was used to build a Neighbour‐Joining tree which displayed 26 well‐supported clades (Figure 6a, Figure S25, Table S22). Each multi‐breed clade comprised two to 18 breeds that clustered together with > 50% bootstrap support (82% on average; exception of the small‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound dogs, Fonni's dog, Mannara's dog, Miniature Pinscher and Catahoula Leopard Dog, that were assigned to a clade with a support < 50%). The combined analysis of the data generated in this study, including published datasets on other worldwide dogs (Tables S3 and S4) allowed for the refinement of the dog cladistic relationships. Compared to the phylogeny of Parker et al. (2017), six new clades emerged and three regrouped with others. Nine out of 11 Portuguese breeds were assigned to six distinct clades—Water Dogs, Pointers and Setters, Iberian Livestock Guarding Dogs, Sighthounds and related breeds, Continental herders and Terrier. These clades comprised six to 15 breeds that clustered with > 60% support, (90% support on average, exception of the small‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound dogs that were assigned to the Terrier clade with a support of 48%), reflecting shared function (e.g., Pointers) and/or morphology (e.g., Terrier), and/or geography (e.g., Iberian Livestock Guarding Dogs). The Saint Miguel Cattle Dog and Terceira Cattle Dog (represented by a single individual) could not be assigned to any clade but clustered together with American dog breeds (e.g., Xoloitzcuintle). Eight out of 11 breeds, (except for the Terceira Cattle Dog, Transmontano Mastiff and Alentejo Mastiff) formed well‐supported monophyletic clusters (> 90%), indicating that they are genetically differentiated. The mentioned individuals that were not grouped by breed, often clustered with breeds from the same geographical region and/or function, for example, Transmontano Mastiff close to the Spanish Mastiff.

(2017), the addition of the Portuguese breeds rearranged the cladistic relationships, creating 6 new groups—Water Dogs, Shepherds and related breeds, Sighthounds and related breeds, Iberian Livestock Guardian Dogs, German Spitz and Bichons—and three groups—New World, Poodle and Small Spitz—were regrouped with others. Black branches represent wild canids. Asterisk identifies those clades that include Portuguese native dog breeds. The Portuguese Pointing Dog, the Algarve Barrocal Dog and the medium/large‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound, were the only Portuguese breeds showing significant haplotype sharing with other breeds exclusively within their own clade. The full name of each breed is listed in Table S5.

IBD Haplotype sharing was also estimated from 140 426 SNPs and calculated between all pairs of dogs from 213 breeds and 11 wild canids (Figure 6b). Haplotype sharing graphs for each Portuguese breed are available in the supplemental material (Figures S26–S29). Considering a minimum of haplotypes > 250 Kb, the total length of the shared haplotypes was summed for each pair of dogs. Only in Portuguese breeds assigned to a clade, individuals from within the same clade share significantly nearly three times more of their genome in IBD than individuals across clades (median shared haplotype lengths of 28.7 Mb and 9.3 Mb, respectively; Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p < 2.2 × 10−16; Figure S30), as expected since individuals from the same clade share a common ancestor. The median within‐breed value for the Portuguese dogs (Figure S31a, Table S23) ranged from 45.2 Mb in the Alentejo Mastiff, probably related to its heterogeneous ancestry, to 1751.5 Mb in the Portuguese Water Dog_US, much lower when compared to the global dataset analyzed here (26.1 Mb to 6127.3 Mb; Table S23) The median across‐breed value for the Portuguese dogs (Figure S31b, Table S24) ranged from 5.7 Mb in the Algarve Barrocal Dog to 16.4 Mb in the Portuguese Water Dog_US. These results are in accordance with the lower inbreeding levels observed in the Portuguese dog breeds, as less inbred breeds have less within breed haplotype sharing.

Nine cases of significant haplotype sharing links were detected across clades between apparently distantly related breeds such as the Portuguese Water Dog (with the clades “Shepherds and related breeds” and “Continental Herder”), Portuguese Sheepdog (“Mastiff”, “Shepherds and related breeds”, “Water Dogs” and “Iberian Livestock Guarding Dogs”), small‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound (“Toy Spitz”) and Livestock Guarding Dogs (“Continental Herder” and “Shepherds and related breeds”) (Figure 6b). Considering only significant median total haplotype sharing links (> 51.7 Mb; Table S25, see Figure S32 for per‐breed values) involving Portuguese breeds assigned to a clade, no significant difference in haplotype sharing was observed between within clade and across clade breed links (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, p = 0.151), despite the different number of links (median shared haplotype lengths of 88.9 Mb (24 links) and 73.5 Mb (30 links), respectively).

The Portuguese LGDs grouped together with other breeds of the same type of function/behaviour and geography, such as the Pyrenean mastiff, Spanish Mastiff and Great Pyrenees, forming the Iberian Livestock Guarding Dogs' clade, reflecting regional adaptation (e.g., climate, orography, pathogens) and a close evolutionary history. These dogs traditionally accompanied humans and livestock (mainly sheep for wool production) during historical seasonal transhumance migrations, by foot, across Iberia, primarily to protect the flocks from wolf attacks (Carreira dos Santos 2025; Carvalho and Marques 2020; Coppinger and Coppinger 2002; Mier and Tente 2018), often mating with other dogs from different regions/owners. Coutinho‐Lima et al. (2024) detected a close genetic relationship among 36 LGDs breeds across Eurasia suggesting a shared ancestry and a high level of gene‐flow among them. The mentioned transhumance migrations ended in 1999 in Portugal (Oliveira Cruz 2007). Presently, the Transmontano Mastiff and the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog are the only breeds that still co‐exist with the Iberian wolf, in the sense that they share a geographical range. For these two breeds, dogs are selected mainly on the basis of functional parameters, since their activity for flock protection against predators is still important.

The genetic and geographic isolation, already seen in the PCA (Figure 5a) and admixture (Figure 5c) of the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog within the Portuguese LGDs was confirmed by the phylogeny (node support of 50%–69%; Figure 6a) and NeighborNet (Figure S33). As evidenced by the haplotype sharing analysis (Figure 6b) this breed did not share any significant (> 51.7 Mb) haplotypes with the other Iberian LGDs breeds (a) high haplotype sharing with Transmontano Mastiff was detected, although not significant (< 51.7 Mb; Figure S26b). A few cases of inter‐clade significant haplotype sharing (> 51.7 Mb) with breeds of the “Shepherds and related breeds” cluster, such as the German Shepherd, were detected (Figure 6b, Figure S26b). However, more recent admixture between Castro Laboreiro Watchdog and German Shepherd was not detected (Figure S24a). The introduction of exogenous dogs into the region (e.g., German Shepherd dogs) by local people that emigrated to other regions since the 19th century (Geraldes 1996), and returned, could possibly justify the result found.

The Estrela Mountain Dog, the Alentejo Mastiff and Transmontano Mastiff clustered more closely, reflecting historical contact during transhumance routes. The Estrela Mountain dog and Transmontano Mastiff are more adapted to mountainous areas in the centre and north‐east of Portugal, respectively, and the Alentejo Mastiff to the plains, in the south. The Estrela Mountain Dog was the only breed that did not show significant haplotype sharing with other breeds in the dataset (Figure 6b). Nevertheless, the Treemix analysis (Figure S23) suggested significant gene flow between Estrela Mountain Dog and Transmontano Mastiff (jackknife estimate of the migration weight of 33.9% ± 18.1%, p < 0.05), likely restricted to the Estrela Mountain Dog short‐hair variety dogs (see Admixture) which is the variety preferred by the shepherds. In fact two intra‐breed clusters (node support of 100%), related to the different hair lengths in Estrela Mountain Dogs, were observed in the Neighbor‐Joining tree (Figure S34), supporting the Admixture results (Figure 5c) for this breed. A high but not significant sharing (< 51.7 Mb) was detected between the Estrela Mountain Dog and both the Alentejo Mastiff and the Transmontano Mastiff (Figure S26c).

The Alentejo Mastiff showed significant inter‐clade haplotype sharing (Figure 6b) and a moderate gene flow (Figure S23) with the Portuguese Sheepdog from the “Continental herders” clade (jackknife estimate of the migration weight of 20.5% ± 6.8%, p < 0.05). Although these breeds clustered in different clades (Figure 6a), in accordance with their very distinct morphological and behavioural patterns, both breeds were developed within the same environment (rural pastures/sheep flocks) and geographical area (Alentejo). In such circumstances, mating opportunities were possible. Nevertheless, no reports of intentional matings between the two breeds were found in the bibliography nor from the breeders.

A significant intra‐clade IBD sharing was detected between Transmontano Mastiff and the Spanish Mastiff Dog (Figure 6b). Coutinho‐Lima et al. (2024) also detected a closer genetic relationship between these breeds. Due to their close geographic range, these breeds may interact more often with each other, and individuals from both breeds can be seen as guardians of the same flock.

The Portuguese Sheepdog is the only sheepdog breed found in the Iberian continental flat lands. It clustered in a specific clade—the Continental herders (node support of 95%). Their two other Iberian counterparts, the Pyrenean Sheepdog and the Catalan Sheepdog, originated in the Pyrenees mountainous area, clustered together (Figure 6a). The IBD analysis showed an inter‐clade haplotype sharing with the Alentejo Mastiff (already seen in Treemix (Figure S23)) and Portuguese Water Dog (restricted to the Portuguese population, despite the TreeMix analysis (Figure S23) showing no support). According to Cabral and Romeiras (1930) the Portuguese Sheepdog could have descended from the Pyrenean Sheepdog and improved through the recruitment of the Briard, a French breed. Later, Vasconcelos (1995) suggested that the Portuguese Sheepdog could be considered a small‐sized Portuguese Water Dog, adapted to the herding task, possibly through breeding with herding breeds such as the Pyrenean Sheepdog or Catalan Sheepdog. A high level of sharing with the Pyrenean Sheepdog, Catalan Sheepdog and Briard breeds could be observed, although not significant (< 51.7 Mb; Figure S27a).

The other two herding breeds, the Saint Miguel Cattle Dog and the Terceira Cattle Dog, are insular breeds, from the Azores archipelago. Although clustering together in the phylogenetic tree (node support > 90%), they could not be assigned to any clade, likely due to the fact that they result from admixture between several Continental breeds, but have subsequently evolved in relative isolation on the islands (Amaral and Veiga 2004; Molinari 2024). The phylogeny of the Terceira Cattle Dog relies on one individual—interpretation of these results requires caution. Amaral and Veiga (2004) mentioned that the Saint Miguel Cattle Dog resulted from the crossbreeding of the Iberian mastiff type dogs (the precursors of the present‐day Livestock Guarding Dogs) as well as the Iberian Alano (extinct), resulting in a lighter type of molosser dog. Whereas Terceira Cattle Dog probably descends from medium‐sized, long‐haired herding or hunting dogs brought by European settlers, which were used to control and herd wild cattle (Oliveira 1998). By the mid‐19th century, the arrival of other foreign breeds (e.g., Dogue de Bordeaux, Mastiff and Bulldog), influenced the Saint Miguel Cattle Dog (Amaral and Veiga 2004). Our study recovered a strong signal for the Dogue de Bordeaux, English Mastiff and Bulldog breeds, although not significant (< 51.7 Mb; Figure S27b). Instead, significant IBD sharing was detected between the Saint Miguel Cattle Dog and the Great Dane and Boxer breeds, representatives of the Mastiff type dog. Based on microsatellite data, (Rocha 2002) reported genetic affinity between the Terceira Cattle Dog and the Saint Miguel Cattle Dog, which is supported by the haplotype sharing results (Figure 6b). Although these results also recovered a significant sharing between Terceira Cattle Dog and Portuguese Water Dog (both US and Portuguese population), no historical reports were found to justify this genetic relationship.

As expected, significant haplotype sharing between the small‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound dogs and the medium/large‐sized dogs was not detected (Figure 6b). The small body sized subtype dogs clustered, at the basal position, of the Terriers clade, apart from their counterparts, which grouped together in the Sighthound and related breeds clade (Figure 6a). This corroborates the within‐breed genetic sub‐structure related to body size in this breed, suggesting that the small‐sized variety has a distinct ancestry probably related to a small body type. The NeighborNet phylogeny (Figure S33), PCA (Figure 5a,b) and Admixture (Figure 5c) results recapitulate this intra‐breed structure. These results were also supported by the significant haplotype sharing between the small‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound dog with the Pug and with the Wire Fox Terrier breeds (Figure 6b). (Parker et al. 2017) highlighted the extensive influence of the Pug in many small type breeds, which can be related to the massive exportation of the Pug breed from Asia in the early 16th century (Watson 1906).

Regarding the Portuguese Water Dog, the dogs sampled in Portugal and in the United States clustered together with high statistical support, > 90%, within the Water dog clade, with significant haplotype sharing between the specimens (Figure 6b) (the highest total haplotype sharing value observed; Table S25). These two nuclei are separated for around 50 years; thus, genetic drift has accentuated their divergence (SNP array‐based F ST = 9%; Table S14). These two populations differed slightly in their patterns of significant haplotype sharing with other breeds. Both shared haplotypes with breeds within their clade, such as the Poodle varieties: Miniature, Standard (which showed higher affinity with the Portuguese population), and Toy (exclusively with the Portuguese population) (Figure 6b). However, the US population had a higher level of significant IBD sharing with a few more breeds from the Shepherds and related breeds clade (Figure S29b, Table S25).

The Portuguese Pointing Dog segregated within the “Pointers & Setters” clade and was monophyletic to the English Pointer breed with 100% node support (Figure 6a), which confirms these breeds' genetic similarity, reinforced through significant haplotype sharing (Figure 6b). The Portuguese Pointing Dog is probably a direct representative of the extinct Iberian Pointing Dog, which could have influenced the development of other European pointing breeds such as the English Pointer (with origin in the 18th century) (Rodrigues 1993). The Portuguese Pointing Dog, together with Algarve Barrocal Dog and medium/large‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound were the only Portuguese breeds showing significant haplotype sharing with breeds restricted to their own clades, indicating a close genetic pool and breed development by selection based on initial founder population.

The Barrocal Algarve Dog, clustered within the “Sighthounds and related breeds” clade, was monophyletic to the medium/large‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound. Several results concur in showing a high genetic affinity between these two breeds (see Admixture (Figure 5c), Treemix (Figure S23), Haplotype Sharing (Figure 6b) and NeighborNet (Figure S33)). It probably results from occasional crosses—undocumented by breeders since dogs from both breeds were used (and still are) in the same hunting packs. The putative influence from the Saluki breed, a potential source population for the Barrocal Algarve Dog, was not detected in this analysis.

In the present genomic study, no significant haplotype sharing between the Iberian wolf and the Portuguese native breed‐dogs was detected (Figure 6b). A previous study has reported limited hybridization between dogs and wild wolves in the Iberian Peninsula, such occurrences being mostly restricted to the edge of the wolf population distribution range and most likely with free‐ranging dogs (Lobo et al. 2023).

Genomics and multi‐analysis approaches play a valuable role in unravelling the evolutionary trajectories of domesticated animals. In summary, this study captured the genetic signatures in the genomes of the Portuguese native dog breeds and supports historical records of their origins and development, revealing distinct evolutionary processes. For instance, shared ancestry and extensive gene flow have predominated in the case of the Portuguese native LGDs, while local adaptation and geographical isolation have contributed to their differentiation. Founder effects, genetic drift, and more recent exogenous influences have marked the evolutionary history of island breeds. The Portuguese Warren Hound exhibits intra‐breed stratification due to different founder populations, exogenous influences, and sexual isolation of the most distinct small‐sized dog variety. Evidence of admixture (geneflow) could be recovered between the following breeds: the Algarve Barrocal Dog and the medium/large‐sized Portuguese Warren Hound breeds; the Alentejo Mastiff and the Portuguese Sheepdog Dog, and between the Estrela Mountain Dog and the Transmontano Mastiff. Demographic bottlenecks, founder effects, isolation by distance, genetic drift, and inbreeding are paradigmatic of the Portuguese Water Dog breed. Demographic bottlenecks, past events of inbreeding, and a complex relationship with other breeds of its own phylogenetic clade were observed in the case of the Portuguese Pointing Dog breed. The inbreeding levels in Portuguese breeds were, in general, of low concern; nevertheless, higher than 10%, and their management as open‐breeding populations should be maintained. Special actions should be implemented to retain the genomic diversity observed. To prevent further genetic differentiation between the US and Portuguese populations within the Portuguese Water Dog breed, gene flow between these groups should be promoted.

The evolutionary processes and the traditional dog selection practices, mainly based on functionality, lead to ecotypes adapted to their environments. In such circumstances, genetic variability was preserved more than homogeneity. The specific genomic compositions of the Portuguese dog breeds can be regarded as an important reservoir of genetic diversity or even a source of genetic variation for the species and their conservation should be assured. Portuguese native dog breeds are a good example that it is possible to maintain distinct and functional breeds without relying on excessive inbreeding.

Breed‐specific genetic variation in association with adaptive and selective traits is certainly relevant to be addressed in future research.

Ethics Statement

Our work analyses the genomes of healthy animals, specifically dogs. From these dogs, blood samples (2–5 mL according to the body size) were collected only once/animal by a veterinarian with owner consent. Blood samples were collected via venipuncture from the cephalic vein on the anterior paw. It took place in the dogs' environment with minimal discomfort, and the animals remained well afterwards. A few samples were collected during a clinic evaluation. This study is approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Review Body (ORBEA) of CIBIO, Portugal, under the Project code 2025‐15.
