Patriarchal dominance, employment anxiety and subjective career success among career widows in Nigeria
Authors: Happiness Ozioma Obi-Anike, Julius Ovuefeyen Edore, Ogohi Cross Daniel, Benedict Ogbemudia Imhanrenialena, Joy Ifeoma Enemuo, Divine Chikosolu Nnam, Chidinma Adanso Onyemachi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this research is to explore how patriarchal dominance relates to employment anxiety and subjective career success among career women, with a specific focus on the understudied marginalized career widows in Nigerian work settings. Further, the moderating roles of family economic condition and social support in the relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety were investigated.Design/methodology/approachWe collected primary quantitative data from 491 career widows employed in Nigerian organizations using a structured questionnaire. We used descriptive analysis to analyze the respondents’ demographic profile and deployed partial least squares structural equation modeling to test our hypotheses.ResultsThe findings suggest that patriarchal dominance significantly relates to employment anxiety among Nigerian career widows. Contrary to expectations, patriarchal dominance does not significantly relate to subjective career success. The outcomes from the moderation analysis suggest that while economic conditions significantly moderate the relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety, the relationship varies in strength and direction. For widows from low economic backgrounds, employment anxiety was found to decrease as patriarchal dominance increases. But for widows from medium economic backgrounds, the result suggests that as patriarchal dominance increases, employment anxiety increases. The positive relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety is strongest among working widows from high economic backgrounds. Conversely, no moderating role was found in patriarchal dominance’s negative relationship with employment anxiety, indicating that employment anxiety is not weaker for career widows with high social support.ConclusionThe study is the first to provide insights into how patriarchal dominance relates to employment anxiety and subjective career success and the moderating roles of family economic conditions and social support in such links among Nigerian career widows using PLS-SEM. The outcomes suggest that patriarchal dominance significantly relates to employment anxiety, with family economic conditions playing moderating roles among Nigerian career widows. The outcomes have vital implications for African governments, organizations, and practitioners, as they can be motivated to activate appropriate actions that will help emancipate career widows from the yoke of patriarchal dominance.

Introduction

It is documented that although people are aware of the rising inequalities in access to opportunities in many societal contexts, the issues of inequalities in career opportunities continue to receive blind spots from career researchers [1–3]. Patriarchy beliefs are one of the major factors that breeds inequalities against women [4, 5], particularly career opportunities in the Nigerian context [6, 7]. Patriarchy is conceptualized as an individual belief system that supports men’s domination of women both at home (private patriarchy) and in organizations (public patriarchy), and it broadly recognizes the institutional power of men, the inherent inferiority of women, and gendered domestic roles [4, 5]. It is noted in the literature that the patriarchal beliefs among Nigerians are so strong that patriarchal dominance and gender discrimination against women in all areas of life are rife [6, 7]. Reports from many qualitative research suggest that the patriarchal perceptions of women in Nigerian organizations as inferior to men in many work roles, impede females’ chances of securing decent jobs. For example, female respondents in qualitative research on Nigerian organizations narrated their personal experiences of how their applications for vacant positions were rejected based on their gender despite meeting the job specifications [6]. Another qualitative study by Adisa et al. [8] (p. 153) reported a female employee as saying, “patriarchal attitude [which happens quite often] always upsets and unsettles me.” We assume that these career experiences are capable of resulting in employment anxiety and a loss of career satisfaction among the Nigerian female workforce.

In the present study, the effects of patriarchal dominance on career women will be examined specifically among the understudied marginalized career widows in work settings. Focusing on career widows will provide a new dimension to the understanding of how patriarchy impacts career women in diverse areas of their lives. For example, many studies have provided understanding of how patriarchy impacts career women [6–8] without a specific focus on widows who are more vulnerable due to the loss of their husbands [9, 10]. Reed [10] (p. 674) specifically documented that “ambiguity remains as to whether widow employment should be celebrated or prevented given the vulnerable position of widows.” However, due to the lack of widow-specific patriarchal dominance literature, this study will mainly draw on patriarchal studies on women in general for theoretical underpinnings and hypothesis development. This is justified, as it is pointed out that the high orientation of many Nigerians towards patriarchal-induced gender-based inequalities positions patriarchal theory as a theoretical framework to provide insights into how patriarchal dominance relates to employment anxiety and subjective career success among the Nigerian female workforce [11]. Widowhood practices are a part of patriarchal dominance, as widowers are not subjected to the harmful practices widows face [9, 12]. Patriarchal theory is a feminist framework for understanding how individuals’ patriarchal values and belief systems impact women both in the homes and in organizations in diverse cultural contexts [4, 5].

Providing insights into how widows experience patriarchal dominance in employment settings (public patriarchy) is important, as the outcomes will motivate the Nigerian government and organizations to come up with policies and programs that will help women to overcome patriarchal dominance in the workplace. For example, it is argued that the labor market environment is a good example of a setting where patriarchal dominance is prevalent in Nigeria, as men have unequal advantages over women in spite of their skills and capabilities [6, 7, 11].

Patriarchy and widowhood practices in the Nigerian context

Patriarchal beliefs are rife in Nigeria, and such beliefs position men as the dominant group with greater advantages over women, who are the subordinate group in all spheres of life [13]. In Nigeria, patriarchal beliefs influence behaviors [6, 7]. Regarding work environments, patriarchal beliefs are highly rampant in Nigerian work environments, and they encourage domination of women in recruitment and allocations of job functions, as women are generally regarded as the weaker sex that is incapable of functioning in certain work roles [14]. For example, it is noted that patriarchal beliefs are so impactful in Nigerian organizations that they created a strong career dichotomy between males and females [7]. Such patriarchal beliefs are argued to possess the capability of distorting women’s career paths. It was reported that Nigerian male managers with strong patriarchal beliefs regard female workers as a gender that is supposed to be handling domestic responsibilities [7, 8, 15]. The current study focuses on how patriarchy impacts career widows in work environments, as it is noted that the work environment is a good example of settings where patriarchal dominance is perpetuated against women in Nigeria, as males have biased advantages over females despite women’s skills and capabilities [6, 16].

Aside from the patriarchal dominance women generally grapple with within Sub-Saharan African work environments, career widows also contend with patriarchal-induced widowhood stigmatization and isolation that widowers who are counterparts are not subjected to [11, 16, 17]. For instance, widowhood status often militates against widows’ reentry into the labor force, as there is a societal expectation for a woman who loses her spouse to strictly observe the cultural social isolation norms, unlike their widowers [11, 16, 17]. African traditional widowhood practices impose “social death” on women who lose their husbands to death based on forced cultural social isolation for widows, and this often results in the loss of vital social networks among them [16, 17].

Theoretical underpinning

The current study deploys patriarchal theory as a theoretical framework to provide understanding of how patriarchal dominance relates to employment anxiety and subjective career success among the Nigerian female workforce, with particular focus on the understudied young career widows. Patriarchal theory is a feminist framework for understanding how patriarchal value and belief systems impact women in diverse cultural contexts [4, 5]. Theorizing patriarchy, Walby [5] argues that patriarchy supports the domination of women and opposes egalitarian structures both at home (private patriarchy) and in the workplace (public patriarchy). Further, the theory posits that the more individuals believe in patriarchal ideology, the more they support the domination and exploitation of women [4].

Research evidence shows that patriarchal beliefs are very strong in Nigerian organizations [11–13]. Outcomes from qualitative studies suggest that female employees who work with Nigerian men who have strong patriarchal beliefs suffer patriarchal-induced sadness [8, 13] and work role marginalization [6]. Based on the foregoing, we argue that patriarchal beliefs in Nigerian organizations relate to employment anxiety and negative subjective career success among Nigerian women.

Patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety

Employment anxiety refers to the fear, uneasiness, and nervousness experienced by individuals when they think about searching for employment [14, 18]. Employment uncertainty can result in intense anxiety among intending and actual job seekers [18, 19]. It is documented that individuals’ career expectations, employers’ perceptions of applicants in the labor market, and individuals’ unequal chances for career success are shaped by patriarchy [6, 7].

It is observed in the literature that the patriarchal-induced biased family care responsibilities imposed on women by African society lead to limited job opportunities for career women [13]. Also, outcomes from many qualitative studies suggest that the patriarchal-related norms in Nigerian work settings consider females inferior to males in many work roles, thereby impeding females’ chances of securing decent jobs in Nigerian organizations [6, 8, 13]. For example, Adisa et al. [6] reported in qualitative research that female applicants who applied for the head of operations position declared vacant in their organization had their applications turned down in favor of the only male applicant despite their job experiences and outstanding performance in the interview. Another qualitative study reported a female employee as saying, “The position of assistant operations manager became vacant last year. I was the best fit for the position in terms of qualification and experience at work, but a man was appointed even though I was more qualified. I lost out because I am a woman” [8] (p. 156). In another dimension, the patriarchal-biased widowhood practices often militate against widows’ entry into the labor force, as there is an expectation for a woman who loses her spouse to strictly observe the long cultural social isolation norms, unlike her male counterparts [11, 17]. This suggests that the thought of the culturally imposed challenges on widows’ reentry into the workforce can result in employment anxiety. For instance, it is argued that an individual’s thought of an eventual search for employment when in need of it with a high possibility of not being successful at it was found to put such an individual under pressure and anxiety [14, 20]. Relying on the foregoing discussion, we argue that patriarchal dominance results in employment anxiety among Nigerian career women. As a result, we propose the following:H1: Patriarchal dominance negatively relates to employment anxiety among career widows.

H1: Patriarchal dominance negatively relates to employment anxiety among career widows.

H1: Patriarchal dominance negatively relates to employment anxiety among career widows.

Patriarchal dominance and subjective career success

Career success is conceptualized in both objective and subjective forms [21, 22]. The objective career success dimension depicts the career success that is visible to external parties, such as employees’ income level, promotion frequency rate, and hierarchical level [15, 23]; the subjective career success perspective represents employees’ perception of their overall accomplished, personally valued career outcomes, such as overall career goals, income aims, and career advancement targets [24, 25]. This study focuses on the subjective dimension of career success because it fully captures individuals’ overall personal evaluations of their career satisfaction, which represent a more holistic reflection of overall career success than objective indicators alone [15, 25, 26]. For example, Tlaiss [26] (p. 760) documented that “Given that subjective career success promotes a broader interpretation of career success, it has been generally perceived as more suitable to describe women’s personal experiences of career success.” In another dimension, studies on career women also suggested that female employees’ subjective career success is highly dependent on sociocultural factors [25, 27].

Outcomes from prior studies indicate that discrimination and gender bias against women can significantly hamper their subjective career success [28–31]. For example, research examining the consequences of racial and gender discrimination against Black women in American organizations observed that Black female leaders often struggle to realize their career aspirations despite persistent efforts to resist such biases [28]. Similarly, participants in a qualitative study on workplace discrimination against Black women in the United States described these experiences as “really depressing” [29]. These outcomes suggest that such inability to attain career aspirations, coupled with the psychological distress arising from persistent bias, is likely to erode women’s perceptions of subjective career success.

In the Nigerian context, patriarchal studies have demonstrated that males are considered more competent in many job functions and that this patriarchal practice impedes women’s ability to secure enviable jobs that will lead to career satisfaction in Nigerian establishments [6, 8]. Also, the patriarchal dominance female workers experience in the hands of men with strong patriarchal beliefs in work environments seems to have a negative influence on their subjective career success. Adisa et al. [8] (p. 153) reported a female employee as saying in a qualitative study that, “I have worked with men with strong patriarchal attitudes in the past, and my working life was terrible. I was always very sad to go to work, and even at work.” Nwagbara [12] (p. 363) reported in another qualitative study that a participant complained as follows: “I have been working here for God knows when but do not feel happy.” Similarly, a study found gender dominance to hamper subjective career success [32]. Taken together, these findings suggest that Nigerian career widows’ perceived patriarchal marginalization is likely to negatively influence their subjective perceptions of career success. Flowing from the discussion, we propose that:H2: Patriarchal dominance has a significant negative relationship with subjective career success among career widows.

H2: Patriarchal dominance has a significant negative relationship with subjective career success among career widows.

H2: Patriarchal dominance has a significant negative relationship with subjective career success among career widows.

Moderating role of family economic condition

Occupational researchers have long recognized the importance of economic status in individuals’ occupational goal realization. For instance, Wan et al. [18] documented that children from low-income families grapple with unjust competition from children from rich families who could exploit their family members’ “nepotism” to get enviable jobs in prestigious workplaces. Aside from using one’s parents’ nepotism to secure a job, wealthy families use their connections to important individuals to assist their children in securing internship programs in big establishments, which in turn gives the children easy access to important occupational experts in the establishments who offer them occupational role model advantages for employability after graduation [33, 34]. Belle et al. [14] noted that securing internship engagements in big companies by children from high economic classes does not only afford them vital professional learning experiences, but it also puts them ahead of children from lower economic classes in securing decent employment in the future. Duffy et al. [35] proposed economic condition as a moderator in the link between contextual factors of marginalization and access to decent work in the psychology of work model. Duffy et al. [35] argued that the effects of social marginalization on access to decent employment will be moderated by the victim’s economic condition. It is documented that women’s economic empowerment “increases women’s real power over economic decisions that influence their lives and priorities in society” [36] (P. 193). In the Nigerian context, Akanle and Nwaobiala [37] reported that as Nigerian women gradually gain access to education, which in turn leads to access to employment, this sometimes results in many of them becoming breadwinners in their families, thereby diminishing the traditional patriarchal dominance of biased gender roles. Since women’s economic status increases their effective control over economic decisions affecting their lives and important issues in society [36], and a qualitative study in the Nigerian context suggests that the economically empowered Nigerian women are recalibrating the age-long patriarchal dominance [37], we argue that the degree of employment anxiety experienced among Nigerian career women in relation to patriarchal dominance is moderated by the career women’s family’s economic conditions. We therefore proposed that:H3: The negative relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety is curbed at higher levels of economic conditions.

H3: The negative relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety is curbed at higher levels of economic conditions.

H3: The negative relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety is curbed at higher levels of economic conditions.

Moderating role of social support

Social support is conceptualized as the rate at which one feels assisted by relatives, associates, significant others, and the larger community in managing the challenges that emanate from marginalization [38]. Blustein [39] stated that relationships are important for the career development process at all stages among individuals. Studies suggest that career-related support increases self-efficacy [40] and reduces perceived career barriers among individuals [41]. It is reported that support predicts career goal realization for students who come from weak socioeconomic statuses and individuals that are facing marginalization, such as gender marginalization [42].

It is argued that when social support is about the interpersonal resources made available to an individual in times of difficulty, it is best regarded as a moderating variable between difficult situations and well-being [38]. Many studies have reported that the effects minority challenges have on well-being are reduced when social support is high [43, 44]. In a study involving 400 unemployed individuals in Japan, Omori et al. [45] reported that job-search interventions received by unemployed persons improve their optimism that the job search efforts will end in success. Duffy et al. [35] suggest in the psychology of work propositions that contextual marginalization factors pose hindrances to individuals’ efforts to secure decent work, and these direct links are moderated by social support. Applying these insights to the Nigerian context, it suggests that career women who receive social support will be less anxious about securing employment such that the negative relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety is curbed at higher levels of social support. We therefore propose that:H4: The negative relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety is curbed at higher levels of social support.

H4: The negative relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety is curbed at higher levels of social support.

H4: The negative relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety is curbed at higher levels of social support.

Methods

Participants

This study employed a cross-sectional research design to collect primary data from Nigerian career widows. This design is appropriate as it allows for the efficient collection of data to examine the associations between patriarchal dominance, employment anxiety, and subjective career success among career widows in Nigeria [46]. The population of the study consisted of Nigerian career widows employed in public and private establishments in Nigerian organizations. The study’s population is restricted to career widows to understand how patriarchal dominance relates to employment anxiety and subjective career success among Nigerian career women, as such a group of women is rarely included in such studies’ samples. This is a direct response to calls for research into the unique experience of young widowhood that remains unexplored in research [11, 17, 47].

Procedure

This study was conducted in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and the research ethics guidelines of the authors’ institutions. A non-probability sampling approach was adopted due to the absence of a finite population of Nigerian career women in employment across the country [14, 48]. The sample inclusion criteria were that the widow must not be older than 50 years [9, 11, 47] and must be currently employed by a Nigerian organization. To identify career widows, we first consulted the biography sections containing staff profiles published in annual reports, compendia, and websites of purposively selected public and private establishments. Next, we identified the entire population of female workers in the staff profile using binary gender identification techniques commonly used in Nigeria, such as feminine first names, gender-specific pronouns (i.e., “she”), and forms of address (Miss, Ms., and Mrs.) [15]. Using the email addresses accompanying each of the female workers in the staff profiles, we contacted them, explained the aims of the research, and requested that they link us only to the career widows among them, who are the unit of analysis for the study. Through this process, the female workers graciously assisted us in identifying and convincing many widows to participate in the study, resulting in snowball sampling. Finally, an introductory letter containing the study objectives and the informed consent form was sent along with the questionnaire via their respective emails. All eligible respondents voluntarily completed the informed consent form and were informed of their right to withdraw from the research at any time. They were also assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of the study. No financial inducement of any kind was offered to encourage participation. A total of 510 questionnaires were administered, and 497 were returned (97%). However, only 491 copies were used for analysis, while 6 were discarded due to response inconsistencies (i.e., multiple responses to single-choice items).

Measures

Patriarchal dominance

Patriarchy is conceptualized in the literature as a social belief system that supports men’s domination of women [4, 5]. Therefore, the Yoon et al. [49] validated patriarchal beliefs scale was adapted and utilized in this study. The scale measures patriarchy from three dimensions, namely, the institutional power of men, which focuses on beliefs in men’s superior authority and leadership qualities; the inherent inferiority of women, which is about the beliefs in the inherent inferiority of women and their subordinate status; and gendered domestic roles, which measures the patriarchal restriction of women to household and family duties. The “Gendered Domestic Roles” dimension of the measures was left out of the scale because the focus of the study is on how patriarchal belief systems affect women in work environments (public patriarchy) and not in the family (private patriarchy). Examples of the items on the scale are “My male colleagues and superiors believe a man should be the head of a company” and “My male colleagues and superiors believe women do not belong in the workforce.” Patriarchal dominance was measured on a 5-point Likert scale as follows: “Strongly disagree (coded 1), disagree (coded 2), neutral (coded 3), agree (coded 4), strongly agree (coded 5).” A Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.908 was recorded in this study.

Employment anxiety

We adapted the employment anxiety scale from Wan et al.’s [18] work. We adapted the scale to suit Nigerian career women under employment stress [35]. The examples of the items on the scale include “Each time I think about the possibility of getting another job when I lose my job, I feel panic” and “When I think about the possibility of securing my ideal job, my heart beats fast, and I feel very nervous.” We found 0.877 Cronbach’s alpha for this scale. Employment anxiety was measured on a 5-point Likert scale as follows: “Strongly disagree (coded 1), disagree (coded 2), neutral (coded 3), agree (coded 4), strongly agree (coded 5).”

Subjective career success

Items from the career satisfaction scale developed by Greenhaus et al. [50] were used to assess how subjectively satisfied the career widows are with their careers. Items on the scale include “I am satisfied with the success I have achieved in my career” and “I am satisfied with the progress I have made towards meeting my overall career goals.” A Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.795 was obtained in the current study. Subjective career success was measured on a 5-point Likert scale as follows: “Strongly disagree (coded 1), disagree (coded 2), neutral (coded 3), agree (coded 4), strongly agree (coded 5).”

Family economic conditions

The scale for measuring economic status developed by Cuc and Griffin [51] was adapted to measure the family economic condition of the respondents. Respondents whose families own a wide range of luxury houses and cars and live in a two- or three-level house were categorized as upper class; those whose families have enough food and own a range of necessary items that are not luxury were regarded as middle class, while those whose families lack food or high-quality meals and own very simple, basic, and inexpensive items were classified as lower class [51]. Examples of the items are “My family owns a wide range of luxury houses and cars, and we live in a two- or three-level house,” “My family has enough food and owns a range of necessary items that are not of high quality (not luxury),” and “My family lacks food or high-quality meals and owns very simple, basic, and inexpensive items.” This variable was measured on a 5-point Likert scale as follows: “Strongly disagree (coded 1), disagree (coded 2), neutral (coded 3), agree (coded 4), strongly agree (coded 5).” The analysis yielded a 0.855 Cronbach’s alpha value.

Social support

The social support scale designed by Zimet et al. [52] was utilized in measuring social support among the respondents. The scale comprises three dimensions, with each dimension measuring support from family members, friends, and significant others. Sample items are “My family members are willing to help me make decisions,” “I can talk about my problems with my family,” and “There is a special person in my life that cares about my feelings.” This was measured on a 5-point Likert scale as follows: “Strongly disagree (coded 1), disagree (coded 2), neutral (coded 3), agree (coded 4), strongly agree (coded 5).” A Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.795 was found in the present study.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

The instrument’s convergent validity was established using the average variance extracted estimate (AVE). The AVE coefficient values of 0.64, 0.78, 0.62, 0.71, and 0.62 for patriarchal dominance, family economic condition, employment anxiety, social support, and subjective career success are each greater than the 0.5 benchmark and the constructs’ cross-loading. This result is a confirmation that the convergent validity condition is satisfied [54]. We used the variance inflation factor (VIF) to check for common method bias (CMB). Although a VIF value of 1 represents a total absence of collinearity, a VIF value of 2.5 to 5 is acceptable [55]. As Table 3 shows, each item’s VIF coefficient in all the variables is less than 3.

The fit statistics for the PLS-SEM measurement model were assessed using absolute fit measures, incremental fit measures, and parsimony fit measures benchmarks. The absolute fit indices, comprising SRMR, Chi-Square, GFI, and RMSEA, assess the degree of agreement between the model’s a priori predictions and the sample data. The SRMR, the normed chi-square (X2/df), GFI, and RMSEA values of 0.07, 2.18, 0.93, and 0.05, respectively, met their corresponding benchmarks. Incremental fit metrics evaluate how much the tested model has improved over a baseline model where all variables are assumed to be uncorrelated. A benchmark of 0.90 indicates an adequate match for both NFI and CFI. The study’s CFI score of 0.98 and NFI score of 0.95 indicate an acceptable and appropriate research model. Parsimony fit indices were used to compare models and evaluate how well they fit samples from the same population. The PCFI value of 0.68 is higher than the 0.50 cutoff. The d_ULS and d_G values of 0.99 and 0.58 are above the 0.50 threshold. These outcomes demonstrate that the model successfully fits the data and satisfies the necessary parameters.

Results from the structural model

Direct effects

The hypothesized direct and moderating relationships were tested in the structural model (see Fig. 4 and Table 4). The PLS-SEM predictive relevance of the constructs of measurement as well as the data points of indicators was assessed using the Q2 values. The Q2 values obtained in the current study are greater than zero (Q2 = 0.50 > 0, Q2 = 0.32 > 0), an indication that the PLS path model has predictive relevance for the constructs (see Table 4). For example, Q2 of 0.50 and 0.32 clearly shows that the model has a strong predictive relevance for the endogenous constructs.

Having satisfied the Q2 and F2 requirements, we advanced to the path analysis stage. As shown in Table 4, H1 is accepted, indicating that patriarchal dominance significantly relates to employment anxiety among Nigerian career widows (β = 0.52, T-value = 7.61 > 1.96, p-value = 0.001 < 0.05, R2 = 0.27). Surprisingly, patriarchal dominance does not have a significant negative relationship with subjective career success among career widows, contrary to our H2 proposition (β = 0.10, T-value = 2.61 > 1.96, p-value = 0.189 > 0.05, R2 = 0.01) (see Fig. 4 and Table 4).

Moderation effects

The outcomes of the moderating analysis are presented in Figs. 2, 3 and 4, and Table 4. The results suggest that the relationship of patriarchal dominance with employment anxiety is moderated by career widows’ family economic conditions in line with the H3 proposition (β = 0.63, T-value = 9.77 > 1.96, p-value = 0.001 < 0.05, R2 = 0.40). However, the moderation interaction plot indicates that the direction and strength of the moderating role of economic conditions vary across career widows with low, medium, and high economic statuses (see Fig. 2). For widows from low economic backgrounds, the interaction plot indicates a negative slope, suggesting that as patriarchal dominance increases, employment anxiety decreases. For career widows in medium economic backgrounds, the curve indicates a positive slope, and this suggests that as patriarchal dominance increases, employment anxiety increases. The positive relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety is strongest among working widows from high economic backgrounds, as the curve indicates the steepest positive slopes. Contrary to expectations, the negative relationship patriarchal dominance has with employment anxiety is not moderated by the social support received by career widows (β = 0.10, T-value = 2.31 > 1.96, p-value = 0.177 > 0.05, R2 = 0.01). The moderation interaction of social support depicted in Fig. 3 indicates that the slopes for low, moderate, and high social support are nearly parallel and flat, confirming that social support does not significantly moderate the patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety relationship.

Discussion

The current research investigates how patriarchal dominance relates to employment anxiety and subjective career success among career women in Nigeria. Further, the moderating roles of family economic conditions and social support in the relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety were investigated. Based on the results, Hypothesis One was confirmed, indicating that patriarchal dominance significantly relates to employment anxiety among Nigerian career widows. This outcome conforms to similar previous qualitative research that suggests that patriarchal dominance hinders women’s chances of getting jobs [6, 8, 12] and that the thought of an eventual search for employment with the possibility of not being successful at it puts one under pressure and anxiety [14, 20]. The social isolation norms widows are expected to observe, unlike widowers, which militate against widows’ reentry into the labor force [11, 17], may account for employment anxiety in career widows’ experiences. Secondly, the unequal advantages men have over women in securing jobs irrespective of women’s skills and capabilities could also explain the employment anxiety career widows experience.

Contrary to expectations, H2 was not confirmed, suggesting that patriarchal dominance does not significantly relate to subjective career success among career widows. This result contradicts the outcomes in previous qualitative studies that suggest that the patriarchal challenges facing career women may result in low career satisfaction among them [12, 32]. Downward social comparison may explain the strange results of Nigerian career widows experiencing subjective career success in spite of the patriarchal-biased widowhood practices’ challenges they face as widows. Downward social comparison is said to result in subjective satisfaction when individuals compare themselves with other people they believe to be doing worse than them on the dimension of the comparison [56]. This implies that Nigerian career widows experience subjective satisfaction in their careers when they compare themselves with the majority of women who could not find jobs due to the patriarchal practices.

The outcomes from hypothesis 3 analysis suggest that the moderating role of economic conditions in the relationship between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety varies in strength and direction across career widows with low, medium, and high economic status. When patriarchal dominance increases, employment anxiety decreases among career widows from low economic backgrounds, whereas for career widows in medium and high economic backgrounds, employment anxiety increases as patriarchal dominance increases. These outcomes contradict Duffy et al.’s [35] proposition which suggests that economic condition moderates the link between contextual factors of marginalization and access to decent work in the psychology of work model. The reason for these unique findings among widows from low economic backgrounds may be that the strongly disadvantaged economic condition of these career widows may overshadow the influence of patriarchal-biased widowhood practices on them such that they give up on the hope of securing employment, which in turn diminishes employment anxiety among them. Regarding career widows from medium and high economic backgrounds, the reason for the result may be that the consequences of patriarchal-biased widowhood practices, such as the isolation norm, may be more heightened among career widows from middle-income and wealthy families due to the anticipated loss of their sources of income and family reputation, leading to greater employment anxiety.

Surprisingly, hypothesis four was not accepted. This indicates that social support does not significantly moderate the negative relationship of patriarchal dominance with employment anxiety among Nigerian career widows. This result contradicts previous similar research that found a moderating effect of social support in such a link [43, 45]. For instance, Omori et al. [45] reported in Japan that job-search support received by unemployed persons improves their optimism that the job search efforts will end in success. The reason that social support fails to moderate the negative relationship of patriarchal dominance with employment anxiety among career women in the Nigerian context could be a result of the patriarchal nature of Nigerian society. For example, the social support that emanates from Nigerian patriarchal society in general could bear patriarchal colorations, such that social support will not be designed to address the domination and subjugation of career women, particularly career widows.

Theoretical implications

The theoretical value of this research is profound in the following dimensions: First, investigating how patriarchal dominance relates to employment anxiety and subjective career success among career widows, with particular focus on career widows provides a novel dimension in understanding how patriarchal beliefs affect a unique segment of the women’s population. This answers the call by researchers for studies to understand how patriarchy relates to every facet of women’s lives in Africa (9). Second, focusing the present study on career widows in Nigeria fills an important gap in the literature. For example, Jones et al. [46] documented that widowhood is a unique experience among women yet receives little in-depth attention in research. In the same vein, Ude and Njoku [11] documented that empirical research that investigated widowhood among women of 50 years and below is scarce.

Practical implications

The findings of this research have vital implications for government, organizations, and practitioners. First, the Nigerian government should strengthen and strictly enforce laws that prohibit workplace discrimination against women, particularly widows, with violators brought to book. This will allay career widows’ employment anxiety by boosting their confidence in securing decent jobs in the labor market. Second, the National Orientation Agency and other non-governmental organizations should embark on aggressive national sensitization campaigns against patriarchal-biased harmful widowhood practices, particularly in the workplace. Third, the Nigerian Federal Ministry of Labor, as well as the Ministry of Women Affairs and Social Development, can influence organizations’ equal employment opportunities policy for all policy by openly recognizing inclusive employers regarding career widows. Fourth, non-governmental organizations and faith-based institutions can help career widows to deal with employment anxiety by offering free or subsidized mental health counseling services aimed at dismantling employment anxiety among them. Fifth, in the workplace, organizations should enforce inclusive hiring and widow-friendly HR policies that detest discriminatory questioning about marital status during hiring. Organizations may also consider implementing gender-sensitive HR practices that designate working widows as a protected class of workers. Similarly, HR male managers should be trained on unconscious discrimination and inclusive organizations.

Limitations and future directions

While this research offers valuable insights into the career experiences of working widows in Nigerian patriarchal work environments, it is important to highlight areas where future studies could strengthen the generalizability of these findings. First, the lack of finite official data on Nigerian career widows forced the current study to adopt a non-probability sampling approach for sample selection. As a result, the generalizability of the outcomes of this research to all Nigerian career widows is not clear. To accurately generalize this study’s outcomes, future studies in countries in Africa where accurate data

on career widows exist should utilize a probability technique for sample size determination. Second, the use of cross-sectional data for analysis prevents establishing directional relationships among variables. Future research may consider using longitudinal data to provide a deeper insight into the causal relationship among the variables. Third, this study is skewed toward working widows with higher education, thereby limiting the generalizability of the outcomes to the entire population of working women. We advise future research to replicate this study among all classes of widows to enhance generalizability. Additionally, future research could conduct a comparative study of patriarchal dominance between working widows and working non-widows to determine whether there is a significant difference in the level of patriarchal dominance experienced by these groups. Fourth, the study used questionnaire data collected on the respondents’ perceptions of their personal experiences of patriarchal dominance, employment anxiety, economic status, and social support. This research approach is susceptible to perceptual variations in individual interpretation, which could influence the accuracy of responses. To overcome this limitation, we encourage future studies to use objective measures. Fifth, the scale for measuring economic status developed by Cuc and Griffin [50] was used to assess the family economic conditions of the working widows in this study, and the results may have differed if a different measure had been used. Therefore, we suggest that future research consider adopting the Lived Poverty Index developed by Afrobarometer, which captures the frequency with which individuals have gone without five basic necessities over the past year, including food, potable drinking water, cooking fuel, medical care, and cash income.

Conclusion

The current research examines how patriarchal dominance relates to employment anxiety and subjective career success among career widows in a patriarchal African context. The moderating roles of family economic condition and social support in the link between patriarchal dominance and employment anxiety were also investigated. As an answer to the calls for research on how patriarchal dominance relates to employment and career success, this research shows that patriarchal dominance significantly relates to employment anxiety among Nigerian career widows, with family economic conditions playing a significant moderating role with varied strengths and directions. Conversely, patriarchal dominance does not negatively relate to subjective career success. With these unique findings, this research enriches career literature by extending our understanding of how patriarchal dominance relates to employment anxiety and subjective career success in the unexplored African context that is highly characterized by patriarchal dominance.
