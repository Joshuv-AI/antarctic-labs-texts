Efficacy of Percutaneous Kyphoplasty in the Treatment of Kümmell's Disease and Osteoporotic Vertebral Compression Fractures: A Clinical Comparative Study
Authors: Shuo Yuan, Yu Xi, Ruiyuan Chen, Tianyi Wang, Aobo Wang, Ning Fan, Peng Du, Minghui Liang, Lei Zang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveKümmell’s disease (KD) represents a delayed form of osteoporotic vertebral collapse and shares clinical features with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF). Percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) is commonly performed for both conditions, yet comparative evidence and predictors of 1‐year outcomes in KD remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of PKP for the treatment of stage I and II KD and to identify factors associated with the outcomes at 1‐year follow‐up.MethodsWe included 387 inpatients with KD or OVCF who underwent PKP from January 2016 to December 2022. All patients were assigned to the KD group (n = 107) and the OVCF group (n = 280). The difference of demographic data (age, gender, surgical segment, osteoporosis severity and disease duration), clinical efficacy (visual analog scale and Oswestry disability index of pre‐operation, 3‐day post‐operation, 3‐month post‐operation, and 1‐year post‐operation), complications (bone cement leakage during surgery and postoperative refractures), and radiographic parameters (anterior vertebral height and kyphotic angle of pre‐operation, post‐operation, and 1‐year follow‐up) was analyzed. Intergroup comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the Student's t‐test. Repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction was used to evaluate intra‐group differences of visual analog scale (VAS), Oswestry disability index (ODI), anterior vertebral height (AVH) and kyphotic angle (KA) across different time points. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was employed to identify the independent factors influencing VAS and ODI scores during the follow‐up period.ResultsThe disease duration of the KD group was much longer than that of the OVCF group. Significant improvements in VAS and ODI were observed at three‐day, three‐month, and one‐year after PKP. Multivariate regression analysis identified the blocky cement distribution pattern, higher preoperative VAS, and higher preoperative ODI as independent risk factors for suboptimal recovery during follow‐up. Besides, the KD group had lower AVH and larger KA than the OVCF group preoperatively. Both groups showed significant improvements in AVH and KA after PKP. However, the KD group had a higher rate of type II bone cement leakage (BCL) and more severe cemented vertebral collapse at the final follow‐up. The mean bone cement volume was significantly greater in the KD group. Refracture rates were similar between the two groups during follow‐up.ConclusionsPKP can effectively alleviate back pain, improve functional impairment, and correct local deformity in KD patients, with the risks of BCL and vertebral collapse or refractures during follow‐up. It is suitable for KD treatment without nerve injury symptoms. Appropriate measures should be taken to reduce the risk of complications.

Introduction

Kümmell's disease (KD) is a distinct form of vertebral compression fracture characterized by delayed vertebral body collapse and dynamic instability. KD was first described by Hermann Kümmell in 1891 [1]. Its pathogenesis is not fully understood but is commonly attributed to vertebral osteonecrosis and post‐fracture biomechanical instability. It typically follows minor trauma and progresses with vertebral osteonecrosis and nonunion, manifesting as persistent pain, kyphotic deformity, and intravertebral instability. In current literature, KD has also been referred to as intravertebral pseudoarthrosis, delayed post‐traumatic vertebral osteonecrosis, avascular necrosis of the vertebral body, vertebral fracture nonunion, and ischemic vertebral collapse [2]. With advances in medical imaging technology, including high‐resolution computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), KD has been increasingly recognized and reported in recent years. It is not rare in clinical practice and has been reported to account for approximately 7%–37% of vertebral compression fractures. It is more prevalent in older adults [2, 3, 4]. Untimely treatment of osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF) may develop into KD [1].

Conservative treatment is an important treatment strategy for OVCF and KD. However, approximately a quarter of patients with osteoporotic vertebral fracture experienced no significant relief of symptoms after 1 year of conservative treatment [3]. Nearly all patients with KD have undergone months of conservative therapy, such as bed rest, brace wearing, or antiosteoporosis drug therapy. As the disease progresses and long‐term bed rest, some complications may occur, such as pneumonia, venous thrombosis, infections, or even lower limb paralysis [5, 6]. Additional treatment is often recommended for cases unresponsive to conservative therapy.

The Li staging system for KD is widely used [7]. According to this system, stage 1 and stage 2 are considered stable and typically do not require spinal instrumentation. Percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP), a kind of minimally invasive surgery, is effective for treating OVCF. PKP can quickly relieve pain, correct kyphosis, and reduce long‐term bed complications [8]. Therefore, PKP is also frequently performed in patients with KD. However, the existing literature has several limitations [8, 9, 10]. Despite increasing recognition of KD, the evidence base for PKP remains limited. Most published studies have limited sample sizes, which may restrict statistical power and limit the generalizability of their findings. Besides, the available literature does not provide a comprehensive understanding of PKP outcomes in KD, particularly regarding durable pain and functional recovery, deformity correction, and the incidence of surgical complications. Moreover, there is a paucity of predictors of postoperative outcomes in KD.

On this basis, this retrospective comparative study comprehensively evaluates the clinical efficacy, radiographic outcomes, and prognostic determinants of percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) in treating KD relative to OVCF. The purposes of this study were to: (i) evaluate and compare the clinical efficacy, safety, and radiographic outcomes of PKP in patients with stage I‐II KD versus OVCF; (ii) identify factors associated with postoperative clinical outcomes in KD; (iii) summarize the advantages, limitations, and implications of PKP in KD.

Materials and Methods

Patient Characteristics

This was a single‐center, retrospective, observational study. We investigated 978 patients with vertebral fractures who underwent PKP in our hospital from January 2016 to December 2022. According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 387 cases were included in this study, including 107 cases with KD and 280 cases with OVCF. The inclusion criteria were: (1) single‐level vertebral compression fracture, (2) diagnosis confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) scans, (3) follow‐up over 1 year, (4) X‐rays were performed preoperatively, postoperatively, and 1 year after operation, and (5) at least one month of conservative treatment, like osteoporosis treatment and analgesic. Exclusion criteria included: (1) surgical intolerance or contraindications, such as coagulation dysfunction, severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction, and local or systemic infection, (2) pathological fractures, or long‐term use of cortisol, (3) burst fractures or KD stage III with neurological symptoms, (4) multiple vertebral compression fractures, (5) history of open spinal surgery, and (6) incomplete data during follow‐up (Figure 1). The research conducted has been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval for the study was obtained from the ethics committees of the Beijing Chao‐Yang Hospital (2023‐KE‐242).

Flowchart of this study.

There are no established radiological diagnostic criteria for KD. The diagnostic criteria for KD in this study are as follows: (1) it should conform to the typical characteristics of disease progression, including minor trauma, the period of mild or no symptoms and no disability, persistent and localized pain with or without root pain, and the stage of permanent kyphosis; (2) the common imaging manifestations, such as the IVC with sclerosis of the cleft margin, vertebral and pedicle ossification, double‐line sign, fracture of spinous process, and paravertebral callus; (3) excluding the vertebral collapse caused by tumor, infections, and acute trauma [11, 12, 13].

Surgical Procedures

PKP was conducted in the prone position under local anesthesia. All procedures were performed through a unilateral transpedicular approach. A puncture needle and inflatable balloon (Medtronic Sofamor Danek, Memphis, TN, USA) were inserted through the working channel into the fractured vertebral body under X‐ray guidance. The expansion balloon distracted the collapsed vertebral body and restored the height of the vertebral body as much as possible. After balloon removal, polymethyl methacrylate (Mendec Spine Cement; Tecres SPA, Verona, Italy) was slowly injected into the fractured vertebra under the C‐arm observation, stopping immediately if leakage occurred. Surgical time and cement volume were noted. Patients wore rigid spinal braces for 1 month postoperatively and received antiosteoporosis therapy during and after hospitalization.

Data Extraction

The demographic data in this study included age, sex, fracture segment, osteoporosis severity, and disease duration. Osteoporosis severity was assessed using CT attenuation values of L1 vertebral body, expressed in Hounsfield units (HU). The final HU value was calculated as the mean of three regions of interest (ROIs). ROIs were placed on axial images, with care taken to avoid cortical bone and heterogeneous regions, such as bone islands, the posterior venous plexus, and areas of compressed bone. In patients with L1 vertebral fracture, the mean HU value of T12 and L2 was used in place of the L1 HU value [14]. The disease duration was defined as the time from trauma to hospitalization. This information was extracted from the medical records. This study used visual analog scale (VAS) and Oswestry disability index (ODI) to evaluate symptom severity and the clinical efficacy of PKP. It was evaluated 1 day before surgery, 3 days and 3 months post‐operation, and at the final follow‐up. Telephone follow‐up was conducted for patients who did not attend their scheduled outpatient appointments. Patients with a VAS score greater than 3 at the final follow‐up were assigned to the high VAS group, whereas those with a score of 3 or less were included in the low VAS group. Similarly, patients were stratified into the high ODI group (follow‐up score > 20) or low ODI group (follow‐up score ≤ 20).

All patients underwent preoperative standing X‐ray, supine CT, and MRI within 1 week before PKP, as well as radiographs at both 3 days and the final follow‐up. Anterior vertebral height (AVH) and kyphotic angle (KA) were measured from CT or X‐ray images (Figure 2). This information was measured by two clinicians, and the average was used as the final result. The intact AVH was defined as the estimated height before fracture, calculated as the mean AVH of the two adjacent vertebrae. AVH compression rate = (AVHintact − AVHpre‐op)/AVHintact; AVH restoration rate = (AVHpost‐op − AVHpre‐op)/AVHintact; AVH loss rate = (AVHpost‐op − AVHfollow‐up)/AVHpost‐op; KA restoration rate = (KApre‐op − KApost‐op)/KApre‐op; KA increase rate = (KAfollow‐up − KApost‐op)/KApost‐op. Rates were expressed as proportions (multiplied by 100% when reported as percentages). Besides, the cement distribution patterns are divided into two types on radiography. The blocky type is defined by a localized and compact cement mass, while the spongy type is characterized by a diffuse and filamentous dispersion [15].

The anterior vertebral height (AVH) is measured in the midmost computed tomography image in the sagittal view of the vertebra. The kyphotic angle (KA) is the angle formed between the upper and lower endplate extensions of the vertebral body on X‐ray radiographs.

The occurrence and classification of BCL were evaluated using postoperative X‐ray or CT images. Based on Bermejo's study [16], BCL was divided into four subtypes: type I (via the segmental vein), type II (via the cortical defect into extraosseous tissues), type III (intradiscal leakage), type IV (intraspinal leakage, including leakage through cortical defects, basal veins, or intervertebral foramen into the spinal canal) (Figure 3). Post‐operative refracture was also assessed, which was divided into cemented vertebral refracture, adjacent segment refracture, and remote segment refracture. In addition, we compared the bone cement injection volume extracted from medical records between the two groups.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0.1 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and intergroup comparisons were performed using the Student's t‐test. Intra‐group differences in VAS, ODI, AVH, and KA across different time points were analyzed using repeated‐measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and comparisons between groups were conducted using the chi‐square test. The association between the volume and distribution type of bone cement and the final postoperative outcomes (VAS and ODI) was assessed using logistic regression analysis. The baseline data, including age, gender, fracture level, disease duration, preoperative VAS, and preoperative ODI, were incorporated as covariates in the analysis. Given that a patient can experience multiple subtypes of BCL or refracture simultaneously, the four‐cell chi‐square test and Fisher's exact probability method were conducted to assess the occurrence of these events. p < 0.05 indicated a significant difference.

Clinical Outcomes

Both groups demonstrated significant and sustained improvements in pain and functional disability following PKP, as measured by VAS and ODI scores at all postoperative time points (Figure 4A,B). Preoperatively, the KD group presented with significantly higher baseline VAS (7.93 ± 0.71 vs. 7.62 ± 0.60) and ODI scores (83.42 ± 6.33 vs. 70.03 ± 7.56) compared to the OVCF group. However, no significant differences in VAS or ODI were observed between the two groups at the 3‐day, 3‐month, or final follow‐up assessments. Repeated‐measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction confirmed a significant effect of time on both VAS and ODI scores, indicating substantial postoperative improvement in both cohorts. Furthermore, a significant interaction effect between time and group was identified for both measures. Univariate analysis demonstrated that the pattern of bone cement distribution was significantly associated with patient‐reported outcomes at the final follow‐up, with the blocky type linked to higher VAS (p = 0.023) and high ODI scores (p = 0.028). In contrast, cement volume showed no significant relationship with pain or functional outcome at the final follow‐up (p = 0.828 and 0.668 respectively). After adjustment for covariates, including age, gender, fracture level, disease duration, and preoperative VAS and ODI, multivariate logistic regression identified a spongy cement distribution (p = 0.039, OR = 3.289, 95% CI: 1.059–10.204) and lower preoperative VAS (p = 0.029, OR = 2.667, 95% CI: 1.104–6.452) as independent predictors of a low VAS at the final follow‐up. Likewise, a spongy distribution pattern (p = 0.015, OR = 3.185, 95% CI: 1.248–8.130) and lower preoperative ODI (p = 0.015, OR = 1.093, 95% CI: 1.017–1.174) were independent predictors of a low ODI at the final follow‐up.

Refracture rates were similar between the two groups, with adjacent segment refractures being the most common in both groups (Table 3). Besides, the KD group had a significantly higher incidence of type II BCL, and the OVCF group had a significantly higher incidence of type I BCL (Table 4). No serious complications caused by BCL were observed, like pulmonary embolism or spinal cord compression. The KD group had a higher bone cement injection volume (7.075 ± 2.251 mL) compared to the OVCF group (5.543 ± 1.798 mL).

Radiographic Parameters

There was no significant difference in AVH‐Intact between the two groups (Table 5). The KD group showed significantly lower preoperative AVH than the OVCF group, indicating more severe vertebral compression. In both groups, AVH significantly increased after PKP, with no significant intergroup difference at the postoperative stage. During follow‐up, a marked decrease in AVH was observed in both groups. Overall, the KD group experienced more severe vertebral compression, greater height restoration after surgery, and more pronounced collapse during follow‐up. The changes in KA showed a similar pattern. In both groups, KA significantly improved after PKP but deteriorated significantly during follow‐up. The KD group had significantly higher KA values both preoperatively and at the final follow‐up than the OVCF group, whereas postoperative KA did not differ significantly between groups. The KD group exhibited a greater degree of kyphotic correction and a higher rate of kyphotic progression than the OVCF group. Discussion

PKP is a key treatment for acute OVCF, offering quick pain relief, vertebral height restoration, and fewer complications caused by prolonged bed rest. Its minimal invasiveness and fast recovery make it popular in clinical setting [17]. Although KD was recognized for over a century, there is still no consensus on its treatment [8]. This study aimed to evaluate the effect and safety of PKP on KD.

Demographic Data

In this study, most KD patients were elderly females. The possible reason is postmenopausal osteoporosis, which is the most important risk factor for KD [18]. The affected segments were similar to those in the OVCF group, primarily T11–L1. Besides, the KD group had a longer disease duration than the OVCF group. This may be attributed to the prolonged asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic period of patients with KD.

Clinical Efficacy

The KD group had higher preoperative VAS and ODI scores, indicating more severe symptoms and disability compared to the OVCF group. This may be due to greater vertebral instability, more pronounced deformities, and more severe local inflammation in patients with KD [19]. MRI scans show high‐brightness edema signals near the IVC in KD cases, supporting these findings. Vertebral ischemia is more significant in patients with KD [20], which may also aggravate patient discomfort. However, both KD and OVCF groups showed significant symptom improvement postoperatively, aligning with previous research [9, 18, 21, 22]. In addition, the pattern of bone cement distribution demonstrated significant predictive value for both pain intensity and functional disability at follow‐up. The diffuse spongy cement distribution was strongly associated with lower VAS and ODI scores, suggesting that a well‐dispersed morphology provides more effective biomechanical stabilization of the treated vertebra. In contrast, the blocky distribution pattern lacks sufficient interdigitation with the surrounding cancellous bone, which may lead to uneven stress transmission and persistent micro‐instability. These unfavorable mechanical conditions may predispose patients to vertebral recompression, cement displacement, or even adjacent‐level fractures [23, 24, 25]. In this study, the proportion of blocky type was significantly higher in the KD group than in the OVCF group. The bone cement is more likely to form a compact distribution pattern in vertebrae with IVC [26]. During surgery, the sclerotic rim surrounding the IVC can be disrupted using a curette to facilitate the interconnection of the cleft with the surrounding cancellous bone, thereby promoting a more diffuse dispersion of the cement. Notably, the volume of injected cement did not exhibit a significant association with patient‐reported outcomes during follow‐up.

The study found no significant difference in refracture incidence between patient groups during follow‐up. 1.9% patients with KD and 3.9% patients with OVCF experienced refracture of cemented vertebrae. Chen et al. demonstrated that enough bone cement volume, bone mineral density, and antiosteoporosis treatment are the main protective factors influencing refracture of cemented vertebrae [27]. In our study, the KD group received more bone cement than the OVCF group, which may contribute to the low incidence of refracture. In addition, water signals occurring around the bone cement in T2 image indicate unstable fixation and symptom recurrence, which can be an indication of reinjection of bone cement [28]. In this study, patients with cemented vertebral refracture were retreated with PKP and all achieved satisfactory results.

BCL is one of the most common intraoperative complications of PKP and may lead to severe adverse consequences. For instance, BCL through the segmental vein can result in pulmonary embolism [29]; BCL into the intervertebral disc may increase the risk of adjacent vertebral fractures [30]; and BCL into the spinal canal may cause neurological compression that often requires secondary surgical intervention [31]. Our study revealed that patients with KD exhibited a distinct leakage pattern compared with the OVCF group, which may be attributed to their pathological features such as the presence of the intravertebral pseudoarthrosis and avascular necrosis. The KD group had a higher BCL rate, particularly for type II leakage, aligning with previous research [32]. This may occur because balloon expansion widens cortical fissures connected to the IVC. Meanwhile, the features of ischemic vertebrae reduce the risk of BCL through veins. Besides, type III leakage is slightly higher in the KD group, which needs to be avoided because it may cause adjacent vertebral disease. To reduce the refracture risk of adjacent vertebrae, cement injection should stop once disc leakage. If preoperative imaging reveals IVC connections to vertebral endplates, measures like reducing cement volume or using gelatin sponge before cement injection should be taken [32]. A previous study reached a contrary conclusion [33]. They observed lower BCL rates in IVC cases. We believe it might be due to the smaller cement volume (average 3.1 mL) compared with our study. Due to the chronic nature of KD, the IVC is surrounded with a fibrocartilage membrane, which creates a closed capsule. This results in a low BCL rate when injected in small amounts.

Imaging Parameters

The vertebral morphology in KD patients appeared more unstable. Preoperatively, the KD group demonstrated more pronounced deformities, characterized by a higher compression rate, reduced AVH, and greater KA, which may reflect greater vertebral instability with IVC and delayed intervention associated with the longer disease course. Following PKP, these patients showed a greater restoration of AVH and KA, likely owing to the mobility of the intravertebral pseudarthrosis and the balloon expansion effect. The intravertebral pseudarthrosis, also known as the opening and closing sign, is a potential risk factor for serious local complications after PKP [34]. Despite this initial improvement, KD patients experienced more substantial vertebral collapse during follow‐up, consistent with previous reports [35, 36]. This subsequent deterioration may be explained by the tendency of bone cement to accumulate as a compact mass within the intravertebral cleft, resulting in limited interdigitation with the surrounding cancellous bone. Such a distribution pattern increases local stress and predisposes the vertebra to continued collapse. To mitigate this risk, KD patients are advised to adopt more aggressive conservative management strategies, such as prolonged brace wearing.

Compared with PKP performed for OVCF, PKP for KD differs in both treatment goals and procedural priorities. For patients with KD, the primary objectives of PKP are symptom relief and restoration of intravertebral mechanical stability by eliminating persistent motion associated with intravertebral pseudarthrosis. Accordingly, the IVC in KD requires a more comprehensive evaluation, including morphologic characteristics, the connection with the endplate and cortical bone, and the extent of dynamic instability of the pseudarthrosis. A relatively larger cement volume is often required for patients with intravertebral pseudarthrosis. Cement injection should be tailored to achieve spongy dispersion especially within the cancellous bone both cranial and caudal to the IVC, to provide broader internal augmentation and minimize micromotion at the IVC, cement displacement, and postoperative vertebral collapse. In addition, a conservative balloon inflation strategy, with lower inflation pressures and avoidance of aggressive height correction, is recommended. Excessive balloon pressure may further expand the cortical or endplate defects, increasing the risk of BCL and adjacent vertebral fracture.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has some limitations. Initially, it is important to note that this is a retrospective study. To more accurately evaluate the efficacy of PKP, multicenter prospective studies are warranted. Second, in certain patients, the detection of BCL was reliant on postoperative X‐ray imaging, which may have resulted in missed diagnoses. Third, because the dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (T‐scores) measurements were not systematically available for all patients, we used CT attenuation (Hounsfield units) as a surrogate for bone density, which may have introduced measurement error and affected the accuracy of osteoporosis assessment. Lastly, some indicators with potential value, like the opening and closing sign, were not included in our study. Further research is needed in the future.

Nevertheless, this study possesses several notable strengths. First, it represents one of the largest single‐center cohorts to date that directly compares KD with OVCF. Second, the study provides a comprehensive evaluation that integrates clinical outcomes, radiographic parameters, complication profiles, and multivariate prognostic analyses. Due to these features, the study offers valuable insight into the therapeutic effects of PKP for KD and clearly delineates disease‐specific characteristics and challenges, thereby contributing important evidence for optimizing clinical decision‐making in surgery.

Conclusions

In summary, this study retrospectively analyzed the efficacy and safety of PKP in the treatment of more than 100 cases of KD. PKP is an effective minimally invasive surgery for the treatment of KD. It can significantly relieve symptoms and restore function. However, defects such as unsustainable deformity improvement and high risk of type II BCL should also be considered. Patients with KD need more aggressive treatment.
