Operationalizing diffusion magnetic resonance imaging positivity for detection of vascular contributions to impairment and dementia
Authors: Mingzhao Hu, Scott A. Przybelski, Terry M. Therneau, Emily S. Lundt, Heather J. Wiste, Carly T. Mester, Michael Kamykowski, Robert I. Reid, Mary M. Machulda, David S. Knopman, Ronald C. Petersen, Clifford R. Jack, Jonathan Graff‐Radford, Prashanthi Vemuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWhite matter lesions on fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery magnetic resonance imaging (FLAIR‐MRI) reflect small vessel disease (SVD) and aid in identifying vascular contributions to impairment and dementia (VCID). Emerging diffusion MRI (dMRI) biomarkers are sensitive to early SVD but the lack of validated approaches for defining dMRI abnormality has limited their clinical utility.METHODSIn 3934 participants (8749 observations) from a population‐based sample, we classified 9% as vascular cognitive impairment versus not (VCI+/VCI−) using abnormal FLAIR‐MRI and cognitive testing. We evaluated four distinct dMRI positivity criteria and assessed performance in an independent clinical sample.RESULTSThe young referent criterion performed best and yielded an estimated prevalence of dMRI positivity aligned with pathology literature. About 40% of the population were dMRI positive, and these individuals had lower baseline cognition and faster longitudinal decline. Cutpoints were validated in an independent clinical sample.DISCUSSIONThe proposed dMRI abnormality framework can detect VCID and aid in identification of individuals at risk of VCI.

BACKGROUND

Vascular contribution to impairment and dementia (VCID) is prevalent 1 , 2 , 3 but often not considered in the diagnosis or treatment planning unless the vascular contribution is the most significant pathology driving the impairment. VCID is also frequently used as an exclusion criterion in Alzheimer's disease (AD) clinical trials and treatment planning. 4 Among vascular imaging biomarkers, small vessel disease (SVD) assessments using white matter hyperintensities (WMH) seen on fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery magnetic resonance imaging (FLAIR‐MRI) are widely used as imaging markers of cerebral SVD and to support VCID ascertainment in aging and dementia studies. 5 , 6 , 7 Although there is evidence that WMH are not etiologically specific to SVD and may be linked to axonal degeneration due to neurodegenerative etiologies specifically in early onset AD studies, 8 , 9 vascular risk factors are major contributors to WMH in the population 10 and WMH is an important feature of VCID. 11

Recent studies have shown that changes seen on diffusion MRI (dMRI) occur earlier than WMH changes on FLAIR‐MRI and can aid in earlier detection of disease‐related changes. 12 , 13 , 14 Additionally, dMRI is useful for detecting both early white matter injury caused by SVD and secondary degeneration of white matter resulting from established lesions. 15 , 16 However, the lack of operationalization of abnormal or positive dMRI markers makes the interpretation of these markers difficult in both clinical and research settings.

The overall goal of this work was to operationalize dMRI markers for detection of VCID by establishing cutpoints and evaluating their clinical relevance for capturing early SVD. We hypothesized that establishing optimal cutpoints for dMRI measures would allow us to detect participants with early VCID. In addition, we aimed to evaluate whether dMRI defined biomarker positivity was associated with cognitive trajectories, to assess the utility of these cutpoints in identifying individuals at elevated risk for cognitive decline. We selected four dMRI‐based biomarkers that are proposed to capture early SVD and quantify the extent of its associated white matter damage using different approaches. The first measure, fractional anisotropy of the genu of the corpus callosum (FA‐Genu), was proposed by our lab 17 and validated as being useful for capturing early vascular disease using clinical and pathological studies by us 13 , 18 , 19 and others. 20 Decreasing FA‐Genu reflects microstructural changes associated with interhemispheric disconnection in the frontal lobes seen with worsening vascular risk. 13 , 17 The MarkVCID consortium selected three dMRI measures as global measures of SVD 21 . Peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD), which quantifies the mean diffusivity along the skeletonized white matter tracts, has been shown to capture diffuse vascular injury and SVD progression. 22 , 23 Free water (FW), a quantitative dMRI measure estimated in the white matter, has been shown to correlate with functional and cognitive performance. 24 , 25 The ARTerioloSclerosis (ARTS) marker, developed as a combination of demographics, WMH, and dMRI measures, provides a likelihood of arteriolosclerosis based on autopsy data and has been shown to correlate with cognitive decline. 26 In this work, we built on the systematic approach taken previously by Jack et al. 27 for establishing cutpoints for Alzheimer's disease biomarkers. The uniqueness of this work is evaluating cutpoints for ascertaining early VCID using dMRI.

METHODS

Participants

All participants in this study were enrolled in the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging (MCSA), a prospective population‐based study cohort that was designed to investigate the incidence and prevalence of mild cognitive impairment and dementia among the residents of Olmsted County, MN. 28 The Olmsted County population was enumerated using the Rochester Epidemiology Project medical records linkage system. 29 This allowed us to ascertain vascular risk factors from detailed health care records instead of relying on self‐report. The MCSA study initially recruited participants between 70 and 89 years old, starting in 2004. In 2012, the age range for enrollment was expanded to include individuals 50 and older, and then again to include those 30 and older in 2015. Individuals included in this study were required to have at least one visit with imaging that assessed WMH and Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) to measure VCID. A total of 3934 MCSA participants were included, with visits spanning 2005–2024.

Demographics

Sex was recorded during the clinical evaluation. Age at the time of the MRI scan was used as the age variable in our analyses. The count of cardiovascular metabolic conditions (CMC) was abstracted by nurses from medical records 7 and was defined as the sum of the presence or absence of seven conditions: diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, stroke, congestive heart failure, cardiac arrhythmias, and coronary artery disease.

These studies were approved by the Mayo Clinic and Olmsted Medical Center Institutional Review Boards, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

MRI

All participants underwent MRI scans using 3 Tesla systems from either GE Healthcare or Siemens. Imaging protocols were harmonized across platforms and followed a consistent acquisition scheme. The protocols included T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE), T2‐weighted FLAIR, T2*‐weighted gradient echo imaging, and dMRI sequences. Detailed imaging parameters and acquisition protocols have been reported previously. 30 The details for the processing of all imaging values are provided in the Supplementary Material, with Tables S1 and S2 giving characteristics for the samples used for crossover Deming regressions.

Development of dMRI cutpoints

Among the 3934 participants from MCSA, 2826 participants had usable FA‐Genu data, 3017 had FW and PSMD measures, and 3024 had ARTS, reflecting that not all participants underwent every diffusion sequence at each imaging visit due to differences in scanner availability, acquisition protocols, or image reconstruction. WMH volumes were derived from FLAIR‐MRI using a fully automated segmentation pipeline and normalized to intracranial volume, 31 with 32% of participants meeting the abnormal WMH criterion. A total of 15% of the study participants were cognitively impaired (global CDR ≥ 0.5). Participants meeting both the established criteria of high WMH (Fazekas ≥ 2) and global CDR ≥ 0.5 were classified as vascular cognitively impaired (VCI+, 9% of the cohort), representing cognitive impairment associated with SVD. This is aligned with the VasCog‐2‐WSO Criteria 32 and enabled a principled comparison of cutpoint derivation strategies for vascular cognitive decline. Utilizing this VCI+ definition as a working standard for identifying those with VCID (selection based on both high WMH indicating SVD and cognitive impairment based on CDR), cutpoints for each biomarker were derived within the training dataset using predefined diagnostic subsamples (Table 1, right hand columns). The assumption that most individuals in the VCI+ group were dMRI positive is plausible given that dMRI changes are seen earlier than appearance of WMH. 12 , 13 , 14 We then applied the derived cutpoints to the independent testing dataset and subsequently to an external clinical validation cohort.

The four different criteria used to ascertain dMRI positivity cutpoints were based on subsets of participants shown on the right of Table 1 and details on the specific subsample definitions and cutpoint derivation strategies are provided below.

Training and testing samples

The training subsample (80% of the full sample) was used for model development and cutpoint selection, while the independent testing subsample was reserved for evaluating classification performance and generalizability. This design ensured that both subsets included participants with imaging data, while the testing cohort remained entirely independent of model derivation. The training subsample consisted of 3148 randomly selected individuals with concurrent imaging data. Their mean age was 73.9 years (SD = 12.6), and 1529 (49%) were female. Participants had an average of 14.5 years of education (SD = 2.7) and a mean CMC score of 2.3 (SD = 1.4). VCI+ was present in 9% of participants, and the mean WMH burden was 0.93% of intracranial volume (SD = 1.00). The testing subsample comprised 786 unique individuals with vascular health indicators and imaging data who were not part of the training cohort. Their mean age was 73.6 years (SD = 13.1), and 376 (48%) were female. They averaged 14.4 years of education (SD = 2.6) and had a mean CMC score of 2.3 (SD = 1.4). VCI+ was observed in 10% of participants, and WMH burden averaged 0.93% of intracranial volume (SD = 1.01) (Table 1).

We then performed internal validation by applying the cutpoints derived in the training subsample to the independent testing subsample. For each biomarker and thresholding strategy, we classified participants in the testing set as biomarker positive/negative (Biomarker ±) depending on whether their diffusion biomarker value was above or below the corresponding cutpoint determined in the training sample and compared these assignments to the WMH/CDR based working definition of VCI ± . Age‐binned sensitivity and positive predictive values (PPV) were then computed across 3‐year intervals, providing a direct assessment of classification performance in the testing sample. This design ensured that the operationalized cutpoints were evaluated on data independent of model derivation to assess their generalizability within the MCSA.

Statistical modeling

To establish thresholds for defining abnormal biomarker values, we employed and compared four criteria, each prioritizing a different perspective for classifying VCI+: (1) a control tail percentile criterion based on young VCI− controls, (2) a case tail percentile criterion based on VCI+ cases, and (3) maximizing Youden's index with either young VCI− controls, or (4) age‐ and sex‐matched VCI− controls as the referent group against VCI+. All referent/control groups were derived exclusively from the training subset, ensuring independence from the held‐out test cohort. Young controls were defined as cognitively unimpaired individuals aged 30–49 with available amyloid positron emission tomography (PET), tau PET, and MRI measures. VCI+ cases were individuals aged ≥ 60 who met both CDR ≥ 0.5 and high WMH burden criteria, while VCI− matched controls were unimpaired individuals aged ≥ 60, strictly WMH− and CDR−, selected one‐to‐one to match the VCI+ group on age (± 3 years) and sex. Together, these four thresholding strategies reflect complementary priorities. The control tail percentile and threshold are conservative and emphasize true negatives in a younger population and the case tail percentile threshold is anchored to clinically defined cases to prioritize case coverage and maximize true positives. The two Youden referent thresholds prioritize diagnostic discrimination by maximizing separation between VCI+ and unimpaired groups for early detection with the unimpaired groups defined in two different ways.

The control tail percentile criterion focused on individuals likely to be free of SVD pathology based on the distribution of young VCI− controls aged 30 to 49 from the MCSA cohort. We designated abnormality as any biomarker value exceeding the 95th percentile of the empirical cumulative distribution function found via kernel density methods. We prioritized ensuring that only individuals with values far from those seen in young unimpaired controls were labeled abnormal, making it suitable for flagging early deviation from a healthy brain baseline. This upper‐tail thresholding technique, commonly used in clinical diagnostics, 33 was applied uniformly across all four diffusion biomarkers.

Another strategy, the case tail percentile, utilized the distribution of VCI+ cases where we isolated individuals who were aged 60 or older with Fazekas ≥ 2 and global CDR ≥ 0.5. The cutpoint was determined as the 10th percentile of biomarker values in this group, capturing the threshold below which 90% of true positives fell. As with the control tail percentile threshold, a smoothed cumulative distribution function derived from kernel density estimation was used for percentile computation. This method is more selective in defining VCI by excluding unimpaired individuals and builds on earlier approaches we developed in 2017. 27 We prioritized maximizing detection of clinical cases of VCI+ even if it captures more false positives, tuned to capture disease presence rather than detecting early abnormality.

We also designed two Youden's index criteria targeting the point of maximum difference between groups. The first one is Youden‐based young referent, where the difference is taken between the smoothed cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of VCI+ and young VCI− from 30 to 49 years of age. This is functionally equivalent to maximizing “specificity + sensitivity − 1” (i.e., the Youden's index), which provides a performance‐driven metric ideal for a diagnostic boundary based on a neutral trade‐off between sensitivity and specificity. The second is the Youden‐based age/sex‐matched referent, which uses an older control group with VCI+ compared against VCI− participants matched by age and sex to controls for confounding. This helps identify biomarker values that are extreme relative to what is typical for the same age and sex characteristics, thus isolating deviations due to VCI+. Together, these criteria emphasize subtle abnormalities that may precede clinically apparent disease in the aging process.

After deriving biomarker cutpoints, we formally evaluated their performance using regression‐ and contingency‐based approaches. First, we fit logistic regression models with spline terms for age to estimate the probability of biomarker abnormality across the lifespan under each criterion. These models provided smooth prevalence curves and allowed comparison of influences on the proportion classified as abnormal from cutpoints based on different criteria. Second, we quantified discriminative performance using contingency tables and derived sensitivity and PPV. To assess how reliably these cutpoints separate VCI+ from controls in new individuals, we estimated their sensitivity and PPV in the test set. We used nonparametric bootstrap resampling of the test cohort to quantify the uncertainty around these operating characteristics. Reported bootstrap confidence intervals therefore describe variability in classification performance conditional on the chosen cutpoint, not uncertainty in the cutpoint location itself.

External validation sample

The Mayo Clinic Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (Mayo ADRC) cohort included 184 participants with concurrent dMRI, FLAIR‐MRI, and CDR data. Mayo ADRC is a longitudinal research cohort recruited from clinical practice. Unlike the population‐based MCSA, the Mayo ADRC represents a clinic‐based sample enriched for individuals with cognitive impairment and related clinical concerns. Their mean (standard deviation) age was 72.8 (8.5) years, and 77 (42%) were female. A total of 112 (62%) were apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 carriers. Cognitive impairment (CDR ≥ 0.5) was observed in 144 (78%). All participants (100%) were classified as WMH+, defined as Fazekas ≥ 2. Participants had an average of 15.7 (2.8) years of education. Overall, 144 (78%) met the VCI+ definition. Compared with the population‐based MCSA cohort, the Mayo ADRC sample was enriched for APOE ε4 carriers and cognitively impaired individuals, reflecting its clinic‐based recruitment. We applied the operationalized cutpoints derived from the MCSA training subsample directly to the Mayo ADRC cohort to classify Biomarker ± and compare against the VCI ± defined by WMH and CDR. Sensitivity and PPV were then calculated across three‐year age bins, using the same procedure as in the MCSA internal validation. This external validation was to confirm whether cutpoints established in a community‐based sample retain performance in a clinically ascertained cohort with potentially different demographic and clinical characteristics.

Using these cutpoints, we classified participants as Biomarker+ or Biomarker− and summarized performance in 2×2 tables (Figure 2) that report the proportions of VCI+ and VCI− within each classification for all four biomarkers. An effective biomarker for early SVD should classify most VCI− individuals as Biomarker− to reduce false positives and increase specificity, while keeping the number of VCI+ individuals classified as Biomarker− (lower‐left cell in each table) as low as possible to limit false negatives and preserve sensitivity. Because Biomarker+ are designed to capture early SVD, it is expected that a sizable proportion of participants will be classified as Biomarker+ but are still VCI−. For example, using the Youden‐based young referent for PSMD, we observed 332 VCI− individuals classified as Biomarker− and only two VCI+ individuals classified as Biomarker− (Figure 2; PSMD—Young). In contrast, when using the Youden‐based age/sex‐matched referent criterion for PSMD, nine VCI+ individuals were classified as Biomarker− (Figure 2; PSMD—age/sex), alongside 428 VCI− categorized as Biomarker−. This suggests that the age/sex‐matched criterion is somewhat more conservative than the young referent and may underestimate the frequency of early SVD. Similarly, when analyzing the table for Youden‐based young referent criterion for FA‐Genu, 280 VCI− individuals were classified as Biomarker−, and three VCI+ individuals were classified as Biomarker− (Figure 2; FA‐Genu—Young). These findings indicate that different biomarkers and cutpoint selection criteria yield varying degrees of classification performance. A lower number of VCI− individuals classified as Biomarker+ indicates fewer false positives and therefore improved specificity. This highlights the importance of carefully selecting biomarkers and cutpoint criteria for accurate VCI ± risk stratification.

Contingency tables for four biomarkers under each of the four cutpoints selection criteria. Number of participants for each biomarker from the training subsample used in constructing this table varies since not all participants underwent every diffusion sequence at each imaging visit.

To further evaluate the classification performance of each thresholding strategy over age, we plotted age‐binned sensitivity in solid lines and PPV in dashed lines for each biomarker and cutpoint (Figure 3). The control tail percentile‐based and Youden‐based young referent cutpoints yielded similarly high sensitivities (typically near 1.0) across most age bins, but they diverged in PPV. The Youden‐based young referent consistently provided equal or higher PPV in clinically relevant ages while maintaining maximal sensitivity. For ARTS, both cutpoints achieved high sensitivity at older ages, yet the Youden‐based young referent showed superior PPV. FW and PSMD followed the same pattern, with higher PPV under the Youden‐based young referent while sensitivity remained maximal. The FA‐Genu exhibited broadly similar PPV values between the two strategies, though the Youden‐based young referent afforded slightly better sensitivity at mid‐range ages. By contrast, the Youden‐based age‐/sex‐matched referent cutpoint showed lower sensitivity than the young referent in earlier age bins, particularly for FA‐Genu and FW. At older ages, this strategy tended to sacrifice sensitivity for modest PPV gains, as seen with PSMD. This reflects the fact that the age‐/sex‐matched referent group carries some underlying disease burden, which shifts cutpoints upward and leads to missed true positives at younger ages with only limited improvements in precision later. Taken together, the Youden‐based young referent offers the most balanced and stable performance, maintaining sensitivity while improving PPV across markers and ages. Therefore, we used it as the primary cutpoint in subsequent analyses.

For FA‐Genu, the Youden‐based young referent criterion identified the largest proportion of VCI− individuals as Biomarker+, followed closely by the case tail percentile and control tail percentile criteria. The Youden‐based age‐/sex‐matched referent criterion classified up to 30% fewer individuals as positive. For both FW and ARTS, the relative performance of the criterion was similar, with the percentage of VCI− individuals exceeding the cutpoint decreasing in the order of control tail percentile, Youden‐based young referent, case tail percentile, and Youden‐based age‐/sex‐matched referent at any given age. Validation of cutpoints

Internal validation in the MCSA testing subsample confirmed that the Youden‐based young referent cutpoints preserved high sensitivity while providing superior PPV compared with alternative thresholds. As shown in Figure S2, sensitivity (solid lines) and PPV (dashed lines) for each biomarker under the four cutpoint definitions were evaluated in the held‐out MCSA testing subsample. The Youden‐based young referent criterion (orange) consistently maximized sensitivity and, while not always the top in PPV, maintained relatively higher PPV with minimal reduction in sensitivity compared with alternative thresholds. For ARTS and PSMD, sensitivity remained near‐perfect from age 70 onward. FA‐Genu and FW showed similar patterns, with both case tail percentile and young referent criteria yielding more favorable trade‐offs than the other thresholds. These findings indicate that cutpoints selected in the training sample generalize well to independent MCSA participants, confirming the robustness of the young referent approach.

External validation in the Mayo ADRC cohort further demonstrated that the young referent criterion retained favorable performance in a clinically ascertained sample. Figure S3 presents the external validation of cutpoints in the Mayo ADRC cohort. Although PPV values were modest overall, reflecting the high prevalence of impairment in the Mayo ADRC sample, the young referent criterion consistently retained PPV advantages without sacrificing sensitivity. For FA‐Genu and ARTS, sensitivity remained uniformly high across age bins, while FW showed instances where PPV exceeded sensitivity. This indicates that FW cutpoints are relatively conservative: a positive result strongly signals VCI+ (high PPV), but many true cases are missed (lower sensitivity). This pattern highlights the trade‐off between detecting all cases and ensuring that biomarker positivity meaningfully indicates VCI. By contrast, PSMD showed sensitivity near 100% across all age bins, but PPV was constrained by the cohort's base rate of VCI. This poor generalization arose because PSMD values in the Mayo ADRC cohort were shifted upward relative to MCSA, causing nearly all participants to exceed the MCSA‐derived cutpoints. Furthermore, individuals classified as Biomarker+ by the young referent cutpoints were consistently more likely to be VCI+ than those classified as Biomarker−. Across all four diffusion biomarkers, individuals classified as Biomarker+ had more severe cognitive impairment than those classified as Biomarker−, as reflected by higher mean CDR values (all p < 0.01 by Wilcoxon test). Among VCI+ participants, 96.8% were identified as Biomarker+ using the FA‐Genu cutpoint, and 100% were identified as Biomarker+ using FW, ARTS, and PSMD cutpoints. Because nearly all Mayo ADRC participants exceeded the PSMD threshold, no valid Biomarker− comparison could be made for that metric.

Selection of the optimal cutpoint criterion and evaluating clinical relevance

Among all the methods, the Youden‐based age‐/sex‐matched referent performed the worst, i.e., estimated the lowest prevalence of biomarker positivity with estimates between 19% and 34% at age 80, which is inconsistent with the fact that the prevalence of cerebrovascular disease by age 80 is close to 80% in large pathology studies. 34 Further, a greater number of VCI+ participants were classified as Biomarker− using the age‐/sex‐matched referent criterion. The case tail percentile criterion‐based cutpoints were closer to the age‐/sex‐matched referent criterion for the three global measures. This suggests that utilizing VCI+ participants for defining positivity may result in higher cutpoints that will not detect early SVD but late SVD (only when participants are likely VCI+). The control tail percentile criterion, on the other hand, provided the lowest cutpoints among all methods and labeled a large proportion of the VCI− population as Biomarker+, highlighting that it likely overcalls dMRI positivity on the basis of the low cutpoints.

The dMRI cutpoints based on the young referent criterion performed the best, with a low number of VCI+ participants classified as Biomarker− and with prevalence estimates that aligned with expectations for early SVD in the population. Therefore, we utilized the young referent criterion in the test set and defined participants as Biomarker+/Biomarker− for each of the four biomarkers to evaluate the clinical relevance of dMRI positivity. The selected cutpoint was used to classify participants into FA‐Genu ± , FW ± , ARTS ± and PSMD ± . To illustrate the clinical usefulness of the cutpoints, we computed model‐based trajectories from linear mixed‐effects models of executive function cognitive measures (most impacted by SVD) with random intercepts and slopes in Figure 6, comparing biomarker‐positive versus biomarker‐negative groups for each diffusion marker. These model‐based predictions illustrate that individuals with abnormal biomarker values exhibit systematically lower cognition at baseline and steeper decline over time, confirming the usefulness of the cutpoints in identifying those with early SVD and higher risk of cognitive impairment in the population.

DISCUSSION

Recent work has brought dMRI to the forefront as an early indicator of SVD. While strides have been made in developing biomarkers for early SVD, 13 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 26 , 35 cutpoints for defining dMRI biomarker positivity have not previously been established which is an important gap for identification of early SVD in clinical and research settings. In this work, we developed and validated four different approaches for establishing dMRI positivity and selected the optimal method to illustrate its clinical usefulness. Our operationalization identified a large proportion (> 35%) of VCI− participants as Biomarker+ based on dMRI indicating preclinical SVD, and these individuals were at higher risk of cognitive decline. These are findings with important implications for early detection and prevention of VCID.

Development of dMRI cutpoints using the distributions of VCI+ and young normals

High WMH classification, especially using the Fazekas scale, 36 is a well‐established methodology for identifying SVD and is associated with greater risk of cognitive impairment, stroke, 37 and significant VCID. Coupling WMH positivity (based on Fazekas ≥ 2) with early cognitive impairment (CDR ≥ 0.5) (similar to 5 , 38 ) allowed us to identify a VCI+ population that guided dMRI cutpoints. About 15%–18% of the general population age 65+ is cognitively impaired, 39 , 40 consistent with our observations. We found that 56% of our cognitively impaired population (9% of the total cohort) were VCI+ based on the WMH classification. While this is a gross underestimate relative to the pathological literature, 34 the VCI+ classification served two important purposes: to provide a definitive disease group for cutpoints, and to serve as a calibration benchmark to assess alignment with expected disease timing, as nearly all VCI+ individuals should already be Biomarker+ (dMRI markers change earlier than WMH 12 , 13 , 35 ). The young normal group was also an important anchor because SVD is minimal among those younger than 50 years of age. While some variability exists (e.g., an individual under 50 with early SVD), using these two groups as representative extremes of dMRI normality and abnormality aided our study.

Systematic evaluation of cutpoint methods

To date, a significant gap in the field has been the lack of systematic operationalization of dMRI cutpoints, which the current study addresses. A recent study established an optimal cutoff of 0.293 for FW content to predict WMH progression, using the Liu method with sensitivity 0.70 and specificity 0.66. 41 No other work has defined cutpoints for PSMD, ARTS, FA‐Genu, and FW together within a single framework anchored to VCI positivity. The proposed approach offers a foundational framework for these four dMRI biomarkers, defining biologically meaningful thresholds anchored to empirical distributions and classification performance among VCI ± individuals from the MCSA.

Among the four thresholding strategies evaluated, the Youden‐based young referent criterion consistently demonstrated the most balanced and robust classification performance across age. In Figure 4, it yields a proportion of VCI− individuals labeled as Biomarker+ that stably increases with age while remaining moderate compared to the overly permissive (control tail percentile‐based) or overly restrictive (age‐/sex‐matched) thresholds. At age 80, it identifies 78% of the VCI− cohort as abnormal for FW and 88% for ARTS, placing it between the control tail percentile (90% for FW, 96% for ARTS) and age‐/sex‐matched (19% for FW, 33% for ARTS) thresholds. In Figure 3, this criterion also achieved high sensitivity with minimal loss of PPV, particularly between ages 75 and 90 where VCI+ prevalence is greatest.

The age/sex‐matched referent criterion performed worst, likely due to the significant proportion of the older population who have some degree of SVD but are still VCI−. Both case tail percentile and control tail percentile criteria biased performance toward sensitivity and specificity and did not outperform the young referent criterion. The young referent group likely represents the cleanest control group, underpinning its superior performance. At age 80, the estimates of Biomarker+ in the VCI− population for FA‐Genu were: FA‐Genu 62%, FW 78%, ARTS 88%, and PSMD 61%, with FA‐Genu and PSMD being more conservative than FW and ARTS.

Clinical relevance of operationalizing dMRI positivity

Establishing dMRI positivity is clinically important for tracking evolution of SVD pathology prior to symptom onset, enhancing the interpretability of longitudinal trajectories. These empirically defined thresholds can also be incorporated into biomarker staging frameworks such as those developed for Alzheimer's disease. Based on results from Figure 2, Figure 3, and Figure 4, we selected cutpoints contrasting the young referent group against VCI+, yielding 0.36 for 1 − FA‐Genu (i.e., 0.64 for FA‐Genu), 0.22 for FW, −0.47 for ARTS, and 0.000172 for PSMD. These cutpoints were further evaluated in longitudinal models of executive function, stratifying participants by dMRI positivity. In Figure 6, Biomarker+ groups showed consistently lower baseline executive function and more rapid decline across all four diffusion markers, supporting the clinical relevance of the cutpoints and their utility in identifying individuals at higher risk for vascular cognitive decline prior to overt diagnosis. When applied in the clinic‐based Mayo ADRC cohort, patients classified as Biomarker+ by young referent–based cutpoints were more likely to be VCI+, demonstrating that the thresholds capture VCID in a heterogeneous clinical setting where cognitive variability may stem from multiple etiologies. Rather than failing to generalize, the cutpoints consistently identified VCID outside the population‐based MCSA, underscoring both their robustness and translational utility. Given the lack of a definitive in vivo gold standard for VCID, these thresholds should be interpreted as research‐grade cutpoints for harmonized reporting and cohort stratification of vascular cognitive risk, rather than as pathology‐confirmed diagnostic criteria.

Practical interpretation of dMRI positivity

A strength of this work was the broad range of dMRI quantification methods chosen, each capturing white matter changes in a distinct way. FA‐Genu is a simple regional measure of water molecule directionality in the frontal interhemispheric connections. PSMD summarizes the whole brain signal by estimating peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity of the white matter. Free water quantifies unrestricted water molecules in the white matter. ARTS combines demographics, FLAIR MRI, and regional diffusion features into a composite score reflecting arteriolosclerosis‐related risk. Given the absence of a gold standard, differences reflect marker behavior under the operational definition, not definitive diagnostic distinctions. While the four dMRI measures are computed differently, each classified a substantial proportion of VCI+ participants as Biomarker+ under the young referent criterion, and associations with executive function z‐score trajectories were broadly similar across markers (Figure 6). At age 80, all markers were at least 60% positive in the VCI− population (FA‐Genu 62%, FW 78%, ARTS 88%, PSMD 61%). However, the age pattern differed: FA‐Genu and PSMD showed a more gradual Biomarker+ prevalence increase over age (Figure 4), whereas FW and ARTS reached near‐universal positivity by age 90, suggesting ceiling effects at advanced ages. The Religious Orders Study and Rush Memory and Aging Project (average age 89) report that roughly one‐third of non‐demented individuals do not have substantial vessel pathology 2 , highlighting that near‐universal positivity at advanced ages should not be interpreted as true VCID prevalence. FA‐Genu cutpoints were stable across the other criteria (except age/sex‐matched referent), suggesting relative stability of this threshold. Formal head‐to‐head comparison against a shared ground truth will require pathology‐anchored or adjudicated VCID outcomes and prespecified cross‐marker calibration.

Limitations and future research

Given the predictive role of white matter abnormalities for cognitive decline, 42 we evaluated age‐related Biomarker+ trajectories across the four criteria to assess their generalizability across the aging continuum (Figure S1). Further research is needed to examine the age neutrality of the cutpoints. Although the Youden‐based young referent criterion consistently yielded strong early classification performance (Figure 3), FW and ARTS trajectories show over 70% positivity by age 80, indicating reduced discriminatory power in advanced age. The Youden‐based age‐/sex‐matched referent cutpoints maintained a more stable percentage of Biomarker+ at advanced ages but showed lower sensitivity for early detection. These findings reflect the trade‐offs inherent in cutpoint selection and echo concerns about applicability across the aging continuum and in mixed pathology settings. 17 , 43 For early or preclinical detection, a threshold defined against younger unimpaired controls is preferred.

Pathology remains a definitive gold standard for VCID. We relied on the working definition for VCI+/− using WMH burden and global CDR that aligns with the current consensus in the field (VasCog‐2‐WSO Criteria 32 ). This may misclassify some individuals. The cutpoints should therefore be interpreted as research thresholds conditional on this working definition. Consistent with prior work, 44 WMH should be viewed as a non‐specific marker reflecting vascular injury and axonal degeneration due to neurodegenerative pathology, motivating our combined operational VCI definition rather than WMH alone as ground truth. Our primary contribution is the comparative evaluation of cutpoint derivation strategies for early VCID detection, supported by held‐out testing, external validation, and longitudinal cognitive associations, with future recalibration expected as adjudicated or pathology‐anchored standards become available.

These cutpoints, derived within the MCSA, may require calibration in broader populations or alternative acquisition protocols. Future work should integrate harmonization approaches and leverage these cutpoints in multimodal models alongside AD‐related biomarkers to improve mechanistic understanding of disease co‐progression. They may also inform composite risk scoring systems or serve as eligibility criteria in cerebrovascular trials, moving our findings beyond methodological development toward translational applications in clinical research and precision medicine.
