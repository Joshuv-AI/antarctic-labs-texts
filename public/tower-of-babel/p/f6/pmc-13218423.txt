Directed evolution of APOX for proximity labeling using phenols with high redox potentials
Authors: Sifei Fang, Leslie D Acevedo, Alexander J Solivais, Xu Zhou, Swapnali S Patil, Elliot S Delfosse, Hang Yin, Zachary B Ziolkowski, Brian L Frey, Edwin R Chapman, Lloyd M Smith, Jeffrey D Martell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYProximity labeling (PL) can identify endogenous proteins in specific subcellular regions. APEX2 (enhanced ascorbate peroxidase 2) enables PL with high temporal resolution by oxidizing biotin phenol (BP) into a radical that promiscuously tags nearby proteins, followed by the isolation and identification of the tagged biomolecules. However, the BP radical has a relatively large diffusion radius, limiting spatial resolution. Replacing the phenol in BP with nitrophenol (NP) could shorten the labeling radius by shortening the radical lifetime, but existing PL heme peroxidases cannot efficiently oxidize substrates with high redox potentials. We report the ultrahigh-throughput directed evolution of APOX (APEX with high redOX potential), a quadruple mutant of APEX2 with a higher reduction potential. Using APOX with a membrane-permeable alkyne-NP probe, we demonstrate PL in living mammalian cells, including proteomic mapping with excellent subcellular compartment specificity.

INTRODUCTION

Proximity labeling (PL) is a powerful method for determining which biomolecules reside in specific subcellular locations.1,2 In PL, a catalyst is targeted to a subcellular region or fused to a protein of interest, where it tags endogenous biomolecules within a defined radius, allowing for the subsequent isolation and identification of the tagged molecules (Figure 1A). Most PL catalysts convert a small molecule substrate into a reactive intermediate that tags proximal biomolecules but is quenched before it can diffuse far enough to tag distant biomolecules. The longest-standing PL methodologies employ genetically encodable enzymes, such as biotin ligases3-6 and peroxidases.7-18 Recently, synthetic photocatalysts have been developed for PL using highly reactive intermediates with shorter diffusion radii.19-23

The heme peroxidase APEX2 is active in all subcellular compartments24 and affords excellent temporal resolution (10-s labeling time) in proteomic mapping,25-27 enabled by its rapid kinetics in oxidizing the probe biotin phenol (→). APEX2 catalyzes the H2O2-dependent oxidation of BP to a phenoxy radical, which covalently tags endogenous proteins through oxidative coupling to tyrosines (Figure 1B).28 Despite the advantages of the APEX2/BP PL system, BP radicals diffuse a substantial distance in some contexts, limiting spatial resolution due to tagging of endogenous proteins not immediately proximal to APEX2 (Figure 1C). In extracellular environments, biotin phenoxy radicals generated by heme peroxidases can diffuse >250 nm.29,30 Within living cells, radical quenchers restrict the labeling radius,31 but proteins outside the immediate area of interest are nonetheless labeled in intracellular PL experiments.16,25

One approach to tune the APEX2 labeling radius would be to replace the BP radical with a radical that has a shorter lifetime (Figure 1C). Indeed, toward this goal, a variety of phenol and aniline derivatives have been evaluated as potential peroxidase substrates for PL.12,32 Replacing BP with biotin-aminophenol derivatives altered which endogenous proteins were labeled, suggesting potential changes in the diffusion radius.12 However, phenols with high oxidation potentials, such as nitrophenol (NP), were poor substrates. NPs exhibit 0.2–0.4 V higher redox potentials and 4–13 kcal/mol larger O-H bond dissociation energies (BDEs) compared to unsubstituted phenols,33,34 due to the nitro group stabilizing the phenol while destabilizing the phenoxy radical (Figure 1D).34-36 NP-based probes would be promising for restricting the PL diffusion radius because para-nitrophenoxy radicals have shorter lifetimes than unsubstituted phenoxy radicals based on time-resolved spectroscopy experiments.37 We hypothesized that ortho-nitrophenoxy radicals would be similarly short-lived or shorter-lived than para-nitrophenoxy radicals because strong σ-withdrawing groups destabilize phenoxy radicals35 and the ortho-nitro group is adjacent to the phenoxy radical. Furthermore, NP radicals have a smaller diffusion radius based on super-resolution microscopy (vide infra). Despite the potential benefits of NP-based probes, they have not yet been successfully employed for PL, despite multiple attempts.7,12

The lack of reactivity of APEX2 with NPs can be attributed to the reduction potential of the enzyme intermediate in the rate-limiting step (Compound II, ~1.0 V)38-41 being lower than the oxidation potential of nitrophenol (~1.2 V)42 (Figures 1D-1F). One potential approach to overcome this low reactivity would be to identify natural heme peroxidases with higher reduction potentials. Lignin peroxidases are naturally occurring heme peroxidases that exhibit high reduction potentials (>1.25 V)43; however, lignin peroxidases are not promising PL catalysts because they suffer from rapid inactivation,44 and they are not applicable in most subcellular compartments owing to their essential disulfide bonds. An alternative approach would be to evolve APEX2 to increase its reduction potential, thus maintaining the benefits of the APEX2-BP PL system while tuning the labeling radius through the use of high redox potential phenols.

Here, we report the directed evolution of APEX2 to increase its reduction potential and enhance its activity for the oxidation of NPs and other high redox potential substrates (Figure 1G). Two campaigns of yeast display selection from libraries of >107 variants, followed by the combining of beneficial mutations, yielded the quadruple mutant APEX2 (F41V-S69I-Y235F-H239Y), which we call “APOX” (APEX with high redOX potential). APOX is 6-fold more active than APEX2 in oxidizing NPs and several other substrates, including 2.5-fold improved activity in oxidizing BP (the established probe for PL), making it the most active APEX variant reported to date. The improved activity of APOX can be attributed to its higher reduction potential, which increased progressively through the directed evolution campaigns, demonstrating the application of ultrahigh-throughput evolution to increase enzyme reduction potential. Using APOX together with a membrane-permeable nitrophenol-alkyne (NP-A) probe enabled intracellular PL in multiple sub-compartments of living mammalian cells using a 1-min labeling procedure. We demonstrated the utility of the APOX-NP PL system for subcellular proteomic mapping by profiling the mitochondrial matrix, resulting in the detection of thousands of peptides and a proteome with 94% specificity, demonstrating the viability of intracellular PL using an NP probe.

DESIGN

We hypothesized that directed evolution to increase activity toward a high redox potential NP substrate would select for APEX2 mutants with increased reduction potential, analogous to previous selections that increased the reduction potential of laccase.45 We further hypothesized that an NP-based selection would discover mutants with enhanced activity not only toward NPs, but also toward other phenol substrates, given the promiscuity of the heme peroxidase superfamily.46 We used yeast surface display as our screening platform to take advantage of its ultrahigh throughput (screening >107 mutants), which exceeds the throughput of the spectrophotometric assay previously used to screen 2.4 × 103 laccase mutants.45

RESULTS

Yeast display evolution

Figure 2A shows our workflow for yeast display selection, which builds upon previous selections for the oxidation of para-alkyl substituted phenols.10,13,47 A library of yeast cells is prepared with each cell displaying many copies of an APEX2 mutant. Next, either biotin nitrophenol (BNP) or nitrophenol-alkyne (NP-A) is introduced. High-activity APEX2 mutants generate NP radicals, which we hypothesized would tag endogenous proteins on the same yeast cell but become quenched before tagging neighboring cells. Finally, the yeast cells are stained with a fluorescently labeled streptavidin, and the brightest cells are isolated using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS).

We first synthesized the probes BNP and NP-A (Figures S94-S97), then established a yeast self-labeling procedure for the selection. Use of substrate concentrations that are standard for BP PL (100 μM BNP and H2O2) gave no detectable labeling. However, BNP staining was detectable when much lower concentrations of H2O2 were used (1–5 μM), with 1 μM H2O2 giving the strongest staining (Figure S5). These findings are reminiscent of the original APEX enzyme, which exhibited slow BP oxidation at high H2O2 concentrations.10 APEX2 oxidizes BP faster than APEX does, resulting in greater resistance to inhibition by H2O2.10 We hypothesized that APEX2 was an analogously poor catalyst for BNP oxidation and thus prone to inhibition by H2O2. We selected 5 μM H2O2 and 100 μM BNP as promising substrate concentrations for the selection.

We next prepared a library of yeast cells displaying APEX2 mutants. We used error-prone PCR, which distributes mutations throughout the protein sequence,48 to create a library of 1.6 × 107 variants exhibiting an average of two mutations.10,13 We hypothesized that we could identify mutations that influence APEX2 reduction potential without necessarily being close to the substrate binding site.49-52 We labeled the yeast library using BNP and sorted cells exhibiting the highest ratio of BNP labeling to enzyme expression. After four rounds of selection, activity as a function of expression had increased for the yeast population (Figure 2B and (Figures S6-S8). Enriched APEX2 mutant plasmids were extracted from yeast and transformed into E.coli for DNA sequencing. We identified F41V as a promising mutation due to its proximity to the heme cofactor (Table S11). As discussed later in discussion, APEX2 (F41V) exhibited a 2-fold improvement in NP oxidation activity relative to APEX2 (vide infra).

We performed a second campaign of directed evolution, using APEX2 (F41V) as the template for error-prone PCR to create a new yeast library of 1.5 × 107 variants. Here, we used the NP-A probe instead of BNP, owing to its preferability for mammalian cell applications (vide infra), and we applied more stringent selection conditions: 50 μM NP-A, 100 μM H2O2, and 1 min labeling time, as compared to the 10-min labeling time in Evolution I. After 4 rounds of sorting, the activity of the yeast population increased substantially (Figure 2C). DNA sequencing revealed several recurring mutations, including R31G, S69I, S69R, A94G, P178S, and the double mutant Y235F + H239Y (Figure 2D and Table S7). As discussed later in discussion, several of these mutations are near the heme cofactor or the substrate binding site.

In vitro characterization of the purified enzymes

To determine which APEX2 variants were most promising, we expressed and purified His6-tagged APEX2 mutants from E. coli (Figure S9)9,10 and measured their activities by monitoring the depletion of NP-A using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Figure 3A and Figure S11). Enzyme-catalyzed oxidation of NP probes generated multiple new products with mass-to-charge ratios corresponding to oxidatively cross-linked NPs, as characterized by ultra-HPLC-mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS) (Figure S10). Next, we characterized the initial rates of NP-A depletion by the APEX2 variants, using substrate concentrations relevant for PL in mammalian cells (Figure 3A). The F41V mutation increased the initial rate 2-fold, while the installation of additional mutations from the second evolution campaign increased activity further. APEX2 (F41V-Y235F-H239Y) and APEX2 (F41V-S69I) exhibited 5.2-fold and 4.8-fold faster initial rates, respectively, compared to APEX2. A different S69 mutant, APEX2 (F41V-S69R), showed a somewhat smaller 3.6-fold improvement compared to APEX2. APEX2 (F41V-A94G) was also 3.6-fold more active than APEX2.

We next combined beneficial mutations to generate the quadruple mutant APEX2 (F41V-S69I-Y235F-H239Y), which we call “APOX.” APOX exhibited 6-fold faster NP depletion than APEX2. APOX exhibited approximately 6-fold higher activity than APEX2 at NP-A concentrations ranging from 100 to 2400 μM, which spans the phenol concentration range previously used for PL in mammalian cells (Figure 3B and Figures S13-S54).7,12,15,16,32 We also used HPLC to measure the depletion of a variety of phenols (Figure 3C and Figures S12-S60). APOX exhibited a 2.5-fold faster initial rate of BP depletion compared to APEX2, as well as 10- and 8.8-fold improved activity toward acetyl-phenol and cyano-phenol, respectively, which have oxidation potentials of ~1.17 V and ~1.25 V.42 These results demonstrate that the enhanced activity of APOX extends to electron-deficient phenol substrates beyond NPs. Using a guaiacol oxidation assay, APOX exhibited a similar total turnover number to APEX2 (Figure S61). APOX was the most active variant we tested; adding the R31G, A94G, or P178S mutation onto APOX led to decreased activity, possibly due to the destabilization of the protein (Figure 3A and Figure S49).

To gain insight into potential mechanisms of activity enhancement, we prepared Rosetta structural models54,55 of APEX2 and APOX based on the structure of wild-type ascorbate peroxidase (Figure 3D).53 In the relaxed APEX2 Rosetta model, the F41 residue is located ~3.5 Å from the heme cofactor, behind the ironoxo in Compound II and away from the δ-heme edge where phenol substrates bind.56 We hypothesize that the F41V mutation removes a π stacking interaction, thus increasing the reactivity of Compound II and accelerating the rate-determining step of the catalytic cycle (Figure 1A).57 This hypothesis is consistent with previous findings that mutations at residue 41 can influence peroxidase structure, heme spin state, and catalytic activity.9,58-64 The Y235F and H239Y mutations, which arose in the second evolution campaign and only occurred together, also alter residues that are close to the heme cofactor (~5 Å) but far from the phenol binding site. Both of these mutations (Y235F and H239Y) were necessary for the enhanced activity of the double mutant; APEX2-F41V-H239Y exhibited similar activity to APEX2-F41V, while APEX2-F41V-Y235F exhibited a ~60% decrease in activity relative to APEX2-F41V (Figure S49). This result underscores the power of ultrahigh-throughput evolution to discover synergistic combinations of activity-boosting mutations. Finally, the S69I mutation is at the phenol substrate binding site, >10 Å above the δ-heme edge. Considering that horseradish peroxidase (HRP) contains a Phe residue at the homologous position that forms part of a hydrophobic binding pocket implicated in binding phenol substrates,65,66 we hypothesize that the hydrophobic Ile residue in the S69I mutant might facilitate the recognition of phenol substrates at low concentrations, rather than influencing enzyme redox potential. Consistent with this hypothesis, the S69I mutation increased the rate of phenol oxidation while avoiding H2O2-induced inhibition, including for the low redox potential substrate guaiacol (Figure S62 and Note S2). This behavior is reminiscent of previous observations for the A134P mutation in APEX2, which is also near the δ-heme edge (Note S2).10 Several other beneficial mutations characterized in Figure 3A are close to the heme cofactor and may modulate reduction potential (Note S2 and Figures S42-S63).

To test the reduction potentials of our APEX2 variants, we characterized APOX, APEX2, and several intermediate variants using the xanthine/xanthine oxidase dye reduction assay (Figure 3E).39,67,68 This assay determines the heme Fe3+/Fe2+ reduction potential, which correlates to the Fe4+ = O (Compound II)/Fe3+ reduction potential that is relevant for the rate-determining step in the peroxidase catalytic cycle (Figure 1E).69 In this assay, xanthine oxidase continuously reduces a heme peroxidase and a reference dye, and the extent of reduction of each component depends on its reduction potential (see STAR Methods, Note 3, and Figures S64-S76). We characterized the Fe3+/Fe2+ reduction potential of APEX2 using the reference dye phenosafranin, which has a reduction potential of −252 mV (Figure 3F and Figure S64). We determined the APEX2 Fe3+/Fe2+ reduction potential to be −237 mV, which, according to a previously reported correlation formula, indicates an APEX2 Fe4+ = O (Compound II)/Fe3+ reduction potential of +1002 mV (Note S3 and Figure S64).69 As a control, we determined the HRP Fe3+/Fe2+ reduction potential to be −263 mV, in good agreement with the previous reports (Figure S67).70 When we tested APOX under matched conditions, it was reduced to a much greater extent than phenosafranin, indicating it has a higher reduction potential than APEX2 (Figures 3F-3H and Figures S64-S66). Because the xanthine oxidase assay is only reliable when the difference in reduction potential is < 30 mV, we cannot precisely quantify the APOX Fe3+/Fe2+ reduction potential based on this result, but we can conclude that it is > −222 mV (Figure S66). APEX2 (F41V) exhibited an intermediate reduction potential between APEX2 and APOX (Figure 4H and Figure S65). We additionally tested APEX2, APEX2 (F41V), and APOX against the reference dye Nile Blue, which has a reduction potential of −116 mV. All three enzymes exhibited reduction potentials < −156 mV, with APOX being reduced to the greatest extent, indicating it has a higher reduction potential than APEX2 (F41V) and APEX2 (Figures 68-70). Collectively, these results indicate that the Fe3+/Fe2+ reduction potential of APOX is between −212 mV and −156 mV, corresponding to a Fe4+ = O (Compound II)/Fe3+ reduction potential between 1045 mV and 1107 mV,69 substantially higher than the Fe4+ = O (Compound II)/Fe3+ reduction potential of APEX2. These findings suggest that even for APOX, the oxidation of NPs (oxidation potential ~1.2 V42) is likely endergonic, but less endergonic than the corresponding reactions for APEX2 and HRP. Heme peroxidases can oxidize phenol substrates in endergonic reactions, with the reaction becoming slower as ΔG becomes more positive.69 Even for endergonic reactions, the forward reaction can occur to some extent, and in this case, the reaction product is a phenoxy radical that diffuses away from the enzyme and is quenched irreversibly.

Activity of evolved mutants in mammalian cells

We next investigated whether APOX can be used for NP-based PL in living mammalian cells. We evaluated two probes: BNP and NP-A. BNP was unsuitable for intracellular PL in living cells, likely owing to a lack of plasma membrane permeability (Note S4 and Figures S77-S80). These results indicate that previous attempts to use APEX with BNP for PL7 failed not only because of low enzymatic activity, but also due to a lack of probe access to the relevant subcellular compartments. We hypothesized NP-A would have improved membrane permeability compared to BNP, considering that phenol-alkyne was previously shown to be more permeable to the cell interior compared to BP.11,71,72

To test the suitability of NP-A for intracellular PL, we cloned plasmids encoding APEX variants fused to a mitochondrial matrix targeting peptide and transfected them nito HEK293T cells. After intracellular NP-A labeling, alkyne-tagged proteins can be detected by the attachment of azide-biotin/desthiobiotin using copper-click chemistry, followed by fluorescent streptavidin staining and imaging (Figure 4A). Alternatively, cells can be lysed, followed by the copper-click attachment of biotin and detection of labeled proteins using western blotting or mass spectrometry. NP-A labeled endogenous proteins were clearly detected by western blotting, and omission of transfection, NP-A, or the copper-click reagents abolished the signal (Figure 4B).

We assessed whether the activity trends observed for in vitro purified enzymes (Figure 3A) correlated with the NP-A labeling signal in HEK293T cells, considering that some APEX variants previously performed well in vitro but not in mammalian cells.9 APEX2(F41V) gave substantially improved signal compared to APEX2 in HEK293T cells, and additional beneficial mutations boosted activity further (Figure 4C). The same trends were observed between in vitro and HEK293T experiments for the variants, with APOX being the most active mutant (Figure 4C and Figures S81-S83 and S101). We compared NP-A labeling intensity by APEX2 and APOX in multiple cellular compartments, including the mitochondrial matrix, the ER-Golgi lumen, and the cytosol (Figure 4D and Figures S81-S83). APEX2 gave detectable staining in a subset of transfected cells, but APOX gave a dramatically brighter signal in all compartments. Increasing the NP-A concentration from 100 to 500 μM boosted the signal substantially for APOX, but did not improve the staining for APEX2 (Figure S84). We observed no differences in enzyme localization or organelle morphology for APOX relative to APEX2 in identical expression contexts, indicating that the higher redox potential of APOX did not lead to changes in subcellular localization or toxicity (Figures S99 and S100). To assess the suitability of APOX as a non-perturbing genetic tag, we fused it to connexin43 (Cx43). The endogenous function of Cx43 is to form gap junctions at cell-cell contacts, but when perturbing genetic tags, such as dimeric proteins, are appended to its C-terminus, it fails to form gap junctions and instead localizes to intracellular aggregates.9,10 The Cx43–APOX fusion protein did indeed properly form gap junctions, as visualized using an mCherry tag, suggesting that APOX behaves similarly to APEX2 as a genetic tag (Figure 4E and Figure S85). To test if APOX-NPA is suitable for studies in primary cells, we also tested APEX2 and APOX with NP-A for PL in cultured rat cortical neurons under similar expression levels (Figure 4F and Figure S86). APOX again showed high labeling intensity while APEX2 yielded a low level of labeling in both fluorescence imaging and western blotting (Figure S87).

Super-resolution imaging to characterize labeling radius

To investigate the PL radius of nitrophenoxy radicals, we used a stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy assay (Figure 5A and Figures S1-S4).29 We coated glass coverslips with the protein bovine serum albumin (BSA), targeted HRP to the BSA surface using antibodies, and added BP or BNP along with H2O2 to generate phenoxy radicals. Finally, the surface was stained with streptavidin-AlexaFluor594 (sAv-AF594), and STED microscopy was performed to quantify the radii of individual puncta (see Note S1 and STAR Methods). We opted to characterize the labeling radius using this in vitro surface labeling and microscopy assay, rather than a live-cell labeling and microscopy assay, to avoid the previously reported complication of labeled proteins diffusing from the site of tagging, which can lead to a diffusive signal that is difficult to interpret.15,16,73

Using 50 μM BP, a typical concentration used for PL,71 we detected fluorescent puncta on the BSA surface with an average full-width at half maximum (FWHM) of 140 ± 13 nm (Figures 5B and 5C). Using BNP instead of BP, we also observed fluorescent puncta, indicating that NP radicals are capable of tagging BSA. The BNP puncta exhibited a substantially shorter average FWHM of 83 ± 12 nm, even though we used 500 μM BNP, which was 10-fold higher than the BP concentration used (Figure 5C). Higher phenol substrate concentrations increased the apparent labeling radius for both BP and BNP, but the BNP labeling radius remained shorter under all conditions tested (Figure S3). Because the endogenous cellular environment is crowded and contains quenchers,1 the labeling radii are likely shorter inside living cells than indicated by this assay. To gain further insights into the labeling radius of BNP versus BP using a different assay, we performed in vitro labeling using carbonic anhydrase as a model protein, with varying degrees of dilution. With 10 μM carbonic anhydrase and 1 μM peroxidase, both BNP and BP exhibited strong biotin labeling as detected by western blotting (Figure S98). Decreasing enzyme and carbonic anhydrase concentrations by increasing the reaction volume caused a substantial decrease in biotin labeling for BNP, but not for BP, suggesting that labeling by BNP radicals had a stronger dependence on the average intermolecular distance. Overall, these results indicate that installing electron-withdrawing substituents on phenoxy radicals is a promising strategy to modulate the labeling radius.

Proteomic mapping using nitrophenol probes

To test the utility of the APOX/NP-A PL system for mass spectrometry proteomics, we first determined the nature of the chemical linkages between the NP-A probe and endogenous proteins. Although the western blotting results in Figure 5B demonstrate tagging of endogenous mammalian proteins, they do not reveal which amino acids were tagged. We performed in vitro labeling of the model protein carbonic anhydrase with NP-A,74 then performed copper-click to add desthiobiotin, streptavidin pull-down to enrich the desthiobiotin-labeled proteins, and elution of the labeled proteins using biotin (Figure 6A). After digestion and mass spectrometry analysis, we detected numerous labeled peptides with a mass shift of 688.35 Da, matching the expected addition of the NPA-desthiobiotin conjugate (Figures S88-S90 and Table 13). We detected labeling at five Tyr and two Trp residues on carbonic anhydrase at solvent-exposed positions (Figure 6B). The propensity of NP radicals to tag Tyr and Trp residues is consistent with previous reports on unsubstituted phenoxy radicals.7,28,74,75

We applied the APOX/NP-A PL system for spatially resolved proteomic mapping of the mitochondrial matrix in living cells. HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids that expressed APOX in either the mitochondrial matrix or the cytosol. Transfected cells were labeled with 500 μM NP-A/H2O2 for 1 min and lysed, and copper-click chemistry was used to conjugate desthiobiotin-PEG3-azide to NP-A-labeled proteins. Portions of the cell lysates were then incubated with streptavidin-agarose beads to enrich NP-labeled proteins. Bound proteins were eluted with biotin, yielding samples enriched for mitochondrial matrix proteins (Mito Eluate) and cytosolic proteins (Cyto Eluate). Additionally, portions of the cell lysate from both the mitochondrial matrix and cytosolic labeling conditions were reserved prior to enrichment as negative controls (Mito Input and Cyto Input, respectively). Successful enrichment was confirmed by western blotting (Figure S91).

We evaluated the specificity of the mapping procedure through mass spectrometry-based proteomic profiling and label-free quantification (Figure 6C). Five biological replicates from each condition (Mito Eluate, Cyto Eluate, Mito Input, Cyto Input) were prepared and analyzed with LC-MS/MS. Approximately 3000 proteins were identified and quantified in each replicate. We performed differential expression analysis to identify proteins that were differentially abundant between the Mito Eluate and Cyto Eluate conditions (comparison I) and between the Mito Eluate and Mito Input conditions (comparison II). We used Gene Ontology Cellular Component (GOCC) annotations to identify the localization of each protein to a specific subcellular compartment (Tables S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, and S6). The results of comparisons I and II are shown in Figure 6D and Figures S92 and S93. For both comparisons, the majority of proteins that were significantly enriched in the Mito Eluate condition were annotated with localization to the mitochondrial matrix or the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM). A few proteins that were less abundant in the Mito Eluate condition were also annotated with IMM localization, but these proteins reside on the outer face of the IMM, without exposure to the matrix subcompartment (where APOX resides), and thus were not expected to be enriched.76

To determine the spatial specificity of the APOX/NP-A PL system, we compiled lists of proteins that were significantly enriched after APOX labeling of the mitochondrial matrix and streptavidin pull-down. The lists of proteins enriched in the Mito Eluate condition from Comparison I (Mito Eluate vs. Cyto Eluate) and Comparison II (Mito Eluate vs. Mito Input) were filtered based on statistical significance (adjusted p-value <0.05). Additionally, proteins that were observed only in the Mito Eluate condition (and thus could not be assigned a p-value) were retained if they were observed in at least 4/5 replicates. This filtering gave rise to two enriched proteomes: The “Mito vs. Cyto” Proteome I, which contains 229 proteins; and the “Mito Eluate vs. Mito Input” Proteome II, which contains 346 proteins. Both Proteomes I and II showed high specificity, with 201/229 (88%) proteins in Proteome I and 278/346 (80%) in Proteome II annotated as mitochondrial by GOCC (Figure 6E). The intersection of the two datasets had an even higher specificity, with 176/187 proteins (94%) annotated as mitochondrial. We note that previous studies using the APEX-BP system yielded mitochondrial matrix proteomes with similar specificities without any need for a cytosolic spatial reference sample (such as our Proteome I),7 which may reflect their use of higher accuracy but more expensive stable isotope labeling by amino acids in cell culture (SILAC) rather than label-free quantitation.

An exceptionally high mitochondrial sub-compartment specificity was observed for both Proteomes I and II, based on comparison to MitoCarta 3.0.77 Among all proteins with mitochondrial annotation, 97% proteins had either mitochondrial matrix or mitochondrial inner membrane annotation (Figure 6F). This result indicates that NP-based PL has high spatial specificity and that the NP-A radical exhibits minimal cross-membrane labeling. Nearly all electron transport chain proteins lacking exposure to the mitochondrial matrix were not detected in Proteome I or Proteome II, as expected (Figure 6G, Table S1). One exception was Complex III subunit H, which is not expected to have exposure to the mitochondrial matrix based on structural studies.78,79 Interestingly, this protein was previously detected in a mitochondrial matrix proteome using the PL photocatalyst miniSOG.80 Compared to mitochondrial matrix proteomes previously determined using PL methodologies,7,80 our proteomes exhibited a higher coverage of electron transport chain proteins with exposure to the mitochondrial matrix (Table S1).

DISCUSSION

The APEX2-BP system has been widely deployed for PL with high temporal resolution, but the diffusion of biotin phenoxy radicals limits spatial resolution.29,30 One idea to tune the APEX2 labeling radius is to replace BP with phenols that correspond to more reactive radicals,7,12 but high redox potential phenols have previously been inaccessible owing to the inability of existing PL enzymes (e.g., APEX2 and HRP) to oxidize these substrates. We have overcome this limitation by developing APOX, a quadruple mutant of APEX2 with a higher redox potential. We developed APOX through two sequential campaigns of ultra-high-throughput directed evolution using high redox potential NP probes, which enabled us to identify beneficial mutations throughout the enzyme structure. Three of the mutations in APOX (F41V, Y235F, and H239Y) are positioned near the heme cofactor but far from the substrate binding site, and they collectively increase the enzyme reduction potential (Figures 3F-3H). The fourth mutation, S69I, does not affect enzyme reduction potential (Figure S73) and enhances phenolic substrate recognition (Figure S62 and Note S2).65,66

The enhanced activity of APOX allowed us to perform spatially-resolved proteomic mapping using a high redox potential phenol probe. We determined that the biotin nitrophenol (BNP) probe, which had been tested in prior studies,7,12 is not permeable across the membrane of living mammalian cells. We performed successful PL using the membrane-permeable NP-A probe together with APOX. Importantly, APOX exhibits good expression and high activity in multiple compartments of mammalian cells. The increased reduction potential of APOX relative to APEX2 does not hinder its expression or catalytic activity, illustrating a benefit of activity-based ultrahigh-throughput evolution, which selects for enzymes that express well and exhibit a high turnover number.

Although the nitrophenol probes reported here offer a smaller labeling radius than BP, the peroxidase-BP PL system is a powerful tool and should continue to be used in situations where its larger labeling radius can be tolerated. APEX2 is highly active in the oxidation of BP, making it suitable for most BP PL experiments. The 2.5-fold greater activity of APOX toward BP may make it a valuable alternative to APEX2 for BP PL when the peroxidase needs to be expressed at low levels.

The development of APOX sets the stage for future investigations of PL using probes with even higher redox potentials to further tune the labeling radius and expand the scope of labeling chemistries. We determined here that nitrophenoxy radicals predominantly react with Tyr and Trp side chains, similar to phenoxy radicals.28 A promising future direction will be to generate radicals that can tag diverse amino acids to improve the detection of proteins that lack solvent-exposed Tyr and Trp residues. It will be promising to explore whether high redox potential substrates can enhance tagging of nucleic acids, which do not react efficiently with biotin phenoxy radicals,32 hampering applications in spatially resolved transcriptomics. While replacing BP with biotin-aniline derivatives enhances reactivity toward nucleic acids,32 APOX expands the scope of accessible substrates and opens opportunities to generate more reactive intermediates.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we demonstrate that NP radicals have a shorter labeling radius than unsubstituted phenoxy radicals using in vitro protein surface labeling and super-resolution microscopy. A limitation of the current study is that we did not apply NP probes for proteomic mapping in non-membrane bounded compartments, where the shorter labeling radius would be expected to yield higher-specificity proteomic datasets. Although it is likely that NP radicals will give a shorter labeling radius than unsubstituted phenoxy radicals in a variety of cellular contexts, given the shorter in vitro STED labeling radius and the higher redox potentials33 and O-H BDEs of NPs,34 the effective labeling radius may vary with subcellular context. The effective concentrations of probe, enzyme, and potential quenchers will be different depending on the biological sub-compartment. For researchers interested in using APOX for phenol-based PL, we recommend performing side-by-side experiments with both NP-A and the widely-used phenol-based probes such as BP. Considering that APOX is highly active toward phenol- and NP-based probes, researchers can readily clone identical APOX constructs and apply them for parallel PL and proteomics experiments with multiple probes.

Like APEX2, APOX requires H2O2 for activity, which introduces potential for toxicity in live-cell labeling procedures. It is important to perform live-cell H2O2 for the shortest possible time prior to cell lysis to minimize perturbation. We demonstrate here that APOX provides sufficient NP probe labeling to enable proteomics after only 1 min of H2O2 treatment, and based on immunofluorescence we saw no evidence of morphological perturbation for APOX/NP-labeled cells as compared to APEX2/BP-labeled cells (Figures S99 and S100).

HEK 293 T cells from ATCC (CRL-3216, passage number < 25, female origin, authenticated using STR profiling and mycoplasma testing) were cultured as a monolayer in DMEM (ATCC) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS (Corning) and 2 mM L-glutamine (ATCC) (DMEM-FBS) at 37 °C under 5% CO2. Methods

Mammalian cell culture

Prior to cell plating, plates were treated with 13 μg/mL human fibronectin (Sigma) for 30 min at room temperature to improve cell adherence and were washed once with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS, Sigma), pH 7.4 with MgCl2 and CaCl2. HEK293T cells in 48-well plates were transfected at 50–90% confluency using Lipofectamine2000 (L2K; Life Technologies, 11668019). For transfection of samples in 48-well plates, the transfection solution for each well contained 1.33 μL L2K, 100 ng BFP-NLS, and 500 ng plasmid encoding the desired enzyme mutant were mixed in 30 μL DMEM. The transfection solution was allowed to incubate for 30 min and was diluted with an additional 120 μL DMEM. 150 μL diluted transfection solution was added to each well, and the plate was moved into CO2 incubator for 2 h. After 2 h, the transfection solution was replaced with 250 μL DMEM-FBS. For transfection of samples in 6-well plate, the transfection solution contained 10 μL L2K, 2500 ng plasmid encoding the desired enzyme mutant, and 240 μL DMEM. The transfection solution was allowed to incubate for 30 min and was diluted with an additional 1.2 mL DMEM. 1.4 mL diluted transfection solution was added to each well, and the plate was moved into a CO2 incubator for 2 h. After 2 h, the transfection solution was replaced with 2 mL DMEM-FBS. For transfection of samples in tissue culture dishes, 15000 ng plasmid encoding the desired enzyme mutant was mixed with 24 μL L2K in 1.5 mL DMEM. The transfection solution was allowed to incubate for 30 min and was diluted with an additional 5 mL DMEM. 6.5 mL transfection solution was added to each dish, and the dishes were moved into a CO2 incubator for 2 h. After 2 h, the transfection solution was replaced with 10 mL DMEM-FBS.

Labeling of fixed mammalian cells with BP and BNP

16-24 h after transfection, HEK293T cells in 48-well plates were taken out from the incubator and were allowed to sit for 15 min to equilibrate to room temperature. The media was removed by aspiration, and 200 μL 4% formaldehyde in DPBS was added to fix cells for 30 min at room temperature. After 30 min, cells were washed twice with 200 μL DPBS and were labeled with either 200 μL BNP labeling solution (250 μM BNP and 1 mM H2O2 in 200 μL DPBS), or 200 μL BP labeling solution (100 μM BP and 100 μM H2O2 in 200 μL DPBS) for 1 min (BP) or 10 min (BNP). The labeling solution was removed, and cells were washed with 200 μL 10 mM sodium ascorbate once and 200 μL DPBS twice. Cells were permeabilized by treatment with 200 μL cold methanol (−20°C) for 15 min at −20°C. After permeabilization, cells were washed three times with DPBS, blocked with 200 μL DPBS-1% BSA for 15 min on ice, and labeled with 200 μL 1/500 diluted Streptavidin-phycoerythrin conjugate (S886, SA-PE) or SA-AlexaFluor568 (S11226) in DPBS-1% BSA for 30 min on ice. Cells were washed 2 times with DPBS, then submerged in 200 μL DPBS before imaging.

Labeling of living mammalian cells with biotin phenol (BP) and biotin nitrophenol (BNP)

16-24 h after transfection, HEK293T cells in 48-well plates were taken out from the incubator and were allowed to sit for 15 min to equilibrate to room temperature. The media was removed by aspiration. 200 μL BNP incubation solution (250/500/750/1000 μM BNP in DPBS) or BP incubation solution (250 μM BP in DPBS) was added to each well to increase intracellular probe concentration for 30 min at room temperature. After 30 min, the incubation solution was replaced with 200 μL BNP labeling solution (250 μM BNP and 1 mM H2O2 in DPBS) or BP labeling solution (250 μM BP and 1 mM H2O2 in DPBS) for 1 min at room temperature. After 1 min, the labeling solution was removed. Cells were washed with 200 μL 10 mM sodium ascorbate in DPBS once and 200 μL DPBS twice. 200 μL 4% formaldehyde in DPBS was added to each well to fix cells for 30 min at room temperature. After fixation, cells were washed with DPBS twice and were permeabilized by treatment with 200 μL cold methanol (−20°C) for 15 min at −20°C. After permeabilization, cells were washed three times with 200 μL DPBS, blocked with 200 μL DPBS-1% BSA for 15 min on ice, and labeled with 200 μL 1/500 diluted Streptavidin-phycoerythrin conjugate (S886) or SA-AlexaFluor568 (S11226) in DPBS-1% BSA for 30 min on ice. Cells were washed 2 times with DPBS, then submerged in 200 μL DPBS before imaging.

Nitrophenol alkyne (NP-A) labeling in living mammalian cells for fluorescence imaging

16–24 h after transfection, HEK293T cells in 48-well plates were taken out from the incubator and were allowed to sit for 15 min to equilibrate to room temperature. The media was removed by aspiration. 200 μL incubation solution (100/200/500 μM NP-A in DPBS) was added to each well to increase intracellular NP-A concentration for 30 min at room temperature. After 30 min, the incubation solution was replaced with 200 μL labeling solution (100/200/500 μM NP-A with 100/200/500 μM H2O2 in DPBS) for 1 min at room temperature. NP-A labeling in living cells followed by cell lysis and western blotting

16-24 h after transfection, HEK293T cells in 6-well plates were taken out from the incubator and were allowed to sit for 15 min to equilibrate to room temperature. The media was removed by aspiration. 1000 μL incubation solution (300 μM NP-A in DPBS) was added to each well to increase intracellular NP-A concentration for 30 min at room temperature. After 30 min, the incubation solution was replaced with 1000 μL labeling solution (300 μM NP-A and 300 μM H2O2 in DPBS) for 1 min at room temperature. After 1 min, the labeling solution was removed. Cells were washed with 1000 μL 10 mM sodium ascorbate once and 1000 μL DPBS twice. Cells were submerged in 1000 μL DPBS, scraped off the plate using a cell scraper (BX70-1180), and transferred to 1.5 mL epi-tubes. Cells were spun down at 2000 RCF for 4 min. Supernatant was removed by aspiration and cells were resuspended with 200 μL RIPA lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS in Millipore water) and 2 μL broad spectrum protease inhibitor cocktail (Boster Bio, AR1182-A). The copper-click reaction solution was agitated using a ThermoMixer at 500 RPM for 10 min at 4°C. The membrane was stained with Ponceau S staining solution (0.1% w/v Ponceau S (EMD Millipore) in 5% acetic acid/water, 5 min) and washed with DI H2O to visualize total protein. The membrane was then blocked with 2% BSA in 1.2× TBS-T (Tris-Buffered Saline pH 7.5 containing 24 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, 0.6% v/v Tween 20 (DOT Scientific)) for 60 min. After 60 min, the membrane was stained with 1:1000 SA-HRP (ThermoFisher) in 1.2× TBS-T 1% BSA for 60 min. The membrane was washed three times with 1.2× TBS-T (each was 10 min in duration). To visualize labeling, 1 mL Radiance Q chemiluminescent substrate (Azure Biosystems) and 1 mL Radiance Peroxide (Azure Biosystems) were mixed and then immediately added to the membrane. The chemiluminescence was imaged with an Azure C400 Gel Imaging System (Azure Biosystems) using the Chemi setting.

NP-A labeling in living HEK293T cells for label free proteomics

HEK293T cells were plated in 10 × 100 mm × 20 mm tissue culture treated dishes (Dot Scientific 667621) for label-free proteomics. After cells reached ~50% confluency, 5 dishes were each transfected with 15000 ng CMV-MITO-APOX (mitochondrial matrix). The other 5 dishes were each transfected with 15000 ng pCDNA3-whole-cell-APOX (cytosol). 16–24 h after transfection, cells were removed from the incubator and allowed to sit for 15 min to equilibrate to room temperature. The media was removed by pouring. 5 mL incubation solution (500 μM NP-A in DPBS) was added to each dish for 30 min at room temperature. After 30 min, 5 μL 500 mM H2O2 was spiked into each dish to initiate proximity labeling (500 μM NP-A, 500 μM H2O2) for 1 min at room temperature. After 1 min, the labeling solution was removed by pouring. Each dish of cells was washed twice with 5 mL 10 mM sodium ascorbate and 5 mL DPBS. Cells were submerged in 5 mL DPBS, scraped off the plate using a cell scraper (BX70-1180), and transferred to individual 15 mL conical tubes. Cells were centrifuged at 2000 RCF for 4 min. The supernatant was removed by aspiration, and the cells were resuspended with 1.2 mL RIPA lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS in Millipore water) and 12 μL broad spectrum protease inhibitor cocktail (Boster Bio AR1182-A). Proteins were precipitated by mixing with 1 mL room temperature CHCl3, vortexing, mixing with an additional 5 mL−20 °C MeOH, and vortexing again. The samples were then centrifuged at 4300 RCF for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatant was removed, and the protein pellets were washed again with 5 mL−20 °C MeOH. The protein pellets were centrifuged at 4300 RCF for 10 min at room temperature. The protein pellets were air dried at room temperature and resuspended with 600 μL 10 mM no potassium phosphate buffer with 2% SDS pH 7.4 (PBS without K+, with 2% SDS; 10 mM sodium phosphate, 130 mM NaCl, 2% SDS, pH 7.4) using a sonicator. 408 μL homemade copper-click master mix (23.8 mM sodium ascorbate, 980 μM desthiobiotin-PEG3-azide (Sigma 902020), 2.62 mM CuSO4 and 13 mM THPTA in PBS without K+ pH 7.4 (PBS without K+; 10 mM phosphate with no potassium, 130 mM NaCl)) was added to each sample for the copper-click reaction (final concentrations: 9.5 mM sodium ascorbate, 400 μM Desthiobiotin-PEG3-azide, 1.06 mM CuSO4, 5.3 mM THPTA). The copper-click reaction solution was mixed using an orbital shaker at 120 RPM for 60 min at room temperature. After 60 min, the proteins were precipitated with 6 mL 1:5 CHCl3:MeOH (−20°C) and washed with 5 mL−20°C MeOH, following the same procedure described above. The precipitated protein pellets were centrifuged at 4300 RCF for 10 min at room temperature. The supernatants were removed, and protein pellets were air dried. The protein pellets were resuspended with 1 mL PBS without K+ −2% SDS using a sonicator.

Pull-down

200 μL Pierce Streptavidin Agarose beads (Thermo 20349) for each sample were washed once with 1 mL PBS without K+. Supernatant was removed by aspiration. 1 mL solutions containing desthiobiotin-labeled proteins in PBS without K+ −2% SDS were added to beads. Samples were incubated at room temperature on a rotator at 60 RPM for 3 to 15 h. After incubation, the beads were centrifuged at 2000 RCF for 3 min. The supernatants were saved for Western blotting analysis. The beads were washed with 7 × 1 mL PBS without K+ −2% SDS, with centrifugation at 2000 RCF for 3 min for each wash. After 7 washes, the supernatant was removed by aspiration. 1 mL elution solution (30 mM biotin, 10 mM phosphate without K+, 170 mM NaCl, 2% SDS, pH 7.4) was added to each sample. Samples were incubated with rotation at 60 RPM at room temperature for 1–3 h for elution. After elution, the beads were centrifuged at 2000 RCF for 3 min. Supernatants were saved and concentrated with Amicon 3 kDa MWCO filters (13000 RCF × 10 min × 3 times) to a final volume ~100 μL. The concentration of protein in each sample was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. The effectiveness of the pull-down was assessed by analyzing 10 μL of each sample using Western blotting, following the procedure described in the previous section (“NP-A labeling in living cells followed by cell lysis and western blotting“).

Trypsin digestion of proteins and S-trap purification to generate peptides for mass spectrometry

After confirming that the pull-down was successful in isolating labeled proteins, 4 μg of each sample (~70 μL) was diluted to 84 μL with 14 μL 20% SDS. Disulfide bonds were reduced by adding 1.8 μL of 500 mM dithiothreitol (DTT; Thermo R0861) and heating to 55 °C for 15 min. After 15 min, samples were cooled at room temperature for 5 min and were alkylated in the dark at room temperature for 20 min by adding 5.4 μL of 500 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma I1149-5G). Samples were acidified by adding 8.8 μL of 27% phosphoric acid and subsequently diluted by addition of 560 μL binding buffer (100 mM Tris buffer in 1:9 H2O:methanol). Samples were loaded into S-Trap micro columns (Protifi C02-micro-40) in 5 aliquots by centrifuging after each addition; columns were washed 4 times with 150 μL binding buffer. Proteolytic digestion was performed within the S-traps at 47 °C for 75 min after adding 1 μg of trypsin (Promega V5111) in 20 μL of 75 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC; Sigma 285099-1KG). Peptides were eluted with 40 μL of 50 mM ABC, followed by 40 μL of 0.2% formic acid (Sigma 399388), followed by 40 μL of 1:1 ACN:H2O. The elution fractions were combined, and the peptide samples were dried using a SpeedVac (ThermoFisher Savant SPD131DDA), then dissolved in 8 μL 5% acetonitrile 0.2% formic acid in water. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was performed with 2.5 μL of each Input and Eluate sample. Two technical replicates were collected for each sample.

Mass spectrometry proteomics

Samples were analyzed using an ultra-high performance LC-MS/MS (UPLC-MS/MS) system consisting of a Vanquish Neo ultra-high-pressure liquid chromatography system and an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID:SCR_020562). Injected peptide samples were loaded at a pressure of 400 bar onto a 20-cm long fused silica capillary nano-column packed with C18 resin (1.7-μm diameter, 130 Å pore size from Waters). Peptides eluted over 120 min at a flow rate of 350 nL/min with the following gradient, where buffer A was aqueous 0.2% formic acid and buffer B was 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid: time 1 min-5% buffer B; time 52 min-30% buffer B; time 80 min-42% buffer B; time 90 min-55% acetonitrile; time 90-110 min-85% buffer B. The nano-column was held at 60 °C using a column heater constructed in-house.

The nanospray source voltage was set to 2,300 V. Full-mass profile scans were performed in the Orbitrap between 375 and 1,500 m/z at a resolution of 120,000, followed by MS/MS higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) scans in the Orbitrap of the highest intensity parent ions in a 3-s cycle time at 30% relative collision energy and 15,000 resolution, with a 2.5 m/z isolation window. Charge states 2–6 were included, and dynamic exclusion was enabled with a repeat count of one over a duration of 30 s and a 10 ppm exclusion width both low and high. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to “standard,” maximum inject time was set to 50 ms, and 1 μscan was collected for the MS/MS orbitrap HCD scans.

Statistical analyses are described in the “quantification and statistical analysis“ section.

Mass spectrometry to identify amino acid labeling sites on in vitro labeled protein

Samples were analyzed using an ultra-high performance LC-MS/MS (UPLC-MS/MS) system consisting of an Waters nanoAquity ultra-high-pressure liquid chromatography system and an Orbitrap Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, {RRID:SCR_020545}). Injected peptide samples were loaded at a flow rate of 400 nL/min onto a 20-cm long fused silica capillary nano-column packed with C18 resin (1.7-μm diameter, 130 Å pore size from Waters). Peptides eluted over 120 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min with the following gradient, where buffer A was aqueous 0.1% formic acid and buffer B was 80% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid: time 1 min-8% buffer B; time 81 min-34% buffer B; time 91 min-44% buffer B. At 92 min, the flow rate was increased to 400 nL/min with the following gradient: 92-98 min-64% acetonitrile; time 103-122 min-equilibrate at 2% buffer B. The nano-column was held at 60 °C using a column heater constructed in-house.

The nanospray source voltage was set to 2,200 V. Full-mass profile scans were performed in the Orbitrap between 375 and 1,500 m/z at a resolution of 120,000, followed by MS/MS higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) scans in the Orbitrap of the highest intensity parent ions in a 3-s cycle time at 30% relative collision energy and 15,000 resolution, with a 2.5 m/z isolation window. Charge states 2–5 were included, and dynamic exclusion was enabled with a repeat count of one over a duration of 15s. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to 1e5 and the maximum inject time was set to 500 ms.

LC-MS/MS data were analyzed with the open-source search software program MetaMorpheus (v.1.0.6, https://github.com/smith-chem-wisc/MetaMorpheus/releases). Data files were searched against the MetaMorpheus contaminant database, which contains an entry for bovine carbonic anhydrase II downloaded from Swiss-Prot (11/2019), as well as a FASTA database that contained the sequence of the mutant version of the APEX2 enzyme. MetaMorpheus was used to calibrate the raw data files then subject them to global posttranslational modification discovery (GPTMD) to identify posttranslational modifications (PTMs) not annotated in the reference database, including: the Common Biological, Common Artifact, and Metal Adduct categories within MetaMorpheus.83 Additionally, custom nitrophenol-desthiobiotin (NPD) modifications were considered with a modification mass of 688.3544 Da and neutral loss and neutral diagnostic ion masses of 239.1627 Da and 508.3027 Da, corresponding to the ions shown in Figure S88. Next, the mass spectra files were searched against the GPTMD databases using the following search settings: protease = trypsin; maximum missed cleavages = 2; minimum peptide length = 7; maximum peptide length = unspecified; initiator methionine behavior = Variable; fixed modifications = Carbamidomethyl on C, Carbamidomethyl on U; variable modifications = Oxidation on M; max mods per peptide = 2; max modification isoforms = 1,024; precursor mass tolerance = ±5 ppm; product mass tolerance = ±20 ppm; report PSM ambiguity = True. The resulting list of peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) and peptides were filtered to a false discovery rate of 1%.

Initial analysis of the carbonic anhydrase labeling data showed that NPD preferentially labels tyrosine and tryptophan. In this analysis, every amino acid was considered as a potential site for NPD modification by the GPTMD engine. The NPD modification was identified in 177 PSMs, the majority of which (147/177) showed that the modification occurred on Y or W residues. In the remaining PSMs, localization of the modification was spread across ten additional amino acids. This data is shown in Figure S89. The analysis was repeated with only Y/W considered as a potential site for NPD modification by the GPTMD engine. NPD labeling sites identified in this analysis were validated by manual inspection of the PSMs. Sites validated in this way are shown in Table S13, and examples of manually validated PSMs are shown in Figure S88.

Yeast cell culture

Yeast culture media YPD, SDCAA and yeast induction media SGCAA were prepared following a published procedure.84 Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain BJ5465(-Trp, -uracil) was cultured in SDCAA media supplemented with 40 μg/mL tryptophan and 20 μg/mL uracil. YIP-Aga1p-AP-V5 plasmid was transformed into the BJ5465 yeast to generate Aga1P-fusion-expressing yeast(-trp) using EZ transformation kit (Zymo, ZT2001). After transformation, Aga1P fusion-expressing yeast (-trp) stably express aga1p-AP-V5 fusion and were cultured in SDCAA media supplemented with 40 μg/mL tryptophan. pCTCON2-Aga2p-APEX2-myc plasmid was transformed into the Aga1P fusion-expressing yeast to generate APEX2 expressing yeast using EZ transformation kit. The APEX2 expressing yeast were cultured in SDCAA at 30°C and passaged every 2 days. APEX2 expression was induced in SGCAA (1:10) at 30°C 18 h before the experiment.

Generation of APEX2 library

The APEX2 libraries with various mutation frequencies were generated using a previously reported protocol.48 The template soybean APEX2 in vector pCTCON2 was amplified for 10 rounds during the first phase of amplification. The amplification mix contained 500 ng APEX2 template, 2 mM MgCl2, 200 μM dNTPs, 400 nM Con2.Fwd primer (Table S10), 400 nM Con2.Rev primer, 1 μM 8-oxo-dGTP, 1 μM dPTP, and 5 U Taq polymerase. The amplified product was purified using gel excision. The mutated insert was further amplified for 30 rounds under normal PCR conditions with HomR.Fwd primer, HomR.Rev primer, and Taq polymerase.

The mutated inserts (4300 ng) were then mixed with restriction digested pCTCON2 backbone (1300 ng, digested with NheI and BamHI restriction enzymes), and then resulting DNA was subjected to ethanol precipitation. The insert-backbone DNA pellet was then dissolved in 10 μL NF H2O and was electroporated into Aga1P fusion-expressing yeast. The yeast library was rescued with 200 mL SDCAA at 30 °C for 2 days. The library contained 1.6 × 107 mutants.

Generation of F41V mutant library

The F41V mutant library was generated using the same previously reported protocol as described above.2 The template APEX2-F41V in vector pCTCON2 was amplified for 20 rounds during the first phase of amplification. This mixture contained 500 ng of APEX2-F41V template, 2 mM MgCl2, 200 μM dNTPs, 400 nM Con2.Fwd primer, 400 nM Con2.Rev primer, 2 μM 8-oxo-dGTP, 2 μM dPTP, and 5 U Taq polymerase. The amplified product was then purified using gel excision. The mutated inserts were then further amplified for 20 rounds under normal PCR conditions with HomR.Fwd primer, HomR.Rev primer, and Taq polymerase. The annealing temperature, however, was raised to 68 °C for 2 min to minimize primer dimerization. To obtain 4300 ng of DNA, the PCR reaction was scaled up to a total volume of 200 μL with final purified DNA concentration 159.0 ng/μL.

The mutated inserts were then mixed with restriction digested pCTCON2 backbone (1300 ng, digested with NheI and BamHI restriction enzymes), and the resulting DNA was subjected to ethanol precipitation. The insert-backbone DNA pellet was then dissolved in 10 μL NF H2O and was electroporated into Aga1P fusion-expressing yeast. The yeast libraries were rescued with 125 mL SDCAA at 30 °C for 2 days. The library size was 1.5×107.

Yeast labeling and selection

Evolution I

480 million yeast cells (1st round) or 60 million yeast cells (2nd through 4th round) were labeled and screened in each round of selection. The OD600 of the yeast culture was determined by measuring the absorbance of 1:10 dilution of the yeast culture. The appropriate volume of cell suspension was spun down using a tabletop centrifuge (14000 RCF, 1 min). The cells were washed twice with PBS with 0.1% BSA (PBS-B) and labeled with 100 μL 1/100 diluted Chicken-anti-myc (MilliporeSigma NO.AB3252) on ice for 30 min. After 3× PBS-B washes, the cells were labeled at room temperature for 10 min (pre-evolution) in 15-mL conical tubes with 5 mL BNP labeling solution containing 100 μM BNP and 5 μM H2O2 in PBS. The reaction was quenched with 1 mL 120 mM ascorbic acid in PBS and the cells were washed 3 times with PBS-B. Secondary labeling was performed on ice for 30 min with 1:100 diluted Goat anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 647 (Invitrogen NO.A21449) and 2.6:100 diluted streptavidin-phycoerythrin (SA-PE, Invitrogen S866). After secondary labeling, samples were washed twice in PBS-B and resuspended to a final density of 10 million cells per mL of PBS-B for cell sorting.

For each round of sorting, 480 million (1st round) or 60 million (2nd through 4th round) yeast cells were analyzed by BD FACSAria cell sorters with green and red lasers and corresponding filters (582/15 for phycoerythrin, 660/20 for Alexa Fluor 647). Trapezoidal gates were drawn as shown in Figure S8 to collect yeast cells that were positive for Alexa Fluor 647 signal that also had high PE signal. Gates were drawn to collect the following percentages of cells: 0.7% for round 1, 0.5% for round 2, 0.1% for round 3 and 0.1% for round 4. After sorting, cells were propagated in 10 mL SDCAA media containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco NO.15140122), 50 μg/mL kanamycin, 50 μg/mL gentamicin, 50 μg/mL vancomycin, and 10 μg/mL tetracycline. The yeast cultures were typically dense enough to be transferred into SGCAA media to induce expression 2 days post sorting. In each round of sorting, the number of yeast cells passed through the sorter were at least 10-fold larger than the diversity size of the yeast pool, except for round 1, in which case 3-fold oversampling was employed. Round 1 had lower sampling because of the practical difficulties and expenses of labeling large quantities of yeast cells due to the substantial diversity size of this round.

APEX2 variants expression and purification in BL21-DE3 E. coli

Equal concentrations of purified, heme-bound holoenzyme were used for each experiment. Concentrations of APEX2 variants were calculated based on holo protein content, as determined using absorbance at 405 nm and assuming ϵ405 = 128 mM−1 cm−1.10 HRP (Biosynth, cat. No. FP159434) was dissolved into PBS, pH 7.4, from a lyophilized form, and enzyme concentration was calculated using ϵ405 = 100 mM−1 cm−1. Guaiacol (Sigma) was diluted in room temperature PBS, pH 7.4, to a concentration of 10 mM with agitation at 37 °C for 30 min, and then vortexed thoroughly to ensure the guaiacol was dissolved. This solution was subsequently diluted to the desired working concentration using PBS at room temperature. Reactions were assembled in disposable plastic cuvettes by adding 750 μL guaiacol solution and 2.5 μL of a stock H2O2 solution. Reactions were initiated by addition of enzyme (2.5 μL from a stock solution), followed by manual mixing and immediate monitoring of absorbance at 470 nm. Oxidation to tetraguaiacol was measured using ϵ470 = 22 mM−1 cm−1.57 The initial rates collected between 250 μM and 5 mM H2O2 represent lower bounds and have large errors because the uninhibited initial rates could not be captured in our assay. Both enzymes exhibited Vo/[E]t > 100 under these conditions, but the rate of product formation had already declined within a few seconds—faster than the timescale of the manual mixing used in our assay—owing to accumulation of inactivated enzyme. For the total turnover experiments, reactions were performed using final concentrations of 1 mM H2O2, 0.25 mM guaiacol, and 10 nM enzyme.

Reduction potential assay

Reduction potential assays were performed in semi-micro polystyrene cuvettes (Brandtech 759075D). 289 μL PBS 7.0, 10 μL 500 mM glucose (Dot scientific DSG32040) in Millipore water, 15 μL 10 mM xanthine (Sigma X0626-5G) in 10 mM NaOH pH 12, and 10 μL of 10 μM benzyl viologen (Sigma 271845-250MG) in Millipore water were mixed in a cuvette. The cuvette was then sealed with parafilm and purged with argon at room temperature for 15 min. After 15 min, 10 μL 10 mg/mL catalase (Sigma C9322) in PBS 7.0 (PBS 7.0; 10 mM NaHPO4, 1.8 mM KH2PO4, 137 mM NaCl, and 2.7 mM KCl, pH adjusted to 7.0), 25 μL 5 mg/mL glucose oxidase (Sigma G2133) in PBS 7.0, 40 μL 16–25 μM heme peroxidase variant in PBS 7.4, and 1 μL reference dye (5 mM phenosafranin (Sigma 199648-1G) or 2.5 mM Nile blue (Sigma 370088) in PBS 7.0) were added to the cuvette. The solution was then mixed by shaking gently at room temperature for 5 s. The solution was put under Argon for another 15 min at room temperature to decrease the amount of O2. The reduction was then initiated by injecting 100 μL xanthine oxidase solution (250/500/1000 nM xanthine oxidase (Sigma 10110434001) in PBS 7.0 at room temperature) to reach the final conditions specified in Table S12. Change in absorbance was monitored by a NanoDrop One UV-Vis spectrometer at 407 nm (peroxidase), 520 nm (phenosafranin), and 628 nm (Nile blue). The reduction was run for 30–60 min until the rate of reduction had slowed down dramatically. 10 μL 10 mM sodium dithionite (Sigma 157953) in PBS 7.0 was added to the cuvette to fully reduce both the heme enzyme and dye, after which UV-vis absorbance continued to be monitored. Data was processed following the procedure published by Efimov et al.67 The data processing procedure is described in detail in Note S3.

Coverslip preparation for STED microscopy

Coverslips (PolyAn, Po-104 00 226) were sequentially immersed in room temperature acetone and isopropanol for 5 min for initial washes. After washing, the solvent was dried with air. Coverslips were then coated with 1% v/v (3-glycidyloxypropyl)trimethoxysilane (GPTMS; Sigma-Aldrich, 440167-100ML) in toluene at room temperature for 2 h in a Coplin staining jar. After 2 h, the coated coverslips were washed by sequentially immersing in toluene and acetone for 5 min at room temperature. The coverslips were then dried with air.

Silicon isolators (Grace Bio-Labs JTR12R-0.5) were applied to each GPTMS-coated coverslip in order to create isolated regions of the coverslips that could be exposed to different labeling probes in subsequent steps. The isolators allow small regions of the coverslips to be stained individually, such that the volume of solution needed to cover each region is 20 μL. The coverslips were then immersed in 20 μL 1 mg/mL IgG-free BSA (Sigma, 9048-46-8) in 200 mM carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.4) at room temperature overnight in a Coplin staining jar. After overnight incubation, the coverslips were washed with 2× saline sodium citrate buffer pH 7.0 (SSC) with 0.1% SDS (30 mM sodium citrate, 300 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS) 3 times at room temperature, then immersed in epoxide block buffer (50 mM ethanolamine (Sigma, E9508-100ML) in 0.1 M Tris buffer pH 9) for 4 h at room temperature in a Coplin staining jar. After 4 h, the coverslips were immersed in 20 μL 0.1× SSC (1.5 mM sodium citrate, 15 mM NaCl) 2 × 10 min at room temperature. Each well on the coverslips was then immersed in 20 μL 5% BSA in DPBS at room temperature for 1 h. After BSA blocking, wells were washed with 20 μL 1.2× Tris-buffered saline pH 7.5 (1.2× TBS-T; 24 mM Tris, 300 mM NaCl, 0.6% v/v Tween 20 (DOT Scientific DSP20370–0.5)) for 10 min. Each region on the coverslips was stained with 20 μL 2 μg/mL mouse anti-BSA (Sigma SAB4200688-100UL) in 1.2× TBS-T at room temperature for 2 h. After primary labeling, the wells were washed 3 times with 20 μL 1.2× TBS-T for 10 min. Secondary labeling was performed with 20 μL 1 μg/mL goat anti-mouse HRP conjugate (Sigma AP124P) in 1.2× TBST at room temperature for 2 h. After secondary labeling, each well was washed with 20 μL 1.2× TBST 5 times at room temperature for 1 min.

Biotin phenol and biotin-nitrophenol labeling for STED microscopy

Labeling solution was prepared by mixing the probe (BP or BNP, 50 mM in DMSO) with 30% H2O2 (9.8 M) in 50 mM PBS 7.4 at room temperature to reach a final concentration 50–5000 μM probe, 1 mM H2O2 in PBS 7.4. Each well was then immersed with labeling solution for 15 min at room temperature. After the corresponding labeling time, the labeling solution was removed with a pipette, and the labeling was quenched with 10 μL 10 mM sodium ascorbate in Millipore water. The wells were then immersed in 20 μL 1× Tris-Buffered Saline pH 7.5 (1× TBS-T; 20 mM Tris, 250 mM NaCl, 0.5% v/v Tween 20 (DOT Scientific DSP20370–0.5)) for 3 × 20 min for washes and stained with 20 μL 13.3 μg/mL Streptavidin-AF594 conjugate (Invitrogen S11227) in 1× TBST at 4 °C overnight. After sAv-AF594 labeling, wells were washed with 20 μL 1.2× TBST 5 times. Labeling intensity was monitored using an Echo Revolve Epifluorescence Microscope. The coverslips were then dried with a duster (Chemtronics ES1617). 15 μL ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen P36982) was applied to each well. Immediately after adding antifade mountant, a glass slide (Fisher 125495C) was gently mounted to each coverslip. The coverslips were stored at 4 °C overnight before STED microscopy.

Synthesis of biotin-nitrophenol

NHS-biotin was synthesized using a published protocol.87 To synthesize biotin-nitro-phenol, 170 mg NHS-biotin (341.38 g/mol, 0.498 mmol) was mixed with 1.2 eq of 3-nitrotyramine (MilliporeSigma) (182.18 g/mol, 0.600 mmol, 109 mg) and 2.0 eq of triethylamine (101.19 g/mol, 1 mmol, 102.2 mg) in 2 mL DMF. The reaction was run for 3 h or overnight. 20 mL ethyl acetate and 20 mL PBS were added to quench the reaction. The product was extracted and separated from the aqueous layer using ethyl acetate until the aqueous layer turned from yellow to colorless. The ethyl acetate was removed by rotary evaporation, and the product was separated using a silica column (85% EtOAC:15% MeOH, Rf = 0.7). The first yellow band with intense color was the product. 62 mg of product was collected (31% yield).

Synthesis of nitrophenol-alkyne (NP-A)

Two-step synthesis of nitro-phenol-alkyne (NP-A).

Synthesis of 2,5-dioxopyrrolidin-1-yl hex-5-ynoate (NHS-alkyne)

100 mg hexynoic acid (112.13 g/mol, 0.891 mmol) was mixed with 1.2 eq disuccinimidyl carbonate (256.17 g/mol, 1.07 mmol, 274 mg) and 2 eq of triethylamine (101.19 g/mol, 1.782 mmol, 180.3 mg) in 1 mL DMF. The reaction was run at room temperature for 2 h. After 2 h, 10 mL PBS were added to the reaction. The product was extracted with 40 mL CH2Cl2 followed by 20 mL ethyl acetate. The organic layers were combined, and the organic solvents were removed by rotary evaporation to obtain a mixture containing unreacted hexynoic acid, hydrolyzed disuccinimidyl carbonate, and the desired product. The crude mixture was used for the next step.

Synthesis of N-(4-hydroxy-3-nitrophenethyl)hex-5-ynamide (nitro-phenol alkyne (NP-A))

The product mixture containing NHS-alkyne was mixed with 2 eq of 3-nitrotyramine (323.96 mg, 1.78 mmol, 182.18 g/mol) (MilliporeSigma) and 2.0 eq of triethylamine (180 mg, 1.78 mmol, 101.19 g/mol) in 1 mL DMF. The reaction was run at room temperature for 15 h. After 15 h, the product was extracted with 40 mL CH2Cl2 followed by 20 mL ethyl acetate. The product was further extracted and separated from the aqueous layer using ethyl acetate until the aqueous layer turned from yellow to colorless. The organic layers were combined, and the organic solvent was removed by rotary evaporation. The product was purified using silica gel chromatography (EtOAC, Rf = 0.8). The first yellow band with intense color was the product. 120 mg of NP-A were isolated after the column (48% yield).

Note: Even a small amount (<1%) of NHS-alkyne, which is undetectable by 1H NMR, could cause dramatic non-specific alkyne labeling in cellular assays due to labeling of endogenous proteins with the alkyne functional groups. It is critical to completely remove NHS-alkyne, which is why we used excess 3-nitrotyramine and ran the reaction for 15 h. Optional K2CO3 washes during the extraction step could also help hydrolyze any remaining NHS-alkyne.

Rosetta calculation of APEX2, APEX2 (F41V), and APOX structures

The Rosetta 3.13 relax application55 was used on a Linux based computational cluster to estimate protein structure after introducing mutations. Manipulations of protein structures were performed using the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 3.0 Schrödinger, LLC. The crystal structure of wild type soybean ascorbate peroxidase (PDB:1OAG)53 was used as a base structure for calculation. The heme cofactor was separated from the original 1OAG structure and individually saved as a mol file. For Rosetta to correctly recognize the heme cofactor, hydrogens were added to the heme, and the Rosetta molfile_to_params script was used to generate a HEM.params file and a HEM.pdb file. The heme in the original 1OAG structure was deleted, and the content of the HEM.pdb file was appended to the 1OAG structure to ensure consistency with the Rosetta atom naming convention. The new structure was saved as 1OAG-HEM.pdb. To calculate the APEX2 structure, K14D, E112K, W41F and A134P mutations were added to the 1OAG-HEM structure using the mutagenesis function in PyMOL, and the file was saved as APEX2.pdb. 500 Rosetta relaxation calculations were performed using the code below on APEX2.pdb, and the energies of the structures were scored based on the total protein energy given by the 2015 Rosetta Energy Function.89 The lowest energy structure was relaxed 500 more times, and the new lowest energy structure was saved as the final APEX2 structure. To calculate the APEX2 (F41V) structure, F41V and the APEX2 mutations were added to 1OAG-HEM.pdb (K14D, E112K, F41V and A134P) and the file was saved as F41V.pdb. 500 calculations were initially performed as described above, and the lowest energy structure was relaxed with another 500 calculations. The relaxed APEX2 (F41V) structure with the lowest total energy was saved as the final structure. To calculate the APOX structure, K14D, W41V, S69I, E112K, A134P, Y235F, and H239Y mutations were added to the 1OAG-HEM structure, and the file was saved as APOX.pdb. Rosetta was used to relax APOX.pdb, using the code below to output 1000 structures. The lowest energy structure was saved and passed into the next round of relaxation to produce 1000 more structures. This process was continued until no further energy decrease was observed, for a total of 7 rounds of 1000 outputs each. The final lowest energy structure was saved as the estimated structure of APOX.

Code used for calculation: rosetta/bin/relax -database rosetta/database -s stucture.pdb -nstruct {number_of_structures} -in:au-to_setup_metals -in:file:fullatom -extra_res_fa HEM.params

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Statistical details of experiments, such as the number of replicates performed for experimental conditions, are included in the figure and supplemental figure legends.

LC-MS/MS data were analyzed with the open-source search software program MetaMorpheus (v.1.0.6, https://github.com/smith-chem-wisc/MetaMorpheus/releases). The Swiss-Prot Human FASTA database containing 20,435 protein entries (UniprotKB, RRID:SCR_004426. https://www.uniprot.org/(accessed 12/2023)) was used, along with MetaMorpheus’s contaminants database. MetaMorpheus was used to calibrate the raw data files. The mass spectra files were searched using the following search settings: protease = trypsin; maximum missed cleavages = 2; minimum peptide length = 7; maximum peptide length = unspecified; initiator methionine behavior = Variable; fixed modifications = Carbamidomethyl on C, Carbamidomethyl on U; variable modifications = Oxidation on M; max mods per peptide = 2; max modification isoforms = 1,024; precursor mass tolerance = ±5 ppm; product mass tolerance = ±20 ppm; report PSM ambiguity = True. The experimenters were not blinded to the samples’ treatment because the proteomic data analysis was not biased.

The intensity of each peptide/protein was determined using FlashLFQ (v2.0.0, https://github.com/smith-chem-wisc/FlashLFQ), and the false discovery rate of peptide-identity-propagation was controlled using PIP-ECHO.90,91 The following settings were used for quantification: Peptide-identity-propagation = True; Require MSMS in condition = True; PPM tolerance = ±10 ppm; MBR RT-Window = 1.5 min. The protein intensities reported by FlashLFQ were log2 transformed, and median normalization was performed by calculating the median log-intensity across all proteins, p, for a given sample, s. Subtracting the median log-intensity for the sample from each protein log-intensity in the sample yields a list of normalized intensities, Ip,s′=Ip,s−Med(Is). Differential expression analysis was performed using limma.92 A linear model was fit for each comparison using the lmFit function with default parameters. The eBayes function, with the “trend” parameter set to True, was used to compute empirical Bayes moderated t-statistics. This yielded a log2 fold-change and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value for each protein.

Gene ontology analysis was performed to annotate proteins with compartments and sub-compartments using Uniprot ID mapping tool (https://www.uniprot.org/id-mapping). The protein list was sorted by adj.p-value and was uploaded to Uniprot. After removing keratin entries, percent mitochondria analysis was performed with annotations from Gene Ontology Cellular Component (GOCC). For sub-compartment specificity, all proteins with mitochondrial annotation were further annotated with a sub-compartment using the Human MitoCarta3.0 database as primary evidence. Entries with more than one sub-compartment affiliation were counted for all affiliated sub-compartments. The entries that are missing from Human MitoCarta3.0 were assigned with annotations from GOCC.

SIGNIFICANCE

This study demonstrates the use of ultrahigh-throughput evolution to increase the reduction potential of an enzyme. APOX exhibits 6-fold higher activity than APEX2 on NPs and up to 10-fold improved activity on other phenols, including 2.5-fold faster oxidation of BP. By applying APOX and the alkyne-NP for spatially resolved proteomics in living cells, we established the viability of PL using high redox potential NP probes. APOX expands the heme peroxidase toolkit by granting access to high redox potential probes, opening opportunities to further tune the diffusion radius and enhance the tagging of biomolecules beyond proteins.
