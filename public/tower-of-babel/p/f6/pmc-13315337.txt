Molecular Imaging in Primary Aldosteronism Subtyping: A Promising Complement but Not Yet a Replacement for Adrenal Venous Sampling
Authors: Bo‐Ching Lee, Chin‐Chen Chang
Published: 2026

Primary aldosteronism (PA) is increasingly recognized as one of the most prevalent causes of secondary hypertension, accounting for ≈5% to 10% of hypertensive cases and an even higher proportion among patients with resistant hypertension. 1 , 2 Beyond its contribution to elevated blood pressure, PA confers disproportionate cerebrovascular and cardiovascular morbidity compared with essential hypertension. 3 , 4 , 5 Although PA is increasingly recognized as a spectrum, its management still relies on the dichotomous distinction between unilateral and bilateral disease, with adrenal venous sampling (AVS) remaining the gold standard for subtyping. 6 , 7 However, despite its diagnostic value, AVS is invasive, technically demanding, and largely limited to specialized centers with experienced interventionalists. These limitations have stimulated growing interest in noninvasive alternatives, particularly molecular imaging.

In this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), the authors present a systematic review evaluating the diagnostic agreement between molecular imaging and AVS for PA lateralization. 8 Across 20 studies (843 patients), molecular imaging and AVS demonstrated moderate but highly variable agreement for PA subtyping. Study‐level concordance ranged widely (κ=0.33–0.93), reflecting heterogeneity in tracers, protocols, and populations. While some tracers such as 11C‐metomidate, 131I‐labeled 6‐β‐iodomethyl‐19‐norcholesterol, 18F‐CETO positron emission tomography/computed tomography showed relatively consistent performance, others such as 68Ga‐pentixafor exhibited more broad variability across studies. Of note, some 68Ga‐pentixafor studies lacking quantitative data were excluded but showed promising concordance rates with AVS (75.7%–86.5%). 8 These heterogenous results likely reflect fundamental biological differences: AVS directly measures glandular aldosterone secretion, whereas molecular imaging reflects enzyme activity or receptor expression at the adrenal gland. The authors conclude that molecular imaging currently occupies a complementary rather than replacement role. At present, prospective outcome‐based comparative data between molecular imaging and AVS remain relatively limited, 9 and without such evidence, its clinical implications remain uncertain.

AVS has long been criticized for requiring specialized expertise and limited availability, often due to difficulty cannulating the right adrenal vein. However, contemporary data suggest that AVS can be performed successfully even in medium‐volume centers when appropriate protocols are in place. Success rates at referral centers are high, with the AVIS‐2 study (Adrenal Vein Sampling International Study‐2) reporting 80.1% across 19 international centers and combined Japanese and European registries demonstrating a cumulative success rate of 89.3%. 10 , 11 With careful preprocedural assessment, 12 use of rapid cortisol assays, 13 and the widespread installation of C‐arm computed tomography, AVS may no longer be as technically demanding as it was a decade ago. The learning curve is ≈30 cases, and satisfactory outcomes can be maintained with a moderate annual case volume. 14 , 15 These findings indicate that, beyond high‐volume tertiary centers, well‐trained single operators in medium‐volume hospitals can also achieve reliable AVS performance. 16

With increasing recognition of PA, the demand for AVS to guide subtyping is greater than ever; therefore, its appropriate and efficient use is essential until more scalable subtyping methods become available. Despite guideline recommendations, AVS remains underused and often inefficient. Data from the Japan PA registry show that ≈30% of patients with unilateral PA do not undergo surgery, and only about 1 in 4 AVS procedures ultimately leads to identifying a surgical candidate. 11 , 17 Inefficiency arises from technical failure, bilateral disease, and post‐AVS changes in management, often due to preexisting clinical factors such as adequate blood pressure control, normokalemia, or inconclusive imaging. 11 As a result, a substantial proportion of patients with PA undergo an AVS without direct therapeutic benefit, highlighting the need to improve patient selection and reduce unnecessary procedures.

Molecular imaging is a promising noninvasive option for PA subtyping. Rather than replacing AVS, it should be viewed as complementary: useful for triaging patients, increasing diagnostic confidence, and resolving equivocal cases, particularly when AVS is inconclusive or expertise is limited. However, its inherent economic and practical limitations should not be overlooked. Like AVS, advanced nuclear imaging is resource‐intensive, costly, and not widely accessible. Tracer‐related constraints further limit feasibility: short half‐life agents such as 11C‐metomidate require an on‐site cyclotron, 18 while longer‐lived 18F tracers still depend on specialized production centers, 19 and 68Ga‐based tracers require radiochemistry facilities. 20 Beyond access, tracer variability is a key limitation. Consistent with the heterogeneous findings from this review, differences in tracer biology, protocols, patient preparation, and interpretation lead to variable performance, limiting comparability and hindering widespread implementation.

In summary, molecular imaging is a promising tool for PA localization, showing moderate to substantial agreement with AVS and supporting its growing role in clinical practice. However, limitations remain, including tracer heterogeneity, lack of standardization, and limited outcome‐based validation. For now, molecular imaging should be considered complementary rather than a replacement for AVS, particularly in cases of equivocal AVS results or clinical complexity. Whether it can replace AVS will depend on demonstrating comparable or superior patient outcomes; until then, AVS remains the reference standard.
