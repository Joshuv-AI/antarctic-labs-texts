Three‐Year Post‐Treatment Outcomes Following Cessation of Peanut Oral Immunotherapy: Comparisons by Clinical Allergy Status and Treatment Group
Authors: Paxton Loke, Xiaofang Wang, Sophie Rosser, Melanie Lloyd, Sarah E. Ashley, Adriana C. Lozinsky, Julie Burns, Patrick Quinn, Michael D. O'Sullivan, Michael Gold, Francesca Orsini, Mimi L. K. Tang
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Studies examining long‐term outcomes by clinical allergy status (remission, desensitization) and changes in health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) years after completion of peanut oral immunotherapy (OIT) are lacking. A randomized trial of low dose peanut OIT followed patients for a median of 2.3 years ‘on‐treatment’ and showed reduced reactions over time; however, rescue adrenaline use remained unchanged [1, 2]. An observational peanut OIT study reported 57% of sustained unresponsiveness subjects were eating greater than 3 g of peanut protein, and 73% were consuming peanut weekly at median follow‐up of 1.5 years post‐treatment [3].

We previously reported 2‐year post‐treatment outcomes in participants who completed the probiotic peanut OIT (PPOIT‐003) randomized trial and rolled over onto the follow‐on study (PPOIT‐003LT) [4]. Two years after completing probiotic peanut OIT (PPOIT) or peanut OIT, participants with remission experienced fewer and less severe reactions and greater HRQOL improvement compared to those who were desensitized without remission [4]. Likewise, PPOIT and peanut OIT treatment groups reported significant improvement in HRQOL and reduced reactions over time, reflecting the high remission rates achieved. Here we report 3‐year post‐treatment outcomes (peanut ingestion patterns, peanut reactions, HRQOL) examined by clinical outcome group and treatment allocation.

Briefly, PPOIT‐003 was a randomized controlled trial evaluating 18 months of high‐dose (2000 mg) PPOIT, standalone high‐dose (2000 mg) peanut OIT, and placebo. End‐of‐treatment efficacy outcomes were remission, desensitization without remission (DWR) or allergic (see Supporting Information for definitions) [5]. Post‐treatment peanut ingestion advice was: (1) remission, consume peanut ad libitum; (2) DWR, consume two peanuts daily; (3) allergic, peanut avoidance [5]. 151 of 176 eligible participants enrolled in PPOIT‐003LT (69 remission, 38 DWR, 44 allergic). Participants were followed up on a yearly basis, that is, at each post‐treatment timepoint, with access to study clinicians for advice regarding reactions and dose adjustments in the intervening period between each yearly visit. Each timepoint represents data for the preceding 12‐month period. Peanut consumption and reactions were recorded via a standardized questionnaire and mobile diary app. HRQOL was assessed via the Food Allergy Quality of Life Questionnaire‐Parent Form (FAQLQ‐PF). Results were analyzed by clinical outcome (established at the completion of 18 months treatment) and treatment allocation. Statistical methods are described in Supporting Information.

At 3‐years post‐treatment, 85.4% of participants (129/151) (58 remission, 33 DWR, 38 allergic) remained on‐study. Baseline demographics of participants were similar to the original parent PPOIT‐003 population (Table S1).

The majority of remission (91.4%) and DWR (87.9%) participants were still consuming peanut, unchanged from earlier post‐treatment years [4]. Five (13.2%) allergic participants had accidental peanut exposures, and 4 (10.5%) had allergic reactions from the exposure (Table 1). There was one participant each from the DWR and remission group who reported accidental peanut exposure with reactions. Among remission participants, peanut ingestion at 3‐years post‐treatment (moderate, 3–8 peanuts, 46.3%; large, 8–16 peanuts, 20.4%; very large, > 16 peanuts, 9.3%) remained similar to previous post‐treatment years; median ingestion frequency was 1–2 times‐a‐week; 51.9% enjoyed peanut or were eating it frequently (Figure 1A–C). Among DWR participants, 46.7% maintained the recommended daily ingestion and 26.7% reported difficulty with daily intake ‘almost all the time’ (higher than the 15.9% reported at 1‐year post‐treatment) (Figure 1D,E). This pattern is likely to reflect increasing difficulty with adherence or compliance fatigue associated with the burden of ongoing daily peanut ingestion required to maintain the desensitized state. This is consistent with real‐world data showing that social factors, including difficulty with adherence and communication challenges, are among the most common reasons for discontinuation of OIT [6].

As in previous years, the proportion of DWR participants experiencing reactions was more than 2‐fold that of remission participants (27.3% vs. 12.1%) at the 3‐year post‐treatment timepoint (Table 1) [4]. There were no moderate or severe reactions in remission participants, whereas moderate and severe reactions accounted for 16.7% and 11.1% of reactions in DWR participants, and 0% and 33.3% in allergic participants. Rescue adrenaline was used in 0% remission, 6.1% DWR and 7.9% allergic participants (Table 1). Cofactor‐associated reactions were more frequent in DWR (27.8%) than remission participants (4.2%). Although not formally assessed in this study, declining adherence to ongoing daily peanut ingestion among DWR participants may limit the durability of desensitization and contribute to increased incidence of allergic reactions. Consistent with this, the PALISADE‐ARC004 follow‐on study showed that reducing the frequency of maintenance dosing from once daily to twice weekly was associated with a higher rate of allergic reactions during peanut oral immunotherapy [2].

Remission participants experienced significantly greater improvement in HRQOL, reflected by larger decreases in total scores from screening to 3‐years post‐treatment (adjusted for baseline) compared with both DWR (MD −0.67; 95% CI: −1.14, −0.19; p = 0.006) and allergic participants (MD −1.29; 95% CI: −1.77, −0.82; p < 0.001), exceeding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) (Table 1 and Figure 2). DWR participants experienced a delayed but clinically meaningful improvement in HRQOL compared with allergic participants at 3‐years post‐treatment (MD −0.63; 95% CI: −1.15; −0.10; p = 0.020), which was not observed in the first two post‐treatment years [4]. Notably, the magnitude of improvement in the DWR group was meaningfully smaller than that observed in remission participants, highlighting distinct HRQOL differences between the remission and DWR outcomes (Figure 2).

Findings for treatment group comparisons were consistent with previous years (Table S2) [4]. Larger proportions of PPOIT (82%) and OIT (79.2%) participants were still consuming peanut compared to placebo (15.4%), and larger proportions reported peanut reactions (PPOIT 16.0%, OIT 17.0%) than placebo (11.5%). The placebo group included one participant who had remission, and three with an allergic outcome who reported reactions due to accidental exposures to peanut. PPOIT reported more moderate and severe reactions than OIT (PPOIT moderate 15.8%, severe 10.5%; OIT moderate 0.0%, severe 4.2%). PPOIT and OIT groups experienced significantly greater HRQOL improvements from baseline compared to placebo (PPOIT vs. placebo: MD −0.35; 95% CI: −0.49, −0.21; p < 0.001; OIT vs. placebo: MD −0.63; 95% CI: −0.91, −0.34; p < 0.001).

In conclusion, remission participants on ad libitum intake experienced fewer and less severe reactions than DWR participants. HRQOL (parental proxy) improvement remained greatest in remission participants, likely related to removal of dietary restrictions and continuing off‐treatment protection [5, 7]. HRQOL benefits for DWR participants emerged for the first time at 3‐year post‐treatment. Both PPOIT and high‐dose peanut OIT provided lasting HRQOL improvement compared with placebo. Findings support the validity of applying an 8‐week elimination period prior to standard diagnostic challenge as a surrogate measure for clinical remission and lasting protection and reinforce the lasting protection provided through this clinical endpoint. Remission participants were able to eat peanut without limitation, that is, without the need for structured maintenance dosing or allergen avoidance, which translated to substantial and lasting improvements in HRQOL, beyond the benefits conferred by desensitization alone.
