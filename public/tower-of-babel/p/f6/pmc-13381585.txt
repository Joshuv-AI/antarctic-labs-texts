A versatile method for designing biosensors via regulatory domains of allosteric enzymes
Authors: Zhaoqi Kang, Rong Xu, Ping Han, Hui Zhang, Shuang Hou, Dan Xiao, Yan Zhang, Yidong Liu, Leilei Guo, Jie Wu, Weikang Sun, Xiaoxu Tan, Xianzhi Xu, Kaiyu Gao, Chuanjuan Lü, Cuiqing Ma, Rong Qiang, Ping Xu, Chao Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Genetically encoded fluorescent biosensors (GEFBs) are invaluable tools for spatiotemporal metabolite monitoring in cellular metabolism, yet their development for many key metabolites is hampered by a lack of specific biorecognition elements. Here, we report a versatile strategy to engineer metabolite-responsive GEFBs by leveraging the allosteric properties of regulatory domains from allosteric enzymes. Using regulatory domains from chorismate mutase, 2-acetolactate synthase, and d-citramalate synthase as biorecognition elements, we construct three biosensors for specific l-phenylalanine, l-valine, and l-isoleucine detection. We further demonstrate that multi-ligand-binding regulatory domains can be exploited to derive diverse specific biosensors, and apply this strategy to develop two S-adenosyl-l-methionine biosensors and an S-methyl-5’-thioadenosine biosensor. We also showcase the utility of these biosensors for real-time, in situ tracking of target metabolites in living cells, as well as bioprocess monitoring and clinical diagnostics. Overall, this study establishes a flexible strategy that provides insights to construct GEFBs targeting other metabolites.

Introduction

Metabolites in cellular metabolism furnish essential building blocks for living organisms. Beyond this, they modulate cell physiology and fate by interacting with metabolic enzymes, signal transduction proteins, and transcriptional regulators1–3. Thus, acquiring spatiotemporal information regarding the dynamics of intracellular metabolites is crucial for a deep understanding of fundamental life processes. Genetically encoded fluorescent biosensors (GEFBs), composed of a biorecognition element and a fluorescent reporter element, represent an emerging technology for metabolite detection4,5. Binding of a target analyte to the biorecognition element alters the optical properties of the fluorescent reporter within the GEFB, resulting in precise monitoring of the analyte. Owing to their rapid, sensitive, and non-invasive characteristics, many GEFBs have been developed and applied to visualize diverse metabolites in cellular metabolism5.

Metabolite sensing and subsequent metabolic regulation constitute two fundamental life processes2. Transcriptional regulator-mediated gene expression represents a canonical mechanism of metabolic regulation. Various metabolite-responsive transcriptional regulators have been exploited as biorecognition elements for GEFB development6,7. However, most of the reported transcriptional regulators prefer sensing extracellular metabolites or metabolites at non-physiological levels3,8. GEFBs for many metabolites, such as some intermediates in central carbon metabolism, amino acids, and cofactors, are still lacking5. Beyond modulating metabolic enzyme expression, organisms can also coordinate metabolic homeostasis via allosteric regulation of enzymes by intracellular metabolites9,10. Canonical allosteric enzymes are composed of catalytic domains and regulatory domains9–11. The non-covalent reversible binding of specific metabolites induces conformational changes in the regulatory domains, thereby altering the active states of the catalytic domains (Fig. 1a). Allosteric regulation, which fine-tunes most biological processes under physiological conditions, is regarded as the second secret of life10. In this study, we present a versatile strategy for developing metabolite GEFBs using the regulatory domains of allosteric enzymes as biorecognition elements (Fig. 1a). We first select the aspartate kinase-chorismate mutase-prephenate dehydrogenase (ACT) domain in the homomultimeric allosteric enzyme chorismate mutase (PheA) for proof-of-concept. Based on this l-phenylalanine-specific biorecognition element and a circularly permuted fluorescent protein (cpFP), we design and develop a biosensor enabling the specific detection of l-phenylalanine. Then, we also use the LeuA regulatory (LA) domain in the homomultimeric allosteric enzyme d-citramalate synthase (CimA) and the regulatory domain (existing in the form of the regulatory subunit IlvH) in the heteromultimeric allosteric enzyme 2-acetolactate synthase as the biorecognition elements to construct two specific biosensors for the distinction of l-isoleucine and l-valine, both in vitro and in living cells. In addition, we demonstrate that multiple biosensors can be derived from the same multi-ligand-binding regulatory domain. Two S-adenosyl-l-methionine (SAM, the second most chemically active cofactor in cells12)-specific biosensors with complementary detection ranges and an S-methyl-5’-thioadenosine (MTA)-specific biosensor can be developed based on PH1780 with multi-ligand-binding cystathionine β-synthase (CBS) domains. The applicability of the as-designed biosensors for l-phenylalanine, l-valine, l-isoleucine, and SAM detection is comprehensively characterized in living cells, fermentation systems, and clinical human body fluids.

Results

ACT domain in PheA as biorecognition element for l-phenylalanine biosensor

The ACT domain (Pfam ID: PF01842) is a well-studied regulatory domain in allosteric enzymes13. Specific amino acids can bind to the interface of the ACT dimer and modulate the activity of ACT-containing enzymes (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 1). For example, PheA is an allosteric enzyme in the biosynthesis of l-phenylalanine (Supplementary Fig. 1)14. It consists of two catalytic domains at the N-terminus (chorismate mutase [PheACM] domain and prephenate dehydratase [PheAPDT] domain) and an ACT (PheAACT) domain at the C-terminus (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 2a). The PheACM domain catalyzes the conversion of chorismate to prephenate, while the PheAPDT domain catalyzes the conversion of prephenate to phenylpyruvate. l-Phenylalanine, produced through the transamination of phenylpyruvate, act as a feedback inhibitor of PheACM and PheAPDT activities (Fig. 1c)14,15.

We constructed four biosensors by fusing a circularly permuted superfolder yellow fluorescent protein (cpSFYFP) to the C-terminus of full-length PheA (PheACM-PheAPDT-PheAACT) or truncated PheA (PheACM-PheAPDT, PheAPDT-PheAACT, or PheAACT)7. Subsequently, we analyzed the fluorescence ratio (F488 nm/F405 nm, the ratio of fluorescence at 528 nm when excited at 488 and 405 nm) changes (ΔR/Rmin) of these biosensors before and after the addition of chorismate, prephenate, phenylpyruvate, or l-phenylalanine. The biosensor PheACM-PheAPDT-PheAACT-cpSFYFP responded to all four analytes (Fig. 1d), while the biosensors PheACM-PheAPDT-cpSFYFP and PheAPDT-PheAACT-cpSFYFP obtained by truncating the PheAACT domain or the PheACM domain lost their responsiveness to l-phenylalanine and chorismate, respectively (Fig. 1e, f). Notably, the biosensor with only the PheAACT domain (PheAACT-cpSFYFP) exhibited a specific and dose-dependent response to l-phenylalanine (Fig. 1g, h). In addition, none of the other 19 amino acids or different l-phenylalanine analogs, including d-phenylalanine, 2-phenylacetamide, N-acetyl-l-phenylalanine, and d-phenyllactate, were able to elicit fluorescence changes in PheAACT-cpSFYFP, demonstrating its high specificity for l-phenylalanine (Fig. 1i and Supplementary Fig. 3a–c).

Next, we identified the l-phenylalanine binding sites of the PheAACT dimer through structural prediction and molecular docking analysis (Fig. 1j). Mutating residues at the l-phenylalanine binding sites, including T304, A308, G309, A310, L311, M325, L328, and E340, to Ala or Cys significantly diminished the response of PheAACT-cpSFYFP to l-phenylalanine (Fig. 1k). The substitution of T326 with Pro in PheA was previously reported to confer resistance to feedback inhibition by l-phenylalanine16. As expected, the T326P mutation in PheAACT-cpSFYFP also abolished the responsiveness of the biosensor to l-phenylalanine (Fig. 1k). These results demonstrate that the fluorescence response of PheAACT-cpSFYFP to l-phenylalanine is attributed to the binding of l-phenylalanine to the ACT domain of PheA. The PheAACT domain can be used as a biorecognition element for developing l-phenylalanine-specific GEFB.

Optimization and application of l-phenylalanine biosensor

We performed a systematic optimization of the l-phenylalanine biosensor to improve its performance in l-phenylalanine assays (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). Firstly, we constructed 50 biosensor variants by inserting cpSFYFP between amino acid residues located in the loop regions of the PheAACT domain with an N-terminal linker of Ser-Ala-Gly and a C-terminal linker of Gly-Gly-Cys (Supplementary Figs. 2, 4). The 38th variant (denoted as Phesor-0.38, with cpSFYFP inserted between residues 365I/366T of the PheAACT domain) exhibited a higher maximum fluorescence ratio change (ΔRmax, ΔR/Rmin at ligand saturation) of 67.2 ± 2.9% and an appropriate apparent dissociation constant (Kd) of 440 ± 80 μM, making it suitable for l-phenylalanine detection both in living cells and in human body fluids (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 2). Subsequently, we conducted random mutagenesis and high-throughput screening for linkers connecting the cpSFYFP and the PheAACT domain (Supplementary Fig. 2b, d). Among a total of 672 mutants, the biosensor with the N-terminal linker of Ala-Arg-Leu and the C-terminal linker of Gln-His-Leu possessed the highest response magnitude to l-phenylalanine (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). This biosensor was denoted as Phesor. Purified Phesor existed as a dimer both in the absence and presence of l-phenylalanine (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). It had two excitation peaks around 416 and 490 nm, with a single emission peak around 508 nm (405 nm excitation) or 516 nm (488 nm excitation) (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Upon binding with 1 mM l-phenylalanine, Phesor exhibited a 5.30-fold increase and 2.30-fold decrease in fluorescence when excited at 488 and 405 nm, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Dose-response curve analysis indicated that the detection range of Phesor encompasses the physiological concentrations of l-phenylalanine both in bacteria and in mammalian cells (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, it achieved a high ΔRmax (4030 ± 40%), an appropriate Kd (1760 ± 40 μM; close to the value of 2.69 ± 0.13 mM determined by thermal shift assay [TSA]), and a Hill slope close to 1 for l-phenylalanine (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 5e, and Supplementary Table 4). The pKa value of Phesor was determined as 8.92, and decreased to 8.10 upon l-phenylalanine binding (Supplementary Fig. 5f). Its extinction coefficients, quantum yields, and brightness in both apo and ligand-bound states are summarized in Supplementary Table 4. As a ratiometric biosensor, Phesor displayed a robust response independent of its own concentration (Supplementary Fig. 5g–i). In addition, Phesor showed high specificity for l-phenylalanine, with no significant fluorescence changes in response to the other 19 amino acids, as well as d-phenylalanine, phenylpyruvate, 2-phenylacetamide, N-acetyl-l-phenylalanine, and d-phenyllactate (Supplementary Figs. 3g–i, 5j). It also responded rapidly to variations in l-phenylalanine concentration within the assay system (Supplementary Fig. 5k, l). The increased fluorescence ratios induced by l-phenylalanine addition could return to basal levels upon the removal of l-phenylalanine from the system (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 5l). Consistent with other cpFP-based biosensors6,7, Phesor exhibits a pH-sensitive fluorescence response. However, upon correction with the l-phenylalanine-unresponsive control biosensor cpSFYFP7, its fluorescence ratio remained stable across the pH range of 6.6 to 8.2 (Supplementary Figs. 5m, n, 6a).

Next, we analyzed the feasibility of Phesor for l-phenylalanine assays in living bacteria. Phesor expressed in permeabilized Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) specifically responded to the exogenous gradient of l-phenylalanine addition in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 6b). TyrR recognizes l-phenylalanine, l-tyrosine, and l-tryptophan, and inhibits the expression of 3-deoxy-d-arabino-heptulosonate 7-phosphate synthase (AroF), the initial enzyme in the biosynthesis of three aromatic amino acids (AAAs)14. We deleted tyrR in E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔlacZΔfrdAΔldhAΔpoxBΔpflBΔptaΔackA and introduced the A294S mutation into phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase (Supplementary Fig. 7). The A294S mutation of phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase exhibited an increased Km for l-phenylalanine17, while the deletion of tyrR would result in increased l-phenylalanine synthesis. As expected, higher levels of intracellular l-phenylalanine in the mutant strain were detected using Phesor. Interestingly, the accumulated l-phenylalanine was catabolized during carbon starvation and was re-accumulated after 10 min of glucose supplementation (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 6c). These results suggest that Phesor can sensitively monitor endogenous l-phenylalanine fluctuations in live bacterial cells.

In addition, Phesor allowed for visual detection of l-phenylalanine in human cells (Fig. 2g). Phesor expressed in human embryonic kidney (HEK293FT) cells exhibited a dramatic response to exogenous l-phenylalanine addition and endogenous l-phenylalanine depletion (maximum ratio change of 709 ± 27%), with no observable fluorescence ratio changes to l-tyrosine and l-tryptophan fluctuations (Fig. 2g, h and Supplementary Figs. 6d, e, 8, 9). The l-type amino acid transporter 1 (LAT1) was reported to function in the uptake of l-phenylalanine in human cells18. As expected, the overexpression of LAT1 in HEK293FT cells enhanced the fluorescence ratio of Phesor under conditions of l-phenylalanine addition (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 10). Phesor could also be expressed in different subcellular compartments and responded to endogenous l-phenylalanine fluctuations (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12). We further investigated the subcellular distribution of l-phenylalanine in HEK293FT cells and found a relatively lower abundance of l-phenylalanine in the mitochondria than in the cytosol and nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 12c), which was consistent with previous results obtained using complicated mass spectrometry technology19. These results demonstrate that Phesor can monitor l-phenylalanine fluctuations in human cells with high spatiotemporal resolution.

l-Phenylalanine is a well-established biomarker for phenylketonuria20. We collected 14 serum and 9 urine samples from healthy individuals, and prepared 14 serum and 16 urine samples from simulated patients with hyperphenylalaninemia or phenylketonuria by spiking various concentrations of l-phenylalanine (Fig. 2j). Quantification of l-phenylalanine in these samples using Phesor demonstrated good correlation and consistency with high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) measurements, and the results were unaffected by the presence of different l-phenylalanine analogs (Fig. 2k, l and Supplementary Fig. 13). The measured l-phenylalanine concentrations in serum and urine from healthy individuals were 72.55−102.17 μM and 68.30−87.06 μM, respectively, consistent with previously reported values (Supplementary Table 2). Furthermore, we collected authentic dried blood spot samples from 19 healthy children and 23 children with phenylketonuria. Blood l-phenylalanine concentrations measured using Phesor ranged from 31.10 to 119.91 μM in healthy subjects, while those in phenylketonuria patients ranged from 393.45 to 1,821.13 μM, higher than the clinical diagnostic cut-off value of 360 μM (Fig. 2m)20. These results demonstrate that the GEFB Phesor based on the regulatory domain PheAACT in allosteric enzyme PheA is applicable for sensitive and precise detection of l-phenylalanine in human body fluids.

l-Valine biosensor using regulatory subunit IlvH in 2-acetolactate synthase

We constructed seven biosensor variants by inserting cpSFYFP into the following locations: the N-terminus or C-terminus of the intact IlvH, the middle of the two ACT domains of IlvH, the N-terminus or C-terminus of the separated IlvHACT1 domain, or the N-terminus or C-terminus of the separated IlvHACT2 domain (Supplementary Figs. 15, 16). The variant Valsor-0.03, created by inserting cpSFYFP into the middle of the two ACT domains of intact IlvH, possessed a higher ΔRmax of 109.2 ± 2.1% and an appropriate Kd of 414 ± 43 μM for l-valine (Fig. 3b). Among the three BCAAs, only l-valine increased the fluorescence ratio of Valsor-0.03 (Supplementary Fig. 17a–c). Subsequently, we systematically optimized the linker in Valsor-0.03 and identified a high-response magnitude biosensor, Valsor, with the N-terminal linker of Tyr-Thr and the C-terminal linker of Ala-Gly-Leu, from 173 mutants (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 15b, c). Valsor existed as a dimer and had two excitation peaks around 426 and 504 nm, with a single emission peak around 509 nm (405 nm excitation) or 518 nm (488 nm excitation) (Supplementary Fig. 18a–d). Dose-response curve analysis indicated that Valsor achieved a wide detection range, with an increased ΔRmax (875 ± 52%), an appropriate Kd (2170 ± 110 μM; close to the value of 4.46 ± 0.50 mM determined by TSA), and a Hill slope close to 1 for l-valine (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 18e, and Supplementary Table 4). The pKa value of Valsor was determined as 8.56, and decreased to 8.20 upon l-valine binding (Supplementary Fig. 18f). Its photophysical properties are summarized in Supplementary Table 4. In addition, Valsor demonstrated a robust ratiometric response independent of its own concentration, high specificity, good reversibility, and correctable pH-stability from pH 6.6 to 8.2 for l-valine detection (Supplementary Figs. 17d–f, 18g–m, 19a).

Next, we analyzed the applicability of Valsor for l-valine assays in living bacteria and human cells. Gradient concentrations of l-valine induced a dose-dependent increase in the fluorescence ratio of Valsor expressed in permeabilized E. coli BL21(DE3), while the other two BCAAs, l-leucine and l-isoleucine, could not (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 19b). LivJ and LivK are two periplasmic proteins involved in the BCAA transport of E. coli22. The knock-out of LivJ and LivK individually or in combination diminished the response of Valsor expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) to exogenous l-valine addition, suggesting both of them function in the uptake of l-valine in E. coli BL21(DE3) (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 19c).

Valsor also enabled the real-time monitoring of l-valine fluctuations in HEK293FT cells with a maximum ratio change of 340 ± 13% (Fig. 3g, h and Supplementary Figs. 19e, f, 20, 21). BCAA transaminases (cytosolic BCAT1 and mitochondrial BCAT2) catalyze the conversion of BCAAs to their corresponding branched-chain 2-ketoacids (Supplementary Fig. 22)23. Treatment with the pharmacological BCAT1 inhibitor gabapentin, or the BCAT1/2 inhibitors octyl-d-2-hydroxyglutarate and octyl-l-2-hydroxyglutarate, increased the fluorescence ratio of Valsor-expressing HEK293FT cells (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 23). The overexpression of IDH1R132H, a mutant isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 that causes supraphysiologic accumulation of endogenous d-2-hydroxyglutarate, also led to a 49.5 ± 9.8% increase in the fluorescence ratio of Valsor. Co-administration of the IDH1R132H inhibitor, AGI-5198, reduced the elevated fluorescence ratio of Valsor to near basal levels (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 23). Valsor could be correctly expressed in the cytosol, mitochondria, and nucleus, and responded to exogenous l-valine addition (Supplementary Fig. 24). Analysis of the subcellular distribution of l-valine demonstrated its lower abundance in the mitochondria (Supplementary Fig. 25). In addition, Valsor facilitated compartment-specific l-valine kinetic analysis. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 25f, gabapentin treatment induced a rapid and significant increase in the fluorescence ratios of cytosolic and nuclear Valsor. However, a delayed and slower signal increase of mitochondrial Valsor was also observed, which may be attributed to the influx of cytosolic l-valine.

l-Valine is a high-value bioactive compound, widely used in food, pharmaceutical, and feed industries24. We conducted fed-batch fermentative production of l-valine using the Klebsiella oxytoca VKO-9 strain24. As shown in Fig. 3j, k, Valsor exhibited a high correlation and consistency with HPLC results in the quantitative detection of l-valine in fermentation medium samples. The presence of different distractors did not affect the quantitative results obtained using Valsor (Fig. 3l). These results suggest that Valsor, developed based on the regulatory subunit IlvH in allosteric enzyme 2-acetolactate synthase, can serve as a reliable tool for precise l-valine detection both in living cells and in vitro.

Cystathionine β-synthase (CBS) domain as biorecognition element for the SAM biosensor and the MTA biosensor

Many allosteric enzymes possess multiple ligand binding sites and thus respond to diverse ligands (Supplementary Table 1)28,29. For example, the CBS domain (Pfam ID: PF00571) is a well-studied multi-ligand-binding regulatory domain present in allosteric enzymes29. It typically appears in pairs or quads in homomultimeric or heteromultimeric proteins. As part of the RIKEN Structural Genomics Initiative (RSGI) project, the X-ray structure of PH1780 in complex with SAM (PDB: 2YZQ) has been determined30. Interestingly, the upstream gene of ph1780, mth1 (ph1779), was predicted to encode an rRNA uridine N3 methyltransferase with SAM as the methyl donor (Supplementary Fig. 36). Most of the reported proteins with the CBS domain undergo conformational changes upon binding to adenosine derivatives or metal ions29. We constructed an initial biosensor by inserting cpSFYFP into the middle of the four CBS domains of PH1780 and determined its responsiveness to SAM, MTA, ATP, ADP, AMP, NAD+, NADH, Mg2+, or Zn2+. Changes in the fluorescence ratio of this biosensor were observed in the presence of SAM or the SAM deaminocarboxypropyl product MTA (Fig. 5b, c). In addition, PH1780 shares a high structural similarity with the CBS domain of MJ0100, an l-aspartate semialdehyde sulfurtransferase regulated by SAM and MTA (Supplementary Fig. 38)31. These results suggest that PH1780 may also interact with SAM or MTA and allosterically regulate the activity of MTH1 through multiple ligand binding (Supplementary Fig. 36).

Ligands of the CBS domain induce allosteric effects through binding at different sites29,31. We speculated that insertion of cpSFYFP into PH1780 may disrupt the binding pocket of one ligand without affecting the binding of the other ligand. Consequently, we constructed 90 biosensor variants by inserting cpSFYFP between amino acid residues located in the loop regions of PH1780 (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Figs. 37, 39). The specificities of these biosensor variants were characterized by assaying the ratio of their response magnitudes to SAM and MTA (Fig. 5d). The 85th variant (denoted as SAMsor-0.85, with cpSFYFP inserted between residues 234V/235I of PH1780) showed high selectivity for SAM (Fig. 5d). Neither MTA nor other analogs, including S-adenosyl-l-homocysteine (SAH), adenosine, or l-methionine, were able to elicit fluorescence changes in SAMsor-0.85 (Fig. 5e, f). In addition, a variant with high specificity for MTA (denoted as MTAsor-0.64, with cpSFYFP inserted between residues 182L/183R of PH1780) was also identified (Fig. 5d). MTAsor-0.64 responded exclusively to changes in MTA concentration, with no significant fluorescence changes in response to SAM, SAH, adenosine, or l-methionine (Fig. 5g, h).

We also identified two sets of SAM binding sites and two sets of MTA binding sites in PH1780 through structural alignment with MJ0100 (Supplementary Fig. 38). Mutations at the SAM binding sites in CBS3-CBS4 of PH1780 abolished the response of SAMsor-0.85 to SAM (Fig. 5i). Mutations at the MTA binding sites in CBS1-CBS2 of PH1780 diminished the responsiveness of MTAsor-0.64 to MTA (Fig. 5j). These results demonstrate that PH1780, containing four CBS domains can be used as a biorecognition element for developing an SAM-specific GEFB and an MTA-specific GEFB.

Exogenous gradient concentrations of SAM induced a dose-dependent increase in the fluorescence ratio of SAMsor expressed in permeabilized E. coli BL21(DE3), while MTA, SAH, adenosine, and l-methionine could not (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 41b). In E. coli, SAM is produced by SAM synthetase (MetK)-catalyzed l-methionine adenylation (Supplementary Fig. 42)33. The addition of 10 mM l-methionine to the medium of SAMsor-expressing E. coli BL21(DE3) failed to enhance the fluorescence ratio of SAMsor, whereas overexpression of MetK under the same condition resulted in a 24.0 ± 1.3% ratio increase (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 41c). The synthesis of l-methionine, the precursor of SAM, is repressed by the transcriptional regulator MetJ (Supplementary Fig. 42)33. The knock-out of MetJ in E. coli BL21(DE3) also led to an 18.3 ± 1.5% increase in the fluorescence ratio of SAMsor. The rat liver SAM synthetase (RLSS) and the bacteriophage T3 SAM hydrolase (T3SH) were previously reported to be capable of modulating endogenous SAM levels in E. coli34. As expected, overexpression of RLSS increased the ratio of SAMsor-expressing E. coli BL21(DE3) by 31.7 ± 3.1%, while overexpression of T3SH decreased the ratio by 43.5 ± 1.1% (Fig. 6f). These results suggest that SAMsor can sensitively monitor endogenous SAM fluctuations in live bacterial cells.

SAMsor expressed in HEK293FT cells also exhibited a specific and dramatic response to fluctuations in SAM levels with a maximum ratio change of 920 ± 39% (Fig. 6g–i and Supplementary Figs. 41d, 43, 44). In HEK293FT cells, methionine adenosyltransferase (MATase) catalyzes the conversion of l-methionine to SAM (Fig. 6j)35. The exogenous addition of l-methionine or suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA), an up-regulator of MATase transcription35, to the medium of SAMsor-expressing HEK293FT cells significantly increased the fluorescence ratio of SAMsor (Fig. 6j and Supplementary Fig. 45). Treatment with the pharmacological inhibitors of MATase, such as cycloleucine and AG-270, led to a substantial decrease in the fluorescence ratio of SAMsor-expressing HEK293FT cells (Fig. 6j)35. SAMsor expressed in different subcellular organelles also enabled compartment-specific SAM detection, with a lower mitochondrial SAM distribution observed (Supplementary Figs. 46, 47). These results suggest that SAMsor developed based on PH1780 is applicable for sensitive and precise detection of SAM in living cells.

Optimization and application of the SAM biosensor SAMsorL with a low detection range

SAM is also recognized as an important biomarker for evaluating human disease and health status36. Elevated SAM levels in body fluids are highly associated with the occurrence of diseases, including digestive cancer, lung cancer, and pneumonia (Fig. 7a)37,38. The LOD of SAMsor for SAM was calculated to be 310 nM (Fig. 6c), which exceeds the reported serum SAM concentrations in healthy individuals (~73.5−120 nM) (Supplementary Table 2). Fortunately, we found that the 34th variant (denoted as SAMsor-0.34, with cpSFYFP inserted between residues 111T/112V of PH1780) exhibited higher sensitivity to SAM among the 90 insertion variants of the SAM biosensor (Fig. 7b and Supplementary Figs. 37b, 39). Dose-response curve analysis indicated that SAMsor-0.34 achieved a ΔRmax of 80.0 ± 4.4%, a low Kd of 900 ± 110 nM, and a low LOD of 144.10 nM for SAM (Fig. 7b). Then, we conducted a systematic optimization of the linker in SAMsor-0.34 and identified a more sensitive biosensor SAMsorL with the N-terminal linker of Leu-Glu and the C-terminal linker of Thr-Pro from 2,715 mutants (Fig. 7c). We prepared authentic serum and urine samples from 13 healthy individuals and 15 patients with lung cancer. Quantification of SAM in these samples using SAMsorL exhibited good correlation and consistency with HPLC measurements, and the results were unaffected by the presence of different SAM analogs (Fig. 7e–h and Supplementary Fig. 48). The measured SAM concentrations in serum and urine from healthy individuals were 100 ± 40 nM and 11.9 ± 1.7 μM, respectively, consistent with previously reported values (Fig. 7i, j and Supplementary Table 2). Notably, serum SAM levels were significantly elevated in lung cancer patients, approximately 2.81-fold higher than those in healthy individuals (Fig. 7i). However, no significant differences were observed in urine SAM levels between the two population groups (Fig. 7j). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis indicated that serum SAM could effectively differentiate patients with lung cancer from healthy subjects, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.759, while urine SAM could not (AUC = 0.585) (Fig. 7k, l). These results demonstrate that GEFBs with adjustable affinities can be derived from the same allosteric regulatory domain. Furthermore, the SAM biosensor SAMsorL is applicable for sensitive and precise detection of SAM in human body fluids.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated the feasibility of utilizing regulatory domains of allosteric enzymes as biorecognition elements for GEFBs development. By coupling the ACT domain from homomultimeric enzyme (PheAACT), ACT-containing regulatory subunit from heteromultimeric enzyme (IlvH), or LA domain from homomultimeric enzyme (CimALA) with cpSFYFP, we developed three biosensors for the detection of l-phenylalanine, l-valine, and l-isoleucine, respectively. We also confirmed that the multi-ligand-binding PH1780 can serve as a biorecognition element to derive diverse specific biosensors, and then constructed two SAM-specific biosensors with complementary detection ranges based on PH1780 and cpSFYFP. These biosensors assembled into proper quaternary structures consistent with those of the native PheAACT domain, IlvH, CimALA domain, and CBS domain13,15,21,25,29. Each ligand molecule bound independently to individual monomers within the multimeric biosensors (hill slope close to 1). These results demonstrate that the developed biosensors preserved the intrinsic ligand-binding characteristics of their native allosteric regulatory domains. The methodology for developing GEFBs based on allosteric regulatory domains could be summarized in four steps: (i) selection of specific biorecognition elements, (ii) identification of optimal cpSFYFP insertion sites within loop regions to obtain a variant with obvious responsiveness and suitable affinity, (iii) optimization of linkers to maximize responsiveness, and (iv) functional testing (Fig. 1a). Notably, the affinities of allosteric regulatory domain-based GEFBs can be strategically modulated by shifting the insertion site of cpSFYFP. Besides SAMsor-0.34, multiple insertion variants with lower Kd values than Phesor, Valsor, and Ilesor were identified, such as Phesor-0.04 (57 ± 12 μM), Valsor-0.01 (35 ± 29 μM), and Ilesor-0.17 (59.4 ± 5.4 μM) (Supplementary Figs. 4, 16, 28). The higher affinities of these variants might be attributed to the insertion site of cpSFYFP being positioned distal to the binding pocket, which minimizes structural disruption and maintains higher architectural fidelity of the biorecognition element to its native conformation (Supplementary Fig. 49). This structure-function relationship provides a rational foundation for future GEFB engineering, thereby expanding the toolbox for metabolic imaging and in vitro diagnostics across varying concentration scales. Furthermore, significant ligand-induced fluorescence changes were observed in the as-designed biosensors. Analysis of pKa and photophysical parameters reveals that changes in extinction coefficients and quantum yields, rather than altered chromophore protonation equilibria, may primarily account for this fluorescence behavior (Supplementary Table 4)39. Future high-resolution structural analyses will be required to elucidate how conformational changes remodel the chromophore microenvironment to drive these photophysical shifts40,41.

The biosynthesis of many structurally similar metabolites often involves a common precursor and is co-regulated at the expression level. Due to their structural similarities and the promiscuity of the related transcriptional regulators, the distinction and specific tracking of these metabolites through transcriptional regulator-based biosensors are technically unavailable. For example, the AAA biosensor can be constructed based on TyrR, but it is unable to distinguish l-phenylalanine from the other two AAAs, l-tyrosine and l-tryptophan14,42. In addition, a method for developing GEFBs using ANL superfamily enzymes as biorecognition elements has been reported to achieve specific detection of l-phenylalanine and l-isoleucine in vitro. However, these GEFBs rely on adenylation reactions between analytes and ATP. The potential metabolic disturbances may limit their applications in living systems43. In this study, we successfully used the allosteric regulatory domain of PheA, the key enzyme involved in l-phenylalanine biosynthesis, to construct a specific biosensor Phesor. Phesor allowed for high spatiotemporal resolution detection of l-phenylalanine in living cells. Traditional screening of inhibitors of LAT1, a cancer therapeutic target involved in l-phenylalanine transport, was achieved using cumbersome radioisotope technology18. The in situ assay of intracellular l-phenylalanine through Phesor may also support high-throughput screening of LAT1 inhibitors. In addition, l-phenylalanine is a critical biomarker for phenylketonuria diagnosis and therapeutic monitoring20. Compared with traditional HPLC- or mass spectrometry-based methods, Phesor provides a point-of-care, easy-to-handle, cost-effective, and high-throughput alternative for clinical serum and urine testing (Supplementary Table 5). However, due to the relatively high detection range of Phesor, additional sample enrichment was incorporated to ensure the precise quantification of l-phenylalanine from dried blood spots. Future development of a higher-affinity variant based on Phesor-0.04 would effectively streamline the workflow for large-scale, dried blood spot-based newborn screening20. We also used the concept of Phesor construction to obtain Valsor and Ilesor for the specific detection of l-valine and l-isoleucine. l-Valine and l-isoleucine have distinct effects on human metabolism and health44. Deeper insights into their physiological and pathological roles may be achieved through Valsor- and Ilesor-supported spatiotemporal monitoring of l-valine and l-isoleucine. In addition, both biosensors enabled convenient and precise bioprocess monitoring for fermentative production of l-valine or l-isoleucine (Supplementary Table 5). Such biosensor-based high-throughput screening may accelerate the engineering of the l-valine and l-isoleucine producing strains43,45. Importantly, many other intracellular metabolites also allosterically regulate specific enzymes involved in their metabolism. For example, l-leucine allosterically inhibits 2-isopropylmalate synthase (LeuA), an enzyme solely involved in l-leucine biosynthesis of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Supplementary Table 1). An l-leucine-specific GEFB may be generated based on the LA domain of LeuA, and combined with Valsor and Ilesor to achieve the distinction and specific tracking of three structurally similar BCAAs, l-valine, l-isoleucine, and l-leucine.

Besides being exclusively regulated by one specific metabolite, many allosteric enzymes can recognize and thus be regulated by different allosteric effectors28,29. Fortunately, most allosteric enzymes with multiple effectors possess distinct allosteric centers. For example, the activity of inosine-5’-monophosphate dehydrogenase (IMPDH) is allosterically regulated by both ATP and GTP. ATP and GTP can bind to two separate allosteric centers within the regulatory domain of IMPDH, and modulate their own biosynthetic fluxes by activating or inhibiting IMPDH activity28. The separation of these allosteric centers makes the regulatory domains of multi-ligand-binding allosteric enzymes ideal elements for the development of different specific GEFBs. We successfully derived an SAM-specific GEFB, SAMsor, along with an MTA-specific GEFB, MTAsor-0.64, from PH1780. Specificity analysis of different insertion variants was conducted to screen for MTA-specific or SAM-specific GEFB in this study. Key amino acid residues involved in the binding of different allosteric effectors can be identified based on the resolved or predicted structures of the regulatory domains in allosteric enzymes. The destruction of allosteric centers for untargeted effectors through site-directed mutagenesis may also be effective for the development of specific GEFBs based on PH1780 and other multi-ligand-binding allosteric enzymes. SAM is an important metabolic intermediate and a universal cofactor in various cellular processes12,32. The RNA aptamer-based SAM biosensor exhibited a detectable response to the SAM demethylated product SAH and adenosine46. The SAMsor-enabled specific, real-time, and in situ assays of SAM may provide deeper insights into the functions of enzymes in SAM metabolism, support high-throughput screening of SAM-dependent methyltransferases, and facilitate the development of efficient inhibitors targeting methyltransferases related to various diseases47,48. In addition, SAM level in body fluids is an important indicator of disease status36–38. A more sensitive SAM biosensor, SAMsorL, was also developed in this study with PH1780 as biorecognition element. Besides binding-pocket engineering, the screening of cpSFYFP insertion sites proved to be an effective strategy for affinity optimization. Importantly, SAMsorL enables point-of-care quantification of SAM in human body fluids (Supplementary Table 5), offering a practical tool for the clinical diagnostics and therapeutic monitoring of SAM-related diseases.

Allosteric regulation of proteins is regarded as the second secret of life10. More than 30,000 chemicals, including many key intracellular metabolites for which GEFBs have yet to be developed, have been identified as allosteric ligands of allosteric enzymes (Supplementary Table 1)11. The corresponding allosteric regulatory domains may serve as promising biorecognition elements for the development of GEFBs. In addition, a single metabolite may participate in different metabolic processes and allosterically regulate multiple allosteric enzymes. For example, SAM has been demonstrated to allosterically regulate PH1780, l-threonine synthase in Arabidopsis thaliana49, and cystathionine β-synthase in humans50. The diversity of allosteric enzymes responding to one specific metabolite provides opportunities for the development of GEFBs with distinct properties, such as affinity, response magnitude, and applicable species. Beyond cpFP-based platforms, the regulatory domains of allosteric enzymes can also be integrated with signal transduction systems, such as Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) or bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) to develop other types of biosensors. In addition, the metabolite-responsive allosteric regulatory domains may also contribute to the design of controllable biological processes51,52. For example, incorporating an allosteric regulatory domain of interest into the interior of Cas9 proteins may enable the construction of specific metabolite-dependent allosteric CRISPR-Cas9 switches51. Fusing allosteric regulatory domains with a split ferredoxin, an electron carrier participating in various redox reactions, may allow for specific metabolite-induced production of reduced metabolites52.

In summary, we presented a versatile method for developing metabolite GEFBs using the regulatory domains of allosteric enzymes as biorecognition elements. Six highly sensitive and specific intracellular metabolite GEFBs (Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, SAMsor, SAMsorL, and MTAsor-0.64) were constructed using cpSFYFP and different regulatory domains. The applicability of the as-designed GEFBs was comprehensively characterized in living cells, fermentation systems, and clinical human body fluids. We hope that this study will be a starting point for systematic utilization of the regulatory domains of allosteric enzymes for developing GEFBs and thus broadening the accessibility of metabolite GEFBs.

Methods

Ethical statement

This study complied with all relevant ethical regulations. Sex and/or gender was not considered in the study design. The protocols of the studies on patients with phenylketonuria or lung cancer were approved by the Ethics Committee of Northwest Women’s and Children’s Hospital (Approval No. 2024-132) and Shanghai Fifth People’s Hospital affiliated with Fudan University (Approval No. 2020-052×1), respectively. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants or their legal guardians. No participant compensation was provided.

Bacterial strains and cell lines

The bacteria strains used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 2. E. coli DH5α, E. coli BL21(DE3), and their derivatives were cultured in Luria−Bertani (LB) broth at 37 °C and 180 rpm. Antibiotics were used at the following concentrations: ampicillin at 100 μg mL−1; chloramphenicol at 40 μg mL−1; kanamycin at 50 μg mL−1; spectinomycin at 50 μg mL−1.

The HEK293FT cell line (CL-0313) used in this study was purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology (China). The HEK293FT cell line stably expressing IDH1R132H was constructed previously8. All cells were cultured in high glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (ThermoFisher, USA) supplemented with 10% (vol/vol) fetal bovine serum (Biological Industries, Israel), 100 units mL−1 penicillin, and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin (ThermoFisher, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Construction of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, SAMsor, and SAMsorL

Insertion site screening

The plasmids and primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 3 and Supplementary Data 4, respectively. The gene encoding pheA or ilvH was obtained from the extracted genome of E. coli K-12. The gene encoding cimA or ph1780 was synthesized and cloned into the bacterial expression plasmid pETDuet-1 by General Biology Co., Ltd (China). To construct Phesor-0.38 (a Phesor variant with cpSFYFP inserted between residues 365I/366T of the PheAACT domain of PheA), the DNA fragment of PheAACT was PCR-amplified from the genome of E. coli K-12 using primer pair PheAACT-BamHI-F/PheAACT-HindIII-R, and cloned into pETDuet-1 plasmid using the BamHI and HindIII restriction sites. The obtained plasmid was then linearized by inverse PCR using primer pair 365I/366T-F/365I/366T-R. The DNA fragment of cpSFYFP was PCR-amplified using primer pair cpSFYFP-F1/cpSFYFP-R1. The obtained cpSFYFP fragment and linearized plasmid were then ligated using the T5 exonuclease DNA assembly (TEDA) method53. The final recombinant plasmid, pETDuet-Phesor-0.38, was transferred into E. coli BL21(DE3) for further protein expression and purification. The construction of the other insertion mutants of Phesor, as well as the insertion mutants of Valsor, Ilesor, SAMsor, and SAMsorL was performed using the same procedure.

Random linker mutagenesis combined with high-throughput screening

To construct the randomized linker library of Phesor-0.38, the plasmid backbone of pETDuet-Phesor-0.38 was linearized by inverse PCR using primer pair Phesor-0.38 inverse PCR-F/Phesor-0.38 inverse PCR-R. The DNA fragment of cpSFYFP carrying random linkers was PCR-amplified using degenerate primers containing NNB or VNN codons (for example, primer pair Phesor-0.38-3N-mutation/Phesor-0.38-3C-mutation). A theoretical diversity of 7.09 × 107 distinct sequences was generated by combinatorially pairing the N-terminal and C-terminal linkers of varying lengths (0, 1, 2, or 3 random amino acids). Then, the obtained cpSFYFP fragment and linearized plasmid were ligated by TEDA method and transferred into E. coli BL21(DE3) strain. The transformants were incubated on LB plates containing 100 μg mL−1 ampicillin at 37 °C for 12 h, followed by incubation at 23 °C for 24 h. Bright colonies were batch-picked and incubated in 5 mL LB medium containing 100 μg mL−1 ampicillin and 1 mM isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) for 16 h at 23 °C. The cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, resuspended in 2.5 mL of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), transferred to 48-well deep well plates, and then lysed by a SCIENTZ-48TD multi-channel ultrasonic disrupter (Scientz Biotechnology, China) on ice. The supernatants were extracted by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C and mixed with 5 mM l-phenylalanine at a volume ratio of 3:1. The changes in fluorescence ratio were then high-throughput detected using an EnSight microplate reader (PerkinElmer, USA). Mutants with high response magnitude were then subjected to Sanger sequencing. The random linker mutagenesis and the high-throughput screening of Valsor-0.03, Ilesor-0.23, SAMsor-0.85, and SAMsor-0.34 were performed using the same procedure.

Ligand binding site mutagenesis

Taking the construction of PheAACTT304A-cpSFYFP as an example, the DNA fragment of PheAACT containing the T304A mutation was PCR-amplified using primer pair PheAACTT304A-cpSFYFP-F/PheAACTT304A-cpSFYFP-R. The plasmid backbone of pETDuet-PheAACT-cpSFYFP was linearized by inverse PCR using primer pair PheAACTT304A-cpSFYFP inverse PCR-F/PheAACTT304A-cpSFYFP inverse PCR-R. The obtained DNA fragment and linearized plasmid were ligated by the TEDA method and transferred into E. coli BL21(DE3) strain for further protein expression and purification. Other mutants of PheAACT-cpSFYFP, mutants of SAMsor-0.85, and mutants of MTAsor-0.64 were constructed using the same procedure.

Protein expression and purification

All biosensor variants used in this study were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) strains by pETDuet-1 expression plasmid with a His6-tag. The expression strains were cultured in 1 L LB medium containing 100 μg mL−1 ampicillin at 37 °C and 180 rpm until the OD600 (optical density at 600 nm) reached 0.6. Then, 1 mM IPTG was supplemented to induce protein expression for 14 h at 23 °C and 160 rpm. The cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, washed twice with 67 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), resuspended in buffer A (20 mM Na2HPO4, 500 mM NaCl, and 20 mM imidazole, pH 7.4) containing 10% (vol/vol) glycerol and 1 mM PMSF, and lysed by an AH-BASIC high-pressure homogenizer (Antuos Nanotechnology, China) at 4 °C. The supernatants were extracted by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 40 min at 4 °C, and then loaded onto a 5 mL HisTrap HP column (GE Healthcare, USA) pre-equilibrated with buffer A. Elution of the protein from the column was achieved by a gradient of buffer B (20 mM Na2HPO4, 500 mM NaCl, and 500 mM imidazole, pH 7.4). The purified protein was then checked by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), quantified by the Bradford method, concentrated to ~5 mg mL−1, and stored at −80 °C.

Characterization of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor in vitro

Dose-response curve determination

Purified biosensors were diluted to a final concentration of 1 μM with 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) and mixed with increasing concentrations of l-phenylalanine (for Phesor), l-valine (for Valsor), l-isoleucine (for Ilesor), or SAM (for SAMsor) in a black 96- or 384-well plate at a volume ratio of 3:1 (biosensor to ligand, total 20 μL per well). After a 10-min incubation at room temperature, the fluorescence intensities of the biosensors at 528 nm with excitation at 405 or 488 nm were measured using an EnSight microplate reader. To precisely evaluate the detection sensitivity across both direct-response (F488 nm/F405 nm-increasing, e.g., Phesor) and inverse-response (F488 nm/F405 nm-decreasing, e.g., Phesor-0.38) biosensors, the response magnitude (ΔR/Rmin) is calculated as the absolute amplitude of the signal change divided by the lowest fluorescence state. Specifically, for direct-response biosensors, ΔR/Rmin = (R – Rapo) / Rapo × 100%; for inverse-response biosensors, ΔR/Rmin = (Rapo − R) / R × 100%. The maximum response magnitude (ΔRmax) is defined as the ΔR/Rmin at saturating ligand concentrations. Specifically, for direct-response biosensors, ΔRmax = (Rsat − Rapo) / Rapo × 100%; for inverse-response biosensors, ΔRmax = (Rapo − Rsat) / Rsat × 100%.

Oligomeric state analysis

To determine the native molecular weight of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor in the absence or presence of target ligands, gel filtration chromatography was performed using a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare, USA) and standard proteins including ferritin (440 kDa), aldolase (158 kDa), conalbumin (75 kDa), ovalbumen (43 kDa), and aprotinin (6.5 kDa). The elution buffer consisted of 50 mM H3PO4 and 150 mM NaCl (pH 7.2) with or without the addition of 1 mM l-phenylalanine, 1 mM l-valine, 1 mM l-isoleucine, or 100 μM SAM. The oligomeric states of these biosensors were inferred by comparison of the native molecular weight with the theoretical molecular weight of the protein monomers.

Spectra properties analysis

Fluorescence spectroscopy of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor was analyzed using appropriate concentrations of purified biosensors and 1 mM l-phenylalanine, 1 mM l-valine, 1 mM l-isoleucine, or 100 μM SAM. For excitation spectra, emission was measured at 530 nm with excitation from 350 to 514 nm in steps of 2 nm. For emission spectra, emission was measured from 505 to 650 nm in steps of 1 nm with excitation at 405 nm or 488 nm.

Affinity analysis through TSA

For analysis of the binding properties of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, or SAMsor to target ligands by TSA, 6 μM purified different biosensors, 5× SYPRO Orange, and increasing concentrations of l-phenylalanine (0−50 mM), l-valine (0−70 mM), l-isoleucine (0−150 mM), or SAM (0−20 mM) were mixed in 50 µL TSA buffer (20 mM Na2HPO4 and 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4). 25 µL of the mixture was then transferred into a 96-well white PCR plate (Roche, Switzerland). The Tm values were determined according to the derivative melt curves by calculating the first derivative of the raw fluorescence data at each temperature. The ΔTm values were calculated by subtracting Tm values of the control group (without any target ligands) from Tm values of the experimental group. Specificity analysis

Purified Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, SAMsor, and their variants were mixed with 20 amino acids or different structural analogs, such as phenylpyruvate (for Phesor), pyruvate (for Valsor), d-citramalate (for Ilesor), and SAH (for SAMsor), respectively, and the changes in fluorescence ratio were determined 10 min after incubation at room temperature.

Reversibility analysis

Purified Phesor, Valsor, and Ilesor were first mixed with different concentrations of target ligands, after which the ligands were eluted by centrifugation at 3800 × g for 30 min at 4 °C using a 10-kDa ultrafiltration centrifuge tube. The sample volume was then replenished to the initial level using ligand-free 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4). For SAMsor, given its significantly lower LOD, the same elution process was performed three times to completely remove detectable SAM from the retentate. The reversibility of the biosensors was analyzed by measuring the changes in the fluorescence ratio before and after ligand elution.

Kinetic analysis for ligand dissociation through enzymatic reactions

Purified Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor were initially incubated with different concentrations of target ligands. Subsequently, specific enzymes along with corresponding cofactors and co-substrates were introduced to catalyze ligand decomposition. For Phesor, 25 mg mL−1 aromatic aldehyde synthase from Petroselinum crispum (AAS) and 50 μM pyridoxal 5’-phosphate (PLP) were used. For Valsor and Ilesor, 30 mg mL−1 l-leucine dehydrogenase from Bacillus subtilis 168 (LeuDH) and 3 mM NAD+ were used. For SAMsor, the system included 25 mg mL−1 catechol-O-methyltransferase from Solanum lycopersicum (COMT), 100 mM Mg2+, 2 mM dithiothreitol, and 500 μM catechol. The kinetic responses of these biosensors upon ligand dissociation were assessed by continuously monitoring the changes in fluorescence ratios before and after enzyme addition using an EnSight microplate reader.

pH-stability analysis

The pH-stability of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor was analyzed by determining their fluorescence responses to target ligands between pH 5.0 and 10.0. The pH correction of different biosensors by cpSFYFP was realized by dividing the fluorescence ratio of these biosensors by the fluorescence ratio of cpSFYFP. The pKa values were determined by fitting the curve of fluorescence intensities against pH to a sigmoidal function represented as: f(pH) = base + (max − base) / (1 + 10pKa − pH).

Extinction coefficient determination

Extinction coefficients (ε) were determined as previously described6. Briefly, Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor were diluted in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) in the absence or presence of their respective ligands (1 mM l-phenylalanine, 1 mM l-valine, 1 mM l-isoleucine, and 200 μM SAM). Absorption spectra (300−650 nm) were recorded using an Ultrospec 2100 pro UV-VIS spectrophotometer (Amersham Biosciences, USA). Values for ε were calculated according to the Beer-Lambert law, ε = Abs / (c × l), where c is the molar protein concentration, l is the optical path length, and Abs is the optical density at the specific absorption wavelengths (λabs): 416 and 490 nm for Phesor; 426 and 504 nm for Valsor; and 420 and 504 nm for both Ilesor and SAMsor.

Quantum yield determination

Quantum yields (Φ) were measured as previously described6. The four biosensors and the EGFP reference standard (ΦEGFP = 0.6) were diluted to various concentrations in 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), with or without their respective ligands. Absorbance at specific wavelengths (416 and 490 nm for Phesor; 426 and 504 nm for Valsor; 420 and 504 nm for Ilesor and SAMsor) was measured using the Ultrospec 2100 pro UV-VIS spectrophotometer. Fluorescence emission spectra were subsequently recorded upon excitation at these respective wavelengths: for Phesor, emission was recorded from 435 to 650 nm (416 nm excitation) and 505 to 650 nm (490 nm excitation); for Valsor, emission was recorded from 441 to 650 nm (426 nm excitation) and 519 to 650 nm (504 nm excitation); for Ilesor and SAMsor, emission was recorded from 435 to 650 nm (420 nm excitation) and 519 to 650 nm (504 nm excitation). Φ was calculated using the equation: Φbiosensor = ΦEGFP × (mbiosensor / mEGFP), where m represents the slope derived from the linear regression of the integrated fluorescence intensity (spectral area) against absorbance.

Characterization of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor in permeabilized bacteria

E. coli BL21(DE3) strains harboring either pETDuet-Phesor, pETDuet-Valsor, pETDuet-Ilesor, or pETDuet-SAMsor were cultured in 50 mL LB medium containing 100 μg mL−1 ampicillin at 37 °C and 180 rpm until the OD600 reached 0.6. Then, 1 mM IPTG was supplemented to induce protein expression for 14 h at 23 °C and 160 rpm. The cultures were harvested by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, washed twice with wash buffer (17 mM KH2PO4, 24 mM K2HPO4, 16 mM NaH2PO4, and 24 mM Na2HPO4, pH 7.0), and resuspended to an OD600 of 5 with permeabilization buffer (wash buffer supplemented with 9.5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA], 0.1% [vol/vol] toluene, and 1% [vol/vol] glycerol). The suspensions were then vortexed for 2 min and placed for 24 h at 4 °C.

Permeabilized cells were collected by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 2 min at 4 °C, resuspended in 10-fold volume of distilled water, and then mixed with a gradient concentration of target ligands or different analogs in a black 96-well plate (PerkinElmer, USA) at a volume ratio of 99:1 (total 100 μL per well). The changes in fluorescence ratio were detected immediately by an EnSight microplate reader. The instrument parameters were set as follows: excitation at 405 and 488 nm, emission at 528 nm, time intervals of 1 or 2 minutes, temperature at 30 °C, and shaking at 180 rpm. Alternatively, samples were manually collected for analysis every 1 or 2 minutes. cpSFYFP was used in parallel experiments to exclude potential effects due to pH fluctuations.

Construction of E. coli mutants

Knock-out and replacement of genes in E. coli BL21(DE3) were performed using CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing technology. Taking the knock-out of tyrR in E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔlacZΔfrdAΔldhAΔpoxBΔpflBΔptaΔackA as an example, the homologous arm upstream of the tyrR gene and the homologous arm downstream of the tyrR gene were PCR-amplified using primer pairs ΔtyrR-F1/ΔtyrR-R1 and ΔtyrR-F2/ΔtyrR-R2, respectively. The PCR products were then fused together through overlap PCR to constitute the knock-out fragment. The sgRNA targeting tyrR was designed using Chopchop (http://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/) and expressed by the pEcgRNA plasmid. Then, 250 ng recombinant pEcgRNA plasmid and 1000 ng knock-out fragment were electrically transferred into the E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔlacZΔfrdAΔldhAΔpoxBΔpflBΔptaΔackA strain harboring the pEcCas plasmid. Positive colonies were selected on LB plates containing 50 μg mL−1 kanamycin and 50 μg mL−1 spectinomycin and verified by PCR and Sanger sequencing. The pEcgRNA and pEcCas plasmids were then sequentially eliminated by successive incubation in LB medium containing 10 mM rhamnose and LB plates containing 10 g L−1 sucrose. Knock-out or replacement of other genes in E. coli BL21(DE3) was accomplished using the same procedure.

Application of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor in living bacteria

Expression of Phesor in E. coli BL21(DE3) and E. coli BL21(DE3) 7Δ-ΔtyrRΔpheRS::pheRSA294S was carried out as described above. The bacterial pellets were collected by centrifugation at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, washed twice with carbon-free M9 minimal medium (34 mM Na2HPO4, 22 mM KH2PO4, 8.5 mM NaCl, 9.3 mM NH4Cl, 1 mM MgSO4, 300 μM CaCl2, 149 μM Na2EDTA, 6 μM ZnCl2, 600 nM CoCl2, 80 nM MnCl2, 31 μM FeCl3, 760 nM CuCl2, and 1.62 μM H3BO3, pH 7.4), resuspended to an OD600 of 5, and incubated under carbon-starved conditions for 8 h. Then, glucose at a concentration of 20 mM was added to the cultures to terminate carbon starvation. The fluorescence ratios of Phesor-expressing E. coli BL21(DE3) and E. coli BL21(DE3) 7Δ-ΔtyrRΔpheRS::pheRSA294S before carbon starvation, 8 h after carbon starvation, and 10 and 20 min after glucose supplementation were recorded using an EnSight microplate reader, respectively.

For analysis of the functions of LivJ and LivK in BCAAs uptake of E. coli BL21(DE3) using Valsor or Ilesor, E. coli BL21(DE3), E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔlivJ, E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔlivK, and E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔlivJΔlivK expressing either Valsor or Ilesor were collected, washed twice with carbon and nitrogen-free M9 minimal medium, and resuspended to an OD600 of 5. Then, 99 μL suspensions were mixed with 1 μL l-valine (final concentrations of 0, 100, and 500 μM; for Valsor) or 1 μL l-isoleucine (final concentrations of 0, 100, and 500 μM; for Ilesor) in a black 96-well plate. The changes in fluorescence ratio were detected 2 minutes after incubation at 30 °C.

For analysis of MetJ and T3SH functions using SAMsor, SAMsor-expressing E. coli BL21(DE3), E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔmetJ, or E. coli BL21(DE3)-pACYCDuet-T3SH were collected, washed twice with carbon and nitrogen-free M9 minimal medium, and resuspended to an OD600 of 5. Then, the fluorescence ratios of these strains were recorded, respectively. For analysis of MetK and RLSS functions using SAMsor, SAMsor-expressing E. coli BL21(DE3), E. coli BL21(DE3)-pACYCDuet-MetK, or E. coli BL21(DE3)-pACYCDuet-RLSS were collected, washed twice with carbon and nitrogen-free M9 minimal medium, and resuspended to an OD600 of 5. Then, 90 μL suspensions were mixed with 10 μL l-methionine (0 or 10 mM) in a black 96-well plate, and the changes in fluorescence ratio were detected 2 min after incubation at 30 °C.

Expression of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor in human cells

The DNA sequences of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor were human codon-optimized, synthesized, and cloned into the mammalian expression plasmid pcDNA3.1(+) behind a Kozak sequence by General Biology Co., Ltd (China). HEK293FT cells were inoculated into poly-l-lysine-coated 35-mm glass-bottomed dishes in appropriate numbers to achieve 70−90% confluency 24 h after plating. The medium was replaced with fresh complete medium 2 h before transfection. The transfection was carried out strictly in accordance with the Lipofectamine 3000 instructions (ThermoFisher, USA). Briefly, 0.5−3 μg pcDNA3.1(+)-based plasmids encoding either Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, or SAMsor were mixed with 4.5 μL lipofectamine 3000 reagent and 4.5 μL p3000 reagent in 150 μL Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Medium. The mixture was incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature and then uniformly added to the HEK293FT cell cultures.

Characterization of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor in permeabilized human cells

Imaging of HEK293FT cells expressing either Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, or SAMsor was performed 24 h after transfection using a Zeiss 900 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany) equipped with a 405 nm diode laser, a 488 nm argon laser, a 20×/0.8 objective, and a full-spectrum fluorescence detector. To determine the dose-response curves and specificity of these biosensors in HEK293FT cells, the transfected cells were first washed twice with 37 °C pre-warmed HHBSS buffer (1× Hank’s balanced salt solution supplemented with 20 mM 4-[2-hydroxyethyl]-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid [HEPES], ThermoFisher, USA). Then, 80 μM digitonin was added to the imaging medium to permeabilize cell membranes and remove basal intracellular ligand levels. Finally, gradient concentrations of target ligands or different analogs were added separately, and changes in fluorescence were imaged by the following procedure: excitation at 405 and 488 nm, emission at 503−617 nm, 1024 × 1024 frame size, 8-bit depth, and time intervals of 30 s. For each set of experiments, images were acquired using the same confocal settings. The ratiometric image analysis was carried out using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). cpSFYFP was used in parallel experiments to exclude potential effects due to pH fluctuations.

Analysis of fluorescence responses of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor to extreme metabolite fluctuations in human cells

To analyze the fluorescence responses of the biosensors to extreme endogenous metabolite fluctuations, HEK293FT cells expressing Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, or SAMsor were cultured under specific conditions for 48 h, with the medium refreshed every 24 h. Specifically, Phesor-expressing cells were cultured in l-phenylalanine-free DMEM, standard DMEM, and DMEM supplemented with 10 mM l-phenylalanine. Valsor- or Ilesor-expressing cells were cultured in BCAA-free DMEM, standard DMEM, and DMEM supplemented with 10 mM l-valine (for Valsor) or 10 mM l-isoleucine (for Ilesor). SAMsor-expressing cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 5 μM AG-270, standard DMEM, and DMEM supplemented with 500 μM SAM. Subsequently, changes in the fluorescence ratios of the respective biosensors were measured. cpSFYFP was used in parallel experiments to exclude potential effects due to pH fluctuations.

Application of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor in living human cells

For analysis of endogenous l-phenylalanine fluctuations using Phesor, 0, 5, or 10 mM l-phenylalanine was added into the medium of HEK293FT cells transfected with Phesor or co-transfected with Phesor and LAT1. Changes in the fluorescence ratio of Phesor were imaged 20 h after l-phenylalanine addition. For analysis of endogenous l-valine or l-isoleucine fluctuations using Valsor or Ilesor, gabapentin (2 or 20 mM), octyl-d-2-hydroxyglutarate (1 mM), or octyl-l-2-hydroxyglutarate (1 mM) was added into the medium of HEK293FT cells transfected with Valsor or Ilesor to inhibit the activity of BCATs. In addition, AGI-5198 at a concentration of 3 μM was added into the medium of HEK293FT-IDH1R132H cells transfected with Valsor or Ilesor to inhibit the activity of IDH1R132H. Changes in the fluorescence ratio of Valsor or Ilesor were imaged 20 h after treatment with different inhibitors. For analysis of endogenous SAM fluctuations using SAMsor, l-methionine (1 or 10 mM), SAHA (50 or 100 μM), cycloleucine (10 or 30 mM), or AG-270 (0.1 or 1 μM) was added into the medium of HEK293FT cells transfected with SAMsor to activate or inhibit the activity of MATase. Changes in the fluorescence ratio of SAMsor were imaged 20 h after treatment with different compounds. Images were obtained with the same confocal settings for each set of experiments. cpSFYFP was used in parallel experiments to exclude potential effects due to pH fluctuations.

Subcellular localization of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor

The pcDNA3.1(+) plasmid was used for the subcellular expression of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, SAMsor, or cpSFYFP in HEK293FT cells. For cytosolic localization, two repeats of the MAPKK nuclear export signal (MALQKKLEELELDEQQRKRLEDL)2 were fused to the N-terminus of these biosensors. For mitochondrial localization, a mitochondrial localization signal (MLSLRQSIRFFKPATRTLCSSRYLL) was fused to the N-terminus of these biosensors. For nuclear localization, three repeats of the SV40 large T antigen nuclear localization signal (DPKKKRKV)3 were fused to the C-terminus of these biosensors. The expression of subcellularly localized Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, SAMsor, or cpSFYFP in HEK293FT cells followed the same procedure as that used for the non-targeted biosensors. Functional characterization of these subcellularly localized biosensors in HEK293FT cells was performed 24 h after transfection. The cells were washed twice with 37 °C pre-warmed HHBSS buffer, permeabilized with 80 μM digitonin, and then treated with 750 μM l-phenylalanine, 750 μM l-valine, 5 mM l-isoleucine, or 40 μM SAM. Fluorescence ratios were measured before and after the addition of different target ligands using a Zeiss 900 confocal microscope. Different subcellularly localized cpSFYFP were used in parallel experiments to exclude potential effects due to pH fluctuations.

Application of Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsor for subcellular detection of target ligands in human cells

For the detection of endogenous l-phenylalanine fluctuations at subcellular resolution using Phesor, 0 or 15 mM l-phenylalanine was added into the medium of HEK293FT cells transfected with different subcellularly localized Phesor or co-transfected with different subcellularly localized Phesor and LAT1. Changes in the fluorescence ratios of different subcellularly localized Phesor were imaged 20 h after l-phenylalanine addition. For the detection of endogenous l-valine or l-isoleucine fluctuations at subcellular resolution using Valsor or Ilesor, 0 or 10 mM gabapentin was added into the medium of HEK293FT cells transfected with different subcellularly localized Valsor or Ilesor. Changes in the fluorescence ratios of different subcellularly localized Valsor or Ilesor were imaged 20 h after gabapentin treatment. In addition, time-course imaging of Valsor was performed at specified intervals. For the detection of endogenous SAM fluctuations at subcellular resolution using SAMsor, 0 or 5 μM AG-270 was added into the medium of HEK293FT cells transfected with different subcellularly localized SAMsor. Changes in the fluorescence ratios of different subcellularly localized SAMsor were imaged 20 h after AG-270 treatment. Different subcellularly localized cpSFYFP were used in parallel experiments to exclude potential effects due to pH fluctuations.

Preparation of human body fluid samples and bacterial fermentation medium samples

Serum samples from healthy individuals (5 males and 9 females for l-phenylalanine analysis; 8 males and 5 females for SAM analysis; age not recorded) and 15 patients with lung cancer (12 males and 3 females; age not recorded) were obtained by collecting venous blood in clot activator tubes, clotting for 2 h at room temperature, and then centrifuging at 2000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Urine samples from healthy individuals (3 males and 6 females for l-phenylalanine analysis; 8 males and 5 females for SAM analysis; age not recorded) and 15 patients with lung cancer (12 males and 3 females; age not recorded) were collected in sterile centrifuge tubes, filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, and then stored at −80 °C.

Dried blood spot samples from 19 healthy children (9 males and 10 females; age not recorded) and 23 children with phenylketonuria (9 males and 14 females; age not recorded) were prepared by spotting ~25−75 μL of whole blood onto filter paper cards. To maximize the recovery of l-phenylalanine from the matrix, the blood spot areas were completely excised, divided into small fragments, and immersed in 500 μL of Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4). The mixture was sonicated for 20 min, vortexed for 2 min, and stirred overnight, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min to collect the supernatant. To concentrate the samples for Phesor detection, the resulting supernatant was then completely dried in a vacuum centrifuge (Concentrator Plus, Eppendorf, Germany) at 45 °C, resuspended in an appropriate volume of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), and stored at −80 °C.

The l-valine-containing bacterial fermentation medium samples were generated by fed-batch fermentation of the K. oxytoca VKO-9 strain using glucose as the substrate24. 1 mL samples were collected at specific time points, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and then filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. The l-isoleucine-containing bacterial fermentation medium samples were generated by fed-batch fermentation of the C. necator H16 WT strain, CnIle6 strain, and CnIle8 strain using glucose as the substrate27. 1 mL samples were collected at specific time points, centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and then filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. The prepared bacterial fermentation medium samples were stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Determination of l-phenylalanine, l-valine, l-isoleucine, and SAM by HPLC

For the determination of l-phenylalanine in human body fluids using HPLC, the serum samples were mixed with methanol at a volume ratio of 1:1, vortexed for 2 min, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. The urine samples and extracted dried blood spot samples were boiled at 105 °C for 15 min, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. The prepared samples were then analyzed by an HPLC system (Agilent 1100 series, Agilent Technologies, Germany) equipped with a ZORBAX SB-C18 column (5 μm, 4.6 mm × 150 mm, Agilent Technologies, Germany) and a diode-array detector. The quantification was carried out using a 10 μL injection volume, 10% acetonitrile as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.6 mL min−1, a detection wavelength of 210 nm, and a total analysis time of 30 min.

For the determination of l-valine and l-isoleucine in bacterial fermentation medium using HPLC, the samples required pre-column derivatization with phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC)-acetonitrile and triethylamine (TEA)-acetonitrile. Briefly, 400 μL samples were mixed with 200 μL of 0.1 M PITC-acetonitrile and 200 μL of 1 M TEA-acetonitrile, and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Then, 800 μL of n-hexane was added, and the mixture was vortexed for 1 min. After being incubated for 10 min at room temperature, the lower liquid layer was extracted, filtered through a 0.22 μm filter, and subjected to HPLC analysis. The column and detector used for HPLC analysis were identical to those used for l-phenylalanine detection. The quantification was carried out using a gradient elution program consisting of mobile phase A (0.76% [wt/vol] NaAc and 7% [vol/vol] acetonitrile, pH 6.5) and mobile phase B (80% [vol/vol] acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 0.6 mL min−1: 0−11 min, from 0% to 7% B; 11−13.9 min, from 7% to 12% B; 13.9−14 min, from 12% to 15% B; 14−29 min, from 15% to 34% B; 29−37 min, 100% B; 37−45 min, 0% B. The injection volume was 5 μL, the column temperature was 40 °C, and the detection wavelength was 254 nm.

For the determination of SAM in human body fluids using HPLC, the serum samples were mixed with methanol at a volume ratio of 1:1, vortexed for 2 min, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. The urine samples were boiled for 15 min at 105 °C, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min, and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter. The column and detector used for HPLC analysis were identical to those used for l-phenylalanine detection. The quantification was carried out using a gradient elution program consisting of mobile phase A (8 mM octanesulfonic acid sodium salt and 50 mM NaH2PO4, adjusted to pH 3.0 with H3PO4) and mobile phase B (methanol) at a flow rate of 0.5 mL min−1: 0−16 min, 20% B; 16−16.9 min, from 20% to 40% B; 16.9−42 min, 40% B; 42−42.9 min, from 40% to 20% B; 42.9−63 min, 20% B. The injection volume was 20 μL, the column temperature was 25 °C, and the detection wavelength was 254 nm.

Determination of l-phenylalanine, l-valine, l-isoleucine, and SAM in different biological samples by Phesor, Valsor, Ilesor, and SAMsorL

For the determination of l-phenylalanine in human serum and urine using Phesor, various concentrations of l-phenylalanine were spiked into the prepared serum and urine samples from healthy individuals to simulate body fluids from patients with hyperphenylalaninaemia or phenylketonuria. Both authentic samples from healthy subjects and simulated samples were mixed with diluted Phesor in a black 384-well plate at a volume ratio of 1:1, and then the fluorescence ratios of Phesor were determined. The absolute quantification of l-phenylalanine was achieved by substituting the measured Phesor fluorescence ratio into the formula of the dose-response curve established based on serum or urine standards with known concentrations of l-phenylalanine. Measurement and quantification of l-phenylalanine in authentic dried blood spot samples were performed using the same procedure.

For the determination of l-valine or l-isoleucine in bacterial fermentation medium samples, the prepared samples were diluted to an appropriate concentration using 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4). Then, 10 μL samples were mixed with 10 μL diluted Valsor or Ilesor in a black 384-well plate. After incubating for 20 min at room temperature, the fluorescence ratios of Valsor or Ilesor were determined. The absolute quantification of l-valine or l-isoleucine was achieved by substituting the measured fluorescence ratio of Valsor or Ilesor into the formula of the dose-response curves established based on fermentation medium standards with known concentrations of l-valine or l-isoleucine.

Measurement and quantification of SAM in authentic human serum and urine samples from healthy individuals and patients with lung cancer using SAMsorL were performed using the same procedure as Phesor, with the urine samples required to be diluted to an appropriate concentration using 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4).

Structural prediction, molecular docking, and structural alignment

The structural prediction of PheA, Phesor-0.04, Phesor-0.38, IlvI, Valsor-0.01, Valsor-0.03, CimA, Ilesor-0.17, Ilesor-0.23, MTH1, PH1780, SAMsor-0.34, and SAMsor-0.85 was performed using AlphaFold3 (https://alphafoldserver.com/). The molecular docking analysis between PheA and l-phenylalanine was carried out using Maestro’s Induced Fit Docking Protocol in Schrödinger software (Schrödinger, USA). The docking conformation analysis and mapping were performed using PyMOL software (DeLano Scientific, USA). The PLIP website (https://projects.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip) was used to analyze the binding sites between PheA and l-phenylalanine. The structural alignment between the regulatory domain of MJ0100 (which consists of two CBS domains, PDB: 3KPC) with the CBS1-CBS2 domains (1M-134Y) or the CBS3-CBS4 domains (135Y-285A) of PH1780 was performed using the Structure Alignment Tool on the PDB website (https://www.rcsb.org/alignment). The structural alignment and RMSD determination between the regulatory domains in allosteric enzymes and those in the corresponding biosensor variants were performed using PyMOL software.

Statistics & Reproducibility

The acquisition of EnSight microplate reader data was performed using Kaleido 3.0 (PerkinElmer, USA). The acquisition of Zeiss 900 confocal microscope images was accomplished using Zen 3.1 (Zeiss, Germany). The processing of ratiometric fluorescence images was achieved using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA). OriginPro 2019 (OriginLab, USA), Graphpad Prism 5 (Graphpad Software, USA), and Graphpad Prism 8 (Graphpad Software, USA) were used to perform statistical analyses. A one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test or an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used to determine statistical significance, as appropriate; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001; ns, no significant difference (P ≥ 0.05). Exact P values are reported in the Source Data file. Statistical details and replication information are described in the legend of each figure. Similar results were obtained from at least three biological replicates. Blots or micrographs are representative of at least three experiments that showed similar results. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. In live-cell applications of the designed biosensors, overexposed cells were excluded from the analysis. No data were excluded from other analyses. Starting materials (purified biosensors, bacteria, or mammalian cells) were randomly assigned to different experimental conditions. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
