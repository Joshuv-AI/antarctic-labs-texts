Tailoring electronic structure and magnetic anisotropy in spray-pyrolyzed NiFe2O4 thin films for spintronic applications
Authors: J Patra, P Parida, P Patel, S K Sahoo, K Parvin, B J Babu, K Amemiya, A Kandasami, V R Singh, V K Verma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, we examined how substrate temperature affects the structural, morphological, electronic, and magnetic properties of nickel ferrite (NiFe2O4) thin films synthesized via spray pyrolysis. We investigated the effect of substrate temperatures (300 °C, 325 °C, 350 °C, and 400 °C) on NiFe2O4 thin films. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis revealed an inverse spinel structure with enhanced crystallinity at lower substrate temperatures. We employed high-resolution scanning electron microscopy (HR-SEM) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) to examine the surface morphology and elemental composition. We used vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM), X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), and X-ray magnetic circular dichroism (XMCD) to study the magnetic and electronic behaviors at room temperature. VSM measurements revealed ferrimagnetic behavior with magnetic anisotropy. XAS and XMCD analyses revealed that Ni2+ ions occupy octahedral sites and are susceptible to structural defects, which lead to spin canting via spin-orbit coupling. Fe3+ ions were found in both tetrahedral and octahedral sites. The orbital magnetic moments were determined to be 0.112 ± 0.006 µB/ion for Ni and 0.2412 ± 0.0121 µB/ion for Fe. This was attributed to structural distortions and Fe 3d–O–2p hybridization. These results demonstrate the potential of NiFe2O4 thin films for use in spintronic devices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43296-z.Keywords: NiFe2O4 thin films, Spray pyrolysis, X-ray absorption spectroscopy, X-ray magnetic circular dichroism, Magnetic anisotropy, Orbital magnetic momentSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

Spinel oxide compounds with the general formula AB2O4, where A-site cations are divalent and B-site cations are trivalent, commonly Fe3+ in spinel ferrites. These compounds possess a cubic crystal structure with a face-centered cubic (fcc) lattice formed by O2− anions, which creates both tetrahedral (Td) and octahedral (Oh) coordination sites for cations. In a typical spinel structure, one-eighth of the Td sites and half of the Oh sites are occupied by cations. Magnetically, the Td and Oh sublattices primarily exhibit antiferrimagnetic interactions, whereas weaker ferrimagnetic couplings can occur within the Td–Td and Oh–Oh sublattices1,2. NiFe2O4 is well-known as an inverse spinel ferrite, in which Fe3+ ions are distributed equally between Td and Oh sites, leading to near-complete cancellation of their ~ 5 µB magnetic moments due to antiparallel alignment. The net magnetization mainly arises from Ni2+ ions, which occupy the remaining Oh sites and contribute approximately 2 µB per ion3. The resulting cation distribution can be expressed as [Fe3+]A[Ni2+Fe3+]BO4, where eight Ni2+ ions preferentially occupy the B-sites (octahedral), while 16 Fe3+ ions are evenly distributed across the A-sites (tetrahedral) and B-sites.

Owing to this unique cation arrangement and magnetic structure, NiFe2O4 exhibits a combination of desirable structural, morphological, electrical, and magnetic properties. These characteristics make it an attractive material for spintronic applications, where both the charge and spin of electrons are used to develop advanced technologies such as magnetoresistive random-access memory (MRAM), spin valves, and magnetic sensors. In particular, NiFe2O4 thin films are of significant interest due to their ferrimagnetic behavior, magnetocrystalline anisotropy, and tunable electronic properties, which are strongly influenced by structural parameters and synthesis conditions. Among various fabrication techniques, spray pyrolysis, a cost-effective and scalable deposition technique, enables precise control over these properties through modulation of processing parameters such as substrate temperature. This method involves atomizing a precursor solution, spraying it onto a heated substrate, and inducing thermal decomposition, oxidation, and crystallization to form homogeneous, well-adhered NiFe2O4 films4–6. Importantly, spray pyrolysis allows precise control over key deposition parameters such as substrate temperature, which enables systematic tuning of crystallinity, morphology, and magnetic properties7,8. Due to their high resistivity, appreciable saturation magnetization, and magnetic anisotropy, spray-deposited NiFe2O4 thin films continue to attact considerable attention for next-generation electronic and magnetic device applications. The energy barrier for magnetic thermal stability, given by ΔE = Keff·V (where V is grain volume and Keff is the effective magnetic anisotropy constant), governs the stability of the magnetic state. In cubic crystals, magnetocrystalline anisotropy (MCA) is described by a series expansion involving first- and second-order anisotropy constants, K1 and K2, with Keff = K1/4 along hard axes and Keff = K2/12 along easy axes. Deviations from bulk values often arise in thin films due to variations in particle size, morphology, and synthesis conditions, which can enhance surface anisotropy through altered atomic symmetry. Additional contributions from surface and bulk defects, spin canting, and magnetic dipole–dipole interactions due to particle agglomeration can further increase anisotropy. Except for Fe3O4, most magnetic spinel oxides are semiconductors or insulators, requiring the stabilization of functional spinel or inverse spinel phases for devices such as magnetic tunnel junctions and other spintronic components9. X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) and X-ray magnetic circular dichroism (XMCD) are powerful, site-specific techniques that provide insight into the electronic and magnetic states of cations in inverse spinel structures10. For instance, XAS and XMCD at the Fe and Co L2,3 edges have been employed to determine the cationic and magnetic configuration in cobalt-zinc ferrite nanoparticles11. These techniques reveal crucial information about spin canting, superparamagnetism, and site preference in ferrite nanoparticles, which are relevant in applications ranging from ferrofluids and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to data storage and biomedical carriers. However, the complex magnetic behaviour of these nanoparticles arises from surface effects, cation distribution disorder, crystallinity, and size-dependent phenomena. XMCD, being chemically selective and sensitive to local environments, is instrumental in disentangling these interactions12–14. For example, the preferential octahedral occupancy of Co2+ ions critical for enhancing magnetocrystalline anisotropy in cobalt ferrite has been confirmed via XMCD. Additionally, this method enables the acquisition of element-specific hysteresis loops and the analysis of diluted ferrites through the comparison of isotropic XAS and XMCD spectra3. In a study of Co0.73yZn0.73(1−y)Fe2.18O4 nanoparticles (2.8–3.7 nm in size), XMCD revealed that Co2+ ions preferentially occupy octahedral sites, consistent with bulk behavior. The magnetic moment and cationic distribution were shown to vary with composition and particle size, highlighting the technique’s relevance in characterizing complex systems15–17. This underscores the importance of understanding cationic site occupancy and magnetic anisotropy to tailor the magnetic behavior of ferrite-based nanostructures for targeted applications11. Theoretically, the net magnetization in NiFe2O4 is predominantly derived from Ni2+ ions at octahedral sites, with a calculated moment of approximately 2 µB per formula unit18,19. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the magnetic, electrical, and structural properties of NiFe2O4 are highly sensitive to the cation distribution between A and B sites, which can be modulated through synthesis and doping strategies.

The magnetocrystalline anisotropies of NiFe2O4 have been extensively investigated using density functional theory (DFT), employing approaches such as the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) and local spin density approximation (LSDA) for electronic and structural analyses20. To account for electron correlation effects in transition metal oxides, several studies have adopted LSDA + U and GGA + U methods, which have been instrumental in exploring the influence of p–d hybridization, magnetoelastic properties, epitaxial strain, and magnetostriction in NiFe2O421–25. Pénicaud et al.26 conducted detailed analyses of magnetic moments, electronic spin-density functional energy bands, and the density of states (DOS) in NiFe2O4 using these methods. Additionally, XAS and XMCD spectra have been theoretically modelled using fully relativistic dirac linear muffin-tin orbital (LMTO) calculations within the LSDA and LSDA + U frameworks. Other computational efforts have utilized the linear combination of atomic orbitals (LCAO) method within DFT to determine magnetic moments, Mulliken population (MP) analysis, spin-resolved DOS, and Compton profiles (CPs) for NiFe2O427.

In the present work, NiFe2O4 thin films were synthesized via chemical spray pyrolysis at varying substrate temperatures, leading to systematic modulation of their structural, electronic, and magnetic properties. XAS, XMCD, and vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM) were performed at the Fe and Ni L2,3 edges to probe the local magnetic and electronic structure. The XMCD results revealed a pronounced orbital magnetic moment associated with Fe ions at 300 K, contributing significantly to the observed ferrimagnetic behaviour and magnetic anisotropy of the films. Variations in substrate temperature were found to directly influence film thickness, surface morphology, crystallite size, and microstructural evolution parameters that, in turn, strongly affect the magnetic characteristics of the films.

These results demonstrate the need for exact control over synthesis parameters, especially substrate temperature, in creating high-quality NiFe2O4 thin films with specific magnetic characteristics for innovative spintronic applications. The potential of NiFe2O4 for next-generation spintronic devices is demonstrated by the presence of a significant orbital magnetic moment in Fe ions at room temperature. In order to effectively utilize the material’s electronic and magnetic properties, our work shows the necessity of further research into synthesis methodologies and process optimization. Additionally, using experimental data, the study investigates the fundamental cause of the higher orbital moment seen at 300 K.

Experimental details

To investigate the influence of substrate temperature on film properties, NiFe2O4 thin films were synthesized via the spray pyrolysis method at four different substrate temperatures: 300 °C (S1), 325 °C (S2), 350 °C (S3), and 400 °C (S4). The precursor solution was prepared by dissolving nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO3)2·6H2O) and ferric nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O) in a stoichiometric molar ratio of 1:2 in deionized water. The solution was stirred vigorously to ensure complete dissolution of the metal salts. To enhance solubility, prevent precipitation, and promote uniform film formation, a complexing agent citric acid was added to the mixture. The resulting solution was then filtered to remove any undissolved particulates prior to deposition. Glass substrates were cleaned ultrasonically using acetone followed by drying to eliminate surface contaminants. The filtered precursor solution was atomized into fine droplets using a pneumatic and sprayed onto preheated substrates maintained at the target temperatures. Upon contact with the heated substrate, the solvent evaporated rapidly, and the precursor underwent pyrolytic decomposition to form nickel ferrite. Spray parameters, including the nozzle-to-substrate distance (20 cm), spray rate (3–4 mL/min), and carrier gas flow rate (air), were optimized to ensure uniform and reproducible film deposition. The deposition duration ranged from 10 to 30 min, depending on the desired film thickness. The thickness of the NiFe2O4 thin films is approximately 0.6–0.8 μm, as measured from cross-sectional HR-SEM images shown in the supplementary material (Fig. S1). To enhance the crystallinity of the deposited films, a post-deposition annealing process was performed at a temperature of 600 °C.

During the spray pyrolysis process, the spinel NiFe2O4 phase forms through the reaction of metal cations (Ni2+ and Fe3+) with oxygen, as confirmed by structural analyses. Substrate temperature plays a critical role in determining the morphology and crystallinity of film, which subsequently influences the structural, morphological, electronic, and magnetic properties of the material.

The structural and magnetic properties of NiFe2O4 thin films were comprehensively investigated. X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements were carried out using a Rigaku diffractometer to determine the lattice constant and crystallite size. Morphological features and elemental composition were analysed using High-resolution scanning electron microscopy (HR-SEM) and Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), employing a Carl Zeiss EVO 18 Research system and an FEI-QUANTA-250-FEG operated at 20 kV. Bulk magnetization measurements were performed using VSM at NIT Rourkela. For element-specific magnetic characterization, XAS and XMCD were employed. These measurements were conducted at the L2,3 edges of Ni and Fe using the undulator beamline 16 A at the Photon Factory (KEK), Japan. Circularly polarized light was used to probe the magnetic characteristics of the NiFe2O4 thin films. The XMCD signal was derived by comparing the absorption coefficients (µ⁺ and µ⁻) corresponding to parallel and antiparallel helicities of the incident photons relative to the predominant spin orientation. To explore the interplay between spin and orbital magnetic moments, the magnetic field dependence of the XMCD intensity was analysed. The monochromator at BL-16 A provided a circular polarization degree of approximately 87% ± 4% with an energy resolution of E/ΔE > 10,000. All measurements were performed at 300 K in a high-vacuum chamber with a base pressure of 10− 9 Torr. Data were acquired in total electron yield (TEY) mode, which offers surface sensitivity with a probing depth of ~ 5 nm. The XAS and XMCD spectra were recorded in an out-of-plane geometry under an applied magnetic field of 2 T.

Results and discussions

Structural analysis by x-ray diffraction

XRD using Cu Kα radiation was employed to investigate the structural properties of NiFe2O4 thin films over a 2θ range of 28° to 65°. The XRD patterns of samples S1 to S4 are shown in Fig. 1. Prominent diffraction peaks observed at 2θ values of approximately 30.28°, 34.91°, 37.32°, 43.38°, 53.81°, 57.39°, and 63.02° correspond to the (220), (311), (222), (400), (422), (511), and (440) crystallographic planes, respectively. These peaks confirm the formation of a cubic spinel structure with Fd-3m (space group 227) symmetry. The obtained diffraction data are in good agreement with the standard JCPDS database. As illustrated in Fig. 1, all NiFe2O4 thin film samples exhibit a strong preferential orientation along the (311) plane. This preferred orientation is significant, as it directly influences the magnetic and electrical properties of the films, which are highly dependent on the underlying crystallographic structure. The intensity of the (311) diffraction peak is highest at a substrate temperature of 300 °C (S1) and decreases with further increases in temperature. The (311) reflection was plotted without taking vertical offsets (see inset of Fig. 1) showing systematic reduction in peak intensity that can be attributed to decreased film thickness, as confirmed by cross-sectional HRSEM analysis (Fig. S1) and limited grain growth. Similar observations where increased substrate temperature results in reduced crystallite size and effective film thickness due to re-evaporation and enhanced nucleation have been reported28 for ferrite thin films prepared by spray pyrolysis technique. Elevated temperatures may promote partial re-evaporation or desorption of precursor species during deposition, resulting in thinner films and reduced grain growth, thereby lowering the effective diffracting volume and peak intensity.

XRD spectra of NiFe2O4 thin films. The zoomed-in picture corresponds to the most intense (311) peak.

The calculated interplanar spacing (d311) values are 2.514 ± 0.002, 2.512 ± 0.003, 2.509 ± 0.004, and 2.507 ± 0.005 Å for samples S1, S2, S3, and S4, respectively. All values are approximately ~ 2.51 Å and closely match the standard value reported for cubic spinel and JCPDS file (No. 44-1485), confirming the formation of the expected spinel phase without secondary phases.

This structural feature is critical to the material’s magnetic behavior, as cubic symmetry generally supports more isotropic magnetic properties. Crystallite size for films with preferred orientation along the (311) plane was estimated using the Debye–Scherrer formula. This analysis offers valuable insights into how grain size influences the magnetic and structural characteristics of the films. Additionally, the lattice parameter a for each sample (S1–S4) was calculated using standard crystallographic relations, further validating the formation of the spinel phase.

The estimated average value of the lattice parameter, a = 8.3391 Å.

The cubic spinel structure of NiFe2O4, belonging to the Fd-3m space group, was further confirmed through lattice parameter calculations. A shift of the diffraction peaks toward lower 2θ angles in the XRD patterns suggests an increase in the lattice parameter, which can be attributed to thermal expansion or redistribution of cations within the lattice. Crystallite size plays a crucial role in determining the structural and magnetic behaviour of NiFe2O4 thin films and is thus an important parameter in assessing their functional performance.

To accurately evaluate crystallite size and lattice strain, four analytical methods were employed: the Debye–Scherrer method, Modified Debye–Scherrer method, Williamson–Hall (W–H) analysis, and the Size–Strain Plot (SSP) technique. These methods account for the broadening of XRD peaks, which arises from both finite crystallite size and microstrain within the crystal lattice. The calculated parameters are shown in Table 1. Among these techniques, the W–H and SSP analyses revealed a clear trend: as the substrate temperature increases from 300 °C to 400 °C (samples S1–S4), microstrain tends to increase while crystallite size decreases. This behaviour can be attributed to elevated lattice strain at higher temperatures, rapid solvent evaporation, and enhanced nucleation rates, which collectively hinder grain growth and coalescence. As a result, the films exhibit finer nanocrystalline structures. This trend aligns with previous reports indicating that higher substrate temperatures induce additional strain due to lattice mismatch with the substrate. All four analytical approaches yielded consistent results, underscoring the strong influence of substrate temperature on the microstructural evolution of NiFe2O4 thin films29.

High resolution scanning electron microscopy

HR-SEM images of NiFe2O4 thin film samples S1–S4 are presented in Fig. 2(a-d). The images reveal a uniform, granular, and nanostructured surface morphology with minimal surface agglomeration. The observed agglomerations may result from van der Waals interactions between nanoparticles. As the substrate temperature increases to 400 °C (S4), the films maintain their granular and homogeneous surface texture; however, a noticeable increase in particle agglomeration is observed30. Notably, at 325 °C (S2), the film surface becomes disrupted, showing separation into isolated islets, indicative of surface deformation. This morphological evolution highlights the sensitivity of film uniformity and particle distribution to variations in substrate temperature during deposition.

(a-d) HR-SEM morphology corresponding to energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy mappings for Ni, Fe, and O of NiFe2O4 thin film samples S1-S4.

EDS mapping was employed to investigate the elemental composition of NiFe2O4-based ferrite samples (S1–S4). The surface of S1 is smooth and thick, suggesting higher crystallinity, fewer pores, and more uniform grain growth. In contrast, surfaces in S2, S3, and S4 exhibit increased roughness, indicating the presence of voids, agglomerations, or defects resulting from the synthesis conditions. The alignment of magnetic domains is strongly influenced by surface morphology. Smooth, crystalline grains facilitate better domain alignment, enhancing saturation magnetization (Ms). However, rough or disordered surfaces can lead to magnetic frustration, reducing magnetic strength. Elemental maps of S1 and S4 [Fig. 2(a–d)] show a uniform distribution of Ni, Fe, and O, indicating a well-formed spinel crystal structure (AB2O4) without significant elemental segregation. HR-SEM images further confirm the smooth and uniform surface morphology of these films. In S2 and S3, the distribution of Fe and Ni is less uniform, possibly due to non-stoichiometry or incomplete phase formation. Such irregularities in cation distribution can weaken the A–O–B magnetic coupling, crucial for ferrimagnetism in spinel ferrites, particularly in NiFe2O431,32. The magnetic properties of the material depend on the relative alignment of Fe3+ ions, which interact with oxygen (O2−). These interactions are directly influenced by cation distribution and crystallinity: a strong A–O–B interaction and homogenous cation dispersion result in higher Ms, whereas defects or cation disorders coupled with poor crystallinity reduce the A–O–B interaction and lower Ms. Due to their smooth surfaces and uniform elemental distribution, S1 is expected to exhibit superior magnetic properties. In contrast, S2, S3, and S4 may show diminished magnetization due to structural and chemical inhomogeneity33. The HR-SEM results align with the XRD findings, confirming that substrate temperature significantly impacts surface quality. This gives the importance of controlled synthesis conditions for optimizing the magnetic characteristics of NiFe2O4 thin films.

Vibrating sample magnetometer

Figure 3(a) presents the magnetization curves for NiFe2O4 thin film samples S1–S4. At room temperature, the M versus H curves exhibit an “S” shape with no observable hysteresis, indicating very low coercivity and remanence, which approach the resolution limit of the VSM. The samples exhibit ferrimagnetic ordering with soft magnetic behaviour, characterized by near-closed hysteresis loops rather than truly hysteresis-free paramagnetism. The S1 sample demonstrates the highest Ms and remanent magnetization (Mr), along with the lowest coercivity (Hc). As the substrate temperature increases from S1 to S4, both Ms and Mr decrease, while Hc increases, as depicted in Table 2 The NiFe2O4 thin films exhibit well-defined ferrimagnetic ordering, with negligible secondary phases, as confirmed by XRD and HRSEM, and a well-formed spinel structure, contributing to the high values of Ms and Mr. As the substrate temperature increases, grain growth becomes excessive, and crystallite size decreases, as observed in the XRD analysis. These structural changes can affect the A–O–B superexchange interactions, which are crucial for maintaining high magnetization levels. Elevated substrate temperatures may lead to the formation of structural defects or secondary phases (e.g., Fe-rich or Ni-rich phases), which reduce the number of magnetically active Fe3+ ions at the octahedral and tetrahedral sites, thereby diminishing magnetization. Coercivity is strongly influenced by factors such as particle size, defect density, and magnetocrystalline anisotropy. At lower substrate temperatures, ferrite grains exert weaker pinning effects on domain wall motion, resulting in decreased coercivity. However, at higher substrate temperatures, secondary phases or internal stresses within the lattice can further hinder domain wall motion, leading to an increase in coercivity. The films deposited at lower substrate temperature exhibit relatively larger crystallite size along with reduced coercivity, whereas samples grown at higher substrate temperatures show increased coercivity. This trend is consistent with widely reported behavior in spinel ferrite systems34,35, where larger crystallite size and improved structural ordering lead to reduced domain wall pinning and hence lower coercivity, while increased defect density, strain, and grain boundary effects enhance pinning and coercivity. Similar correlations between crystallite size, defect structure, and coercivity have been extensively reported34,35 for Ni-based spinel ferrites and rare-earth-doped ferrite systems.

The experimental data and the Langevin function fit show slight discrepancies. These deviations from the Langevin function fit to the magnetization data can be attributed to surface spin disorder36. The Langevin function, a theoretical model, is commonly used to describe the magnetization behavior of systems with non-interacting magnetic moments, such as paramagnetic or superparamagnetic materials, under an applied magnetic field. In this model, magnetic moments align with the external field, leading to linear magnetization at low fields, which saturates as the moments fully align at high fields. The Langevin fit to the experimental data, representing the ferrimagnetic component, is depicted by the blue lines. The fit suggests that a paramagnetic or superparamagnetic contribution, where magnetic moments interact independently of the strong exchange interactions characteristic of pure ferrimagnetism, partially contributes to the total magnetization. The Langevin function also implies that some magnetic entities, such as domains, follow the classical thermal agitation model, indicative of superparamagnetism, despite the predominantly ferrimagnetic behaviour (steep rise at low fields) observed in the samples. The total ferrimagnetic response is superimposed on this. For non-interacting magnetic moments, such as those in superparamagnetic materials, the Langevin function describes their magnetization behaviour under an applied magnetic field37. This model is particularly appropriate for systems where thermal fluctuations are strong enough to cause rapid alignment and randomization of magnetic moments. According to the BMP model, localized charge carriers influence the magnetic moments of transition metal ions, such as Fe3+ and Ni2+ in NiFe2O4. A polaron is formed when spins align ferrimagnetically within a “bound” region created by these charge carriers. The collective behavior of these polarons contributes to the overall magnetization, which may explain deviations from classical ferrimagnetic or superparamagnetic models, such as the Langevin model. The BMP model, typically fitted with expressions involving hyperbolic functions or exponential terms adjusted to the material’s parameters, predicts a field-dependent magnetization that includes contributions from both the size and density of polarons. The M-H curves, shown previously, are analyzed using the BMP model to extract parameters related to magnetic interactions and polaron generation. These fits generally involve adjusting parameters like the polaron concentration, the effective magnetic moment per polaron, and the polaron binding energy. The higher saturation magnetization observed in the S1 and S2 samples suggests a stronger BMP contribution. This is consistent with the steeper initial magnetization rise, which may indicate higher polaron concentrations or larger polaron sizes. Enhanced spin alignment within the polarons is likely due to improved crystallinity or fewer defects. In contrast, the S3 and S4 samples exhibit smaller or fewer polarons, as indicated by their lower saturation magnetization (~ 1.0 µB/u.c.). The BMP fit for these samples may suggest greater disorder or lower polaron binding energy, leading to a slower increase in magnetization. This observation aligns with the softer curves and potentially smaller surface effects or particle sizes. The BMP model provides a mechanism for the localized spin coupling responsible for the superparamagnetic-like behavior observed in these thin films. Unlike the Langevin fit, which assumes non-interacting moments, the BMP model incorporates charge carrier-mediated interactions, offering a more complex explanation for the observed magnetization. The decrease in both Ms and Mp values likely results in a corresponding decrease in the BMP fitting parameters (e.g., polaron radius, exchange interaction strength) from S1 to S4 (Table 4). According to the BMP model, localized charge carriers in polaronic ferromagnetism, which enhance the alignment of magnetic moments, contribute significantly to the magnetization in these NiFe2O4 thin films. Surface imperfections and effects that localize charge carriers are particularly important in thin films. The decreasing magnetization trend (S1 > S2 > S3 > S4) could reflect a reduction in polaron size or concentration, potentially driven by smaller grain sizes in subsequent samples, thinner film growth, or an increased number of defects, as evidenced by XRD and HRSEM analysis. The BMP fit quantifies these variations, providing insights into the microstructure. The success of the BMP model in fitting these curves suggests that both intrinsic ferrimagnetic order and extrinsic polaronic effects contribute to the magnetic properties of NiFe2O4 thin films. These effects can be modulated by adjusting the deposition conditions, such as substrate temperature. The value of Mo is highest for S1 and lowest for S4, although the spontaneous magnetization shows the opposite trend. Similar findings have been reported by Mohanty et al.39, who observed that meff varies inversely with spontaneous magnetization. This behaviour has been attributed to competing exchange interactions between the matrix and BMPs. Moreover, the highest N values were observed for S1, suggesting improved ferrimagnetic behaviour in this sample, which confirms both the matrix’s contribution and the formation of BMPs as a result of substrate temperature.

Higher substrate temperatures lead to a reduction in the K, primarily due to cation redistribution, as we confirmed from XPS analysis (Fig. S2-S4 in supplementary material) within the spinel lattice. As substrate temperature increases, structural and microstructural modifications including changes in crystallite size, defect density, and cation migration contribute to a decrease in magnetic anisotropy energy, as evidenced by lower K values. The experimental data clearly demonstrate a declining trend in both magnetic anisotropy and saturation magnetization with increasing substrate temperature, indicating thermally induced modifications in the material’s internal structure. The high magnetic anisotropy observed in NiFe2O4, a spinel-structured ferrite, can be explained by the one-ion model, where Ni2+ ions occupying the B sites play a crucial role. The spinel structure of NiFe2O4 features two types of cation coordination sites: A and B. While Fe3+ ions are distributed across both A and B sites, Ni2+ ions preferentially occupy the octahedral B-sites. Due to the 3d⁸ electronic configuration of Ni2+, crystal field splitting at the B-sites results in a partial separation of d-orbitals into t2g and eg levels. However, the crystal field strength is insufficient to completely lift orbital degeneracy, leaving the orbital angular momentum partially unquenched. This incomplete quenching enables significant spin–orbit coupling (SOC), in which spin and orbital angular momenta interact. SOC contributes to the alignment of magnetic moments along preferred crystallographic directions, thereby giving rise to large magnetocrystalline anisotropy. The strength of SOC and thus the magnitude of the anisotropy constant is directly influenced by the occupancy of Ni2+ ions at the B-sites. Elevated substrate temperatures can lead to a reduction in Ni2+ occupancy at these sites, either due to cation migration or increased defect formation, weakening SOC and resulting in a lower K. Consequently, the reduction in preferred magnetic moment alignment renders the material magnetically softer41,42.

The low coercivity values observed for all samples further confirm that NiFe2O4 films behave as soft ferrites. As the substrate temperature decreases, saturation magnetization increases43, a trend that is consistent with earlier findings by Ateia et al.44. and Sharma et al.45. In NiFe2O4, which exhibits an inverted spinel structure, the cation arrangement is typically represented as [Fe3+]A[Ni2+,Fe3+]B. Fe3+ ions occupying A-sites have a magnetic moment of 5 µB, while B-sites are shared by additional Fe3+ and Ni2+ ions (µ ≈ 2 µB). The superexchange interaction between the A and B sublattices is antiferrimagnetic in nature, meaning the Fe3+ ions at A-sites align antiparallel to those at B-sites. Therefore, any changes in the distribution of Fe3+ and Ni2+ across the two sublattices, resulting from synthesis parameters such as substrate temperature, will directly impact the net magnetization (MB − MA)46–48. In this context, redistribution or partial inversion of cations alters the superexchange interactions and, consequently, the total magnetic response. The highest saturation magnetization is observed in S1, where enhanced sublattice ordering and reduced thermal agitation promote stronger A–B interactions.

To further probe the nature of magnetic phase transitions, Arrott plots are employed shown in Fig. 4(b). These are graphical tools based on the Arrott–Noakes equation of state, which help distinguish between ferrimagnetic and paramagnetic behaviour by plotting M² against H/M. The linearity and intercept of these plots provide insights into the presence of spontaneous magnetization and the type of magnetic ordering in the material.

Where M is the magnetization, H is the applied magnetic field, and C and b are material-dependent constants, plotting M² versus H/M yields straight-line graphs if the material adheres to the mean-field theory of ferromagnetism. This approach forms the basis of the Arrott–Belov–Kouvel (ABK) plot, which is widely used to evaluate the nature of magnetic ordering and to confirm the existence of spontaneous magnetization. The ABK plots for the NiFe2O4 thin film samples exhibit convex curvature, indicating ferrimagnetic ordering at room temperature, with finite values of spontaneous magnetization even at H = 049. This behavior confirms the presence of long-range magnetic order and supports the results obtained from VSM. To determine Ms, a polynomial fit was applied to the high-field linear region of the Arrott plots, as shown in Fig. 4(b). The extrapolation of the linear region to the positive intercept on the M²-axis yields Ms², enabling an estimation of the spontaneous magnetization. This analysis also offers qualitative insight into the presence of lattice strain or magnetic inhomogeneities within the NiFe2O4 thin films at room temperature. The interpretation of Arrott plots is grounded in Landau’s theory of second-order phase transitions, where the equilibrium magnetization can be derived from the minimization of Gibbs free energy, expressed as dF/dM = 0. The theory assumes contributions solely from the ferrimagnetic component, as originally proposed in Weiss’ molecular field theory. The curvature and slope of the Arrott plots are indicative of the magnetic phase transition’s order. Following Banerjee’s criterion50, a positive slope throughout the magnetized region is characteristic of a second-order (continuous) magnetic phase transition. Furthermore, the intercept of the linear portion of the Arrott plot with the M²-axis corresponds to the square of the spontaneous magnetization. A larger intercept indicates a higher spontaneous magnetization, whereas an absence of intercept or a plot that passes through the origin may signify paramagnetic or antiferrimagnetic behavior. Based on this analysis, samples S1 and S2 display larger intercepts and steeper positive slopes compared to samples S3 and S4, indicating higher spontaneous magnetization and more robust ferrimagnetic character. In contrast, samples S3 and S4 exhibit comparatively lower intercepts, consistent with weakened ferrimagnetic ordering and the possible emergence of structural disorder or spin canting at elevated substrate temperatures.

To further study the microscopic explanation of the observed magnetic behaviour, XAS and XMCD were applied. These element-specific methods offer comprehensive information about the magnetic contribution, site occupancy, and valence state of Ni and Fe ions in the NiFe2O4 matrix. While XMCD provides quantitative measurements of the spin and orbital magnetic moments, XAS spectra provide the determination of oxidation states and local electronic structures. The combined XAS/XMCD results are consistent with the trends observed in the VSM experiments, confirming the relationship between cationic distribution, local environment, and magnetic characteristics in these ferrite thin films.

X-ray absorption spectroscopy and x-ray magnetic circular dichroism

The Ni 2p XAS of NiFe2O4 thin films recorded in TEY mode for positive (µ⁺) and negative (µ⁻) helicities is shown in Fig. 5(a). The Ni L3 and L2 edges, which represent electronic transitions from the 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 core levels, respectively, show distinctive absorption patterns in the spectra. There is a strong peak at about 852.7 eV and a lesser secondary peak at around 854.2 eV on the L3 edge (850–857 eV). The “E” and “F” peaks on the L2 edge have similar strengths. These spectral characteristics are similar across all samples and match previously reported Ni spectra in ferrite compounds51–53. The peak at ~ 852.7 eV at the L3 edge is caused by transitions of 2p3/2 electrons into empty 3d states, indicating Ni2+ in ferrites. Even though it is less bright, the secondary peak at 854.4 eV is clearly visible. The existence of peaks “A” and “B” at the L3 edge suggests that these electronic transitions produced different end states. On the L2 edge, transitions from the 2p1/2 level to the vacant 3d states are also present. The L3 edge’s location verifies that the thin films are in the Ni2+ oxidation state. Ni ion coordination configuration and spin state are reflected in the branching ratio between the L3 and L2 edges. Ni magnetic characteristics are recorded by the XMCD signal, which is the difference between the µ⁺ and µ⁻ spectra. In Fig. 5(c, d), the Ni and Fe L2,3-edge XMCD spectra show a systematic decrease in intensity from S1 to S4, indicating a progressive reduction of element-specific magnetic moments with increasing substrate temperature. The stronger XMCD signal in S1 reflects enhanced spin polarization and spin–orbit coupling, while the reduced intensity in S2–S4 suggests increased magnetic disorder. Changes in peak intensities during film growth, which are impacted by substrate temperature, show strain effects and internal structural deformities. A substantial reliance of the XAS spectra on the helicity of the circularly polarised x-rays is observed. The µ⁺ intensity is higher than the µ⁻ intensity near the L3 edge, suggesting a preferred absorption caused by spin-polarized unoccupied states. On the other hand, the dichroic response reverses at the L2 edge, where the µ⁻ signal predominates.

The XMCD spectrum (Δµ = µ⁺ − µ⁻) is shown in Fig. 5(e), identifying both spin and orbital contributions to magnetization and emphasising the element-specific magnetic response of Ni ions. The opposing dichroic signals at the L3 and L2 edges are in line with the expected behaviour of Ni2+ in octahedral coordination and reflect the spin-orbit interaction within the 2p core levels. The alignment of spin and orbital moments is revealed by the XMCD data, but the XAS spectra reveal details about the local electronic structure. Ni2+ has two unoccupied spin-down d orbitals in its initial state. A spin-conserving transition of a spin-down 2p electron into one of these states, leading to a triplet final state, is the source of Peak A in the XAS spectrum. Peak B results from a spin-up electron changing into a spin-down d state, which causes the 2p–3d exchange interaction to move the singlet final state to a higher energy. This spin-flip transition is made possible by a high 2p spin-orbit coupling, which violates the ΔS = 0 selection rule. This interpretation is supported by a two-step absorption model in the XMCD spectrum. First, spin-orbit coupling causes the 2p core level to split into j = 3/2 and j = 1/2 states, with spins aligned either parallel or antiparallel to orbital angular momentum. The direction of magnetization and the helicity of the source x-rays combine to select for the preferential emission of spin-polarized photoelectrons. A negative XMCD signal at the L3 edge and a positive signal at the L2 edge result from these electrons filling spin-down valence states in the second stage. Both spin-down and spin-up core-hole states are shifted to lower and higher energies via exchange interactions between the valence electrons and the core hole, respectively. At the L3 boundary, triplet and singlet final states are thus responsible for peaks A and B. This assignment is supported by the triplet state’s (spin-down core hole) negative dichroism and the singlet state’s (spin-up core hole) positive dichroism. There is less of a dichroic effect on the L2 edge, which is caused by less pure spin states. Finally, the XMCD spectrum provides a quantitative method for calculating the reduction factor associated with core-valence exchange interactions54.

The Ni L2,3-edge XAS and XMCD spectra of sample S1 exhibit well-resolved multiplet features labelled A–C at the L3 edge and D–F at the L2 edge, characteristic of Ni2+ ions in octahedral coordination. The dominant negative XMCD peak A at the L3 edge originates from transitions into exchange-split eg states of octahedrally coordinated Ni2+, confirming ferrimagnetic alignment of the Ni sublattice. The energy separation between features A and B (~ 0.171 eV) reflects exchange- and crystal-field-induced multiplet splitting associated with t2g–eg final states and Ni 3d–O 2p hybridization, while the broader shoulder feature C indicates additional spin-mixed final-state contributions. At the L2 edge, the positive dichroic features D–F arise from spin–orbit-split 2p1/2 core levels, with the energy separation between features D and E (~ 0.61 eV) governed by exchange interaction and final-state spin mixing. The reduced amplitude of the L2-edge XMCD compared to the L3 edge is attributed to stronger spin–orbit coupling in the 2p1/2 core hole. The systematic increase in XMCD intensity with magnetic field (0.1–2 T) indicates progressive alignment of Ni2+ magnetic moments, while the relative intensity ratios among features A–F remain largely unchanged, demonstrating that the local electronic structure and octahedral site symmetry of Ni are preserved under applied magnetic field.

In contrast to conventional magnetometry, XMCD provides an element-specific method for measuring the spin and orbital magnetic moments of Fe in NiFe2O4 thin films. At 300 K, XAS measurements were carried out at the Fe L2,3 edges (690–730 eV) using a 2 T magnetic field that was applied parallel to the x-ray beam and perpendicular to the film plane. The µ⁺ intensity is greater than µ⁻ in the L3 area (705–711 eV), which indicates a positive XMCD signal. The contribution of Fe 2p3/2 → 3d transitions to the net magnetic moment is reflected in this, indicating higher absorption when the helicity of the circularly polarized x-rays matches the direction of magnetization of the Fe atoms. In contrast, a negative XMCD signal is produced in the L2 region (717–723 eV), where µ⁻ exceeds µ⁺, indicating an inverse relationship. This reversal results in an opposite magnetic response compared to the L3 edge and is caused by the unique angular momentum properties of the 2p1/2 core electrons and their interaction with spin-orbit coupling. The difference between the µ⁺ and µ⁻ spectra is the source of the XMCD signal, which is extremely sensitive to the applied magnetic field. As illustrated in Fig. 5(b), the Fe L2,3 edges split into the L3 (2p3/2) and L2 (2p1/2) edges, separated by approximately 13 eV, as a result of the spin-orbit interaction in the 2p core shell. The impact of spin–orbit coupling is captured by this splitting. The XMCD sum rules were used to quantify the spin and orbital contributions to Fe total magnetic moment.

The magnetic moment of Fe is proportional to the number of empty states in its 3d shell, determining the availability of electronic transitions. The XAS spectra were normalized to a maximum intensity of 1, which covered the energy range from below the L3 edge to above the L2 edge, in order to eliminate the background slope brought on by charge absorption across the L3 and L2 edges. The absorption coefficients for the majority spin direction of Fe for circularly polarized photons with helicity µ⁺ and µ⁻ are displayed in Fig. 5(f). The difference between these two spectra under an applied magnetic field is the XMCD spectrum, which shows the magnetic dichroism signal. It is defined as Δµ = µ⁺ − µ⁻. Electronic transitions from the Fe 2p core levels to the unoccupied 3d states are responsible for the Fe L-edge absorption, which occurs at about 700–730 eV. The L-edge separates into L3 edge (2p3/2 → 3d) and L2 edge (2p1/2 → 3d) as a result of spin-orbit coupling in the 2p core shell. The spin and orbital magnetic moments connected to the Fe 3d electrons can be directly probed to XMCD high reactive to the magnetic characteristics of materials. The five electrons in Fe3+ high-spin 3d5 structure have parallel spins in the t2g and eg orbitals (S = 5/2). Although symmetry usually quenches orbital moments in an octahedral crystal field, spin-orbit coupling may continue to contribute slightly. The dichroic signal is caused by variations in x-ray absorption based on how the photon helicity aligns with the magnetization of the sample. This results in varying intensities for µ⁺ and µ⁻ at the L3 and L2 edges. Because of the increased degeneracy of the 2p3/2 state, the L3 peak is typically more intense than the L2 peak. When electron correlation is weak, the L3:L2 branching ratio is about 2:1. Intraatomic electron-electron interactions and the crystal field splitting of the Fe 3d orbitals are reflected in the multiplet structure seen in the L3 and L2 peaks, such as the shoulder close to the L3 edge. The isotropic XAS is represented by the sum spectrum (µ⁺ + µ⁻), which reflects the electronic structure of Fe3+ and is not affected by magnetic polarization. The total absorption intensity is obtained by integrating the isotropic spectrum, and it is proportional to the number of vacant 3d states. The magnetic polarization or ordering of Fe3+ ions under the applied magnetic field is confirmed by the XMCD signal. The magnetic behaviour of Fe3+ can be directly inferred from the XMCD magnitude. The spin and orbital magnetic moments can be quantitatively derived using the XMCD sum rules. In contrast to the orbital magnetic moment, which is proportional to the sum of the integrated XMCD intensities of the L3 and L2 edges, the spin magnetic moment is proportional to the difference between them. In ferrimagnetic oxides such as NiFe2O4, Fe3+ (3d5, high-spin) ions are generally expected to exhibit strong orbital quenching due to half-filled shells and cubic crystal fields. However, XMCD is sensitive to deviations from ideal symmetry, and our results indicate that local symmetry breaking induced by strain, oxygen vacancies as confirmed from XPS study (Fig. S4 in supplementary material), and Fe–O–Ni hybridization leads to partial unquenching of the orbital moment. This phenomenon is strained or defect-engineered ferrite thin films and is highly relevant for enhancing magnetocrystalline anisotropy and spin–orbit-driven functionalities in spintronic devices.

The orbital magnetic moment (morb) and spin magnetic moment (mspin) of Ni and Fe in NiFe2O4 thin films were quantitatively calculated using existing formulas by applying the XMCD sum rules to the integrated XMCD and total XAS spectra at the L2,3 edges14,17,35.

XMCD sum rules applied to integrated spectral parameters p, q, and r, generated from the XAS and XMCD spectra, were used to calculate the morb and mspin. The integral of the XMCD signal over the L3 edge is denoted by p, the integral of the summed XAS spectra for both helicities by r, and the overall XMCD integral over the L2 and L3 edges by q. Furthermore, as previously stated in the literature55,56, nh is the effective number of 3d holes, and mt represents the magnetic dipole term. For Fe3+, a nominal value of nh = 3.0 was used64, multiplied by a correction factor of 1.46 as reported in the literature56. For Ni2+, an effective value of nh = 1.755 was used, consistent with previous XMCD studies on NiFe2O4. The magnetic dipole term mt was neglected in the analysis, as it is generally insignificant for cubic spinel symmetry, even in the presence of moderate surface symmetry breaking64.

Both Ni and Fe exhibit a systematic reduction in spin and orbital magnetic moments from S1 to S4 at 2T, indicating a strong substrate-temperature-dependent weakening of magnetic ordering. In conventional ferrimagnetic spinel systems, the orbital magnetic moment is largely quenched due to strong crystal-field effects and high structural symmetry, resulting in magnetism dominated primarily by the spin contribution. However, our XMCD results reveal a clear enhancement of the orbital-to-spin moment ratio, particularly at the transition-metal sites, indicating a pronounced unquenching of the orbital magnetic moment. This unquenching arises from substrate-temperature-induced structural distortion, reduced local symmetry, and enhanced metal–oxygen hybridization, as evidenced by lattice strain (XRD) and modified spectral line shapes (XAS/XMCD). Such unquenched orbital moments play an important role in determining magnetocrystalline anisotropy, spin–orbit coupling strength, and magnetic stability, key parameters governing spintronic device performance, including anisotropy control, spin polarization, and interfacial spin transport. The experimentally observed orbital moment enhancement therefore provides a quantitative and physically grounded pathway for tuning spintronic functionality using substrate temperature as a control parameter. The observed unquenching of orbital magnetic moments, rare in ferrimagnetic oxides, constitutes a significant and non-trivial outcome of this work, directly linking the measured electronic and magnetic structure to potential spintronic applications.

This behaviour is consistent with the established concept of spinel ferrites, in which magnetic ordering is caused principally by antiferrimagnetic superexchange interactions between the A and B-site cations. The presence of a measurable, though smaller, morb indicates significant spin–orbit coupling, which is typical of transition metal oxides. While in classical ferrimagnetic systems such as γ-Fe2O3, the orbital contribution is nearly negligible morb/mspin ≈ 061, the observed morb/mspin ratio in NiFe2O4 is higher. This reflects stronger spin–orbit interactions and suggests possible deviations from ideal crystal symmetry or electronic configurations57–60.

The Ni site in NiFe2O4 shows that approximately all of the XAS signal comes from eg orbital transitions. This is because transitions are only permitted into the eg states in Ni2+ (3d⁸ configuration) due to the full occupancy of the t2g orbitals. In contrast, the XAS spectrum at the Fe sites indicates that the lowest energy peak primarily arises from transitions into the t2g ​ orbitals, with a partial contribution from the eg orbitals. Off-diagonal terms in the transition matrix elements influence the overall intensity. The main peak in the Fe XAS spectrum is the consequence of a mixture of t2g, eg, and off-diagonal contributions, with the t2g component dominating. The L2-edge region two-peak structure is caused by a large t2g feature, which contributes to both peaks. The second peak is further amplified by the eg states. The off-diagonal matrix elements suppress the first peak while amplifying the second, emphasising the complexity of the electronic transitions involved51,62,63.

To understand the origin of the strong orbital magnetic moment and large magnetocrystalline anisotropy energy (MAE) in NiFe2O4, the electronic and structural properties of Fe3+ ions positioned at the B sites inside the inverse spinel structure must be investigated. These characteristics are mainly ascribed to significant Fe 3d–O–2p hybridization and structural distortions at the octahedral sites.

In an ideal octahedral crystal field, Fe3+ ions (3d⁵, high-spin state) adopt a t2g3eg2​electronic configuration, resulting in a 6 ground state with quenched orbital angular momentum (L = 0) and a nearly spherical charge distribution. However, in NiFe2O4, local trigonal distortions of the FeO6 octahedra break the perfect octahedral symmetry, lifting the degeneracy of the t2g orbitals and introducing anisotropic orbital character. Such symmetry lowering enables partial unquenching of the orbital angular momentum (L ≠ 0), particularly when combined with strong Fe 3d–O 2p hybridization. As reported in earlier studies64, off-center displacement of Fe3+ ions within distorted octahedra and anisotropic charge transfer from O 2p to Fe 3d orbitals preserve orbital degeneracy and enhance the effective orbital moment40,64. This directionally dependent hybridization gives rise to anisotropic orbital polarization, which through spin–orbit coupling, contributes to large MAE. The spin–orbit interaction energy, , highlights the role of the induced orbital moment coupling to the Fe3+ spin moment (S = 5/2). The finite orbital contributions observed in XMCD are consistent with this distortion-driven unquenching mechanism reported in the literature40,64. Consequently, the combined effects of local crystal-field distortion, anisotropic Fe 3d–O 2p hybridization, and spin–orbit coupling provide a robust microscopic framework for understanding enhanced orbital moments and magnetocrystalline anisotropy in NiFe2O4 thin films, underscoring their relevance for magnetoelectronic and spintronic applications55,64–66.

Conclusions

The structural, morphological, electronic, and magnetic properties of NiFe2O4 thin films synthesized by spray pyrolysis were examined at various substrate temperatures (300 °C, 325 °C, 350 °C, and 400 °C). XRD investigation revealed the creation of a cubic inverse spinel structure, with crystallinity increasing considerably at lower substrate temperatures. Enhanced structural order was revealed at lower temperatures through quantitative structural characterization using a various of techniques, such as the Scherrer equation, Modified Scherrer Method, Williamson–Hall analysis, and Size–Strain Plot method, to determine crystallite size and lattice strain. HR-SEM revealed detailed surface morphology, demonstrating improved grain growth and uniformity in films deposited at lower temperatures, whereas EDS confirmed compositional uniformity and stoichiometric distribution of Ni, Fe, and oxygen. Magnetic properties were measured using VSM at room temperature and revealed ferrimagnetic behavior across all samples, with films deposited at lower temperatures exhibiting enhanced magneto-crystalline anisotropy and ferrimagnetic ordering due to improved crystallinity and grain size. These characteristics make the films potential candidates for spintronic applications. The local electronic structure and magnetic moments of Ni and Fe ions were investigated using XAS and XMCD. XAS showed that Ni is mostly in the divalent Ni2+ state, while Fe is in the trivalent Fe3+ state, with Fe3+ ions occupying both tetrahedral and octahedral sites and Ni2+ ions preferentially occupying octahedral B-sites in the spinel lattice. Surface-sensitive, total electron yield mode XMCD measurements revealed that structural defects cause large crystal field distortions in Ni2+ ions at octahedral sites, which results in spin canting driven by unquenched orbital moments and strong spin–orbit coupling. The orbital magnetic moments were measured as 0.112 ± 0.006 µB/ion for Ni and 0.2412 ± 0.0121 µB/ion for Fe, with increased anisotropy attributed to broken symmetry, structural defects, and hybridization effects. Strong Fe 3d–O–2p hybridization and antiparallel spin alignment between Ni and Fe ions were further confirmed by XMCD, especially at distorted octahedral oxygen sites, which increases orbital momentum and magnetic anisotropy. These results show the importance of cation distribution, microstructural quality, and local electronic interactions in determining the magnetic characteristics of NiFe2O4 thin films. These films’ promise for advanced spintronic and magnetoelectronic applications is made clear by their strong spin–orbit coupling, large orbital moment contributions, and optimized synthesis conditions.
