Tailoring the optoelectronic properties of MoOX nanoparticles: a novel microwave-assisted synthesis for near-infrared absorbing polyoxometallic clusters
Authors: Daniel Truchan, Adriana Hvizdošová Annušová, Guilhem Curé, Matej Mičušík, Vojtech Nádaždy, Mário Kotlár, Giulia C Fadda, Peter Nádaždy, Matej Jergel, Claire Wilhelm, Aurore Van de Walle, Peter Šiffalovič, Yoann Lalatonne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe emergence of novel catalytic, electrochemical, and biomedical applications of nanomaterials requires an understanding of their structural basis for stimuli-responsive performance. The chemistry of polyoxometallic nanomaterials, with a variety of interesting properties, remains poorly explored. In this study, microwave-assisted non-aqueous sol-gel synthesis was used for the first time to prepare nanoparticles based on polyoxomolybdates. Their optoelectronic properties, focusing on laser-triggered photothermal response, were investigated in detail depending on the synthesis temperature. Significant differences were observed between products prepared from the same precursor via a fast protocol by varying the synthesis temperature. Only low-temperature synthesis (≤ 90 °C) yielded near-infrared (NIR) photothermally active MoOX nanoparticles. The regular packing with large lattice defects of these clusters, along with a low reduction degree, allows water molecules to penetrate and interact with surface Mo = O bonds, forming intermediate electron states within the bandgap. These intermediate electron states are responsible for the NIR laser response suitable for photothermia. Additionally, the NIR response can be modulated in a controlled manner even after synthesis through electrochemical impedance spectroscopy. These findings have direct implications for MoOX photothermal therapy, targeted defect engineering of polyoxomolybdate structures, and their electrochemical and biological applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-025-04378-5.

Introduction

Modern challenges in sustainability and circular economy, ever-increasing energy demands, and health and well-being set new requirements on the performance of functional nanomaterials. Since their discovery, nanomaterials have proven to be very promising, high-performance and modular active components in cutting-edge devices. Particular interest is held by stimuli-responsive nanocomponents as they offer controllability of their response which is valued notably in biomedical applications. Before upscaling the devices from research to industrial level, the cost of active nanocomponents needs to be reduced without compromising their performance. Nanomaterials based on molybdenum oxides represent unique and affordable alternatives to precious metals in various applications, including but not limited to catalysis [1, 2], energy storage [3, 4], and photothermal therapy [5, 6].

One of the important emergent applications of nanomaterials is photothermal therapy (PTT). It is based on the assumption that a specific therapeutic effect can be achieved or improved by selective heating of the tissue after a localized injection of nanomaterials and their irradiation with light of a suitable wavelength. Specifically, when the PTT agent is activated by light, electrons transit from the ground state to the excited state. The subsequent relaxation from the excited state is realized by non-radiative decay that manifests macroscopically as heat. Therefore, using purely physical mechanisms, conversion of light energy into heat allows to alleviate the side effects of conventional therapy, with cancer being the most common target. The PTT as a cancer treatment induces the cancer cell death by the heat generated in tumor tissue (hyperthermia) usually exposed to near-infrared light [7]. Near-infrared (NIR) light is employed as both water and hemoglobin have their lowest absorption coefficient in the NIR-I region between 650 and 900 nm [8] and the NIR-II region between 1000 and 1350 nm [9] (hence the term “biological windows”). This is reflected in high penetration of such light into the tissue, ranging from 400 μm to cm, or even beyond [10]. Additionally, photothermal treatment can induce an anti-tumor immunological response of the body thanks to the release of tumor-antigens, which can prevent or even eradicate metastases and reduce tumor recurrence [11]. The NIR-absorbers (or heating-enhancing agents) can be used to improve heat conversion and thus enhance treatment specificity. This ensures high precision of the thermal ablation/hyperthermia, restricted only to the tumor location where both light and the agent are present.

The specific nature of the complexes of Mo and O atoms explains certain discrepancies in their terminology. Whereas in chemistry, it is common to call them polyoxomolybdates (particularly isopolyoxomolybdates) [12–15], in nanoscience and technology they are often denoted as MoOX [5, 16, 17] due to their non-stoichiometric nature. Throughout this study, the formula MoOX will be used to denote different nanostructured Mo-O complexes as a synonym for polyoxometallic nanoparticle. In order to fully benefit from molybdenum oxide nanomaterials in the multitude of applications, rigorous knowledge of the structural patterns behind optoelectronic performance and its changes is crucial for any preparation strategy. Multiple preparation procedures documented in the literature have been employed to prepare nanometer-scale molybdenum oxides. The first preparation procedures included the processes based on the sonication of various precursors. MoO5 nanostructures were initially prepared sonochemically from molybdenum hexacarbonyl in decalin [18]. Recently, we have explored a similar approach to the formation of blue MoOX nanoparticles from microcrystalline MoS2 [19, 20] and MoO2 [6] in an ethanol-water solution. Liquid-phase exfoliation does not offer much control over the process. However, it only requires available microcrystalline precursors and an appropriate solvent with no heating, minimizing side reactions and organic contamination. Nevertheless, a prominent position in the synthesis of MoO3-based nanomaterials is currently held by hydrothermal synthesis, where the temperature serves as a crucial controlling parameter. It was used for the preparation of orthorhombic (α-MoO3) and hexagonal (h-MoO3) crystal structures for electrochemical performance studies [21], MoO3 nanobelts subjected to lithiation employed in batteries [22] or template-assisted synthesis of crystalline TiO2-MoO3 composite materials for electrochemical applications [23]. A similar option to hydrothermal synthesis is solvothermal synthesis. It was explored in isopropyl alcohol to produce MoO3 nanoparticles for catalysis with egg albumin as a matrix [24]. Michailovski et al. employed this method to study the synthesis of molybdenum oxide nanomaterials, using fibrous MoO3 as a model to understand MoO3 structure formation [25]. The present work builds on the principles of non-aqueous sol-gel solvothermal synthesis with particular attention to temperature-driven changes in the material’s optoelectronic properties.

The pioneering work of Niederberger and Pinna, proposing benzyl alcohol as both a reducing and stabilizing agent in the solvothermal synthesis of nanomaterials [26, 27], outlined fine-tuning of particle size and low amount of organic impurities, both being achieved in the same process. Previously, various organic ligands and stabilizing agents in precise ratios were needed to be present during the process to achieve control over the final product, contributing to organic contamination and waste. Nanoparticle agglomeration was often still present, and the need for ultrasonic treatment [28], hydrothermal processing [29] or calcination [30] in order to achieve crystallization complicated the process, compromising its robustness. In addition to the standard reaction vessel heating in the original study, the versatility of the benzyl-alcohol route was explored by heating in an autoclave [31] as well as in an oil bath [32] providing highly reproducible results. When the nonaqueous solvothermal process was moved to a microwave reactor vessel [33], a very efficient yet simple ‘synthetic tandem’, namely microwaves—benzyl alcohol, was born. The microwave-assisted solvothermal synthesis in benzyl alcohol has since excelled in achieving highly controlled preparation of different nanostructures, with the advantage of precise and fast control over the reaction temperature and the heat energy input compared to conventional methods [34]. The group of as-prepared d-metal oxides encompasses oxide nanostructures of Ti, Mn, Zn [33], Fe [35], and more recently, even heavy elements like Hf [36]. Concerning the metal oxides from the group 6 of the periodic table, the approach was used in the case of Tungsten (W) [37], as well as Cr (although in ethylene glycol) [38]. However, the work presented here is the first to exploit the versatility and reproducibility of the synthetic tandem in the preparation of nanomaterials based on molybdenum oxide.

Concerning the mechanisms driving the microwave synthesis, in addition to very efficient microwave heating, recent works have reaffirmed only the role of microwave-specific thermal effects, which cannot be reproduced by conventional heating systems, while the role of existing microwave non-thermal effects in synthesis has not been confirmed [39, 40]. Microwave-specific thermal effects include microwave superheating [41], selective heating of a strongly-absorbing constituent in the reaction mixture [42], or the formation of microscopic hotspots on micro- or nanoscale solid particles [43]. Other authors agree on the existence of the effects but report superheating mitigation by stirring [44]. Generation of microscopic hotspots is not the case for very low temperatures, but for heating already above 300 K, the effect should be accounted for [45].

As reported, for the microwave synthesis of nanomaterials, exclusively thermal effects are fundamental to their nucleation and growth [46]. Upon microwave heating, ionic or neutral MoVIO4 complexes are expected to be released from the acetylacetonate organic framework. Similarly to tungsten [47], free forms of MoO3(OH) and MoO2(OH)2 can afterwards aggregate and crosslink to form nanoparticles. Interestingly, even the first nucleation steps including dimerization can result in coordination asymmetry providing certain Mo atoms with uncompensated electrons [47]. This means that oxygen vacancies can be created during the nanoparticle formation complementing the post-synthesis introduction as achieved by heating in case of MoO3 nanoblades [48]. Low synthesis temperatures were proven to be able to provide metastable phases during synthesis with further influence on the nanoparticles formation [49]. For microwave assisted synthesis, nanoparticle size was reported as highly dependent on the heating time, with other parameters playing a substantial role in the morphology and crystallinity [33]. Organic molecules present in the reaction mixture can have substantial role in stabilizing metastable nanoparticle intermediates [50]. In our work, temperature will be the primary parameter of synthesis.

The current work presents a pilot study of the use of non-aqueous sol-gel microwave-assisted synthesis to fabricate the molybdenum oxide nanoparticles. Efforts are focused on minimizing the temperature of microwave-assisted synthesis as the nanostructured molybdenum oxide represents a very sensitive system. A variety of products is prepared in a range of 140 °C with a maximum temperature of the synthesis not exceeding 210 °C. Contrary to similar physical characteristics observed for products throughout the temperature range, deeper analysis by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Raman scattering unveiled structural transformations responsible for the changes of optoelectronic properties. Optoelectronic properties exhibit remarkable sensitivity to temperature during the microwave-assisted synthesis in reducing solvent. At the low temperature range (70–90 °C), produced molybdenum oxide nanoparticles exhibited photothermal properties, while at moderate (120–160 °C) and high (190–210 °C) temperatures, pivotal changes in electronic structure observed directly by the energy-resolved electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (ER-EIS) hindered the heating-enhancing effect. Subsequently, the heating performance of NIR-absorbing MoOX nanoparticles was assessed. On this basis, a comprehensive overview of the properties of the prepared molybdenum oxide nanoparticles and their potential use in advanced applications with the focus on photothermal therapy is provided.

Experimental

The reagents were stocked in a glovebox (MB-Easylab V2.0 Braun) under a controlled atmosphere (O2 < 0.5 ppm, H2O < 0.5 ppm). Microwave-assisted synthesis was performed by Monowave 300 from Anton Paar (Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Austria).

The precursor solution was prepared within the glove box, 400 mg of Molybdenyl acetylacetonate (Sigma Aldrich, Germany) was dispersed in 10 mL of benzyl alcohol (anhydrous 99.8%, Sigma Aldrich) in a 30 mL microwave vial, which was sealed and retrieved from the glove box within Ar (oxygen and moisture below 0.5 ppm). Original reaction vial G30 (Anton Paar, Austria) made of borosilicate glass was used. The dispersion of the precursor powder was facilitated by sonication, resulting in a dark yellow/orange homogeneous solution. Microwave-assisted synthesis (Monowave 400, Anton Paar, Austria) follows a simple protocol: heating the solution as fast as possible (this step typically less than 2 min) to desired temperature (70–210 °C) controlled by IR camera probe, holding the temperature for desired time (typically 5 min) and cooling, all steps under constant stirring. Increasing the time of the synthesis from 5 min to a few tens of minutes did not significantly influence the optical response, although at high temperatures and over 1 h-long synthesis times, agglomeration of nanoparticles to 10 nm complexes was observed (Fig. S2A, B). The resulting solution was transferred to 20 mL of dichloromethane (synthesis grade, Sigma Aldrich, Germany) in a 50 mL tube, and 15 mL of water (Millipore, 18 MΩ) were added to the mixture and shaken vigorously on a shaker for 1 h to achieve the extraction of nanoparticles to the water phase. After 1 h, the solution was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to get two separate phases. The upper water phase with nanoparticles was carefully collected with a pipette and transferred to a separate flask. Afterward, more water was added to the organic phase with residual water to reach the final volume of around 45 mL. The process is repeated at least two times to maximize the extraction of nanoparticles from the organic phase. Residual dichloromethane is evaporated from the water solution of nanoparticles overnight at room temperature in a laminar flow cabinet.

The morphology and crystalline structure of the MoOX nanoparticles were characterized using a JEOL JEM ARM200CF high-resolution transmission electron microscope (TEM) equipped with an image corrector operated at 200 kV. The nanoparticles from the solution were dispersed on a copper mesh grid covered with an amorphous carbon layer. The images were recorded with a Gatan UltraScan CCD camera (2k × 2k) and a Gatan Orius Model 830 SC200 CCD camera (2k × 2k).

Small Angle X-ray Scattering (SAXS) experiments were performed at Laboratoire Léon Brillouin, CEA Saclay, on a XEUSS 2.0 device (Xenocs, France), with a Cu-Kα source generating an X-ray beam at a wavelength of 1.54 Å and a Pilatus3 detector (Dectris, Switzerland). Samples were loaded in quartz capillaries of 1.5 mm optical path (WJM-Glas/Müller, Germany). The experiments were realized at two sample-to-detector distances: at 1190 mm, with a collimated beam size of 0.8 mm × 0.8 mm and 2488 mm with collimation grids of 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm, to access a scattering wave vector q-range of 0.003 Å−1 to 0.62 Å−1, where q = 4π sin(θ)/λ is the scattering vector and 2θ is the scattering angle. Solvent scattering was subtracted using a buffer-only measurement. Scattering curves were corrected for background and instrumental effects prior to analysis. All measurements were performed at room temperature. Fitting was performed using SasView 6.

XPS signals were recorded using a Thermo Scientific Nexsa G2 Surface Analysis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) equipped with a micro-focused, monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source (1486.68 eV). An X-ray beam of 400 mm size was used. The spectra were acquired in the constant analyzer energy mode with a pass energy of 200 eV for the survey. Narrow regions were collected using the pass energy of 50 eV. Charge compensation was achieved with the system dual beam flood gun. The Thermo Scientific Avantage software, version 6.9.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), was used for digital acquisition and data processing [51]. Spectral calibration was determined by using the automated calibration routine and the internal Au, Ag and Cu standards supplied with the K-Alpha system. The surface compositions (in atomic %) were determined by considering the integrated peak areas of the detected atoms and the respective sensitivity factors. The absorbance measurements were realized by a SolidSpec-3700 UV–vis–NIR spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan), using an integrating sphere. Deionized water was used for the baseline correction prior to the measurements and also as a reference sample during the measurements. As-prepared water solutions of MoOX were properly diluted in ultrapure water and measured by UV-vis spectroscopy, or alternatively, lyophilized powders stored at 4 °C were freshly diluted right before the measurement. In the case of UV-vis-NIR spectroscopy of MoOX films deposited at indium-tin oxide (ITO), clean ITO was used as a reference sample. For MoOX film preparation, lyophilized powders were diluted in water (50 µL at 3 mg/mL) and spincoated on ITO (50 rpm for 30 s) after 20 min of UV-lamp activation before the UV-vis-NIR measurement. Next, after the sample measurement by Energy-resolved electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (ER-EIS), absorbance changes induced on MoOX film by the method were assessed again by the UV-vis-NIR spectroscopy.

Energy-resolved electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (ER-EIS) was used to determine the density of states (DOS). This method provides the electronic structure of an organic solid in contact with an electrolyte via a redox reaction [52] and has also been employed to quantify the DOS in nanocrystal thin films [53]. Measuring the charge transfer resistance of a semiconductor/electrolyte interface at a frequency where the redox reaction determines the real component of the impedance allows obtaining a direct information about the DOS at the energy given by the electrochemical potential of the electrolyte. For sample preparation, lyophilized powders were diluted in water (50 µL at 3 mg/mL) and spincoated on ITO (50 rpm for 30 s) after 20 min of UV-lamp activation. Micro electrochemical cells with a volume of 200 µL were fabricated by gluing the plastic cone on the layers. Impedance was measured with Solartron Analytical (model 1260) and potentiostat in a 3-electrode configuration with Ag/AgCl reference and Pt auxiliary wire electrodes immersed in the electrolyte, the MoOX layer/ITO stack serving as the working electrode. The electrolyte solution used was a 0.1 M solution of tetrabutylammonium hexafluorophosphate (TBAPF6) in acetonitrile. The experiments were performed in a glove box with a protective Ar atmosphere (oxygen and moisture below 20 ppm and 2 ppm, respectively), which allowed an energy window of ± 3 eV with respect to the Ag/AgCl reference electrode. To obtain the entire spectrum, the measurement was performed in two steps, each time in a new electrochemical cell. One measurement with a positive bias sweep relative to the Ag/AgCl reference electrode is used to obtain the branch for the hole transport states (positive scan) and one with a negative bias relative to the Ag/AgCl reference electrode for electron transport states (negative scan).

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded on a Tensor 27 spectrophotometer (Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany) at a resolution of 1 cm−1, and the data treatment was performed with the Opus program. Lyophilized MoOX powders were measured in transmission mode after grinding in a mortar with a pestle and properly mixing with KBr microcrystalline powder.

The Raman spectra of lyophilized MoOX powders were acquired through a multimode optical fiber of 50 μm diameter, also acting as a pinhole, providing confocality to a spectrometer (UHTS 300, WITec, Ulm, Germany) equipped with a 600 gr/mm grating (blazed at 500 nm) and coupled to an EMCCD camera (Newton DU970N-BV-353, Andor, Belfast, U.K.). A Zeiss EC Epiplan-Neofluar Dic 50x / 0.8 objective was used. A low power of 0.2 mW of 532 nm laser was used to prevent sample oxidation to MoO3, which was observed for the powers greater than 0.5 mW. The integration times ranged from 12 to 180 s with 3 to 5 accumulations depending on the Raman signal intensity of the sample.

The grazing-incidence X-ray diffraction (GIXRD) was measured on a powder diffractometer D8 Discover SSS (Bruker AXS, Karlsruhe, Germany) with a quasi-parallel X-ray beam of 0.5 mrad divergence. A dried drop of MoOx solution or lyophilized MoOx powder on a silicon substrate was measured at an incident angle of 1 degree to suppress the signal from the substrate. A high-power 18 kW X-ray generator with rotating Cu anode allowed fast step scans with CuKα1 radiation delivered by Goebel mirror, typically at 40 kV/300 mA exposure.

The Mo content in the final water solution was determined by ICP-AES (Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy - Spectrogreen, SPECTRO). Up to 24 µL of the sample were redispersed in 867 µL of concentrated HNO3 and left at room temperature for 48 h to allow its full dissolution. The volume was then filled up to 10 mL with ultrapure water before measuring the total amount of Mo atoms.

For photothermal measurements, MoOX nanoparticles (initial stock at 10 mg/mL) were diluted to 0.1 mg/mL in MilliQ water. Photothermia was performed on 50 µL. Solution was placed in 1.5 mL Eppendorf Safe-Lock tubes (Eppendorf, 0030120086). The tube was kept at room temperature for a few minutes before exposure to reach the initial temperature. An 808 nm laser (Laser Components S.A.S France) was placed above the tube at a distance of 3 cm from the top of the tube. In this study, each solution was irradiated with the laser for 2 min at 0.5 W before allowing the solution to cool down for 5 min. Temperature evolution was recorded with an infrared thermal camera (FLIR A6751) at the frequency of 2.0 Hz. Analysis was then performed using the FLIR ResearchIR software and MATLAB (version R2024a, The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Preparation of MoOX nanoparticles

Acetyl-acetonate organic precursor was chosen as an available source of molybdenum(VI)-oxygen complex. It has been used previously as an organic-soluble molybdenum(VI) source in various experimental settings, ranging from being a simple source of molybdenum for clock reaction [54] up to the stabilizer of solar cells [55] and more conventionally for the preparation of a catalytic complex [56]. We have opted for this organic precursor because of its availability and well-established use in microwave-assisted synthesis of metal nanoparticles.

The synthesis scheme is depicted in Fig. 1. The synthesis of MoOX nanoparticles was carried out under inert conditions using a microwave-assisted solvothermal approach. A molybdenum-based precursor was dispersed in a high-boiling organic solvent to form a homogeneous solution, which was then subjected to rapid heating for controlled reaction times. The microwave process enabled precise temperature control, allowing for the fine-tuning of nucleation and growth conditions. Another monitored parameter—pressure, did not interfere with the preparation procedure as no increase was observed over the preparation temperature range. While short reaction times were sufficient to obtain well-dispersed nanoparticles, extended durations at elevated temperatures led to partial agglomeration (Figs. S1E and S2). Following synthesis, a phase transfer procedure was employed to extract the nanoparticles into the aqueous phase, ensuring removal of the organic medium. For more details, see the Experimental section. This method provides stable, water-dispersible MoOX nanoparticles with reproducible optical and structural properties.

Synthesis scheme of the microwave-assisted synthesis of MoOX nanoparticles. Starting material is Molybdenyl acetylacetonate (MoVIO2(Acac)2), which is heated in microwave for 5 min for the desired temperature from the 70 to 210 °C range. Afterwards, benzyl alcohol is retained in the dichloromethane phase, whereas MoOX nanoparticles are extracted to the water phase

Extraction efficiency can be qualitatively assessed by the color of the organic phase. For the low temperatures (70 °C and 90 °C), the organic phase becomes transparent/yellowish after 2 or 3 repetitions. For the other temperatures, extraction to the water phase is slower, necessitating extension of the extraction time as well as the number of repetitions, hinting at lower hydrophilicity of the products. For the highest tested temperatures (190° C and 210° C), it can take up to several days to have a satisfactory quantity of nanoparticles extracted to the water phase, while the organic phase still retains its dark color even after extensive repetitions. Quantitatively, the exact molybdenum content determined by ICP-AES in the final solution (25 mL) was 89%, 32% and 5% of the precursor total Mo content for 90 °C, 120 °C and 190 °C samples, respectively. Optimizing the extraction procedure to improve the yield, especially for higher temperatures, was not the objective of this study and remains the subject for future microwave-assisted protocol optimization study.

Effect of the synthesis temperature on physicochemical properties and electronic structure of the prepared MoOX nanoparticles

The first sign suggesting a difference between the products prepared at different temperatures was the color of the prepared nanoparticles after their extraction into water. A color change took place right after the transition from benzyl alcohol-dichloromethane mixture into water phase. For the 90 °C temperature, a remarkable shift from dark orange to deep blue was observed in a few minutes of agitation, whereas for higher temperatures, such a marked change did not occur (Fig. 2A). In the following paragraphs, the effects of this behavior and the changing properties of the nanoparticles prepared at different synthesis temperatures will be described.

Nanoparticle size distributions determined by TEM for different temperatures do not show significant differences (B). A similar size of 2.5 nm can be observed equally at 90 °C (2.4 ± 0.5 nm), 120 °C (2.6 ± 0.7 nm), and 210 °C (2.4 ± 0.7 nm), with minor mean size increase and distribution broadening with temperature. Despite the common understanding that color shifts often indicate variations in nanoparticle size or shape, this is not the case according to our findings. TEM size distributions with a typical TEM image are shown in Fig. 2B. The TEM contrast was not high (Fig. 2C), hinting at nanoparticles with a thin profile (See also Fig. S1). Still, individual grains can be distinguished by the unilateral orientation of the atomic planes. The morphology of the prepared nanoparticles is not clearly discernible, except for their elliptic shape, although sometimes larger areas of higher electron density can be observed (Fig. S1). Very similar sizes (Fig. 2B) along the elliptical shapes (Figs. 2C, S1A–E) are present in the nanoparticles synthesized throughout the range of temperatures. The size (which was determined as the longest distance between edges of an ellipsoidal nanoparticle) for all temperatures was 2.48 ± 0.62 nm (N = 122), ranging from 1.53 to 4.71 nm. Increasing the temperature of synthesis does not induce significant change in the size distribution – for 90 °C synthesis, the mean size was 2.4 ± 0.5 nm (n = 41) compared to 2.6 ± 0.7 nm (n = 41) for 120 °C synthesis and 2.4 ± 0.7 nm (n = 40) for 210 °C synthesis (Fig. 2B). SAXS data acquired for nanoparticles synthesized at 90 °C confirm nanoparticle size determined by TEM (Fig. 2C). They were well fitted with an ellipsoid form factor, with a polar radius rp=12.55 ± 0.24 Å and an equatorial radius re=23.23 ± 0.12 Å. Based on the similarity of the products prepared at different temperatures, we can conclude that the size is not the factor determining optoelectronic properties and considerably similar nanoparticles, both in term of size and shape, were prepared for the whole range of temperatures.

On the other hand, XPS of the products revealed distinct changes in the oxidation states of molybdenum depending on the temperature of the microwave-assisted synthesis. Whereas the predominant Mo-oxidation state is Mo6+ (Mo3d5/2 at 233.5 eV) [51] in the samples prepared at 70 °C and 90 °C, its proportion rapidly decreases for 120 °C when, the Mo6+ to Mo5+ intensity ratio drops from 2.5 to around 0.3 (Fig. 3A).

As a result, the Mo5+ oxidation state (Mo3d5/2 at 232.1 eV) [51] dominates for 120 °C synthesis as well as in the samples prepared at higher temperatures. Three different points were measured on each sample and as can be seen from Fig. 3B, there were almost no deviations in the shape of the Mo3d signal (Fig. 3C), indicating the homogeneity of chemical changes at a certain temperature. Interestingly, the Mo4+ oxidation state (Mo3d5/2 at 229.8 eV) [51] is not present in any of the samples apart from MoO2 reference (Fig. 3B). Similar Mo6+ signal prevalence over Mo5+ was observed for MoOX nanoparticles prepared by the liquid phase exfoliation [6]. The observed flipping of this ratio corresponds to enhanced action of the reducing solvent during the short synthesis, favoring its oxidation, and thus formation of the Mo–O clusters with a higher degree of reduction. Interestingly, the change is abrupt, suggesting a remarkable change in the electronic structure of the molybdenum atoms constituting the nanoparticles with an impact on their properties. The change can be clearly seen on the XPS spectra corresponding to the two samples synthesized at 90 °C and 120 °C. Mo3d signal with significantly split spin-orbit components (Δ3.15 eV) is displayed in Fig. 3C. Two spin-orbit doublets for 90 °C sample are replaced by a single doublet for 120 °C synthesis. The presence of several oxidation states of metal atoms in products after the microwave-assisted synthesis is well-known [57–59]. Additionally, there is a continuous temperature-driven process present, which is observable by XPS – incorporation of carbon into the Mo-O array. A higher overall presence of the carbon moieties in the sample is evident from the ratio of C-related signal to Mo-related signal, which increases more than twofold from 90 °C to 210 °C (Fig. S3). It is particularly the C-sp2 signal at 284.4 eV, which is significantly enhanced for high-temperature products, confirming increased reduction on the carbon atoms (from an average of 5.83 atomic % for 70 °C synthesis to an average of 12.8 atomic % for high temperatures). In addition, the C-C signal at 285.0 eV increases even more dramatically from 8.6 atomic % for 70 °C synthesis to 22.4 atomic % for 210 °C synthesis. It indicates greater incorporation of the organic part into the Mo–O clusters driven especially by high temperatures of 190 °C and 210 °C. For XPS survey spectra and apparent surface chemical composition, see Fig. S4 and Tables S1–S9.

Figure 4 presents Tauc plots derived from the temperature-dependent absorption spectra of the nanoparticles (see Fig. S5), used to estimate the optical bandgap. To facilitate comparison across species with differing molar absorption coefficients, all spectra were normalized such that the value of absorption coefficient α at 5 eV was set to 10 cm−1 (αN). In our case, precise determination of the bandgap is prevented by an elevated sub-bandgap intensity compromising the linear fit, but a typical value can be estimated at around 4.5 eV. Nevertheless, the Tauc plot reveals significantly lower edge energies of the samples synthesized at moderate temperatures (120, 130 and 160 °C) compared to both low- and high-temperature products. Such an increased intensity at lower energy indicates the creation of a more tightly connected array of molybdenum atoms [60] at elevated temperature, contrary to more isolated atomic centers present in the low-temperature samples, highlighting important lattice differences. Interestingly, when the temperature is further increased from 160 °C to 190 °C, and even tighter Mo-atom packing would lead to a more pronounced redshift [61], the low edge energy in the Tauc plot completely disappears. As evidenced by XPS showing increasing content of carbon at the expense of the Molybdenum-related signal (Fig. S3), such a high temperature leads to an increasing incorporation of the organic moieties into the nanoparticle structure. Obviously, intercalation of more organic molecules increases the average distance between neighboring Mo atoms, leading to the erased low-energy edge, however, without an influence on the nanoparticle size (See Fig. 2B).

The samples synthesized in the medium temperature range (120–160 °C) exhibit an intensity bump at the energies associated with a tighter packing of the Mo atoms in the nanostructure propelled by the elevated temperature. Interestingly, high-temperatures samples (190 and 210 °C) do not follow this trend due to an increasing share of organic parts in the structure (Fig. S3) and the elevated intensity at lower energy is absent

Figure 5 shows the density of states (DOS) of the layers deposited from an aqueous solution of the MoOX nanoparticles prepared at 90 °C and at 120 °C, which were determined by the ER-EIS technique. For the low-temperature sample (90 °C), distinct defect states in the bandgap are present. Higher degree of reduction achieved at moderate temperatures (120 °C) during microwave-assisted synthesis (Fig. 3) results in the significant reduction of these bandgap states. It was observed that the presence of oxygen vacancies, especially at the bridging oxygens in the Mo-O lattice, correlates with the defect states [62, 63] both for non-stoichiometric reduced MoO3 and a set of stoichiometric intermediate oxides. It is noteworthy that both Mo and O atoms provide a possible valence band for the intermediate electron states responsible for the optoelectronic performance. These intermediate states are responsible for specific optical properties of the low-temperature sample as they allow absorption of photons of sub-bandgap energies with almost continuous distribution. Furthermore, altered DOS makes the relaxation mechanisms of the excited state different for the 90 °C and 120 °C samples. Namely, two times higher photoluminescence intensity is observed for the sample prepared at 120 °C compared to the lower temperature (Fig. S6). The concurring relation between photoluminiscence intensity and absorption due to localized surface plasmon resonance is known for MoOX nanomaterials [64]. Non-radiative relaxation is favored in the low-temperature synthesized samples, enabling their use in photothermal treatment.

The GIXRD patterns of the dropcasted microwave products presented in Fig. 6 show broad peaks that progressively disappear by increasing the synthesis temperature. The broad peaks are due to the absence of the long-range structural order, as documented by Fig. 1B and C, where only short-range structural order, namely regions of < 3 nm size (clusters), can be seen. A similar amorphous character of the spectra showing GIXRD patterns without discrete crystalline peaks was reported for thermally deposited MoOX layers [65].

Additionally, FTIR analysis was performed and can be accessed in Supplementary Information (Fig. S7 and S8). It shows that increasing temperature during microwave-assisted synthesis promotes the organization of Mo and O atoms from octahedral to more rigid tetrahedral structural subunits joined together into a sheet structure (Fig. S7). Bands related to Mo-OH and MoO3 vibrations were identified prominently in low-temperature synthesized samples. Additionally, Mo = O stretching position shifts with increasing temperature to lower sub-1000 cm−1 and temperature-dependent shifts in various Mo-O bond positions are present as well. Apart from these, no other systemic and gradual changes were distinguished throughout the spectra at the set of temperatures.

A comprehensive review by Mestl and Srinivasan [66] was used as a guide for evaluating the Raman spectra. Figure 7 presents the Raman spectra of MoOX prepared at different temperatures by the microwave-assisted synthesis in benzyl alcohol. Raman spectroscopy confirms gradual changes in their structure with increasing temperature. All samples exhibit some peaks that could be associated either with native MoO3 or MoO2 spectra, which are used as references. Increasing temperature during the synthesis results in broadening of the peaks. This temperature-driven broadening of the inelastic scattering peaks shows a higher energy dispersion of chemical bonds, which can be attributed to MoOX structure amorphization, confirming the GIXRD results (Fig. 6). With increasing temperature, two broad bands emerge at 275 rel. cm−1 (with a broad shoulder at 355 rel. cm−1) and 855 rel. cm−1 (including peak/shoulder at 982 rel. cm−1) (Fig. 7A). In addition, some carbon-related signals appear in the 1100–1700 rel. cm−1 spectral range (Fig. S9B). With increasing temperature of the microwave-assisted synthesis, a shift of Raman peak from around 960 rel. cm−1 for the synthesis carried on at 70 and 90 °C to around 989 rel. cm−1 for the samples synthesized at moderate and high temperatures (120, 140, 160, 190 and 210 °C) is observed (Fig. 7B). The shift is discontinuous rather than gradual, taking place between 90 and 120 °C. As was also reported, H2O molecules interaction with Mo = O bonds resulted in downshifted positions of the Raman peak [67]. Based on these findings we can expect that increased desorption of water molecules at high temperature prevents the band shift to lower wavenumbers. Moreover, the process is reversible and repeatable [67]. Recently, a more detailed explanation of this shift was proposed as the conversion of terminal monoxo-molybdenum species to dioxo-molybdenum in the presence of water [68], leading to the wavenumber shift.

However, in our microwave-synthesized MoOX products, other factors must also be taken into account and the observed shift of Mo = O vibration from 960 to 989 rel. cm−1 results from a more complex process. Although the synthesis takes place at different temperatures and in reducing solvent, where the presence of water is ruled out, all products are extracted into the water phase in the subsequent steps of the preparation procedure. For temperatures higher than 120 °C, the resulting nanostructures do not allow the penetration of water molecules into the structure of the oxometallic cluster, and the Mo = O bond vibration is not affected. However, in the case of the clusters prepared at 70 °C and 90 °C, water molecules penetrate the cluster and the peak is shifted. Similar Mo = O peak positions identified in Mo7O246− were reported at around 949 rel. cm−1 and in Mo8O266− at around 960 rel. cm−1 [69] at ambient conditions.

The low-temperature prepared products have the maximum of the broad peak at 790 rel. cm−1 which corresponds to Mo-O-Mo asymmetric vibrations in Mo7O246– [70], with a collateral 770 rel. cm−1 band which can be attributed to a similar yet lower energy stretch. Presumably, lower wavenumber position reflects longer bonds between two adjacent Mo atoms. A remarkable gradual shift in the position of the Mo-O-Mo vibration to higher wavenumbers can be seen in the Raman spectra with increasing temperature (Fig. 7B). The maximum of the peak corresponding to Mo-O-Mo stretch for medium and high temperatures is present at 820 rel. cm−1 similarly to other polyoxomolybdate complexes [71, 72], it is located at around 813 rel. cm−1 in the MoO3 reference. The presence of these peaks hints at a larger extent of Mo–O–Mo crosslinking [72] achieved at increased temperatures. We can assume that this crosslinking accompanies a more compact packing of Mo atoms.

From the structural point of view, the 880 rel. cm−1 vibration present at moderate and high temperatures indicates tetrahedrally coordinated MoO4− 2 with a slightly distorted structure [73, 74] as suggested by FTIR analysis (in the case of ideal symmetry, its position is at 868 rel. cm−1) [75]. On the other hand, the peaks in the interval from 930 to 970 rel. cm−1 present at 70 and 90 °C samples can be attributed to octahedral structure [76, 77] along with the peaks at 370 and 220 rel. cm−1. However, a similar octahedra-specific peak at 860–870 rel. cm−1 is not present. Furthermore, The MoOX prepared at low temperatures exhibits decreased distortion in lateral interactions compared to the high-temperature synthesis products—an effect that was observed on a planar substrate when surface molybdenum concentration (coverage) decreased [78], linking decreased distortion to the structure of more isolated Mo centers in the low-temperature samples. Similar longer Mo-O-Mo bonds were also detected in planar molybdenum oxide complexes on ZrO2/SiO2 support heated to 632 K [79] and after a catalytic oxidation of methanol MoOX supported on Al2O3 and ZrO2 support at 503 K [80].

For the low-temperature products (70 and 90 ° C), the bending δMo–O–Mo (δMo2O) vibration is manifested by a broad peak centered at 233 rel. cm−1, encompassing the whole set of bands corresponding to the vibration, namely that at 191 rel. cm−1 and mainly those at 198, 218, 228 rel. cm−1 (Fig. 7B). A wide continuous spectrum suggests an even distribution of the bending energies. This band around 220 rel. cm−1 is indicative of distorted octahedral polymeric units and their deformation vibration [81]. Interestingly, it was observed that a higher degree of reduction leads to its disappearance [82]. Indeed, with increasing temperature of the microwave synthesis, a higher degree of reduction is reached, which is confirmed by XPS (Fig. 3). As temperature increases, the 246 rel. cm−1 band persists as a shoulder, even in the samples synthesized at higher temperatures. However, the 275 rel. cm−1 peak becomes the dominant feature in this spectral region. It is worth noting that Raman peaks below 200 rel. cm−1 are shifted toward low frequencies compared to the commercial MoO3 microcrystalline powder. It was explained by the presence of suboxides (MoO3−x), which indicates that at this stage, the reduction process interfered with the structure. This effect was observed by Mestl and Srinivasan in a reduction experiment of α-MoO3 [66]. Complete Raman analysis can be acceded in Supplementary Information.

Concerning the peaks at the wavenumbers higher than 1000 rel. cm−1, there are new carbon-related peaks compared to the reference MoO3 and MoO2 microcrystalline powders (Fig. S9B). With increasing temperature, the Raman signals reveal a greater presence of organic bonds, providing evidence for the integration of carbon moieties into the material. This observation aligns with XPS where the sum of the carbon-related signals compared to the sum of the molybdenum-related signals increases significantly (see Fig. S3). The incorporation of an organic part into an inorganic nanostructure during the microwave-assisted synthesis in benzyl alcohol has been known from the very beginning of this synthesis and could be exploited even to prepare hybrid nanomaterials [31]. In our case, this is not a goal but rather a side-effect of the high-temperature synthesis leading to amorphous clusters. Two broad bands associated with surface graphitic carbon are present in the region of D-band at 1355 rel. cm−1 and G-band at 1575 rel. cm−1 [83] (Fig. S9B).

A thorough Raman analysis revealed several processes affecting the spectra of the MoOX products depending on the temperature of synthesis, which allowed to elucidate their properties. Most interestingly, a discontinuous shift in the Mo = O bond position to higher wavenumbers between 90 °C and 120 °C samples reveals a crucial structural change, showing the impenetrability of the high-temperature processed nanostructure by water molecules. Secondly, compact packing of Mo-O subunits with more distorted lateral bonds and crosslinking were present at increased temperatures of the synthesis, aligning with Tauc plots and FTIR spectra. The third finding is the apparent richness of Mo-O vibrations throughout the spectra of the low-temperature synthesized samples, some of them being exclusively present at low-temperature syntheses, correlating with their lower degree of reduction observed by XPS. The last remark is the proceeding amorphization of the samples with increasing temperature, linked with increasing carbon content in the nanostructure. These changes together elucidate the dramatic change in the MoOX optoelectronic properties between 90 and 120 °C.

NIR absorption properties for controlled photothermal treatment

The variation in MoOX properties with synthesis temperature was investigated by a multitude of spectroscopic and electronic methods. The striking color shift upon water extraction, most pronounced at 90 °C, could not be attributed to nanoparticle size or morphology change, as TEM confirmed nearly identical ellipsoidal nanoparticles of ~ 2.5 nm across all temperatures. On the contrary, XPS revealed a sharp inversion of the Mo6+/Mo5+ ratio between 90 °C and 120 °C syntheses, signaling a fundamental change in oxidation state. Raman spectroscopy corroborated this transition: low-temperature products (70–90 °C) displayed multiple discrete Mo–O vibrations, including Mo = O peaks near 960 rel. cm−1 and Mo–O–Mo stretches around 790 rel. cm−1, consistent with octahedral polyoxomolybdate units. Simultaneously, major lattice defects in low-temperature MoOX can accommodate water molecules entering into specific interaction with Mo = O bond. At higher synthesis temperatures, however, a discontinuous shift of the Mo = O vibration to ~ 989 rel. cm−1 and a gradual upshift of the Mo–O–Mo band to 820–862 rel. cm−1 were observed, accompanied by peak broadening and convolution, indicating increased crosslinking, compact Mo–O packing, and amorphization. FTIR analysis further supported this structural reorganization, with the emergence of tetrahedrally coordinated MoO42− vibrations near 880 rel. cm−1 at elevated temperatures, replacing the octahedral signatures characteristic of lower temperatures. These spectroscopic changes align with Tauc plot, which showed a reduction in bandgap energy above 120 °C, consistent with denser Mo–O networks and larger electronic domain sizes. Photoluminescence measurement revealed several times higher radiative relaxations at the higher temperatures compared to lower ones. Together, the varied evidence converge to the dramatic shift in MoOX optoelectronic and photothermal behavior arising not from size effects, but from temperature-driven structural reorganization, oxidation state evolution, and bond crosslinking most pronounced between 90 and 120 °C. Direct observation of the electronic structure by ER-EIS further supports this evidence and hints at applications of low-temperature MoOX in photothermal treatment.

Along with MoOX electronic structure, the absorbance spectra of the products are substantially different from each other. The structural differences suggest the altered lattice arrangement with profound impact on relaxation mechanisms. Well-established theory of non-radiative transitions [84, 85] explains that minor lattice defects of high-temperature samples are responsible for observed radiative relaxation (including luminescence), whereas major lattice defects promote non-radiative relaxation and thus elevated photothermal conversion in low-temperature samples.

Notably, these changes also determine absorbance in the NIR region, which is of paramount importance for laser-triggered heat release in the two biological windows (650–900 nm and 1100–1350 nm) with drastic modification between syntheses performed at 90 °C and at temperatures higher than 120 °C. The molar absorption (attenuation) coefficients of the nanoparticles synthesized at 90 120 and 210 °C temperatures are shown in Fig. 8A. Complete range of temperatures is presented in Fig. S10A. Only the products prepared at low synthesis temperatures (≤ 90 °C) exhibit significant molar-absorption coefficient presents absorption within the NIR range which renders them applicable to photothermal therapy.

Its efficiency was tested with the sample diluted to 0.1 mg/mL in water with 808 nm laser setup of 500 mW power. A 50 µl sample was irradiated in an Eppendorf tube for 2 min, reaching a temperature increase of 36 °C (Fig. 8B). The heating diagram of control sample shows only a negligible temperature increase (Fig. 8C). Heating curves were used for the estimation of the calculated photothermal efficiency of 31.4% for the MoOX nanoparticles prepared at 90 °C (calculation is provided in Supplementary Information). The obtained conversion is comparable to that of other oxides exhibiting the same performance [86]. The solution retained the same absorbance following heating, indicating stability of the solution (Fig. S10B). It proves the feasibility of MoOX nanoparticles for photothermal treatment and calls for further development, which can benefit from understanding the structural basis controlling their optoelectronic properties as outlined in this work. This facilitates further development, uncovering the possibility to tune the photothermal performance of the polyoxomolybdate clusters by controlling their reduction degree and structural arrangement and even addressing and harnessing biological nanoparticle transformation [87, 88] to unlock the highest efficiency in the real cellular environment.

Interestingly, a process similar to the temperature-driven microwave reduction synthesis can be stimulated by the ER-EIS scans. When the layer of MoOX prepared at 90 °C is measured after the reduction scan, the defect states are suppressed (Fig. S11A). Such a reduced layer also shows a reduction in NIR absorption (Fig. S12). On the contrary, the first oxidation scan creates bandgap states in the layer of highly reduced MoOX prepared at 120 °C (Fig. S11B), leading to a small but measurable increase in its NIR absorption (Fig. S12). This observation suggests possible defect-engineering for MoOX, nanoparticles, allowing reversible control and tailoring of their optoelectronic and photothermal properties for a particular application.

Conclusion

For the first time to our best knowledge, the short microwave-assisted non-aqueous sol-gel synthesis over a range of temperatures was employed to prepare MoOX nanoparticles. Their optoelectronic properties with a focus on their NIR response and potential in photothermal therapy were studied in detail. TEM investigations of the nanostructures obtained at low (≤ 90 °C), moderate (≥ 120 °C) and high (≥ 190 °C) synthesis temperatures revealed the nanoparticles of a few-nanometer size, which did not vary significantly between different temperatures. XPS confirmed reduction of Mo centers with the most abrupt change between the syntheses at 90 and 120 °C, where the ratio between MoVI and MoV was inverted. Higher temperature of synthesis promoted a tighter packing of neighboring Mo atoms in the Mo-O-Mo structure, which was confirmed by Tauc plot energy-edge determination in the near UV region. An increased absorption was observed around 310 nm (4 eV) for the samples synthesized at higher temperatures. The X-ray diffraction patterns showed an ill-ordered structure of the nanoparticles with progressing amorphization toward higher synthesis temperatures. This evolution was confirmed by infrared and Raman spectra, where a substantial broadening and merging of the peaks, especially at higher temperatures, proved increasing amorphization. Furthermore, the infrared and Raman spectra showed the temperature-driven decay of octahedral MoO6 units in favor of tetrahedrally coordinated complexes, which prevent water molecules from penetrating into the cluster structure during the extraction step. The presence of a shifted Mo = O bond resulting from interactions with the water molecules was observed in Raman spectrum from 985 to 960 rel. cm−1 only at low temperatures. The water-permeable structure with major lattice defects is a prerequisite for photothermal properties, and Mo = O bond shift due to interaction with water could be used as a novel Raman signature of photothermally active polyoxomolybdates. The other Raman peaks specific for photothermally active nanoparticles were identified at 770–790 rel. cm−1 and at 220 rel. cm−1 wavenumbers. These peaks correspond to defect states in the bandgap confirmed by ER-EIS, which mediate the absorption at the wavelengths suitable for photothermal therapy. The optical UV-vis spectroscopy validated the presence of photothermal effect with the highest molar absorption coefficient in the NIR observed for the MoOX nanoparticles prepared at 90 °C. The photothermal heating was experimentally demonstrated by the 0.5 W laser irradiating the sample, with the temperature increase of up to 30 °C after 2 min corresponding to 31.4% photothermal efficiency. Microwave-assisted synthesis was thus proved suitable for the tailoring of optoelectronic properties for the controlled synthesis of laser-triggered heating agents.
