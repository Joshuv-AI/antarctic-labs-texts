A bivalent Delta/BA.5 mRNA vaccine elicits broad immune responses against various lineages of SARS-CoV-2 including JN.1
Authors: Kangyin Li, Qi Liu, Yan Wu, Bihao Wu, Shaohong Chen, Xinghai Zhang, Xiaoying Jia, Rui Gong, Yucai Peng, Huajun Zhang, Sandra Chiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A bivalent Delta/BA.5 mRNA vaccine provides protection against BA.5 in K18-ACE2 mice.•The vaccine protects hamsters from both Delta and BA.5 infections.•The animal immune sera neutralizes various lineages of variants including JN.1.

Dear Editor,

The severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic has posed a continuous threat to global public health. A number of vaccines, such as inactivated vaccines, mRNA vaccines, recombinant adenovirus vector vaccines and protein subunit vaccines, have been licensed and have achieved tremendous success in preventing infections, particularly severe hospitalization and death (Feikin et al., 2022; Ssentongo et al., 2022). However, with the emergence of new variants, breakthrough infections have occurred among vaccines, highlighting the need to develop broad-spectrum vaccines (Ukwishaka et al., 2023).

Bivalency is a strategy for increasing protection against new variants (Chalkias et al., 2022). Previously, we developed Delta/BA.1, Delta/BA.2 and Delta/BA.5 bivalent mRNA vaccines and found that Delta/BA.5 induced superior neutralizing antibodies (nAbs) in mice against the Wuhan-Hu-1, Delta, BA.1, BA.2 and BA.5 pseudotyped viruses (Li et al., 2022). Here, we demonstrate the potent efficacy of the Delta/BA.5 vaccine in protecting K18-hACE2 mice and hamsters from both Delta and BA.5 challenges, and that the immune sera from the animals maintain strong neutralizing activities against various variants, including authentic or pseudotyped WIV04, BA.2, BF.7, CH1.1, BQ.1.1, XBB.1.5, XBB.1.9, EG.5 and JN.1 viruses.

Groups of 14 or 16 ​K18-hACE2 mice (6–8 weeks old, female) were intramuscularly immunized with PBS or 5 μg or 15 μg of the Delta/BA.5 mRNA vaccine (day 0) and a boost dose 28 days later (day 28, Fig. 1A). Fourteen and 27 days after the boost (day 42 and day 55, respectively), the vaccinated mice produced high levels of spike protein-specific IgG, with no significant difference between the two-dose or both-day groups (Fig. 1B). A plaque reduction neutralizing test (PRNT) was performed to measure the serum nAb concentration in animals infected with the authentic SARS-CoV-2 BA.5 and Delta viruses (detailed methods in Supplementary Data; Zhang et al., 2022). As shown in Figs. 1C, 15 μg of the vaccine elicited a high level of nAb, with genomic mean titers (GMTs) of 9583 and 1474 for BA.5 and Delta, respectively, while the low-dose group also produced high levels of nAb against BA.5 (GMT of 3320); however, the titers against Delta were significantly lower, with three of the eight animals being below the detection limit. On the 28th day after the second immunization, the mice were intranasally challenged with 3.6 ​× ​105 ​PFU of BA.5 SARS-CoV-2 virus. Four days after infection (dpi), eight mice from each group were sacrificed and subjected to virus detection as previously described (Wu et al., 2022). No infectious virus was detected in the lungs or nasal turbinates of the immunized animals, but an average of 100–1000 ​PFU/g live virus was detected in the control group (Fig. 1D). The viral RNA load determined via qRT-PCR (Fig. 1E) showed that the control animals had an average of 106.4 copies/g in both the lungs and nasal turbinate and 104.4 copies/g in the trachea, while the immunized animals had significantly lower residual viral RNA levels, approximately 101.3 to 103.3 copies/g.

Since K18-hACE2 is a lethal model for SARS-CoV-2 infection (Shuai et al., 2022), to investigate whether the Delta/BA.5 vaccine protects mice from weight loss and death, six or eight mice from each group were weighed every day until two weeks after the challenge. The unvaccinated mice started to lose weight at 4 dpi and died from 6 to 8 dpi (Fig. 1F and G). For the low-dose group, the weight decreased from 2 dpi to 5 dpi and then slowly increased, but there was one death at 9 dpi, for a mortality rate of 12.5%. The high-dose group experienced a slight weight loss from 2 to 4 dpi. These results demonstrated that the Delta/BA.5 vaccine could provide effective protection against BA.5 infection in a lethal hACE2 transgenic mouse model.

To further investigate the protective efficacy of the Delta/BA.5 vaccine, 12 Syrian golden hamsters (4–6 weeks old, female) were immunized as above, but with 10 μg in the low-dose group or 30 μg in the high-dose group (Fig. 1H). On day 43 after the boost (day 71), half of the animals in each group were intranasally challenged with 1 ​× ​104 ​PFU of the Delta virus, and the other half were intranasally challenged with 7.2 ​× ​105 ​PFU of BA.5. The animals were sacrificed at 4 dpi or 3 dpi for Delta or BA.5 virus detection, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1I–N, all the immunized hamsters except one produced high levels of spike-binding IgG and nAb after immunization and had no infectious virus present in any tissues; moreover, there was an obvious reduction in the viral RNA load in the lung or trachea after Delta or BA.5 challenge. The exception, which was immunized at a low dose, had an amount of 1 ​× ​105 ​PFU/g in lung tissues after BA.5 challenge; the animal, which generated no significant spike protein-binding antibody, no nAb against Delta, or low nAb against BA.5, might have malfunctioned the immune response.

Since the emergence of the alpha variant in late 2020, new variants with immune escape properties have continued to cause fluctuations in prevalence every few months on a global scale (Tegally et al., 2022). After BA.5, multiple variants, such as XBB (Roemer et al., 2023), EG.5 (Uraki et al., 2023), and BA.2.86 (https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/coronaviruse/21112023_ba.2.86_ire.pdf), dominated circulation. To investigate the potential broad protective effects of these various variants, mouse and hamster immune sera collected prior to virus challenge were subjected to neutralization assays with authentic or pseudotyped viruses. As shown in Fig. 1C and J, the mice given a high dose had high levels of nAb against the ancestral strains WIV04, BA.2, BF.7, BQ.1.1, XBB.1.5 and XBB.1.9 with GMTs of more than 1000. Medium nAb were observed against CH.1.1 (GMT of 722), while titers decreased but remained significant for EG.5 (GMTs of 536 and 223 for authentic and pseudotyped viruses, respectively) and for pseudotyped JN.1 (GMT of 97); similar results were observed in hamsters with less variation. The data indicated that the Delta/BA.5 bivalent mRNA vaccine has the potential for broad-spectrum protection against different variants and is useful for future vaccine design.
