Measuring adverse and protective experiences in early childhood: development and initial validation of the child and family experiences survey (CAFES)
Authors: Ruofan Gongye, Erinn B Duprey, A Dirk Hightower, Joseph P McFall
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are associated with a wide range of negative health, behavioral, and developmental outcomes across the lifespan. However, many individuals demonstrate resilience, and protective factors play a critical role in buffering the effects of adversity. Yet most existing ACE measures overlook these protective experiences and rely primarily on retrospective adult self-reports. To address this gap, we developed the Child and Family Experiences Survey (CAFES), a caregiver-report tool designed to assess both adverse and protective experiences in early childhood across diverse settings.MethodsCaregivers (N = 157) from two counties in New York State completed the CAFES, which assessed adverse experiences, protective factors, and social determinants of health items. Summary scores were calculated for both adverse and protective experiences. Descriptive statistics, bivariate correlations, and principal components analysis (PCA) were conducted to evaluate item performance and inform item reduction.ResultsDescriptive analyses showed a range of endorsement across items related to adverse experiences, protective factors, and social determinants of health items. Correlations within protective and adverse experience items were moderate, supporting internal consistency. PCA was conducted for protective factors and social determinants of health items, and a four-component solution emerged for each. The CAFES was refined based on results from descriptive findings and the PCA.ConclusionsThe CAFES is a novel, caregiver-report tool designed to assess both adverse and protective childhood experiences, as well as family needs. Developed through a community-engaged process, CAFES addresses key limitations in existing ACE measures by incorporating protective factors and offering applicability across clinical, educational, and research settings. Background

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) encompass a broad spectrum of potentially distressing events that happen during childhood [1], including emotional, physical, and sexual abuse; domestic violence; household substance abuse; mental illness in the household; parental separation or divorce; and incarceration of a household member [2]. The study and measurement of ACEs have become increasingly important. However, there is currently no universally agreed-upon list of ACEs or a single standard screening tool, and existing measures vary in terms of content, format, and target populations. Children exposed to ACEs are at elevated risk for adverse psychosocial, behavioral, and health outcomes across the lifespan [3, 4]. These include increased physical inactivity, obesity, tobacco and alcohol abuse, illicit drug use, psychological distress, suicide attempts, and risky sexual behavior. ACEs have also been linked to an increased risk of adverse health outcomes, including cancer, gastrointestinal disease, diabetes, respiratory disease, somatic pain or headaches, sleep disturbances, fractures, ischemic heart disease, and memory impairment [3, 4].

Although ACEs pose a risk to children’s future development, many individuals demonstrate resilience and achieve success across multiple domains (e.g., interpersonal, social, and psychological) [5, 6]. Resilience is broadly defined as “the capacity of a system to adapt successfully to disturbances that threaten the viability, function, or development of the system” [7]. Resilience theory provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how some young individuals navigate adverse exposures [8], and it informs interventions aimed at reducing risks and fostering positive development [9]. Within this framework, the protective factors model posits that protective factors buffer the negative effects of risk in predicting adverse outcomes [8]. There are two potential protective models: the risk-protective model and the protective-protective model [10]. The risk-protective models suggest that promotive factors help to reduce or mitigate the association between risks and adverse outcomes, while the protective-protective models suggest that one protective factor can amplify the impact of another protective factor in predicting an outcome [10]. Both the risk-protective model and the protective-protective model can be tested using interaction effects in regression or multi-group analysis in structural equation modeling [10]. However, many current measures of ACEs do not include items on protective factors, limiting the ability to identify resilience pathways and to capture both risk and protective influences on child development.

Protective factors, defined as environmental, social, or individual variables that promote resilience and disrupt the trajectory from risk to pathology [8], play a critical role in mitigating the effects of adversity. Resilience research has identified common protective factors such as effective caregiving, supportive relationships, problem-solving skills, self-regulation, self-efficacy, optimism, and beliefs that life has meaning, all of which play important roles in promoting resilience [9]. By facilitating positive development, these protective factors and experiences can help children overcome adversity [8]. Therefore, assessing protective factors alongside ACEs is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of their combined impact on child development.

Previous research on protective factors in childhood development emphasized the importance of parental factors such as parenting styles, parent-child relationships, and parental support. For instance, Yamaoka and Bard’s research on positive parenting found that positive parenting practices demonstrate robust protective effects, regardless of the number of ACEs experienced [11]. Additionally, Donte et al. discovered that positive parent-child relationships and bonding can predict resilience in the context of adverse community experiences [12]. Barnhart et al. further identified family resilience, characterized by qualities like maintaining hopefulness, leveraging strengths, and collaborative problem-solving, as crucial in navigating challenges [13].

Peer support is another important protective factor, shown to increase resilience, foster social skills, build self-esteem, and provide both emotional and practical support [14]. For example, a longitudinal study examining the role of peer relationships found that both peer acceptance and friendship mitigated the association between aspects of family adversity and child externalizing behavior [15]. Moreover, two prospective studies investigating the moderating effect of friendships on the developmental trajectory from early harsh, punitive, and hostile family environments to peer victimization suggest that the predicted associations did not apply to children who had multiple friendships [16].

In addition, studies have shown that community-level protective factors, such as informal support from an extrafamilial adults, involvement in extracurricular activities, and neighborhood support, can also promote resilience among children exposed to ACEs, including child maltreatment [17]. Support from extrafamilial adults (e.g., coaches, neighbors, family friends, camp counselors, or other professionals) is a well-researched community factor [17]. For instance, a longitudinal study on camp counselors and Latino children who experienced maltreatment found that warm relationship with the counselor is associated with higher levels of resilience [18]. Involvement in extracurricular activities may include activities such as attending religious services or participating in general activities [17]. A cohort study of emotionally and physically maltreated youth found that participation in general activities has a protective role [19]: for children who experienced emotional maltreatment, engagement in extracurricular activities was significantly associated with good educational attainment and greater well-being; and for physically abused children, engagement in extracurricular activities was significantly associated with educational attainment [19]. Collective efficacy, a factor of neighborhood support defined as social cohesion among neighbors combined with their willingness to intervene on behalf of the common good [20], is also associated with resilience [17]. Yonaser et al. found that collective efficacy may serve as a protective factor specifically against externalizing behaviors in neglected youth [21].

Over the past decade, there has been a growing interest in the measurement and study of ACEs. Many common ACE measures use the ACE score, an integer from 0 to 10 that captures the cumulative impact of ACEs on children’s health and social outcomes [22, 23]. For example, Brown et al.’s study on the associations between ACEs and attention deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) found significant associations between ACE score, ADHD, and moderate to severe ADHD [24]. Schickedanz et al. used ACE scores to study the intergenerational links between parents’ and children’s ACEs and found that parents with higher ACE scores were significantly more likely to have children with elevated scores as well [25]. Findings from studies using ACE scores consistently demonstrate a dose–response relationship, including but not limited to suicidal thoughts and behaviors, chronic heart disease, major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, and posttraumatic stress disorder [22, 26, 27].

Considering the importance of protective childhood experiences in enhancing resilience and buffering the negative effects of ACEs, it is necessary to assess not only adverse experiences but also protective ones. Several tools have been developed to capture these beneficial experiences. For example, the Benevolent Childhood Experiences (BCEs) measure [28], and the Protective Factors Survey [29]. Although tools like the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), introduced in the 1990 s, have contributed significantly to assessing childhood experiences, there remains a need for updated and comprehensive instruments that evaluate both adversity and protective factors within the same framework.

Additionally, while many ACE measurement tools are designed for adults to retrospectively report on childhood adversities, such as the Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ) [30], the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) ACE module [31], and the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) [32], some tools have been developed to directly assess ACEs through adolescent and teen self-reports for use in research and clinical settings. Examples include the Teen Self-Report version of the Pediatric ACEs and Related Life-events Screener (PEARLS) [33], which includes 19 self-report questions for adolescents aged 12–19, and the Bright Futures Previsit Questionnaire (PVQ) [34], which offers tailored versions available for adolescents aged 11–14 (PVQ 11–14), 15–17 (PVQ 15–17), and 18–21 (PVQ 18–21). However, these tools have notable limitations, including potential misclassification and bias in retrospective reporting [35], as well as ethical concerns that ACE screening could retraumatize patients, particularly when clinicians and support staff lack sufficient training in trauma-informed care approaches [36].

Caregivers are a valuable source of information on children’s early experiences, yet many widely used tools rely on retrospective self-report from adults recalling their own childhoods [37, 38]. Moreover, existing tools often have limited scope, focusing on select domains of adversity. While no single measure can fully capture the breadth of children’s experiences, the Child and Family Experiences Survey (CAFES) aims to address part of this gap by assessing both adverse and protective experiences through caregiver reports, making it suitable for use across research, clinical, educational, and community-based settings.

Another limitation of many ACE screening and measurement tools is the “knowledge-to-action gap,” in which pediatricians and family doctors do not routinely screen for ACEs, as the research evidence has not yet lead to changes in standard clinical practice [39]. Consequently, there is a need for screening tools that can be utilized across various contexts (e.g., pediatric offices, early childhood centers) to assess young children’s exposure to both adverse and protective experiences. The present study aimed to address current challenges in ACE measurement and screening, particularly the need for a caregiver-report tool that captures both adverse and beneficial childhood experiences (i.e., protective factors). In collaboration with an interdisciplinary team of researchers and practitioners, we developed and refined the CAFES, designed for use with caregivers to assess young children’s experiences across multiple domains.

Method

Measure development

The CAFES was developed in 2018 as part of Get Ready to Grow Rochester, a comprehensive community screening initiative for children aged 12 to 60 months, supported by Help Me Grow NY and affiliated with the National Help Me Grow Network. Fourteen “gold standards” work groups consisting of professionals spanning education, support services, and healthcare sectors convened to review best practices and tools for screening across 19 domains of physical health, well-being, and development. One workgroup was specifically tasked with identifying best practices for measuring (a) adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), (b) protective factors, (c) social determinants of health, and (d) social determinants of education. Members included educators, early education support practitioners, a school psychologist, and pediatricians. This team reviewed the literature, assessed both proprietary and open-source screening tools, and made recommendations for implementation.

The work group emphasized the importance of (1) engaging with families and caregiver-report, (2) including protective factors alongside ACEs in screening efforts, and (3) ensuring brevity to respect families’ time and improve completion rates. Further, the group recognized that social determinants of health and education are conceptually aligned, thereby linking these domains and laying the foundation for continued cross-sector collaboration. Given the workgroup’s expertise in early childhood measurement development, they were well positioned to design a novel screening tool that captured essential content from all four domains in a concise format. Drawing on their collective expertise, the multidisciplinary workgroup developed version 1.0 of the CAFES, which comprised brief assessments of protective experiences, adverse experiences, and family needs.

As part of the ongoing development for the present CAFES revision, we used a community-engaged approach that included consulting with a diverse group of parents and caregivers with lived expertise to ensure the measure’s content and language were acceptable and accessible. Specifically, an expert panel comprised of five caregivers with lived experience who had previously participated in the Get Ready to GROW program was invited to provide feedback on CAFES 1.0 items. Panel members received the CAFES 1.0 tool in advance for review and then participated in a one-hour guided discussion. The study team facilitated this discussion using prompts such as, “Which items made you feel comfortable or less comfortable to answer, and why?” and “Were any questions unclear or confusing, and how could they be improved?” Feedback included identifying items that could cause discomfort, hinder completion, appear repetitive, lack clinical actionability, or use confusing language. These insights were combined with quantitative item response patterns and inter-item correlations to guide revisions to the tool.

Pilot data collection

Pilot data for the CAFES were obtained as secondary data from the Get Ready to GROW program, collected between 2020 and 2021 and 2022–2023. Get Ready to GROW is a local affiliate of the Help Me Grow model, which provides coordinated universal and targeted early childhood services [40]. Children received screenings (i.e., gross motor, fine motor, hearing, speech, and vision screening) either at community events or childcare centers, with parental consent obtained prior to screening.

As part of the screening protocol, parents were also asked to complete additional measures, including assessments of their child’s social and emotional development (e.g., via the Ages and Stages Questionnaire [41]), and about their child’s and family’s adverse and protective experiences (i.e., the CAFEs). Because the program was implemented across multiple counties and sites, CAFES data collection procedures varied by location. Depending on the site, parents completed the CAFES at their child’s childcare center following the screening, at the screening event itself, or, in some cases, via mailed surveys.

Variables of interest

Caregivers completed the CAFES to report on their child’s adverse and protective childhood experiences, as well as the social determinants of health. A total of 14 items assesses protective experiences, such as “Does this child go to playgrounds or parks, when it is safe to do so?”, and “Does this child have a home life that is stable and predictable?” Answer choices included “rarely”, “sometimes” and “often”. A total of 13 items assessed adverse childhood experiences, such as “Has this child ever been homeless”, and “Has this child experienced the death of a close family member or friend?” The adversities assessed included seeing a close family member or friend sick, bereavement, moving, homelessness, caregiver separation/divorce, caregiver mental health and/or substance use issues, caregiver incarceration, witnessing or experiencing neighborhood violence, witnessing or experiencing abuse in the home, foster care, living with adults that smoke, and bullying. Response choices included “never”, “a few times” and “many times”. Last, the CAFES includes 20 items about family needs, such as “Do you, as the parent/caregiver, need or want more food for your family?”, and “Do you, as the parent/caregiver, need or want help from a lawyer?” Response choices included “no”, “yes”, and “yes, and help is desired”.

Summary score was calculated for the total number of adverse childhood experiences, with a score of “1” assigned items endorsed as occurring “a few times” or “many times”. Similarly, protective experiences were summed by assigning a score of 1 to items endorsed as occurring “sometimes” or “often”.

Statistical analysis

First, descriptive statistics and frequencies for all CAFES items and summary scores were calculated with R version 4.2.1. Race and ethnicity data were missing for 50.32% of the sample due to systemic issues in the external data system. Specifically, in the early years of data collection, the initial demographics form did not include a race variable, preventing transmission of this information to the system. Missing ZIP code data were also observed, stemming from variation in consent processes across sites. The availability of this information depended on agreements with individual partner organizations, leading to inconsistencies in data collection and contributing to gaps in ZIP code reporting.

Next, we examined bivariate correlations for all CAFES items. Lastly, we conducted a principal components analysis (PCA) to identify underlying dimensions within the protective factors and social determinants of health items and to support item reduction. A PCA was run instead of an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) because the primary purpose of the analysis was item reduction. The rotation method was oblimin, a type of oblique rotation, allowing for correlations between factors. Dimensionality of the PCA solution was chosen based on a variety of criteria (e.g., eigenvalues above 1.0, fit indices, and a scree plot) as per recommendations for best practices for PCA [43]. A team of researchers then reviewed the PCA results and made decisions regarding item retention and refinement.

Item reduction

A PCA was conducted separately for items related to protective childhood experiences and social determinants of health, to decompose the independent components, with the goal of item reduction. PCA was not applied to the adverse childhood experiences items, as each item represents a conceptually distinct experience with unique implications for child development. Therefore, item reduction was not the goal for the ACEs subsection of the CAFEs.

In the PCA for protective childhood experiences, a four-component solution was chosen based on a number of factors including the Scree plot and eigenvalues. See Table 5 for item loadings for each of the four emerging components, and Table 6 for correlations between factors. Components represented family protective factors, childhood activities and socialization, negative family protective factors, and neighborhood protective factors. The family protective factors component had medium correlations with childhood activities and socialization (r =.33) and with negative family protective factors (r = −.42), and a small correlation with neighborhood protective factors (r =.16). The childhood activities and socialization component had small correlations with the negative family protective factors component (r = −.25) and the neighborhood protective factors component (r =.04). Finally, the negative family protective factors and neighborhood protective factors components had a small-sized correlation (r = −.07).

In the PCA on items relating to social determinants of health, a four-component solution was chosen as well. See Table 6 for item loadings for these four factors. Components represented basic and parenting needs; financial needs; self-improvement and work-related needs; and smoking cessation. Most components had correlations that were small in magnitude: Basic and parenting needs correlated with financial needs (r = −.17), self-improvement and work-related needs (r =.24), and smoking cessation (r =.002). Financial needs correlated with self-improvement and work-related needs (r = −.37) and smoking cessation (r =.14); and finally, self-improvement and work-related needs correlated with smoking cessation (r = −.02).

Item refinement

Based on findings from descriptive and bivariate analyses, as well as results from the PCA, two items were removed from the protective factors scale: “Does this child experience a family that protects children from harm?” and “Does this child go to a zoo or museum, when it is safe to do so?”. Additionally, two items were removed from the social determinants of health scale: “Do you need or want a full-time job?” and “Do you need or want to go to school or college?” One item was also reworded from “Help with getting SNAP (food stamps)” to “Help getting benefits such as SNAP, WIC, housing vouchers, or other benefits” to broaden its scope. Several items with low endorsement (below 0.3) were kept in the scale due to their potential importance in clinical settings, including items related to drug or alcohol use and smoking cessation.

Discussion

In summary, we developed the CAFES tool, a caregiver-report measure designed to assess both adverse and protective childhood experiences, making it applicable across research, educational, clinical, and pediatric settings. Item development was guided by a community-engaged approach in collaboration with a transdisciplinary team of researchers and clinicians. The tool was subsequently refined using PCA, resulting in a measure that captures a range of adverse experiences, protective factors, and family needs.

Research on ACEs has received significant attention, as previous studies have linked ACEs to numerous adverse health outcomes later in life [3, 4]. These findings have influenced medical and public health policy and carry potential implications for reducing healthcare costs by enabling early interventions through screening, thereby preventing downstream expenses [44]. In particular, utilizing ACE screening data can support the development and implementation of Trauma-Informed Care (TIC) [45], a promising practice defined as a strengths-based service delivery approach “that is grounded in an understanding of and responsiveness to the impact of trauma, that emphasizes physical, psychological, and emotional safety for both providers and survivors, and that creates opportunities for survivors to rebuild a sense of control and empowerment [46].” In behavioral health, the benefits of integrating TIC extend beyond individuals to include their families, communities, behavioral health organizations, and staff [47]. TIC emphasizes the pervasive impacts of trauma on physical and mental health while creating more opportunities for individuals to access compassionate services [47]. Its implementation enhances screening, assessment, treatment planning, and placement, improves communication between clients and service providers, as well as reduces the risk of retraumatization [47].

However, controversies arose regarding the items in the current ACE inventory, as research suggests they were not selected through a rigorous scientific review process to identify the best predictors for future health outcomes [44]. In addition, previous ACE publications primarily focus on an older demographic, examining adversities from a less trauma-informed historical context, which may exaggerate their effects compared to contemporary experiences shaped by modern trauma-informed practices [44]. For instance, the Kaiser Permanente study, one of the largest investigations into childhood abuse, neglect, and household challenges, assessed their impact on health and well-being in later life [48], with participants having an average age of 55 [26]. Notably, the reliance on questionnaires designed for adults to retrospectively recall their experiences may introduce biases, particularly the tendency for individuals with more problems in adulthood to report greater childhood adversities, which can distort findings [35, 49]. Furthermore, the ACE inventory fails to include several significant childhood adversities, such as peer rejection, bullying, discrimination, and low socioeconomic status—factors that have been recognized and incorporated by other researchers [50, 51]. Therefore, there is a need to develop a more sensitive and specific screening tool through a thorough and systematic review.

Compared to other common ACEs screening tools that focus on adults to retrospectively assess childhood adversities, such as the Adverse Childhood Experiences International Questionnaire (ACE-IQ) [30], Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) adverse childhood experiences (ACE) module [31], and the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) [32], our Child and Family Experiences Survey directly emphasizes childhood experiences as reported by caregivers, thereby addressing potential biases associated with retrospective recall. In addition, existing ACE measurement methods often lack considerations for protective factors and resilience, that most of these methods are recommended for use alongside other questionnaires or as part of a larger survey designed to gather data on resilience and other protective factors, such as supportive relationships in the home, school, or community [52]; while our survey incorporates protective factors from the perspectives of the household, school, and community. To enhance the sensitivity of ACE screening, we also incorporated questions on economic hardship and food insecurity, informed by previous literature [53].

The CAFES was originally developed and tested within early childhood centers as part of the Get Ready to GROW initiative. This setting allowed for the collection of valuable information directly relevant to education programming, helping to identify children’s needs, guide support services, and inform community-based interventions. Its successful implementation in these environments underscores the potential for diverse applications of ACE screening, which we explore further in the following section.

Another strength of our Child and Family Experiences Survey is its suitability for implementation in clinical settings. ACE screening in the clinical setting has demonstrated profound impacts on preventive and clinical medicine, particularly in pediatrics, where it serves as a critical red flag for identifying concurrent child abuse and neglect or suicidal thoughts and intent [54]. Research suggests that trauma and ACEs screening can provide a new perspective of understanding the human experience, particularly for understanding the lives of children and their caregivers, who are increasingly exposed to horrific acts, often amplified through social media [45]. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that clinicians in the medical home identify risk and protective factors for developmental and mental disorders in all children and families under their care [55].

However, several barriers to ACE screening in the clinical setting have been identified in the literature, with one significant challenge being that many medical practitioners lack awareness of the ACE questionnaire and its clinical uses [56]. The absence of a universally accepted ACE screening tool may also contribute to this barrier. Other barriers include medical staff hesitancy due to the potentially upsetting or triggering nature of screening questions (e.g., those addressing domestic violence, suicidal intentions, and sexual abuse) [54], a lack of training and time [57], difficulties in establishing patient-provider trust in the context of ACE screening [58], challenges in delivering an efficient and therapeutically appropriate response [54], and limited referral resources for behavioral health and community treatment [44, 54]. Furthermore, there is ongoing debate regarding the development of a universal standard screening tool, including which ACEs are most critical to include and the lack of established cut-offs for a “positive” ACE screening [59].

To effectively address these issues within the context of ACEs, it is crucial to ensure that data collection and usage are non-diagnostic, multi-generational, trauma-informed, and include assessments of patient resilience [45]. Future efforts should focus on incorporating TIC for children to mitigate the cascading effects of trauma [59]. In addition to recognizing how ACEs impact health, identifying pediatric clinical symptom presentations, and equipping providers with the tools and resources to respond appropriately without re-traumatizing patients, as discussed by Goddard in his paper on ACEs and TIC [60], Negriff et al.’s study on ACE screening in primary care highlights practical strategies such as staff training on TIC, implementing universal screening to ensure inclusivity, and establishing robust support systems for managing positive screens [59]. Notably, Negriff et al. emphasize the importance of de-stigmatizing high ACE scores through family education on stress and its health impacts, broader outreach efforts (e.g., emails, handouts, and online messaging), and collaboration with community organizations to support families and address the systemic causes of ACEs [59].

Establishing patient-provider trust is essential not only for effective ACE screening but also because individuals with ACEs are more likely to report low trust in health advice from healthcare professionals, including hospital doctors, general practitioners, nurses, and pharmacists [61]. In a concept analysis on patient-provider trust of prenatal ACEs screening, researchers define trust in prenatal ACE screening as a network of evidence-based attributes that include the timing of the screening, patient familiarity with the health care provider, cultural competence, demystifying trauma, open dialogue between the patient and health care provider, and patient comfort and respect [58]. Any upset patient or negative response may indicate the need for a more relaxed, skilled approach [54]. Thus, adequate training for practitioners to utilize the CAFEs and other ACE screenings is critical.

Despite the aforementioned advantages of our Child and Family Experiences Survey, we acknowledge the presence of missing demographic data in our analysis, primarily due to omitted questions during the early stages of data collection and issues with data transmission from systems. Additionally, the survey did not capture family-level, individual socioeconomic status (SES), as it only included the family’s residential zip code, which we linked to the 2022 U.S. Census Bureau to obtain the area-level poverty rate. This proxy measure may not fully reflect each family’s socioeconomic status. To address these gaps, future efforts will focus on standardizing the data-sharing process across community screening partners and agencies to ensure consistency and completeness in data collection and analysis. Additionally, we recognize that the sample used in this study is not fully representative. Our pilot data was obtained from a community-based program, Get Ready to GROW, which was implemented in affiliate communities across New York State, including both rural, urban, and suburban locations [62]. While this limits the generalizability of our findings, the sample still provides valuable insights as it reflects the unique challenges and strengths of families. By focusing on the experiences of these communities, our survey offers targeted interventions that align with the needs of the population we aim to serve, contributing to a better understanding of how to address disparities and support community well-being. Notably, recognizing the economic disparities that exist within our communities is crucial; we intend to address these differences by seeking a more representative sample in our ACE screenings. This will involve identifying and engaging populations that are often underrepresented, particularly those from economically disadvantaged backgrounds, to ensure that our findings reflect the diverse experiences of all community members. Additionally, because CAFES relies on caregiver reports rather than direct observation, it shares the inherent limitations of retrospective recall, which may introduce some degree of reporting bias or misclassification, even though the recall period is shorter than adult self-reports of childhood experiences. As the next step, we aim to collect qualitative data through focus group interviews to gain a deeper understanding of caregivers’ experiences, thoughts, and feelings about the survey. Finally, it is important to note that the present manuscript outlines the initial development and preliminary validation of the CAFES using a Principal Component Analysis, with the goal of item reduction and initial factor exploration. Additional work is required to further understand and validate the tool, including tests of construct (e.g., convergent and divergent) validity, as well as item analysis.

In sum, we have presented a novel tool that uses parent responses to assess children’s adverse and protective life experiences, as well as family needs. Next steps and future directions include thorough pilot testing and cross-cultural validation to ensure the tool adequately addresses the unique experiences of multiple populations, such as immigrant populations. Additionally, we aim to further refine the survey to make it more acceptable, user-friendly, and comfortable for caregivers to complete. For example, immigrants may face specific adversities related to their status, cultural adaptation, and access to resources [63]. Pairing the screening process with appropriate support services will also be critical to provide participants with immediate access to assistance, thereby enhancing its overall impact. In addition, future work should focus on further investigating the psychometric properties of the CAFES tool, including validating the CAFES against other psychometrically established measures to establish convergent and divergent validity, as well as to examine measurement invariance and strengthen its utility across diverse populations and settings.
