A functional antiplatelet nanomotor for tri-modal ablation of acute arterial thrombus
Authors: Haonan Wu, Hongyuan Zhang, Shuting Guo, Fu Yao, Yuting Sun, Yuan Sun, Shenwu Zhang, Yuequan Wang, Cong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTimely and safe thrombolysis is a priority in the clinical management of acute arterial embolism. However, current drug therapies face the challenge of balancing therapeutic benefits with bleeding risks. Nanomotors emerge as a promising nonpharmaceutical modality for embolism recanalization, yet transient physical-force-driven antithrombotic therapy cannot achieve long-acting treatment, resulting in unsatisfactory therapeutic outcomes. Here, we exploit a functional antiplatelet nanomotor, which is precisely assembled from a photothermal photosensitizer (DiR), a photothermal-activatable nitric oxide (NO) donor (BNN6), and an antiplatelet drug (ticagrelor [TGL]). Under laser irradiation, the nanomotor achieves deep thrombus penetration through vigorous propulsion enabled by efficient NO generation, while on-site TGL release acts synergistically with NO to enhance antiplatelet function. As expected, the clot-piercing antiplatelet nanomotor displays striking thrombolytic effects in two artery embolism rat models. This study advances a photothermal/mechanical/antiplatelet tri-modal thrombolytic modality toward clinical applicability.

Introduction

Thrombotic diseases pose a grave threat to human health.1 Among them, acute arterial thrombosis with high rates of disability and mortality remains a major clinical challenge.2,3 Since arteries are critical for delivering oxygen and nutrients to tissues, arterial occlusion often results in irreversible tissue damage and organ dysfunction.4,5 Timely and effective revascularization to restore blood flow is the top priority in the treatment of acute arterial thrombus. Although thrombectomy is widely applied, it inevitably causes vascular endothelial injury and increases the risk of recurrence.6,7 Unlike “red” venous thrombi composed of numerous red blood cells, arterial thrombi are typically classified as “white” clots, composed primarily of activated platelets and fibrin.8,9 Accordingly, antiplatelet drugs and fibrinolytic agents are widely applied for acute arterial thrombus therapy. However, antiplatelet drugs (e.g., ticagrelor [TGL]) contribute to preventing platelets activation and aggregation but fail to degrade the preformed clots, while fibrinolytic agents (e.g., urokinase-type plasminogen activator [uPA]) promote clot dissolution by activating the fibrinolytic cascade but lack antiplatelet activity.10,11,12 To achieve better therapeutic effects, antiplatelet/fibrinolytic combination therapy holds promise for improving the efficacy of treatment for arterial thrombus, but it carries an unacceptably high risk of hemorrhage.13,14,15

Emerging nanotechnology and nonpharmaceutical thrombus treatment strategies have gained increasing attention for their superior controllability and low bleeding risk.16,17,18,19 Among these, photothermal therapy (PTT) leverages near-infrared (NIR) laser irradiation to generate localized hyperthermia at thrombotic sites, thereby disrupting the non-covalent interactions within fibrin networks to facilitate thrombolysis.20,21,22 But the thrombolytic efficiency of PTT alone falls short of estimates. The combination of PTT with other thrombolytic strategies offers greater advantages.23,24,25 Recent advancements have seen the integration of PTT with nanomotor technology, giving rise to thermophoretic nanomotors for combined photothermal-mechanical thrombolysis.26,27,28 Nanomotors with autonomous motility, which disrupt the thrombus through mechanical action, have shown promise in thrombolytic therapy.29,30 Particularly, nanomotors composed of photothermal agents and gas-releasing precursor molecules have been reported.31 Under laser irradiation, these nanomotors continuously generate therapeutic gases such as NO, which possesses vasodilatory, antiplatelet, and endothelial protective properties,32 enabling photothermal/bubble dual-driven autonomous motion, thrombus deep penetration, and mechanical thrombolysis, thereby facilitating the rapid restoration of blood flow.33 Although significant progress has been made, physical-force-driven thrombolytic therapy remains unable to sustain long-term therapeutic benefits without continuous laser irradiation, nor can it effectively suppress platelet activation, thus failing to prevent thrombosis progression or recurrence. Furthermore, construction of most nanomotors relies on carrier materials (e.g., PLGA nanoparticles, mesoporous silica, or gold nanocarriers), which results in low “fuel” loading efficiency, heavy nanostructure, and insufficient autonomous movement.27,34,35

In contrast, carrier-free nanomotors are fabricated through self-assembly driven by non-covalent interactions among “fuel” molecules without the use of additional carrier materials.36,37 This approach ensures high “fuel” density and autonomous movement performance of nanomotors, while reducing the toxicity concerns associated with carrier materials.36 Despite these advances, however, the outstanding fuel-loading capacity and mechanical performance does not achieve satisfactory thrombolytic effects due to the limitations of a single mechanical treatment modality. Interestingly, we discovered that the photothermal photosensitizer (DiR), the photothermal-activatable NO donor (BNN6), and the antiplatelet drug (TGL) could spontaneously assemble to form a stable nanosystem. We hypothesized that integrating traditional pharmacotherapy with nanomotor technology could yield a functionalized nanomotor with antiplatelet activity. This strategy is expected to leverage the instantaneous photothermal-mechanical burst of nanomotors and the long-lasting antiplatelet effect of TGL to achieve both rapid and long-term management of acute arterial thrombus. Under laser irradiation, DiR-mediated photothermal effects and NO-driven mechanical disruption synergize for physical thrombolysis, critical for relieving acute arterial occlusion. Concurrently, TGL and NO can be precisely delivered to thrombus to inhibit platelet activation/aggregation, synergizing with physical thrombolysis and mitigating recurrence. Furthermore, the photothermal/mechanical effects facilitate the deep penetration of TGL within the clots, thereby further amplifying its on-site antiplatelet effects. Such a carrier-free antiplatelet nanomotor is expected to realize photothermal/mechanical/antiplatelet tri-modal treatment of acute arterial thrombus.

To validate our hypothesis, a carrier-free antiplatelet nanomotor (P-BDT NAs) was constructed via the precise assembly of DiR, BNN6, and TGL, followed by PEGylation and fibrin-targeting modification. Notably, the motion propulsion module (DiR/BNN6) and TGL reached a high drug loading rate of 75% in the nanomotor. Under laser irradiation, the motion propulsion module generates photothermal effects and triggers NO release, enabling the nanomotor to achieve a movement speed of ∼5.7 μm/s, which benefits mechanical thrombolysis while facilitating deep TGL delivery into clots. Importantly, we observed that TGL and NO exhibit a synergistic antiplatelet effect, achieving nearly 74.9% in vitro antiplatelet efficiency, which was 1.4 times higher than that of TGL alone (52.6%). As expected, the antiplatelet nanomotor showed superior thrombolysis capacity, achieving more than 90% thrombolytic rate within a short irradiation duration in FeCl3-induced acute carotid and femoral artery thrombosis models. Moreover, it demonstrates excellent safety in vitro and in vivo. Of particular significance, the antiplatelet nanomotors manage to prevent the formation of new thrombus post-thrombolysis in the femoral artery thrombosis model, demonstrating excellent therapeutic effects and the ability to prevent thrombosis recurrence compared with rt-PA. It has the potential to reshape the clinical treatment landscape of arterial thrombosis in the future.

Results

Design and fabrication of an antiplatelet nanomotor

We proposed that integrating antiplatelet therapy and nanomotor into one system would achieve high-performance thrombus treatment. To achieve this goal, we designed a clot-piercing antiplatelet nanomotor based on small-molecule self-assembling nanotechnology, consisting of a DiR-/BNN6-based propulsion module and a TGL-based antiplatelet unit (Figure 1A). Interestingly, DiR, BNN6, and the TGL were found to readily co-assemble into carrier-free nanoassemblies (NAs) via the one-step nanoprecipitation method. We first explored the ratio of DiR/BNN6 (3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3) based on their nanoassembly performance, obtaining an optimal molar ratio of 1:1 (Table S1). Based on this, we further screened the mass ratio of TGL to the propulsion module (3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3). As shown in Tables S1 and S2, the unmodified ternary nanoassembly fabricated at a mass ratio of 1:1 (TGL to DiR/BNN6) exhibited favorable properties with mean diameter of 72.6 ± 0.6 nm and small polydispersity index (PDI, 0.11 ± 0.04), possessing regular spherical nanostructures (Figure S1A), which were designated as B-BDT NAs. To enhance both the colloidal stability and thrombus-targeting capacity, DSPE-PEG2K and DSPE-PEG2K-CREKA were modified on B-BDT NAs, obtaining a PEGylated thrombus-targeting antiplatelet nanomotor (P-BDT NAs) (Figure 1A). As shown in Figure 1B and Table S2, PEGylation modification slightly increased the mean particle size of P-BDT NAs (77.5 nm) compared to B-BDT NAs (72.6 nm). Notably, the negatively charged phosphate group in DSPE-PEG2K and the CREKA peptide in DSPE-PEG2K-CREKA shifted the zeta potential from +14.86 mV (B-BDT NAs) to −16.52 mV (P-BDT NAs). The negatively charged nanomedicines have been found to perform better than the positively charged ones in terms of long-term circulation in the blood. Moreover, a ternary nanoassembly incapable of NO production (P-NDT NAs) was prepared using BPA (the precursor of BNN6), and a binary nanoassembly consisting of BNN6 and DiR (P-BD NAs) was prepared as the control NAs (Figure 1C). As shown in Figures 1B, 1D, and 1E, these three NAs (P-BDT NAs, P-NDT NAs, and P-BD NAs) exhibited regular spherical nanostructures, with mean particle sizes ranging from 77 to 90 nm. Moreover, the appearances of the four NAs were also recorded in Figure S1B.

We then investigated the colloidal stability of these NAs. As shown in Figure 1F, the PEGylated NAs (P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs) showed excellent stability over 12 h in PBS (pH 7.4), with no significant change in particle sizes. By contrast, the particle size of B-BDT NAs (the bare NAs) immediately increased as soon as it was placed in PBS. Furthermore, the colloidal stability of NAs was also evaluated in PBS (pH 7.4) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Similarly, the PEGylated NAs (P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs) demonstrated superior colloidal stability over B-BDT NAs in FBS-containing PBS (pH 7.4) (Figure S1C). These results underscored the pivotal role of PEGylation in stabilizing the NAs by resisting the salt/protein-induced precipitation. We also examined the long-term stability of P-BDT NAs, P-BD NAs, and P-NDT NAs, as shown in Figures S1D–S1F, the particle sizes of the NAs did not undergo significant changes within 30 days, further demonstrating their excellent stability. Considering that freeze-dried products are more conducive to long-term storage and clinical use, we further carried out sample freeze-drying of P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs. As shown in Figures S1G and S1H, the particle sizes of all NAs showed no significant changes before and after lyophilization, which indicated that these NAs could be stored via lyophilization in clinical applications. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that we successfully developed an ideal antiplatelet nanomotor that possessed excellent nano-formulation engineering properties and stability.

Insights into the nanoassembly mechanism of antiplatelet nanomotor

To explore the nanoassembly mechanism, we analyzed the molecular interactions among DiR, BNN6, and TGL using the molecular docking technique. As shown in Figure 1G, hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds jointly serve as the driving forces to facilitate the stable formation of the ternary assembly. Furthermore, the molecular simulations of P-BD NAs and P-NDT NAs also revealed similar results (Figures S1I and S1J). Subsequently, these intermolecular interactions were disrupted using sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), sodium chloride (NaCl), and urea to validate the simulation results, respectively. As shown in Figure 1H, upon treatment with SDS, all these NAs experienced a sharp increase in particle size, suggesting the key contribution of hydrophobic interactions. Hydrogen bonds were also found to be involved in nanoassembly. An obvious increase in particle size of three NAs was also observed when incubated with urea. The particle size of the NAs incubated with NaCl had not undergone significant change, suggesting that the contribution of electrostatic interactions to nanoassembly was negligible. We then examined the changes in ultraviolet (UV) and fluorescence spectra that occurred after nanoassembly. As shown in Figure 1I, BNN6 Sol, BPA Sol, and TGL Sol exhibited no significant UV absorption within the range of 550–900 nm. DiR Sol displayed a distinct absorption peak at around 760 nm. Notably, there was a new shoulder peak appearing in three NAs at about 690 nm, which may be attributed to the quantum size effect caused by aggregation of DiR in water. As shown in Figure 1J, the fluorescence of DiR was nearly quenched in P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs, which could be ascribed to the aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ). The quenching of fluorescence also provided another piece of evidence confirming the formation of the NAs.

In vitro thrombus-targeting performance

We constructed artificial whole blood clots and platelet-rich clots, respectively, to verify the CREKA-enabled thrombus targeting ability of P-BDT NAs in vitro. Following incubation with DiR Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs at an equivalent DiR concentration, the fluorescence signals in the artificial clots were detected. In the whole blood clots, very bright fluorescence signals were observed in the clots treated with CREKA-modified NAs (P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs), while DiR Sol exhibited dim fluorescence under the same conditions (Figures 1K and 1L). Similarly, the CREKA-modified three NAs showed obvious fluorescence signals over DiR Sol in the platelet-rich clots (Figures S1K and S1L). Moreover, owing to the higher fibrin content in the platelet-rich clots, the fluorescence intensity of CREKA-modified NAs was stronger than that observed in the whole blood clots. This finding underscored the critical role of CREKA in fibrin-targeting accumulation, which would certainly benefit thrombus-homing drug delivery and thrombolytic therapy.

In vitro photothermal performance

We subsequently evaluated the in vitro photothermal conversion efficiency of P-BDT NAs. We first investigated the influence of varying laser power densities (0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 W cm−2) on the photothermal performance of P-BDT NAs at a fixed DiR concentration (0.196 mg/mL). As shown in Figures S2A and S2B, the temperature variation of P-BDT NAs displayed a laser-power-dependent manner. The temperature of P-BDT NAs rapidly increased to approximately 60°C and 65°C under laser irradiation at 1.5 and 2 W cm−2, respectively, which aroused our concerns regarding the biosafety of these power intensities for in vivo applications. At 0.5 W cm−2, P-BDT NAs exhibited a slow heating rate and only reached a maximum temperature of 35°C, which was inadequate to elicit effective photothermal thrombolysis. In comparison, P-BDT NAs irradiated at 1 W cm−2 demonstrated a moderate heating rate and achieved a maximum temperature of 50°C, which not only facilitates photothermal thrombolysis but also reduces safety concerns arising from local overheating. Accordingly, 1 W cm−2 was selected for all subsequent experiments. We then compared the photothermal efficiency of PBS and DiR-containing formulations under 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W cm−2, 15 min). The temperature of the PBS remained constant at 25°C throughout the irradiation period, whereas all the DiR-containing formulations exhibited a rapid temperature rise, reaching nearly 50°C within 10 min. Moreover, there was no significant difference in the temperature-rise curves of those DiR-containing formulations, indicating the trifling impact of nanoassembly and surface decoration on the photothermal effect of DiR (Figures 2A and 2B). Photothermal-triggered NO generation

We then detected the NO generation of BNN6/DiR/TGL mixed solution (BDT Sol), P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs under laser irradiation (equal to 54 μg/mL of BNN6, 1 W cm−2, 15 min). As shown in Figure S2C, P-BD NAs and P-BDT NAs exhibited comparable cumulative NO release (∼70 μM), indicating that the introduction of TGL had almost no impact on photothermal-triggered NO generation. As expected, P-NDT NAs generated negligible NO under the same conditions due to the absence of BNN6. Notably, the cumulative NO generation of P-BDT NAs (70 μM) was nearly 3.5 times higher than that of BDT Sol (20 μM), suggesting the contribution of nanoassembly to the generation of NO. Nanoassembly significantly shortened the intermolecular distance between BNN6 and DiR, which benefited DiR-to-BNN6 energy transfer and thereby enhanced laser-triggered NO generation. Moreover, the NO generation from P-BDT NAs exhibited a dependence on both laser power (0–1 W cm−2) and BNN6 concentration (25–190 nmol/mL) (Figures S2D and S2E). Interestingly, the NO generation from P-BDT NAs also exhibited a laser on/off switching pattern (Figure 2C), which emphasized the pivotal role of laser irradiation in governing NO generation. These results showed that P-BDT NAs had the ability to efficiently produce NO, which was expected to provide strong driving force for the autonomous motion of P-BDT NAs.

Autonomous motion performance

Efficient NO release enabled P-BDT NAs to perform as an NO-bubble-propelled nanomotor (Figure 2D). Herein, we explored the motility of P-BDT NAs by recording its motion trajectories under different laser conditions. As shown in Figures 2E and 2F, P-BDT NAs exhibited a power-dependent motion trait. In the absence of laser irradiation (0 W cm−2), it showed random Brownian motion with minimal diffusion distance and a slow speed of 0.6 μm/s. In contrast, as the laser power increased to 0.5 and 1 W cm−2, both the motion distance and speed of P-BDT NAs significantly increased. Notably, P-BDT NAs exposed to 1 W cm−2 exhibited more vigorous motion. Furthermore, we compared the motion performance of P-BDT NAs with that of P-BD NAs and P-NDT NAs under laser irradiation (1 W cm−2). As shown in Figures 2G and 2H, P-BD NAs and P-BDT NAs displayed similar motility, indicating a marginal impact of TGL on autonomous motion. Notably, P-NDT NAs exhibited significantly weaker motility than P-BDT NAs under the same conditions. However, due to the thermophoresis effect, P-NDT NAs demonstrated a stronger motility than the ordinary Brownian motion, with a movement speed of 2.1 μm/s (Figure 2H). Real-time motional behaviors of the three NAs were recorded in Videos S1 and S2. These results indicated that we successfully fabricated an NO-driven nanomotor with excellent motility.

Self-propelled thrombus penetration

Based on the excellent motility of P-BDT NAs, we constructed a whole blood clot to verify its thrombus penetration performance in vitro. Notably, to visualize the penetration depth, Coumarin-6 (C6) was utilized as a fluorescence indicator to label the three NAs. As shown in Figure 2I, while C6 fluorescent signals were detected in all groups at depths of 200–600 μm, the C6-positive area at different intra-thrombus depths more accurately reflected their penetration capacity. Relying mainly on photothermal permeation enhancement effect, P-NDT NAs showed a fluorescent area of only 55% of the total clot area at 440 μm depth. By contrast, P-BD NAs and P-BDT NAs integrated mechanical penetration with photothermal effects, achieving over 85% fluorescent coverage at the same depth, demonstrating superior thrombus penetration capability. Moreover, the C6 Sol stayed on the superficial thrombus regions, accounting for only 25% of the total clot area. Furthermore, we quantified the cross-sectional fluorescent intensity of each group at 440 μm depth. As shown in Figures 2J and S2F, after laser irradiation, P-NDT NAs exhibited overall stronger fluorescence signals than C6 Sol, while the fluorescence intensities of P-BDT NAs and P-BD NAs were significantly higher than those of P-NDT NAs. These results confirmed that P-BDT NAs, combining photothermal effects and mechanical propulsion, enabled TGL to be delivered deep into thrombus.

Laser irradiation/shear stress dual-responsive TGL release

In the previous study, we validated that P-BDT NAs could penetrate deep into clots (Figure 2I). Even so, thrombus therapy would still rely heavily on the timely release of TGL within clots (Figure 3A). We hypothesized that laser-induced photobleaching of DiR would facilitate TGL release (Figure 3B). To confirm this, we explored the release profiles of TGL from P-BDT NAs with or without laser irradiation (1 W cm−2). As shown in Figure 3C, P-BDT NAs showed a slow drug release without laser irradiation, with only 60% of TGL released within 12 h. In contrast, more than 90% of TGL was released from P-BDT NAs with laser irradiation at 12 h. Moreover, under laser irradiation, P-NDT NAs displayed very similar TGL release profiles to those of P-BDT NAs, indicating that laser-induced DiR photobleaching was a driving force that triggered the release of TGL. Increasing evidence shows that vascular embolism elevates the shear force at the lesion sites by 1–2 orders of magnitude,20 which also serves as a stimulus to facilitate TGL release from NAs (Figure 3B). Accordingly, we further investigated shear-stress-triggered TGL release via in vitro simulation by subjecting P-BDT NAs to intense agitation (300 rpm). As shown in Figure S2H, 90% of TGL was released from P-BDT NAs under stirring, while only 55% of TGL was released without stirring at 12 h, indicating that shear stress also plays a promoting role in TGL release. Collectively, these findings indicated that P-BDT NAs showed laser irradiation and shear stress dual-responsive TGL release features, which would enable site-specific and on-demand TGL release within the thrombi, thereby enhancing antiplatelet efficacy while minimizing off-target hemorrhage events.

In vitro antiplatelet activity

Efficient TGL release would benefit the antiplatelet action of P-BDT NAs. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that NO can also activate the soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC)/cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP)/protein kinase G (PKG) signaling axis, leading to the phosphorylation of various downstream target proteins (e.g., vasodilator-stimulated phosphoprotein [VASP]) to exert antiplatelet activities.38,39 Therefore, we hypothesized that P-BDT NAs would exhibit a TGL/NO synergistic antiplatelet effect. We then explored the in vitro antiplatelet activity of P-BDT NAs. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was prepared and pre-treated with different formulations and then activated with adenosine diphosphate (ADP). As shown in Figure 3D, laser irradiation alone and DiR Sol under laser irradiation did not exhibit antiplatelet effects. By contrast, BDT Sol (61.3%) and P-BDT NAs (74.9%) exhibited stronger antiplatelet activities than TGL Sol (52.6%) under laser irradiation, suggesting that TGL and NO worked together to exert synergistic antiplatelet effects. Furthermore, P-NDT NAs (45.3%) exhibited antiplatelet activity comparable to that of TGL Sol. P-BD NAs (25.6%) also exhibited certain antiplatelet effects owing to laser-triggered NO release. Notably, P-BDT NAs without laser irradiation (36.0%) displayed weak antiplatelet activity, due to limited TGL and NO release.

To figure out the synergistic antiplatelet mechanism of NO and TGL, we analyzed the signaling pathways through which NO and TGL acted on platelets. We found that NO exerted antiplatelet effects mainly by promoting the phosphorylation of VASP at serine 239 via the sGC/cGMP/PKG pathway.40 Meanwhile, TGL, as a P2Y12 receptor inhibitor, has been reported to block ADP/P2Y12/Gi-mediated signaling, thereby relieving the inhibition of adenylyl cyclase (AC), preserving intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) levels, and activating protein kinase A (PKA). This pathway mainly induced VASP phosphorylation at serine 157 to inhibit platelet activation.40,41 We hypothesized that NO and TGL may suppress platelet activation through distinct upstream pathways but converge on VASP phosphorylation. Furthermore, TGL has been found to reduce the level of phosphorylated protein kinase B (p-Akt) by inhibiting the ADP-induced phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) signaling pathway.42 In parallel, existing evidence confirmed that NO/cGMP/PKG signaling could also reduce the level of p-Akt by inhibiting the PI3K/Akt pathway to exert antiplatelet effects (Figure 3E).43,44,45 Accordingly, we performed western blot (WB) assays to characterize the mechanistic basis underlying the synergistic antiplatelet effect of TGL and NO by detecting the phosphorylation levels of VASP and Akt. As shown in Figures 3F and S2I, treatment with P-BDT NAs under laser irradiation significantly reduced the p-Akt levels compared with the ADP-stimulated group. In addition, the formulations with antiplatelet activity (TGL Sol, P-BD NAs, and P-NDT NAs) also downregulated platelet p-Akt level, whereas P-BDT NAs, capable of simultaneous NO and TGL release, exhibited the lowest level of p-Akt. Moreover, P-BDT NAs elevated the phosphorylation levels of VASP at both serine 157 and serine 239, exerting more potent antiplatelet effects by increasing the overall level of p-VASP in platelets (Figures S2J–S2L). At this point, P-BDT NAs had demonstrated remarkable autonomous movement capability and potent antiplatelet activity. We defined it as an “antiplatelet nanomotor” and anticipated its contribution in clinical treatment of acute arterial thrombus.

In vitro thrombolytic activity

We then evaluated the thrombolytic activity of P-BDT NAs in vitro using two artificial clot models. As shown in Figures 3G, S2M, and S2N, all whole blood clots remained intact, with low concentrations of fibrin and hemoglobin detected in the supernatant without laser irradiation. Different degrees of thrombolysis occurred when clots were exposed to DiR-containing formulations under laser irradiation (1 W cm−2, 15 min). Although TGL Sol showed good antiplatelet effects (Figure 3D), it failed to degrade preformed clots regardless of laser irradiation. DiR Sol (34.5%) and P-NDT NAs (32.7%) displayed weaker thrombolytic activity than P-BD NAs (74.1%) and P-BDT NAs (77.2%) under laser irradiation, indicating that photothermal therapy alone was less effective than combined photothermal and mechanical thrombolysis (Figure 3H). Notably, the high levels of fibrin and hemoglobin in the supernatant confirmed the excellent thrombolytic activity of P-BD NAs and P-BDT NAs under laser irradiation (Figures S2M and S2N). Additionally, we established fibrin-rich clots to investigate the in vitro thrombolytic performance of P-BDT NAs. Similar results were observed, showing that P-BDT NAs and P-BD NAs exhibited potent thrombolytic activity (Figures S2O and S2P).

We then established whole blood clots within capillary tubes and observed the complete thrombolytic length of different formulations under laser irradiation (1 W cm−2, 15 min). The distance from the initial thrombus edge to the terminal edge was measured after different treatments. As shown in Figures 3I and 3J and Video S3, groups treated with DiR Sol or P-NDT NAs exhibited incomplete thrombolysis, with only 0.3 mm boundary retraction. By contrast, P-BD NAs and P-BDT NAs achieved 1 mm regression, almost 3.5-fold longer than that of DiR Sol and P-NDT NAs. These results were consistent with our previous findings, more visually demonstrating the remarkable advantages of P-BDT NAs in thrombus recanalization.

In vivo pharmacokinetics

We then conducted pharmacokinetic experiments. Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were used to investigate the pharmacokinetics of DiR Sol, TGL Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs by detecting plasma DiR fluorescence intensity and TGL concentration at the predetermined time points. As shown in Figures 4A and S3A and Tables S3 and S4, DiR Sol and TGL Sol were rapidly cleared from the bloodstream after intravenous administration, suggesting the poor pharmacokinetic behaviors of free drugs. In contrast, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs exhibited significantly prolonged circulation time of DiR, with the AUC0–36 h increased by more than 35-fold compared to DiR Sol. Meanwhile, P-BDT NAs and P-NDT NAs also extended the plasma retention of TGL, with the AUC0–36 h increased by more than 4.6-fold compared to TGL Sol. These results suggested that formulating DiR and TGL into NAs significantly improves their in vivo delivery efficiency. Favorable pharmacokinetic profiles, together with CREKA peptide-targeting modification, will certainly facilitate the specific accumulation of NAs at the embolization sites.

In vivo antiplatelet activity

Given the strong in vitro antiplatelet effect of P-BDT NAs (Figure 3D), we further evaluated its antiplatelet activity in vivo using a classical mouse tail bleeding assay (Figure 4B). Specifically, bleeding was initiated by tail transection 2 h after intravenous administration of different formulations. As shown in Figures 4C, 4D, and 4E, rapid thrombosis occurred within 5 min in the sham group, due to the intrinsic hemostatic function of mice. The bleeding time and volume of the mice treated with TGL Sol slightly increased compared to the sham group. As expected, both P-BDT NAs and P-NDT NAs exhibited favorable antiplatelet effects in vivo, with prolonged bleeding time and increased bleeding volume following tail transection. Furthermore, the extent of bleeding prolongation followed a TGL dose-dependent pattern, especially in mice treated with P-BDT NAs and P-NDT NAs (Figures 4D and 4E). The poor antiplatelet effect of TGL Sol could be attributed to its rapid clearance from the bloodstream. In contrast, the improved pharmacokinetic profiles of P-BDT NAs and P-NDT NAs facilitated thrombus-homing TGL delivery for enhanced antiplatelet therapy in vivo. To investigate the thrombus-homing accumulation of NAs at the tail stump, we immersed the tail-amputated wounds in DiR Sol, P-NDT NAs, and P-BDT NAs for 3 min, respectively. We further assessed platelet activation in blood samples collected from mice during the tail-bleeding assay using whole-blood flow cytometry. As shown in Figures 4F and 4G, the sham group exhibited the highest level of platelet activation due to hemostasis. In contrast, treatment with TGL Sol, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs resulted in varying degrees of suppression of platelet activation. In vivo thrombolytic efficacy in a carotid artery thrombosis model

Subsequently, we employed a classic carotid artery thrombosis rat model to verify the in vivo thrombolytic efficacy of P-BDT NAs (Figure 5A). The fluorescence probe (DiR) endowed P-BDT NAs with the convenience of self-tracing delivery in vivo. Based on this, we first evaluated the specific targeting ability of P-BDT NAs between normal blood vessels and thrombotic blood vessels. As shown in Figure S3D, the DiR fluorescence signals of DiR Sol were negligible in both normal and thrombotic blood vessels due to rapid clearance. In contrast, P-BDT NAs exhibited stronger fluorescence signals in the thrombosed artery due to its improved pharmacokinetics and CREKA modification, while negligible fluorescence signals were observed in the non-thrombotic artery, indicating that P-BDT NAs could precisely target thrombus under high-shear blood flow. Additionally, we compared the fluorescence signal changes of all DiR-containing formulations over 300 min (DiR 5 mg/kg). As shown in Figures 5B and 5C, DiR Sol exhibited negligible fluorescence in the thrombi over 5 h, due to its poor pharmacokinetic profile (Figure 4A). By contrast, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs showed markedly enhanced fluorescence intensity at the thrombus sites owing to PEGylation and CREKA peptide modification on the NAs. Moreover, the fluorescence signals in thrombi displayed a peak-shaped kinetic feature (Figure 5C). The accumulation of P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs in the thrombi peaked at 120 min, whereas DiR Sol reached its maximum at 30 min. We then evaluated the photothermal performance of P-BDT NAs in vivo. As shown in Figures 5D and 5E, the clot temperature in rats receiving PBS remained constant under laser irradiation, indicating that laser irradiation alone did not cause abnormal heating in normal tissues. The clot temperature in rats receiving DiR-containing NAs rose rapidly to approximately 50°C after 5 min of laser irradiation, whereas DiR Sol showed a lower photothermal performance (∼40°C) than NAs under identical conditions. Despite comparable photothermal performance between NAs and DiR Sol in vitro (Figures 2A and 2B), the prolonged circulation time (Figure 4A) and thrombus-homing accumulation (Figures 5B and 5C) of NAs contributed to stronger photothermal performance in vivo. We then investigated the in vivo thrombolytic efficacy of P-BDT NAs. Given that laser irradiation itself might cause unexpected vessel damage, potentially interfering with the evaluation of actual thrombus treatment efficacy, we first analyzed the vascular histological alterations before and after laser irradiation. As shown in Figure S3E, there was no significant abnormality in the vascular endothelial tissues after laser irradiation (1 W cm−2, 15 min), indicating that laser irradiation itself did not cause vascular damage. We then explored the effects of laser irradiation alone on thrombolysis. As shown in Figure S3F, laser irradiation alone did not induce thrombus disruption. Subsequently, we evaluated the thrombolytic efficiency of different formulations under laser irradiation. As shown in Figures 5F, 5G, and S3G, DiR Sol + L and TGL Sol yielded low thrombolytic rates of ∼14% and ∼37%, respectively. BDT Sol (∼49%) was more effective but remained suboptimal due to rapid clearance and poor thrombus-targeting ability of free drug. By contrast, all the NAs demonstrated better thrombolytic effects via improved drug delivery and clot-targeting efficiency. Among them, both P-BD NAs (∼60%) and P-NDT NAs (∼69%) showed moderate thrombolytic efficiency, whereas P-BDT NAs achieved a striking thrombolytic rate of approximately 95% under laser irradiation. The potent activity of P-BDT NAs stems from the integration of photothermal effect, mechanical motion, on-site NO generation, and precise TGL delivery and release. Notably, P-BDT NAs without laser irradiation only reached a thrombolytic rate of ∼41%, which was attributed to the ineffective activation of photothermal/mechanical thrombolysis and minimal release of NO and TGL.

Subsequently, we assessed the biosafety, especially the potential risks arising from localized temperature elevation to ∼50°C during thrombolysis. As shown in Figure S3H, both the embolism and P-BDT NAs + L groups exhibited varying degrees of endothelial damage compared with normal vessels. Such endothelial injury was primarily induced by FeCl3 during model establishment rather than short-term local photothermal effects. Notably, the embolism group displayed compromised endothelial integrity with evident partial endothelial detachment, whereas the P-BDT NAs + L group exhibited milder endothelial injury. Moreover, CD31 and caspase-3 immunofluorescence showed continuous endothelial CD31 staining and low caspase-3 expression in normal vessels, whereas embolism group reduced CD31 continuity and increased caspase-3 signals, indicating endothelial disruption and apoptosis. P-BDT NAs + L group recovered CD31-positive endothelial integrity and lowered caspase-3 expression (Figures S3I and S3J). This recovery may be attributed to the endothelial-repairing function of NO generated from P-BDT NAs under laser irradiation. The favorable safety of P-BDT NAs was likely due to CREKA-mediated thrombus-targeting modification, which enabled P-BDT NAs to precisely accumulate at the clots rather than on the vessel wall. This design ensured that the photothermal effect generated by P-BDT NAs selectively disrupted the non-covalent connections among fibrin fibers within the thrombus, aiding in thrombolysis while avoiding damage to surrounding tissues. We further evaluated the impacts of different formulations on the coagulation system of SD rats at 24 h post-treatment. As shown in Figures 5H and 5I, compared with the sham group, thrombotic model rats exhibited a significant decrease in thrombin time (TT) and a marked elevation in D-Dimer levels, which suggested the formation of thrombus. In contrast, the rats treated with P-BDT NAs + L showed restoration of TT and D-Dimer levels to the normal range. These findings indicated that P-BDT NAs + L held great potential for efficient thrombus therapy with favorable therapeutic safety. By contrast, the rats treated with other formulations still maintained TT values below the normal range and persistently high D-Dimer levels, suggesting that these formulations failed to achieve satisfactory thrombolytic efficacy. These results fully validated that equipping nanomotors with antiplatelet properties yields prominent thrombolytic therapeutic effect.

In vivo thrombolytic efficacy in a femoral artery thrombosis model

Given that the differences in vascular caliber and local hemodynamic conditions may influence the behavior of the antiplatelet nanomotor, we further verified the effectiveness of P-BDT NAs in a femoral artery thrombosis model that closely mimics the clinically prevalent acute lower extremity arterial thrombosis. In this section, we not only validated its thrombolysis efficacy but also explored its effectiveness in preventing thrombus recurrence post-thrombolysis (Figure 6A). Similarly, we first assessed the specific thrombus-homing capacity of P-BDT NAs in this model. As shown in Figure S3K, DiR fluorescent signals were clearly detected in the right femoral artery with thrombi, whereas no DiR fluorescent signals were observed in the left femoral artery without thrombi. This result was consistent with what we observed in the carotid artery thrombus model. Additionally, we compared the fluorescence signal changes of DiR Sol and P-BDT NAs at thrombus sites over 300 min. As shown in Figures 6B and 6C, similar results to the carotid artery thrombosis model were observed. DiR Sol exhibited low fluorescence intensity in the thrombotic lesions and the fluorescence signals decreased sharply after 30 min. In contrast, P-BDT NAs showed much higher thrombus-homing accumulation than DiR Sol, with peak fluorescence intensity at the thrombus sites observed at 2 h after administration. Subsequently, we evaluated the photothermal performance of DiR Sol and P-BDT NAs under laser irradiation. As shown in Figures 6D and 6E, the temperature of clots treated with P-BDT NAs rose to approximately 50°C, whereas the temperature of DiR Sol only increased to 40°C, consistent with the previous results in the carotid artery thrombosis model. We then assessed the thrombolytic efficacy of P-BDT NAs in this model, and a direct comparison was conducted between P-BDT NAs and rt-PA, which is the first-line clinical thrombolytic agent. Since preventing thrombus recurrence is a key component of thrombus treatment, we anticipated that this antiplatelet nanomotor could effectively prevent thrombus recurrence (Figure 6H). To verify this, vessel segments after treatment were re-exposed to FeCl3 for 5 min at 2 h post-treatment with P-BDT NAs or rt-PA to simulate thrombus formation under secondary stimulation (Figure 6A). Even though rt-PA achieved thrombolytic rates over 90% (Figures 6F and 6G), subsequent FeCl3 stimulation triggered rapid thrombus recurrence, with over 85% of the vessel lumen occluded (Figures 6I and S4B). In contrast, after re-exposure to FeCl3, rats treated with P-BDT NAs maintained a minimal thrombus occlusion area (∼25%). The excellent thrombus recurrence prevention of P-BDT NAs should be attributed to TGL-mediated antiplatelet action and the dual role of NO in inhibiting platelet activation and repairing the vascular endothelium. Following re-exposure to FeCl3, we assessed the coagulation profiles of rats receiving different treatments. Notably, since rt-PA had a significant effect on fibrinogen (FIB), in this model, we further detected FIB levels to compare the differences between P-BDT NAs + L and rt-PA. As shown in Figures 6J–6L, the coagulation indicators (TT, D-Dimer, and FIB) of the rats treated with P-BDT NAs remained comparable to the sham group, indicating a low risk of thrombus reformation. In contrast, the rats receiving rt-PA showed a significant reduction in FIB. These could be mainly attributed to the rt-PA-induced systemic plasmin generation and fibrinogenolysis, while the reduction of FIB further prolonged TT. Moreover, the rats receiving rt-PA also showed a marked elevation in D-Dimer. These findings indicated that rt-PA treatment could sustain systemic fibrinolysis activation, yet this effect was insufficient to prevent arterial thrombus recurrence driven primarily by platelet aggregation. Moreover, these results also indicated the potential bleeding risk of rt-PA. By contrast, P-BDT NAs showed better efficacy and safety than rt-PA in the prevention and treatment of acute arterial thrombus.

Safety evaluation

We further conducted a thorough safety evaluation of P-BDT NAs in vitro and in vivo. We first evaluated the potential impact of P-BDT NAs on red blood cells. As shown in Figures S4C–S4F, hemolysis was not observed with any treatment, indicating their good biocompatibility and suitability for intravenous injections. Next, we investigated whether DiR Sol, BDT Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs did harm to HUVEC or RAW 264.7 under laser irradiation. As shown in Figures S4G and S4H, at DiR concentrations of 0–20 μg/mL and after laser irradiation (1 W cm−2, 5 min), none of the approaches affected HUVEC and RAW 264.7 viability, demonstrating that P-BDT NAs was safe for vascular endothelial tissues.

In the carotid artery thrombosis model, after intravenous injection with different formulations for 24 h, we measured relevant biochemical indicators (ALT, AST, BUN, and Creatinine) of the test rats. As shown in Figures S5A and S5B, the indicators of the rats treated with P-BDT NAs showed no significant abnormalities, indicating good biocompatibility. Concurrently, the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were also collected for H&E staining and microscopic analysis to evaluate potential organ damage. As shown in Figure S5C, the H&E staining of rats treated with P-BDT NAs showed no significant pathological changes. Overall, these findings highlighted the excellent safety of P-BDT NAs in vivo. We also examined the possible pathological changes in rats after 14 days of treatment with P-BDT NAs to evaluate their long-term safety. As shown in Figures S5D–S5F, no significant changes were observed in the H&E staining sections of the main organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) and the liver-kidney functions compared with the rats that did not receive any treatment. These results demonstrated that P-BDT NAs did not cause systemic toxicity over a relatively long period of time.

Discussion

Timely thrombus removal and effective prevention of recurrence are the top priorities in the treatment of arterial thrombus. However, current drug therapies struggle to balance therapeutic benefits with bleeding risks. Emerging photothermal NO-driven nanomotors demonstrate promising clinical translation potential but are limited by short action duration and incomplete clot ablation. Herein, we proposed to integrate the antiplatelet drug therapy with the physical thrombolytic effects of nanomotors and constructed an antiplatelet nanomotor based on carrier-free self-assembling nanotechnology, which ingeniously integrated the long-lasting therapeutic effect of the antiplatelet drug (TGL) and the instantaneous strong thrombolytic effect of the nanomotor. Moreover, it enabled precise targeted delivery, abundant enrichment, on-demand release, and deep penetration of TGL at the thrombus sites while exerting its physical thrombolytic effect. This is an attempt to integrate nonpharmaceutical modality with drug therapy, successfully filling the gaps of traditional drugs and nanomotors. The designed antiplatelet nanomotor precisely meets the clinical requirements for rapid recanalization, recurrence prevention, and high safety in the treatment of acute arterial thrombosis. Such a functional nanomotor (P-BDT NAs) provides a possibility for breaking through the clinical treatment difficulties of arterial thrombosis in a facile and feasible way.

Limitations of the study

While the P-BDT NAs exhibited potent thrombolytic and antiplatelet effects in two rat models of arterial thrombosis, further validation in large animals and clinically relevant thrombosis models would provide more compelling evidence for its clinical translation. In addition, although satisfactory therapeutic efficacy was achieved under laboratory laser irradiation conditions, the lack of clinically available laser equipment remains a main challenge in clinical translation and application.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture

Cell lines were purchased from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Science (Shanghai, China). All the cell lines were authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling by the provider, and were routinely tested to confirm the absence of mycoplasma contamination. HUVEC and RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic solution. Cell cultures were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Animal

Animals were purchased from Changsheng biotechnology co., Ltd (Shenyang, China). All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Shenyang Pharmaceutical University and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shenyang Pharmaceutical University (Protocol Number: SYPU-IACUC-S2025-0716-202 and SYPU-IACUC-S2026-0306-201). Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (6–8 weeks, 180–220 g, male) were raised for pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamics study. Kunming (KM) mice (10–11 weeks, 20–25 g, female) were raised for antiplatelet study in vivo. SD rats and KM mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in a regulated environment. The housing parameters were set as follows: temperature of 22 ± 2°C, relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. All animals had free access to standard rodent diet and autoclaved water.

Note on sex of animals

As single-sex cohorts were used for each experiment, a formal analysis of the influence of sex on the reported outcomes was not performed. This represents a limitation to the generalizability of the findings.

Method details

Uncovering the optimal dose ratio of antiplatelet nanomotor

To explore the optimal ratio between the DiR-BNN6 and TGL in the antiplatelet nanomotor, we priorly regarded the co-assembly of DiR-BNN6 as a propulsion module, and then further explored the optimal co-assembly ratio between it and TGL. In brief, our approach was firstly based on a mass ratio of DiR-BNN6 to TGL of 1:1 and a total concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. Based on this premise, we screened the molar ratio of DiR to BNN6 (3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, 1:3). Subsequently, under the determined ratio of DiR to BNN6, we further adjusted the mass ratio of DiR-BNN6 to TGL (3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, 1:3), to obtain the optimal prescription for the ternary co-assembly. Specifically, DiR, BNN6, and TGL were all prepared into solutions (10 mg/mL). Then, according to the proportional relationship between DiR, BNN6, and TGL in the prescription, a 100 μL ternary mixed solution was prepared. Subsequently, this mixed solution was uniformly dropped into deionized water (2 mL, 3 min, 800 rpm) to get B-BDT NAs. By adjusting the proportional relationship among the above-mentioned three compounds, and characterizing them by Zetasizer (Nano ZS, Malvern, UK) to get their Size and PDI as indicators, respectively, the optimal prescription of B-BDT NAs was found.

Preparation and characterization of nanoassemblies

Through systematic formulation screening, we ultimately ensured the optimal co-assembly molar ratio of DiR to BNN6 within the antiplatelet nanomotor was precisely 1:1, and the optimal mass ratio between DiR-BNN6 and TGL was 1:1. To further improve the colloidal stability of B-BDT NAs and enable it to target at thrombus, a mixed solution consisting of DSPE-PEG2K (15 wt. %) and DSPE-PEG2K-CREKA (10 wt. %) was added dropwise to B-BDT NAs (800 rpm, 2 min). Subsequently, the containing organic solvents were eliminated via vacuum distillation at 32°C, thereby yielding P-BDT NAs. Under identical preparation conditions, we prepared a TGL-loaded non-nanomotor P-NDT NAs by substituting BNN6 with the non-NO donor BPA, and a TGL-free nanomotor P-BD NAs to highlight the advancement of P-BDT NAs. The characteristics of these NAs were characterized by Zetasizer (Nano ZS, Malvern, UK) and the morphological features of these NAs were also characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Hitachi, HT7700, Japan).

The drug loading rate (DLR, %) was calculated using the following formula:DLR(%)=LoadeddrugTotalweightofthenanomedicines×100%

Stability of colloidal dispersions

To evaluate the colloidal stability of the fabricated NAs, 1 mL aliquots of B-BDT NAs, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs (equal to 0.196 mg/mL of DiR) were respectively added to 9 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) and 9 mL of PBS added with 10% (v/v) FBS. These mixtures were then incubated in a shaker for a specific duration (37°C). At 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h time-points, the particle sizes were characterized by Zetasizer (Nano ZS, Malvern, UK).

Long-term stability

P-BDT NAs, P-BD NAs, and P-NDT NAs (equal to 0.196 mg/mL of DiR) were stored at 4°C in the dark. The particle sizes of the above NAs were measured by Zetasizer (Nano ZS, Malvern, UK) at 1, 15, 30 days, respectively.

Ternary co-assembly simulation

The intermolecular forces among DiR, BNN6, and TGL in P-BDT NAs were analyzed through the molecular docking simulation platform (Autodock Vina program). Moreover, the forces were further verified by using force-breaking media (SDS, NaCl, Urea, 10 mM). The specific steps were as follows: 1 mL of the prepared formulation was respectively diluted in 9 mL of the corresponding force-breaking media and co-incubated for a certain period. Supsequently, the particle size of NAs was monitored for changes after co-incubation by Zetasizer (Nano ZS, Malvern, UK). The intermolecular forces existing in the P-BD NAs and P-NDT NAs were also analyzed through the molecular docking simulation platform (Autodock Vina program) and further verified by using force-breaking media (SDS, NaCl, Urea, 10 mM) (n = 3).

Ultraviolet and fluorescence spectra

A Varioskan microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA) was applied to obtain the ultraviolet absorption spectra and fluorescence emission spectra of DiR Sol, BNN6 Sol, TGL Sol, BPA Sol, P-BD NAs, P-NDT NAs, and P-BDT NAs. The detection wavelength range for the ultraviolet absorption spectra spanned from 550 to 900 nm. Regarding the fluorescence emission spectra, an excitation wavelength of 650 nm was employed, and the detection wavelength range for fluorescence emission extended from 700 to 900 nm. The equivalent concentration of DiR was 0.196 mg/mL, the concentration of BNN6 was 0.054 mg/mL, and the concentration of TGL was 0.25 mg/mL.

In vitro thrombus-targeting capacity

The artificial whole blood and platelet-rich clot models were employed to verify the in vitro thrombus-targeting capacity of the NAs conferred by the modification of DSPE-PEG2K-CREKA. Firstly, we collected fresh blood samples from SD rats. After that, 150 μL of the fresh blood sample needed to be pipetted into 96-well plates immediately with CaCl2 (0.3 M) and thrombin (0.1 U/μL) and incubated at 37°C for 2 h until whole-blood thrombus clots were achieved. Platelet-rich clots were prepared from SD rat blood. Following low-speed centrifugation (1000 rpm, 10 min), the isolated PRP (100 μL) was activated with CaCl2 (2.5 mM) and thrombin (1 U/mL), then incubated at 37°C for 1 h to complete clot formation. After that, the constructed clots were co-incubated continually with 50 μL of PBS, DiR Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs (equal to 0.196 mg/mL of DiR) respectively for 15 min. After the incubation, washing the whole-blood thrombus clots three times with PBS (pH 7.4) and detecting the fluorescence signals of the clots in each group by the in vivo IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer) (n = 3).

In vitro photothermal effect

P-BDT NAs (equivalent to 0.196 mg/mL of DiR) were exposed to 808 nm laser irradiation at various power densities (0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 W cm−2) for 15 min to determine the appropriate irradiation condition. After that, PBS (pH 7.4), DiR Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs (all equivalent to 0.196 mg/mL of DiR) were exposed to 808 nm laser irradiation for 15 min (1 W cm−2). During irradiation, the laser beam was positioned perpendicular to the sample surface with a fixed distance of 5 cm between the laser source and the target, and the spot size was adjusted to 1 cm2. An infrared thermal imager (Fotric 226, China) was used to continuously monitor the variation of the sample temperature during the 15 min. Time-temperature curves were plotted based on the recorded data (n = 3).

In vitro laser-triggered NO release

NO-generating capacity of DiR Sol, BDT Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs (equal to 0.196 mg/mL of DiR and 0.054 mg/mL BNN6) was quantitatively analyzed using the NO detection kit (Griess assay). Specifically, 200 μL of each sample to be tested was placed into 96-well plates and exposed to 808 nm laser (1 W cm−2) for 15 min. In accordance with the instructions of the detection kit, the detection reagents were added strictly, and after a certain reaction time, measuring the absorbance at each sample well at a specific absorption wavelength using the Vrioskan microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA). The actual concentration of NO produced by the sample to be tested was then obtained by referring to the standard curve. Through the same detection method, the effects of P-BDT NAs at different BNN6 concentrations (equal to 25, 50, 100, 190 nmol/mL of BNN6, respectively) and different laser power density (0, 0.5, 1 W cm−2) on the NO production could also be detected. In addition, by controlling the presence or absence of laser, the total amount of NO generated could be used to demonstrate that laser served as a switch for triggering the release of NO from the P-BDT NAs (n = 3). During irradiation, the laser beam was positioned perpendicular to the sample surface with a fixed distance of 5 cm between the laser source and the target, and the spot size was adjusted to 1 cm2.

Motion evaluation of clot-piercing antiplatelet nanomotor

The mobility of NAs triggered by 808 nm laser irradiation was observed and documented at ×40 using a Nikon fluorescence microscope. First, the prepared P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs (equal to 0.196 mg/mL DiR and 0.054 mg/mL BNN6) were diluted 10-fold with deionized water to ensure an appropriate number of nanoparticles in the field of view. Then, drop 100 μL of the diluted P-BDT NAs onto the glass slide and keep the bright-field spotlight of the microscope on to provide sufficient light for observing the motion state of the NAs. After the NAs observed under the eyepiece reached a stable Brownian motion state, using 808 nm laser with different power densities (0, 0.5, 1 W cm−2) to trigger the motion of NAs, and the movement tracks of NAs under different laser power density were recorded to verify the correlation between motility and laser power density. Meanwhile, using the same shooting method, the motility of P-BD NAs and P-NDT NAs under 808 nm laser with a power density of 1 W cm−2 was recorded respectively to illustrate the significant kinematic differences between nanomotors and non-nanomotors (n = 10). During irradiation, the laser beam was positioned perpendicular to the sample surface with a fixed distance of 5 cm between the laser source and the target, and the spot size was adjusted to 1 cm2.

Deep thrombus penetration with photothermal/mechanical effect

Artificial whole-blood thrombus clots were adopted to explore the capacity of the P-BDT NAs to penetrate and retain in the deep part of the thrombus clots. Fresh blood was collected from SD rats. Immediately, 15 μL of fresh blood was placed in the EP tubes (200 μL) and kept them at 37°C for 4 h to obtain small whole-blood thrombus clots. Since DiR is prone to fluorescence bleaching under laser irradiation and is not suitable as a fluorescent marker to indicate the penetration depth of the NAs in the thrombus clots. C6, a more stable fluorescent substance, was selected to label the NAs and indicate the delivery depth of the nanomotor in the clots through the fluorescence of C6. The C6-labeled P-BDT NAs were prepared according to the following method: First, prepare a C6 solution (1 mg/mL). Then, 25 μL of this solution was thoroughly mixed with 50 μL of the BDT mixed solution, and then the mixed system was uniformly dropped into 1 mL of deionized water (800 rpm, 3 min) to obtain the C6-labeled P-BDT NAs. Through a similar preparation method, C6-labeled P-BD NAs, P-NDT NAs, and C6 Sol were also obtained (equal to 0.196 mg/mL DiR, 0.25 mg/mL TGL and 25 μg/mL C6). The thrombus clots were respectively incubated in 3 mL of C6 Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs for 20 min, and then exposed them to 808 nm laser (1 W cm−2) for 15 min. During irradiation, the laser beam was positioned perpendicular to the sample surface with a fixed distance of 5 cm between the laser source and the target, and the spot size was adjusted to 1 cm2. After the laser irradiation, the clots were fetched out and washed three times with PBS (pH 7.4). The C6 fluorescence signals at different depths inside the thrombus were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (C2, Nikon, Japan) (n = 3).

Laser/shear stress-triggered TGL release

The dialysis method was applied to explore the release behavior of TGL from both P-BDT NAs and P-NDT NAs with a release medium consisting of PBS (pH 7.4) containing 30% (v/v) ethanol. First, 1 mL of P-BDT NAs and P-NDT NAs, each containing 0.25 mg/mL of TGL, were prepared. For P-BDT NAs, the samples were placed in dialysis bags under two conditions: with laser irradiation (808 nm, 1 W cm−2, 15 min) and without laser irradiation. After that, immerse them in 30 mL of release medium and incubated at 37°C, respectively. Similarly, P-NDT NAs underwent the same operation before being encapsulated in dialysis bags and incubated under identical conditions. To monitor the TGL release process, samples were taken at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h of incubation to measure the TGL concentration in the medium. During irradiation, the laser beam was positioned perpendicular to the sample surface with a fixed distance of 5 cm between the laser source and the target, and the spot size was adjusted to 1 cm2. The shear stress-triggered release of TGL was investigated using the same method, but applying shear stress with a stir bar inside the dialysis bag (300 rpm) instead of laser irradiation. The quantification of TGL concentration was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) under the following conditions: C18 reverse-phase column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm), mobile phase of acetonitrile: water (60:40, v/v), flow rate of 1 mL/min, and UV detection wavelength of 254 nm.

In vitro antiplatelet activity

Fresh blood was collected from the orbital venous plexus of SD rats and loaded in EP tubes containing 3.8% sodium citrate. The blood was then centrifuged (1000 rpm, 10 min) to collect platelet-rich plasma (PRP). Platelet-poor plasma (PPP) was collected using higher centrifugation conditions (3000 rpm, 10 min). The different types of plasma and DMSO were placed in 96-well plates and incubated on a shaker at 37°C for 5 min. Then, 100 μL of the prepared DiR Sol, TGL Sol, BDT Sol, P-BD NAs, P-NDT NAs, and P-BDT NAs (with TGL equivalent concentration of 0.25 mg/mL and DiR of 0.196 mg/mL) were mixed with PRP (150 μL). The mixed system was irradiated (808 nm, 1 W cm−2, 15 min), and its ultraviolet absorption at 570 nm was recorded after irradiation. During irradiation, the laser beam was positioned perpendicular to the sample surface with a fixed distance of 5 cm between the laser source and the target, and the spot size was adjusted to 1 cm2. Finally, 10 μL of 20 μM ADP was added to trigger platelet aggregation, incubated at 37°C for 1 h, and its absorbance was detected at 570 nm. Using the absorbance of PPP as the background value, the inhibitory effect of the drug on platelets was calculated based on the variation in absorbance between PRP before and after drug administration. DMSO served as the baseline. The formula for antiplatelet rates is as follows:Antiplateletrates(%)=AbsorbanceofPRPafterstimulation-AbsorbanceofPPPAbsorbanceofPRPbeforestimulation-AbsorbanceofPPP×100%

Synergistic antiplatelet mechanism of TGL and NO

Fresh blood was collected from SD rats (6–8 weeks, 180–220 g, male) into anticoagulant tubes containing 3.8% sodium citrate. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) was isolated by centrifugation (1000 rpm, 10 min) at 25°C, and the upper PRP fraction was carefully aspirated to avoid contamination with underlying leukocytes and erythrocytes. PRP samples were pre-treated with TGL Sol, P-BD NAs + L, P-NDT NAs + L, or P-BDT NAs + L, respectively (with TGL 0.25 mg/mL and DiR 0.196 mg/mL). During irradiation, the laser beam was positioned perpendicular to the sample surface with a fixed distance of 5 cm between the laser source and the target, and the spot size was adjusted to 1 cm2. Subsequently, platelet aggregation was induced by the addition of ADP (10 μL, 20 μM), and the group treated with ADP alone was set as the control group. Following treatment, platelets were pelleted by high-speed centrifugation (4000 rpm, 10 min), and the supernatant was discarded. The platelet pellet was gently washed 2–3 times with calcium-free buffer via centrifugation (4000 rpm, 10 min) to remove contaminating plasma proteins and residual blood cells, and then resuspended to obtain highly purified platelets. Purified platelets were lysed, and the phosphorylation levels of vasodilator-stimulated phosphoprotein (VASP) and protein kinase B (Akt) in platelets from different treatment groups were detected by Western blot analysis.

In vitro thrombolytic efficacy

The thrombolytic effect of P-BDT NAs in vitro was investigated using the whole blood clot model. Fresh blood was collected from SD rats (6–8 weeks, 180–220 g, male). Subsequently, 50 μL of fresh blood was added into EP tubes (500 μL) without antithrombin and kept them at 37°C for 4 h to obtain whole blood clots. Thereafter, the clots were respectively added to 3 mL of PBS (pH 7.4), DiR Sol, TGL Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs (equal to 0.196 mg/mL of DiR and 0.25 mg/mL TGL). To verify that the self-propelled nanomotor requires laser triggering to achieve effective in vitro thrombolytic effects, it was necessary to further set up an irradiated group and a non-irradiated group for differentiation. For the irradiated group, after placing the clots into the preparation, the clots were exposed to 808 nm laser (1 W cm−2) for 15 min, and the state of the clots after irradiation was recorded with a camera. During irradiation, the laser beam was positioned perpendicular to the sample surface with a fixed distance of 5 cm between the laser source and the target, and the spot size was adjusted to 1 cm2. After the experiment, the supernatants of the irradiated and non-irradiated groups were collected. The absorbance of fibrin and hemoglobin in the supernatant was detected at 415 nm and 540 nm respectively using Varioskan microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA) to reflect the thrombolytic effect in vitro. Before the formal experiment, the thrombus clots used in the experiment should be weighed. The thrombus clots after thrombolysis should also be promptly taken out and weighed, and the thrombolytic rate was calculated accordingly (n = 3).

Furthermore, whole blood clots were established in capillaries (inner diameter: 0.9–1.1 mm) using a prior method. Following this, DiR Sol, P-NDT NAs, P-BD NAs, or P-BDT NAs (equal to 0.196 mg/mL of DiR) were introduced to the opposite side of the capillary. The extent of complete thrombus dissolution was then measured under 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W cm−2).

The formula for thrombolytic rates is as follows

Thrombolyticrates(%)=Weightofclotbeforetreatment-WeightofclotaftertreatmentWeightofclotbeforetreatment×100%

Animal studies

All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Shenyang Pharmaceutical University and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shenyang Pharmaceutical University.

Pharmacokinetics

DiR Sol, TGL Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs (DiR 5 mg/kg, TGL 6.4 mg/mL) were injected intravenously into SD rats (6–8 weeks, 180–220 g, male, n = 5). After that, collecting blood from SD rats at pre-planned time intervals (0.05, 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, and 36 h) and putting into EP tubes (500 μL) containing heparin. Subsequently, relevant blood cells were excluded by centrifugation (8000 rpm, 5 min), and the plasma containing DiR or TGL was obtained. DiR or TGL in the plasma was extracted using the protein precipitation method. The fluorescence intensity of DiR (Ex/Em = 748/790 nm) was measured using Varioskan microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA), and the content of DiR in SD rats at different times was obtained. TGL was detected using UPLC-MS-MS (AB SCIEX, USA). Finally, the pharmacokinetic parameters of DiR and TGL were analyzed using DAS 2.1.1 software.

In vivo antiplatelet activity

Kunming (KM) mice (10–11 weeks, 20–25 g, female) were intravenously injected with TGL Sol, P-BDT NAs, or P-NDT NAs at an equivalent TGL dose of 0.6 mg/kg. 2 h post-injection, approximately 1 cm of the tail tip was amputated and immersed in a tube containing saline. The bleeding time and blood loss were recorded. Additionally, the antiplatelet effects of the different formulations were evaluated at higher equivalent TGL doses (0.75 and 0.9 mg/kg).

Blood was collected from KM mice by tail amputation into EP tubes containing 3.8% sodium citrate as an anticoagulant and platelet activation was analyzed by whole-blood flow cytometry. In brief, whole blood was incubated with PE-conjugated anti-CD62P antibody for 15 min in the dark. Subsequently, the reaction was stopped by addition of 1 mL 1% paraformaldehyde, followed by fixation at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. After centrifugation (3000 rpm) for 5 min, the supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS for flow cytometric acquisition (BD FACSCelesta, USA). At least 10,000 CD61-positive platelets were collected per sample, and the percentage of CD62P-positive platelets was used to quantify platelet activation. All experiments were performed within 4 h after blood collection, and the ambient temperature was strictly maintained at 22°C–25°C to prevent spontaneous platelet activation.

Arterial thrombus fluorescence imaging

The artery embolism model of SD rats (6–8 weeks, 180–220 g, male) induced by 10% FeCl3 was adopted to explore the targeting and accumulation behaviors of the NAs modified with DSPE-PEG2K-CREKA at the carotid or femoral artery thrombosis model in vivo. First, the SD rats received intraperitoneal injection of 3% pentobarbital for anesthesia (30 mg/kg) and then were fixed on the operating table to induce a carotid or femoral artery embolism modal. Briefly, a small incision was made between the neck and chest of the rat, and the neck skin and muscles were dissected with blunt forceps. After the carotid artery was exposed, it was incubated with a filter paper soaked in FeCl3 for 5 min to induce the carotid artery thrombus. For the femoral artery thrombosis model, the femoral artery was exposed via the anteromedial thigh triangle, and thrombosis was subsequently induced using an identical approach. To verify the targeting capacity of P-BDT NAs toward both acute carotid and femoral arterial thrombus, bilateral carotid arteries and bilateral femoral arteries were separately isolated from rats, with the right artery in each pair used to induce thrombosis and the left artery serving as a normal control. After intravenous administration of DiR Sol and P-BDT NAs (DiR 5 mg/kg) via the tail vein, the fluorescence signals of DiR in bilateral carotid and femoral arteries were detected by the in vivo IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer) at the specified time points (0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 h) after administration.

After that, the targeting accumulation of DiR Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs in acute carotid or femoral arterial thrombus was also investigated, respectively. DiR fluorescence at the embolism sites in rats was quantitatively detected by the in vivo IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer) at the specified time points (0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 h) after administration (n = 5).

Photothermal effect on the site of artery embolism

The carotid or femoral artery embolism model of SD rats (6–8 weeks, 180–220 g, male) induced by 10% FeCl3 was used to investigate the photothermal effect generated by NAs at the embolism site under 808 nm laser irradiation. The artery embolism model of SD rats was constructed according to the mentioned method. PBS, DiR Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs were intravenously injected into the test rats (DiR 5 mg/kg). According to the time when the maximum accumulation concentration of the solution or NAs at the embolism site was shown by fluorescence imaging in vivo, the embolized blood vessels were promptly exposed to 808 nm laser (1 W cm−2) for 15 min. During irradiation, the laser beam was positioned perpendicular to the blood vessels with a fixed distance of 5 cm between the laser source and the target, and the spot size was adjusted to 1 cm2. The temperature at the embolism site was continuously monitored and documented using infrared thermal imaging camera (Fotric 266, China) (n = 5).

In vivo thrombus treatment efficacy in arterial embolism models

For carotid artery embolism model, the SD rats (6–8 weeks, 180–220 g, male) were first subjected to the modeling procedure, and then the modeled SD rats were divided into 9 groups at random (n = 5). The groups were set as follows: (I) Sham group; (II) Embolism group; (III) DiR Sol/L; (IV) TGL Sol; (V) BDT Sol/L; (VI) P-BD NAs/L; (VII) P-BDT NAs/L; (VIII) P-BDT NAs; (IX) P-NDT NAs/L. After the successful establishment of the carotid artery embolism model, preparations were injected intravenously into the test rats (DiR 5 mg/kg, BNN6 1.38 mg/kg and TGL 6.4 mg/kg). The optimal irradiation time for each group was determined based on the previous experimental results. At the determined time, the local embolized blood vessels were exposed to 808 nm laser (1 W cm−2) for 15 min. During irradiation, the laser beam was positioned perpendicular to the blood vessels with a fixed distance of 5 cm between the laser source and the target, and the spot size was adjusted to 1 cm2. After 2 h of irradiation, the rats were euthanized, and the blood vessels at the embolism site were removed for H&E staining to compare the actual thrombolytic effects among different treatment groups. Additionally, H&E staining and immunofluorescence staining (CD31 and caspase-3) was performed on normal vascular tissues and those treated with P-BDT NAs + L to evaluate the vascular status after treatment. Meanwhile, using Fiji ImageJ 1.54 to quantitatively analysis the carotid artery embolism area. Moreover, fresh blood was collected from the test rats 24 h after the treatment, centrifuged (8000 rpm, 5 min), and the coagulation parameters including TT and D-Dimer were subsequently determined using commercial assay kits.

For femoral artery embolism model, the SD rats (6–8 weeks, 180–220 g, male) were first subjected to the modeling procedure, and then the modeled SD rats were divided into 4 groups at random (n = 5). The groups were set as follows: (I) Sham group; (II) Embolism group; (III) P-BDT NAs/L; (IV) rt-PA. After the successful establishment of the femoral artery embolism model, preparations were injected intravenously into the test rats (DiR 5 mg/kg, rt-PA 0.9 mg/kg). Subsequently, the thrombotic site was subjected to laser irradiation using the same strategy. After 2 h, the treated blood vessel was harvested for staining analysis to evaluate the intravascular thrombosis. Furthermore, to assess the ability of P-BDT NAs/L to prevent thrombosis recurrence, the treated vessels from the P-BDT NAs/L and rt-PA groups were re-exposed to FeCl3 2 h post-treatment. These arteries were then collected and stained to analyze the thrombus area within the vascular lumen (n = 5). Moreover, fresh blood was collected from the test rats 24 h after the re-exposure to FeCl3, centrifuged (8000 rpm, 5 min), and the coagulation parameters including TT, D-Dimer and FIB were subsequently determined using commercial assay kits.

Safety evaluation

A red blood cell (RBC) suspension was prepared from rats (50 μL of RBCs dispersed in 1 mL PBS) for hemolysis assessment in vitro. PBS, TGL Sol, DiR Sol, BDT Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs (equal to DiR of 0.196 mg/mL and TGL of 0.25 mg/mL, 50 μL each) were separately incubated with the RBC suspension (1 mL) at 37°C for 3 h, with or without 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W cm−2, 5 min). After centrifugation (1800 rpm, 5 min), the supernatant was obtained, and the ultraviolet absorbance of hemoglobin at 540 nm was measured using the Vrioskan microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA) (n = 3). PBS served as negative control, while pure water was used as positive control.

Additionally, we evaluated the cytotoxicity of the formulations in vitro. HUVEC or RAW 264.7 (8 × 103 cells/well) were placed into 96-well plates and cultured for 12 h. Subsequently, DiR Sol, BDT Sol, P-BD NAs, P-BDT NAs, and P-NDT NAs (with equivalent DiR concentrations of 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 μg/mL) were added to the HUVEC or RAW 264.7. After 4 h of incubation, selected groups were subjected to 808 nm laser irradiation (1 W cm−2, 5 min), while others were not irradiated. Following treatment, the cells were continuously cultured in the dark for 44 h. Finally, 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL in PBS) were added to per well. After 4 h of incubation at 37°C, the supernatant was removed, and another 200 μL of DMSO was added. The absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a Varioskan microplate reader (n = 3) (Thermo Scientific, USA).

In the carotid artery thrombosis model, one and fourteen days after thrombolytic treatment, the tested rats were euthanized, and the plasma and major tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were collected. The concentrations of ALT, AST, BUN, and creatinine in the plasma were detected using corresponding ELISA kits. Additionally, the major organs were subjected to H&E staining to evaluate pathological changes.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All data in this study were shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software and the significant differences between groups were analyzed by using Student’s t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The manifestation of the significant differences was as follows: ns represents p > 0.05 (no significance), ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
