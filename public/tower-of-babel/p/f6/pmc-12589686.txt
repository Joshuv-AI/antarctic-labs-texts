Concentration-dependent effects of modified triple antibiotic paste on human periodontal ligament stem cells and antibacterial efficacy: an in vitro study
Authors: Shuya Liu, Zhuoyao Liang, Jiang Guo, Qian Zeng, Jiacheng Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntracanal medication is critical for regenerative endodontic procedures (REPs). The purpose of this study was to examine the effects of modified triple antibiotic paste (mTAP) on the migration, proliferation, and osteogenic/cementogenic differentiation of human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) as well as its antibacterial effectiveness against Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis). hPDLSCs were treated with mTAP across various concentration gradients. hPDLSCs were treated with mTAP across concentration gradients. Cell Counting Kit-8, wound-healing assay, alizarin red staining, real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction, and western blot were used to evaluate cell functions. The antibacterial efficacy was evaluated using microdilution assays and static biofilm models. The results demonstrated concentration-dependent effects on hPDLSCs functionality. At low and medium concentrations (3.9, 15.6, and 62.5 μg/mL), mTAP had no significant effect or slightly promoted hPDLSCs migration and proliferation, whereas high concentrations (≥ 250.0 μg/mL) inhibited these functions. With increasing mTAP concentration, mineralized nodule formation and osteogenic differentiation expression first increased and then decreased, whereas cementoblastic differentiation expression gradually increased. Against E. faecalis, mTAP exhibited a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of 1 μg/mL and a minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of 128 μg/mL. mTAP inhibited 76.33 ± 0.89% early-stage biofilm formation at 32 μg/mL and eradicated ≥ 72.53 ± 1.15% 14-day mature biofilms at concentrations ≥ 125 μg/mL. Balancing biocompatibility and antibacterial efficacy, the optimal concentration range of mTAP during REPs was 128–250 μg/mL, which provides a robust basis for the future clinical application and formulation of safe and effective intracanal medications.Keywords: Regenerative endodontic procedures, Modified triple antibiotic paste, Biocompatibility, Antibacterial activity

Introduction

With the wide application of stem cell-based regenerative medicine, regenerative endodontic procedures (REPs) have emerged as a new treatment option for managing apical periodontitis in immature permanent teeth. As a biologically driven approach, REPs aim to regenerate the pulp-dentin complex by replacing lost tissues, such as dentin and root structures, through the recruitment and differentiation of stem cells (Li et al. 2024). The success rates of REPs can range from 83.3% to 100%, according to clinical research (Li et al. 2024; Lin et al. 2017; Abu et al. 2021). Radiographic evidence also showed further root development and complete recovery of clinical symptoms (Abu et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2024a).

To create a favorable microenvironment for the migration, proliferation, and differentiation of stem cells in REPs, microbial control in the root canal is essential (Wei et al. 2022). Research has demonstrated that residual infections may compromise further root development after REPs, underscoring the necessity of complete canal disinfection (Leveque et al. 2024). Low-concentration triple antibiotic paste (TAP) is advised as intra-canal medication in REPs by the American Association of Endodontics (AAE, 2021). However, the minocycline component in TAP causes tooth discoloration and exhibits anti-angiogenic properties which may compromise the efficacy of REPs (Dubey et al. 2019; Albuquerque et al. 2015). To mitigate these effects, a modification method is to replace minocycline with clindamycin, combining with metronidazole and ciprofloxacin, called modified TAP (mTAP). Clindamycin is effective against most gram-positive aerobic bacteria and anaerobes causing root canal infections, while clindamycin-mTAP exhibits potent antibacterial efficacy with less cytotoxicity and reduced interference with angiogenesis compared with minocycline formulations (Dubey et al. 2019; Rafatjou et al. 2022; Raddall et al. 2022; Cunha et al. 2021) Therefore, the application of mTAP with clindamycin instead of minocycline has been recommended (AAE 2021). However, medicaments within the root canal may be squeezed and spread to the periapical tissues through the apical foramina, resulting in direct contact with the resident stem cells (Ayoub et al. 2020). There is currently little data on how clindamycin-mTAP affects the migration, differentiation, and proliferation of stem cells.

Two available strategies for REPs are cell transplantation and cell homing (Shi et al. 2025). Cell transplantation is currently in the clinical trial stage, whereas cell homing, which attracts endogenous cells to the root canal system to promote tissue regeneration, has wider adoption in clinical practice (Kim et al. 2025). During cell homing-based REPs, stem cells are primarily derived from several sources, such as, stem cells of apical papilla (SCAP), dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), and periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs). Under certain conditions, these stem cells can multiply and differentiate into odontogenic cells (Raddall et al. 2022). However, the precise source of the stem cells recruited in REPs is still unknown. Studies using histological evidence have found that root lengthening and canal wall thickening following cell homing-REPs are induced by the accumulation of cementum-like tissue derived from cementoblasts (Minic et al. 2022; Shamszadeh et al. 2024). As multipotent stem cells, PDLSCs have the ability to differentiate into cementoblast-like cells and to generate cementoid or periodontal membrane-like tissues (Kim et al. 2025). Studies further indicate that the migration capabilities of PDLSCs is 1.5–2 times that of SCAP under both inflammatory and non-inflammatory conditions (Xu et al. 2023). Therefore, in the stage of periapical periodontitis, periapical PDLSCs may be recruited through blood from apical tissues and participate in the regenerative process in REPs. Following this, these recruited PDLSCs are stimulated to proliferate and differentiate by the cementum-extracellular matrix and related growth factors, which in turn promotes the formation of regenerative tissue in REPs (Shi et al. 2025; Minic et al. 2022). However, limited research has been explored on how intracanal medications affect PDLSCs.

In order to establish the optimal concentration range of mTAP that would ensure effective antibacterial efficacy while preserving hPDLSCs viability, this in vitro study was intended to examine the effect of clindamycin-mTAP on the migration, proliferation, and osteogenic/cementoblastic differentiation of human PDLSCs (hPDLSCs) and its antibacterial efficacy against Enterococcus faecalis (E. faecalis), thus providing a theoretical foundation for clinical drug application.

Materials and method

Cell culture and confirmation

The study was carried out in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was acknowledged by the Guanghua Hospital of Stomatology Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (approval number: KQEC-2023–19-01) on March 17, 2023. This in vitro study was conducted at the Guangdong Provincial Key Laboratory of Stomatology, Guanghua School of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University, Guangzhou, China. Healthy impacted third molars and orthodontic premolars were obtained from patients aged 18–25 years with informed consent at the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University. The periodontal tissue at the apical third of the root was scraped and digested with dispase solutions (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and collagenase type I solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 1 h. Then, the suspension was put into a T25 cell culture flask and kept in a cell incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2), supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 20% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) in α-minimum essential medium (α-MEM; Gibco). hPDLSCs from passages 3–6 maintained in complete medium (comprising α-MEM, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 10% (v/v) FBS) were used for subsequent studies.

Colony formation tests were used to determine the self-renewal capability of the isolated hPDLSCs. oil red O, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and Alizarin red staining revealed the multiple differentiation potential of hPDLSCs. Flow cytometry confirmed characteristic mesenchymal stem cell surface marker expression.

Preparation of the stock solutions

A combination of metronidazole (Sigma-Aldrich), ciprofloxacin (Sigma-Aldrich), and clindamycin (Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in distilled water at a weight ratio of 1:1:1 to create antibiotic stock solutions. This resulted in mTAP at a concentration of 100 mg/mL (AAE 2021). The stock solutions were stored at 4℃.

Evaluation of hPDLSCs migration and proliferation

Preliminary experiments confirmed the significant proliferation inhibition of hPDLSCs by mTAP at concentrations > 1 mg/L. For subsequent assays, serial dilutions (3.9, 15.6, 62.5, 250.0 and 1000.0 μg/mL) of mTAP were prepared across the five concentration groups.

A wound-healing experiment was used to examine the migratory potential of hPDLSCs. A 200-μL sterile pipette tip was used to generate a standardized scratch when cells grown in full media reached 90% confluence. Serum-free α-MEM was used in place of the culture medium, and an inverted light microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) was used to measure the baseline wound width. mTAP was given to the experimental groups in serum-free media at concentrations varying from 0.0 to 1000.0 μg/mL. Scratch width was measured using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) following a 24-h incubation period.

The proliferation rate of hPDLSCs was determined utilizing the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). hPDLSCs were cultivated in full media supplemented with mTAP at ranges of 0–1000 μg/mL. The medium was changed to 90 μL α-MEM and 10 μL CCK-8 solution on days 1, 3, 5, and 7, and the mixture was then incubated for two hours. Absorbance at 450 nm was detected by a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Osteogenic differentiation assays

hPDLSCs were cultivated in osteogenic induction media (OS) that contained 0.1 μM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich), 50 μM ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich), 10 mM β-glycerophosphate (Sigma-Aldrich), 1% antibiotics, and α-MEM with 10% FBS. Every three days, the medium was replaced. In the subsequent tests, hPDLSCs were cultivated for seven days in osteogenic media that contained 0.0, 3.9, 15.6, 62.5, 250.0, and 1000.0 μg/mL mTAP.

Alizarin red staining assay

Following 7 days, hPDLSCs were frozen with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) and stained with alizarin red (Cyagen Biosciences, China). An inverted light microscope was used to image mineralized nodules. After stained samples were dissolved in 0.2 M cetylpyridinium chloride monohydrate (Sigma-Aldrich) for 15 min, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 520 nm utilizing a Hybrid Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek).

Detection of alkaline phosphatase activity

For ALP staining, hPDLSCs were frozen with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, dyed with an ALP staining solution (Beyotime, China) for 20 min, and photographed with an inverted light microscope (Carl Zeiss). hPDLSCs were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Cwbio, China) to measure ALP activity, and a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Cwbio) was used to measure the total protein concentration. A microplate reader (BioTek) was used to measure the absorbance at 520 nm using an ALP activity detection kit (Beyotime). The BCA assay was used to adjust the results to the total protein concentration.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was isolated from the grown cells using an RNA-Quick purification kit (YISHAN Biotechnology, China). Using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), the concentration and purity of RNA were evaluated. PrimeScriptTM RT Master Mix (Takara, Shiga, Japan) was used for reverse transcription. A LightCycler 480 Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) was used for SYBR Green I-based amplification (Yeasen, China). The comparative 2−ΔΔCt technique was used to measure each mRNA's relative expression level. All samples were normalized to GAPDH expression. Table 1 lists the primers used for mRNA detection.

Western blot analysis

Protein concentration was assessed using a BCA assay after total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Cwbio) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime). Proteins have been isolated using 4%−20% gradient polyacrylamide gels (Smart-Lifesciences, China) and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). After blocking the membranes with 5% milk for one hour at room temperature, the primary antibodies anti-RUNX2 (1:1000; Affinity Biosciences), anti-BMP2 (1:1000; Affinity Biosciences, Cincinnati, OH, USA), and anti-GAPDH (1:5000; Affinity Biosciences) were incubated for the entire night at 4 °C. Chemiluminescence reagents (Millipore) and an ImageQuant LAS 4000 mini system (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Chicago, IL, USA) were used to observe immunoreactive bands following a one-hour incubation period at room temperature with a secondary antibody (1:5000; Affinity Biosciences). ImageJ (NIH) was used to quantify band intensities, and relative ratios representing target protein expression adjusted to GAPDH were displayed.

Antimicrobial activity assessment

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration and minimum bactericidal concentration by the microdilution assay

The microdilution test was used to determine the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of mTAP against E. faecalis (ATCC 29212). Bacterial suspensions (10⁶ colony-forming units/mL, CFU/mL) in brain–heart infusion (BHI; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) broth were incubated with two-fold serially diluted agents (1–1024 μg/mL) in 96-well plates for 24 h, including controls. Using a BioTek microplate reader, the optical density (OD) at 600 nm was used to measure the growth of the bacteria. MIC was determined as the lowest concentration that inhibited the bacteria's observable growth, with an OD change of ≤ 0.05. For MBC determination, cultures from wells ≥ MIC were subcultured on agar plates; after 48 h incubation, MBC was recorded as the lowest concentration resulting in no colony growth.

Biofilm formation inhibition

The inhibitory impacts of mTAP on static biofilm formation of E. faecalis were assessed by culturing bacterial suspensions (10⁶ CFU/mL in 1% sucrose-BHI) in 96-well plates (37 °C, 24 h), followed by a 24 h treatment with the test compounds (1–1024 μg/mL). Biofilms were washed, dyed with 10% formaldehyde, stained with 0.2% crystal violet, solubilized in 33% acetic acid, and quantified by OD570 measurement.

Mature biofilm eradication

14-day E. faecalis static biofilms (10⁶ CFU/mL in 1% sucrose-BHI; medium refreshed every 48 h) in 48-well plates were treated with mTAP (30, 60, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 μg/mL) for 7 days, followed by saline washing to remove planktonic cells. The 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT; Beyotime) assay was used to assess residual metabolic activity, followed by formazan dissolution and OD570 measurement with a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

The sample size for each experiment was established using preliminary data and previously published studies with comparable experimental designs (Raddall et al. 2022; Tsai et al. 2009). All studies included at least three separate biological replicates (n = 3) to ensure repeatability and statistical reliability. Statistical power was established by observing similar effect sizes in pilot experiments and using multiple technical replicates per condition. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was verified using Levene's test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed for data that met both assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances. This was followed by Tukey's honest significant difference (HSD) post hoc test for all pairwise comparisons. For data that did not meet the assumptions of parametric tests, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis H test was used, followed by Dunn's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The proliferation and migration of hPDLSCs

Wound-healing assays (Fig. 2A, B) demonstrated that mTAP at a low concentration (3.9 μg/mL) enhanced cell migration (p < 0.05), but exhibited a concentration-dependent inhibitory effect at higher concentrations, with significant suppression at 1000 μg/mL (p < 0.0001).

CCK-8 assays further revealed that mTAP dose-dependently inhibited hPDLSC proliferation at concentrations ≥ 250 μg/mL across all time periods (p < 0.001), whereas no significant inhibition was observed at lower concentrations (p > 0.05). At the maximum concentration (1000 μg/mL), cell viability was considerably lower than the control (p < 0.0001).

Given its marked cytotoxicity, the 1000 μg/mL group was excluded from further experimental analyses.

The mineralization ability of hPDLSCs

Quantitative analysis and alizarin red staining (Fig. 3A and C) demonstrated that mTAP had concentration-dependent effects on the formation of mineralized nodules. It hindered mineralized nodule development at 250.0 μg/ml (p < 0.0001); it weakened at 62.5 μg/mL and 15.6 μg/mL; and it encouraged mineralized nodule formation at 3.9 μg/mL (low-concentration) (p < 0.01).

ALP staining and activity tests (Fig. 3B and D) showed that ALP activity was not significantly impacted by mTAP at 3.9, 15.6, and 62.5 μg/mL. Compared with other concentration groups, 250.0 μg/mL mTAP presented a significant inhibitory effect (p < 0.0001), indicating that a high concentration of mTAP could strongly inhibit the activity of alkaline phosphatase.

The osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs

Following seven days of osteogenic induction, RT-qPCR was used to assess the mRNA expression of the cementoblastic and osteogenic markers of hPDLSCs (Fig. 4A). At low and medium concentrations (3.9, 15.6, and 62.5 μg/mL) of mTAP, the mRNA expressions of ALP, BMP2, and RUNX2 were promoted (p < 0.01), but were inhibited at high concentration (250.0 μg/ml) (p < 0.0001). For cementoblastic genes, the mRNA expression of CAP gradually increased with increasing mTAP concentrations (p < 0.01). CEMP-1 only significantly increased at 250.0 μg/ml (p < 0.0001), with no obvious changes at other low and medium concentrations. The aforementioned findings suggest that mTAP has concentration-dependent and gene-specific effects on the expression of osteogenic and cementoblastic genes.

Antibacterial efficacy of mTAP against Enterococcus faecalis

The MIC and MBC of mTAP against E. faecalis were 1 μg/mL and 128 μg/mL (Fig. 5A and B). In the biofilm elimination assays, mTAP at concentrations ≥ 32 μg/mL effectively inhibited ≥ 76.33 ± 0.89% biofilm formation, while at ≥ 125 μg/mL it achieved ≥ 72.53 ± 1.15% eradication of 14-day mature biofilms (Fig. 5C and D).

Discussion

The purpose of this work was to examine how mTAP affected the migration, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation of hPDLSCs.

Cell migration promotes to recruit stem cells into root canals, while cell proliferation prevents the loss of differentiation potential and cellular senescence (Liang et al. 2021). These processes enable more stem cells, particularly hPDLSCs, to participate in REPs, thereby promoting tissue repair and regeneration. In our study, mTAP (≥ 250 μg/mL) suppressed the migration and proliferation of hPDLSCs in a concentration-dependent manner. Previous research has demonstrated that mTAP exerted a concentration-dependent inhibition on SCAP activity within the 100–800 μg/mL range, consistent with the outcomes of Raddall et al. (2022) and Tsai et al. (2009). However, the precise mechanisms by which mTAP influenced migration and proliferation of hPDLSCs remain unclear, potentially involving multiple cellular pathways. Tsai et al. demonstrated that ciprofloxacin inhibits tenocyte migration by suppressing focal adhesion kinase phosphorylation, suggesting that ciprofloxacin in mTAP may cause inhibition of cell migration (Tsai et al. 2009). Regarding the effect of medicaments on cellular proliferation, metabolic regulatory mechanisms may be implicated. Ciprofloxacin reduces the level of checkpoint protein 1 while increasing Polo-like kinase 1, leading to a delay in the cell cycle and subsequently impacting cell proliferation (Tsai et al. 2009). Besides, drug-induced cytotoxicity may be mediated through pH alterations. J. Bi et al. reported that TAP-containing media became increasingly acidic (Bi et al. 2018). The acidic components of mTAP (clindamycin and ciprofloxacin) induce extracellular acidification, causing intracellular pH disruption and growth arrest or cell death (Takeuchi et al. 2013; Wigler et al. 2013).

Results from our mineralization assays demonstrated that mTAP inhibits the development of mineralized nodules and ALP activity in hPDLSCs in a concentration-dependent manner, with the detrimental effect being strongest at 250 μg/mL. The pH modulation may contribute to these outcomes. Alkaline environments promote osteogenesis of MSCs while acidic conditions suppress it (Wang et al. 2021). According to studies, 2.5 μg/mL ciprofloxacin significantly inhibited mineralized nodules formation in osteoblasts, exhibiting a concentration-dependent inhibitory effect (Holtom et al. 2000). In contrast, clindamycin at 150 μg/mL promoted mineral deposition in human osteoblasts (Olvera-Huertas et al. 2022). Based on these findings, clindamycin may partially counteract the inhibitory effects of ciprofloxacin on osteogenic differentiation. As for early expression of osteogenic markers, our study found that 250 μg/mL mTAP inhibited the mRNA expression of BMP2 and RUNX2 but did not affect the protein expression. The inconsistency in mRNA and protein expression may be caused by post-translational regulation. Multiple stimulatory and inhibitory factors collectively regulate the osteogenic differentiation of MSCs through extracellular signaling pathways and transcription levels (Mollentze et al. 2021; Yun et al. 2023). Inhibition of specific signaling pathways may trigger compensatory activation mechanisms. Another reason of inconsistency may be caused by insufficient protein accumulation. Future studies should add time points to track dynamic changes in protein levels and functional results. Additionally, the results also demonstrated that the expression of cementogenic-related genes CAP and CEMP-1 rose substantially as mTAP concentration increased, which supports the notion that the tissue regenerated after REPs was primarily of a cementum-like nature. In order to discover more about the mechanism underlying the inhibition of cementogenic differentiation after REPs, future studies can incorporate protein expression assays of cementogenic genes.

Gram-positive facultative anaerobe E. faecalis, which is frequently present in 90% of primary endodontic infections, creates biofilms that colonize dentinal tubules and facilitates the survival of other bacteria within root canals (Yang et al. 2024b). Besides, it acidifies the microenvironment via acid production, tolerates alkaline conditions (pH ≤ 11.5), demonstrating robust resistance to host immunity and root canal disinfectants (Swimberghe et al. 2019). As a standard strain for dental pulp biofilm models and antimicrobial drug testing, E. faecalis was selected for this study (Swimberghe et al. 2019). The MIC and MBC of mTAP measured in this study were 1 μg/mL and 128 μg/mL, aligning with the findings provided by Khoshkhounejad et al 2019. mTAP inhibited 76.33 ± 0.89% early-stage biofilm formation at 32 μg/mL and eradicated ≥ 72.53 ± 1.15% 14-day mature biofilms at concentrations ≥ 125 μg/mL. Therefore, concentrations over 128 μg/mL mTAP sustained bactericidal effects in both planktonic and biofilm-embedded bacterial eradication. This study focused exclusively on assessing the e antibacterial activity of mTAP against E. faecalis in vitro. Given that root canal infections in clinical practice typically involve polymicrobial communities (Delikan et al. 2021), future research should employ multispecies biofilm models and in vivo animal models to comprehensively evaluate the antimicrobial efficacy of mTAP.

A critical concentration window for mTAP that balanced antibacterial efficacy with biocompatibility of hPDLSCs was established in this study, providing a robust basis for future clinical application and formulation of safe and effective intracanal medications. Although the effective concentration range identified herein is lower than the AAE-recommended range of 1–5 mg/mL, not all intracanal medicaments act effectively on bacteria and cells. Furthermore, it is essential to acknowledge the in vitro nature of these findings, which necessitates cautious interpretation for clinical translation. Given these limitations, further in vivo validation is required to confirm these effects within the complex biological milieu of the root canal system. Additionally, the advanced delivery methods play a role in the efficacy and safety of mTAP. Future research must focus on optimizing slow-release carriers and precise volume-to-surface ratio control to ensure minimizing potential cytotoxic impact while persistent antimicrobial activity on the surrounding periapical tissues, including the crucial resident stem cells.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that mTAP exerted concentration-dependent effects on the functionality of hPDLSCs and its efficacy against E. faecalis. The optimal concentration range of 128–250 μg/mL was proposed, as it effectively eradicated mature bacterial biofilms while preserving the migratory, proliferative, and differential potential of hPDLSCs. These findings establish a solid foundation for the future clinical formulation and application of safe and effective intracanal medications, underscoring the critical role of precise concentration control in REPs.
