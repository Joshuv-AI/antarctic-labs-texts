Acute-on-chronic liver failure: From definition to pathogenesis and therapy
Authors: Yifan Xu, Jingting Liu, Dabao Shang, Robim M Rodrigues, Yu Chen, Xiaogang Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe concept of acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) has been widely accepted around the world since it was proposed nearly 30 years ago, but there are still no universal criteria for the definition and diagnosis of ACLF worldwide. In recent years, the key clinical features for describing ACLF, such as the underlying chronic liver diseases, acute intrahepatic or extrahepatic insults, acute hepatic decompensation, extrahepatic organ failure, short-term high mortality, and the reversible course of the disease, have gradually narrowed the differences and reached a consensus. The pathogenesis of ACLF has not been fully elucidated, and most relevant studies focus on systemic inflammation and immune dysfunction. In this review, we discuss the evolution in the clinical definition of ACLF during the last few years and suggest clear criteria for ACLF diagnostics. In addition, we summarize the current understanding of ACLF pathogenesis and review the latest therapeutic targets against this condition. Lastly, we present a novel experimental mouse model that has been proven to be instrumental for the assessment of novel potential therapies for ACLF.

Introduction

Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) is a severe clinical syndrome characterized by acute hepatic decompensation, extrahepatic organ damage, and high short-term mortality,[1,2] which occurs on the basis of chronic liver diseases. The concept of ACLF was first proposed by Ohnishi et al[3] in 1995, to describe two simultaneous lesions or insults to the liver, namely, an acute and rapid deterioration in liver function in individuals with preexisting chronic liver disease that was characterized by decompensated liver failure, high incidence of organ failure, and extremely high short-term mortality.

In the nearly 30 years since the concept was proposed, it has been widely accepted by scholars all over the world. However, there is still no unified standard clinical definition for the diagnosis of ACLF worldwide. To better understand ACLF, the continuous comprehension and development of the clinical definition of ACLF is reviewed in this study. Furthermore, we have consolidated the current understanding of ACLF pathogenesis and summarized the therapeutic targets for ACLF that are currently under investigation. Last, we propose an experimental mouse model that can contribute to the mechanistic understanding of ACLF and the evaluation of potential new therapies.

Disease Definition of ACLF

Since the concept of ACLF was first proposed in 1995, its definition has been a subject of ongoing debate for nearly 30 years. Nevertheless, with the advancement of large-scale, well-characterized ACLF cohort studies conducted by different organizations and institutions, the definition of ACLF has progressively been clarified and refined.

In 2006, the Chinese Society of Infectious Diseases and the Chinese Society of Hepatology of the Chinese Medical Association (CMA) described ACLF as: acute decompensation of liver function on the basis of chronic liver diseases.[4] In 2009, the ACLF working group of Asian-Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL) published the first expert consensus on ACLF for the Asia-Pacific region. ACLF was defined as: acute deterioration of liver function manifested by jaundice (total bilirubin [TBil] ≥5 mg/dL or ≥85.5 μmol/L) and coagulopathy (international normalized ratio [INR] ≥1.5 or prothrombin activity [PTA] <40%), and complicated clinical ascites and/or encephalopathy within 4 weeks, regardless of whether the patient has previously diagnosed or undiagnosed chronic liver disease (including cirrhosis).[5] In 2012, the CMA released the “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Liver Failure (2012 Edition)”, which also updated the definition of ACLF, pointing out that ACLF is a clinical syndrome of acute or subacute decompensation of liver function that occurs within a short period, usually within 4 weeks, on the basis of chronic liver diseases.[6] Also in 2012, the APASL–ACLF Research Consortium (AARC) was formed to collect prospective data of patients with ACLF.[7]

In 2013, based on the results of a prospective multicenter study (Consortium for the Development of Criteria for Acute-on-Chronic Liver Failure [CANONIC]) involving 1343 patients from eight European countries, the Chronic Liver Failure Consortium (CLIF-C) of the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) described ACLF as an acute deterioration of liver cirrhosis, usually correlating with a preceding disease characterized by organ failure and high short-term mortality (28-day mortality ≥15%).[8] They proposed the CLIF–sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score to define ACLF and used SOFA score to diagnose organ failures.[9,10] According to the number of organ failures, ACLF is divided into three different grades (ACLF grades 1, 2, and 3), with 28‑day mortality rates of 22%, 32%, and 76% and 90‑day mortality rates of 40%, 52%, and 79%, respectively. The EASL–CLIF ACLF score (or CLIF–C ACLF score) does not reflect the severity of liver failure itself but provides useful information for predicting mortality and transplant inefficiency in Western patients with ACLF.

In July 2014, the North American Consortium for the Study of End-stage Liver Disease (NACSELD) observed the outcomes of 507 hospitalized patients with decompensated cirrhosis caused by alcohol or hepatitis C and complicated with infection from 18 centers of liver diseases in the United States and Canada. They proposed the concept of infection-related ACLF (I-ACLF), which was defined as acute deterioration of a patient with cirrhosis due to infection presenting with two or more extrahepatic organ failure, such as grade III or IV hepatic encephalopathy, circulatory shock, need for mechanical ventilation, dialysis or other forms of renal replacement therapy, with high short-term mortality.[11] This disease definition does not include changes in liver function and coagulation function. In October 2014, APASL updated the expert consensus based on the prospective study cohort data (AARC data) from the Asia–Pacific region. The cohort was constituted with 1363 patients from 14 countries. The updated definition of ACLF adopted some of the concepts of EASL–CLIF–C, and the characteristic of “high short-term (28-day) mortality” was added.[7]

Also in 2014, to bridge the gap between the disease definitions of ACLF from different agencies in the East and the West, the World Gastroenterology Organisation (WGO) proposed a new consensus, integrating existing concepts and ideas, defining ACLF as a syndrome that occurs in patients with chronic liver diseases with or without cirrhosis, characterized by acute liver failure (ALF) (jaundice and INR prolongation) caused by acute liver decompensation, single or multiple extrahepatic organ failures, resulting in an increased mortality rate within 3 months. In addition, ACLF was divided into three types: (1) type A, ACLF without cirrhosis; (2) type B, ACLF with compensated cirrhosis; and (3) type C, ACLF with decompensated cirrhosis.[12]

In 2018, the “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Liver Failure (2018 Edition)” of the CMA was updated, further improving the definition of ACLF as: on the basis of chronic liver diseases, a syndrome characterized by acute jaundice deepening and coagulation dysfunction as manifestations of liver failure caused by various precipitating factors, which may be combined with complications such as hepatic encephalopathy, ascites, electrolyte disorders, infections, hepatorenal syndrome, and hepatopulmonary syndrome, as well as other extrahepatic organ failure.[13] At the same time, the update in this guideline also included the different ACLF types as proposed by the WGO, dividing ACLF into three types: (1) type A, ACLF occurring on the basis of chronic noncirrhotic liver diseases; (2) type B, ACLF occurring on the basis of compensated cirrhosis, usually within 4 weeks; and (3) type C, ACLF occurring on the basis of decompensated cirrhosis.

In 2018, through the establishment of Chinese Group on the Study of Severe Hepatitis B (COSSH), Chinese scholars completed a study involving 1322 hospitalized patients with acute decompensation of hepatitis B cirrhosis or acute injury of chronic hepatitis B from 13 centers of liver diseases. Considering the fact that the clinical characteristics of hepatitis B virus infection-related ACLF (HBV–ACLF) in the East are different from those of alcoholic liver disease-related ACLF (ALD–ACLF) in the West, they summarized the clinical characteristics of HBV–ACLF in the East and defined HBV–ACLF as: in patients with chronic liver disease due to HBV infection, acute decompensation with liver or extrahepatic organ failure is associated with high short-term mortality. When TBil ≥12 mg/dL and prothrombin time (PT)–INR ≥1.5, ACLF should be diagnosed. This approach refines the HBV-related ACLF definition, filling the gap in EASL–ACLF criteria, which are not fully applicable to patients with hepatitis B in China.[14]

In 2019, based on the prospective multicenter study cohort data of 5228 patients from 43 centers in 15 countries in the Asia–Pacific region, the APASL–AARC once again updated the APASL consensus on ACLF, adding the description that “ACLF is a reversible syndrome” to the original disease definition, taking the reversibility of the disease as an important feature of ACLF, and pointing out that this reversibility is manifested as the decrease of TBil and PT–INR, as well as the improvement of hepatic encephalopathy or ascites.[15]

In 2022, the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) released the “Clinical Guidelines for Acute-on-Chronic Liver Failure” for the first time based on the three international ACLF guidelines launched by APASL, EASL, and NACSELD, pointing out that ACLF is a potentially reversible disease that occurs in patients with chronic liver diseases with or without cirrhosis, and may lead to multiorgan failure and death within 3 months in the absence of basic treatment for liver disease, liver support treatment, or liver transplantation. ACLF is identified by chronic liver diseases, elevated serum TBil, and prolonged INR, and the occurrence of kidney, respiratory, circulatory, or brain function failure during the course of the disease, which is a further basis for ACLF diagnosis. The disease description of ACLF by the ACG is similar to the definitions of the CMA and APASL guidelines, and emphasizes more on the reversibility of the disease.

In April 2023, the EASL once again updated the clinical practice guidelines for ACLF, evaluating and comparing the disease definitions and standards of ACLF proposed by APSAL, NACSELD, and COSSH, pointing out the deficiencies in APSAL and NACSELD, and emphasizing that the update of this guideline aims to help clinicians identify ACLF, rationally allocate treatment resources, and develop management strategies. The disease definition of ACLF described in this version of the guideline is adjusted and improved from the 2013 version to: ACLF is a severe form of acute decompensation of cirrhosis, associated with a high 28-day mortality rate (≥20%), manifested as the failure of one up to six organ system (including the liver, kidney, brain, coagulation, circulation, and respiration), and the onset of acute factors that lead to systemic inflammation (SI), including intrahepatic and/or extrahepatic damage.[16]

In June 2023, during the 12th National Conference of the China Network for Severe Liver Diseases (CNSLD) held in Dalian, China, we proposed that ACLF could be divided into two types, based on the characteristics of intrahepatic and extrahepatic organ failures at the onset of ACLF [Figure 1].[171819]

Type I ACLF: liver failure without extrahepatic organ failures

Acute liver injury on the basis of chronic liver diseases, mainly manifested as jaundice and coagulation dysfunction. At the onset of ACLF, the affected organ is the liver, and some patients have hepatic encephalopathy. Liver failure is the first manifestation, and other organ failures do not occur simultaneously, and the disease definition is close to the content of APASL and CMA.[15,20]

Type II ACLF: liver failure with extrahepatic organ failures

Mostly acute decompensation of liver function on the basis of cirrhosis, manifested as elevated TBil, prolonged PT–INR, and the occurrence of complications of cirrhosis, accompanied by liver and at least one extrahepatic organ failure within 1 week, and the disease definition is close to the content of EASL–CLIF, COSSH, and NACSELD.[21,22] This classification and disease definition are of positive significance for bridging the gap between different associations in the East and the West regarding ACLF. However, it is worth further carrying out prospective multicenter studies to expand and confirm this classification and disease definition.

In November 2023, the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) released a practice guideline proposing that any definition of ACLF needs to include the presence of hepatic dysfunction as well as the presence of extrahepatic organ failure. They recognized ACLF as multiorgan failure in patients with chronic liver disease, emphasizing its reversibility and the role of liver transplantation and supportive care.[23]

In March 2024, similar classification for ACLF (types A and B) was proposed in India.[24] Moreover, at the APASL annual meeting held in Kyoto in March 2024, the “Kyoto APASL Consensus” proposed a unified ACLF definition based on the two-type classification (types A and B) and further refined the specific content of this definition. Type A ACLF excludes patients with earlier decompensation or those experiencing extrahepatic organ injury, and its course is considered reversible. Type B ACLF is defined similarly to the COSSH definition of ACLF and may include patients with earlier decompensation and organ failure caused by intra- or extrahepatic injuries (including infections), which is associated with increased mortality. Type A ACLF encompasses definitions from APASL and Japanese definitions, while Type B ACLF corresponds to the AASLD, EASL–CLIF, NACSELD, and COSSH definitions. Type A ACLF can be further stratified into grades A1, A2, and A3 based on the AARC score. ACLF defined by NACSELD can be reclassified into B2 and B3 based on the number of organ failures.[25] This has made certain contributions to unifying the definitions of ACLF between the East and the West.

In December 2024, the CMA released the “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Liver Failure (2024 Edition)”, incorporating key concepts from the COSSH standards.[26] In April 2025, the Chinese Society of Hepatology of CMA released the first “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Acute-on-Chronic Liver Failure (ACLF) (2025 Edition)” in China, which builds upon the content related to ACLF from the “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Liver Failure (2024 Edition)”. It systematically classifies ACLF into type I (liver failure without extrahepatic organ failure) and type II (liver failure with extrahepatic organ failure) for separate description in clinical practice.[27]

By sorting out the descriptions for the disease definition of ACLF by various societies or organizations in recent years, we can clearly recognize the research progress that has been made in the field of ACLF [Table 1]. The APASL definition is simple and easy to use, emphasizing liver failure itself, which aligns with the epidemiological trend in the Asia–Pacific region, where viral hepatitis is predominant. This definition facilitates early diagnosis and treatment of ACLF, helping prevent progression to multiorgan failure. However, it pays insufficient attention to other organ failures and is not suitable for patients outside the Asia–Pacific region. Compared to the APASL definition, the EASL–ACLF includes patients with decompensated cirrhosis in its definition, emphasizing multiorgan failure and SI. It is more comprehensive and suitable for patients with multiorgan failure in Europe, particularly in cases requiring a holistic assessment of prognosis. However, it is not applicable to noncirrhotic patients. Additionally, NACSELD focuses on defining infection-related ACLF, making it particularly suitable for populations in North America where infections are prevalent, especially in cases of infection-induced multiorgan failure. Nevertheless, it is limited to infection-related ACLF, narrowing its scope of application, and does not emphasize liver failure itself.[28]

Although scholars around the world still have some different understanding of ACLF, the global research on ACLF is indeed gradually deepening and the understanding of ACLF is gradually improving. Thereby, a consensus for a general description of the disease definition of ACLF is gradually approaching. ACLF is different from ALF and chronic liver failure (CLF) and has unique clinical characteristics and prognostic outcomes. Therefore, establishing a clear disease definition of ACLF is of great significance for the early detection, diagnosis, and treatment of ACLF. With a deeper understanding of ACLF, global multicenter large-sample cohort studies are warranted to reach a general consensus on the definition of ACLF.

Current Understanding of the Pathogenesis of ACLF

In China and the Asia–Pacific region, the basic chronic liver disease in ACLF is mainly chronic hepatitis B, while in European and American countries, it is mainly alcoholic hepatitis and chronic hepatitis C.[293031] Although there are differences in the underlying liver diseases between the East and the West, the common pathological basis of most patients with ACLF is usually liver fibrosis or cirrhosis caused by long-term chronic liver injury.[32] After suffering from acute intrahepatic or extrahepatic injury, acute decompensation of liver function occurs, such as jaundice, coagulation dysfunction, hepatic encephalopathy, and ascites, and eventually progresses to systemic multiorgan failure.[33]

At present, it is generally believed that the systemic inflammatory response induced by acute liver injury and bacterial infection, on the basis of long-term chronic liver injury, plays an important role in the outcome of ACLF.[34] A large amount of clinical data also shows that the high short-term mortality of patients with ACLF is related to bacterial infection.[35363738] In a multicenter ACLF cohort study of 407 patients, the EASL–CLIF research group found through that 37% of patients had bacterial infection at admission, and 46% of the remaining patients without infection at admission developed bacterial infection within 4 weeks of follow-up, mainly spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, urinary tract infection, and pneumonia, suggesting that bacterial infection is widespread in patients with ACLF (>66%) and directly affects the clinical outcome.[36] Alcohol-associated hepatitis is a major form of ACLF in Western countries,[39] neutrophils are well known to play an important role in the pathogenesis of alcohol-associated hepatitis.[40] A recent study demonstrated that the self-sustaining interleukin 8 (IL-8+) neutrophils are the major driver to promote alcohol-associated hepatitis disease progression and liver failure,[41] which are potential therapeutic targets for alcohol-associated hepatitis.[42] It would be interesting to determine whether IL-8+ neutrophils also play an important role in the pathogenesis of ACLF induced by other etiologies, such as HBV and HCV.

In 2016, based on the CANONIC cohort, the EASL–CLIF research group proposed the well-known “SI hypothesis”, believing that ACLF is an acute exacerbation of the existing SI in patients with decompensated cirrhosis.[43] The research group evaluated the systemic inflammatory response by detecting the levels of 29 cytokines in the serum of 522 patients with decompensated cirrhosis (237 patients with ACLF) and 40 healthy controls. They clinically confirmed the SI hypothesis and found that SI is the primary driving force for the progression of ACLF in patients with cirrhosis.[44]

In May 2020, renowned experts in the field of ACLF systematically concluded the definition, clinical features, pathophysiology, and prevention and treatment principles of ACLF caused by cirrhosis. They further pointed out that although the pathogenesis of ACLF is not entirely clear currently, systemic inflammatory response plays an important role in the course of ACLF.[45] In patients with cirrhosis, the massive release of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) activates immune cells by binding to specific pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) of innate immune cells, leading to excessive systemic inflammatory response, causing immune-mediated tissue damage, and then causing single or multiple organ damage, and finally progressing to ACLF.[45] Severe SI can lead to insufficient tissue perfusion, immune-mediated tissue damage, and mitochondrial dysfunction.[21,46] Among them, mitochondrial dysfunction is related to the progression of ACLF. In patients with ACLF, oxidative phosphorylation and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production are reduced, which may aggravate organ failure. Excessive release of proinflammatory cytokines consumes a large amount of energy. Coupled with the obstruction of energy production, it will eventually lead to immune exhaustion/immune paralysis.[45474849] This immune dysfunction will increase the risk of secondary infection and lead to a higher mortality rate.[37]

In March 2021, core members of the EASL–CLIF research group again emphasized that the SI hypothesis is a new paradigm of acute decompensation and multiorgan failure in cirrhosis.[43] Dr. Didier Samuel wrote an Editorial in the same issue and commented on the SI hypothesis proposed by Arroyo et al[45], pointing out that this hypothesis has introduced two new concepts in the field of ACLF: (1) Systemic inflammatory response is the driving factor for the development of decompensation in cirrhosis and (2) the increased incidence of bacterial infections in patients with cirrhosis is also the result of immune exhaustion or immune paralysis after systemic inflammatory response.[44] It was further pointed out that with the elucidation of the mechanism of systemic inflammatory response in ACLF, it will lead to the application of anti-inflammatory therapies in the field of ACLF, and he also indicated that more work should be done to determine whether SI and immunoparesis are drivers or complications of decompensated cirrhosis.[44]

In conclusion, the pathogenesis of ACLF is comprehensive and complex, yet remains not fully elucidated. It is typically explained by the PIRO concept, including predisposition (P), injury (I), response (R), and organ failure (O). ACLF develops on the basis of long-term chronic liver damage caused by an underlying liver disease (predisposition). Following the sustention of various acute intrahepatic or extrahepatic injuries (injury), patients initially present with acute decompensation of liver function. This condition manifests as hyperbilirubinemia, coagulation disorders, hepatic encephalopathy, and ascites. The immune system response (response) induced by this injury can be divided into two aspects. One aspect is that, following acute liver injury, the cellular immunity and the reticuloendothelial cell system in the liver become imbalanced, and the ability of the liver to synthesize various anti-infectious proteins decreases, increasing the risk of bacterial infections (e.g., spontaneous bacterial peritonitis caused by intestinal flora translocation, urinary tract infections, pneumonia, and skin and soft tissue infections). Bacterial infections will lead to the presence of a large number of PAMPs in patients’ circulation. The other aspect is that necrotic hepatocytes release a large number of DAMPs into the systemic circulation. Circulating DAMPs and PAMPs bind to specific PRRs of innate immune cells, thereby activating the immune system. This activation then triggers the release of numerous proinflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-17, and interferon gamma (IFN-γ), which results in SI and a cytokine storm. This will stimulate a systemic inflammatory response that leads to subsequent immune-mediated tissue damage. After patients experience excessive systemic inflammatory response in the early stage of the ACLF, the late stage of the disease progresses to a state of immune exhaustion or immune paralysis, with bacterial infections further aggravated. Meanwhile, single or multiple organ dysfunction may occur. Subsequently, patients may progress to liver failure either without or with extrahepatic organ failure (Organ failure). Eventually, ACLF progresses to multiple organ failure, ultimately leading to the death of the patient [Figure 2].[203543505152535455]

Three-stage ACLF Mouse Model and Pathogenesis Progress

At present, the existing experimental animal models for ACLF can be classified into three major categories: (1) hepatotoxic reagent-induced ACLF, (2) immune response-induced ACLF, and (3) surgical procedure-induced ACLF models. These models are designed to simulate different aspects of the disease pathogenesis by combining various induction methods. In brief, hepatotoxic reagent-induced ACLF models include protocols using carbon tetrachloride (CCl4), thioacetamide (TAA), and acetaminophen (APAP) to induce chronic liver injury followed by acute insults, such as D-galactosamine (D-GalN) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS). These models are widely used for studying liver fibrosis and acute injury. These models tend to have high mortality rates and short survival time, especially after the acute insult. Immune response-induced ACLF models primarily focus on immune responses by using substances, such as human serum albumin (HSA) and porcine serum (PS) for chronic injury, followed by D-GalN and LPS for acute injury. These models are particularly useful for studying autoimmune or viral hepatitis-related ACLF. While they provide valuable insights into immune-driven ACLF, they lack direct hepatotoxicity modeling and have high variability in immune responses. Surgical procedure-induced ACLF models include protocols such as bile duct ligation (BDL) with LPS, or BDL combined with hepatic ischemia/reperfusion injury. These models are suitable for studying cholestatic liver diseases and ischemic hepatitis. However, they require advanced surgical skills and have high mortality rates due to infection or complications from surgery.

However, none of the existing ACLF models, whether induced by hepatotoxic reagents, immune responses, or surgical procedures, could fully replicate the entire disease course seen in patients with ACLF, making them imperfect representations of the condition. Based on the clinical characteristics of patients with ACLF, the optimal experimental animal model should be able to simulate the core pathological features of ACLF, including chronic liver injury, acute exacerbation, and multiorgan failure. Moreover, the reagents used for modeling should be easily accessible, and the induction methods should be simple and straightforward. Additionally, the model should allow for a reasonable survival period, ideally around one week, to facilitate intervention studies and better assess therapeutic efficacy.

Due to the lack of optimal experimental animal models for ACLF, the progress of basic investigation of ACLF is limping. Although several experimental ACLF models have been established, none of them can recapitulate and simulate the whole pathological process of patients with ACLF.[56,57] Current research on the mechanisms of ACLF suggests that bacterial infection and systemic inflammatory response are crucial in the progression of the disease.[58,59] Based on cohort studies in patients with ACLF, it was found that the key clinical courses of most patients with ACLF can be divided into three stages: (1) long-term chronic liver injury, (2) acute liver injury, and (3) bacterial infection and systemic inflammatory response with extrahepatic organ damage. Recently, according to these three stages, our group has designed and developed a three-stage ACLF mouse model combining chronic liver injury, induced by injection of CCl4 for 8 weeks, acute hepatic insult, obtained by injection of a double dose of CCl4, and bacterial infection caused through intraperitoneal injection of Klebsiella pneumoniae [Figure 3].[56,57] Using this mouse model, we were able to successfully simulat the key courses and major clinical features of patients with ACLF. Moreover, this model can also well simulate the extrahepatic organ damage that occurs during the progression of the disease, including acute kidney injury and multiple organ failure. It has an appropriate survival period for intervention research and provides a reliable tool for mechanistic investigation of ACLF.[56,57] The three-stage ACLF mouse model provides a comprehensive simulation of the key clinical features of ACLF, including chronic liver injury, acute insults, bacterial infection, and multiorgan injury. It is particularly useful for therapeutic research due to its ability to mimic the pathophysiological progression of ACLF, offering a survival window of 5–7 days suitable for preclinical intervention studies. The inclusion of viable bacterial infections, a critical feature in ACLF, further enhances its relevance.[60,61]

Using the three-stage ACLF mouse model, our group found that the imbalance of signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1)/STAT3 activation in the liver affecting liver regeneration is an important mechanism for the continuous progression of ACLF. It is mainly affected by excessive expression of IFN-γ and bacterial infection. Liver tissue and peripheral blood of patients with HBV–ACLF and ALD–ACLF confirmed that liver regeneration was significantly inhibited during the systemic inflammatory response. By regulating the imbalance of STAT1/STAT3 activation in the liver in the ACLF mouse model, the mortality rate of ACLF mice can be improved. Subsequently, it was found that the innate immune-related cytokine interleukin-22 (IL-22) can reverse the imbalance of STAT1/STAT3 activation in the liver and promote liver regeneration, thereby blocking the disease progression in ACLF mice.[60,62] Based on this study, a multicenter phase II clinical trial on treating ACLF with IL-22 fusion protein (IL-22Fc, F-652) is currently underway.

At present, most studies on the mechanism of ACLF are related to systemic inflammatory response and immune dysfunction. Zhang et al[63] found that the expression of V-domain Ig suppressor of T-cell activation (VISTA) in peripheral blood and liver tissue of patients with ACLF was significantly reduced. The excessive differentiation of Th17 cells and the massive release of proinflammatory cytokines were related to the downregulation of VISTA expression. Using the three-stage ACLF model, activating VISTA expression in ACLF mice can inhibit the activation of CD4+ T cells, reduce inflammation, and reduce mortality. He et al[64] found through transcriptomic studies that the expression of transcription factor ETS Proto-Oncogene 2 (ETS2) in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of patients with ACLF was significantly higher than that in patients with chronic liver disease and healthy control. Moreover, ETS2 can be used to predict the 28/90-day mortality rate of patients with ACLF. It was confirmed that ETS2 has the effect of inhibiting excessive inflammatory response in mice model. Bernsmeier et al[65] found that the expression of MER receptor tyrosine kinase (MERTK) was upregulated in patients with ACLF. MERTK is mainly expressed on monocytes–macrophages and inhibits innate immune responses. The number of monocytes–macrophages with high expression of MERTK in patients with ACLF increased significantly, inhibiting innate immune responses and increasing the risk of bacterial infection in patients with ACLF. Bernsmeier et al[66] also found that CD14+CD15-HLA-DR-myeloid-derived suppressor cells (M-MDSCs) were significantly increased in patients with ACLF. This type of cell can inhibit T-cell function and weaken the antimicrobial innate immune response. It is related to secondary infection, disease severity, and prognosis of patients with ACLF. Yao et al[67] detected the situation of T cells in the peripheral blood of patients with HBV–ACLF by single-cell sequencing technology and found that peripheral blood T cells in patients with ACLF are diverse, including naive T cells, effector T cells, and exhausted T cells. The frequency of exhausted T cells in patients with ACLF increased significantly during the progression of ACLF, suggesting that T-cell exhaustion promotes the occurrence of immune dysfunction (immune exhaustion/immune paralysis) in patients with ACLF. Peng et al[68] used single-cell RNA sequencing to analyze liver nonparenchymal cells (NPCs) and PBMCs from healthy controls, and patients with liver cirrhosis and ACLF. They also examined a series of inflammation-related cytokines and chemokines in liver and plasma samples, as well as the lipid metabolome targeting free-fatty acids in the liver. The results revealed macrophage reprogramming in the liver during ACLF. Immunosuppressive triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2)+ macrophages were enriched in ACLF livers and contributed to an immunosuppressive hepatic microenvironment. The accumulation of unsaturated fatty acids in ACLF livers promoted macrophage reprogramming, highlighting the regulation of lipid metabolism as a potential target for improving immune dysfunction in patients with ACLF.[68] Yang et al[69] found that semaphorin-6B (SEMA6B) is significantly overexpressed in HBV-related patients with ACLF compared to healthy individuals. It mediates SI by inducing macrophages and inhibits proliferation through G0/G1 cell cycle arrest, thereby promoting hepatocyte apoptosis. These findings highlight SEMA6B as a promising early therapeutic target for patients with HBV–ACLF.[69] Yu et al[70] found that the levels of B- and T-lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA) were significantly increased in circulating and intrahepatic CD4+ T cells in patients with HBV–ACLF, which were positively correlated with disease severity, prognosis, and infection complications. Using the three-stage ACLF mice model, BTLA knockdown or administration of anti-BTLA antibody indicated increased cytokine secretion, reduced mortality and bacterial burden, showing a potential therapeutic target.[70]

Therapeutic Strategies and New Advances

Currently, there is no specific and effective treatment for ACLF, the core principles of managing ACLF revolve around organ support and targeted interventions.[71] Organ support aims to stabilize critical functions such as the liver, kidneys, and circulatory system, while targeted interventions focus on addressing specific triggers, such as infections or SI. Early identification and treatment of these triggers are crucial for improving patient outcomes. Traditional treatment concepts for ACLF mainly include etiological treatment, supportive and symptomatic treatments, artificial liver support systems, and liver transplantation.

The precise triggering factors of ACLF remain elusive; however, in 40%–50% of ACLF cases, bacterial translocation linked to SI is regarded as a critical determinant. When the trigger is identified, early recognition and targeted intervention of the causative factor would significantly improve the prognosis of patients with ACLF. However, a significant proportion of patients exhibit no identifiable triggering factors, substantially complicating the treatment process.[8]

Bacterial infection represents the most common trigger in patients with ACLF, exacerbating the disease course and significantly worsening the prognosis. A recent study revealed that approximately 37% of patients with ACLF presented with bacterial infections at the time of diagnosis. An additional 46% of patients developed bacterial infections within the subsequent 4 weeks.[36] Regardless of the severity of ACLF, the prognosis of infected patients is significantly poorer compared to that of uninfected patients.[36] Therefore, systematic pathogen detection and antimicrobial susceptibility testing should be performed upon hospital admission. Empirical antibiotic therapy should be initiated promptly upon the appearance of infection signs and subsequently adjusted based on pathogen detection and susceptibility results.[45] For patients without infections, recent studies indicate that prophylactic use of antibiotics may reduce the risk of infection during the ACLF course, thereby decreasing mortality rates.[72,73] HBV reactivation is one of the most common triggers of ACLF in Asian countries and is associated with a high short-term mortality rate.[74] Therefore, once HBV reactivation is suspected, immediate initiation of antiviral therapy with oral nucleotide or nucleoside analogues is recommended. For patients with ACLF and alcoholic liver disease, alcohol abstinence and corticosteroids remain the first-line therapeutic strategy.[75]

Artificial liver support system (ALSS) is a critical component of the comprehensive medical treatment of ACLF and serves as an essential bridging therapy for patients awaiting liver transplantation.[76] These modalities include plasma exchange, blood/plasma adsorption, hemofiltration, hemodialysis, and various integrated blood purification systems. Extracorporeal liver support systems can rapidly improve the body’s internal environment and overall condition, mitigating further damage to the liver or other organs, while creating conditions for liver regeneration and recovery. In ACLF, liver cell injury is predominantly driven by cytokine storms and toxin accumulation.[20] ALSS could effectively remove toxins produced during liver failure and reduce the levels of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. Additionally, it also ameliorates cholestasis, enhances liver and renal function, and stabilizes hemodynamics, thereby providing patients with ACLF and improved survival opportunities or a better chance to await liver transplantation.[77] Compared to conventional drug therapy, ALSS has been shown to significantly reduce the mortality rates in HBV–ACLF.[78] However, according to the latest EASL clinical guidelines, extracorporeal liver support therapies, whether artificial or biological, are not routinely recommended for ACLF outside of research trials.[16]

Liver transplantation is one of the most effective approaches for managing end-stage liver failure resulting from diverse etiologies. It is indicated for patients who show inadequate response to active medical treatment and/or artificial liver support therapy, or who fail to achieve improvement or recovery with these interventions. However, the widespread application of liver transplantation is significantly constrained by factors such as donor liver shortages, high procedural costs, technical complexity, and the risk of posttransplant rejection.

Cell-based therapies

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)[79,80] and hepatocyte transplantation[81] offer potential for liver regeneration and functional recovery, though large-scale clinical trials are needed to confirm their efficacy.

Immunomodulation therapies

Agents such as thymosin α1[82] and BTLA blockers[70] aim to restore immune balance and enhance anti-infective capacity, addressing the immune dysregulation observed in ACLF.

Cytokine-targeted therapies

Drugs such as IL-22 promote liver regeneration[60,83] and have shown promise in early-phase clinical trials with good safety and a certain degree of efficiency (phase IIa in China, unpublished).

Artificial liver support systems

Nonbioartificial (e.g., double plasma molecular adsorption system [DPMAS] combined with plasma exchange [PE])[84,85] or bioartificial liver systems,[86] and novel devices like the DIALIVE liver dialysis system provide temporary support, bridging patients to recovery or liver transplantation.[87]

Gut–liver axis modulation

Engineered carbon bead adsorbents reduce intestinal endotoxin levels, mitigating liver injury and SI.[88]

Pharmacological interventions

Statins have shown potential in preventing the progression of cirrhosis to ACLF and reducing ACLF-related mortality, though further randomized controlled trials are needed.[899091]

Despite these advancements, challenges remain, particularly in understanding the heterogeneous triggers of ACLF and standardizing treatment protocols. Future research should focus on large-scale, multicenter studies to validate these emerging therapies and refine therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion and Prospect

Although there is still no unified standard for the disease definition and diagnosis of ACLF worldwide, and there are still some differences in the descriptions of ACLF disease definitions by various associations or organizations around the world, with the gradual deepening of research, the clinical definition of the ACLF is more accurate and substantial progress is being made in the understanding of the pathogenesis of ACLF. ACLF has been widely recognized as a clinical syndrome different from ALF and CLF. Yet, only in recent years, key features of ACLF disease description, such as the basis of chronic liver diseases, acute intrahepatic or extrahepatic injury, acute hepatic decompensation, extrahepatic organ failure, high short-term mortality, and the reversibility of the disease course, have been systematically introduced, gradually narrowing the differences in disease definition between liver disease associations in the East and the West. However, there are still some differences in the evaluation standards for describing these key features, and it is urgent to carry out large-sample global multicenter prospective studies covering different etiologies and populations to achieve unification of the disease recognition and reach a consensus on ACLF definition in the East and the West.
