Associations of Biological Age and Heart Age Accelerations With New‐Onset Stroke in Individuals With Cardiovascular‐Kidney‐Metabolic Syndrome Stages 0–3: Evidence From a Chinese Longitudinal Study
Authors: Hao Zhu, Hannah Lui, Li He, Jing Zhang, Zhi Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIndividuals with cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic (CKM) syndrome face an elevated risk of stroke, yet effective risk stratification indicators remain limited. This prospective study aims to investigate the associations of biological age acceleration (BioAge‐gap) and heart age acceleration (HeartAge‐gap) with new‐onset stroke in individuals with CKM syndrome Stages 0–3, utilizing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) collected between 2015 and 2020. Baseline BioAge‐gap and HeartAge‐gap were calculated via the Klemera–Doubal method (KDM) and Framingham risk score (FRS), respectively. The primary outcome was self‐reported physician‐diagnosed stroke identified during the 2018 and 2020 follow‐ups. Analysis of 7283 eligible participants aged 45–75 years revealed a positive association between BioAge‐gap and new‐onset stroke (odds ratio [OR] = 1.05, p < 0.01). Furthermore, compared to participants with a non‐positive HeartAge‐gap, those with HeartAge‐gaps of 0–5 years and ≥5 years exhibited significantly increased risks of stroke by 74% and 141%, respectively. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests identified a significant gender interaction in the association between HeartAge‐gap and stroke, indicating that men with accelerated heart age represent a vulnerable subgroup. Restricted cubic splines (RCSs) confirmed a linear association, and sensitivity analyses validated the robustness of these findings. Integrating these intuitive indicators into clinical practice could facilitate primary stroke prevention and mitigate CKM syndrome progression.

Introduction

Stroke remains the second leading cause of death and the third leading cause of disability worldwide, accounting for over 6 million deaths and 130 million disability‐adjusted life years lost annually [1, 2]. China bears the highest lifetime risk of stroke globally [3], with over 3 million new‐onset cases reported each year [4, 5], representing one‐quarter of the global total [4].

In recent years, as the interconnections between cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease, and metabolic disorders have become increasingly elucidated [6], cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic (CKM) syndrome has been proposed to emphasize the synergistic progression of risk factors across these conditions [7]. This syndrome provides a novel clinical framework for disease prevention and management [8]. Emerging evidence suggests that individuals with CKM syndrome may face an elevated risk of stroke and an earlier age of onset compared to those without the condition [9, 10]. Given that the prevalence of CKM syndrome in China is reported to exceed 80% [11, 12], the precise identification of high‐risk stroke subgroups in this population has become particularly urgent.

Biological age and heart age, as emerging physiological metrics, are gaining attention. By integrating anthropometric characteristics, clinical biomarkers, and medical records into validated algorithms [13, 14], they are recognized to provide a more precise reflection of an individual's functional impairment and disease susceptibility. This effectively addresses the inherent limitations of chronological age, which merely quantifies the passage of time and fails to account for the significant heterogeneity in individual aging trajectories [15, 16]. Existing studies have supported significant associations of biological and heart age with CVD [17, 18, 19]. Compared to traditional risk indicators, these metrics not only show potentially superior predictive value but also offer greater clinical translational value by intuitively communicating health risks and eliciting a higher willingness for behavioral modification [20, 21, 22, 23, 24].

However, while the associations between these metrics and CVD are established, their associations with new‐onset stroke within the clinical stratification framework of CKM syndrome remain underexplored. The proposition of CKM syndrome offers a comprehensive perspective for understanding the synergistic impairment of cardiac, renal, and metabolic functions, as well as the co‐evolution of related risk factors [25]. Individuals at different CKM stages may exhibit significant differences in physiological impairment and risk factor accumulation patterns [26], which could lead to distinct variations in their biological decline trajectories and clinical risk profiles. Examining the potential associations between biological age, heart age, and new‐onset stroke in preclinical CKM populations will provide valuable insights for mitigating CKM progression and facilitating primary stroke prevention. Therefore, utilizing a large‐scale prospective cohort, this study was designed to investigate the associations of biological age acceleration (BioAge‐gap) and heart age acceleration (HeartAge‐gap) with new‐onset stroke in individuals with CKM syndrome Stages 0–3.

Methods

Study Population

This study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative survey initiated in 2011, which has completed five waves of data collection to date. CHARLS employed a four‐stage, probability‐proportional‐to‐size sampling design, with detailed methodological information available in a previous publication [27]. Trained interviewers conducted face‐to‐face household interviews using computer‐assisted structured questionnaires among participants aged 45 years and older, covering 450 villages or urban communities across China. The survey was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with ethical approval obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB 00001052–11014, 00001052–11015).

In the present study, Wave 2015 was selected as the baseline, with follow‐ups completed in 2018 and 2020. Eligible participants were defined as individuals at CKM syndrome Stages 0–3 who provided complete data necessary for the calculation of biological age and heart age. Participants were excluded if they had a history of stroke, lacked stroke outcome response during follow‐up, were aged <45 or >75 years, or had a biological age outside three standard deviations (SDs) from the mean. Measurements

Data Collection

In accordance with the study objectives, the following data were collected: (1) sociodemographic characteristics: chronological age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, and educational background; (2) anthropometric measurements: height, weight, waist circumference, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP); (3) behavioral and health status: smoking, drinking, depression, hypertension, antihypertensive treatment, and diabetes mellitus; (4) clinical biomarkers: fasting blood glucose (FBG), non‐fasting blood glucose (NFBG), glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), triglyceride (TG), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), platelets (PLT), C‐reactive protein (CRP), serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and total cholesterol (TC).

CKM Syndrome Stages 0–3

CKM syndrome, as defined in the 2023 presidential advisory by the American Heart Association [6], is a progressive disorder categorized into distinct stages. Stage 0 is defined by the absence of any CKM risk factors. Stage 1 refers to the presence of excess adiposity and/or prediabetes. Stage 2 is marked by metabolic risk factors (including hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, metabolic syndrome, or diabetes mellitus) and/or moderate‐ to high‐risk CKD. Stage 3 is typically characterized by a high predicted 10‐year CVD risk and/or very high‐risk CKD. Detailed staging criteria can be found in Supporting Information S1.

Exposures

The exposures of interest in this study were BioAge‐gap and HeartAge‐gap. Following previous research [28], eight biomarkers (PLT, CRP, Scr, BUN, TC, TG, HbA1c, and SBP) were used to estimate biological age via the Klemera–Doubal method (KDM) [13]. Before calculation, non‐normally distributed biomarkers (PLT, CRP, Scr, BUN, TC, TG, and HbA1c) were logarithmically transformed [30]. Heart age was estimated using the laboratory‐based Framingham risk score (FRS) [31], which is calculated based on age, HDL‐C, TC, SBP, antihypertensive treatment, smoking, and diabetes. Detailed procedures of heart age estimation are provided in Supporting Information S2. An individual's heart age is defined as the chronological age of someone with the same predicted CVD risk but with all cardiovascular risk factors at ideal levels [32, 33]. As the calculated heart age includes continuous values as well as cutoff points (<30 and >80 years), we categorized the HeartAge‐gap (heart age minus chronological age) into three groups based on previous research [32]: low (HeartAge‐gap ≤ 0), high (0 < HeartAge‐gap < 5), and very high (HeartAge‐gap ≥ 5).

Outcome

New‐onset stroke was the primary outcome in this study, which was determined via self‐reported responses to the question: “Since the last survey, have you been diagnosed with stroke (including cerebral infarction and intracerebral hemorrhage) by a doctor?”

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed to summarize participant characteristics according to baseline BioAge‐gap groups. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies (percentages). Differences between groups were assessed using t tests for normally distributed variables and Mann–Whitney U tests for non‐normally distributed variables. The Chi‐square test was employed to evaluate group differences in categorical variables.

To investigate the associations of BioAge‐gap and HeartAge‐gap with new‐onset stroke, hierarchical logistic regression models were constructed, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated. Before analyses, missing covariates, as specified in Supporting Information S3, underwent multiple imputation. As a sensitivity analysis, the same statistical procedures were applied to the complete‐case dataset to validate the robustness of results. Multicollinearity among variables was evaluated using the generalized variance inflation factor (GVIF). Restricted cubic splines (RCSs) were employed to explore the potential nonlinear association between BioAge‐gap and new‐onset stroke. Subgroup analyses stratified by chronological age, gender, body mass index (BMI), ethnicity, marital status, and educational level, along with interaction tests, were conducted to identify population‐specific effects. All analyses were executed using R software (version 4.4.1), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

Among all participants, those with CKM Stage 2 accounted for the majority (3103, 42.61%), followed by Stage 3 (2434, 33.42%), Stage 1 (1330, 18.26%), and Stage 0 (416, 5.71%). The mean chronological age of participants was 59.25 ± 7.39 years, with no significant between‐group difference observed (p = 0.34). Females comprised a larger proportion of the sample (3890, 53.41%), most participants were married (6566 participants, 90.16%), and 3130 individuals (42.98%) had no formal education.

Across all models, a higher baseline BioAge‐gap was significantly associated with an increased risk of new‐onset stroke (final model: OR = 1.05, 95% CIs: 1.02–1.08, p < 0.01). Compared to the younger group, individuals in the older group had a 25% greater risk of new‐onset stroke (final model: OR = 1.25, 95% CIs: 1.01–1.56, p = 0.04). Furthermore, relative to the low HeartAge‐gap group, participants in the high and very high groups exhibited a 74% (OR = 1.74, 95% CIs: 1.19–2.53, p < 0.01) and 141% (OR = 2.41, 95% CIs: 1.80–3.22, p < 0.01) elevated risk of new‐onset stroke, respectively.

Multicollinearity diagnostics presented in Supporting Information S4 showed that all covariates had GVIF values below 2, indicating no substantial multicollinearity. Additionally, analyses of the non‐imputed dataset showed consistent results, confirming the robustness of these findings (Supporting Information S5).

To address potential over‐adjustment bias arising from the inclusion of variables already used in the calculation of biological age or heart age, we conducted sensitivity analyses by excluding overlapping components from the final model's covariates (e.g., CRP, TC, and TG were excluded when assessing BioAge‐gap; TC and HDL‐C were excluded when assessing HeartAge‐gap). The results demonstrated that the associations of BioAge‐gap and HeartAge‐gap with new‐onset stroke remained significant (Supporting Information S6).

RCS and Subgroup Analysis

RCS analyses with three knots were used to explore the potential nonlinear association between BioAge‐gap and new‐onset stroke. The optimal number of knots was determined based on the minimum Bayesian information criterion (Supporting Information S7). As illustrated in Figure 2, the association between BioAge‐gap and new‐onset stroke was significant across all models (p for overall < 0.001), while no evidence of nonlinearity was identified (p for nonlinearity > 0.05 for all models).

Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted to assess population‐specific association. As shown in Figure 3, a higher BioAge‐gap was consistently associated with an increased risk of new‐onset stroke, with no significant interaction effects detected (all p values for interaction > 0.05). Incremental Prediction Analysis

To evaluate the incremental predictive value of BioAge‐gap and HeartAge‐gap for new‐onset stroke, we performed an incremental prediction analysis by comparing the Framingham 10‐year CVD risk model with augmented models incorporating these indices. As shown in Supporting Information S8, the inclusion of BioAge‐gap (Model b) and HeartAge‐gap (Model c) significantly improved the area under the curve (AUC) of the base model, with AUC increasing from 0.664 to 0.677 (p for DeLong's test = 0.014) for Model b, and from 0.664 to 0.675 (p = 0.024) for Model c. Furthermore, both augmented models exhibited a significant improvement in the Net Reclassification Index (NRI) (Model b: 0.204, p < 0.001; Model c: 0.190, p = 0.002). These results indicate that BioAge‐gap and HeartAge‐gap provide additional predictive information beyond conventional risk factors. Given that CKM syndrome is characterized by the synergistic accumulation of multi‐system risk factors, these indices may be more sensitive to capturing aberrations in an individual's overall physiological status.

Discussion

Based on a representative prospective cohort, this study is the first to demonstrate that elevated BioAge‐gap and HeartAge‐gap are significantly associated with an increased risk of new‐onset stroke among individuals with CKM syndrome Stages 0–3. Individuals with accelerated biological age (BioAge‐gap > 0) exhibited a 25% higher risk of stroke compared to those without. Furthermore, relative to the non‐positive HeartAge‐gap, HeartAge‐gaps of 0–5 years and ≥5 years were associated with 74% and 141% increases in stroke risk, respectively. Additionally, a significant gender interaction was observed in the association between HeartAge‐gap and stroke, indicating that men with a positive HeartAge‐gap may be more susceptible to stroke.

BioAge‐gap, as a comprehensive indicator reflecting severe metabolic disorders, is increasingly recognized as an important predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes [34]. Prior studies have demonstrated that an elevated BioAge‐gap is associated with reduced ventricular volume and decreased ejection fraction [35], and linked to atherosclerosis, vascular stiffness, and hemodynamic instability [36, 37]. The current study further supports a significant association between BioAge‐gap and new‐onset stroke in the CKM population. Against the backdrop of the synergistic impairment of cardiac, renal, and metabolic systems in CKM syndrome [7], biological age may more sensitively reflect the cumulative effects of multi‐organ functional decline compared to traditional risk factor counting [38, 39]. By integrating multi‐system biomarkers, biological age overcomes the limitation of traditional risk scoring that fails to account for potential significant variations in metabolic profiles among individuals with the same chronological age and equivalent risk exposure [40]. Consequently, the incorporation of biological age into stroke management for CKM populations may facilitate the identification of high‐risk subgroups who have not yet reached the high‐risk thresholds defined by conventional scoring but exhibit trends toward accelerated metabolic disturbances.

The HeartAge‐gap investigated in this study inherently represents the excess clinical burden resulting from the long‐term accumulation of traditional cardiovascular risk factors [14]. Given that the HeartAge‐gap is derived by comparing heart age with chronological age, and that heart age itself originates from 10‐year CVD risk scores [31], a positive association between the HeartAge‐gap and new‐onset stroke is to be expected. A key innovation of the present study is that it does not merely treat heart age as an absolute risk prediction tool, but rather as a universal ruler reflecting the cumulative physiological impairment of the cardiovascular system. Building on this basis, our study focuses on the relative acceleration process of clinical risk (heart age > chronological age) and its association with adverse cardiovascular events [41, 42]. Our study findings confirm that the HeartAge‐gap serves as an effective indicator for identifying individuals at high risk of stroke. In clinical practice, integrating such metrics of relative acceleration alongside traditional absolute risk scores may facilitate the identification of occult high‐risk populations.

Furthermore, our study identified a significant gender interaction in the association between the HeartAge‐gap and new‐onset stroke. It was observed that, compared to women, men with a positive HeartAge‐gap faced a higher risk of stroke. This discrepancy may be attributed to men's lower perception of adverse health outcomes and their inadequate management of modifiable cardiovascular risk factors [43, 44, 45]. Among middle‐aged and elderly adults in China, the prevalence of unhealthy lifestyle behaviors such as smoking and alcohol consumption is markedly higher in men [46]. More importantly, men with health risks are often more prone to neglecting potential adverse health outcomes [43]. The combined effects of poor willingness to engage in risk control and accumulated physiological impairment may amplify men's susceptibility to stroke. In contrast, women typically exhibit earlier adoption of health‐promoting behaviors and more proactive health‐seeking behaviors [44], potentially resulting in more standardized management of traditional risk factors such as hypertension and diabetes. This sex‐specific pattern provides valuable insights for understanding gender‐targeted strategies for CVD management, highlighting men with accelerated heart age as a vulnerable subgroup warranting focused attention in stroke prevention.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates significant positive associations of baseline BioAge‐gap and HeartAge‐gap with the risk of new‐onset stroke among individuals with CKM syndrome Stages 0–3. The prospective study design, extensive adjustment for confounding factors, and comprehensive sensitivity analyses support the robustness of these findings. However, the findings should be interpreted with the following limitations. First, the ascertainment of stroke in the CHARLS relied on self‐reported physician diagnosis. The lack of imaging or medical record verification may lead to recall bias and misclassification. Although self‐reported stroke has demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in Western developed nations such as the United States and Norway [47, 48], the reliance on self‐reporting remains a notable limitation in Chinese cohorts given the high proportion of rural and uneducated individuals among the aging population [49]. Future studies should incorporate more precise medical record data to validate the robustness of these findings and further explore the differential effects of these indicators across various stroke subtypes. Second, the estimation of biological age in the present study relied on limited clinical biochemical indicators. Although the established hallmarks of aging framework posits that cellular and molecular damages eventually manifest as deviations in metabolic, circulatory, and organ‐specific parameters [50], the absence of direct molecular markers (e.g., telomere characteristics), proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL‐6 and TNF‐α), and specialized organ function indicators (e.g., cystatin C and albumin) remains a notable limitation. Conventional biochemical markers capture the macroscopic reflection of multi‐systemic homeostatic imbalance, but they may not accurately reflect the details of aging at the cellular and molecular levels. Future research should strive to integrate multi‐omics data to construct more granular biological aging models and further validate these findings. Fourth, although incorporating BioAge‐gap and HeartAge‐gap yielded significant improvements in AUC and NRI, the incremental gain beyond conventional risk factors was modest. Given that these indices are derived from established risk factors, they may partially represent a comprehensive repackaging of existing vascular risks rather than novel measures of biological aging. Their clinical utility in distinguishing biological aging from vascular risk requires further validation in independent cohorts. Additionally, the multistage sampling employed by CHARLS leads to unequal selection probabilities for participants. Due to the dynamic nature of longitudinal medical data, where participant follow‐up is subject to entry and exit from the cohort, the incorporation of sampling weights proved challenging and may thus compromise the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the lack of precise timing for both stroke events and mortality in the CHARLS cohort precluded the application of Cox proportional hazards and competing risk models. Consequently, future studies with detailed time‐to‐event data are required to further explore the potential temporal relationships while accounting for death as a competing risk.

Conclusion

BioAge‐gap and HeartAge‐gap are positively associated with the risk of new‐onset stroke among individuals with CKM syndrome Stages 0–3. Incorporating these two intuitive indicators into clinical practice could optimize CKM syndrome management and facilitate the primary prevention of stroke.
