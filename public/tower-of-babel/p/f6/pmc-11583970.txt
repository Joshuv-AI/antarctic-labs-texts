Click Chemistry Enables [89Zr]Zr-DOTA Radioimmunoconjugation for Theranostic 89Zr-immunoPET
Authors: Ryota Imura, Jaewoong Jang, Atsuko Nakanishi Ozeki, Hiroyuki Takahashi, Hiroyuki Ida, Youichiro Wada, Yoshitaka Kumakura, Nobuyoshi Akimitsu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
There have been predictions that the use of the macrocyclic chelating agent 1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid (DOTA) in zirconium-89 (89Zr) immuno-positron emission tomography (89Zr-immunoPET) could enhance the in vivo stability of 89Zr radioimmunoconjugates. However, conjugating [89Zr]Zr-DOTA to a monoclonal antibody (mAb) remains a challenge as the heat treatment required for [89Zr]Zr-DOTA chelation can lead to thermal denaturation of the mAb moieties. We developed a method for synthesizing [89Zr]Zr-DOTA-mAb based on a tetrazine (Tz)-conjugated bifunctional DOTA derivative 2,2′,2″-(10-(1-(4-(1,2,4,5-tetrazin-3-yl)phenyl)-3,21,26-trioxo-6,9,12,15,18-pentaoxa-29-carboxy-2,22,25-triazanonacosane-29-yl)-1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7-triyl)triacetic acid (DOTAGA-Tz) and the inverse electron-demand Diels–Alder (IEDDA) click chemistry reaction where trans-cyclooctene-modified mAbs are conjugated to [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA without being exposed to heat. The stability of IEDDA-derived [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab was confirmed by in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo testing and comparative analysis against the conventional deferoxamine (DFO) counterpart [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab. The in vivo immunoPET imaging using [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab clearly visualized human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive malignancies in murine xenograft models. Greater tumor contrast was observed from [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab at a 72-h delayed scan compared with [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab. These findings suggest that our IEDDA ligation approach can be an effective means of synthesizing [89Zr]Zr-DOTA-mAb and can enhance the theranostic potential of 89Zr-immunoPET in DOTA-mediated radioimmunotherapy.

Introduction

Zirconium-89 (89Zr) immuno-positron emission tomography (89Zr-immunoPET) has become an increasingly important companion diagnostic for radioimmunotherapy. Radioimmunotherapy is a form of targeted radionuclide therapy utilizing the tumor specificity of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) and the cytotoxicity of particle radiation.1 Positron-emitting radionuclides conjugated to mAbs can emulate the pharmacokinetics of therapeutic counterparts, facilitating treatment planning, dosimetric assessment, and prognosis prediction.289Zr is a positron emitter having a physical half-life of 78.4 h sufficient for the antibody moieties to reach their target cells, and a mean positron range of 1.27 mm which is short enough to provide sufficient spatial resolution.3,4 Additionally, its efficient production method via the 89Y(p,n)89Zr reaction5 renders 89Zr a particularly promising positron emitter for immunoPET.

Considerable interest has been expressed in the use of a macrocyclic chelator in 89Zr-immunoPET. Conventionally, the acyclic chelator deferoxamine (DFO) has been widely used in 89Zr radioimmunoconjugation.6 However, studies7−9 have shown that [89Zr]Zr-DFO coordination complexes can be unstable in vivo and can increase the radiation dose by releasing dechelated 89Zr4+ ions into the bloodstream. The weak coordinate bond of [89Zr]Zr-DFO has also been demonstrated by a comparative stability test against the macrocyclic derivative 1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid (DOTA), where the complex stability of [89Zr]Zr-DOTA was shown to be markedly greater than that of [89Zr]Zr-DFO.8 Furthermore, it remains unclear whether [89Zr]Zr-DFO-mAb is an optimal companion diagnostic for DOTA-chelated therapeutic counterparts such as [177Lu]Lu-DOTA-mAb and [225Ac]Ac-DOTA-mAb, because the use of different chelators could affect their pharmacokinetics. For these reasons, DOTA has been suggested to be a more suitable chelating agent than DFO for 89Zr-immunoPET.8,10

Despite the increasing need for [89Zr]Zr-DOTA-mAb, its synthesis remains challenging. This is mainly due to the intrinsically slow chelation kinetics of DOTA and the thermosensitivity of mAbs: a certain degree of heating is required to promote the chelation between the DOTA moieties of DOTA-mAb constructs and Zr4+ ions,11,12 but typical mAbs lose their immunoreactivity when exposed to heat.

To synthesize [89Zr]Zr-DOTA-mAb while preserving mAbs from thermal denaturation, we developed a two-step radioimmunoconjugation method based on a tetrazine (Tz)-modified bifunctional DOTA derivative 2,2′,2″-(10-(1-(4-(1,2,4,5-tetrazin-3-yl)phenyl)-3,21,26-trioxo-6,9,12,15,18-pentaoxa-29-carboxy-2,22,25-triazanonacosane-29-yl)-1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7-triyl)triacetic acid (DOTAGA-Tz) and the inverse electron-demand Diels–Alder (IEDDA) reaction,13−15 a click chemistry variant. The method starts with heat-assisted chelation between DOTAGA-Tz and 89Zr4+ producing [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz, followed by room-temperature IEDDA cycloaddition between the [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz construct and trans-cyclooctene (TCO)-conjugated mAb, thereby forming [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-mAb.

The objectives of this study were to explore the feasibility of our two-step method for synthesizing [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-mAb and to investigate its pharmacokinetics. We synthesized [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab using the IEDDA reaction through Tz and TCO, and prepared [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab as a positive control using a conventional protocol. We then measured the in vitro stability values of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab and [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab against ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and murine serum, and compared their ex vivo biodistribution profiles and in vivo positron emission tomography (PET) images using SK-OV-3 xenograft mice expressing human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2).

Syntheses of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Trastuzumab and [89Zr]Zr-DFO-Trastuzumab

[89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab was synthesized in two steps (Scheme 2): (i) 89Zr chelation by DOTAGA-Tz to obtain [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz, and (ii) the IEDDA ligation between the Tz moiety of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz and the TCO moiety of TCO-trastuzumab to yield [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab. The radiochemical yield (RCY) was 59.3 ± 14.9% for the first step [89Zr]ZrCl4 + DOTAGA-Tz → [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz, and 57.8 ± 17.6% for the second step [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz + TCO-trastuzumab → [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab. The radiochemical purity (RCP) of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab measured using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) analysis exceeded 95% (Figure 2A).

Ex Vivo Biodistribution Studies

The biodistribution profiles of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab and [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of percent injected dose per gram (%ID/g) are presented in Figure 4. The accumulation of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab in the tumor peaked at 72 h (30.8 ± 7.3%ID/g) and subsequently decreased at 144 h (24.5 ± 8.3%ID/g) after administration. By contrast, the accumulation of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab in the tumor increased over time reaching its highest level (31.1 ± 12.3%ID/g) at 144 h postinjection, which was 2.1 times greater than that at its 72 h time point (14.7 ± 2.3%ID/g). On the other hand, the accumulation of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab in the bone decreased, whereas that of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab increased, over the course of observation. The tumor-to-blood ratios (TBRs) presented as the mean ± SD at the three time points were 1.1 ± 0.7, 3.8 ± 1.4, and 3.9 ± 1.1 for [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab, and 0.7 ± 0.0, 1.9 ± 0.4, and 9.2 ± 7.2 for [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab. Both radioimmunoconjugates exhibited greater uptake in the skin than in some normal tissues and organs, including the stomach, the large and small intestines, muscle, and bone.

Biodistribution profiles of (A) [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab and (B) [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab at 24, 72, and 144 h after administration (P.I.). The uptake data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation with a sample size of n = 4 at each postinjection time point for each radioimmunoconjugate.

The two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed that the uptakes of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab in the kidney, bone, and skin were lower than those of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab at every observed time point. The two radioimmunoconjugates exhibited statistically different temporal profiles in the tumor (p = 0.0239) and bone (p = 0.0104). The difference in TBRs was not statistically distinguishable (p = 0.44).

Discussion

Zirconium-89 immunoPET is becoming increasingly recognized as a companion diagnostic tool for radioimmunotherapy. DFO, an acyclic chelating agent providing a means of rapid radioimmunoconjugation at room temperature, has been the most commonly used 89Zr binder in the absence of effective alternatives in 89Zr-immunoPET. While this holds true for diagnostic purposes, employing DOTA for 89Zr chelation can be a suitable theranostic strategy: by using the same chelator for diagnostic and therapeutic radionuclides, unified chelator-mAb conjugation protocols can be established, which can facilitate efficient theranostic implementation.8 This is particularly true for 177Lu and 225Ac radioimmunotherapy, where DOTA is commonly used as chelating agents.16−19 Additionally, enhanced resistance to 89Zr demetalation is expected from [89Zr]Zr-DOTA,8 which can improve the in vivo stability of 89Zr radioimmunoconjugates. In an attempt to explore the potential of DOTA in 89Zr-immunoPET in such a theranostic context, we synthesized [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab using the IEDDA cycloaddition between Tz and TCO and performed comparative biodistribution studies against [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab.

This study showed that the IEDDA reaction can be an effective means of formulating [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab. Both the RCY and RCP of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab were similar to those of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab, suggesting that the antibody conjugation performance of the IEDDA ligation is comparable to that of the conventional DFO protocol. On top of that, the high transchelation stability, high serum stability, and high immunoreactivity of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab confirmed by the in vitro assays support the effectiveness of the IEDDA approach in preparing [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab.

Sufficient tumor uptake and retention of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab were observed in both the ex vivo biodistribution and in vivo PET imaging studies, demonstrating its potential as an immunoPET imaging agent. The overall tumor uptake profiles between [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab and [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab were similar. However, differences were observed at the postinjection time points of 72 and 144 h. The 72 h time point is particularly interesting, at which the tumor %ID/g of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab was approximately two times greater than that of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab; this observation was consistent in the PET images. Such rapid tumor uptake of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab can be beneficial in aiding clinicians to identify HER2-positive malignancies at earlier times, thereby improving the cancer management and treatment planning.

The successful synthesis of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab and its high tumor uptake demonstrated in this study suggest that DOTA can be an alternative chelator to DFO in 89Zr-immunoPET. Although the promising potential of [89Zr]Zr-DOTA over [89Zr]Zr-DFO has already been suggested in the past,8 synthesizing the immunoconjugate version of [89Zr]Zr-DOTA, namely [89Zr]Zr-DOTA-mAb, remained a challenge; this was because mAbs would lose their immunoreactivity by heat treatment required for the chelation between DOTA and Zr4+. We circumvented this limitation by synthesizing [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz and TCO-trastuzumab separately and then conjugating them via the IEDDA ligation at room temperature. The tumor imaging capabilities of the synthesized [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab were comparable to those of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab, corroborating that DOTA can be a viable chelating agent in 89Zr-immunoPET.

The increasing bone and tumor uptake of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab with time is attributable to the in vivo instability of [89Zr]Zr-DFO. This parallel accumulation pattern has also been observed by Deri and colleagues,20 where the bone concentration was ascribed to 89Zr4+ dechelated from its [89Zr]Zr-DFO coordination complex. The concentration of 89Zr4+ ions in the bone matrix has been widely reported,6,7,20,21 and is thought to be driven by the high affinity of 89Zr4+ for phosphates.7 On the other hand, Severin et al.21 have shown that free 89Zr can concentrate in neoplastic tissues, in addition to bone mineral, using FaDu and HT-29 xenograft mice. These observations suggest that the prolonged tumor retention of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab in our study is indicative of the dissociation of 89Zr4+ ions from DFO-trastuzumab.

The high skin uptake of both trastuzumab-containing radioimmunoconjugates could be the result of the expression of HER2 in normal skin tissues. In their study investigating the coexpression patterns of receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), Krähn and coauthors22 observed that isolated HER2 expression was found in normal skin tissues, and coexpression of HER2 with other RTKs were found in malignant skin tissues. This explains why the HER2-targeting [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab and [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab were uptaken by skin preferentially over other HER2-negative normal tissues and organs. This uptake pattern is also consistent with the study by Vugts et al.,23 where the uptake values of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab of the skin were greater than those of the stomach, ileum, large intestine, and muscle at 24, 72, and 144 h postinjection, and greater than that of the femur at 24 and 72 h postinjection.

The influence of a Tz-TCO construct on the biodistribution of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab is expected to be negligible, as Tz-TCO is lighter than trastuzumab by 3 orders of magnitude. Indeed, the IEDDA-synthesized [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab exhibited neither binding affinity change nor off-target accumulation in our in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo assays. By the same token, the difference between the maximum LAR of TCO-trastuzumab and the maximum CAR of DFO-trastuzumab is thought to play a minimal role in the pharmacokinetics of their respective radioimmunoconjugates; this was evidenced by the statistically indistinguishable TBRs of the two radioimmunoconjugates.

The potential influence of hydrophobicity imparted by Tz and TCO moieties on pharmacokinetics is also considered negligible under our experimental conditions. Increased hydrophobicity of TCO-conjugated mAbs has been hypothesized to affect their uptake patterns.24,25 For instance, Rossin and colleagues24 reported that among CC49 mAbs modified by TCOs with different hydrophobicity, the more hydrophobic variant exhibited reductions in blood circulation time and tumor uptake in murine models, which they attributed to the enhanced surface hydrophobicity of CC49. On the contrary, Zeglis et al.26 demonstrated that the most hydrophobic 64Cu-labeled Tz construct among three candidates was cleared through the urinary tract in mice, which is atypical for a hydrophobic molecule. This suggests that although the hydrophobicity of IEDDA precursors can influence the pharmacokinetics of the final product, the extent of the interaction can vary depending on the synthesis conditions and involved radiometals. Additionally, strategies are available for mitigating hydrophobicity-driven uptake shifts; examples include the introduction of hydrophilic PEG linkers to TCO.24,27

The modularity of IEDDA click chemistry can facilitate and optimize radioimmunoconjugate preparation. By unifying the synthetic protocols for 89Zr and therapeutic radioimmunoconjugates to the IEDDA cycloaddition, different types of TCO-mAbs can be conjugated to DOTAGA-Tz/radiometal complexes in an on-demand manner.15 The IEDDA radioimmunoconjugation of widely explored therapeutic radiometals such as 90Y, 177Lu, and 225Ac has already been demonstrated by other studies,28−31 supporting the viability of the IEDDA-driven theranostic radioimmunoconjugation concept.

Conclusion

This work demonstrated that the IEDDA click chemistry can be an effective means of synthesizing [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab. The in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo stabilities of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab were experimentally confirmed and validated against [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab. The immunoPET imaging of the formulated [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab successfully visualized HER2-positive malignancies in SK-OV-3 murine xenograft models, and even showed a greater tumor contrast at 72 h delayed PET compared with [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab. Offering an efficient [89Zr]Zr-DOTA-mAb synthesis method, the IEDDA click ligation can enhance the theranostic value of 89Zr-immunoPET in DOTA-mediated radioimmunotherapy.

Experimental Procedures

Unless otherwise specified, chemicals and cell culture media were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical, Japan, Dojindo Laboratories, Japan, or Sigma-Aldrich, USA. The bifunctional chelating agent NH2-DOTAGA was purchased from CheMatech, France, and p-SCN-Bn-deferoxamine from Macrocyclics, USA. The click chemistry reagents Tz-PEG5-NHS and TCO-NHS esters were purchased from Click Chemistry Tools, USA.

Preparation of Radiolabeling Precursors

DOTAGA-Tz was synthesized by modifying NH2-DOTAGA with Tz-PEG5-NHS ester. NH2-DOTAGA (38.0 mg, 0.073 mmol), Tz-PEG5-NHS ester (44.3 mg, 0.073 mmol), and triethylamine (0.021 mL, 0.15 mmol) were added to 0.76 mL of dimethylformamide, and the mixture was reacted at room temperature overnight. DOTAGA-Tz was fractionated by preparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with a semipreparative column (YMC-Actus Triart C18, YMC, Japan) and a mobile phase consisting of 0.1% tetrafluoroacetic acid (solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B) in a 5–95% solvent B gradient applied over 30 min at a flow rate of 2 mL min–1. The fractionated DOTAGA-Tz was lyophilized. The molar mass of DOTAGA-Tz was then measured using LC–MS (Q Exactive Orbitrap, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). NMR spectra were recorded at 298 K on a JEOL JNM-ECS400 (400 MHz for 1H NMR) spectrometer.

TCO-trastuzumab was prepared by conjugating trastuzumab to TCO-NHS ester. Two milligrams of trastuzumab was dissolved in a buffer solution of 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate (pH 9.0), and TCO-NHS ester was added to the trastuzumab solution in a TCO-to-trastuzumab stoichiometric ratio of 10:1. The resulting solution was incubated with gentle shaking at 37 °C for 1 h. Next, the buffer contained in the TCO-trastuzumab solution was substituted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) using centrifugal filter units (Amicon Ultra-4, 50 kDa cutoff, Merck Millipore, Germany). The prepared TCO-trastuzumab was stored at −80 °C.

DFO-trastuzumab was prepared by the same method used for TCO-trastuzumab, except that p-SCN-Bn-deferoxamine was used in place of TCO-NHS ester.

The LAR of TCO-trastuzumab and CAR of DFO-trastuzumab were measured using LC–MS. Before the LC–MS measurement, glycans, which have different molar masses for each trastuzumab, were removed by PNGase F (P705S, New England Biolabs, USA) to avoid mass spectral splitting. The resulting data were deconvoluted to zero-charge mass. LC–MS was also carried out on intact trastuzumab for comparison.

Synthesis of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Trastuzumab

Zirconium-89 was prepared using the same method as described in our previous study:32 in brief, 89Zr was produced via the 89Y(p,n)89Zr reaction by a cyclotron (Cyclone 18, IBA, Belgium), and purified in the form of [89Zr]ZrCl4 using a modified version of the purification method of Holland and colleagues.5

Preliminary chelation tests involving our previous oxalate addition strategy,32 which was applied for the DOTA-containing peptide PSMA-617, showed marginal improvement in the RCY of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA. The minimal RCY increase may be due to the greater number of carboxylic acid arms in DOTAGA-Tz (four) than in PSMA-617 (three), which can render DOTAGA-Tz more reactive to 89Zr. We therefore decided not to use oxalate in this study.

[89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab was synthesized in two steps: (i) 89Zr chelation by DOTAGA-Tz to obtain [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz, and (ii) the IEDDA ligation between the Tz moiety of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz and the TCO moiety of TCO-trastuzumab to yield [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab. In the first step, 89Zr chelation was performed by adding 1 μL of DOTAGA-Tz (10–2 M), 499 μL of HEPES (0.5 M, pH 7.0), 1.2 mL of methanol, and 300 μL of [89Zr]ZrCl4 (40–60 MBq) into a 2 mL microtube and then incubating the reaction mixture at 90 °C for 30 min. The RCY of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz was measured using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) with a silica gel stationary phase (iTLC-SG, Agilent, USA) and a mobile phase consisting of 1 M ammonium acetate and methanol in a 1:1 ratio. Under this condition, the expected Rf values of free 89Zr and [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz were 0.0 and 0.5, respectively. Methanol was removed by nitrogen bubbling at 60 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, TCO-trastuzumab (200 μL, 2 mg mL–1) was allowed to react with the [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-Tz solution at room temperature for 15 min. The buffer solution contained in [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab was then exchanged with PBS using centrifugal filter units.

Synthesis of [89Zr]Zr-DFO-Trastuzumab

[89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab was prepared according to previously reported methods5,33,34 and is briefly described as follows: 100 μL of [89Zr]Zr-oxalate prepared using a 1 M oxalic acid solution, 45 μL of 2 M sodium carbonate, 655 μL of 0.5 M HEPES, and 200 μL of DFO-trastuzumab (2 mg mL–1) were added to a microtube and reacted. The reaction was allowed to proceed at 25 °C for 30 min.

Measurement of Radiochemical Purity

The RCPs of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab and [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab were measured using SEC (COSMOSIL 5Diol-300-II Packed Column, Nacalai Tesque, Japan) and HPLC instruments, the latter of which was equipped with a 280 nm ultraviolet absorbance detector and a gamma-ray detector. A 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.2–7.0) including 150 mM sodium chloride and 10 mM sodium azide was applied at a flow rate of 1.0 mL min–1.

In Vitro Stability Testing

The transchelation stability indices of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab and [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab were assessed through EDTA challenge assays. Each of these radioimmunoconjugate was added to a microtube and diluted to 200 μg mL–1 with PBS, and mixed with a 1000-fold greater mol of EDTA. The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C and pH 7.0, and its aliquots were analyzed using a silicic acid TLC plate (iTLC-SA, Agilent, USA) and a 50 mM PBS solution (pH 7.4) containing diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid at 24, 72 and 144 h after the EDTA addition.

The two radioligands, [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab and [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab, also underwent serum stability testing using murine serum. One hundred microliters of each radioimmunoconjugate (200 μg mL–1) and 500 μL of murine serum were added to a microtube, which was then incubated at 37 °C and pH 7.0. TLC analysis was also performed at the same time points as the EDTA challenge testing. All in vitro stability assays were conducted in triplicate.

Immunoreactivity Testing

The immunoreactivity fractions of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab and [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab were evaluated using the Lindmo assay.35 An aliquot of each radioimmunoconjugate (10–10 M) was added to SK-OV-3 cells (human ovarian cancer, ATCC HTB-77, American Type Culture Collection, USA) suspended in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 1% bovine serum albumin (1.6 × 105 to 2.0 × 107 cells mL–1). The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 1 h with continuous mixing throughout the incubation period to keep the cells in suspension. The cells were then separated from the supernatant by filtration, and washed with PBS twice. Next, the 89Zr activity contained in the washed cells was measured using a gamma counter (Hidex Automatic Gamma Counter) and the fraction of SK-OV-3 cells bound with the radioimmunoconjugate was calculated, yielding a cell-binding fraction. The reciprocal of the cell-binding fraction was then plotted against the reciprocal of the concentration of SK-OV-3 cells. The y-intercept of the plot was calculated by linear regression analysis using GraphPad Prism 7.

Tumor Xenograft

All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the institutional review board of animal research at Isotope Science Center, The University of Tokyo, and conducted in accordance with the institutional (University of Tokyo Conduct no. 129: Code of Practice for Animal Research) and ARRIVE guidelines.36

Nude mice (BALB/cSlc-nu/nu, female, 7 wk, Japan SLC, Japan) were housed under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle and provided with food and water ad libitum. The temperature and humidity were maintained at 20–25 °C and 50–60%, respectively.

The SK-OV-3 cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with heat-inactivated 10% fetal bovine serum. Cell culture was carried out under standard conditions at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The cultured cells were harvested using trypsin–EDTA (0.25% trypsin, 0.02% EDTA). Finally, SK-OV-3 cells (5 × 106) suspended in 100 μL of 50% Matrigel (Corning, USA) were administered subcutaneously into the right shoulder of each mouse.

Ex Vivo Biodistribution Studies

SK-OV-3 xenograft mice were injected with either [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab or [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab (n = 4 for each radioimmunoconjugate group, approximately 5 μg, 0.1 MBq per mouse) via the tail vein, and sacrificed at 24, 72, or 144 h after administration (n = 4 for each time point). Euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation under isoflurane anesthesia to minimize pain. Blood and the following tissues and organs were collected and weighed: the liver, spleen, kidney, stomach, large intestine, small intestine, heart, lung, tumor, muscle, bone, and skin. Their 89Zr activities were then measured using a gamma counter (Cobra Quantum, PerkinElmer, USA), decay-corrected, and expressed as %ID/g. TBRs of the two radioimmunoconjugates were calculated using the corresponding %ID/g values at each time point (see Tables S1 and S2 in Supporting Information).

In Vivo PET Imaging Studies

SK-OV-3 xenograft mice other than those used in the ex vivo biodistribution studies were administered with either [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab or [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab (n = 4 for each radioimmunoconjugate group, approximately 50 μg, 3 MBq per mouse) via tail vein. PET images of the mice were then acquired at 24, 72, and 144 h postinjection using a small-animal PET scanner (Clairvivo, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan). The PET scan time was 30 min, during which the mice were maintained under isoflurane anesthesia.

Statistical Analysis

All data were presented as the mean ± SD. The biodistribution profiles of [89Zr]Zr-DOTAGA-trastuzumab and [89Zr]Zr-DFO-trastuzumab were compared by a two-way ANOVA analysis using GraphPad Prism 7. The two-way ANOVA analysis was also used to assess the influence of chelating agents, postinjection time, and their interaction on the accumulation of radioimmunoconjugates in the organs (%ID/g). Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.
