Maturation inhibitors: a lost cause?
Authors: Anton Pozniak
Published: 2025

The emergence of HIV-1 resistance to antiretroviral therapy, particularly to WHO-recommended dolutegravir-based regimens, is becoming a significant global concern.1 These resistance mutations may lead to cross-resistance against other antiretroviral drugs, including those used in pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). This growing threat highlights the need for programmatic strategies to prevent resistance development and transmission, as well as continued innovation in antiretroviral drug design targeting novel mechanisms of HIV-1 replication. New therapeutics would also provide an option for people living with HIV who have limited treatment options due to comorbid conditions, side effects, or drug–drug interactions.

Ideally new drugs should exhibit robust HIV-1 antiviral activity against a broad range of subtypes without cross-resistance to currently used therapies, have few drug interactions, and an excellent side effect profile. In addition, simplified dosing regimens, especially having long-acting formulations and being used in two-drug combinations, are increasingly important features for new drugs.

HIV-1 maturation inhibitors (MIs) are a novel class of drug. They are small-molecule compounds that block a late step in the viral protease-mediated processing of the Gag polyprotein precursor, the viral protein responsible for the formation of virus particles.2 The first-in-class HIV-1 maturation inhibitor was bevirimat, which was highly effective in blocking HIV-1 replication; however, it was ineffective against viruses containing naturally occurring sequence polymorphisms within Gag. This, combined with its high plasma protein-binding properties, led to the discontinuation of clinical development for bevirimat. Bevirimat analogs, or “second-generation” maturation inhibitors were designed to overcome these issues.3

Four other key MIs have undergone evaluation in phase 1–2b clinical trials. GSK2838232 had potent antiviral properties,4 but its pharmacokinetic properties required it to be boosted with the potent inducer ritonovir, which led to the termination of its development. The MI GSK3532795 also showed a potent antiviral response regardless of any polymorphism and did not require pharmacokinetic boosting. It showed a week 24 efficacy of viral suppression rate of approximately 82% when used in combination with two NRTIs in treatment-naïve adults with HIV-1.5 However, this compound was terminated for high rates of emergent resistance, both to the NRTI backbone and to the MI itself, and because of gastrointestinal intolerability (mainly abdominal pain and diarrhoea).

In a phase 1 study, VH3739937 (VH-937), also known as GSK3739937, administered to healthy participants was shown to be generally well-tolerated, and have antiviral properties against HIV-1 strains with diverse Gag sequences and polymorphisms generally conferring resistance to prior MIs.6 However, its progress was halted as it apparently did not satisfy ViiV's target product profiles.7

In eClinicalMedicine, Samit R Joshi and colleagues report two phase 2b studies of GSK3640254 (GSK’254) in treatment-naïve adults with HIV-1: DOMINO8 and DYNAMIC.9 DOMINO was a dose ranging double-blind study of once-daily oral GSK’254 in combination with two nucleoside reverse transcriptor inhibitors (NRTIs) using a control arm of a dolutegravir-based regimen. DYNAMIC was of a similar design but studied GSK’254 in a two-drug combination with dolutegravir, having dolutegravir plus lamivudine as a control.

Both DOMINO8 and DYNAMIC9 trials showed similar rates of virologic response at week 24 to the control regimen, there were acceptable safety/tolerability profiles and no treatment-emergent resistance, and the studies were planned to go to at least 52 weeks, However, the sponsors stopped the study early. This decision appeared, as it was for VH-937,7 to be for commercial (rather than clinical) reasons, because there were no drug-related concerns by week 24 in the studies. The sponsors decided that the intended fixed-dose combination of GSK’254 containing daily oral regimen would not be differentiated enough from existing two-drug daily oral regimens. The potential for GSK’254 to become the first MI to make it to market was not pursued and it did not advance into phase 3.

Two decades of research in MIs has led to much scientific knowledge, but no final product, and their development appears to have become less of a priority to large pharmaceutical companies. However, newer maturation inhibitors (such as HRF-10071 produced by a pharmaceutical company focused on generic drugs) are in early testing. In a phase 2a dose ranging study,10 presented in abstract form at the 2025 International AIDS Society conference, the higher doses of HRF-10071 showed about a 2-log reduction in HIV viral load after 14 days monotherapy and was well tolerated. A potential weakness of the drug is that three (10%) of 30 participants developed the same resistance mutations (in particular the V362M) seen in the development of other MIs. The compound will now be investigated in combination with other ARVs and its long-acting prodrugs explored.

The strategy of maturation inhibition should remain a priority in the search for anti-HIV compounds. Perhaps the original class of compounds, based on the action of bevirimat and to which the term MI was applied, is a lost cause and developing other compounds that block HIV-1 maturation (such as capsid inhibitors and allosteric integrase inhibitors) may be a more fruitful venture.
