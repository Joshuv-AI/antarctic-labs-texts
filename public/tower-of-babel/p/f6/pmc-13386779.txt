Multi-drug resistant Staphylococcus aureus from Bovine Mastitis: epidemiological and molecular insights from Central Ethiopia
Authors: Endashaw Fikru, Fitsum Dulo, Melaku Sombo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMastitis, a major concern in dairy production, significantly affects dairy cows and milk quality. Staphylococcus aureus represents one of the predominant pathogens implicated in bovine mastitis. This study aimed to isolate, identify, and characterize S. aureus from mastitis-positive cows in Central Ethiopia.MethodsA cross-sectional study on the isolation, identification, and characterization of S. aureus was conducted from October 2022 to August 2023 in the Hadiya and Kembata zones. Mastitis was diagnosed in lactating cows based on clinical examinations and the California mastitis test (CMT). All mastitis-positive cows were aseptically sampled (milk) for bacterial culture. Isolates were identified through colony characterization, Gram staining, slide catalase, oxidase, and coagulase tests, with species confirmation by MALDI-TOF biotyping. Antimicrobial susceptibility was assessed using the disk diffusion method, and resistance genes were detected by PCR. ResultsOut of 376 randomly screened lactating cows, mastitis was identified in 213 (56.6%; 95% CI = 0.52-0.62). Among them, 69 (32.4%) were positive for S. aureus. Significant associations were found between S. aureus mastitis and factors such as the age, tick infestation, and lesions on the skin of the udder and/or teat. All tested S. aureus isolates exhibited resistance to at least two of the nine antimicrobials tested, with 70% being multidrug resistant. The highest resistance rates were observed for penicillin G (100%) and amoxicillin (90%), whereas no resistance was found for cephalothin. All tested isolates possessed the nuc gene, and 90% had blaZ genes; however, mecA genes were not found, including those resistant to cefoxitin. These findings should be interpreted with caution, as antimicrobial susceptibility testing and molecular genotyping were carried out on 10 randomly chosen S. aureus isolates, which may not reflect all isolates.ConclusionThese findings highlight the prevalence of S. aureus-associated bovine mastitis and the occurrence of antimicrobial resistance in Central Ethiopia. The absence of mecA, despite phenotypic resistance, suggests alternative resistance mechanisms. These results underscore the urgent need for antimicrobial stewardship and effective mastitis control strategies to safeguard dairy production and animal health in the region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05588-4.

Introduction

Bovine mastitis is a common disease that affects the production and quality of milk in the dairy industry worldwide [1, 2]. It is primarily caused by microorganisms, although physical trauma to the udder can also contribute [3, 4]. Mastitis appears in two forms: clinical, with clear signs such as inflammation of the udder, visible abnormalities in the milk, and systemic symptoms [5, 6], and subclinical, which does not exhibit outward symptoms or changes in milk appearance [3, 6].

Staphylococcus aureus is a leading pathogen in both clinical and subclinical mastitis, causing economic losses and posing antibiotic resistance and food safety concerns [4, 7]. It is the most important cause of mastitis in dairy cows in Ethiopia [4, 7–9].

The growing resistance of S. aureus underscores the need for antimicrobial susceptibility monitoring to ascertain the circulation of resistant strains within specific ecological niches and enable successful therapy [7, 10].

Conventional culture and biochemical techniques, though commonly employed, often require considerable time, which can postpone the initiation of targeted treatments. In contrast, Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) presents a robust alternative, capable of rapidly identifying in mere minutes by analyzing their distinct protein patterns. In the realm of mastitis research, MALDI-TOF MS has effectively verified S. aureus isolates from bovine milk and is increasingly in conjunction with antimicrobial resistance work flows [11].

Accurate pathogen identification is crucial, because regional variation and poor diagnostic practices have led to the indiscriminate use of antibiotics, thereby increasing drug resistance and facilitating the rapid emergence of multidrug-resistant strains [3, 12]. Although numerous studies have investigated the pathogens responsible for bovine mastitis in Ethiopia [4, 7, 13], no study examined current prevalence and resistance profiles of S. aureus from bovine mastitis in the central Ethiopian region. The aim of this study was to address this gap by investigating its prevalence, antibiotic susceptibility, and resistance genes.

Materials and methods

Study areas

This study was conducted in purposively selected areas of the Kembata Tembaro (Kedidagamela, Kachabira, and Damboya districts) and Hadiya zones (Hosana and Lemo districts) (Fig. 1). These districts were selected because they are considered major milk sheds in Ethiopia’s central regional state. The total livestock population in the study areas of Hossana town and Lemo Woreda were estimated as follows: cattle, 118,120; sheep and goats, 54,312; equine, 22,213; and poultry, 182,940 (Lemo District Animal and Fishery Development Office: Annual report, Unpublished, 2022).

According to the Kembata Tambaro Zone Agricultural Office, the estimated livestock population of the zone was 812,175 cattle (composed of 575,020 local zebus and 237,155 crossbreeds), 255,003 sheep, 195,280 goats, 72,657 equines, 1,373,784 poultry, and 32,640 bee colonies (Kembata Tambaro Zone Agricultural Office: Annual report, Unpublished, 2020).

Collection of data on farm management

Data were collected using direct methods, specifically face-to-face interviews. A series of questionnaires was employed to systematically gather information on various aspects such as husbandry practices and individual animal attributes. The questionnaires [8, 14], which included both close and open-ended items, were administered by researchers (veterinarians) who were native speakers of the local language to ensure clarity and accurate response. This study incorporated different predictor variables, including animal demographic factors (such as age, breed, lactation stage, parity, and body condition score) and management-related information (such as type of floor, rearing methods, shed cleanliness, overall farm hygiene, grazing type, milking techniques, and bedding materials).

Study design and sample size determination

A cross-sectional study was conducted between October 2022 and August 2023, with aim of isolating and identifying S. aureus in mastitis-positive cow milk samples.

Sample size was calculated by considering 46.2% as the earlier prevalence reported in similar agro-climate zones [16]. This corresponded to the minimum required sample size of 376 lactating cows.

Screening for mastitis and milk sampling

After checking and discarding the first two or three streams of milk, udder quarter milk samples were collected from each quarter. This process was followed by a clinical examination and CMT screening. Samples exhibiting clots, flakes, blood, and consistency changes in the milk, or with signs of illness and/or visible inflammatory signs in the udder, were categorized as clinical mastitis samples [9]. In contrast, subclinical mastitis was indirectly evaluated by CMT. Briefly, milk samples from each quarter assessed by mixing equal parts of milk and CMT reagent on a paddle, then gently swirling the mixture and the reaction was scored using the Scandinavian scoring system, whereby a score of ≥ 3 in at least one of the four quarters signifies a positive result [3, 17]. The teat ends were cleaned with 70% alcohol before aseptic milk sample were collected in a 10 mL sterile sampling tube [18].

The samples were then transported to the Sodo Regional Veterinary Laboratory on the same day, in an ice-cooled box for further analysis. Upon arrival at the laboratory, the samples were either immediately processed or stored at + 4 °C for a maximum of 12 h until processing. All presumptive colonies were biochemically characterized at the Sodo Regional Veterinary Laboratory in Wolaita Sodo, Ethiopia.

Isolation and identification of S. aureus

Milk samples were cultured for bacterial isolation, as previously described [4, 19]. Brief, a loop-full sample from mastitis-positive cow milk was cultured on a blood agar base (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) enriched with 7% sheep blood. The inoculated plates were incubated under aerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24–48 h. After Gram staining, suspected S. aureus colonies were examined under a light microscope to assess their size, shape, and cell arrangement. Colonies suspected to be S. aureus were identified by the presence of Gram-positive cocci in irregular grape-like clusters [14].

Suspected colonies were subcultured on nutrient agar (Oxoid) plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h to obtain a pure culture [19]. Suspected S. aureus colonies were selected and subcultured on Mannitol Salt Agar (Oxoid), followed by aerobic incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Colonies of S. aureus that produced a yellow pigment on the medium were subjected to catalase, coagulase [4, 20], and oxidase tests [4, 19]. The final conventional identification of S. aureus was performed when colonies were characterized as Gram-positive cocci, hemolytic on sheep blood agar, slide catalase and coagulase test positive, oxidase test negative, and yellow on mannitol salt agar [14, 20].

The isolates were further confirmed as S. aureus using MALDI-TOF MS following standard protocols, as described previously [3, 14, 21] at the Animal Health Institute, Sebata, Ethiopia. In summary, a single colony from a 24-hour BHI agar (Oxoid) culture was carefully picked using wooden applicator sticks and spread as a thin film on a designated spot on a MALDI target plate. The film was then covered with 70% formic acid and dried at room temperature. The sample was subsequently coated with a matrix, specifically a-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (HCCA), and left to dry at room temperature. E. coli (ATCC 8739) was used as a quality control for machine reading. The plate was then inserted into a MALDI-TOF machine, which was analyzed using the MALDI Biotyper system (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany), and the results were displayed on a connected computer.

Identification scores were interpreted following the manufacturer’s guidelines, where a log(score) greater than or equal to two was considered to confirm the species-level identification. Those isolates only showing concordant identification results between the traditional biochemical and MALDI-TOF-MS were regarded as positive for S. aureus and included for subsequent analysis.

Antimicrobial resistance profile of S. aureus

After MALDI-TOF MS confirmation, antimicrobial susceptibility testing and genotyping were performed on a subset of 10 randomly selected S. aureus isolates selected from the 69 confirmed isolates. This subset size was predetermined based on resource limitations and the exploratory nature of resistance analysis. Susceptibility testing was performed using the standard disk diffusion method [20, 22], with nine antimicrobial agents: trimethoprim+sulphmethoxazole (1.25/23.75 µg/disk), tetracycline (30 µg/disk), penicillin (10 IU/disk), amoxicillin (10 µg/disk), gentamicin (30 µg/disk), ciprofloxacin (5 µg/disk), erythromycin (15 µg/disk), cefoxitin (30 µg/disk), and cephalothin (30 µg/disk). The zone of inhibition of individual disks was measured and interpreted as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R) according to CLSI guidelines [22]) for veterinary pathogens. S. aureus isolates exhibiting resistance to ≥ 3 classes of antimicrobial agents were classified as MDR [23].

Molecular detection of antibiotic resistance genes

PCR amplification

Target genes (mecA, nuc, and blaZ) were amplified using the Qiagen Taq Master Mix Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen, Germany). Each reaction included appropriate positive (ATCC 43300 as the MRSA reference strain) and negative (nuclease-free water) controls. PCR was performed on a Flex Cycler (Biometra GmbH, Germany) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, extension at 72 °C for 1 min per cycle, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Gel electrophoresis and visualization

The PCR products were separated on a 1.5% (w/v) agarose gel containing ethidium bromide (2 µL) which intercalates into, the DNA and is used for visualization. An 8 µL amplification product of each sample with 2 µL loading dye was applied to the slots of agarose gel. A 100 bp DNA ladder spanning 100–1000 bp was used as the molecular weight marker to determine the sizes of PCR amplicons. Electrophoresis (Apelex Midigel XL, France) was performed in 1 x TBE buffer at 120 V for 1 h. The PCR products were visualized under UV illumination (Gel Doc™ XRþ, BioRAD, Germany) using a gel documentation system. Images were captured from the gel documentation system display for presentation. Expected amplicon size were 279 bp for nuc, 846 bp for blaZ, and 163 bp for mecA.

Data management and analysis

Data obtained from the field and laboratory studies were entered into Microsoft Excel, and the coded data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) V-20 statistical software. Descriptive statistics, including percentages, were used and are presented in the tables. The associations between the prevalence of S. aureus infection and mastitis-positive cow was analyzed by Univariate logistic regression followed by multivariate logistic regression. The significance of the associations was determined at 95% CI, and the p-value was set at < 0.05.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from overnight cultures of S. aureus using a Qiagen DNA extraction kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol for bacterial cultures (Thermo Scientific, Germany). Purified DNA was eluted in AE buffer and stored at -20 °C until further analysis.

Results

Farm management and study animal descriptions

In the present study, 82 dairy farms were observed in the Hadiya and Kembata Tambaro zones of the Central Ethiopia Region. Dairy cows were managed in both intensive and semi-intensive systems; the majority of the farms had muddy floors (57%), and farmers did not use bedding materials (84% of the time). A total of 376 lactating cows, irrespective of age, breed, parity, or lactation stage, were included in this study. The majority of the sampled cows (96.5%) were crossbred (Holstein–Friesian and Jersey–Zebu crosses), whereas a few were of the indigenous Zebu type (3.5%). All cows were hand-milked twice daily in the morning and evening.

Isolation rate of S. aureus in mastitis-positive cow milk

Of 376 lactating cows examined, 213 (56.6%) were positive for mastitis, of which 151 (40.2%) were having subclinical mastitis and 62 (16.5%) with clinical mastitis (Table 2). Subsequent bacterial culture and isolation of S. aureus were performed exclusively on 213 mastitis-positive milk samples. The overall S. aureus isolation rate in this study was 32.4% (N = 69), with 25.4% (N = 54) and 7.0% (N = 15) from subclinical and clinical cases, respectively. All isolates presumptively identified as S. aureus by conventional methods were successfully confirmed by MALDI-TOF-MS, indicating complete concordance between the two identification approaches.

Detection of antibiotic resistance genes

The antibiotic susceptibility testing of S. aureus isolates was checked to determine whether they harbored the drug resistance genes blaZ, mecA, and nuc. Molecular genotyping results revealed that all the tested S. aureus isolates harbored the 279 bp long nuc gene (Fig. 2), and 90% of the selected isolates harbored blaZ (846 bp) genes (Fig. 3). However, none of them possessed the 163 bp mecA gene.

Discussion

Mastitis caused by S. aureus is an ongoing challenge in the successful management and operation of the dairy industry worldwide, with Ethiopia being no exception. In the present study, the overall isolation rate of S. aureus was 32.4%, which is consistent with previous reports of 32.8% and 31.5% S. aureus in Shire [25] and Adama [9] towns, respectively. However, our results contradict the findings of previous studies in Turkey [26], Bangladesh [23, 27], Kenya [6], and Eastern Ethiopia [28], which were 28.3, 20.4, 22.2, and 24.2%, respectively. This discrepancy across reports may be related to variations in farm management methods, such as breed, farm size, lack of teat dipping before and during milking, absence of diagnostic facilities, and absence of dry cow therapy in dairy farms in the study areas. S. aureus is commonly found on the udder or teat surfaces of infected cows and is the main source of infection in infected and uninfected udder quarters, commonly through milking [7, 17].

In this study, the prevalence of S. aureus-associated mastitis was significantly associated with the advancing age of lactating cows (p < 0.05). As cows grow older, they encounter a higher cumulative risk of pathogen exposure due to the frequency of milking and prolonged contact with contaminated environments. At the same time, their susceptibility to infection increases with age, influenced by host-related such as damage to the teat canal, deterioration of udder tissue, and a decline in immune response efficiency over successive lactations. Additionally, unresolved or recurring intramammary infections may persist, increasing the likelihood of future infections in older cows. This finding agrees with previous studies conducted in Southern Ethiopia, which reported that the risk of S. aureus-associated mastitis increases significantly with age [4]. However, this result contrasts recent report by Feyissa et al. [14], who did not find any association between advancing age and prevalence of S. aureus-associated mastitis.

The S. aureus isolation rate in this study did not show statistically significant association with the lactation stage (early, middle, and late). However, the prevalence increased progressively, being higher in the middle stage than in the early stage and higher in the late stage than in the middle stage. This finding is consistent with that of another study from Ethiopia [13], but contradicts research revealing a significant difference in S. aureus prevalence between different lactation stages [4, 8]. The increasing trend in S. aureus isolation rate across lactation stage might be biologically linked to factors such as prolonged exposure, damage to the teat canal, and a decline in udder defenses as lactation advances.

In this study, the isolation rate of S. aureus was not significantly associated with housing conditions or husbandry systems, similar to findings in Addis Ababa and Asella [4, 29]. However, in contrast to these findings, reports from Adama revealed that a statistically significant proportion of S. aureus was isolated from cows kept in muddy housing conditions compared to those kept on concrete floor [13]. These variations may be attributed to differences in management and herd size, since S. aureus is a contagious pathogen that spreads easily from cow to cow in the environment, particularly on the floor, where cows are kept close together, and unsanitary milking procedures involving milkers’ hands, utensils, towels, flies, and cross-suckling serve as foci of infection [30].

The isolation rate of S. aureus did not vary significantly across animal parity levels in the current study (p > 0.05), which is in accordance with the results of Feyissa et al. [30], who reported insignificant variations across parity levels. However, they differed from previous research that revealed a significant difference in S. aureus prevalence between different parity levels in and around Asella Town, southern Ethiopia. Higher parity may be linked to a decreased likelihood of recovery because it either indicates the development of a larger mammary gland and bulk of tissue or indicates that an antibiotic is unable to reach the affected area and eliminate the infection [4].

The S. aureus isolation rate in the current study was higher in cows with tick infestations and/or lesions on the skin of the udders and/or teats (p < 0.05) than that in cows without these factors. These findings align with those of previous studies on the relationship between tick infestations and/or injuries to the skin of the udders/teats and mastitis caused by S. aureus in lactating cows [14]. This could be the result of alterations in the teat tissue, namely, the skin of the barrel, teat end, and teat canal, which may facilitate the entry of pathogenic bacteria into the udder and cause mastitis and lesions in milking cows [31].

In the present study, S. aureus was isolated at higher rate from subclinical mastitis cases (25.4%) than clinical cases (7.0%). The higher recovery of S. aureus from subclinical mastitis cases may reflect the propensity of this pathogen to establish chronic and persistent intramammary infection that often remain subclinical, as widely documented in previous studies [17, 32].

The antimicrobial susceptibility tests carried out in this study revealed low susceptibility of S. aureus to the commonly used antimicrobials. The average susceptibility of S. aureus to all antimicrobials tested in this study was 64.4%, which was in line with reports of 67.7% [4] and 69.4% [13] in dairy farms in Ethiopia but disagrees with previous reports of 73.6% [33] in Holota, Western Ethiopia. The differences between these studies may be due to variations in pathogen strain, resistance level, antibiotic disc used, and antibiotic concentration, and other relevant variables that can influence the zone of inhibition during an antimicrobial susceptibility test.

The antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed that all 10 randomly tested S. aureus isolates were resistant to penicillin G, while no resistance was observed to ciprofloxacin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, or cephalothin. In the study area, mastitis treatment is often empirical and commonly relies on β-lactam antibiotics, particularly penicillin-based combinations, due to their availability and routine use in veterinary practice. This finding is in accordance with the reports of 0% susceptibility of S. aureus to penicillin G in West Shewa Zone [14] and Asella town [4].

Resistance to other tested antimicrobial variables, included high resistance (90%) to amoxicillin and moderate susceptibility of S. aureus to tetracycline (30%), erythromycin (20%), and gentamicin (20%). Overall, in the present study, the detection of multidrug resistance in 70% of the isolates raises significant public health concerns, especially with the consumption of unpasteurized dairy products. Consistent use of these antibiotics for treating cows may have led to MDR S. aureus, which may have resulted in the spread of resistant strains in the study area. This result was in accordance with reports from earlier studies in Ethiopia and other countries, implying that the prolonged and inconsistent use of some antimicrobial agents may lead to the development of resistance [5].

All ten randomly selected isolates were found to harbor the nuc gene, whereas 90% of the tested isolates carried the blaZ gene. The blaZ gene, which encodes the β-lactamase enzyme that hydrolyzes β-lactam antibiotics, has been consistently reported to be responsible for the penicillin resistance in Staphylococcus species [34]. Interestingly, in this study, the highest percentage of penicillin-resistant S. aureus strains tested for blaZ was found to contain this gene (90%).

The nuc gene encodes thermonuclease enzymes that allow bacteria to withstand heat and create heat-stable toxins [35]. Thus, the detection of the nuc gene in the S. aureus isolates suggests that either the isolates or the toxins they yield are resistant to heat treatment [36]. The findings of the present study cosistent with those reported by Feyissa et al. [14] and Sutejo et al. [36], who identified the nuc gene in S. aureus isolates obtained from milk samples.

Notably, no mecA gene was detected in the isolates tested in this study. Several previous studies have revealed cases in which cefoxitin-resistant staphylococcal isolates did not carry the mecA gene [37, 38]. Although cefoxitin disk diffusion is a reliable surrogate for mecA-mediated methicillin resistance [39], the absence of mecA despite the cefoxitin resistance suggests that MRSA cannot be excluded solely by mecA PCR and occasionally arise through alternative pathways. One plausible explanation is the presence of mecC, a mecA homologue that encodes an altered penicillin-protein binding (PBP2C) and confers methicillin resistance but is not detected by mecA-specific PCR assays. Because mecC was not evaluated in this study, we cannot rule out mecC-positive MRSA as the basis of the cefoxitin phenotype. Other potential mechanisms include the borderline oxacillin resistance (BORSA) driven by β-lactamase hyperproduction, mutations altering native PBPs, and cell wall changes that reduce β-lactam susceptibility [40].

A key limitation of this study is the antimicrobial susceptibility testing and molecular detection of resistance-associated genes were performed on a limited number of randomly selected isolates. While random selection minimizes the selection bias, the relatively small sample size may not fully capture phenotypic and genotypic diversity of all S. aureus identified in this study. Consequently, the molecular findings should be interpreted with caution, and studies including a larger number of isolates are warranted. In addition, using just a single loopful of milk for culturing might have lowered the sensitivity of detection, which could result in an underestimation of the prevalence of S. aureus, particularly in cases of subclinical mastitis where bacterial levels are typically low or shed intermittently.

In the context of Central Ethiopian (Hadiya and Kembata Zones) dairy farming, which is largely characterized by smallholder and semi-intensive operations, there is an evident requirement for effective and locally applicable strategies to control mastitis. Key actions include enhancing the hygiene of udders and teats, implementing robust tick control measures, promptly treating udder and teat injuries, adhering to an appropriate milking order. These affordable interventions can significantly curb the spread of S. aureus. Furthermore, where feasible, the application of dry cow therapy could be beneficial in reducing prevalence of S.aureus associated intrammary infections.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that S. aureus-associated mastitis remains the major problem in the major milk-producing areas of Central Ethiopia, with a higher occurrence of subclinical mastitis. The age of cow, and the tick infestation status and/or lesions on the udder and teat were identified as significant risk factors associated with S. aureus isolation. A high proportion of the tested isolates exhibited multidrug resistance and carried blaZ gene, indicating widespread resistance to penicillin G. The findings underscore the need for improved mastitis control strategies, including enhanced udder and teat hygiene, effective tick control, appropriate milking order, implementation of dry cow therapy, and the judicious selection of antimicrobials for the treatment of mastitis. Additionally, ongoing monitoring of newly emerging antimicrobial-resistant species through molecular and larger sample size studies is recommended.
