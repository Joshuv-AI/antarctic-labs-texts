GraphTME: graph-based framework for predicting immunotherapy response by interpreting tumour microenvironment interactions using spatial transcriptomics
Authors: Hoyeon Jeong, Junghan Oh, Yoon-La Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), which reactivate T-cell responses against tumours, show limited clinical efficacy due to low response rates and the lack of robust predictive biomarkers. Cell–cell interactions within the tumour microenvironment influence therapeutic response and can be analysed at single-cell resolution using imaging-based spatial transcriptomics. We present GraphTME, a spatially informed and biologically interpretable framework that predicts anti-PD-1 response by modelling pathway-specific ligand-receptor signalling as a multi-relational directed graph with edge weights inversely scaled by spatial distance. Using CD8+ T cells from single-cell RNA sequencing data of ICI-treated non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients, we trained a model to infer immune responsiveness. GraphTME achieved an F1 score exceeding 0.83 in predicting ICI response and was examined using MERFISH data from NSCLC patients with clinical responses. In these patients, CD8+ T cells predicted as responders exhibited higher abundance and prominent directional signalling towards tumour cells. These cells also expressed genes associated with antitumour activity. GraphTME is among the first frameworks to quantitatively capture single-cell-level interactions within spatial tumour architecture and leverage them for ICI response prediction. It offers a spatially resolved, biologically grounded biomarker for immunotherapy and a tool for dissecting immune dynamics in situ.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Immunology, Oncology

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have emerged as key modalities in cancer immunotherapy by reactivating cytotoxic T lymphocytes, particularly CD8+ T cells, against tumour cells1. This is achieved by blocking immune checkpoints, such as PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4, thereby enhancing the recognition of T cell receptors and CD8 coreceptors in peptide-MHC complexes present on the surface of cancer cells. However, despite their success in certain patient populations, ICIs have limited response rates, highlighting the need for precise biomarkers to guide treatment2. Currently, PD-L1 immunohistochemistry is widely used in the clinical setting to stratify patients likely to respond to ICIs. In parallel, next-generation sequencing-based assessments such as tumour mutational burden and neoantigen prediction have been investigated as complementary biomarkers3,4. Nonetheless, these approaches largely focus on tumour-intrinsic features and often fail to capture the complexity of the tumour microenvironment (TME), which plays a critical role in modulating therapeutic response. The TME is largely composed of vascular endothelial cells that promote cancer cell growth and suppress T cells, and it is important to understand the interactions between these constituent cells for effective cancer treatment5. Understanding these spatially organised intercellular interactions is essential for identifying the determinants of ICI responsiveness beyond the intrinsic properties of cancer cells6.

Spatial transcriptomics (ST) is a useful platform for capturing gene expression data while preserving the spatial context of tissue sections. Imaging-based ST technologies that use fluorescence in situ hybridisation, such as MERFISH and Xenium, provide subcellular resolution and single-cell segmentation, making them particularly suitable for analysing cell-cell interactions within the TME7,8. For instance, a recent study using MERSCOPE observed that fibroblasts recruit T cells and link them to the ICI response in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)9. However, that study lacked a quantitative modelling framework and reproducible prediction capability, limiting its translational impact.

Recent advances in ST have spurred the development of computational frameworks to model cell–cell interactions (CCIs) within the TME. Several representative models have been proposed to leverage spatial and molecular contexts for interaction inference and therapeutic response prediction. HoloNet10, spaCI11, and SpaRx12 use graph-attention-based mechanisms. The spaCI study predicted immune patterns by simulating ligand-receptor (LR) interactions in MERSCOPE data from patients with colon cancer and melanoma using adaptive graph attention. Spacia13 extended the modelling scope to include both intra- and intercellular interactions using a Bayesian multiple-instance learning framework. This study used MERSCOPE data from patients with prostate cancer to infer interactions between cells in the TME, but the inferred interactions were not visualised or quantified in the context of tumour-immune modulation. SpaTalk14 aims to resolve CCIs at a single-cell resolution using a biological knowledge graph. SpaCET15 also applies spatial colocalisation at single-cell resolution via Spearman correlation, demonstrating a tumour-immune interface, particularly between cancer-associated fibroblasts and M2 macrophages. Nonetheless, none of these models quantifies spatial interactions within the TME in a way that enables their use for predicting immunotherapy responses.

In parallel, recent studies have explored transcriptome-based graph neural network (GNN) approaches to predict ICI responses, particularly using bulk expression data and pathway-level features. Among these, ICInet16 and IRnet17 represent applications of graph learning to transcriptomic data integrated with prior biological knowledge. Although target cells were not specified and spatial resolution was lacking, these studies highlight pathway-based modelling. ICInet demonstrates the strength of leveraging curated gene-gene interaction networks and pathway knowledge to guide feature selection, whereas IRnet focuses on interpretability by projecting gene expression onto pathway embeddings and learning inter-pathway relationships via attention-based GNNs. Together, these studies underscore the suitability of GNNs for modelling immune responses from transcriptomic data while emphasising the importance of incorporating pathway-aware biological contexts to enhance interpretability and predictive performance.

Building on this, recent single-cell studies have demonstrated that cell-resolved transcriptomic states can be linked to patient-level ICI outcomes. Kim et al.18 showed that baseline peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) profiles could predict both ICI efficacy and immune-related adverse events in NSCLC patients by aggregating cell-type-specific gene expression and inferred intercellular interactions into patient-level scores. Similarly, Ren et al.19 employed a GNN on multi-omics and single-cell data in bladder cancer to generate pathway-based response scores, translating cell-level interactions into patient-level predictions of ICI response. These studies provide a rationale for leveraging single-cell resolution while retaining interpretability and predictive relevance at the patient scale.

ICI efficacy is governed by the local TME, where not only cell identities and transcriptional programmes but also their spatial arrangement and proximal cell-cell interactions determine immune activation or suppression. Traditional bulk transcriptomics averages signals across heterogeneous tissues and thus obscures microregional programmes, while dissociative scRNA-seq recovers cell-resolved states but loses positional context that is critical for paracrine signalling, immune niches and tissue architecture driving ICI response. ST therefore bridges these gaps by mapping genome-wide expression onto histological context, enabling detection of intratumoural niches that associate with response or resistance and inference of LR interactions that better stratify responders from non-responders6,20.

Here, we present GraphTME (graph-based framework for tumour microenvironment), a relational graph convolutional network (RGCN)21-based framework that integrates single-cell-level spatial proximity and pathway-specific LR interactions within the TME. By modelling intercellular interactions in situ, GraphTME enables quantitative prediction of ICI responses, thereby providing a biological rationale.

Results

ST resolved at single cell level is suitable for integrated analysis of histology and spatial expression profiling to elucidate the TME

First, we examined the spatial profiles and cellular composition of a Human Lung Cancer sample using Xenium Prime. A representative region of 1 mm2 was selected using the highest selection score. The haematoxylin and eosin staining (Fig. S1A) revealed heterogeneous histological structures, with regions of solid tumour growth and dense immune cell infiltration, particularly concentrated in the right area. The spatial distribution of TME constituent cells (Supplementary Fig. S1B) revealed that lymphocytes, including CD8+ T cells, infiltrated the tumour parenchyma. Notably, CD8+ T cells (in navy) were spatially enriched at the tumour-immune interface, suggesting potential cytotoxic activity. To further probe immune functionality, we mapped the spatial expression of the immune checkpoint genes PDCD1 (encoding PD-1) and CTLA4. Both the genes exhibited spatially restricted expression patterns (Supplementary Fig. S1D, E), with high levels near the tumour margins, suggesting locally activated or exhausted T-cell phenotypes. These findings provide a high-resolution view of not only the histomorphology but also the spatial expression landscape, facilitating TME characterisation.

This pattern was also confirmed by Xenium Prime data from Human Breast Cancer (Supplementary Fig. S2). As expected, immune infiltration within the tumour parenchyma was observed in the representative region, accompanied by low expression of checkpoint genes.

GraphTME elucidates pathway-specific individual interactions between cells within the TME

We extended beyond these traditional phenomenological approaches to elucidate and visualise spatial interactions and functional relationships between all cells within the region. We first investigated the interaction patterns across signalling pathways within the representative region within the Xenium Prime dataset of the Human Lung Cancer sample, comparing cell-type levels with individual cellular scales. At the cell-type level, as determined by HoloNet (Supplementary Fig. S3), a distinct signalling axis between B cells and T cells was identified in the IL16:CD4 (cytokine) pathway. In the LGALS9:CD44 (immune checkpoint) pathway, activation of interactions among the same lymphocyte populations was likewise observed, alongside communication between a subset of tumour cells and alveolar macrophages (considered as tumour cells). Collectively, these patterns indicate that, despite the tumour-induced immunosuppressive milieu, coordinated lymphocyte-mediated immune signalling remains functionally active within the TME. Beyond these observations, GraphTME (Supplementary Fig. S4) provides a more granular and comprehensive perspective by analysing interactions at the single-cell level. Growth factor signalling is tumour-centric with outgoing interactions, whereas cytokine and antigen binding signalling reflect active bidirectional tumour-immune communication, and checkpoint signalling is dominated by B and T lymphocytes, forming a dense immune regulatory network. This high-resolution mapping by GraphTME suggests that it can exquisitely reconstruct subtle signalling flows at the individual cell level—which remain obscured in broader analyses—thereby uncovering critical interactions. Although specific pathways are not clearly delineated in the spaCI results (Supplementary Fig. S5), a consistent and prominent signalling flow from B cells and T cells towards tumour cells was observed, supporting that lymphocyte-mediated immune activity is organised and functionally coordinated within the TME.

Next, we examined the interactions within the representative regions of the MERFISH dataset from patient Pt73, as reported by Chen et al. Initially, we revealed pathway-specific patterns through LR analysis based on the MERFISH gene panel (Supplementary Fig. S6). Cytokine signalling was characterised by macrophage-regulating communication, while the category of Other interactions was dominated by immune recognition events (e.g. HLA-CD4), reflecting immune-cell engagement. In other words, interaction profiles at the genetic level captured the heterogeneity of tumour-immune communication across multiple signalling pathways. We further stratified the inferred interactions by signalling pathway to examine functional differences in CCI within the tumour microenvironment (Supplementary Fig. S7). In the Checkpoint and Growth factor pathways, tumour cells and lymphocytes contributed comparably. In contrast, the Cytokine pathway exhibited prominent and targeted signalling from T cells to antigen-presenting cells, such as macrophages and conventional dendritic cells type 2 (cDC2), indicating a focused immune activation. This suggests that Cytokine signalling plays a central role in immune activation, with T cells engaging immune effectors in a focused and coordinated manner.

Among these, we focused specifically on the interactions between tumour cells and CD8+ T cells, as they are key to immune phenotype characterisation. From the spatial distribution in Fig. 1A, CD8+ T cell infiltration into tumour tissue was evident. The corresponding gene expression patterns projected onto the 2D embedding space (Fig. 1B) revealed distinct expression profiles for each cell type. Looking at the aggregated pathway weights for functional interactions between the two cell types (Supplementary Fig. S8), tumour cells showed higher growth factor and Checkpoint signalling towards T cells. We selected the cytokine-mediated pathway as representative for analysing the immune response at the single-cell level, as it captures key effector interactions between the two cell types. We visualised individual cell–cell interactions within spatial regions exhibiting prominent communication patterns. Notably, CD8+ T cells displayed dominant cytokine signalling in the lower-left region, as indicated by the prevalence of red interaction edges in Fig. 1D. This dominance becomes even more pronounced when considering the bidirectional communication between CD8+ T cells and tumour cells (Fig. 1E). These cumulative interaction weights confirmed that, despite their numerical inferiority (Fig. 1C), CD8+ T cells exhibit dominant cytokine secretion towards tumour cells (Fig. 1E). Finally, we examined the total cytokine signalling emitted by both CD8+ T cells and tumour cells towards all other cell types (Fig. 1F). Both cell types interact with a diverse set of targets, including macrophages, stromal cells, endothelial cells and lymphocytes. Here, in lung cancer tissue, macrophage alveolar cells are often considered phenotypically similar to tumour cells. More specifically, the CD8+ T cell signalling was directed toward major antigen-presenting cell types and other immune populations. These findings suggest that cytokine signalling within the TME contributes to active immunomodulation through cell-type-specific communication strategies.

GraphTME model predicts ICI response at the single-cell level for NSCLC with high accuracy

We not only examined the interaction patterns of cells but also built a model to predict responsiveness to the ICI treatment, using Chromium-based scRNA-seq data from CD8+ T cells of NSCLC patients treated with ICIs (Fig. 2A, B). Clinical annotations based on binary anti-PD-1 response labels (Fig. 2D, E) were used to supervise model training, and the expression patterns of responder and non-responder cells were confirmed to separate in a 2D latent space. From the perspective of class balance, we confirmed that the numbers of responder and non-responder cells were equalised as far as possible (Fig. 2E). We next examined rank genes between predicted responders and non-responders (Fig. 2F). Responder cells expressed higher levels of CCL5, a prognostic biomarker for immunotherapy22, whereas non-responders showed elevated expression of LTB, a marker associated with cancer progression and metastasis23. For dataset partitioning, we collected samples to ensure that the training set contained more instances than the test set while maintaining a balanced number of responders and non-responders; accordingly, the dataset was divided into training (P1, P2, P3, P6, P7-pre) and test (P4, P5, P7-post) sets (Fig. 2E, G, H). Evaluation results of the ICI response prediction model on the test set, along with confusion matrices (Fig. 2I, J), showed high sensitivity and specificity. We also confirmed that the F1 score exceeded 0.83 (Fig. 2K). These results indicate that the GraphTME model generalises well across patients and accurately captures clinically relevant immune phenotypes for predicting ICI responses from spatial CD8+ T cells.

Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis revealed a modest yet discernible signature in CD8+ T cells from the responder group. Upregulation of gene sets related to protein ubiquitination (Supplementary Fig. S9A, B) indicated enhanced protein regulation in responder T cells24. These molecular features observed at the single-cell level were consistently recapitulated in bulk RNA-seq data for external validation. The increase in ubiquitin-binding activity was observed alongside activation of RNA splicing pathway (Supplementary Fig. S9E)25 and cytokine/chemokine production pathways (Supplementary Fig. S9D)26. Such concurrent increases in post-transcriptional and protein-level regulation are associated with enhanced immune functional activity, supporting their relevance to anti-tumour immune responses.

The ICI response predictions derived from the GraphTME model on the ST dataset exhibited trends that paralleled the observed outcomes

To assess the predictive capacity of the GraphTME model on actual ST data, we extracted CD8+ T cells from the MERFISH dataset from the patient Pt73 with NSCLC and performed inference on these at the single-cell level. The predicted responder and non-responder cells were spatially intermingled (Fig. 3A), whereas the 2D projection of the model’s learnt embeddings, integrating both node and edge features, revealed a clear separation between response states (Fig. 3B). This indicates that the latent encoding based on functional differences effectively captures biologically meaningful variation and serves as a foundation for response classification. Considering the predictions of individual cells together, most indicated a responder phenotype (Fig. 3C), consistent with the patient’s known clinical partial response (PR) status. To gain mechanistic insight, we examined rank genes between predicted responders and non-responders in Fig. 3D. ANXA1, which is prominently expressed in the responder group, is known as a biomarker exhibiting either pro-tumour or anti-tumour effects depending on the cellular context27. Annexin-1 has been reported to regulate T-cell activation and T-cell receptor (TCR) signalling within the context of T-cell immunity28. These observations suggest that elevated ANXA1 expression in responder CD8+ T cells may enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy by fine-tuning T-cell responsiveness required for effective adaptive anti-tumour immunity. In contrast, the COL3A1 gene, which was prominent in the non-responder group, is known to be associated with poor prognosis, such as tumour growth and metastasis in NSCLC29. In addition, considering the target cells interacting with the two cell groups, responder cells directed a greater total signalling toward tumour cells and macrophages (Fig. 3E), reflecting their more dominant immune-related signalling. These findings suggest that spatially localised CD8+ T cells with effector-like signatures are functionally involved in antitumour responses.

We also applied our model to MERFISH data from another patient, Pt43 (Supplementary Fig. S10), demonstrating its potential utility in independently profiled tissues. This patient, who achieved a complete response (CR), had a higher proportion of predicted responding cells compared with Pt73, who reached only a PR (Supplementary Fig. S10C). Among the responding cells, CXCR4 signalling was enriched, consistent with previous reports linking it to chemokine-mediated T cell infiltration into tumours30. In contrast, non-responding cells exhibited a TAP1-centred profile, in line with prior studies associating impaired antigen presentation with resistance to ICI therapy31 (Supplementary Fig. S10D).

To benchmark our model, we compared the GraphTME with IRnet, a framework originally designed for bulk data but here treated as single-cell profiles (Supplementary Fig. S11). This cross-level comparison between single-cell and bulk transcriptomic predictors of immunotherapy response is supported by prior integrative analyses demonstrating that such cross-modal signatures can capture clinically relevant immunological programmes32. Both Pt73 (PR) and Pt43 (CR) showed a consistent trend in response ratios, with more responding cells observed in CR than in PR, in line with clinical outcomes (Supplementary Fig. S11A, E). However, while GraphTME embeddings separated responding and non-responding populations in both patients, IRnet produced only partially mixed clusters without clear boundaries (Supplementary Fig. S11B, F). At the single-cell level, the limited concordance between the two models appeared to arise from the inability of IRnet to capture the separable structure of tumour-immune interactions that was clearly delineated by GraphTME (Supplementary Fig. S11C, D, G, H). In summary, IRnet captures response trends at the bulk level that are comparable across samples, whereas GraphTME delineates response states at the single-cell level more clearly.

Discussion

Understanding how tumour-infiltrating immune cells interact with the surrounding cells within the spatial organisation of the TME is critical for advancing precision immunotherapy. In this study, we introduced GraphTME, a spatially grounded, biologically informed, graph-based modelling framework that predicts ICI responses at single-cell resolution by explicitly modelling pathway-specific CCIs. Our approach leverages curated LR interactions, spatial proximity, and transcriptomic signatures to construct multi-relational graphs and applies RGCNs for predictive modelling. To the best of our knowledge, GraphTME is among the first frameworks to relate spatially resolved CCIs to functional immunotherapy response inference in situ.

While existing methods are limited to the descriptive modelling of CCI or the ICI prediction from bulk transcriptomes, GraphTME offers three distinct advantages. First, it analyses LR interactions via signalling pathways so that they can be interpreted in the context of immune surveillance and tumour evasion. Second, it predicts anti-PD-1 response in situ using a subcellular-resolution interaction graph of individual cells. Third, it provides clear evidence and quantitative outputs, rather than just trends or patterns. Thus, GraphTME is a robust and interpretable tool for both discovery and diagnosis.

Despite these promising results, the study has several limitations. First, publicly available single-cell-level expression datasets with clinical ICI outcomes remain scarce. Previous studies33–35 have relied on bulk expression profiles, limiting their relevance for fine-grained single-cell-level modelling. Addressing this limitation will require larger, prospectively collected cohorts. Second, imaging-based spatial transcriptomics platforms such as Xenium, MERSCOPE and MERFISH provide high cell-level resolution but rely on targeted gene panels rather than whole-transcriptome measurements. Nevertheless, these platforms offer immuno-oncology-focused panels, enabling meaningful analysis of intercellular interactions within the scope of this study. Third, the quadratic form and inverse-square law introduced for interaction modelling are reasonable and interpretable, although they may not capture all nonlinear and multifactorial aspects of biological processes.

The GraphTME model is scalable. Although not restricted to any specific ST platform, it is particularly well-suited to imaging-based ST for cell-level investigations, while also applicable to sequencing-based ST for whole-transcriptome analysis. In addition, not confined to a specific tissue or organ, the GraphTME model can be applied to various cancer types beyond the lung and breast cancers presented in this study, provided that appropriate datasets are available.

Importantly, the GraphTME framework offers a promising approach for clinical applications. This may serve as a predictive biomarker for selecting patients who are most likely to benefit from anti-PD-1 therapy. In immunotherapy, which has a low response rate, the use of this prediction model is expected to reduce unnecessary treatment burden36. Moreover, it can also be used for the quantitative comparison of the TME before and after immunotherapy by tracking changes in spatially resolved CCI patterns, immune cell infiltration, and pathway-specific signalling strength. Ultimately, this framework may serve as a milestone in translational medicine by linking microscopic cellular reactions to macroscale clinical phenotypes, while also providing a future direction for studying cellular interactions in the context of spatial biology. As ST technologies become more widespread, modelling the spatial logic of CCIs will be useful not only for immuno-oncology but also for neurodegeneration and infectious disease research37.

In summary, GraphTME bridges the gap between descriptive ST and functional immunotherapy modelling by enabling interpretable, single-cell-level prediction of ICI responses based on spatially resolved CCIs. This integrative framework may serve as a foundation for future efforts to personalise cancer immunotherapy using spatially grounded computational models.

Data preprocessing and cell type annotation

The ST data were processed using Scanpy44. Mitochondrial quality control filtering was performed to remove low-quality cells. To allow comparison across gene groups, expression values were normalised to 10,000 and log-transformed. Cell types were annotated using the TACCO framework45, leveraging scRNA-seq reference atlases specific to the cancer type.

Construction of a graph with directed LR interaction for each pathway

To model signalling-based CCIs, we constructed pathway-specific graphs using LR interaction data from the TICCom database (Fig. 4A). For each curated signalling pathway, we generated a sparse, directed adjacency matrix representing gene-gene connectivity and then propagated gene-level information through cell-level expression profiles. Figure 4B illustrates the computation of the distance-normalised interaction matrix between cells, while the linear algebraic operations for graph construction were performed using the NumPy library. Each directed edge from a source cell to a target cell was quantified by LR co-expression, scaled by spatial distance-adjusted normalisation. Let X∈RN×g be the cell-by-gene expression matrix, where N is the number of cells across all cell types, and g is the number of genes. Let Apgene∈Rg×g denote the directed LR adjacency matrix for pathway p, where the (l, r)th entry encodes the interaction strength from ligand l to receptor r. This gene-level adjacency matrix encodes curated ligand-receptor pairs from the TICCom database. We compute a CCI matrix Apcell∈RN×N for each pathway p as the quadratic form Apcell=XApgeneX⊤, weighting edges by observed expression. Here, Apcell[i,j] quantifies the curated ligand-receptor pairs from cell i to cell j via pathway p by aggregating ligand expression in cell i and receptor expression in cell j using known LR pairs. To incorporate spatial proximity, we compute a pairwise distance matrix D∈RN×N, where D[i, j] is the squared Euclidean distance between cells i and j based on their spatial coordinates. From the resulting matrix A~pcell, we extract all nonzero entries as weighted directed edges, forming the edge list Ep={(i,j,wij)∣A~p[i,j]cell>0}. Repeating this process for each pathway p yields a collection of graphs Gp = (V, Ep), where V is the set of all cells and Ep encodes the pathway-specific directed interactions. These graphs collectively form a multi-relational graph structure G={Gp}p=1P, which serves as the input to the RGCN model.

Construction of the GraphTME model based on relational graph convolutional networks

The GraphTME framework for ICI response prediction was built based on two phases: (1) a training phase using scRNA-seq data from CD8+ T cells of patients with NSCLC treated with anti PD 1 therapy (Fig. 4C), and (2) an inference phase in which the trained GraphTME model is applied to ST data with characterised cell-cell interactions to predict individual T cell responsiveness in situ (Fig. 4D).

To predict ICI response from CD8+ T cells, we constructed a relational graph convolutional network (RGCN)21 to model the pathway-specific signalling relationships between cells. The Python code was implemented using the PyTorch Geometric library46.

Graph definition

The model operates on a multi-relational graph G=V,{Ep}p=1P, where V = {v1, …, vn} is the set of n nodes, each corresponding to a CD8+ T cell, and Ep denotes the set of directed edges of pathway type p, each corresponding to an LR interaction in pathway p ∈ {1,…, P}. Edges are defined as (i, j, w) for all Ep, indicating a directed connection from node vi to node vj with weight w > 0 under relation type p.

Edge connectivity

During training, the graph contained only node features derived from single-cell transcriptomic profiles, without any edge connectivity. This is because dissociative single-cell data lack spatial information, preventing the construction of distance-based cell-cell relationships and thus making it impossible to define meaningful edges between cells. Accordingly, the input training graph is defined as Gtrain=(V,∅), allowing the model to learn state-specific representations purely from node features. During inference on spatial transcriptomics data, however, we leveraged multi-relational directed edges Ep derived from pathway-specific ligand-receptor interactions. These edges provide additional context by incorporating proximal cell-cell interactions that are spatially resolved, enabling the GraphTME model to refine node embeddings and better capture local microenvironmental effects while preserving the representations learned from the single-cell training stage.

Model architecture

The GraphTME model consists of three main components (Fig. 4E). (1) The input projection layer applies a linear transformation X(1)=reluXW(0), mapping the input features to a hidden space. Here, X is the node feature matrix defined as X∈Rn×g, where n is the number of CD8+ T cells, and g is the dimensionality of the gene expression feature space. Subsequently, layer normalisation47 and dropout (rate 0.3) are applied. W(0) is the trainable weight matrix in the first layer, and relu denotes the ReLU activation function. (2) The hidden representation stage consists of three stacked RGCN layers. Each layer l applies relational aggregation and transformation as X(l)=relu∑p=1PA^pX(l−1)Wp(l−1), where A^p is the normalised adjacency matrix for pathway p, and Wp(l) is the trainable weight matrix for pathway p in layer l. Graph normalisation48 and dropout (rate 0.3) are applied after each layer. (3) The output layer is a fully connected linear layer Y^=softmaxX(L−1)W(L−1), projecting onto a two-class softmax space for ICI response classification (response or non-response).

Training the model

The model was trained in a supervised manner using scRNA-seq data from patients with NSCLC treated with ICI (Fig. 4C). CD8+ T cells were extracted from each sample and annotated with binary labels derived from RECIST 1.1: response (CR and PR) or non-response (SD and PD). To correct for batch effects, latent embedding extraction was performed using the scVI framework49. After this batch correction, inter-patient variation was reduced. The resulting features were then used as inputs for the RGCN.

During training, the graph convolution was intentionally configured without edge connectivity, such that only node features were used. In this setting, the graph convolution reduces to a node-wise transformation, corresponding to a degenerate case of relational graph convolution without inter-node message passing, given by X(l)=reluX(l−1)W(l−1). Thus, the model learns intrinsic representations solely from gene expression features.

The model was optimised using the cross-entropy loss L=−∑iyilogy^i, where yi ∈ {0, 1} is the ground truth label and y^i is the model-predicted probability. Training was performed over 400 epochs using the Adam optimiser with a learning rate of 0.01. To ensure reproducibility, all random seeds were fixed across the NumPy50, PyTorch, and CUDA backends. The training and inference were conducted on an NVIDIA H100 GPU with CUDA acceleration.

Selection of a representative spatial region

Only a subset of tumour regions actively engages in the immune response, making the identification of representative immune-active areas a basis for assessing tumour-immune interactions and predicting patient response to ICI therapy51. To prioritise biologically relevant regions for CCI analysis, we implemented a grid-based spatial screening strategy that systematically detects tumour-immune interfaces enriched in immune infiltration and spatial heterogeneity. From this process, we selected representative regions of interest (ROIs) measuring 1 mm2, characterised by CD8+ T-cell infiltration into tumour tissue. The choice of this scale is supported by previous studies: a 1 mm2 region was used to assess the correlation between CD8+ T-cell density on immunohistochemistry images and survival in patients with prostate cancer52, and similarly, a 1 mm2 patch from H&E images of NSCLC patients to classify the immune phenotype of cytotoxic T cells53.

Let Ω denote the spatial domain of a tissue section and define a partition {Ωk}k=1K of Ω into non-overlapping 1 × 1 mm2 regions. For each region Ωk, we computed the following three key metrics: (1) nt(k) is the number of CD8+ T cells in region Ωk; (2) ntumour(k) is the number of tumour cells in region Ωk; and (3) IMoran(k) is Moran’s I statistic calculated based on categorical cell-type labels to evaluate the spatial distribution of cell types in Ωk.

The region-wise selection score is defined as s(k)=1−IMoran(k)⋅nt(k)⋅ntumour(k). This formulation ensures that the score s increases with the abundance of both immune and tumour cells, while also favouring regions with lower spatial autocorrelation. In particular, lower values of Moran’s I indicate reduced spatial clustering and greater intermixing between cell types, which may reflect more active tumour-immune interactions. The PySAL library54 was used for spatial autocorrelation analysis. Finally, the region Ω^ selected for analysis is Ω^=argmaxΩks(k).

We then re-examined the cytomorphology of the highest-scoring region, reviewing individual cell identities, their spatial relationships, and overall tissue context. It confirmed that the searching method provides a robust characterisation, supporting the selection of a representative hotspot for downstream analysis.

Inference of ICI responses to the ST data

To explore the potential of inferring anti-PD-1 ICI responsiveness in ST data, we performed inference on CD8+ T-cells located within the previously selected 1 mm2 representative regions (Fig. 4D). Using the trained GraphTME model, we predicted single-cell-level response probabilities. Following inference, CD8+ T cells were spatially projected and classified as responders and non-responders. First, we compared the feature distributions of responder and non-responder T cells by projecting the node embeddings from the final RGCN layer into a two-dimensional latent space using UMAP. Second, we overlaid the predicted labels onto the original tissue coordinates to visualise response heterogeneity across the tumour microenvironment.

During inference, the trained GraphTME model integrates multi-relational edge information through relational graph convolution operations without modifying its parameters. Specifically, given node features X and adjacency matrices A^p≠0, the node embeddings at layer l are updated from X(l−1) to X(l). In this phase, message passing is enabled, allowing node representations to be propagated and aggregated; consequently, the classifier preserves the feature representations learned from single-cell data while exploiting edge-based interaction context.

Data representation and visualisation

Visualisations of the results were produced using widely adopted, peer-reviewed Python libraries to ensure clarity and reproducibility. Multi-framed layouts were arranged using Matplotlib55. Spatial maps, UMAP projections, ranking of genes by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and dot plots of gene expression were rendered using Scanpy56. Gene set enrichment analysis and visualisation were performed using GSEApy57. Graph-structured data, including cell-cell interaction networks, were visualised with NetworkX58. Directed signalling dynamics were illustrated using Sankey diagrams drawn with pySankey2, and chord diagrams in polar coordinates were rendered using pyCirclize. Finally, H&E-stained tissue sections were processed and visualised with SpatialData59, enabling spatially resolved integration of histological and transcriptomic signals.
