Pear scab resistance gene Rvn1 from Ussurian pear is located in a cluster of receptor-like protein ethylene-inducing Xylanase (EIX) genes
Authors: Sogo Nishio, Shingo Terakami, Takushi Yoshida, Miho Tatsuki, Yukie Takeuchi, Norio Takada, Kakeru Miyata, Kenta Shirasawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPear scab, caused by Venturia nashicola, is one of the most serious diseases affecting Asian pear (Pyrus spp.) production. While single-gene resistance has been used in breeding, it is often overcome by evolving pathogens. Therefore, integrating multiple resistance genes from diverse genetic backgrounds is essential for developing durable cultivars. Although resistance genes have been identified in various pear species, their nucleotide sequences remain largely unknown.ResultsWe fine-mapped the scab resistance gene Rvn1 from Japanese pear ‘Kinchaku’ to a 30,281-bp region using genotyping of 4,297 seedlings across 23 populations. Comparative sequence analysis with apple (Malus spp.) and pear pseudomolecules revealed conserved regions containing receptor-like ethylene-inducing xylanase (EIX) gene clusters, which have undergone distinct tandem and segmental duplications. Within the mapped region, only four transcripts were predicted. Their full-length sequences were obtained using Iso-Seq, enabling precise characterization of candidate resistance genes. RNA-seq analysis showed that among these genes, the gene for a receptor-like protein EIX was significantly upregulated in the resistant cultivar ‘Hoshiakari’ relative to the susceptible ‘Kosui’ and was induced upon inoculation. Population structure analysis of the region surrounding Rvn1 suggested that Rvn1 originated from Ussurian pear (P. ussuriensis), indicating potential non-host resistance against V. nashicola. Molecular markers tightly linked to Rvn1 were developed for use in marker-assisted selection.ConclusionsWe identified a receptor-like EIX gene as a strong candidate for Rvn1, a resistance gene against V. nashicola in Asian pear. Its expression pattern—being significantly upregulated in the resistant cultivar and induced upon inoculation—strongly supports its role in scab resistance. Molecular markers tightly linked to this locus were developed and are applicable to breeding programs. These findings provide a foundation for durable resistance breeding and future identification of additional resistance genes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07209-y.

Background

Pear scab caused by Venturia nashicola Tanaka et Yamamoto is one of the most serious diseases affecting Asian pear production. V. nashicola is distributed across temperate regions of East Asia and is pathogenic to Japanese pear (Pyrus pyrifolia Nakai), Chinese sand pear (P. pyrifolia), Chinese white pear (P. bretschneideri Rehd.), and Chinese Ussurian pear (P. ussuriensis Maxim.) [1]. In commercial pear cultivation in Japan, fungicides are applied more than 10 times per growing season. Despite this, pear orchards occasionally experience severe outbreaks of this pathogen, depending on temperature, precipitation, and the emergence of fungicide-resistant strains, particularly those resistant to benzimidazoles and sterol demethylation inhibitors [2]. V. nashicola primarily induces lesions with sporulation on leaf veins and petioles, leading to premature defoliation and a consequent decline in fruit quality. In addition, sporulation on the fruit surface significantly reduces its market value. Therefore, developing scab-resistant pear cultivars is one of the most important breeding targets for pear breeding programs.

The genetic loci carrying pear scab resistance genes Rvn1 through Rvn7 were identified through both intraspecific and interspecific crossing [3–9]. Rvn1, located on chromosome 1 of the Japanese pear cultivar ‘Kinchaku’, was the first resistance gene identified and has been most commonly used in breeding programs in Japan [3, 10, 11], leading to the release of the resistant cultivars ‘Hoshiakari’ and ‘Hoshimaru’ in 2015 and 2022, respectively. Rvn2 and Rvn3 were mapped to chromosomes 2 and 6, respectively, using populations derived from interspecific crosses between Asian and European pears [4–6]. Rvn4 was mapped to chromosome 7 of the Chinese pear cultivar ‘Hong Li’. Although it was initially identified as a single dominant resistance gene [12], its inheritance appeared more complex in ‘Yutaka’, a descendant of ‘Hong Li’ [13]. Quantitative trait loci (QTLs) associated with scab resistance were also identified on chromosomes 7 and 10 in populations derived from a cross between an interspecific pear hybrid and the Japanese pear ‘Shinko’. These loci were designated Rvn5 and Rvn6, respectively [8]. Rvn5 and Rvn6 had weak additive effects on resistance, but their origin remains unidentified. Rvn7 was identified in populations derived from crosses between Japanese pears and the resistant Chinese white pear ‘Changxili’. Although the loci of these genes (except for Rvn7) have been mapped and associated molecular markers have been developed [9], their nucleotide sequences have not yet been determined.

Studies in apple (Malus ×domestica) have identified at least 20 genes for resistance to Venturia inaequalis (Cooke) G. Winter, a species closely related to V. nashicola [14, 15]. However, some of these genes have been overcome by newly emerging isolates, highlighting the importance of pyramiding multiple resistance genes in apple breeding programs. Rvi6 (Vf), the most widely utilized resistance gene in apple breeding, originates from the wild species Malus floribunda 821 and encodes a leucine-rich repeat receptor-like protein (LRR-RLP) [16, 17]. Several other genes, including Rvi1, Rvi3, and Rvi15, have been mapped within or near clusters of nucleotide-binding site LRR (NBS-LRR) genes. In European pear (P. communis), V. pyrina Aderh. is a prevalent and economically significant pathogen, and several resistance genes have been mapped [4, 18]. Notably, a major gene against V. pyrina derived from the European pear ‘Wilder’ colocalizes with Rvn1, a resistance gene against V. nashicola originally identified in the Japanese pear ‘Kinchaku’. In addition, Rvp1, a V. pyrina resistance gene derived from the European pear ‘Navara’, is located near Rvn2 on chromosome 2. Interestingly, the mapping of multiple V. inaequalis resistance genes to this region suggests that this locus is evolutionarily conserved and plays a crucial role in disease resistance within the Pyrinae subtribe [6].

Pyramiding resistance genes from diverse genetic backgrounds with distinct mechanisms through both interspecific and intraspecific hybridization is highly beneficial for pear breeding programs. In East Asia, including Japan, Taiwan, and China, at least seven races of V. nashicola have been identified [1]. In Japan, Race 1, which is pathogenic to ‘Kosui’ and other major cultivars such as ‘Housui’ and ‘Niitaka’, but not to ‘Mamenashi 12’, is the most prevalent and widely distributed. Notably, ‘Mamenashi 12’ was historically believed to be a hybrid between Asian pea pear and large-fruited Asian pear; however, a recent genetic classification study has revealed that it carries the genetic composition of Chinese white pear [19]. In addition to Race 1, Race 2 (originating in China and pathogenic to ‘Mamenashi 12’ but not to ‘Kosui’) and Race 3 (pathogenic to both ‘Kosui’ and ‘Mamenashi 12’) have been detected in Tottori Prefecture, Japan. Meanwhile, Races 4, 5, and 7 have been identified in China, and Race 6 has been reported in Taiwan [1]. To deal with the large number and variety of pathogen races, it is important to clarify the origin of known resistance genes and develop cultivars with durable resistance by accumulating resistance genes. Interestingly, ‘Kinchaku’, which carries Rvn1, did not show any visible symptoms following inoculation with any of the seven identified races, so Rvn1 may confer broad-spectrum resistance to V. nashicola across East Asia.

Although tracing the origins of resistance genes back to specific species or cultivars is crucial, the classification of Asian pear species remains a subject of debate, due primarily to the overlapping distributions of certain wild and cultivated species, as well as the frequent hybridization that occurs among them [20, 21]. Meanwhile, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data, including both short-read and long-read sequences, have been published in several studies of Asian pears [22–30]. Using these WGS datasets allows for the efficient detection of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (indels) within target regions. Furthermore, the WGS data facilitate population structure analysis of specific genomic regions and the identification of gene duplications, both of which are valuable for elucidating the origins of resistance genes.

The objective of this study was to fine-map the scab resistance gene Rvn1, the most widely utilized resistance gene in pear breeding programs, and to elucidate its origin. Rvn1 was derived from the Japanese pear cultivar ‘Kinchaku’, which traces its origin to grafted seedlings purchased from a Westerner living in Yokohama in 1877 [31]. However, there are no earlier written records of its origin, and its true species and regional origin are unclear. In addition, determining the sequence of Rvn1 will be highly beneficial for enhancing marker assisted selection (MAS) in breeding programs and providing deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms of resistance.

Results

1st screening of Rvn1 using Amplicon sequencing

Before mapping Rvn1, we obtained 161 recombinants between TsuENH101 (Chr01:9845756 in the ‘Nijisseiki’ reference genome) and TsuENH157 (Chr01:12536169) from 14 populations consisting of 2570 seedlings (Table 1) using capillary electrophoresis. Out of the 161 recombinant seedlings, 100 were phenotyped through inoculation tests, revealing 44 resistant and 56 susceptible individuals (Fig. 1; Table 2). Seedlings exhibiting poor growth or intermediate phenotypes were excluded from the analysis. Amplicon sequencing was carried out for the 100 seedlings using 15 SNP and 16 simple sequence repeat (SSR) markers (Supplementary Table 1). Of the 31 markers analyzed, 9 SNPs and 3 SSRs exhibited polymorphisms that effectively differentiated resistance alleles linked to Rvn1 from those associated with rvn1 across the 14 populations (Table 2, Supplementary Table 2). Rvn1 was located within a 419,122-bp region in the genome of ‘Nijisseiki’ between Chr1_11869256 and Chr1_12288480 markers.

Detecting Rvn1-flanking sequence using long-read sequencing

To detect the sequences flanking Rvn1, we obtained eight homozygous (Rvn1/Rvn1) seedlings from a cross between ‘Tsukuba 65’ (Rvn1/rvn1) and ‘Tsukuba 63’ (Rvn1/rvn1). These seedlings were bulked and their DNA was sequenced using HiFi long-read sequencing technology. A single SMRT cell was used, generating 1,350,227 HiFi reads with a total length of 26.1 Gb, an N50 length of 19.4 kb, and a genome coverage of 51.8×. The reads were assembled into 1595 primary contigs with a total length of 676.7 Mb and an N50 of 54.1 kb. The 419,122-bp sequence in the genome of ‘Nijisseiki’ that was identified in the 1 st screening was BLAST-searched against the assembled contigs. A 256,124-bp region was extracted from the assembled contigs on the basis of the primer sequences of Chr1_11869256 and Chr1_12288480. This extracted sequence was designated Kinchaku_homo, as it was derived from Rvn1-homozygous seedlings containing Rvn1 originating from the resistant cultivar ‘Kinchaku’. Because the sizes of the Rvn1-flanking region mapped by the 1 st screening in ‘Nijisseiki’ and Kinchaku_homo were quite different, we investigated the synteny of the two sequences. The Rvn1 region in ‘Nijisseiki’ had a higher degree of duplication than the corresponding region in Kinchaku_homo (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, multiple segmental duplications were identified within the ‘Nijisseiki’ genome, as well as in comparisons between ‘Nijisseiki’ and Kinchaku_homo and within Kinchaku_homo itself (Fig. 2b). Using AUGUSTUS software, gene annotation was added to the 256,124-bp sequence, revealing 34 putative transcripts that included five genes predicted to encode receptor-like protein EIX2 (Supplementary Table 3). In contrast, 52 transcripts, including 10 predicted to encode receptor-like protein EIX2, were annotated in the 419,122-bp region of ‘Nijisseiki’.

2nd screening of Rvn1 using mapping of short-reads sequencing

For the 2nd screening of Rvn1, 56 recombinants between Chr1_11869256 and Chr1_12288480 were obtained by amplicon sequencing from 23 populations consisting of 4297 seedlings. Out of the 56 recombinants, 40 seedlings were phenotyped by inoculation test, revealing 16 resistant and 24 susceptible individuals (Table 1). As in the 1 st screening, recombinants with poor growth and intermediate phenotypes were excluded. The 24 recombinants that could be reliably classified as either resistant or susceptible were used for short-read mapping. An average of 13.9 Gb of sequencing reads was obtained from each recombinant, corresponding to approximately 27.6× coverage of the pear genome. For mapping, both the ‘Nijisseiki’ genome and the Kinchaku_homo sequence were used as reference genomes, following the GATK4 Best Practices pipeline. In the genome of ‘Nijisseiki’, Rvn1 was mapped within a 135,166-bp region between SNP markers Chr1:12019088 and Chr1:12154254. In contrast, within the Kinchaku_homo sequence, Rvn1 was mapped within a 30,281-bp region between positions 256k:114,567 and 256k:144,848 (Fig. 3; “256k:” designates the 256,124-bp contig of Kinchaku_homo sequence from the first screening).

Comparison of Rvn1-flanking sequences in apple and pear species

Sequences homologous to the 256,124-bp region identified in Kinchaku_homo were extracted from genomes of several apple and pear species, including apple ‘Golden Delicious’ doubled haploid 13 (GDDH13) (419,291 bp) [22], P. communis ‘Bartlett’ (294,381 bp) and ‘d’Anjou’ (307,454 bp) [22, 29], P. betulifolia (349,588 bp) [26], P. bretschneideri ‘Dangshan Suli’ (422,155 bp) [23], P. pyrifolia ‘Cuiguan’ (418,954 bp) and ‘Yunhong No. 1’ (397,511 bp) [27, 30], and P. ussuriensis × communis ‘Zhongai 1’ (370,821 bp) in [25]. Kinchaku_homo had the shortest sequence. Comparative sequence analysis revealed that these regions shared conserved sequences across multiple apple and pear species but had undergone distinct tandem or segmental duplication events, particularly within a 50,000- to 150,000-bp region of Kinchaku_homo (Fig. 4). The genomes of ‘Nijisseiki’, ‘Cuiguan’, and ‘Dangshan Suli’ had a nearly identical genetic structure, with consecutive tandem duplications in this region. Furthermore, within the fine-mapped Rvn1 region (114,567 to 144,848 bp), the Asian pear genomes (‘Nijisseiki’, ‘Cuiguan’, ‘Dangshan Suli’, and ‘Yunhong No. 1’) had a triplicated structure. In contrast, the European pear genomes (‘Bartlett’ and ‘d’Anjou’), as well as P. betulifolia, lacked this duplication pattern, resembling the structural organization observed in Kinchaku_homo.

Expressions of candidate genes

Augustus software predicted 4 transcripts in the 30,281-bp region in Kinchaku_homo that was identified by fine mapping, but 27 transcripts within the corresponding 135,166-bp region in ‘Nijisseiki’ (Fig. 5a). Among the 4 putative transcripts in Kinchaku_homo, the expression levels of g17 (impaired sucrose induction-like transcript) and g18 (receptor-like protein ethylene-inducing xylanase [EIX] 2-like transcript) before inoculation were significantly higher in the resistant ‘Hoshiakari’ than in the susceptible ‘Kosui’ (Fig. 5b). Also, the expression of g18 was significantly higher at 5 days after inoculation in ‘Kosui’ than before inoculation, suggesting that the expression of g18 in ‘Kosui’ was induced by inoculation. Among the 27 transcripts predicted in the genome of ‘Nijisseiki’, 14 had an average TPM (transcripts per million) > 1 (Fig. 5c). The expression levels of Ppy01g0678.1 (impaired sucrose induction-like transcript), Ppy01g0679.1 (receptor-like protein EIX2 like transcript), and Ppy01g0686.1 (aquaporin PIP1-3-like transcript) were significantly higher in ‘Hoshiakari’ than in ‘Kosui’ before inoculation, whereas that of Ppy01g0685.1 was significantly lower in ‘Hoshiakari’ (Figs. 5c and 6). Furthermore, in ‘Kosui’, the expression levels of Ppy01g0666.1, Ppy01g0673.1 (receptor-like protein EIX2-like transcript), and Ppy01g0679.1 (receptor-like protein EIX2-like transcript), were significantly higher at 5 days after inoculation than before inoculation, whereas those of Ppy01g0682.1 and Ppy01g0687.1 were significantly lower at 5 days after inoculation than before inoculation (Figs. 5c and 6). A Venn diagram summary of the results shows that Ppy01g0679.1 is the only transcript that exhibited significant differential expression between ‘Hoshiakari’ and ‘Kosui’ as well as before and after inoculation in ‘Kosui.’ Moreover, this transcript is encoded within the genomic region refined by fine mapping, making it a strong candidate for Rvn1.

The g18 transcript predicted in Kinchaku_homo is homologous to Ppy01g0673.1, Ppy01g0679.1, and Ppy01g0692.1 in ‘Nijisseiki’. These transcripts exhibited similar gene expression patterns, suggesting that some reads mapping to g18 in Kinchaku_homo may also map to these three homologs in ‘Nijisseiki’. To determine the precise sequence of g18, we performed Iso-Seq using RNA extracted from the leaves of Rvn1-homozygous seedlings with resistance originating from ‘Kinchaku’ (i.e., the ‘Tsukuba 65’ × ‘Tsukuba 63’ seedlings that were the source of Kinchaku_homo) at 5 days after inoculation. The longest read mapped to the g18 region was selected as the sequence of g18, which contained an open reading frame (ORF) of 3252 bp, encoding 1084 deduced amino acids, and included a single intron of 197 bp (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Orthologs of g18 were identified in other genomes, and a phylogenetic tree based on amino acid sequences was constructed (Supplementary Fig. 1b). The g18 transcript in Kinchaku_homo clustered within the same clade as Pdr1g006410 from the ‘Zhongai 1’ genome, sharing 91.3% sequence identity. However, it was not possible to definitively determine the exact species of origin for g18, as the bootstrap value for the clade containing g18 and Pdr1g006410 was moderate (0.68), and the sequence similarities between g18 and other orthologs were also high.

Developing molecular markers associated with Rvn1

To facilitate MAS for Rvn1, we designed SSR markers using sequences outside the duplication-rich region (from 50,000 to 150,000 bp in Kinchaku_homo). Two SSRs, vnk256124_05, which is located at Chr1:11920226 in the genome of ‘Nijisseiki’ and at 256k:49,796 in Kinchaku_homo, and vnk256124_12, which is located at Chr1:12213796 in the genome of ‘Nijisseiki’ and at 256k:183,050 in Kinchaku_homo, produced clear and distinguishable fragments across a diverse set of pear genetic resources (Table 3, Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). The 141-bp allele of vnk256124_05 in ‘Kinchaku’ and the 166-bp allele of vnk256124_12 in ‘Kinchaku’ were linked to Rvn1, and the combination of these alleles was unique to founders of Japanese pear breeding programs. While some native Japanese and Chinese white pear cultivars carried either the 141-bp allele of vnk256124_05 or the 166-bp allele of vnk256124_12, no cultivar of either type was found to possess both alleles simultaneously. Interestingly, the high prevalence of these alleles in wild Ussurian pear populations in Japan and China suggests that this genomic region originated from wild Ussurian pear.

Putative origin of Rvn1

To estimate the origin of Rvn1, we performed population structure analysis using short-read sequencing data of 10 Japanese pear, 10 Chinese white pear, 10 Ussurian pear, and 10 European pear accessions [32]. When genome-wide SNPs were analyzed, the population structure analysis at K = 2 differentiated the Occidental and Oriental pear populations. At K = 3, the wild Ussurian pear group was differentiated from the Oriental pear groups. Finally, at K = 4, Japanese pear and Chinese white pear populations formed distinct clusters. The resistant ‘Kinchaku’ carried genetic components only from the Japanese pear cluster. To further investigate the genetic composition of the region surrounding Rvn1, we analyzed SNPs from the genomic region PPY_r1.0chr01:11869228–12,154,254, identified through the 1 st screening. At K = 2, Occidental and Oriental pear populations were differentiated, consistent with genome-wide SNP analysis. At K = 3, Chinese white pear and Ussurian pear groups were roughly separated from the Japanese pear group. At K = 4, some Japanese pear and Chinese white pear cultivars formed a distinct new cluster. Notably, at K = 3 and K = 4, ‘Kinchaku’ carried genetic components from both Japanese pear and Ussurian pear. Analysis of SNPs from the refined genomic region PPY_r1.0chr01:12019088–12,288,540, identified through the 2nd screening, revealed similar clustering patterns. At K = 2, Occidental and Oriental pear populations were differentiated. At K = 3, Chinese white pear and Ussurian pear groups were roughly separated from the Japanese pear group. At K = 4, some Chinese white pear cultivars and Ussurian pear individuals formed a new cluster. At K = 3 and K = 4, ‘Kinchaku’ carried genetic components not only from the Japanese pear cluster but also from Chinese white pear and Ussurian pear groups. Those results suggest that the origin of Rvn1 was Ussurian pear.

Discussion

We narrowed down the pear resistance gene Rvn1 to 30,218 bp in Kinchaku_homo by fine mapping. The molecular markers TsuENH101 and TsuENH157 [33], which have been used in Japanese pear breeding programs [11], lie genetically distant from Rvn1, ~ 2.4 and ~ 0.4 Mbp, respectively, away in physical distance, and 5.3 and 3.4 cM, respectively, apart on the linkage map. To improve MAS, we developed two new molecular markers, vnk256124_05 and vnk256124_12, which are 123,371 bp apart in Kinchaku_homo. The estimated recombination rate between these markers of 0.5 cM indicates that recombination events are rare, thus making them highly suitable for MAS. The genomic region spanning these markers exhibits a duplicative structure conserved across several pear species (Fig. 4). In addition, a candidate gene, Receptor-like protein EIX2, was identified within this region. This gene belongs to a large family comprising 89 homologs in ‘Nijisseiki’. To avoid marker allele imbalance caused by the duplicative structure and multi-locus amplification of the EIX2 gene family, we designed SSR markers outside the duplicative region rather than within the EIX2 gene family. The target al.leles, 141 bp in vnk256124_05 and 166 bp in vnk256124_12, proved to be rare in Japanese pear and Chinese white pear. This suggests that these markers are valuable for MAS in Asian pear breeding programs, providing effective tools for developing scab-resistant cultivars.

Within the 30,281-bp region narrowed down by fine mapping of Kinchaku_homo, only four transcripts (g15 to g18) were predicted by AUGUSTUS software, and the actual transcript sequences were determined by Iso-Seq. The annotations for these four genes included aquaporin PIP1-1, probable glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase 8, impaired sucrose induction, and receptor-like protein EIX2, respectively. The first two genes had similar expression levels in both the resistant ‘Hoshiakari’ and the susceptible ‘Kosui’. However, the expression of the latter two genes was significantly higher in ‘Hoshiakari’ than in ‘Kosui’ (Fig. 5a). Interestingly, the expression of g18, which encodes receptor-like protein EIX2, was enhanced in ‘Kosui’ only after inoculation; additionally, its expression was higher in ‘Hoshiakari’ than in ‘Kosui’ both before and after inoculation. The putative transcripts of the other three genes (g15 to g17) have not been previously associated to resistance genes. Given the putative functions of the four genes and their expression patterns, we consider g18 the most likely candidate gene for pear scab resistance. EIX is a potent elicitor of plant defense responses [33–35]. In tomato, LeEIX2, an LRR receptor-like protein, plays a role in binding of fungal elicitor EIX and triggering a hypersensitive response (Ron and Avni, 2004). Similarly, Nicotiana benthamiana NbEIX2 mediates the perception of an EIX-like protein from Verticillium dahlia [36]. However, the resistance reaction mediated by Rvn1 is not associated with a hypersensitive response, as resistant cultivars carrying Rvn1 exhibit no visible symptoms on inoculated leaves [3, 12]. Interestingly, in this study’s inoculation tests, some resistant individuals (i.e., those without sporulation) displayed necrosis and chlorosis, suggesting that g18 may act as a trigger for plant defense responses involving necrotic and chlorotic reactions.

The population structure analyses of the region surrounding Rvn1 and the common alleles present in vnk256124_05 and vnk256124_12 in both ‘Kinchaku’ and Ussurian pear accessions suggest that Rvn1 originated from P. ussuriensis, not from P. pyrifolia (Supplementary Table 5; Fig. 7). The Ussurian pears examined in this study, including cultivated Chinese Ussurian pear and wild P. ussuriensis from China, Russia, and Japan, grow in relatively cool areas, where pear scab is not a major problem. In contrast, the main putative ancestor of Japanese pear cultivars is P. pyrifolia from Zhejiang province in China [37]. Therefore, Rvn1 may confer non-host resistance to the races that are pathogenic to P. pyrifolia in Japan and China. This hypothesis is supported by Ishii et al. [1], who reported that ‘Kinchaku’, a cultivar carrying Rvn1, showed no visible symptoms after inoculation with any of the seven races extracted from cultivated P. pyrifolia from Japan, China, and Taiwan.

In plants, many resistance genes, including those encoding nucleotide-binding site (NBS) proteins, have undergone tandem and segmental duplication events, resulting in clusters of paralogous sequences and translocations to unlinked genomic regions [38–41]. These duplicated resistance genes may acquire novel host defense functions and are often retained under selective pressure. The number of tandem copies varies among plant species. Notably, apple—a close relative of pear—has the highest proportion of tandemly duplicated genes among 39 surveyed plant species, accounting for 26.1% of predicted genes [42]. Furthermore, the Rosaceae family exhibits a higher percentage of NBS-encoding genes (1.05–2.05%) compared to the Cucurbitaceae (0.19–0.27%) [43]. In our study, we identified a duplicative structure within the 419,313-bp region flanking the Rvn1 gene in ‘Nijisseiki’, consisting of 10 transcripts encoding receptor-like protein EIX among 52 putative transcripts. This structure was also observed in other Asian cultivated pears, including ‘Dangshan Suli’, ‘Cuiguan’, and ‘Yunhong No. 1’ (Figs. 2 and 4). In contrast, Kinchaku_homo possesses a shorter sequence (256,124 bp) in this region and fewer duplicated genes (Fig. 2), yet still carries a resistance gene for V. nashicola. This may suggest that the ancestors of ‘Kinchaku’ experienced a reduction in duplicative structure, potentially due to relaxed selective pressure following the acquisition of Rvn1.The retention of tandem duplication–rich regions in Asian cultivated pears may reflect adaptation to temperate and humid environments, which are conducive to V. nashicola and other pathogens. Such conditions could have contributed to the maintenance of diverse resistance gene clusters, including those encoding receptor-like protein EIX. While these observations suggest a possible link between environmental pressure and gene duplication patterns, further functional validation is needed to confirm the role of these duplications in disease resistance. Overall, the variation in duplication patterns surrounding Rvn1 across different pear cultivars may provide insights into the evolutionary dynamics of plant-pathogen interactions and resistance gene diversification.

Conclusions

We identified a candidate gene for resistance to V. nashicola, one of the most common diseases in Asian pears. We developed molecular markers tightly linked to this trait, which will be applied in Asian pear breeding programs. The resistance gene is presumed to have originated from Ussurian pear (P. ussuriensis), which is genetically distinct from the Asian large-fruit groups, including P. pyrifolia, so Rvn1 may function as a non-host-resistance gene. Previous studies have shown that the cultivation of cultivars with a single resistance gene can lead to the breakdown of resistance. Unfortunately, a new race pathogenic to cultivars carrying Rvn1 was recently reported [44]. Therefore, it is crucial to develop new cultivars incorporating different resistance genes derived from diverse genetic backgrounds. This study represents an important first step toward resistance gene pyramiding in Asian large-fruit pears, as well as in clarifying pathogen races and identifying the responsible resistance genes.

Methods

Plant materials and DNA and RNA extraction

For Rvn1 genetic mapping, we used 23 populations comprising a total of 4297 seedlings (Table 1). In the 1 st screening, recombinants between molecular markers TsuENH101 and TsuENH157 were selected from 14 populations containing 2570 seedlings. Since the 138-bp allele of TsuENH101 and the 173-bp allele of TsuENH157 are linked to Rvn1, individuals carrying only one of these alleles were defined as recombinants. In the 2nd screening, recombinants between markers Chr1_11869256 and Chr1_12288480 were identified from all 23 populations. A total of 24 recombinants were selected for whole-genome sequencing, along with four resistant cultivars carrying Rvn1 and 13 susceptible cultivars (Supplementary Table 6). Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves of the seedlings and cultivars using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany).

RNA-seq analysis was performed with the resistant ‘Hoshiakari’, which carries Rvn1 and is a descendant of ‘Kinchaku’, along with the susceptible ‘Kosui.’ Leaf samples were collected before inoculation and at 2 days and 5 days after inoculation. Inoculation was performed by spray application. Total RNA was extracted from frozen samples using the hot borate method [45].

Inoculation with V. nashicola

For the genetic mapping study, seedlings were inoculated with V. nashicola following the methods of Terakami et al. [3] and Takeuchi et al. [6] with slight modifications. Inoculation was performed when five to eight newly unfolded leaves had emerged, typically from late April to early May depending on the year. Fresh conidia of V. nashicola were collected from infected leaves of the susceptible ‘Chojuro’ and ‘Kosui’ or obtained from an isolate, HA-38, distributed by a National Agriculture and Food Research Organization (NARO) Genebank project (Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (MAFF) No. 615029). The conidia were suspended in 0.1% sucrose, 0.01% Tween 20 solution and adjusted to a concentration of 2.5 × 10⁵ conidia per mL before inoculation. The conidial suspension was sprayed over the entire plant surface from a mist sprayer (200 mL per 100 plants). After air-drying, the inoculated plants were incubated in a moist chamber at 20°C with 90–95% relative humidity for 48 h, then transferred to a greenhouse where the temperature was maintained at 20–30°C through natural ventilation and humidity remained at ambient levels. Scab resistance was evaluated 4 weeks after inoculation using the top 10 leaves from the shoot apex. Plants exhibiting black lesions with sporulation were classified as susceptible, whereas those showing no symptoms, chlorosis, or only pinpoint brown or black spots were classified as resistant. ‘Hoshiakari’, a cultivar resistant to V. nashicola, was used as the negative control, while ‘Kosui’, a susceptible cultivar, served as the positive control. Phenotypic data for the genetic mapping study were collected from 2020 to 2023, with most individuals and cultivars evaluated over multiple years.

For the RNA-seq experiment, the isolate HA-38 was used for inoculation. Untreated individuals served as the negative control, representing plants prior to inoculation. In 2022, scions of ‘Kosui’ and ‘Hoshiakari’ were grafted onto rootstocks, and the resulting grafted plants were inoculated using the same method as described for the genetic mapping experiments.

Amplicon sequencing for 1st screening of Rvn1

Amplicon sequencing was conducted using a simplified AmpSeq approach, which uses a mixture of two primer sets [46], the first consisting of tailed target primers, the second of primers that contain flow-cell binding sites, indexes, and tail sequences complementary to those in the first set. SSR loci in the target region were identified using MISA software (http://pgrc.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/). A Genomic variant call format (GVCF) file was generated using the GATK Best Practices pipeline [47], incorporating data from four resistant cultivars carrying heterozygous Rvn1 and 13 susceptible cultivars. SNPs that were heterozygous in the four resistant cultivars and homozygous in the 13 susceptible cultivars were selected for amplicon sequencing. Both SSR and SNP primers were designed in Primer3-2.4.0 software [48]. The sequences of the primers used for simplified AmpSeq are listed in Supplementary Table 1. PCR amplification was performed in 10 µL containing 5 µL of 2× Green GoTaq G2 Hot Start Master Mix (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), 0.2 µL of the 1 st primer set (1 µM), 1 µL of the 2nd primer set (1 µM), 2.8 µL H2O, and 1 µL of genomic DNA (2.5 ng/µL) with an initial denaturation of 94°C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 60°C for 1 min, and 72°C for 30 s; and a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. The products were mixed equally by volume in a single tube and purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Inc, Bree, CA, USA) following the AMPure XP PCR Purification protocol. The libraries were sequenced by PE 150-bp sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). For SSR genotyping, the SSR-GBS pipeline created by Nishio et al. [49] was used. For SNP calling, the Genome Analysis Toolkit 4 (GATK4) best practice pipeline was used.

Long-read sequencing and primary assembly

To obtain the sequence of the Rvn1-flanking region, we generated Rvn1 homozygous genotypes by crossing ‘Tsukuba 65’ and ‘Tsukuba 63’, both of which are heterozygous for Rvn1. After selecting Rvn1 homozygous genotypes using Rvn1-flanking SSRs, we bulk-extracted DNA from eight Rvn1 homozygous genotypes using Genomic-tip (Qiagen, Germany) for SMRTbell library preparation for long-read sequencing. The bulked DNA was used for HiFi SMRTbell library preparation with the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (PacBio, Menlo Park, CA, USA). The resultant library was separated in BluePippin (Sage Science, Beverly, MA, USA) to remove short DNA fragments (< 20 kb), and sequenced with a SMRT Cell 8 M on Sequel II and Sequel IIe systems (PacBio). The obtained HiFi reads were assembled in Hifiasm v. 0.15.2 [50] with the default parameters. The target sequence of the Rvn1-flanking region derived from ‘Kinchaku’ (designated Kinchaku_homo) was searched using Blast+ [51] and extracted in SAMtools software [52].

Short-read sequences

PCR-free DNA libraries for 150-bp paired-end DNA sequencing of the 17 cultivars were constructed with a TruSeq DNA library preparation kit (Illumina), and then sequenced on a NovaSeq sequencer. Genomic DNA libraries for the 24 individuals with recombination in Rvn1-flanking regions were prepared with a PCR-free method using the Swift 2 S Turbo Flexible DNA Library Kit (Swift Biosciences, Ann Arbor, MI, USA), and converted into a DNA nanoball sequencing library with the MGI Easy Universal Library Conversion Kit (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China). The library was sequenced on the DNBSEQ G400RS (MGI Tech) instrument in paired-end, 150-bp mode. Raw reads were analyzed in Trimmomatic v. 0.39 software [53] to remove reads with poor-quality ends (Q < 30). Sequences were aligned to Japanese pear genome ‘Nijisseiki’ sequence and to the Kinchaku_homo partial sequence obtained in this study by using the “mem” command of Burrows–Wheeler aligner v. 0.7.17 software (BWA) [54] with default parameters and avoiding multiple-mapping reads. SNPs and indels were mined by following the GATK4 Best Practices pipeline [47].

Transcriptome analysis

RNA-seq libraries were prepared from leaves of ‘Hoshiakari’ and ‘Kosui’ collected before inoculation and at 2 and 5 days after inoculation. They were sequenced on the DNBSEQ G400RS sequencer (MGI Tech) in paired-end, 100-bp mode. Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were trimmed in Trimmomatic v. 0.39 [53]. To investigate gene expression associated with both Rvn1 susceptible and resistant haplotypes, we used two reference genomes: (1) the ‘Nijisseiki’ genome and (2) a modified version of the ‘Nijisseiki’ genome in which 419,313 bp of the rvn1-flanking sequence was replaced with the 256,124 bp Rvn1-flanking sequence obtained from long-read sequencing of Kinchaku_homo. For the replaced 256,124-bp region, ab initio annotation was performed in AUGUSTUS v. 3.5.0. Reads were mapped in HISAT2 v. 2.1.0 [55], and gene expression levels were quantified and normalized as transcripts per million (TPM) in StringTie v. 1.3.4d [56]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the R package DEGseq [57] by comparing gene expression between ‘Hoshiakari’ and ‘Kosui’ before inoculation and between ‘Kosui’ before and at 5 days after inoculation.

For Iso-Seq analysis, total RNA was extracted from leaves of an Rvn1 homozygous individual at 5 days after inoculation. Iso-Seq libraries were prepared following the manufacturer’s protocol (PacBio) and sequenced on a Sequel system with a SMRT cell. Reads were clustered using the Iso-Seq 3 pipeline implemented in SMRT Link. The modified ‘Nijisseiki’ genome carrying the Rvn1 region was used as the reference genome for mapping and collapsing transcript clusters. The longest sequence in each cluster was selected as the final transcript.

Among the candidate transcripts narrowed down by fine mapping of Rvn1, the protein sequence of the receptor-like protein EIX2-like transcript was translated from the candidate transcripts and aligned with orthologs of the receptor-like protein EIX2-like genes at the Rvn1 region from the genomes of other pear species in MEGA 11 [58]. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method with default parameters.

Analysis of genome structure, segmental duplication, and population structure of the Rvn1-flanking region

To compare the Rvn1-flanking haplotype from ‘Kinchaku’ with those of other apple and pear genomes [22–30], we used primer sequences spanning Chr1_11869256 to Chr1_12288480 to extract sequences corresponding to the Rvn1-flanking haplotypes in SAMtools. The haplotype from ‘Kinchaku’ and those from other genomes were aligned using NUCmer in MUMmer package v. 3.0 to detect sequence variations [59]. The segmental duplication structure was identified in BISER [60] and visualized with the R package genoplotR [61]. Population structure was analyzed in ADMIXTURE v. 1.30 [62] on the basis of both whole-genome data and sequences from the PPY_r1.0chr01:11869228–12,154,254 and PPY_r1.0chr01:12019088–12,288,540 regions, for 10 Japanese pear, 10 Chinese white pear, 10 Ussurian pear, and 10 European pear accessions.
