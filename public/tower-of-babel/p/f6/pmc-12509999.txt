Percutaneous transcarotid and transbrachial access in the endovascular management of tracheoinnominate artery fistulas and review of current therapies
Authors: Catherine Chinhchu Lai, Aldin Malkoc, Brianna-Marie Riu, So Un Kim, Angel Guan, Samuel Schwartz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tracheoinnominate fistulas (TIFs) are rare but lethal complications of tracheostomy placement. Prevention of death by hemorrhagic shock requires early recognition and definitive surgical management. We present two cases of TIFs managed with endovascular techniques using a unique percutaneous access strategy. The first case involved a 36-year-old man with profound hypoxic brain injury after cardiac arrest owing to cocaine use, who underwent percutaneous tracheostomy for chronic ventilator management. On postprocedure day 16, he developed severe hemorrhage from the tracheostomy site. The tracheostomy was removed, the patient was intubated, and the Utley maneuver was performed for temporary hemostasis. Retrograde percutaneous access of the right common carotid artery allowed delivery of a 10 × 39 mm GORE VBX stent to the innominate artery, successfully controlling hemorrhagic shock with no change in neurological status. The second case involved a 69-year-old woman with cirrhosis, atrial fibrillation on apixaban, and respiratory failure, who presented with a 400-mL herald bleed from her tracheostomy. Imaging showed a vascular connection to the trachea at the balloon cuff level. Retrograde percutaneous access of the right brachial artery was used to deliver a 9 × 29 mm GORE VBX stent to the innominate artery. Post procedure, the brachial artery was repaired with a polytetrafluoroethylene graft. The patient stabilized and was eventually discharged. Hemorrhage from a TIF remains a lethal complication, but endovascular approaches can provide rapid hemorrhage control in unstable patients with survival outcomes comparable with prior reports in similar high-risk populations. Percutaneous access for stent graft delivery can be obtained from various sites.

Tracheoinnominate fistulas (TIFs) are rare but often fatal complications, with mortality reaching 100% if not surgically treated.1 A TIF is an abnormal erosion between the trachea and the innominate (brachiocephalic) artery, most commonly arising as a complication of tracheostomy.2 Other causes include penetrating trauma, infection, malignancy, and postradiation changes in the neck.3, 4, 5 Patients may initially present with minor bleeding around the tracheostomy tube that resolves, followed by sudden, massive hemorrhage.

The traditional surgical treatment for TIF involves emergent median sternotomy with ligation of the innominate artery. This technique was first described in 1968, and since then surgical management has evolved significantly.6 In 1975, repair using an innominate vein graft to restore arterial continuity after ligation was reported.7 Later advances included various graft materials and configurations, such as cryopreserved arterial allografts and endovascular stent grafts.8 The first successful endovascular stent placement for TIF was reported in 2001, offering a minimally invasive repair option.9

Despite these advances, optimal management of TIF remains controversial. Open surgical repair is considered definitive, but carries high operative risk and postoperative complications.10,11 Endovascular interventions, including stent placement and transarterial embolization, allow rapid bleeding control but may pose risks of rebleeding, especially in patients with compromised vessel integrity owing to malignancy or prior radiation.5,12 Comparative evidence between these approaches is limited and mostly based on case reports and small series.13

In this study, we analyzed published TIF cases alongside two cases from our institution to compare the outcomes of open vs endovascular treatments, focusing on recurrent bleeding and mortality. We also present two cases using novel percutaneous access sites, providing unique technical insights.

Methods

We present two cases of TIF from 2022 seen at Arrowhead Regional Medical Center, a level 1 trauma center. A literature review was conducted using PubMed and SCOPUS to identify peer-reviewed articles, case reports, case series, and retrospective and prospective studies published in English from 2000 to the present. Keywords included tracheoinnominate fistula, treatment outcome, stent, endovascular surgery, and surgery. Inclusion criteria were studies involving surgical, endovascular, or hybrid therapies for TIFs in human adults aged 19 years or older. Exclusion criteria included non-peer-reviewed articles, editorials, expert opinion pieces, and animal or cadaveric studies. Extracted data included patient demographics, comorbidities, cause of TIF, treatment modality, complications, bleeding recurrence, and survival.

Case report

Case 1

A 36-year-old man with anoxic brain injury secondary to cardiac arrest from cocaine use underwent percutaneous tracheostomy for chronic ventilatory support. Sixteen days post procedure, he developed massive hemorrhage from the tracheostomy site. Emergent contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography confirmed a TIF with an innominate artery diameter of 11.5 mm. Computed tomography angiography had been performed concurrently with airway stabilization and did not delay intervention. Owing to progressive hypoxemia (SpO2 dropping to 60%), percutaneous access via the right common carotid artery (CCA) was selected for urgent stent deployment.

In the interventional suite, after removal of the tracheostomy and placement of an endotracheal tube, the Utley maneuver was applied to digitally compress the innominate artery, improving oxygen saturation to 70% to 80%. The Utley maneuver involved inserting a finger through the tracheostomy site to digitally compress the innominate artery against the posterior aspect of the sternum. The right CCA was selected as the access site because the patient was profoundly hypoxemic and had unfavorable aortic arch anatomy, which would have delayed transfemoral or transbrachial catheterization. There were no groin wounds or iliac occlusions to deter transfemoral access; however, the carotid provided the most direct route to the innominate artery, allowing rapid stent deployment.

Ultrasound guidance and prior operator experience facilitated safe puncture despite the concurrent Utley maneuver. Ultrasound-guided retrograde micropuncture of the right CCA facilitated insertion of a 6F sheath, later upsized to 8F. A 10 × 39 mm GORE VBX stent was deployed and postdilated to 10 mm. Incomplete proximal stent apposition was corrected with additional balloon dilation (12 × 20 mm). Final angiography confirmed patency of the stent and preservation of flow in the right subclavian artery, right CCA, and left CCA (Fig). The carotid access site was closed with a Proglide device. During deployment of the Proglide, the sheath was withdrawn carefully to confirm vessel integrity, and prolonged manual pressure was applied after device deployment. Direct ultrasound visualization confirmed no intimal flap or thrombus was present. Continuous neurovascular monitoring was maintained periprocedurally. Hemorrhagic shock was controlled, and the patient remained neurologically stable. Case 2

A 69-year-old woman with cirrhosis, atrial fibrillation on apixaban (Eliquis), and respiratory failure presented with approximately 400 mL bleeding from a tracheostomy placed 6 months prior for chronic respiratory failure. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography angiography revealed a vascular fistula at the tracheostomy balloon cuff level, intraluminal plaque in the CCA, and a type 3 aortic arch. Right brachial artery access was deemed the most direct approach for intervention. Although the patient was hemodynamically stable, brachial access was selected owing to more favorable anatomy and catheter angles to the innominate artery compared with femoral access. Ultrasound assessment suggested adequate vessel caliber for the planned sheath. The patient was hemodynamically stable on arrival. Ultrasound-guided retrograde micropuncture of the right brachial artery was performed, and a 6F sheath was inserted with Proglide closure.

A notable disparity between right and left radial artery pressures prompted ultrasound evaluation, revealing a 5-cm thrombosed brachial artery segment at the access site. Surgical exploration exposed a 5-cm arterial injury, attributed to the Proglide device and prior 8F sheath. The injured segment was resected, and a 4-to-7-mm polytetrafluoroethylene graft was anastomosed, using the 4 mm end distally. Postrepair, bilateral radial artery pressures equalized, and no further tracheostomy bleeding occurred. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient's polytetrafluoroethylene graft remained patent.

Results

This study presents a comprehensive review of 38 published cases of TIF, including 5 review articles and 33 case reports. Two cases from our institution pooled our cohort to 39 patients. The median age at presentation was 45 years, with a relatively even sex distribution (21 males, 18 females). Notably, 82% of TIFs (32/29) were associated with tracheostomy, consistent with the previous literature identifying tracheostomy as a major risk factor. The interval from tracheostomy to TIF development was highly variable, ranging from as early as 3 days to as late as 14 years post procedure, reflecting the unpredictable nature of this life-threatening complication.

Treatment modalities included open surgical repair and endovascular intervention, each with distinct risk profiles and outcomes. Among 23 patients treated with open surgery, complications included mediastinitis and transient neurological deficits such as plexopathy, hemiplegia, and stroke. Recurrent bleeding occurred in 17.4% of open cases (4/23), all of which were fatal owing to cardiac arrest. The all-cause mortality rate in this group was 26.1% (6/23). Conversely, among the 16 patients who underwent endovascular treatment, complications included thrombosis. Recurrent bleeding was observed in 37.5% (6/16), with 18.8% (3/16) resulting in death. The overall mortality rate for endovascular cases was 31.2% (5/16). Rescue sternotomy was required in three endovascular cases, and one patient underwent repeated endovascular intervention owing to ongoing hemorrhage. In the endovascular group, thrombosis events referred to stent graft occlusions.

Recurrent bleeding and all-cause mortality were both lower in the open surgery group compared with the endovascular group (odds ratios, 0.35 and 0.78, respectively), but neither difference attained statistical significance (P = .264 and P = .734). Despite a slightly lower overall mortality rate in the open surgery group, the management approach should be individualized based on patient factors and institutional expertise. Reported mortality rates reflected in-hospital or outcomes within 30 days, although heterogeneity in causative factors and patient comorbidities limits a direct comparison between the groups.

Discussion

Our review indicates that endovascular treatment, although less invasive and potentially life saving in unstable patients, carries a substantial risk of rebleeding, particularly when stents are inadequately sized, leading to endoleaks or erosion into the arterial wall. Importantly, in patients with compromised innominate artery integrity owing to prior neck malignancy or radiation, endovascular repair may only serve as a temporizing measure. In such cases, definitive management should involve surgical ligation or bypass grafting once the patient is stabilized.

An important consideration in the endovascular management of TIF is the selection of the access point for stent deployment. Direct routes, such as access through the CCA, can be advantageous in cases where the patient is hemodynamically unstable and rapid control of hemorrhage is critical. However, caution must be exercised when the CCA demonstrates significant atherosclerotic plaque, because manipulation in such vessels can increase the risk of embolic stroke. Alternatively, the brachial artery may serve as a viable access route, particularly in patients with challenging aortic arch anatomy that makes transfemoral access difficult. Nonetheless, the brachial artery is smaller in caliber and may carry a higher risk of complications, including thrombosis and vascular injury. Therefore, access site selection should be individualized based on the patient's anatomy, hemodynamic status, and vascular integrity to optimize procedural success and minimize complications. In hindsight, the second patient discussed in this report should have undergone percutaneous axillary artery access to avoid a brachial artery complication owing to an undersized vessel. In our cases, a balloon-expandable covered stent was chosen to allow precise positioning and deployment in the emergent setting, which is particularly important given the short landing zones available. The ability to postdilate enabled optimal apposition to the vessel wall, which was critical for immediate hemostasis. Although friable arteries raise concern for vessel injury with balloon expansion, appropriate sizing and careful inflation can mitigate this risk. Our access choices were guided by a combination of hemodynamic urgency, anatomical constraints, and operator experience. In case 1, a challenging aortic arch and rapidly worsening hypoxemia favored direct carotid access for speed. In case 2, upper extremity access provided a more favorable angle to the innominate artery but carried a higher risk of vessel injury in a smaller-caliber artery. These cases demonstrate the technical feasibility of alternative access routes but also showcase potential access-related complications.

Adjunctive techniques, such as muscle flap coverage using pectoralis or sternocleidomastoid muscle, were employed in a subset of patients (7 cases), which may provide additional protection against recurrent hemorrhage and promote vascular healing. However, the impact of these techniques on long-term outcomes requires further exploration.

Conclusions

This pooled analysis highlights the complexity of TIF management and underscores the importance of early recognition and individualized treatment planning. Although both open and endovascular approaches have merits and limitations, careful patient selection and technical precision—particularly in stent sizing and vascular access—are critical to optimizing outcomes. Future studies should focus on standardized management protocols and long-term outcomes to guide best practices in the treatment of this rare but catastrophic complication.
