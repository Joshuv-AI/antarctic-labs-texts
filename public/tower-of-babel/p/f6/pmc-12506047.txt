Using the ‘F-words’ to adopt a holistic and child rights promoting approach in developmental assessment clinics in Sydney: a quality improvement study
Authors: Zien Vanessa Tan, Sinthu Vivekanandarajah, Jenna Ohlsen, Bronya Cruickshank, Lydia So
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe way childhood disability is understood and represented in various domains is evolving. The ‘F-words for Child Development’, derived from The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health, is a framework that is gaining increasing prominence. This paper reports on a quality improvement project in South Western Sydney, designed to encourage a more holistic, strengths-based approach to assessment, formulation and documentation in a child developmental assessment service. We implemented the ‘F-words’ framework (Function, Family, Fitness, Fun and Friends) into routine clinical practice.MethodsThe framework was incorporated into standardised clinic proformas, and regular team education and awareness building activities were conducted. We audited clinical reports pre-intervention and post-intervention to assess how well the ‘F-words’ framework was incorporated. We also assessed clinician and family perspectives and experiences with this tool using electronic surveys. Thematic analysis was performed on the open text responses.ResultsAn audit was conducted on 88 reports completed pre-intervention and 112 reports post-intervention. There was an improvement in the reporting of strengths post-intervention. 95% (n=106) of reports clearly documented the child’s strengths at the start of the report compared with 74% (n=65) pre-intervention and 85% (n=95) documented at least 4 of the 5 F-words domains, compared with 8% (n=7) pre-intervention. Multidisciplinary clinicians (n=21) and families (n=18) responded to the electronic surveys. The ‘F-words’ framework was very well received, with most clinicians and families finding it an effective, easy and helpful tool for enhancing clinical assessment, presenting a balanced, non-deficit focused reflection of the child, broadening understanding of the child’s strengths and empowering families by fostering a more collaborative approach. None of the respondents rated the tool as unhelpful, although a minority were undecided.ConclusionThe ‘F-words’ framework can be easily incorporated into clinical practice. Clinicians and families engaged with the service were positive about this approach.Keywords: Child, Child Health, Caregivers, Qualitative research

Introduction

Children with neurodevelopmental disability experience unique and personalised challenges across many domains of their life such as learning and applying knowledge, communication, self-care, domestic activities, interpersonal relationships, school and community life.1 However, they also present with unique strengths and measures of resilience that can influence their trajectory.2 The way childhood disability is understood and represented in various domains is undergoing evolution. An important trigger has been the WHO’s International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF).

The ICF framework

In 2001, WHO published the ICF framework which expands connections between health and health conditions to provide a more holistic manner in which to consider health issues. In the ICF framework (figure 1), health is the evidence of the ability to function despite limitations and is impacted by interactions between health conditions, environmental factors and personal factors.3 This universal framework focuses on an individual’s level of ability and unique strengths instead of their disabilities.

The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health framework.7

In 2012, this framework was used by Rosenbaum and Gorter from the CanChild Centre for Childhood-Onset Disability Research in Canada to pioneer the concept of ‘F-words’ in child neurodevelopmental disabilities—Function, Family, Fitness, Fun, Friends and Future (figure 2).4 This provides a more enhanced and holistic representation of the child and allows service providers to recognise each child’s strengths and adopt strengths-based goals and intervention plans.

‘F-words for child development’

The ‘F-words’ have been cited in many studies around the world to operationalise the ICF, inform interventions and support a holistic approach towards childhood disability.5 An international survey demonstrated that many service providers are integrating these ‘F-words’ into their practice and have found that the ‘F-words’ guide good practice, are helpful to families of children with disabilities and support strengths-based approaches.6

Locally in Australia, the ‘F-words’ framework has been adopted in the recently released National Framework for assessing children’s functional strengths and support needs.7 This framework guides the assessment of a child’s strengths, challenges, aspirations and support needs to develop a holistic understanding of each child and family for service access across health, disability, education and community services.7 This promotes wider use of this framework within Australia as service providers and clinicians try to present functional needs more accurately to other stakeholders.

Strengths-based approach

The American Academy of Pediatrics encourages a strengths-based approach to offer a more holistic perspective on children’s health rather than the traditional deficit approach. It acknowledges that parents are experts on their children and encourages shared decision-making with clinicians.8 This approach allows interactions and therapy to be more personalised, meaningful and works towards improving quality of life. Identifying the broader holistic experiences of children and their families allows for stabilisation and reinforcement of the resilience factors as a way to mitigate the negative impact of the child’s deficits and disability.9 This aligns with the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child and the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with a Disability by emphasising the importance of upholding children’s rights to be valued and celebrated in all contexts.10 11

Setting

South Western Sydney is one of the largest local health districts in the state of New South Wales, Australia. It is one of the most culturally and linguistically diverse regions in the country with 53% of residents born internationally and 68% of households speaking a language other than English.12 The child developmental assessment service (CDAS) in South Western Sydney Local Health District (SWSLHD) is a government-funded tertiary multidisciplinary service that provides comprehensive developmental assessments to over 1000 children annually, who present with concerns about developmental delay and neurodevelopmental disability. The service uses a district-wide template for reports which attempts to document the child’s strengths as well as medical issues/diagnoses. However, the consistency and quality of how the strengths are represented accurately is highly variable. Currently, the majority of clinical reports produced from CDAS clinics have a deficit focus, centred on the limitations and difficulties experienced by the child. This focus leads to families wanting to work towards ‘fixing’ those deficits to achieve ‘normality’, which is an inaccurate and often unrealistic approach.4

Aims

The aim of this quality improvement project was to implement the ‘F-words for Child Development’ framework into routine clinical practice within our tertiary CDAS to promote a more holistic and child rights affirming approach to understanding children. The primary objective is to assess how the use of the ‘F-words' improves the documentation of the holistic representation of the child. The secondary objective is to evaluate clinicians’ and families’ opinions and experiences with the use of this framework.

Methods

This quality improvement study used the Model for Improvement and ‘Plan Do Study Act’ cycles.13 It was identified that child developmental assessment reports across SWSLHD were deficit focused and inconsistent in their identification and representation of a child’s strengths. This led to the planning and implementation of the ‘F-words’ framework tool in developmental assessments across SWSLHD in August 2024.

The intervention

The ‘F-words’ framework tool was implemented into the developmental assessment service in two ways. Firstly, child developmental assessment report proformas were standardised to include a strengths table containing the ‘F-words’ as shown in the online supplemental material. Secondly, clinicians were educated about the tool and how to use it in a clinical context. Education was conducted through regular clinician meetings and emails which provided clinicians opportunities for discussion. A variety of forums and education modalities were used to capture the various multidisciplinary clinicians who were spread out across the district and may work part time. A poster from the CanChild Centre was distributed to clinicians and put up at the five clinic sites.14 Other resources were created with deidentified examples of the use of F-words as a resource for clinicians (online supplemental material).

Evaluation and analysis

A retrospective audit was conducted on child developmental assessment reports in April 2024 (pre-intervention) and October 2024 (post-intervention). A calendar month was selected to reduce selection bias by capturing an accurate reflection of the broad range of clientele seen in our service. The audit collated data on patient demographics (age, gender and vulnerabilities), clinic date and site, type of assessment and clinic type. The presence of vulnerabilities was also collated, including children from culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) backgrounds, Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander background, refugee background, in out-of-home care and involvement with the Department of Communities and Justice. The reports were audited for how well they documented the child’s strengths. The audit template used is included in the online supplemental material. The data collected were tabulated and analysed with Microsoft Word and Microsoft Excel.

Electronic surveys were sent to both clinicians and families via email through the Quality Audit Reporting System, a tool developed by the Clinical Excellence Commission in New South Wales Health.15 In addition, families also received the survey through text message via Sinch Message Media.

Survey participants

Surveys were sent to all CDAS clinicians in SWSLHD. Surveys were sent to families of children who attended a CDAS clinic in SWSLHD in October and November 2024 and received a developmental assessment report. The surveys included a 5-point Likert-scaled questions and open-ended questions. The surveys evaluated clinicians and families’ perception of the usefulness, ease of use, positives and negatives of the framework. The survey also requested feedback on how the use of the framework can be improved. Copies of the survey templates are attached as online supplemental material. Descriptive statistics and thematic analysis were used to analyse the survey results and open text responses, respectively.

Patient and public involvement

Neither the families (patients) nor the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this study.

We used the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) guideline and checklist for quality improvement studies in preparing this manuscript (included in online supplemental materials).

In both calendar months, the majority of the children were from a CALD background (64.8% in April; 62.5% in October) (table 1). There was a similar distribution of patients seen across the five clinic sites in SWSLHD in both groups (table 1).

There was improvement in reporting the strengths of a child post-intervention. Clinician experience

A total of 28 multidisciplinary CDAS clinicians in SWSLHD were eligible to be surveyed and were offered participation. Of these, 21 clinicians (75%) participated by responding to the electronic survey. All the clinicians agreed that the ‘F-words’ framework improved their clinical practice. 85% of clinicians found the framework easy or very easy to use and none of them found it difficult (table 2). 90% of clinicians rated it as helpful or very helpful in exploring and understanding the child’s strengths. 67% of clinicians felt that it stimulated discussion and contributed to the formulation (table 2).

Thematic analysis highlighted many advantages in using the F-words framework. Clinicians felt that it facilitated reflection, promoted a strengths-based approach, encouraged a balanced perspective and empowered families (table 3). Clinicians reported a few disadvantages such as difficulty identifying strengths for some children, some families may find it confusing or inaccurate and additional workload for clinicians (table 3). Four clinicians (19%) reported no disadvantages. Clinicians offered several suggestions for improvements. This included additional education and teaching for clinicians, streamlining the framework with the use of prompts or lists, making it easier to understand for families and allowing flexibility by giving clinicians autonomy to adapt the tool to their own clinical practice and encourage upskilling in the broader learnings inspired by the ‘F-words’. Suggestions for educating new team members include organising group discussions, incorporating it into clinicians’ orientation and providing literature and case studies.

Families experience

A total of 196 children seen for a developmental assessment during October and November 2024 were eligible and sent electronic surveys. Of these, a total of 18 families (9.2%) participated in the surveys, 11 families seen in October 2024 and seven families seen in November 2024. Of the respondents, 83% of families (n=15) were happy with their child’s developmental assessment report. 94% (n=17) felt that the ‘F-words’ table was easy to read and understand. 72% (n=13) agreed that the ‘F-words’ table helped others understand their child better. 67% (n=12) agreed that it showed what the child is good at (see online supplemental table B). Families liked that the ‘F-words’ table provided a positive perspective, was clear, easy to understand, descriptive and helped others understand their child better as shown in table 4. One family felt that the strengths highlighted were inaccurate and generic. Responses included a suggestion to ‘add personality points that give an understanding of the nature of the child other than what is appropriate for the report’.

Discussion

This is the first study to evaluate the ‘F-words’ framework as a quality improvement tool in the setting of a tertiary child development assessment service. This study has provided valuable insights into how well the F-words have been incorporated into clinical documentation as well as exploring clinician and families’ perspectives of the framework’s utility. The intervention strategies of incorporating the framework into standardised clinic proformas as well as regular education about the framework via a range of modalities were a successful way of applying this tool within a CDAS context. There was good uptake of the tool with the large majority of reports documenting multiple relevant and specific strengths across several life domains. This framework was well received by both clinicians and families. Both stakeholder groups agree that the framework provides a more holistic assessment and documentation of tertiary child developmental assessments. These strategies were effective in broadening awareness and encouraging a strengths-based focus during routine clinical practice in this service.

The utility of the F-words was previously studied using an international survey of over 90 clinicians in 27 countries.6 Although the conceptual learnings and benefits of using this approach were noted by the majority of respondents, only two-thirds reported actually using the F-words in clinical practice. Barriers to implementation included being fixed on a medical model of disability, inadequate resources and language barriers. Our study has also documented a high level of satisfaction with the tool among clinicians. Incorporating it into standard clinical practice has helped uptake of this tool. However, the results also highlight further areas to be improved, including addressing scepticism about its utility in its current form as a template for documenting strengths. Some clinicians may inherently incorporate aspects of the ‘F-words’ in their clinical practice to varying degrees (history taking, formulation and feedback) without labelling it as such.6 Incorporating it into routine clinical practice within our service has served to strengthen and reinforce these practices and keep a strengths-based approach as forefront in clinical discussion. Importantly, it has also helped junior trainee clinicians adopt the learnings of this framework as standard practice.

A pilot study in Iran introduced the ‘F-words’ framework to parents of children with a developmental disability in a rehabilitation intervention context and found a positive shift in parents’ perspectives of their child’s disability and behavioural intentions by valuing the child’s self-concept and improving abilities rather than focusing on the deficits associated with their disability.16 Although our intervention was more passive from the families’ perspective, the available feedback indicates a similar positive impact on understanding and representation of the child.

The results also highlight areas for further refinement of the intervention as part of the improvement model. Since this study, the ‘F-words’ framework has been incorporated into orientation sessions for staff in the department, in particular, paediatric trainees and allied health clinicians that work in CDAS clinics. The ‘F-words’ are regularly discussed as part of team meetings to encourage ongoing familiarity and practice. Based on the findings of this study, further education sessions may include discussions about identifying and highlighting unique and individual aspects of the child that help the family connect and celebrate their child. Personality factors can be included in the ‘Fun’ domain. There also needs to be flexibility in how the ‘F-words’ are used. In this quality improvement project, we decided to focus on strengths as a strategy for holistic care. However, the utility of this framework goes well beyond being a tool for representation of strengths. The ‘F-words’ prompts are important triggers to examine the child holistically, including to identify issues and challenges and explore the ways the various domains interact.4 For example, difficulty in being able to identify strengths for a particular domain can be a red flag for potential intervention or referral for support.

A strengths-based approach can build positive biopsychosocial development in young children.17 A case study in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander child well-being demonstrated that a strengths-based approach better reflects community values and principles and is more likely to support positive change compared with standard deficit-based models.18 Highlighting the strengths of a child can also empower parents and support family-based and parent-mediated interventions by shifting the focus to resilience and participation within a family and community context.19 In this study, the ‘F-words’ were identified by clinicians as an important trigger for discussions about the child’s strengths and resilience-promoting factors as part of the broader formulation. This is important for shifting clinician perspectives on disability and health such that a child is valued for who they are rather than being defined by what they cannot do. This can lead to affirming the right of the child as a valued human being and in turn empower the family to advocate for them.9,11

Limitations of the study

The developmental assessment reports and surveys were written in English, making it difficult to understand for those from a non-English speaking background. However, despite the large number of families from non-English speaking backgrounds seen in our service, previous studies have shown that interpreter use is low.20 There was a low participation rate from families, resulting in a small sample size. This may be due to the lag time of the surveys being sent out several months after the assessment. The lag time could also result in recall bias for the survey responses. Families likely have competing priorities in the context of caring for a child with high support needs. This is a pragmatic challenge in engaging our clientele in electronic surveys as a research tool, and further research incorporating consumer feedback in the design of the study may help to improve the rate of participation.

In light of the low participation rate of families who responded, we undertook further analysis for any potential bias by comparing vulnerability parameters of the respondent group with those of the whole cohort (both pre-intervention and post-intervention). There were very similar rates of vulnerability among the respondent group and the broader cohort. This suggests that the sample, although small, was likely representative of the larger group.

Future directions

In the future, an email link of the survey could be sent out to families together with the child’s developmental assessment report to improve participation rates and gain further feedback and insights from families. As a large proportion of families are from a CALD background, the surveys could be translated to different languages. Posters on the ‘F-words’ framework from CanChild Research Centre21 are currently available in many languages and could be distributed to families to help them understand the framework better.

Conclusion

The results of this study are applicable to various settings across education, health and community services through a demonstration of how the ‘F-words’ can be readily implemented in a service provision setting using improvement science. Our learnings can guide other clinicians and service providers in using this framework to present functional needs of a child more accurately and, in turn, empower families to advocate for their child.
