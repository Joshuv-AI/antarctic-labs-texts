Deep learning characterizes depression and suicidal ideation in young adults from eye movements
Authors: Kleanthis Avramidis, Woojae Jeong, Aditya Kommineni, Sudarsana R. Kadiri, Marcus Ma, Colin McDaniel, Myzelle Hughes, Thomas McGee, Elsi Kaiser, Dani Byrd, Assal Habibi, B. Rael Cahn, Idan A. Blank, Kristina Lerman, Takfarinas Medani, Richard M. Leahy, Shrikanth Narayanan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective biobehavioral markers for mental health conditions remain elusive, with diagnosis typically relying on self-reports and clinical interviews. We investigate eye tracking as a potential marker of attentional and mood biases associated with symptoms of depression and suicidal ideation from self-reported screening questionnaires. We analyze eye movements from 126 young adults during reading and responding to emotionally loaded sentences. A deep learning framework was designed to account for intra-trial and inter-trial variations in eye movements, achieving an AUC of 0.793 (95% CI: 0.766–0.819) for identifying depression/suicidality against healthy controls, and 0.826 (95% CI: 0.798–0.853) for suicidality specifically. The model also exhibited moderate accuracy in differentiating depressed from suicidal individuals (AUC: 0.609, 95% CI: 0.569–0.646). Discriminative patterns were more pronounced during response generation and for stimuli of negative sentiment. These findings suggest that eye tracking can provide objective markers of self-reported symptom severity by measuring the impact of emotional stimuli on oculomotor control.

Introduction

Depression is one of the most significant and pervasive mental health disorders, affecting an estimated 280 million people globally according to the World Health Organization1. Suicidal ideation, which is frequently comorbid with depression, represents an equally urgent public health concern, contributing to more than 700,000 deaths annually2. Despite extensive research that has focused on identifying biomarkers and behavioral indicators from clinical evaluations, behavioral observations (e.g., social withdrawal, sleep disturbance), and environmental factors (e.g., socioeconomic stress), early detection of depression and suicidality remains challenging due to the stigma surrounding mental health, the covert nature of symptoms, and the frequent overlap of risk factors with psychiatric comorbidities. Identifying objective physiological and behavioral markers for mental health disorders is thus an important research direction. While there are established clinical procedures for assessing and measuring physical health, the domain of mental health is still lacking well-established biomarkers3, and assessment primarily relies on clinical evaluation through direct observation, self-reports, or interviews4. This methodology is limiting, because such measures can be influenced by recall bias, social desirability, and variability in self-awareness5,6, making them less reliable for objective assessment. The unmet need for objective, scalable, and non-intrusive markers for these conditions is underscored by growing societal demands for accessible mental health screening and prevention strategies.

Recent advances in sensing technologies and computational psychiatry have highlighted the promise of integrating physiological and behavioral signals to derive reliable biomarkers for conditions that were previously difficult to quantify7,8. Notably, brain imaging modalities such as electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have been extensively explored to uncover neural correlates of depression and suicidality9–13. Despite these advances, no single measure is considered a definitive diagnostic or prognostic marker, and therefore none can be practical for routine clinical deployment. In this context, eye tracking is a promising yet underexplored tool in mental-health research14. As a direct window into attentional and cognitive processing15, eye movements have been used to assess mood-related biases and maladaptive cognitive patterns characteristic of depression and suicidality16,17. Because gaze dynamics are largely automatic and difficult to consciously regulate18, they offer a complementary perspective that may capture clinically relevant processes not accessible through self-report or deliberate behavioral measures.

Prior work has established that individuals with depressive symptoms tend to show distinct gaze behaviors, including prolonged fixations on negative cues19, avoidance of positive ones20, and reduced diversity in their patterns of visual exploration. However, much of this research has relied on static, handcrafted eye-tracking features that may fail to capture the full spatiotemporal complexity of natural eye movement behavior14. More recently, deep learning on eye-tracking data has been explored to uncover such markers of dementia21 and also depression22 with improved performance compared to handcrafted features. Such approaches typically assume longer, continuous recordings or rely on aggregated representations that obscure trial-level dynamics. This leaves a gap for methods that can model fine-grained oculomotor dynamics in short, event-based experimental paradigms commonly used to probe attentional and cognitive processes23,24.

In this study we introduce a novel deep learning framework designed to model the fine-grained temporal dynamics of eye movements elicited during an affectively loaded sentence-reading task. Our method leverages a bootstrap resampling strategy across multiple trials (Fig. 1E) to regularize model training and improve generalization to novel experimental sets. We applied this framework to investigate potential markers of depression and suicidality in eye-movement data from a cohort of 126 young adults identified as either healthy controls (C), depressed (D) or suicidal (S) through self-reported questionnaires. All subjects undertook a sentence reading task during which they read 160 different sentences varying in affective content, presented word by word (Fig. 1A), and indicated whether they agree or not with each sentence. By time-locking gaze traces to both stimulus onset and response generation, we directly examine how attentional biases unfold as participants process emotionally salient content and make self-relevant decisions. Results

Experimental Setup

A total of approximately 37,000 eye-tracking trials from 126 participants during sentence reading were used to train and validate the deep learning model within a nested cross-validation setup. To that end, we split the participants into 5 distinct folds that were approximately equal in size. We modeled the three available groups in multiple binary classification scenarios, primarily focusing on distinguishing clinical participants (groups D+S, henceforth DS) from healthy controls (group C). We explored two distinct input schemes (Fig. 1C): one centered on the final word of the sentence, which conveyed the affective content, henceforth reading-model, and another centered on the participant’s subsequent response, indicating whether they agreed or disagreed with the presented sentence, henceforth response-model. The former includes eye-movement data starting 500 ms before the final word appeared on the screen, and continuing for 900 ms to capture immediate gaze dynamics upon encountering emotionally salient stimuli, which are crucial for identifying cognitive biases. In contrast, the response-model includes eye movement from 1200 ms before and up until the moment of response, hence captures patterns of attentional processing related to decision making; the temporal evolution of eye movement signals in this case is time-locked to the button press corresponding to this decision.

Model Performance

A summary of our model results is presented in Table 1. The response-model for the main classification task (CvDS) achieved an AUC of 0.793 (95% CI: 0.766–0.819, permutation test pperm < 0.001). Control subjects received an average score of 0.365 (95% CI: 0.332–0.396), whereas depressed and suicidal subjects scored 0.699 (95% CI: 0.669–0.727) and 0.785 (95% CI: 0.760–0.810), respectively (Fig. 2Bleft). Based on those probability scores, the model differentiated the S group from controls with an AUC of 0.826 (95% CI: 0.798–0.853), whereas the differentiation of the D group from controls showed an AUC of 0.759 (95% CI 0.725–0.793). We note that the model was able to identify group S as of higher risk compared to group D, even though such information was not provided during model training. On the other hand, the reading-model (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B) scored a moderate AUC of 0.632 (95% CI: 0.596–0.664, pperm < 0.001). Group-specific effects are compromised in this scenario (Supplementary Fig. 1B-left), as control subjects scored a probability of 0.464 (95% CI: 0.431–0.498), compared to 0.594 for depressed (95% CI: 0.562–0.623) and 0.665 for suicidal (95% CI: 0.633–0.698). Despite the smaller effect sizes, the discrimination ability of the classifier was still significantly higher than random chance, even at the lowest CI bound. At a probability threshold of 0.5, the model yielded a sensitivity of 0.657 (95% CI: 0.625–0.690), a specificity of 0.520 (95% CI: 0.470–0.567), a precision of 0.725 (95% CI: 0.701–0.748, and a negative predictive value of 0.440 (95% CI: 0.404–0.473) for the CvDS task, highlighting the bias of the positive class. The model differentiated the S group from controls with an AUC of 0.661 (95% CI: 0.625–0.697), whereas the D group showed an AUC of 0.599 (95% CI 0.565–0.639) against controls. Notably, the classifier can still, but weakly, differentiate between the two clinical groups D and S with an AUC of 0.583 (95% CI 0.541–0.620, pperm = 0.036). Detailed metrics for all classification tasks are reported in Supplementary Tables 3–6.

Overall, group differences are more pronounced when analyzing the signal time-locked to the button press than to the onset of the final word. The response-model demonstrated better probability calibration for the CvDS task, achieving a Brier score of 0.184 (95% CI: 0.169–0.200), indicating that the predicted probabilities were reasonably well aligned with the clinical categories. In contrast, the reading-model exhibited lower confidence in its predictions, as reflected in Supplementary Fig. 1B. This pattern is corroborated by its higher Brier score of 0.285 (95% CI: 0.266–0.306), highlighting that while the model ranks groups moderately well, its probability estimates should not be directly interpreted as diagnostic. Nonetheless, probability calibration is rarely required in screening or self-report contexts, where threshold-based decisions are standard practice25.

Pairwise Group Comparisons

The remaining binary setups also demonstrate significant group separability, though they do not surpass the performance of the main CvDS training scenario (Fig. 2A). We attribute this difference in performance to the larger number of participants used to train the CvDS model. Nevertheless, we use the additional training setups to get an unbiased (zero-shot) estimation of groups that we kept unseen during training. The probabilities yielded for those groups are highlighted in bold in Table 1. In all cases, the response-models successfully rank an unseen group correctly relative to the others, revealing robust indicators of depression severity, hypothesized schematically as C < D < S (Fig. 2B). Similar insights are deduced for the reading-models (Supplementary Fig. 1B), with the exception of the DvS setup, in which C is actually ranked higher than D. The response-model consistently yielded stronger overall classification accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and negative predictive value, as well as lower Brier scores than the reading-models (p < 0.001) across CvD and CvS, respectively. Detailed reports for these metrics are provided in Supplementary Tables 3–6.

When testing on the CvDS scenario, we found that training a response-model to distinguish group C from D, or C from S, individually resulted in comparable performance with an AUC of 0.758 (95% CI 0.728–0.788) and 0.752 (95% CI 0.725–0.780), respectively (Fig. 2A-left). Both models were also tested for generalization on the respective unseen group (Fig. 2A, rest of columns). In specific, the CvS model underperformed the CvD one when tested on the latter configuration (two-sample t-test p < 0.001), whereas the CvD model achieved an AUC of 0.776 (95% CI 0.743–0.806) on the CvS task and was not significantly lower than the model trained on this task (two-sample t-test p = 0.211). As seen in Fig. 2B-middle, this is because the CvD model achieves better containment of the C-group predictions, whereas the CvS model predicts suicidal individuals with higher confidence. Hence, when tested to differentiate the clinical groups D and S, the CvS model had the best performance with 0.681 AUC (95% CI 0.645–0.718), indicating that the learning efficiency of the CvD task does not translate to better discrimination of the suicidal group. With respect to the onset of the final word, cross-group performance for the reading-model was comparable in all cases (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

Effect of Sentence Valence

A key within-subject contrast in our experimental design involves the differential response to sentences with varying affective valence: those congruent with depression and suicidality (i.e., what we call negative) versus a controlled positive/neutral category. To quantify the predictive capability of each stimulus type, we trained separate classifiers using only one of the two conditions, i.e., either negative or positive/neutral, as well as a model with randomly shuffled condition labels (Fig. 3A-left). When trained exclusively on the negative sentences, the response-model achieved an AUC of 0.757 (95% CI: 0.729–0.784), trailing our main model by approximately 4% on average (two-sample t-test p = 0.039). At a probability threshold of 0.5, the model yielded a sensitivity of 0.803 (95% CI: 0.776–0.829), a specificity of 0.602 (95% CI: 0.553–0.644), a precision of 0.796 (95% CI: 0.776–0.815), and a negative predictive value of 0.614 (95% CI: 0.578–0.649), indicating only weak regressions compared to the full model and a more pronounced bias toward the positive class. Post-hoc Interpretability Analysis

The deep learning performance provided evidence of information inherent to eye movements that differentiates self-reported depression and suicidality. However, while our experimental setup yields useful information on the timing and the valence of differentiable responses, it does not pinpoint specific behavioral differences between groups. To that end, we conduct a series of interpretability analyses, shown in Fig. 3.

Gradient attribution analysis (Fig. 3B) revealed that the model predominantly relies on horizontal eye movements during the final 200 ms leading up to the subject’s response. This trend was consistent across sentence valence and groups C, D, though it was particularly pronounced for negative stimuli, which elicited sharper saliency peaks that the model attributed to the control class (i.e., negative attribution). Group S exhibited more distributed effects that point to disengagement or less structured movement patterns. In contrast, vertical saliency remained minimal and flat, suggesting limited relevance of vertical gaze movements in driving model predictions. With respect to final-word onset (Supplementary Fig. 1C), we also observe the strongest activations in the horizontal direction, peaking around 300 ms after word onset for the clinical group, and close to the onset of the response prompt for the control group. Notably, the suicidal group displays a negative attribution near the final-word onset, potentially reflecting predictive processing based on the sentence context.

To complement those observations, we examined the magnitude of the weights of the response-model’s first fully connected layer (Fig. 3C). The resulting temporal profiles confirmed that both horizontal and vertical components of negative stimuli received stronger weighting near the response time. While vertical movements were weighted heavily, that does not translate to differentiable responses in the saliency maps. This model behavior implies the presence of strong signal fluctuations that do not contribute to the training objective. Such movements can be associated with visually locating the off-screen response button, as the observed activations were not repeated in the readout of the reading-model (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

Finally, we performed ablative experiments to directly assess how sentiment and eye movement directionality contribute to classification performance (Fig. 3A-right). In the sentiment-based evaluation, the ablation study confirmed the diagnostic value of responses to negative content, as discussed also in the previous section. In the directionality-based ablation, the model trained with only horizontal (x) eye movements achieved nearly optimal performance with an AUC of 0.782 (95% CI 0.755–0.808), a sensitivity of 0.791 (95% CI 0.763–0.818), a specificity of 0.590 (95% CI 0.544–0.635), a precision of 0.789 (95% CI 0.769–0.809), and a negative predictive value of 0.594 (95% CI 0.557–0.633). In contrast, the vertical-movement-only model dropped below the random-chance level. These findings confirm that differentiable oculomotor dynamics are primarily expressed along the horizontal axis, especially during negative stimuli. Together, these interpretability results offer a mechanistic lens into the drivers of the observed diagnostic performance.

Sensitivity to Modeling Parameters

Given the potential of integrating eye-tracking markers in screening procedures for depression and suicidality, here we examine the effect of the experiment duration, essentially how model performance is affected when reducing the number of experimental trials. We also conduct a sensitivity analysis on two key model parameters, namely the number of trials per bootstrapped set and the number of subject-wise sets created. Discussion

This study demonstrates that combining eye-tracking data and deep learning could aid in the identification and better interpretation of mental health conditions. By leveraging fine-grained gaze measures, particularly during the subject-response phase of the experiment, we build on prior work identifying cognitive markers of depression and suicidality26. With an AUC of 0.793, the classifier demonstrates confident discriminatory power, suggesting that eye movements can serve as an objective measure in self-reported assessment tools. Still, the observed specificity of 0.674 indicates a moderate rate of false positives, with some control participants incorrectly classified. This limitation is partly due to the class imbalance in our training data, which included a larger proportion of clinical subjects than is typical in the general population. Because this work focused on demonstrating the feasibility of extracting discriminative information from eye movements rather than deploying a diagnostic tool, we used a default threshold of 0.5. Determining an optimal threshold for clinical use would require calibration to the prevalence, risk tolerance, and resource constraints of a particular workflow. Nonetheless, prioritizing sensitivity could be advantageous for screening purposes, as it would reduce the likelihood of missing high-risk individuals who would benefit from further clinical evaluation.

The fact that the model consistently assigned higher scores to suicidal (S) than depressed (D) participants further suggests an implicit sensitivity to suicide-specific features. This is also supported by the significant alignment between model scores and measures of anxiety and suicidality (GAD-7 r = 0.51, SIS r = 0.56) that were not determinative of the group classification in the CvDS setup. Notably, the reading-model showed a significant association with SIS (r = 0.26, p < 0.001), but not with GAD-7 (r = 0.15, p = 0.073). These effects should be interpreted cautiously, as differentiation between depressed and suicidal groups was moderate (AUC = 0.609), reflecting the PHQ-9 overlap between groups D ans S. Within this constraint, our results indicate a preliminary signal that eye-movement dynamics may capture behavioral distinctions beyond depressive symptomatology. Our results further reflect an influence of stimulus valence: classification performance was stronger when the model was trained on negative sentences, consistent with prior studies showing that individuals with depression and suicidality exhibit heightened cognitive and emotional responses to negatively valenced stimuli26,27. Moreover, performance reductions observed when evaluating the reading-model highlight the importance of capturing active, decision-making phases of behavior, which likely reflect attentional biases and maladaptive emotion regulation strategies characteristic of depressed populations26.

Within the specific context of our experiment, these findings may also suggest that attentional biases preceding the subject’s response are shaped not only by abstract cognitive markers but also by the tendency to agree or disagree with each sentence. Prior research28,29 has demonstrated that gaze patterns reflect and even influence value-based choices when individuals decide between competing alternatives. In our study, both saliency analysis and classifier weights point to a strong temporal focus near the response moment, particularly for negative trials where group differences in endorsement rates are expected. To test whether participants visually anchor on the response option (e.g., “agree” or “disagree”) they intend to select, we trained a model using only the ratio of time spent fixating on each of those prompts as input. This approach yielded an AUC of 0.694 (95% CI 0.665–0.727), but with a sensitivity of 0.924 (95% CI 0.905–0.941) and a specificity of 0.235 (95% CI 0.195–0.277). While this is significantly above chance, it is notably skewed and lower than our proposed framework on the CvDS task (two-sample t-test p < 0.001). Additionally, we trained a logistic regression classifier with only the participants’ responses as input, and derived an AUC of 0.940 on the same task, clearly indicating that the response-model would attempt to decode the response-group alignment. Overall, we deduce that the proposed model partially fits to prompt-based biases, but is able to incorporate differentiable activity from the eye movement dynamics. Figure 4D depicts the fixations made to the two options shortly before responding to negative sentences, averaged over participants in each group. The histograms point to a clear preference from the control group for the “Disagree” option, whereas selection bias is less pronounced for the clinical groups, with S further indicating less fixations on the prompts.

Our analysis thus reveals that, while fixation on the final response carries moderate predictive information, our eye-movement model captures additional gaze dynamics and covert attentional biases that may not always be evident in overt behavioral choices. Notably, suicidal participants exhibited more discriminative eye movement patterns approximately 500 ms prior to their response—rather than just preceding it—suggesting that differences related to suicidality may arise during an earlier, sustained phase of cognitive processing (Fig. 3A–right). A similar pattern of temporally intermediate activation is observed in the attribution maps of the reading-model for the same group (Supplementary Fig. 1C). This finding aligns with theoretical accounts of suicidality that emphasize heightened psychological pain as a central mechanism underlying suicidal ideation30. We emphasize though that the present setup does not allow disentangling pre-decision attentional mechanisms from post-decision motor preparation. Given the multi-factorial nature of the task, the observed effects should be interpreted as correlates of decision-related processing within a cognitively demanding context, rather than as markers of a specific underlying mechanism. Future work should aim at disentangling those dynamics of decision making by extending to other indicators of cognitive processing, such as neurophysiological measures.

From a technical perspective, our deep learning approach proved effective not only in achieving high discriminatory performance but also in revealing subtle behavioral signatures embedded in eye movement dynamics that may elude traditional analytic methods. The model’s capacity to highlight such signatures opens avenues for new research hypotheses and experimental analyses of the data at hand. Nonetheless, deep models also have limitations: they are data-hungry, sensitive to dataset biases, and often struggle to generalize to new subjects. In our study, we addressed key challenges of short, event-based eye movement analysis by adopting a within-subject trial bootstrapping strategy (Fig. 1E) that resulted in significant performance gains (Fig. 4B) as well as a state-of-the-art architecture to model intra-trial and inter-trial variations. Exposing the model to multiple trials per subject ensured consistency, while randomizing trial order promoted regularization and reduced overfitting. Future studies in computational psychiatry should leverage larger, more diverse populations, including those with mild symptomatology, in order to enhance reliability.

While our study demonstrates the feasibility of deep learning for identifying eye movement patterns associated with depressive symptomatology, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample consists primarily of young adults recruited from a university population in Los Angeles. This demographic homogeneity limits the generalizability of our findings to broader age groups, cultural backgrounds, and clinical settings, and therefore the reported AUC values should be interpreted as specific to this cohort. Future work will aim to validate the model in more heterogeneous samples to assess robustness and general applicability. Second, group assignment was based solely on PHQ-9 and SIS scores, which—despite their widespread use and strong psychometric properties—remain self-reported screening tools. They do not substitute for structured clinical interviews or formal psychiatric evaluation and thus cannot confirm clinical diagnoses. SIS in particular does not differentiate between passive ideation and a history of suicide attempts, and these subgroups may reflect distinct cognitive and neural mechanisms that could influence both behavioral responses and model interpretation. Consequently, our model should be understood not as a diagnostic tool, but as one that identifies behavioral markers correlating with self-reported symptom severity.

In the context of suicide risk screening, ethical considerations warrant particular attention. False positives may lead to unnecessary clinical escalation or emotional distress, whereas false negatives carry the risk of missed opportunities for intervention. As mentioned above, determining an optimal threshold would require calibration to the prevalence and risk tolerance of the disease, as well as to the constraints of each particular clinical workflow. In addition, the use of behavioral data for mental health inference also raises considerations regarding data privacy, informed consent, and governance, particularly when collected outside the lab. Compared to neurophysiological or other biometric modalities, eye tracking relies on overt behavioral signals which may reduce the capture of sensitive information beyond the task context. Even in such cases, algorithmic methods should function as supportive tools and not as autonomous decision making systems.

Methods

Recruitment and Group Assignment

A cohort of 160 young adults (99 females) between 18 and 25 years old was recruited in the city of Los Angeles between September 2023 and September 2024. Recruitment was done through fliers and in collaboration with the Departments of Psychology and Psychiatry and both the Counseling and Mental Health as well as the Psychiatry and Behavioral Health Services of the University of Southern California (USC). The procedures were approved by the USC Institutional Review Board (IRB: UP-23-00071). Personally identifiable information of all participants was de-identified and informed consent was obtained. All participants were fluent in English, which was used for the experiment, and completed a series of questionnaires to determine their eligibility for the study as well as their stress, depression, and suicide risk levels. These included the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-925) and the Suicidal Ideation Scale (SIS31). Based on those two scores, they were grouped into three groups: healthy controls (C, PHQ-9 < 5 indicating minimal or no depression), depressed (D, PHQ-9 > 9 and SIS ≤16 indicating depression but not significant suicidal ideation), or suicidal (S, PHQ-9 > 9 and SIS > 16 indicating depression and significant suicidal ideation). Thresholds were determined from previous studies31,32. Participants also completed the GAD-733 questionnaire, related to anxiety.

The questionnaires were administered once at the recruitment phase and once after the completion of the experiment. 13 participants with unstable PHQ-9 scores between the time of recruitment and the time of data acquisition (i.e., having a shift that caused them to change groups or fall into an ineligible range) as well as 1 participant that did not complete the experiment were excluded from the present analysis. Out of the remaining 146 participants (Mage = 20.63, SDage = 2.03, 91 females), 49 were assigned to group C (Mage = 20.59, SDage = 1.94, 30 females), 47 to group D (Mage = 21.15, SDage = 2.22, 28 females), and 50 to group S (Mage = 20.63, SDage = 2.03, 33 females). The resulting groups showed clear separation in symptom severity on both screening instruments. Group C reported low depressive (PHQ-9: M = 2.27, SD = 1.75) and suicidal symptoms (SIS: M = 10.31, SD = 0.55). Group D exhibited elevated depressive symptoms (PHQ-9: M = 12.37, SD = 3.85) and moderately higher suicidality scores (SIS: M = 13.09, SD = 2.06), while group S reported substantially higher levels on both measures (PHQ-9: M = 17.66, SD = 4.23; SIS: M = 25.42, SD = 6.60). Further, all subjects were excluded who had the following characteristics: (1) current or previous diagnosis of neurological and psychiatric disorders including Schizophrenia, Bipolar Disorder, Epilepsy, Brain Cancer, and Stroke, (2) color blindness, (3) having learned English after the age of 7, and (4) a PHQ-9 score between 5 and 9. We share additional demographic attributes (race, ethnicity, and student and clinical status) in Supplementary Table 1.

Data Collection

The participants underwent a visual sentence viewing task during which EEG, eye tracking, and physiological signals were simultaneously collected. Here we only consider the eye-tracking portion of the data. Eye movements were continuously recorded using the SR Research EyeLink 1000 Plus at a sampling rate of 500 Hz (Fig. 1B). Participants were shown sentences that were presented word by word with an inter-word interval IWI = 300 ms (blank screen). Each word consisted of 5 to 11 letters and was displayed for 300 ms, except the last word, which was displayed for 600 ms (Fig. 1A). After offsetting the final word and an additional 300 ms interval, participants were instructed to press one of two available buttons to indicate whether they agree (congruence) or disagree (incongruence) with the sentence. The prompt remained on the screen until the button was pressed or for up to two seconds; if no response was made within this time, the trial was marked as missed. The whole recording session lasted about 55 min.

Each sentence was presented twice, once at the first and once at the second half of the experiment, in a random place, starting with a central fixation cross. There were 160 distinct sentences in total, generated as 80 pairs; within each pair, the sentences were identical except for the final word, which yielded opposite overall sentiment; for example: “My mental health is not sound” and “My mental health is not problematic”. The negative sentences were assumed to be congruent with depression or suicidality, whereas the positive/neutral sentences were not (Fig. 1F). Only the last word resolved the relationship between a sentence’s meaning and a participant’s experience and reflections; we thus manually excluded 12 of the sentences from further analysis as behavioral outliers. These were sentences for which the intended congruity was contradicted by the group-level response pattern (e.g., more “agree” responses from the control group than the depressed or suicidal groups for a sentence designed to reflect depressive ideation). Including such stimuli could confound our results, as they evoke responses more aligned with a different clinical category than the assigned one. Final words themselves were balanced for valence, including positive, neutral, and negative words, across the two types of sentences. The complete set of sentences along with their sentiment labels is shared in Supplementary Table 7.

Data Pre-processing

Eye-tracking markers for fixations, saccades, and blinks were obtained from the raw recording. This information was used to denoise the signal as follows. First, gaze coordinates were bound to the spatial constraints of the computer display (1920 × 1080 pixels). Artifacts—including blinks and saccadic outliers (defined as velocities exceeding 3 standard deviations from the mean)—were identified and replaced via linear interpolation. The gaze time-series, initially in pixel coordinates, were transformed to a screen-centered coordinate system where the stimulus location defined the origin (0, 0), and the screen edges were mapped to (± 1, ± 1). This transformation was applied to standardize gaze positions relative to the display layout and facilitate model training without altering the spatiotemporal structure of the data. At this stage, 20 participants were excluded from further analysis because their eye-tracking recording was severely distorted. Specifically, 6 participants had their recording entirely missing, 1 participant had the recording annotations entirely missing, and 13 participants had inadequate recordings, which we defined as having at least 50% of data points missing. Finally, the gaze recordings from the remaining 126 participants were downsampled to 250 Hz.

The eye-tracking trials were segmented using two strategies, corresponding to two different phases of participant response. The first strategy focused on the response associated with stimulus viewing. It included the time interval from 500 ms before until 900 ms after the onset of the critical final word of the trial sentence (Fig. 1C–up). The upper bound was selected because at this point the participants were prompted on-screen to state if they agree or disagree with the presented sentence. The second strategy focused on the response associated with decision making (button press). It included the time interval from 1200 ms before to the exact time of the button press (Fig. 1C–down). Here, the lower bound was defined as the mode of the distribution of the response times with respect to final-word onset (Fig. 1D).

Bootstrapping Augmentation

We train the deep learning models using the final 296 trials for each of the 126 participants, that is, about 37,000 trials in total. However, the information to be gained from a single trial input is constrained and the inter-trial variability of spontaneous eye movements is expected to be substantial within each participant. To tackle this limitation and augment the training process, our model does not process eye-tracking trials individually. Instead, training inputs are constructed by aggregating sets of 30 trials drawn at random with replacement from the pool of 296 trials, stratified by sentiment category (e.g., positive or negative). This sampling is performed separately for each participant and sentiment class, ensuring that the input preserves individual-specific and class-specific consistency (Fig. 1E). To make training computationally feasible, the sampling is precomputed: for each participant and each sentiment class, 200 sets of 30 trials are generated ahead of time, resulting in 400 aggregated inputs per participant. Each input is arranged in a 2D structure (time × trials) for x and y coordinates separately (Fig. 1E–right), allowing the model to learn patterns across groups of trials rather than from single instances and without increasing the raw number of trials.

Model Architecture

The model architecture used to process the eye-tracking data is schematically shown in Fig. 1F. It consists of two backbone components, a late fusion mechanism and a classifier. Specifically, two separate backbone networks are employed: one processes trials labeled with negative sentiment and the other handles trials of non-negative sentiment (i.e., neutral or positive). This separation allows the model to specialize its representation learning for different affective contexts. Each backbone is based on the TimesNet framework, a recent state-of-the-art model for time-series analysis34. The core idea that we adopt involves stacking 2D convolutional layers that simultaneously capture intra-trial dynamics (across time within a trial) and inter-trial patterns (across multiple trials) by treating the input as a 2D array (trials × time) per coordinate axis.

To capture dependencies at multiple temporal and spatial resolutions, we implement a series of two Inception35 layers per backbone, as suggested in34. Each Inception layer applies multiple 2D convolutional filters of varying kernel sizes in parallel, enabling the model to learn both fine-grained and global features. In our case, smaller kernels capture rapid, localized eye movement shifts, while larger kernels integrate broader scanpath patterns. The outputs of these parallel convolutions are averaged along the feature dimension and passed through a nonlinear activation, following the Inception design. After the Inception stack, the model performs average pooling across the trials dimension to obtain a single latent per coordinate axis (x and y), which are then concatenated. Finally, the latent vectors from the two backbones are concatenated as well, and the result is passed through a fully connected classifier. The model outputs a scalar probability representing the likelihood that the input set of trials originates from the positively labeled (typically, clinical) group.

Training and Validation Protocol

In all deep learning experiments, we used a nested cross-validation scheme with 5 outer folds for model evaluation and 10 inner folds for early stopping. Each outer fold consisted of a non-overlapping set of participants, so that all participants were in exactly one of the folds and no test participant was parsed in training. Folds were themselves stratified by gender and clinical group to reduce sampling bias. Within each outer training fold, we performed a 10-fold inner cross-validation, initialized with different random seeds and used to tune the parameters of each model via early stopping. All models were trained using class weights for the objective function that are inversely proportional to class distribution in the training set. The trainer class used a batch size of 64 samples, a learning rate of 5e-4 and early stopping after 5 epochs of non-decreasing cross-entropy loss in the validation set. During inference, the model outputs a prediction for all 200 samples per participant. Then, the percentage of positive predictions (i.e., P=1≥0.5) is used as the final participant-level score (Fig. 1F–right). Supplementary Table 2 includes additional model hyper-parameters.

During inference, each participant had 200 bootstrapped trial sets. The model produced a binary prediction for each set, and we defined the participant-level prediction as the proportion of trial sets predicted as positive. This scalar value represents the likelihood of clinical membership for each individual and is used to compute evaluation metrics. We report model performance using Area Under the receiver operating Curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, precision and negative predictive value at a fixed decision threshold of 0.5. Confidence intervals were derived empirically through bootstrap resampling with 1000 iterations, considering the predictions of the entire dataset. To test whether performance was statistically significant beyond chance, we constructed an empirical null distribution. This was done by training on the same models and and on the same data, but with randomly permuted participant labels, and repeating the bootstrap procedure. Statistical significance was quantified by counting the proportion of null-distribution bootstrap means that exceeded the bottom 95% CI of model performance, which effectively let us determine p-values down to 0.001. We used False Detection Rate (FDR) to correct for multiple comparisons, hence we report FDR-corrected p-values unless otherwise noted.
