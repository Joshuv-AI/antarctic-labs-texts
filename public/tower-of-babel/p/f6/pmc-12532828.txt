Intrauterine growth restriction induces persistent adipose inflammation and metabolic abnormalities in rats among various postnatal growth trajectories
Authors: Qian Hu, Zhenjie Zhang, Fan Yang, Zhenxin Fan, Yifei Li, Ping Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) with rapid postnatal catch-up growth has been associated with adipose tissue inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. The long-term persistence of these abnormalities and their relationship with different catch-up growth patterns remain unclear.MethodsTo investigate the long-term metabolic consequences of IUGR in relation to different catch-up growth patterns. An experimental animal study using a rat model of IUGR induced by maternal protein restriction during gestation. Abdominal adipose tissue transcriptome profiles in male rats were analyzed at 3 and 9 months of age, considering variations in catch-up growth patterns. The primary outcomes included markers of adipose tissue inflammation and metabolic function.ResultsAmong IUGR offspring, approximately 50% demonstrated slow catch-up growth and remained undernourished at 3 months of age. Introduction

Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), characterized by failure to achieve optimal fetal weight potential, affects approximately 5–10% of pregnancies worldwide [1]. The “Developmental Origins of Health and Disease” (DOHaD) hypothesis suggests that early-life developmental constraints induce adaptive metabolic modifications that facilitate catch-up growth. However, these adaptations may predispose individuals to chronic diseases in adulthood, particularly cardiovascular and metabolic disorders [2].

Evidence indicates that early postnatal catch-up growth may lead to white adipose tissue (WAT) dysfunction through excessive adipose accumulation. This dysfunction can trigger systemic low-grade inflammation, potentially contributing to metabolic disorders, including insulin resistance [3–5]. While extensive research has focused on populations exhibiting rapid postnatal catch-up growth, limited data exist regarding adipose tissue development and metabolic outcomes in IUGR individuals with normal or slow catch-up growth patterns [4, 6].

Previous investigations have predominantly examined the association between rapid catch-up growth and metabolic consequences, leaving significant knowledge gaps regarding the long-term metabolic implications of different growth trajectories in IUGR. Understanding these relationships is crucial for developing targeted interventions for IUGR-affected individuals across various growth patterns. The present study aimed to investigate the effects of IUGR on adipose tissue development and metabolism using a maternal low-protein diet rat model. Through transcriptome sequencing analysis, we examined adipose tissue developmental characteristics across different age groups and catch-up growth patterns. This approach was designed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which IUGR influences adipose tissue development and metabolism, potentially identifying novel therapeutic targets for preventing and treating metabolic complications in IUGR-affected individuals.

Materials and methods

Establishment of an IUGR rat model

The study complied with ethical approval granted by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of West China Second University Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 092 (2020)). SPF-grade Sprague-Dawley (SSD) (females: 250–300 g; males: 300–350 g) rats were purchased from Dashuo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Chengdu, Sichuan, China), a commercial provider accredited by the Chinese Association for Laboratory Animal Sciences. All animals were housed in specific pathogen-free facilities with controlled temperature (22 ± 1 °C) and 12-hour light/dark cycles. After 7-day acclimatization, female and male rats were paired and co-housed overnight in a 1:1 or 1:2 ratio.

The successful mating was verified the following morning through an examination of vaginal smears from the female rats. The presence of sperm under a microscope marked gestational day 0.5. Pregnant rats were randomly divided into two groups: the control group (named as C group), which received a standard diet containing 20% protein, and the IUGR group (named as R group), which received a low-protein diet consisting of 8% protein. Both diets were sourced from Xietong (Jiangsu, China), with detailed formulations provided in Table 1.

Diets of different groups

Given the influence of estrogen on insulin sensitivity, exclusively male offspring were selected for inclusion in this study [7]. On the day after birth, the weight of each male pup was recorded. The mean weight () and standard deviation (SD) of male pups in the C group were calculated. Male pups in the R group with birth weights falling below 2SD of the C group were included in the study.

During the lactation period, dams in both control (C) and IUGR (R) groups were provided standard laboratory chow ad libitum to ensure adequate lactation. To facilitate catch-up growth, litter sizes in the R group were standardized to 4 pups per dam, while C group litters were maintained at 8 pups per dam. When male pups were insufficient to achieve these standardized litter sizes, female pups were included to maintain consistent litter numbers. All offspring were weaned at postnatal day 21, after which they received unrestricted access to standard laboratory chow (Fig. 1A). Based on the nutritional status at 3 months and 9 months of age, IUGR offspring were categorized into the RM group and RC group.

Growth parameters monitoring

Growth parameters were measured according to a standardized protocol. Initial measurements were obtained on postnatal days 1 and 7, followed by weekly assessments until 3 months of age, and monthly thereafter until 9 months of age. Body weight was measured using a calibrated digital scale (precision, 0.01 g) (Delix, China). Each animal was placed in an appropriately sized container on the pre-zeroed scale, and measurements were recorded after weight stabilization. Crown-rump length was measured with the animal in prone position using a standard ruler (precision, 0.1 cm), measuring from the tip of the nose to the anal opening.

Sample collection

At 3 months of age, 3 male offspring were randomly selected from each of the following groups: C, RC, and RM. At 9 months, sampling was limited to the C and RC groups, as all IUGR rats had achieved normal nutritional status by this time point (Fig. 1A). Animals were euthanized by CO2 inhalation chamber. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines. Immediately post-euthanasia, all adipose tissue from the abdominal wall was collected under temperature-controlled conditions (4 °C). Tissue specimens were flash-frozen and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Library Preparation and RNA sequencing

The entire RNA from adipose tissue was extracted using the Trizol method (Invitrogen, USA). mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and used as a template for cDNA synthesis. PCR amplification was then carried out, followed by denaturation of the PCR product, converting it into single-stranded circular DNA molecules. Sequencing was conducted using combined probe anchor polymerization technology (cPAS). Raw sequencing data were subsequently filtered using SOAPnuke (v1.5.6) to generate clean data [8].

Transcriptome assembly and expression quantification

HiSat2(v2.2.1) was used to align clean data to the rat reference genome (UTH_Rnor_WKY_Bbb_1.0, GCA_023515805.1) with default parameters [9]. Samtools (v1.18) was then used to convert the resulting SAM files to BAM files [10]. Subsequently, StringTie (v2.2.1) was employed to analyze the alignment results for each sample, assemble the respective transcripts, and generate GTF files containing gene expression information [11]. Finally, the expression levels of known genes were extracted from all GTF files to create a gene expression matrix for further analysis [12].

Identification and enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Pups were grouped based on age, IUGR status, and nutritional condition. DEGs between the groups were identified using the DESeq2 (v1.42.1) R package [13]. The screening criteria were Fold Change (FC) > 1.5 or < 0.67, and significance was ensured by multiple testing correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (P.adjust ≤ 0.05). Subsequently, the clusterProfiler (v4.10.1) R package was used to perform Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses on the DEGs to determine their biological functions in rats [14]. The aPEAR (v1.0.0) R package was then used to visualize the enriched pathways. Its default parameters leverage gene set similarity to create an interconnected cluster network, highlighting the most connected pathways within each cluster [15].

Weighted correlation network analysis (WGCNA) and Protein-Protein interaction (PPI)

A weighted gene co-expression network was constructed using the WGCNA (v1.72-5) R package. Through module eigengene and phenotype association analyses, modules significantly associated with the phenotype were identified, along with highly connected key genes [16]. PPI analysis of these key genes revealed potential interaction networks and key regulatory genes [17].

Reverse transcription-quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was fractionated into small RNA and large RNA using the NucleoZOL Kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL, Cat# 740404.200). Genomic DNA removal and reverse transcription for large RNA was performed using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, RR047A). RT-qPCR was performed on the large RNA-derived cDNA using a Bio-Rad CFX96 system and the TB Green Advantage qPCR Premix (Takara Bio, Cat# 639676). The sequences of the qPCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1 (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Using the housekeeping gene Gapdh as the reference, the relative expression level of the target gene was calculated using the 2−△△ct.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R v4.1.1. Inter-group differences were assessed via unpaired Student’s t-tests, with post hoc multiple comparisons conducted using the Student-Newman-Keuls method. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Associations between gene expression and phenotypic parameters were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis.

Results

Establishment of IUGR rat model and postnatal catch-up growth

Of 12 successfully mated female rats, 8 were randomized to the R group receiving an 8% low-protein diet, and 4 were assigned to the C group receiving standard chow. The study yielded 20 male offspring in each group. Mean (SD) birth weights were 7.45 (0.81) g and 5.01 (0.30) g for the C and R groups, respectively (P < < 0.0001). R group birth weights were more than 3 SD below the control mean, confirming successful IUGR induction. Following restoration of standard maternal diet postpartum, R group offspring demonstrated variable catch-up growth patterns. By 3 months, mean body weight in the R group reached within 1 SD around control values, and by 9 months, weights were comparable to controls (Fig. 1B).

BMI-based classification at 3 months of age identified two distinct subgroups within the R cohort (n = 19, following one mortality at 5 weeks): RM group comprising 47.4% (9/19) of offspring and RC group comprising 52.6% (10/19). Analysis of weight trajectories revealed distinct catch-up growth patterns between the subgroups (Fig. 1C). The RC group demonstrated accelerated early catch-up growth, achieving mean control group weights by 3 months of age, while the RM group exhibited delayed catch-up growth, with weights remaining more than 2 SD below control means at 3 months postnatally. By 9 months of age, complete catch-up growth was observed across all IUGR offspring, with weights comparable to control means and no persistent evidence of malnutrition, suggesting the temporary nature of early growth pattern differences between RM and RC subgroups.

DEGs in adipose tissue of IUGR rats

RNA sequencing of 15 adipose tissue samples yielded 16,117 identifiable genes, with a average genome alignment rate of 97.2%. Four comparative analyses were conducted to evaluate differential gene expression: catch-up IUGR vs. control at 3 months (RC_3m vs. C_3m), malnourished IUGR vs. control at 3 months (RM_3m vs. C_3m), catch-up IUGR vs. control at 9 months (R_9m vs. C_9m), and longitudinal changes in catch-up IUGR group (R_9m vs. RC_3m). DEGs were classified as upregulated or downregulated based on relative expression levels between comparison groups.

Comparative transcriptome analysis revealed distinct patterns of differential gene expression across groups. The RM_3m exhibited 150 DEGs compared with age-matched controls, comprising 82 upregulated and 68 downregulated genes. The RC_3m showed similar total DEG counts (n = 148) but with a different distribution: 62 upregulated and 86 downregulated genes. At 9 months, R_9m maintained 156 DEGs relative to controls, with 74 upregulated and 82 downregulated genes. Longitudinal comparison within the catch-up group (R_9m vs. RC_3m) revealed the most extensive transcriptional changes, with 493 DEGs identified, including 305 upregulated and 188 downregulated genes (Fig. 2A).

Despite distinct growth trajectories, the RM and RC groups showed limited overlap in DEGs compared with controls at 3 months, sharing only 35 DEGs (19 upregulated, 16 downregulated) (Fig. 2B). Temporal analysis identified several genes with persistent expression changes: Cpxm2, Serpina3l, and AABR07044383.1 maintained downregulation at both 3 and 9 months, while Dip2a showed dynamic regulation, shifting from downregulation at 3 months to upregulation at 9 months (Fig. 2A). Hierarchical clustering analysis of these shared DEGs revealed distinct expression patterns across groups, as visualized in the heatmap (Fig. 2C).

At three months of age, despite exhibiting two different catch-up growth patterns, RM and RC groups shared few DEGs in adipose tissue compared to the C group, with 19 upregulated and 16 downregulated gene (Fig. 2B). Moreover, genes such as Cpxm2, Serpina3l, and AABR07044383.1 were downregulated at both three and nine months, while Dip2a was downregulated at three months but upregulated at nine months (Fig. 2A). IUGR causes inflammation activation in adipose tissue in three-month-old rats

GO enrichment analysis of DEGs in 3-month-old adipose tissue revealed distinct molecular signatures across groups. In the RM group, upregulated DEGs were predominantly enriched in inflammatory pathways, particularly regulation of inflammatory response (GO:0050727) (Fig. 3A). Chord diagram analysis demonstrated that Cebpb was enriched in both regulation of inflammatory response (GO:0050727) and positive regulation of inflammatory response (GO:0050729) pathways (Fig. 3B). Conversely, downregulated DEGs in the RM group showed enrichment in pathways associated with mitochondrial function and energy metabolism, including cytochrome-c oxidase activity (GO:0004129) and lipase activator activity (GO:0060229) (Fig. 3C).

We performed enrichment analyses of DEGs in adipose tissue from 3-month-old RM, RC, and C groups to evaluate the impact of IUGR on adipose development.

For RM_3m group DEGs, GO enrichment analysis showed that upregulated DEGs were primarily associated with inflammation and its regulation, regulation of inflammatory response (GO:0050729) (Fig. 3A). The chord diagram illustrated the enrichment pathways of DEGs, showing that the Cebpb gene was enriched in both the “regulation of inflammatory response (GO:0050729)” and “positive regulation of inflammatory response (GO:0050729)” pathways (Fig. 3B). Downregulated DEGs in RM group were enriched in pathways related to mitochondrial function and energy metabolism, such as cytochrome-c oxidase activity (GO: 0004129) and lipase activator activity (GO: 0060229) (Fig. 3C).

The RC group exhibited 22 enriched pathways among upregulated DEGs, predominantly involving immune regulation, such as positive regulation of T cell activation (GO:0050870) (Fig. 3D, E). Downregulated DEGs in the RC group were enriched in cellular metabolism and autophagy-related pathways, notably acetyl-CoA metabolic process (GO:0006084) (Fig. 3F). Analysis of non-shared DEGs revealed both concordant and divergent expression patterns between RM and RC groups (Fig. 3G). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated significant enrichment exclusively among downregulated genes in the RM group (Fig. 3H), encompassing pathways related to diabetic cardiomyopathy (rno05415) and energy metabolism, particularly thermogenesis (rno04714). While both IUGR groups showed overlapping pathway enrichment, suggesting common developmental impacts, the incomplete pathway overlap and divergent expression patterns of non-shared DEGs indicated distinct adipose developmental trajectories between growth pattern groups.

IUGR impairs the metabolism of adipose tissue in nine-month-old rats

At 9 months of age, all rats with IUGR achieved normal body weight without evidence of malnutrition. Transcriptomic analysis was performed on adipose tissue obtained from the recovered RC and control groups to characterize the molecular signatures of IUGR adipose tissue at this time point.

GO enrichment analysis revealed 42 significantly enriched pathways among upregulated DEGs. The pathways with the highest gene ratios are presented in Fig. 4A. Network analysis of these pathways identified three primary functional clusters: regulation of osteoclast differentiation (GO:0045670), positive regulation of mononuclear cell migration (GO:0071677), and glucose metabolic processes (GO:0006006) (Fig. 4B). Several DEGs were found to be associated with multiple core pathways, notably Fbn1 and Pik3r1, which showed enrichment in two of the three core pathways (Fig. 4C). Hierarchical clustering analysis demonstrated that DEGs enriched in the glucose metabolic process pathway (GO:0006006) exhibited significant upregulation specifically in the RC group at 9 months, with notable expression of MST1 and Serpina12 (Fig. 4D).

Adipose tissue characterized with immune responses along with normal developmental trajectories

GO enrichment analysis identified 69 significantly enriched pathways among upregulated DEGs. The 10 pathways with the highest gene ratios are presented in Fig. 5A. Network clustering analysis of these functionally diverse pathways revealed three major clusters, with core pathways encompassing positive regulation of fever generation (GO:0031622), regulation of lymphocyte proliferation (GO:0050670), and positive regulation of membrane invagination (GO:0050670) (Fig. 5B). A distinct cluster associated with nucleotide receptor activity (GO:0016502) was also identified. Expression analysis demonstrated that DEGs within these core pathways exhibited predominant upregulation in the 9-month cohort while showing downregulation in the 3-month cohorts (Fig. 5C). The parallel expression patterns observed across these genes precluded definitive attribution of these transcriptional changes to either IUGR effects or age-related phenomena. Analysis of downregulated DEGs revealed significant enrichment in pathways associated with amino acid metabolism and cellular energetics, specifically the amino acid metabolic process (GO:0006520) and energy derivation by oxidation of organic compounds (GO:0015980) (Fig. 5D). KEGG pathway analysis of upregulated DEGs demonstrated enrichment in immune response and cell signaling cascades, exemplified by the Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis pathway (rno04666) (Fig. 5D). Chord diagram analysis identified 14 upregulated DEGs, including Tnfrsf11a, that showed concurrent enrichment in both KEGG core pathways and GO core pathways (Fig. 5E). Downregulated DEGs showed significant enrichment in metabolic pathways, particularly propanoate metabolism (rno00640) (Fig. 5F).

Discussion

In this investigation, we conducted a systematic analysis of growth trajectories in offspring with IUGR and evaluated the long-term metabolic consequences. Our principal findings demonstrated that while IUGR offspring exhibited significant catch-up growth, approximately 50% displayed delayed catch-up growth patterns and manifested clinical indicators of malnutrition at 3 months of age. Transcriptomic analyses revealed persistent adipose tissue inflammation and metabolic dysregulation in IUGR offspring, with these alterations showing progressive deterioration with advancing age. These metabolic perturbations were observed in both rapid and slow catch-up growth phenotypes, with greater severity noted in offspring exhibiting rapid catch-up growth.This implies that IUGR infant should undergo long-term follow-up, requiring monitoring of not only physical development but also metabolic indicators.

The experimental IUGR model was established through maternal protein restriction during gestation, followed by immediate postnatal transition to standard dietary conditions. Catch-up growth was facilitated through reduced litter sizes during the lactation period. This model yielded two distinct catch-up growth phenotypes among IUGR rats. Approximately 50% demonstrated delayed early catch-up growth, while the remainder exhibited accelerated early catch-up growth, consistent with previous observations by An et al. [18]. Notably, all IUGR rats achieved normalization of growth parameters by 9 months of age.

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of DEGs in the 3-month-old RC and RM groups revealed predominant involvement in adipose tissue development, inflammatory regulation, and glucose-lipid metabolic pathways. These findings suggest that IUGR individuals, regardless of their catch-up growth velocity (rapid or slow), demonstrate perturbations in adipose development, adipose inflammation, and glucose-lipid metabolism. A notable example is the Cebpb gene, a crucial transcriptional regulator of adipocyte differentiation and maturation [19]. Previous investigations have demonstrated that sulforaphane exhibits potential therapeutic applications in people with obesity prevention through suppression of Cebpb expression, thereby inhibiting adipocyte differentiation and lipid accumulation [20]. Furthermore, studies have established that Cebpb overexpression in pancreatic tissue accelerates β-cell dysfunction, resulting in decreased serum insulin concentrations and subsequent diabetic manifestations [21]. Analogous effects have been documented in placental trophoblast cells, where elevated Cebpb expression significantly compromises insulin sensitivity [22]. In the present investigation, Cebpb expression was significantly upregulated across all 3-month-old R group subjects, with particularly marked elevation observed in the RC group, characterized by rapid catch-up growth.

To evaluate the persistence of IUGR-induced adipose tissue inflammation and metabolic perturbations into adulthood, transcriptomic sequencing analysis was performed on adipose tissue obtained from 9-month-old IUGR rats. The findings demonstrated that IUGR-associated adipose inflammation and glucose-lipid metabolic dysregulation not only persisted but exhibited enhanced manifestation at 9 months of age. Analysis revealed significant elevation of Ccl9 and Ccr1, key mediators of immune cell chemotaxis, in the 9-month-old RC group [23]. Conversely, Tnfrsf11a, encoding the Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor Kappa B (RANK) protein, showed decreased expression in the 9-month-old RC group compared with age-matched controls. Previous investigations have demonstrated that thymic RANK protein deficiency impairs regulatory T cell (Treg) accumulation in visceral adipose tissue, leading to adipocyte hypertrophy, adipose inflammation, and transgenerational glucose homeostasis disruption [24]. Mst1 demonstrated progressive age-dependent upregulation in the R group. Prior research has established that Mst1 hyperactivation in pancreatic β-cells induces apoptosis and dysfunction, suggesting potentially increasing insulin resistance risk with advancing age in IUGR subjects [25]. Pik3r1 showed significant upregulation in both 3-month-old RM and 9-month-old RC groups. While the PI3K/Akt pathway is fundamental for insulin sensitivity regulation, with Pik3r1 serving as a crucial PI3K regulatory subunit, Pik3r1 overexpression can competitively inhibit insulin receptor substrate binding, thereby attenuating insulin signal transduction [26]. Conversely, Pik3r1 reduction enhances PI3K heterodimer recruitment to insulin receptors and PI3K/AKT pathway activation [27]. Additionally, investigations have demonstrated that Pik3r1-mediated PI3K/Akt pathway activation can induce hepatic lipid metabolic perturbations and hepatocellular death [28, 29].

Concurrent analysis of DEGs in adipose tissue of IUGR subjects revealed the presence of genes that counteract adipose inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. Serpina12, encoding the adipokine vaspin expressed in both visceral and subcutaneous adipose depots, demonstrated significant elevation in 3-month-old RM and RC groups, with persistence of this upregulation through 9 months of age [30]. Previous investigations have established vaspin’s capacity to enhance insulin sensitivity in models with obesity and attenuate inflammatory responses in 3T3-L1 adipocytes through NF-κB signaling pathway inhibition [31]. Atf3, known for its inhibitory effects on adipogenesis and inflammation, exhibited marked elevation in the 9-month-old RC group, potentially indicating heightened adipose tissue expansion and inflammatory responses in these subjects [32, 33]. Hk3, which catalyzes the initial glucose phosphorylation step critical for multiple glucose metabolic pathways, demonstrated an increasing trend in 3-month-old R groups, with significant upregulation observed in adipose tissue of the 9-month-old RC group [34]. These findings suggest the activation of a compensatory feedback mechanism in response to IUGR-induced glucose metabolic perturbations. Furthermore, C1qtnf1, encoding the CTRP1, an adiponectin paralog predominantly synthesized and secreted by adipose tissue, showed significant upregulation in the 9-month-old RC group. Previous research has demonstrated that CTRP1 can partially replicate adiponectin’s effects in ameliorating insulin resistance [35]. In diet-induced models with obesity, CTRP1 overexpression resulted in attenuated weight gain and enhanced insulin sensitivity through augmented fatty acid oxidation [36].

WGCNA identified genes within the blue module as crucial mediators of adipose tissue inflammatory responses. Gpnmb, previously documented to exhibit elevated expression in white adipose tissue of models with obesity, demonstrated an increasing trend in both 3-month-old R groups and achieved significant upregulation in adipose tissue of 9-month-old RC subjects. Gpnmb has been shown to attenuate macrophage-mediated inflammation through CD44-dependent inhibition of NF-κB signaling, thereby suppressing adipose inflammation and associated metabolic perturbations [37]. Cluster of Cd44 expression showed consistent upregulation across all age groups in IUGR subjects, although these elevations did not achieve statistical significance. The temporal pattern of these molecular alterations suggests progressive intensification of adipose tissue inflammatory responses with advancing age in IUGR subjects.

In contrast to previous investigations that primarily focused on associations between rapid catch-up growth and metabolic perturbations [38, 39]. This study systematically evaluated the relationship between diverse growth trajectories and metabolic outcomes in IUGR offspring. The findings demonstrated that IUGR offspring exhibiting slow catch-up growth patterns manifested significant metabolic alterations, challenging the conventional paradigm that rapid catch-up growth represents the predominant pathway to metabolic dysfunction in IUGR offspring. These observations suggest that IUGR per se, rather than the subsequent growth velocity, may constitute a fundamental determinant of long-term metabolic health outcomes. This insight provides a novel perspective on the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying IUGR-associated metabolic disorders.

Strength and limitations

This study focused on both rapid and slow catch-up growth in IUGR offspring, analyzing how different catch-up growth patterns affected adipose tissue development and metabolism. The findings revealed that while catch-up growth was a crucial factor in the development of adipose and metabolic abnormalities in IUGR offspring, the impact of IUGR itself was also highly significant. Meanwhile, this study conducted an extended follow-up to assess adipose tissue development and metabolic profiles in IUGR offspring across various age stages.

This study exclusively employed transcriptome sequencing to investigate potential abnormalities in IUGR adipose development, and it is imperative to emphasize that additional validation and further exploration of the underlying molecular mechanisms are required. At the same time, future research should incorporate proteomics and metabolomics to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the metabolic abnormalities induced by IUGR. Only male IUGR offspring were included in this study. Future investigations could incorporate female offspring to examine how estrogen exposure influences catch-up growth patterns and metabolic risk in IUGR offspring. The current study has a limited sample size. Expanding future cohorts will enhance statistical power to identify additional DEGs and metabolic pathways. It’s important to acknowledge that factors such as maternal malnutrition, placental dysfunction, and fetal developmental irregularities can all contribute to IUGR. However, in this study, IUGR was induced using a low-protein diet, which might not fully capture the entire spectrum of characteristics associated with postnatal adipose development in IUGR individuals. Therefore, future research should consider a broader array of IUGR induction methods to comprehensively understand the complexities of this condition.

Conclusion

Longitudinal analysis demonstrated persistent adverse effects of IUGR on adipose tissue development from 3 to 9 months of age. These alterations were characterized by progressive intensification of inflammatory responses and metabolic dysregulation with advancing age. The deleterious effects were observed irrespective of early growth velocity, with both rapid and slow catch-up growth patterns exhibiting significant adipose tissue abnormalities. Nevertheless, subjects manifesting early rapid catch-up growth demonstrated enhanced susceptibility to adipose tissue inflammation and metabolic perturbations compared with their slow catch-up growth counterparts. These findings underscore the enduring impact of IUGR on adipose tissue function, while highlighting the additional metabolic burden imposed by accelerated early postnatal growth.
