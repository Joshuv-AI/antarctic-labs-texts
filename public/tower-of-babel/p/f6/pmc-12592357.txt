Method to measure blast overpressure exposure on military personnel using orthogonal sensor orientations
Authors: Nicholas W. Kuehl, Catherine E. Johnson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Measuring and recording blast exposure to military personnel from shoulder-fired weaponry or improvised explosive devices to correlate health outcomes like mild traumatic brain injury has been a goal for many years. To ensure overpressure is recorded, sensor manufacturers either recommend multiple sensors on different parts of the body or have integrated multiple sensors into one unit. Despite this, knowing the sensor orientation in relation to the blast source is important to know if it’s a head on, side on or oblique pressure recording. This research investigates whether pressures recorded at three independent orientations in the x, y and z planes can be combined to a representative pressure that is independent of orientation. Important metrics such as peak overpressure and impulse were used to provide a full picture of the exposure measurements. Using the root mean square (RMS) formula, the results from peak pressure and impulse were combined and analyzed as a single vector pressure. Results from the RMS calculations for peak pressure show a small range (70.67 kPa to 87.91 kPa). However, the impulse RMS calculations revealed a consistent value throughout the test series, holding steadily around 33.5 ± 1 kPa.ms. Further analysis revealed that by using the exposed surface are of a sensor face, the averaged pressure values could be linearized. Enabling the calculation of a steady RMS pressure value around 71.0 ± 2.5 kPa. Compared to prior range in RMS pressure values (70.67 kPa to 87.91 kPa) the resultant calculation vastly decreased the range of reported pressure values.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22826-1.

Introduction

Accurately and precisely measuring blast overpressure is crucial when conducting research around the use and exposure to explosive blasts. However, previous research has not led to a defined method when recording and reporting multidirectional blast exposure data from military personnel in the field. Military personnel are commonly exposed to blast overpressure during combat deployments from a variety of incoming and outgoing weapon systems such as improvised explosive devices (IEDs), shoulder fired rocket propelled grenades (RPGs), and other heavy weaponry1. Furthermore, the implementation and use of explosives such as artillery, breeching charges or missile weapon systems during training exercises expose numerous military personnel to repetitive low level blast overpressures2,3. Many studies have been conducted relating the effects of training and battlefield blast exposure5 to medical conditions such as traumatic brain injury6, post-traumatic stress disorders7, and chronic fatigue syndrome4.

To understand health implications from blast exposure, knowing the exposure level in the field is crucial. Studying the effects of exposure to explosive blasts in the field has involved the use of human worn pressure sensors that are either multidirectional, or that collect and record pressure data in multiple locations on the body4. In most cases the soldier wears a sensor on their helmet and/or chest if using a multidirectional sensor5,6. Soldiers have also been fitted with multiple individual sensors on the back of their helmet, shoulder, and chest7. Research conducted on personal protective equipment such as helmets has been used to demonstrate how varying sensor orientation and averaging the individual results can increase and alter results, leading to overestimated pressure and impulse values8. The conclusions from this research support the known fact that measurements from head-on and side-on orientations vary greatly. Additionally, simply averaging the sensor reading show considerable variation, leading to possible misuse and misinterpretation, and prove ineffective for fully interpreting blast exposure data from more than one sensor5. Also, the size of the reflected surface when measuring head-on pressure also affects the magnitude of the reading9. Supplementary work has been conducted on buildings to demonstrate the effect of blast waves on varying surface areas10. With one of the most notable conclusions being that larger surface areas such as floors or windows fail commonly due to their larger surface area the pressure wave can act upon10,11. Relating this conclusion to the human aspect, understanding sensor placement at different parts around the body with varied surface areas, most notably the chest and head, can lead to longer exposure and exposure to various reflections stemming from the respected surface.

The root mean square (RMS), also known as the quadratic mean, is the square root of a set of numbers mean square12. When used with three orthogonal vectors, the root mean square ensures that the individual components along any axis will not cancel each other out. The x, y and z orthogonal orientations essentially results in a right-angle triangle in three-dimensional space where the hypotenuse is the resultant vector calculated with RMS. It is often considered better than a simple average because RMS considers errors created by large deviations from the mean. RMS is used across multiple disciplines in engineering for example mechanical engineering for vibration analysis and acceleration measurement13.

To understand practicality and application of RMS on blast overpressure metrics, an experimental study was conducted using the high explosive, Composition C4 (C4). Multiple sensor orientations were used to record blast pressure data from three orthogonal orientations. The RMS equation was applied with the goal of reaching a single value to express the entirety of the blast event regardless of orientation. The aim of this paper is to develop a simple and repeatable method for reporting objectively measured blast exposures for military personnel using three orthogonally oriented sensors and applying the RMS formula.

Methodology

With the goal of normalizing blast overpressure data independent of orientation in mind, a series of tests was conducted to gather blast pressure data from a set of sensors to combine the results of three changing orthogonal orientations into one representative value. Each sensor was rotated about the z axis in a clockwise rotation at 5 degree increments from their respective 0 degree orientations until reaching the final 85 degree orientations. The 90 degree orientation was determined to be the same as the 0 degree orientation with the x and y sensors in the opposite orientations. To record blast overpressure data, three PCB model 102B18 Flush Mount pressure sensors14 were placed 2.75 m away from a centrally located charge, measured directly from the charge to the sensor face. One PCB model 137B23B pencil probe15 was used as an unaltered control measurement oriented directly toward the blast and as a comparative measurement for the z facing flush mount sensor. The z axis was chosen as the comparative measurement due to its unchanging orientation toward the central charge. Important metrics of peak pressure and impulse were utilized to draw meaningful conclusions from the test. Where peak pressure refers to the maximum pressure value obtained for a blast event, and impulse is the change in pressure over a period of time. The 150 gram charge of C4 was initiated with an electric blasting cap and was placed on a cardboard support tube 0.4 m above a concrete test pad to simulate an air burst explosion event. To ensure charge repeatability, a 3D printed charge mold was used with a blasting cap-well insert within the design. The charge mold was designed for a density of 1.55 g/cm3. Initial calculations using a calculator derived from the Kingery Bulmash equations16 predicted that head on pressure would be 86.18 kPa, and side on pressure would be measured at 37.92 kPa. Values for impulse were also predicted using the Kingery Bulmash equations, where the head on orientation would measure 55.16 kPa.ms and the side on orientation would be at 26.20 kPa.ms. Additional calculations were performed prior to testing to confirm the sensors would measure the blast wave under the triple point17, known as the Mach Stem. Measuring the Mach Stem ensures that little to no reflections from the ground create additional pressure peaks and complication to the initial proof of concept test series. The test setup can be seen in Fig. 1. Figure 2 shows a timeline of photos taken using the phantom high speed camera to trace the path of the triple point height as the Mach stem passes the sensor stand. All recordings used for analysis in this section were taken at a frame rate of 62,000 frames per second. These frames were all taken from the middle of the recordings where the triple point passed over the sensor mount. Tracking the path of the Mach stem ensures no additional pressure peaks create outliers in the data set used for RMS calculations.

Each sensor was placed on their own individual stand. The flush mount sensors were placed on stands with the capability of rotating about the z, vertical, axis. This stand can be seen in Fig. 3. The sensors were initially oriented with one facing directly toward the charge, or along the x-axis, a second facing orthogonally toward the side, representing the y axis, and the final sensor orthogonally directed straight up as the z axis. This orientation combines creating an x, y, z coordinate plane comparable to the multidirectional sensors worn on a soldier’s body. After 3 repeats, the flush mount stands were rotated in conjunction with one another from zero to eighty-five degrees from normal. The orientation angle order was performed at random to ensure weather conditions changing throughout the days did not play a factor in the pressure measurements. The pressure-time data was collected using a Hi-Techniques Synergy series data acquisition system18. A secondary study with a single repeat was conducted for the back half of the circle or “shadow zone” to further understand the RMS relationship.

Results and analysis

Three orthogonally orientated pressure-time waveforms were recorded from 18 angles between 0 and 85 degrees with three repeats each. Figure 4A shows representative waveforms from 0-degree orientation where the x sensor is facing directly toward the charge, y is orthogonally directed toward the side, and z is orthogonally directed straight up. Peak pressures range from 39.64 kPa to 106.87 kPa, and impulse, found by calculating the area under the pressure-time waveform, showed values from 37.2 kPa.ms to 31.7 kPa.ms. Figure 4B shows the same data with the difference being the orientation the stands were set 20 degrees from 0. At this orientation, peak pressure again ranged from about 39.64 kPa to 106.2 kPa, and impulse ranged from a similar 31.02 kPa.ms to 35.85 kPa.ms. Where change truly occurs is with the y axis sensor. Between the two orientations the pressure reading jumped up from 47 kPa at 0 degrees, to 69 kPa at the 20 degree orientation. This change of over 20 kPa, along with the large range in peak pressures between the z and x oriented sensors demonstrates why there is a need for a standard method on what pressure to report.

In comparison to the pencil probe data the z oriented flush mount measured pressure slightly lower than that of the pencil probe. Both the pencil probe data and z oriented flush mount measured pressure slightly higher than predicted by the Kingery Bulmash calculations. Where the pencil probe measured pressure around 5 kPa higher than predicted while the flush mount measured pressure only 2 kPa higher than predicted. Throughout the test series both sensors were in an entirely side on orientation and were not altered at any point during the tests. The average pressure recorded by each sensor at every orientation tested along with the predicted Kingery Bulmash value is shown in Fig. 5. Performing a paired two sample t-Test between both groups of data revealed that the grouped data is statistically similar and therefore significant to the results. Additionally, the standard deviation of both sets of data are also similar with the Pencil probe data having a standard deviation of 0.561 kPa and the Flush Mount data having a standard deviation of 0.734 kPa.

The data from each of the three repeated trials was averaged together. Outliers in the data were identified using the residual Z-score method. The Z-score method is a standard process to measure how far a particular data point is from the mean of a normally distributed data set19. For this study, the general rule of any data point with a Z score greater than two was considered an outlier was followed20. 7 of 216 values were removed from the data set as outliers and not used for average and RMS calculations. While 7 values were removed from the data pool each orientation had two remaining points that were averaged to construct the final data set. Removing said outliers improved the average magnitude for both peak pressure and impulse and led to the development of more accurate conclusions based on precise data points. There are several possible reasons for the various outliers including non-perfect spherical charges and noise from the sensor cables to name a few. Figure 6A displays the averaged peak pressure results obtained throughout the test series, where sensor x is initially front facing at 0 degrees, and sensor y is directed orthogonally to the side with sensor z perpendicularly oriented toward the sky. As expected, the z oriented sensor did not significantly change in peak pressure value throughout the test series. For the total test series, the z oriented flush mount has a calculated standard deviation of 0.734 kPa and a total range of 2.4 kPa. The average pressure value recorded by the top facing sensor is 39.44 kPa, with a range of 2.41 kPa and a standard deviation of 0.76 kPa. The consistency in pressure measurements is due to the fact that the sensor face continuously remained perpendicular toward the blast identical to the pencil probe. As expected, the results from sensor x and y demonstrate that the once front facing orientation will steadily decrease at the same rate as the once side facing orientation increases. The total range in pressure for both the x and y orientations was 61.16 kPa with a max peak pressure at 108.59 kPa and a minimum at 47.43 kPa. It should be noted that the change in peak pressure did not occur until the rotation of the front-oriented sensor reached 40–45 degrees. Likewise, the change in peak pressure ceased to exist once the side-oriented sensor reached 45–50 degrees. Impulse was also calculated and averaged for each orientation. To accomplish this, the integral of the positive phase from the time pressure waveforms was calculated. The impulse values show a linear relationship between the x and y orientations, shown in Fig. 6B. Where the x orientation linearly decreases at the same rate the y orientation increases. The overall range in impulse for both orientations is about 5 kPa.ms with a maximum impulse around 37 kPa.ms and a minimum around 32 kPa.ms. Similar to pressure, the z orientation held at a steady impulse value around 32 kPa-ms. Compared to the predicted impulse values of 55.16 kPa.ms for the head on orientation and 26.20 kPa.ms for the side on orientation; the calculated values from the data set has a much tighter range of values.

Linear regression was used to establish consistency across the orientation range using the results from the z sensor’s pressure measurements. Figure 7 shows the regression line plotted against the filtered z pressure measurements. Looking at the residuals of the z pressure data, there is a balanced distribution of positive and negative residual values. The slope of the linear regression line is also significant in that it is near zero with a value of -0.0007, indicating the average pressure values for the z sensor are nearly perfectly consistent. Additionally, performing a Breusch-Pagan (BP) test using a linear regression of the predicted Z values and the squares of the residuals allows for the computation of a BP test statistic. The BP statistic of 0.80 is then compared to the Chi-square value of 3.84. This value is used for a single degree of freedom at a 5% significance level. Because the BP statistic is less than the Chi-square the conclusion of there being evidence of heteroscedasticity can be rejected. Therefore, there is significant evidence of homoscedasticity, where variability of residuals is constant across the range of orientations. Linear regression analysis was also used to establish the consistency of the final calculated RMS pressure values, shown in Fig. 7, compared to the Z sensor recordings, shown in Fig. 7. Using the same methods, a covariance value and correlation coefficient were calculated using the predicted RMS and Z sensor data points. The covariance value was determined to be 0.003 and the correlation coefficient equaled 0.996. These values show a near perfect linear relationship where there is little to no change in pressure values for both the RMS and Z data points. Supporting the conclusion that the RMS calculations are statistically similar to pressure readings from a lab grade sensor.

RMS impulse was additionally compared to the recorded impulse values from the Z sensor. With the goal in mind to justify the consistency of the calculation in a similar manner to pressure. Displayed in Fig. 8, the linear regression lines were compared based on their slope, covariance, and correlation coefficient. The slopes of the two regression lines are nearly identical with values of 0.0008 and 0.0003 for RMS and Z respectively. These slope values are the closest any have been during analysis and not only show similarity to one another but also support the claim that the RMS calculation provides a consistent impulse value regardless of orientation. The covariance value of 0.0001 and correlation coefficient of 0.996 further support the overall claim by indicating a highly positive linear relationship that nearly doesn’t change in magnitude. Overall, the evidence collected from linear regression and subsequent testing has shown that RMS calculations are just as consistent as recording impulse values using a lab grade sensor.

Figure 9 displays the RMS values from peak pressure and impulse. The pressure values show a range of around 17.24 kPa with a change from 70.67 kPa up to a max of 87.91 kPa at the 45-degree orientation. This phenomenon is likely due to both the x and y sensor orientations in an equally exposed orientation with respect to the centrally located charge. In comparison to predicted pressure values, the calculated values for RMS pressure embody those of the predicted head on value of 86.18 kPa rather than the predicted side on pressure value of 37.92 kPa. While the RMS of peak pressure did not result in a single representative value, the use of RMS was able to decrease the range of presented results by 74.36%. The RMS impulse, however, shows a notably consistent value throughout the orientation range with a range of only 1.92 kPa.ms and a standard deviation of 0.6 kPa.ms. Consequently, the RMS impulse value can be used as representative value for the entirety of the data set regardless of the orientation of the three orthogonal sensors, with one simple function, affording a simple path forward for a single representative value of human blast exposure.

The data for the x and y orientations from Fig. 5 resemble a logarithmic function line. When RMS is applied to this data a nonconstant relationship is found, producing a range in pressure values. To reduce the range in reported peak pressure values the x and y data sets need to be transformed into a linear relationship where the x pressure curve decreases at the same rate the y pressure curve increases. This will ensure the RMS calculated values do not change with respect to orientation. To accomplish this, a correction factor was determined using the proportion of the exposed surface area in relation to the maximum surface area from the head on orientation. So, to find the correction factor for the 45-degree orientation the exposed surface area of the sensor face would be divided by the maximum surface area, or the exposed surface area at the 0-degree orientation. This results in the greatest change occurring at 45 degrees where the proportion stops decreasing and begins to increase again until reaching the 90-degree orientation where the pressure value is again multiplied by 1. Figure 11 displays the peak pressure values once the trigonometric proportions are applied in the described method. The trends for both the x and y orientations now follow a significantly more linear path. Using these values, the same process of applying RMS was followed to calculate the series of pressure values for the surface area corrected data set. To accomplish these calculations in the field, the surface area of a designated target would need to be known along with the angle which it is oriented toward a blast. While the surface area is easily calculated for most objects, the angle at which they are facing the blast is much more difficult to pin point. This is where the use of machine learning can be used to estimate the angle an object is oriented at using the peak pressure difference from three orthogonally oriented sensor measurements from a large number of baseline tests.

Figure 12 shows the calculated pressure values for the trigonometry applied data set. It is observed that the trigonometry corrected RMS calculation is drastically improved when compared to the original RMS pressure calculations. The adjusted RMS pressure data has a range of 5.30 kPa, and a standard deviation of 1.48 kPa. Alternatively, the first set of uncorrected RMS pressures has a much larger range of 18.17 kPa and a larger standard deviation of 5.59 kPa. Applying trigonometric relationships significantly improved the range and standard deviation of reported values for peak pressure. With range being improved by 70.81% and standard deviation by 73.44% the application of RMS for pressure now resembles the RMS impulse data curve where the product is a uniform value that is independent of orientation toward an explosive blast. Using this method provides a simple and repeatable method for pressure and impulse calculations in the field where conditions are unknown to the user.

Conclusion

The goal of this study was to determine a method for finding a representative pressure value to relate to clinical health outcomes for a field blast event that is independent of orientation. An experimental investigation using the explosive C4 detonated in the free field was used to study the effects of orientation on the resultant pressure and impulse values. Three orthogonally orientation sensors in the x, y and z planes were rotated about a central point for angles from 0 to 85 degrees in 5 degree increments for both the front and rear facing orientations. The RMS calculation was applied to the three places to give one resultant vector value for either pressure or impulse. The results from peak pressure show rotating the angle from head-on and side-on orientations resulted in RMS peak pressure values that range from 70.67 kPa to 87.91 kPa maximum. This is a significant decrease of almost 75% compared to the range of reported values for peak pressure alone. The remaining range in peak pressure values result from there being no change in peak pressure for the initial front facing orientation (sensor x) from the 0 to 45-degree orientation. Additionally, peak pressure did not change for the side facing orientation (sensor y) after 45 degrees. A trigonometric method using the effective surface area was applied to the original data to improve uniformity. After applying the trigonometry method to the peak pressure values the RMS calculation was improved in range by 70.81% and 73.44% in standard deviation on top of the improvement by just using RMS alone. Using RMS for impulse, a consistent value was calculated to be 34.5 kPa.ms. This value held consistent throughout the full test series. When compared with the Kingery Bulmash calculation for predicted range of peak pressure and impulse values, using RMS for calculations significantly decreased, and nearly eliminated, the variation in reported pressure and impulse values. Additional data collection and analysis of the rear facing “shadow zone” orientations revealed the method used for the frontal orientations can be applied in a similar manner to derive a singular representative value of pressure. However, peak pressure values were found to be lower value for an identical charge size and distance. In the field this method can be used in a variety of ways to report representative values for blast events. Whether researchers use the highest value for RMS pressure, adjusted RMS pressure, or use RMS impulse there is a simple calculation provided to combine blast data into a representative value irrespective of orientation. However, it should be noted that the effectiveness of the pressure correction method is solely determined on the control of said test stand geometry, and altering its dimensions will cause alterations in the peak pressure data.

A simple method to determine a representative shock physics parameter will allow the industry to further relate clinical outcomes using a consistent method. Another benefit for industry professionals to consider is that small changes in overpressure measurements from the lower sample rate and micro-electromechanical systems used by wearable sensors be less apparent after applying RMS. Future experimental tests should examine the change in peak pressure and impulse over a three-dimensional coordinate grid. Analysis of peak pressure should be performed to establish additional methods with the goal of obtaining one consistent value for all orientations. Dimensions for sensor surface area should be considered when designing further experimental tests to further understand surface area effects.
