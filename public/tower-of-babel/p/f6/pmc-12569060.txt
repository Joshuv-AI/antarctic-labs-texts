Synergistic blood-based diagnostic value of AP3B1 and BMPR2 in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Xiyan Zhao, Li Yang, Yumin Luan, Tao Ding, Xinglong Yang, Xin Geng, Tuo Zhang, Jigang Pan, Ziwen Xiao, Wei Pan, Tengxiang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Reliable blood-based biomarkers for Parkinson’s disease (PD) are needed for minimally invasive diagnosis. We identified a synergistic mRNA biomarker pair, AP3B1 and BMPR2, detectable in blood through an integrative multi-omics workflow. DEGs from a meta-analysis of PD versus healthy controls (HCs) were intersected with DEG-enriched pathway genes and analysed via three-step SMR to identify PD risk candidates, from which machine learning (SVM-RFE and random forest) prioritized AP3B1 and BMPR2. Knockdown of each gene in SH-SY5Y-derived neurons reproduced Parkinsonian phenotypes, with protein docking and co-immunoprecipitation suggesting a direct interaction. An XGBoost model built on PPMI blood RNA-seq (n = 2585) using 25 established PD biomarkers (baseline AUC ~ 0.595) improved to 0.745 with addition of both AP3B1 and BMPR2. qRT-PCR in a cohort of clinical blood samples confirmed their downregulation in PD. These findings support AP3B1 and BMPR2 as a synergistic biomarker pair with speculative biological relevance and possible translational potential.

Introduction

PD is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, with rising incidence and substantial diagnostic delays due to reliance on motor symptoms that typically manifest only after significant dopaminergic neuronal loss1–6. This diagnostic latency hampers early intervention and underscores the urgent need for reliable, non-invasive biomarkers.

Although advanced neuroimaging techniques such as DAT-SPECT and PET offer functional insights into dopaminergic neuron loss, their high cost, limited accessibility, and associated radiation exposure constrain their utility for early or large-scale screening7,8. Cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers, including α-Synuclein, have been explored as valuable diagnostic and prognostic markers in PD, providing insights into disease pathophysiology and aiding early diagnosis and disease monitoring9. However, lumbar puncture is invasive and impractical for routine screening, particularly in early-stage or asymptomatic individuals.

In this context, blood-based biomarkers present a more accessible and scalable alternative. These can be broadly classified into mutation-based and expression-based strategies. Mutation-based markers, such as GBA10 and LRRK211 variants, are well-established familial PD risk factors but require expensive and time-consuming sequencing platforms. Moreover, they fail to reflect real-time disease activity and have limited use in routine clinical diagnosis10,11.

Expression-based biomarkers, in contrast, encompass a variety of molecular types and sample sources. Among serum and plasma proteins, α-Synuclein9, neurofilament light chain (NfL)9,12, and DJ-113 have been extensively studied. However, despite its strong pathological relevance to PD, the clinical utility of α-Synuclein as a biomarker is limited by the heterogeneity of its molecular forms, biofluid distribution, and the unresolved functional role of key post-translational modifications such as Ser129 phosphorylation14. Similarly, while NfL is mildly elevated in PD, it is markedly increased in atypical Parkinsonian syndromes like multiple system atrophy (MSA) and progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), reflecting nonspecific axonal injury12. In contrast, DJ-1 levels in blood are influenced by hemoglobin and platelet contamination, leading to significant variability and limiting its reliability as a consistent PD biomarker13. Additionally, since DJ-1 is predominantly sourced from red blood cells, its levels vary widely between individuals, further hindering its use as a robust diagnostic tool13.

Expanding beyond protein markers, RNA signatures in peripheral blood are gaining interest as non-invasive diagnostic tools. Exosome-based detection of SNCA mRNA, while informative, is limited by labor-intensive procedures and poor reproducibility15. Similarly, NEFL, a blood marker of axonal injury, lacks PD specificity and offers limited value for disease-specific modeling16.

In contrast, whole-blood qPCR is technically simpler, more reproducible, and does not require vesicle isolation, making it better suited for standardized clinical deployment. These features support its potential as a scalable platform for transcriptomic diagnostics.

Noncoding RNAs such as circRNAs and lncRNAs also show differential expression in PD. However, their low abundance, structural complexity, and sensitivity to sample variability reduce their robustness compared with conventional qPCR-based mRNA assays17.

Although various mRNA biomarkers have been proposed, most studies assess them individually, limiting their diagnostic utility. Integrating multiple molecular axes may enhance classification performance and provide a more comprehensive view of PD-related biology. Therefore, there remains a critical need for clinically feasible models that incorporate such multi-dimensional molecular data to enable broader diagnostic deployment.

To address this need, we first prioritized AP3B1 and BMPR2 as a potential synergistic biomarker pair, using a multi-omics strategy that included transcriptomic meta-analysis, pathway enrichment, summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR), support vector machine–recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), and random forest. AP3B1, linked to clathrin-coated vesicle (CCV) transport, and BMPR2, involved in BMP–SMAD1 (BS) signaling, were consistently downregulated in PD across multiple datasets and genetically associated with disease risk.

We then evaluated their diagnostic potential using an XGBoost-based classification model built on RNA-seq profiles from 2,585 whole-blood samples collected by the PPMI consortium, incorporating a panel of 25 predefined PD-related markers (including SNCA and NEFL). While neither gene alone significantly improved model performance, their combined inclusion led to a statistically significant increase in AUC, indicating a synergistic diagnostic effect. Validation was further performed through in vitro assays in neuronal cell models and clinical qPCR analysis of whole blood from 30 PD patients and 21 age-matched controls (Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) stages 1–5). These findings reinforce their potential as robust and reproducible blood-based biomarkers, but their diagnostic applicability as a joint biomarker still needs to be validated in larger, independent, and multi-center cohorts.

Convergent CCV–BS gene set links neural vulnerability and blood detectability in PD

A total of 833 CCV- and 82 BS-related genes were compiled from GeneCards and intersected with meta-analysis-derived PD DEGs, yielding 220 CCV and BS associated genes for downstream analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table 5). Cell-type enrichment analysis via Cell Type-Specific Enrichment Analysis DataBase (CSEA-DB) revealed significant overrepresentation in dopaminergic neurons, astrocytes, and microglia, implicating these genes in central PD pathology (Supplementary Fig. 1B and Supplementary Table 6). Notably, this gene set also showed substantial enrichment in peripheral blood cell types, including neutrophils (e.g.,TS_Blood_neutrophil, TS_Lymph_Node_neutrophil), classical and intermediate monocytes, macrophages from lymphoid tissues, and erythroid progenitor and B cells in bone marrow and umbilical cord. This dual enrichment in both neural and blood compartments underscores their potential as mechanistic contributors to PD and as accessible candidates for blood-based biomarker discovery.

Multi-omics SMR analysis prioritizes CCV- and BS-related genes as causal candidates in PD

To prioritize PD-related causal genes from CCV and BS signaling pathways, we implemented a three-stage multi-omics SMR framework integrating blood-derived GWAS, eQTL, and mQTL datasets. In the first stage, we conducted SMR analysis combining PD GWAS and blood eQTL data, identifying 34 genes whose expression levels were significantly associated with disease risk (p_SMR_multi < 0.05, p_HEIDI > 0.05; Supplementary Table 7). In the second stage, we applied SMR analysis using blood mQTL data to test the association between DNA methylation and PD, identifying 191 CpG probes significantly associated with PD (p_SMR_multi < 0.05, p_HEIDI > 0.05; Supplementary Table 8). In the third stage, we linked the PD-associated CpG sites to gene expression by integrating mQTL and eQTL summary statistics. This analysis prioritized 25 CpG probes with potential cis-regulatory effects and mapped them to 9 nearby genes (p_SMR_multi < 0.05, p_HEIDI > 0.05; Supplementary Table 9). Validation using an independent GWAS dataset (Nalls GWAS), together with the original eQTL and mQTL data, confirmed several regulatory associations. Specifically, these included eQTL-PD associations (p_SMR_multi < 0.05, p_HEIDI > 0.05; Supplementary Table 10) and mQTL-expression regulatory links (p_SMR_multi < 0.05, p_HEIDI > 0.05; Supplementary Table 11), thereby reinforcing the robustness of these findings.

Beyond their regulatory associations, CCV- and BS-related candidates demonstrated striking immunological relevance. CpG sites identified from mQTL-PD analysis were enriched in chromatin regions specific to immune cells, particularly in natural killer cells, T cells, and monocytes, suggesting epigenetic regulation in immune contexts (Supplementary Table 12). In addition, colocalization analysis revealed that the expression of several prioritized genes shared regulatory signals with inflammatory traits in blood (PPH4 > 0.5; Supplementary Table 13 and Supplementary Fig. 2), reinforcing their potential involvement in immune-mediated mechanisms. Taken together, these multi-layered findings support an immuno-pathogenic role for CCV and BS signaling pathways in PD.

Machine learning–based prioritization using SVM-RFE identified a three-gene signature consisting of AP3B1, SMAD6, and BMPR2, which demonstrated optimal classification performance (Fig. 3B). In parallel, random forest analysis ranked DNM2, BMPR2, and AP3B1 as top predictors based on feature importance (Fig. 3C). Intersecting both models consistently prioritized AP3B1 and BMPR2 as core PD-associated genes (Fig. 3D)

AP3B1, encoding a CCV protein, plays a crucial role in synaptic vesicle cycling and membrane homeostasis. Multi-omic SMR analysis confirmed convergence of PD-GWAS, eQTL, and mQTL signals at the AP3B1 locus (Fig. 4A). A regulatory CpG probe, cg05620865, showed a significant negative association with AP3B1 expression (β = –1.54 ± 0.28, Supplementary Table 9), indicating that hypomethylation at this site increases AP3B1 expression. BMPR2, a receptor upstream of SMAD1 in the BS signaling cascade, is involved in axonal development and neuroprotection. SMR analysis also identified colocalized GWAS, eQTL, and mQTL signals at the BMPR2 locus (Fig. 4C). The expression of BMPR2 was negatively associated with methylation at the promoter CpG cg05364508 (β = –1.16 ± 0.22, Supplementary Table 9), indicating that hypomethylation enhances gene expression. This CpG was also under genetic control by PD-associated variants (β = 0.31 ± 0.12, Supplementary Table 8), and BMPR2 expression was inversely associated with PD risk (β = –0.17 ± 0.08, Supplementary Table 7). These findings support a neuroprotective model in which genetically influenced hypomethylation of cg05364508 upregulates BMPR2, thereby reducing disease susceptibility (Fig. 4D).

Collectively, these results position AP3B1 and BMPR2 as core PD-associated genes, whose lower expression is linked to increased disease risk, and whose regulation involves an integrated genetic-epigenetic mechanism. Their convergence across blood-based omics layers, along with their detectability in peripheral samples, supports their potential as minimally invasive biomarkers and therapeutic targets for PD.

Cells with reduced expression of either gene were compared to untreated controls and a PD-like positive control induced by rotenone.

Reduced expression of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), a marker of dopaminergic neuron integrity, was observed in both knockdown conditions, indicating that decreased AP3B1 or BMPR2 expression leads to a loss of dopaminergic phenotype (Fig. 6C). These findings provide functional validation of their causal roles in PD and underscore their value as mechanistic drivers and potential therapeutic targets.

We first constructed a classification model using the XGBoost algorithm based on 2585 samples from PPMI, incorporating a baseline feature set of 25 PD-associated biomarkers reported in the database. To validate the diagnostic relevance of AP3B1 and BMPR2 in independent datasets, we constructed XGBoost-based classification models using transcriptomic data from three GEO cohorts (GSE160299, GSE16508222, and GSE20834723). After batch correction using the ComBat algorithm, gene expression was normalized via TPM and Min-Max scaling. Models were trained on 70% of the samples (n = 18, PD = 8, Control = 10), with hyperparameters optimized via grid search. Feature importance analysis based on the Gini coefficient revealed that AP3B1 and BMPR2 ranked among the top predictors, surpassing multiple canonical PD biomarkers such as SNCA, GBA, and PARK7 (Supplementary Fig. 3A). These findings provide independent support for our diagnostic evaluation in the large-scale PPMI cohort (Fig. 7A), where both genes also showed strong discriminative power.

To further explore their potential diagnostic utility, we developed two models for ROC analysis: the first included only six literature-reported PD biomarkers (PINK124, LRRK225–27, NEFL28, SNCA29,30, GBA10, and PARK731), while the second combined these with AP3B1 and BMPR2. Notably, ROC curve analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3B) showed that incorporating AP3B1 and BMPR2 increased the AUC from 0.650 to 0.844, suggesting that these two genes may jointly improve the discriminatory capacity of existing PD biomarker models. While preliminary, this finding prompted further investigation into their potential interactive or combinatorial value in downstream analyses using the PPMI dataset.

Given the limited sample size and cross-cohort integration, residual heterogeneity may still persist despite batch correction using the ComBat algorithm. Therefore, the ROC-based result suggests a potential joint diagnostic role of AP3B1 and BMPR2, motivating further investigation into their combined diagnostic value rather than serving as definitive confirmation.

Functional interaction between protein AP3B1 and BMPR2 points to a coordinated role in PD pathogenesis and their biomarker potential

Building upon the preliminary diagnostic signal observed in our small-sample ROC model, in which the inclusion of AP3B1 and BMPR2 notably improved AUC, we further investigated whether these two proteins exhibit a functional interaction that could mechanistically underpin their potential joint contribution to PD pathology.

Supporting this hypothesis, transcriptomic profiling of PD samples revealed significant enrichment in clathrin-mediated vesicle transport and bone morphogenetic signaling pathways. Integrative multi-omics and machine learning analyses identified AP3B1 and BMPR2 as central genes in these respective pathways, raising the possibility that AP3B1 may facilitate the intracellular trafficking of signaling components such as BMPR2, thereby contributing jointly to PD pathogenesis.

To test this, we conducted protein-protein interaction predictions using both AlphaFold3 and HDOCK32. Both tools independently predicted a stable interaction between AP3B1 and BMPR2, with a highly overlapping interface. HDOCK yielded a docking score of –590.12 and an RMSD of 0.524, indicating strong binding potential and reliable structural alignment (Fig. 7B, C). Molecular dynamics simulations further confirmed interaction stability, characterized by a plateaued RMSD (~0.8 nm), compact radius of gyration (3.3–3.5 nm), and ~10 stable hydrogen bonds. MM/GBSA analysis supported a strong binding affinity (ΔG = –57.32 ± 7.98 kcal/mol), primarily driven by van der Waals and electrostatic forces (Fig. 7D). Finally, Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments in SH-SY5Y cells validated the physical interaction between AP3B1 and BMPR2 (Fig. 7E).

Together, these results demonstrate that AP3B1 and BMPR2 form a stable intracellular complex, supporting a model of synergistic regulation via vesicle trafficking and signal transduction. This functional interaction not only offers mechanistic insight into PD pathogenesis but also reinforces the plausibility of their combined diagnostic value initially observed in ROC-based analysis. To further assess this potential in a broader and more heterogeneous cohort, we next constructed XGBoost-based diagnostic models using the large-scale PPMI dataset.

A base feature set was defined using 25 PD biomarkers previously reported by the PPMI cohort, referred to as “Other” in the analysis.

Their joint contribution is supported both by improved classification performance in a large-scale machine learning model and by their coordinated expression patterns in patient subgroups. While further validation in independent cohorts is necessary, these findings highlight the potential of integrating transcriptomic signatures such as AP3B1 and BMPR2 into blood-based diagnostic frameworks for PD.

Reduced expression of AP3B1 and BMPR2 in peripheral blood supports their diagnostic utility in PD

To experimentally validate the diagnostic relevance of AP3B1 and BMPR2, we performed two rounds of peripheral blood sample collection followed by qRT-PCR analysis. In the first cohort, peripheral blood was collected from PD patients spanning H&Y stages 1 to 5, including 2 at stage 1, 4 at stage 3, 8 at stage 4, and 1 at stage 5, alongside age-matched HCs without neurodegenerative or diabetic conditions. In this cohort, all PD patients were grouped together for expression analysis. As shown in Fig. 8D, qRT-PCR results revealed significantly lower expression of both AP3B1 and BMPR2 in PD patients compared to controls (P < 0.0001). Sample details are provided in Supplementary Table 14.

In the second cohort, we further validated this finding in a new group of PD patients stratified by H&Y stage, including 5 at stage 1, 6 at stage 2, and 5 at stage 3, along with matched HCs. Expression levels of AP3B1 and BMPR2 were again assessed by qRT-PCR and visualized by disease stage (Fig. 8E), revealing a consistent trend of downregulation in PD, with lower expression levels observed in patients with more advanced stages. Sample information for the second cohort is available in Supplementary Table 15.

Across both cohorts, we analyzed a total of 31 PD patients and 21 HCs, covering multiple H&Y stages, including early-stage PD. In all stages examined, including stage 1, expression levels of both AP3B1 and BMPR2 were consistently reduced in PD patients relative to controls. Notably, in every PD sample analyzed across both cohorts, AP3B1 and BMPR2 expression levels were lower than in any HC sample, without exception. While these results suggest a potential association between reduced gene expression and PD presence or progression, the modest sample size warrants cautious interpretation. Additional validation in larger, independent cohorts will be necessary to confirm the robustness and generalizability of these findings.

Discussion

Our integrative analyses across Asian and European cohorts highlight AP3B1 and BMPR2 as joint blood-based mRNA biomarkers in PD. This conclusion is supported by multiple layers of evidence, including transcriptomic meta-analysis, SMR, Machine learning prioritization, functional perturbation in neuronal models, diagnostic modeling, and peripheral blood validation. Notably, AP3B1 is a component of the CCV trafficking machinery33, while BMPR2 is a type II receptor in the BS pathway34,35. These findings raise the possibility of a mechanistic convergence between vesicle trafficking and neurotrophic signaling pathways in PD.

To investigate this potential convergence, we examined the physical and functional relationship between AP3B1 and BMPR2. The observed protein-protein interaction provides preliminary support. Prior literature suggests that BMPR2 undergoes clathrin-mediated endocytosis, with its long isoform (BMPRII-LF) depending on a dileucine motif for internalization and degradation, thereby modulating downstream SMAD signaling36–38. AP3B1, as part of the AP-3 adaptor complex, may recognize such dileucine motifs and direct cargo proteins to endolysosomal compartments. This functional complementarity supports the hypothesis that AP3B1 may regulate BMPR2’s post-endocytic fate, including sorting, distribution, or degradation. While biologically plausible, this model remains speculative and requires future validation in neuronal systems.

We further investigated the consequences of AP3B1 and BMPR2 knockdown individually in SH-SY5Y-derived neurons. Each knockdown led to hallmark PD-like phenotypes, including neurite retraction, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and α-Synuclein accumulation, supporting a pathogenic role for each gene in promoting neuronal vulnerability. However, we did not perform rescue experiments (e.g., restoring AP3B1 expression in AP3B1-deficient cells or overexpressing BMPR2 in the same background), and thus the extent of their functional convergence in vivo remains to be determined.

To bridge these cellular findings with transcriptomic observations in blood, we examined whether the co-downregulation of AP3B1 and BMPR2 in peripheral blood reflects underlying changes in central neurons during PD pathogenesis. To address this, we conducted a transcriptomic meta-analysis of PD DEGs and intersected them with gene sets involved in CCV trafficking and BS signaling. Analysis via CSEA-DB revealed enrichment of these gene sets in both CNS cell types and peripheral immune cells, such as monocytes and neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 6), suggesting shared transcriptional activity across compartments.

Beyond reflecting central pathology, another possibility is that the dysregulation of AP3B1 and BMPR2 in blood may contribute directly to PD pathogenesis through peripheral mechanisms. To explore this, we applied a three-step summary-data SMR framework integrating blood-based eQTL, mQTL, and GWAS data. Both genes emerged as candidate causal genes for PD in peripheral blood. Additionally, we observed colocalization between CCV- and BMP-SMAD-associated PD loci and inflammatory mediators (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 13), suggesting a potential immune-mediated mechanism, although this remains preliminary.

Alternatively, these transcripts detected in blood may originate from the Central Nervous System (CNS), for example, via brain-derived exosomes. While this hypothesis remains compelling in the context of PD pathogenesis, it presents practical challenges for biomarker development. Specifically, exosome-based assays often suffer from low RNA yield and poor scalability, limiting their clinical utility39–42. In contrast, bulk mRNA profiling offers more robust and scalable approaches, making them more suitable for clinical implementation in large cohorts.

In addition to mechanistic analyses, our study also sought to address population-level biases inherent in existing public datasets. Despite integrating multi-omic datasets from both Western and Asian populations, the majority of public resources (e.g., FinnGen, PPMI, eQTLGen) remain predominantly European. To address this, we performed qRT-PCR validation in two independent recruited Chinese cohorts comprising 31 PD patients and 21 HCs. Strikingly, AP3B1 and BMPR2 were downregulated in all PD patients without exception.

We further examined whether this expression pattern was influenced by clinical variables such as disease stage, sex, or medication status. This signal was consistent across H&Y stages 1–5 (stage 1, n = 7; stage 2, n = 6; stage 3, n = 9; stage 4, n = 8; stage 5, n = 1) and independent of sex (28 males, 24 females) or comorbidity. HCs were age-matched and free of neurodegenerative or metabolic diseases. Some patients were drug-naïve, while others received standard dopaminergic treatments, but expression patterns remained consistent regardless of treatment status. However, the current study did not include stratification by motor- or non-motor-predominant subtypes or genetic backgrounds, nor did it incorporate disease control groups such as patients with Alzheimer’s disease, multiple system atrophy, or progressive supranuclear palsy. These limitations should be addressed in future investigations to better assess the specificity and generalizability of the proposed biomarkers.

In conclusion, this study provides initial evidence that blood mRNA expression levels of AP3B1 and BMPR2 may serve as a complementary biomarker pair for the classification of PD. Their consistent downregulation across transcriptomic datasets, along with improved model performance when evaluated together, suggests a potential synergistic role in blood biomarker development for PD. However, these findings are preliminary and require further validation in larger, independent, and ancestrally diverse cohorts before any clinical application can be considered.

Methods

Meta-analysis of DEGs in Mendelian analysis

Five peripheral blood transcriptomic datasets related to PD (GSE6613, GSE20163, GSE20168, GSE20291, and GSE20292)18–21 were obtained from the GEO database. Each GEO dataset was analyzed using gene-wise Lasso regression models implemented via the glmnet (4.1-10) R package. For each gene, a penalized linear regression was fitted with normalized expression as the dependent variable and disease status (PD vs. HC) as the binary independent variable. Effect-size-based meta-analysis across datasets was then performed using the metaMA (3.1.3) R package, applying an inverse-variance–weighted random-effects model. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic. Genes with meta-analysis p values < 0.05 were retained as final DEGs.

Functional enrichment and pathway analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted on 2569 DEGs (GSE6613, GSE20163, GSE20168, GSE20291, and GSE20292)18–21 derived from a meta-analysis across five datasets, utilizing the R package clusterProfiler (4.16) and the annotation database org.Hs.eg.db. Enrichment was evaluated across the three main GO categories—biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF)—with significance defined as a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Results were visualized using the enrichplot and ggplot2 packages.

Following enrichment results highlighting vesicle transport–related pathways, a total of 833 genes annotated under the term “clathrin transport vesicle” were extracted from the GeneCards database using a relevance score threshold of ≥743,44. GO enrichment analysis of this CCV-related gene set revealed strong enrichment in BS signaling pathways.

To further investigate this association, 82 genes involved in BS were also retrieved using the same selection criteria. After merging and removing duplicates, a combined CCV-BS gene set comprising 906 unique genes was constructed. The intersection of this set with the meta-analysis-derived DEGs yielded 220 overlapping genes, hereafter referred to as DEG-associated CCV and BS genes, which were employed for subsequent analyses.

Cell type-specific enrichment and regulatory annotation

CSEA-DB was used to assess CCV and BS-associated DEGs specificity to cell types, focusing on intestinal and related tissues. Seven tissues and 27 general cell types of interest were identified, with multiple testing corrections applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (FDR < 0.05). Regulatory DNAm sites were annotated using eFORGE, with chromatin states (active/inactive) and histone markers (H3K4me1, H3K4me3) evaluated. DNAm site regions were annotated using Ensembl genome resources.

Multi-omics SMR analysis

The SMR method was used to identify causal SNPs influencing PD through gene expression and DNA methylation (DNAm). SMR integrated GWAS data with cis-eQTLs and mQTLs from blood and brain tissues. Significant causal relationships were determined using a three-step approach, requiring p_smr_multi < 0.05 and p_HEIDI > 0.05. SMR and HEIDI heterogeneity tests were performed using the SMR v1.3.1 tool developed by Yang Lab (https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn/software/smr). In this study, we first used publicly available GWAS data from FinnGen45 for discovery and subsequently validated the findings using GWAS data from Nalls et al.46. Blood eQTLs were obtained from the eQTLGen Consortium47, and mQTLs were sourced from Yang Lab48.

Colocalization analysis

To investigate shared genetic regulation between CCV/BS pathway–associated DEGs and PD-related inflammatory factors in blood, a colocalization analysis was performed using the coloc R package (5.2.3).

Expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data for blood tissue and genome-wide association summary statistics for inflammatory traits were analyzed to assess the likelihood of shared causal variants. The analysis computed posterior probabilities for five hypotheses (H0–H4), with posterior probability of hypothesis 4 (PPH4) > 0.5 considered indicative of a significant colocalization, i.e., the presence of a shared genetic variant affecting both gene expression and the inflammatory trait.

The gene-trait pairs meeting this significance threshold were further investigated for biological relevance to PD pathogenesis.

SVM-RFE feature selection

Seven candidate genes identified from SMR analysis were subjected to recursive feature elimination using an SVM-RFE implemented in R via the caret package (v6.0-94). A radial basis function (RBF) kernel was employed, and five-fold cross-validation was performed at each iteration. For every candidate subset, the average misclassification rate and accuracy were recorded. The feature subset corresponding to the lowest average error rate-prioritizing the smallest number of genes when ties occurred-was selected as the optimal SVM-RFE signature.

Random forest modeling and feature importance

A random forest classifier was constructed in R using the randomForest package (v4.7-1.1) with default settings (ntree = 500, mtry = √p). Model tuning was guided by out-of-bag (OOB) error estimates to select the optimal forest size. Variable importance was quantified by the mean decrease in Gini impurity for each gene. Finally, core candidate biomarkers were defined as the intersection of top-ranked genes from both SVM-RFE and random forest analyses.

Immunostaining

Cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 30525-89-4, USA), permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, Cat# ST1723, China), and blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Beyotime, Cat# ST025, China).

After washing, appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained using DAPI-containing mounting medium (Beyotime, Cat# C1002, China).

Fluorescence images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope, with a 10×objective used for βIII-tubulin staining and a 100×oil immersion objective used for TH and α-synuclein staining. Image processing was conducted with OlyVIA 4.1 (version 27564) and ImageJ 2.9.0.

In parallel, brightfield imaging of live, unstained cells was performed using a Leica DMi1 microscope equipped with a Leica MC170HD camera, using a 10×objective.

Intracellular ROS measurement

ROS levels were assessed using 10 μM DCFH₂-DA (Beyotime, Cat# S0033S, China) incubated for 30 minutes at 37 °C. Oxidized DCF fluorescence was detected by flow cytometry (NovoCyte, Agilent, China) to quantify ROS levels, and by confocal fluorescence microscopy (Olympus FV3000) to visualize intracellular ROS distribution, using a 10× objective. Image processing was performed using OlyVIA 4.1 (version 27564) and ImageJ 2.9.0.

Mito-tracker assay

Mitochondrial activity was assessed using MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Beyotime, Cat# C1032, China) diluted 1:1000 in PBS. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, washed with PBS, and imaged using confocal microscopy (Olympus FV3000) with a 100× oil immersion objective. Image processing was performed using OlyVIA 4.1 (version 27564) and ImageJ 2.9.0.

Protein–protein docking

Three-dimensional structures of AP3B1 (Uniprot ID: O00203) and BMPR2 (Uniprot ID: Q13873) were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database. Protein docking was performed using HDOCK SERVER. Structures were preprocessed in PyMol 2.4 (removal of water/ligands, addition of hydrogens). Ten docking poses were generated, and the top-scoring conformation based on docking score was selected for visualization and further analysis.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

The AP3B1-BMPR2 complex was subjected to all-atom MD simulations using AMBER 22. The system was solvated in a TIP3P water box with a 10 Å buffer, neutralized with Na⁺/Cl− ions, and parameterized with ff14SB (proteins) and GAFF2 (ligands) force fields. Energy minimization, heating (0 to 298.15 K), and equilibration were followed by a 100 ns production run under NPT conditions. Key parameters included a 2 fs time step, PME for long-range electrostatics, a 10 Å cutoff for non-bonded interactions, and SHAKE constraints on bonds involving hydrogen.

MM/GBSA binding free energy calculation

Binding free energy (ΔG_bind) of the AP3B1–BMPR2 complex was calculated using the MM/GBSA approach on the final 10 ns of the MD trajectory (90–100 ns). ΔG_bind was decomposed into van der Waals, electrostatic, polar solvation (GB model, igb = 2), and nonpolar solvation (SASA-based) components. Entropy contributions were not included due to computational cost and uncertainty.

Co-IP

SH-SY5Y cells were lysed in ice-cold IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, Cat# P0013, China) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, Cat# ST506, China). Cell lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove debris. Supernatants were collected and total protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Solarbio, Cat# PC0020, China). For each Co-IP reaction, 500 μg of total protein was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2 μg of either anti-BMPR2 antibody (mouse monoclonal, Santa Cruz, Cat# sc-393304, RRID: AB_11357861) or anti-AP3B1 antibody (rabbit polyclonal, Baijia, Cat# IPB10131, RRID: AB_11205702). The next day, 30 μL of protein A/G agarose beads (Beyotime, Cat# P2179M, China) were added and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with gentle rotation. The beads were washed four times with lysis buffer and resuspended in SDS sample buffer. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and subjected to immunoblotting analysis using anti-AP3B1 (1:1000) and anti-BMPR2 (1:1000) antibodies. Normal rabbit IgG (Beyotime, Cat# P2265, China) was used as a negative control for nonspecific binding.

XGBoost model construction and evaluation based on merged GEO datasets

To evaluate the diagnostic potential of the putative PD-causal genes AP3B1 and BMPR2, we constructed a classification model using whole-blood RNA-seq data from three GEO datasets (GSE160299, GSE16508222, and GSE20834723). Gene expression data were quantified as TPM values and merged using the Pandas library (v2.2.2) in Python 3.10. To minimize inter-dataset variation, all features underwent Min-Max normalization.

The merged dataset (n = 60) was randomly partitioned into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets with balanced representation of PD and HCs. Model development was performed using the XGBoost algorithm (xgboost v2.1.1; scikit-learn v1.5.1). Hyperparameter tuning was conducted via grid search. Model performance was assessed using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).

To evaluate the added diagnostic value of AP3B1 and BMPR2, we compared a baseline model comprising established PD markers reported in prior studies (PINK1, LRRK2, NEFL, SNCA, GBA, and PARK7)47,48 with a combined model that included both established and candidate genes. The combined model outperformed the baseline, achieving an AUC of 0.844 and an accuracy of 0.833, compared to an AUC of 0.65 for the baseline. Feature importance analysis using the Gini metric ranked AP3B1 and BMPR2 among the top predictive variables.

XGBoost model construction and evaluation based on PPMI cohort

An additional diagnostic model was developed using RNA-seq data from 2585 whole-blood samples in the PPMI database. This dataset contained expression values for a curated panel of 25 established PD biomarkers offered by PPMI. The model was trained using XGBoost (v1.7.8.1) with the following optimized hyperparameters: nrounds = 89, max_depth = 4, eta = 0.3, gamma = 0, colsample_bytree = 1, min_child_weight = 1, and subsample = 1.

To avoid batch effects and prevent data leakage, PPMI data and an external validation cohort (GSE661318, n = 72) were preprocessed independently using Z-score normalization. Four feature configurations were evaluated: (i) baseline 25-marker panel, (ii) panel plus AP3B1, (iii) panel plus BMPR2, and (iv) panel plus both AP3B1 and BMPR2.

Models were trained on the PPMI dataset and evaluated on GSE661318 at a classification threshold of 0.5. The addition of both AP3B1 and BMPR2 significantly improved model performance across all metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC. Feature importance assessed using the gain metric confirmed AP3B1 and BMPR2 as key contributors to classification accuracy.

Chi-square evaluation of AP3B1-BMPR2 Co-expression groups

To evaluate the relationship between co-expression patterns of AP3B1 and BMPR2 and PD status, gene expression values were dichotomized at the median. Each sample was classified into one of four expression groups: AP3B1 Low + BMPR2 Low, AP3B1 Low + BMPR2 High, AP3B1 High + BMPR2 Low, and AP3B1 High + BMPR2 High.

A four-by-two contingency table (R × C format) was constructed to summarize the distribution of these combined expression groups between PD patients and HCs. The Pearson Chi-square test of independence was performed using the base R (4.1.1) function chisq.test() to assess whether the distribution of co-expression patterns differed significantly between the two groups.

In addition, pairwise Chi-square tests were conducted across all group combinations, and the resulting P values were visualized as a heatmap using the pheatmap package (1.0.13). Group proportions and overall differences were also visualized using stacked bar plots generated with the ggplot2 and ggstatsplot packages.

All statistical analyses were performed in R (4.1.1), and two-sided P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Human peripheral blood collection

Peripheral blood samples were collected from 31 PD patients and 21 HCs at the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University. Diagnoses followed established PD criteria, and demographic details were recorded in Supplementary Tables 14–15. Blood was collected in EDTA-coated tubes, stabilized with RNAlater (Beyotime, China), placed on ice, and processed for RNA extraction immediately. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (Approval No. 54). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the RNAeasy™ Blood RNA Isolation Kit (Beyotime, Cat# R0091L, China) and quantified with a Nanodrop 300 (ALLSHENG, China). qRT-PCR was performed using Hieff Unicon® TaqMan multiplex qPCR master mix (Yeasen, Cat# 11208ES, China) on a BIORAD Real-Time PCR System. The protocol included: 95 °C for 2 min; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 30 s; and melting curve analysis. GAPDH served as the reference gene, and mRNA expression was analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Statistic analysis

All analyses were performed using R (v4.1.1) and Python (v3.10). DEGs were identified by gene-wise Lasso regression in each GEO dataset, followed by inverse-variance–weighted random-effects meta-analysis (p < 0.05). To infer causal links between gene expression or DNA methylation and PD risk, a three-step SMR was applied by integrating GWAS, cis-eQTL, and cis-mQTL data (p_SMR_multi < 0.05; p_HEIDI > 0.05). Feature prioritization was conducted using SVM-RFE and random forest to identify key PD causal genes.

Two XGBoost diagnostic models were developed using the GEO and PPMI datasets, respectively. Model performance was evaluated using accuracy, AUC, sensitivity, and specificity, while feature importance was quantified by Gini impurity for the GEO model and gain scores for the PPMI model. Finally, co-expression of AP3B1 and BMPR2 between PD and control groups was compared using Pearson’s Chi-square test. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
