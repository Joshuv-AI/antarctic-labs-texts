Design of a hybrid quantum machine learning architecture and analysis of quantum noise effects
Authors: J A Bravo-Montes, A Martín-Toledano, C Velasco-Gallego, G Botella, A A del Barrio, F García-Herrero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantum Machine Learning (QML) is emerging as a promising technology for tackling complex computational challenges, although its practical implementation faces significant obstacles owed to the inherent noise in current quantum devices. This paper presents a hybrid architecture that combines classical and quantum elements for the development and training of QML models under noisy conditions. This research evaluates the impact of noise on superconducting systems through emulation. The study shows that, compared to noise-free configurations, certain noise levels tend to allow for a reduction in the number of qubits, thus simplifying the architecture of the quantum neural network, which has a direct impact on the computational cost and execution times. Experimental validation was performed by applying three biomedical datasets related to breast cancer detection. The experimental findings revealed that the variations in accuracy between noiseless configurations and those subjected to noisy conditions were minimal, with deviations ranging from 0.11% to 1.68%. Additionally, it was observed that the incorporation of noise during training contributed positively to the efficiency of the process for the datasets under test, achieving improvements in training execution times ranging from a factor of 1.61 to 4.39, when the proposed architecture was emulated on Qaptiva 802.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

QML represents a revolutionary paradigm that leverages the unique properties of quantum computing to enhance traditional machine learning capabilities1. This interdisciplinary field promises significant computational advantages over classical approaches, particularly for complex optimization and pattern recognition tasks that are computationally intensive for conventional algorithms. QML has demonstrated transformative potential across diverse areas. In finance, quantum graph neural networks have shown promising results in detecting fraudulent transactions, with studies reporting area under the curve values of 0.85 when compared to classical graph neural networks2, while other QML approaches achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.98 for fraud and non-fraud classification3. The pharmaceutical industry has applied hybrid classical-quantum models for molecular applications, with studies showing improvement in prediction accuracy for protein-ligand binding affinity predictions in drug discovery4. Cybersecurity applications include quantum-powered intrusion detection systems that achieved accuracy in detecting Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks by exploiting QML techniques with outlier analysis and entropy-based methods5. The aerospace industry has explored hybrid classical-QML models for structural health monitoring, showing that integrating quantum layers into classical deep learning architectures enhances damage detection capabilities compared to classical models alone6. Within healthcare, hybrid classical-quantum models demonstrate emerging potential for medical diagnostics. In cardiac applications, quantum support vector machines achieved accuracy for arrhythmia classification using electrocardiogram signal processing on quantum simulators7, while classical Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architectures attained performance on similar tasks8. For oncological applications, quantum transfer learning models showed accuracy in breast cancer detection through optimized quantum kernel methods9, with hybrid classical-quantum architectures on simulators marginally outperforming pure classical approaches in histopathological analysis10.

While these studies demonstrate the potential of QML across various domains, they primarily focus on performance metrics in idealized noiseless environments. However, the practical implementation of quantum algorithms faces significant challenges due to the inherent noise present in current quantum processors. Recent studies suggest that noise may benefit quantum learning in certain cases. For example, the work in11 proposes a framework for training robust observables that maintain their performance even in the presence of noise, thus improving the stability and accuracy of QML models. In contrast, the study from12 demonstrates that noise can be leveraged in quantum reservoir computing to improve information processing capacity and, in some scenarios, enhance system performance. Furthermore, recent studies have shown that designed dissipation can be used to mitigate barren plateaus in variational algorithms13, facilitating the trainability of quantum models even in the presence of noise14. Although these approaches address specific aspects of noise mitigation, they lack a comprehensive analysis of the impact of noise at the qubit and gate levels during QML model training.

Building upon these findings, this study focuses on the proposal, design, and optimization of a classical-quantum hybrid architecture aimed at reducing resource consumption and minimizing the loss of accuracy, considering the limitations imposed by noise in current quantum processors.

The novelty of this study lies in the evaluation of the effects of noise during the training process of the QML model by means of emulation. To achieve this, different levels of noise from current superconducting quantum processors have been implemented on an emulator Qaptiva 802, as well as noise reduction projections for medium-term (target) and long-term (desired) scenarios, whose calibration parameters correspond to an approximation proposed by the authors from15.

To evaluate these noise levels, we utilized datasets from previous studies that proposed hybrid architectures for breast cancer detection16, enabling us to systematically assess how different noise characteristics affect the performance of the model in real-world medical applications and the resources required for training. Although it has been argued that the integration of quantum components into classical flows can provide a balance between expressiveness and noise resilience17, the specific influence of noise on the performance of QML models remains an active research area.

Background

QML encompasses a wide range of approaches, shaped primarily by two dimensions: the nature of the input data (classical or quantum) and the type of hardware used for processing (classical or quantum). A commonly accepted taxonomy organizes the field into four categories, as illustrated in Fig. 118,19.

Taxonomy of quantum-machine-learning approaches by data and hardware type.

Classical–Classical (CC): Both the data and the computation are fully classical, but the algorithms borrow inspiration from quantum mechanics (for example, using Hilbert-space embeddings or tensor-network ideas20). No quantum hardware is involved, and while these methods can offer novel mathematical tools, they do not exploit genuine quantum resources.

Classical–Classical (CC): Both the data and the computation are fully classical, but the algorithms borrow inspiration from quantum mechanics (for example, using Hilbert-space embeddings or tensor-network ideas20). No quantum hardware is involved, and while these methods can offer novel mathematical tools, they do not exploit genuine quantum resources.

Quantum–Classical (QC): Classical machine learning algorithms are applied to data produced by a quantum process—say, measurements from a quantum experiment or outputs of a quantum simulator. The learning itself remains classical, treating quantum-generated data as a special input modality21.

Quantum–Classical (QC): Classical machine learning algorithms are applied to data produced by a quantum process—say, measurements from a quantum experiment or outputs of a quantum simulator. The learning itself remains classical, treating quantum-generated data as a special input modality21.

Classical–Quantum (CQ): In this paradigm, classical (often tabular or image) data are encoded into a quantum circuit, processed by a parametrized quantum subroutine, and then decoded back into classical form for further analysis or classification. CQ models harness quantum superposition and entanglement to enrich feature representations, while relying on mature classical layers for final decision making22.

Classical–Quantum (CQ): In this paradigm, classical (often tabular or image) data are encoded into a quantum circuit, processed by a parametrized quantum subroutine, and then decoded back into classical form for further analysis or classification. CQ models harness quantum superposition and entanglement to enrich feature representations, while relying on mature classical layers for final decision making22.

Quantum–Quantum (QQ): Both the data and the processing are native to quantum hardware: quantum states (possibly unmeasured) feed directly into quantum models, and all training or inference steps occur on a quantum device. While conceptually powerful, QQ methods require advanced quantum memory and interconnects that exceed current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) capabilities23, and are therefore not pursued here.

While conceptually powerful, QQ methods require advanced quantum memory and interconnects that exceed current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) capabilities23, and are therefore not pursued here.

Among these categories, the approach developed in this work belongs to the third, which offers the most promising route for practical applications on near-term quantum devices. Hybrid classical-quantum architectures have emerged as a leading strategy to harness potential quantum advantages while mitigating the limitations of current NISQ hardware17. Hybrid architectures address these limitations by inserting a small variational quantum circuit into an otherwise classical learning pipeline, thereby balancing the representational power of quantum states with the maturity and scalability of classical neural networks.

Hybrid classical-quantum architecture

Figure 2 presents a high-level comparative overview between a fully Quantum model and the Hybrid Classical-Quantum model, providing a visual reference for understanding the flow of data and operations in both approaches.

High-level comparative between fully Quantum model and Hybrid Classical-Quantum model.

The core concept behind hybrid architecture lies in weaving a small parametrized quantum circuit into a classical pipeline24, thereby creating a bridge between the two domains25. First, classical data are normalized and mapped to quantum states by parametric encoding, where each input feature is used to parametrize the rotation angles of single-qubit gates applied to each qubit after Hadamard initialization. This method, implemented as a quantum feature map, allows each scalar value to be represented as a point on the Bloch sphere, granting access to a vastly larger Hilbert space. Next, these qubits traverse a shallow variational circuit composed of alternating layers of trainable single qubit rotations and entangling gates. Within this block, quantum interference and entanglement weave together multiple input features in a high dimensional transformation26 that would demand deeper layers in a purely classical network27. Once the variational processing is complete, each qubit is measured to yield expectation values, which collectively form a quantum feature vector. This vector is then handed off to classical fully connected layers16..

By structuring the model in this four stage flow (Feature Map, Quantum Variational Circuit, Measurement, and Classical Layers) the hybrid design captures two crucial benefits. On one hand, the quantum block can exploit superposition and entanglement to reveal correlations that may be elusive for traditional architectures28; on the other hand, keeping the variational circuit deliberately shallow (often no more than one or two layers) helps contain noise accumulation29, ensuring that the entire system remains compatible with today’s NISQ hardware and can tolerate a certain level of noise. However, the exact thresholds for noise robustness and the scope of its effects on quantum expressivity remain active subjects of research and are not yet fully understood30.

Feature map

The first of these phases is the design of the feature map, the mechanism by which classical inputs are encoded into quantum states. Different maps trade off simplicity, expressivity and circuit depth, and four of the most widely used constructions are:

Angle Encoding: Each numerical feature is mapped to a single qubit in an n-qubit register via angle rotation26,31. The designer can choose any axis for this rotation; for instance, applying an gate directly to the state results in no change, since it is an eigenstate of the Z operator. To ensure non-trivial embedding when utilizing rotations on this axis, it is common practice to precede the operation with a Hadamard gate28.

Amplitude Encoding: It delivers a huge increase in capacity: it can encode up to real inputs into an n-qubit state32,33. This map exploits the full Hilbert space amplitude degrees of freedom, but constructing it via elementary gates can be complex. When pushing amplitude encoding to its limit, some degree of information compression or padding is inevitable, which can result in a partial loss of information or the need to introduce artificial data to fit the quantum state representation.

After processing, the measurement stage extracts classical information by projecting each qubit onto the computational basis. Typically, expectation values are computed empirically by averaging outcomes over many circuit executions, known as shots37. The resulting vector Z of length n constitutes the quantum feature vector, encapsulating the circuit’s high-dimensional mapping of the input data.

Classical layers

A standard feedforward neural network typically consists of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer38. The input layer receives the data that will be processed by the hidden layers. These hidden layers consist of fully connected layers, in which every neuron in one layer is connected to all the neurons in the subsequent layer. In hidden layers, the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function is usually introduced, as it mitigates the probability of the problem of vanishing gradient39. Variations of ReLU, such as Leaky ReLU, Exponential Linear Unit (ELU), Scaled Exponential Linear Unit (SELU), and Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU), are also considered and are further discussed in the methodology section. Regarding the output layer, as this study focuses primarily on binary classification, the sigmoid function is used so that the output of the neural network can be interpreted as a probability that the input falls into the positive class40.

Quantum variational circuit

Following feature encoding, the quantum state is processed by a variational ansatz, a parametrized quantum circuit designed to introduce nonlinearity and entanglement17. It comprises L repeated layers, each consisting of:

The use of nearest-neighbour entanglement reflects both hardware constraints and theoretical results indicating that such patterns are sufficient for universal quantum computation in shallow circuits25.

Measurement

The final stage of the quantum layer is the extraction of classical information via measurement. Specifically, each qubit is measured in the Pauli-Z basis, and the expectation value is computed for each qubit i, resulting in a quantum feature vector , where each component is given by

with denoting the quantum state after the variational circuit. This quantum feature vector constitutes the output of the quantum layer and is directly forwarded as the input to the classical neural network, ensuring that the dimensionality of the quantum output matches the number of qubits and thus maintains architectural compatibility.

In practice, the measurement is implemented using Qiskit’s SparsePauliOp objects, which define the tensor product structure of the observables required for the expectation value calculations. The use of expectation values, as opposed to projective measurements, is motivated by their differentiability, which is essential for enabling gradient-based optimization in hybrid classical-quantum models37. This approach ensures that the quantum-to-classical interface is both mathematically rigorous and practically suitable for end-to-end training of the hybrid architecture.

Integration and data flow

The quantum layer acts as the initial processing block in the hybrid architecture. After data preprocessing, the classical feature vector is encoded, transformed by the variational circuit, and measured to yield a quantum feature vector. This output is then used as the input to the first classical layer, typically a fully connected neural network, as detailed in the next section. The tight coupling between the quantum and classical components ensures end-to-end differentiability and enables the joint optimization of all parameters.

Classical layers

After applying the quantum step to determine the number of qubits, a classical feedforward neural network is introduced to perform a binary classification task.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, the classical feedforward neural network consists of an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. The input layer contains as many neurons as the number of qubits used in the quantum architecture, while the output layer contains a single neuron, as the task involves binary classification. The input neurons receive data from the quantum architecture and pass it through the hidden layers, culminating in the final classification output from the output neuron.

To process information in the hidden layers, Equation 1 is applied, where each neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs. Weights determine the strength of connections between neurons, and biases are constants added to each neuron to provide additional flexibility and learning capability.

The result of each weighted sum is passed through an activation function, which applies a linear or nonlinear transformation46. In feedforward neural networks, the ReLU is commonly used due to its computational efficiency and simplicity47. ReLU helps mitigate the vanishing gradient problem by mapping negative input values to zero and leaving positive values unchanged46. This property induces sparsity in the network and reduces parameter interdependence, which helps prevent overfitting48.

Results

Noise emulation

To properly interpret the results presented in this study, it is essential to detail the methodology used for implementing and evaluating noise in the quantum model. The proposed architecture for noise emulation in the hybrid model was based on the use of Qaptiva 802, a quantum emulator60, which allows noiseless and noisy emulations. In addition, the model was trained using the Qiskit Machine Learning library61, which facilitates the integration of parametrisable quantum circuits into hybrid workflows. In this context, the presence of noise was emulated in the quantum model by simulating imperfections in gate implementation, measurements, and initialization, thus reflecting the main sources of errors present in the hardware.

The noise emulation applied in this study is based on a previously validated methodology from41 in which noise models were calibrated and tuned according to the characteristics of specific quantum backends (e.g., technology, topology, and vendor). Using this approach, a fidelity deviation of less than 1% was obtained when comparing emulator results against real hardware experiments on the emulated superconducting device. While we acknowledge that emulation cannot fully capture all physical effects present in real quantum processors, this calibration provides a reasonable approximation for studying performance trends. The intent of this work is therefore not to replace device-specific outcomes, but to explore the general behavior of hybrid classical-quantum systems under controlled and progressively reduced noise levels, as would be expected in medium-term, higher-fidelity quantum hardware according to the information provided in15. Results should be interpreted within this context, and the emulator parameters would need to be re-tuned for each specific device or quantum technology.

The noise parametrization was based on calibration data obtained from a comparative study conducted on superconducting qubits15, which defines three hardware fidelity scenarios: baseline, target, and desired. The baseline set represents the current state-of-the-art values, the target set is conceived as a promising short-term goal, and the desired set of synthetically generated hardware specifications corresponds to a noise model with approximately twice the error-suppression rate of the target hardware, thereby capturing the dominant error sources encountered in current quantum hardware platforms. Table 2 lists the values used in this study for each scenario. For each noise configuration, stochastic noise was applied independently to every quantum gate operation in the circuit. For instance, the noise associated with each CNOT gate was sampled independently according to the corresponding two-qubit error rate, enabling the emulator to approximate realistic gate-level defects.

Architecture results

The main objective of this study is to propose an architecture that optimizes the design of hybrid quantum neural networks in the presence of noise. Therefore, it is necessary to address the impact of quantum noise and the number of qubits on the performance of hybrid neural networks. Note that the same number of features (seen in Section "Case study and data description") was applied to each dataset, regardless of the number of utilized qubits. Three different types of datasets have been analyzed: one with insufficient information, another characterized by high data redundancy, and a third that constitutes the optimal set for training. To this end, a sensitivity analysis has been conducted, evaluating how the classification performance of the model evolves with respect to the number of qubits and the level of noise in each of the three case studies. The results are graphically presented in Fig. 10. To avoid the influence of neural network hyperparameters on sensitivity analysis, the same fixed architecture was used in all experiments, both with and without noise. This architecture consists of a hybrid neural network with a quantum layer, a classical hidden layer containing 32 hidden units, and a GELU activation function. The learning rate is set to 0.1 and the optimiser chosen is Adam. Under this fixed architecture, only the number of qubits and the noise parameters were varied to isolate their effects. While the experiments were conducted in a simulated environment, the noise parameters were based on calibrated models reflecting realistic device behavior. Although some physical effects may not be fully captured, the observed improvements in training efficiency and generalization under moderate noise levels suggest a potentially robust trend. These findings should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive, motivating future validation on real quantum processors.

A different trend was observed in the WDBC case study. In this instance, the performance of the noise-injected models steadily increased until reaching a peak at 7 qubits. After that point, the performance slightly declined and then stabilizes. A similar trend is observed in the noiseless model; however, in this case, the best performance appears to occur at 10 qubits. This difference can be attributed to the fact that the noiseless model benefits more from the increase in qubits because it can exploit the additional dimensionality without penalty, which explains why it reaches its maximum performance at 10 qubits without the deterioration observed in other cases. This performance gap highlights the need for classical optimization techniques to bridge the difference between noise-injected and noiseless models. Through this optimization process, it is possible to achieve the same accuracy while reducing the required computational resources, demonstrating the effectiveness of classical optimization in mitigating the impact of quantum noise.

Two main conclusions can also be drawn from the analysis of the overall average training runtime: 1) the average training time increases significantly as the number of qubits increases, and 2) there are systematic differences between the noiseless models and those with injected quantum noise. The former can be attributed to the increasing complexity of the hybrid quantum model; as the number of qubits increases, the model’s architecture becomes more complex, thereby raising its computational cost. The latter may be related to the regularization capabilities introduced by the injected quantum noise, which aligns with previous findings that suggest noise can benefit quantum learning in certain cases11,12.

Quantum noise may act as a form of stochastic regularization, helping the model avoid overfitting or escape from local minima. Additionally, this stochastic behavior can accelerate convergence, thereby reducing total training time. During training, noise introduces a perturbation that resembles classical regularization techniques such as dropout or weight decay. This perturbation can reduce the model’s reliance on greater capacity—that is, the need for additional qubits to generalize effectively.

Furthermore, the presence of noise may help mitigate overfitting in higher-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Noiseless models tend to exploit a larger parameter space as the number of qubits increases, which can lead to overfitting when not countered by explicit regularization or sufficient training data. In contrast, noisy models are inherently perturbed, which may encourage the use of more compact and robust architectures.

It should be noted that increasing the number of qubits does not always improve performance. As seen in Figure 11, adding more qubits can degrade accuracy, likely owing to accumulated noise, redundant or non-orthogonal parameter sweeps, or the increased difficulty of optimising a larger space without stochastic guidance. Conversely, as shown in Figure 12, reducing qubits does not guarantee improvement: a low number can limit expressiveness, creating a trade-off that depends on the noise level and complexity of the task.

Figures 16,17,18,19 shows, an analysis of the training execution times across the four noise configurations revealed consistent scalability patterns, with the GBSG demonstrating the best performance, followed by the WDBC, SEER exhibited the steepest runtime growth. The results show a preliminary trend where increasing noise calibration appears to improve computational efficiency: i) Noiseless Fig. 16: 95,000 seconds for SEER at 5 qubits; ii) Baseline Fig. 17: 80,000–90,000 seconds, representing 5–10% reduction; iii) Target Fig. 18: 50,000 seconds, representing 30–40% reduction; iv) Desired Fig. 19: 45,000 seconds, representing 50% reduction. All datasets exhibit exponential scaling with two to three orders of magnitude growth from one to five qubits, exceeding the seconds threshold at or before four qubits. These preliminary findings from emulated environments suggest that strategic noise calibration could potentially provide up to 50% efficiency gains, offering a promising approach to mitigating computational scaling challenges in near-term quantum hardware implementations.

However, the type and amount of introduced quantum noise must be carefully optimized to avoid detrimental effects on model performance.

Discussion and conclusion

The results presented in this study underscore the growing relevance and practical challenges of leveraging hybrid quantum classical machine-learning models. Building on these considerations, this study presents the development of a hybrid classical-quantum machine learning model for classification, incorporating quantum noise injection during training.

Three noise scenarios were emulated: 1) noiseless, 2) baseline noise, 3) desired noise, and 4) target noise. To assess the effectiveness of the proposed architecture and analyze the impact of injected quantum noise, a case study was conducted using three biomedical datasets.

Analogous results were observed across all three implementations: the overall training time was reduced when quantum noise was injected during training, and similar performance levels were achieved across both noiseless and noisy scenarios. This occurred despite the neural network architecture being significantly simpler in noise-injected configurations, thereby reducing the computational cost while maintaining comparable results.

The main contributions of this study are as follows. The hybrid quantum classical QML architecture designed specifically for model training under realistic noise conditions, incorporating noise sources at the qubit level (initialization and measurement) and quantum gate level (single and two-quantum gates), has proven effective in maintaining model performance while reducing complexity. The utilization of calibration parameters derived from realistic noise levels provides a comprehensive evaluation approach for noise impact assessment, enabling the systematic analysis of different noise scenarios. The systematic analysis and optimization of the architecture hyperparameters successfully minimized the resource consumption (qubits, number of quantum gates, and circuit depth) for each evaluated dataset and noise level, demonstrating the adaptability of the proposed architecture to different experimental conditions.

Therefore, it can be concluded that the injection of quantum noise during training has a positive impact on the efficiency of the evaluated QML models while preserving accuracy. The obtained results provide new perspectives for the development of more efficient quantum algorithms. The results suggest that moderate noise levels, traditionally considered an obstacle, can act as a regularizing factor that enables simpler architectures with reduced resource requirements rather than conferring a direct computational advantage. To consolidate these observations and expand their applicability, it is necessary to broaden the study by considering aspects such as validation on a larger number of datasets, evaluation of real quantum devices, and exploration of the generalization of these results to other application domains beyond the biomedical context. These extensions would allow the robustness and general applicability of the proposed methodology to be established, thus contributing to the advancement of the field of QML and facilitating the transition to practical implementations.

The observations from our work align with the concept of noise-assisted learning in QML, where stochastic perturbations or decoherence can enhance trainability and generalization rather than being purely detrimental. Similar to classical regularization techniques such as dropout, moderate quantum noise can prevent overfitting to idealized noiseless dynamics, promote exploration of flatter loss landscapes, and improve robustness to unseen data. Recent studies65,66 support this view by demonstrating that controlled noise or robustness-oriented strategies can strengthen model reliability and balance expressivity with stability through frameworks such as the quantum Fisher information. Complementary approaches, such as dissipative hybrid models67, further suggest that open-system dynamics and dissipation can serve as computational resources to enhance learning performance. Building on these insights, future work could explore the integration of the developed framework and dissipative strategies on real quantum hardware, evaluate their impact across a broader range of datasets and QML architectures, deepen the theoretical understanding of how noise influences generalization and robustness in practical quantum machine learning applications, and focus on validating and extending these results across both this technology and other quantum technologies.
