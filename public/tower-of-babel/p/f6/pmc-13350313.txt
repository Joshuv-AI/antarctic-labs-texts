Geopolitics, international collaborations and publication ethics
Authors: Olle ten Cate, Vanessa C Burch, Stanley J Hamstra, Lindsey M Pope, Jennifer Weller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Short abstractGeopolitics, international collaborations, and publication ethic ‐‐ how does it affect HPE scholarship? #MedEd

The interference of politics in science is a growing concern in universities and health care institutions. 1 , 2 More than in the past, universities face tensions with governments interfering in areas of research that do not support governmental policies. One specific type of interference concerns restrictions on international collaborations, such as exemplified by a recent US federal executive order (14117/28) leading the National Institutes of Health to restrict access to certain scientific databases by citizens of specific countries.

Since 2005, Medical Education has convened a Quality and Standards Advisory Group (QSAG) to advise and support the Editor around matters of ethics, quality and standards in publication. 3 Besides advising about specific instances of concern, often confidentially, QSAG has also been given the opportunity to comment occasionally on matters of general interest in publication. While research, theory development and innovation of education as published in this and other health professions education journals may seem less a target of political interference than some other areas, interference in our domain is no longer hypothetical.

Publications in medical education journals may seem less a target of political interference, but interference in our domain is no longer hypothetical.

Two of the current members of QSAG (the authors of this commentary) recently experienced geopolitical interference that led to the revision of a publication before submission. The publication—an invited book chapter that is currently being processed—was based on an international symposium about competency‐based curriculum implementation processes. Invited presentations from countries in Asia, Latin America, North America and Europe reported on experiences and lessons learned. A horseshoe seating arrangement in the room enabled a lively and open discussion. The invited book chapter was to include comparative summaries of reports from all countries represented at the symposium. Following the symposium, international frictions arose. Country A co‐author could not accept the way Country B would be presented in the publication. And for the co‐author of Country B, it would be unacceptable to follow Country A's rules of formulation. Despite highly constructive preparations for the symposium, the free speech during the meeting and the considerable efforts of all parties to find an acceptable solution, the conclusion was that one of Countries A or B would have to withdraw their contribution to the book chapter. Both intended co‐authors tried to comply with restrictions imposed upon them by their home countries and would face serious consequences for personal careers if these were breached. This forced the difficult decision to leave out one author's contribution, resulting in a less informative chapter. This was regrettable, especially since the purpose of the work was to benefit from the varied perspectives of different authors and countries. In particular, the discussions at the symposium highlighted how dyadic comparisons between neighbouring countries with an apparently similar culture can reveal very different policies and practices for implementing medical curricula; thus, the exclusion of the findings of any member of the group would weaken the overall impact of the science. This example shows how political directives can thwart international collaboration. Of course, in this example, country reports could all be published separately, but the preclusion of a collaborative paper that analyses implementation issues in a comparative way impoverishes science.

Our QSAG's question then becomes: What role does a journal, and a journal editor, have in combating political interference? Is it a matter of publication ethics, quality and standards? Research ethics can be conceived as consisting of three components: the defence of the integrity of science, integrity of research subjects and integrity of authorship. 4 Geopolitical influences can shape the content and integrity of the science, not only the composition of the author team. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors' proposed rules for authorship that are widely endorsed, 5 and the Committee of Publications Ethics COPE and the World Association of Medical Editors WAME are clear about geopolitical interference in scientific publications: Editorial decisions should not be affected by the origins of the manuscript, including the nationality, ethnicity, political beliefs, race or religion of the authors. 6 , 7 However, editors would typically be unaware of prior negotiations about author contributions and exclusions before submission. They can only accept or reject what they see. Yet, this example shows how the integrity of authorship and of science can be hurt, hampering the development of science and innovation, and affecting the quality of literature reviews and the conclusions drawn from meta‐analyses. 8

Editorial decisions should not be affected by nationality, ethnicity, political beliefs, race, or religion of the authors.

Our message is one of significance more broadly. Across the 20th and early 21st centuries, the world has developed to be highly interconnected, and international collaborations in studies and publications have increased beyond imagination compared to a century ago. In health professions education scholarship, international collaboration has gone from being an exception 50 years ago to becoming prominent now in Western countries and quickly emerging more globally. The community is served by active cross‐cultural exchange and collaboration. 9 , 10 , 11

In HPE scholarship, international collaboration has evolved from being an exception 50 years ago to becoming prominent now.

At the same time, however, international tensions have increased. Restrictions on freedom of expression have intensified from 2014–2024 in Europe, North America, South America, Africa and Asia, 12 and we may expect to face more tension between science and politics going forward. Politics can interfere with the freedom to express and collaborate; it is on individual scholars to continue working together across borders and look for ways to avoid political interference in their work. Minoritised communities of scholars deserve advocacy in promoting their scientific work, and international collaborations in publications, courses, workshops, conferences and university exchanges should support this. Open communication and collaboration constitute the very foundation of scientific progress, and inclusive journal policies can be an effective tool to ensure that such progress continues, but authors and educators have a significant role.

Inclusive journal policies can be an effective tool to ensure that progress continues, but authors and educators also play a significant role.
