Highly reliable and decoupled temperature-pressure bimodal sensor based on PVDF-HFP ionogel
Authors: Yumei Lin, Ya-Hsin Pai, Shoubo Li, Chao Wang, Jincheng Liao, Fan Wu, Zhen Xu, Qingfeng Song, Yi Feng, Ziqi Liang, Lidong Chen, Shengqiang Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryExisting bimodal sensors rely on complex multimaterial integration and system topologies, hindering healthcare diagnostics. This work presents a sensor using single-material PVDF-HFP ionogel with a sandwich structure, enabling simultaneous, accurate temperature, and pressure monitoring. Decoupled sensing is achieved via two distinct mechanisms within one material: the ionic thermodiffusion (Soret) effect for temperature and interfacial electrical double-layer (EDL) capacitance for pressure. The sensor exhibits high performance: a temperature sensitivity of 4.2 mV K−1 (limit of detection, LOD: 0.05 K) with < 10 s response time, and pressure sensitivity of 1.35 kPa−1 (LOD: 11 Pa) with < 60 ms response time. Optimized processing yields high-reliability ionogels; unencapsulated samples maintained 93% thermopower after three months of air storage. Finally, sensor arrays integrated into a glove successfully distinguished and mapped pressure and temperature stimuli, demonstrating feasibility for wearable electronics.

Introduction

Flexible sensors are core components of smart wearable devices, showing great promise in health monitoring,1,2,3 human-machine interaction,4,5 soft robotics,6,7 and electronic skins.8 Most current research focuses on single-modal sensors, which can only detect one type of physical or chemical signals, such as pressure,9 temperature,10 strain,11 or humidity.12 Among them, temperature and pressure are fundamental physical stimuli. However, in practical applications, multiple physical parameters typically change simultaneously, requiring comprehensive monitoring for accurate evaluation. Accordingly, highly integrated dual-modal13,14 or multimodal sensors15,16 that maintain portability and miniaturization are strongly desired.

A critical challenge in bimodal sensing lies in achieving effective decoupling while avoiding signal crosstalk to enable independent accurate monitoring.17,18 Current strategies for dual-mode sensing generally fall into two categories. The physical integration of different functional units can achieve independent sensitivities, but it also introduces structural complexity, potential signal interference, and interfacial instability.19,20 An alternative strategy seeks to circumvent these issues by employing a single material with physical quantities capable of responding to multiple stimuli. For instance, the compressible ionogel of PVA can generate current under both pressure and temperature stimuli.21 PVDF-co-CTFE ionogel can generate mixed voltage signals in response to applied pressure and thermal stimuli.22 Although this simplifies device architecture, it requires complex frequency filtering or machine learning algorithms to disentangle the response of different stimuli from a single output channel. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a single-material system featuring dual response mechanisms.

In various ionogel families, poly(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP) is a prime candidate which combine the flexibility from polymer matrix and the ionic conductivity from ionic liquids, offering unique advantages in flexible electronics.23 It possesses a copolymer structure comprising amorphous HFP segments and crystalline PVDF domains, which provides excellent mechanical flexibility and electrochemical stability.24 Moreover, it has been proven to exhibit high performance in individual sensor for both temperature and pressure. PVDF-HFP mixed with 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([EMIM][TFSI]) achieved a thermopower of −4 mV K−1.25 A precise temperature detection across a wide range (+20 ∼ −6 mV K−1) is realized in PVDF-HFP/Na:TFSI system.26 By modulating the solid network structure, an enhanced thermopower of 24.5 mV K−1 achieved in PVDF-HFP mixed with 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium dicyanamide ([EMIM][DCA]).27

For pressure sensing, PVDF-HFP offers multiple transduction mechanisms: the polar β-phase of PVDF-HFP enables piezoelectric sensing,28,29 and the EDL capacitance at the electrode-electrolyte interface support capacitive pressure sensing. PVDF-HFP/[EMIM]Cl films have been utilized as dielectric layers to construct iontronic pressure sensors, demonstrating a high sensitivity of 1.64 kPa−1.30 Capacitive pressure sensors were fabricated by integrating PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] films with flexible electrodes with featuring microstructure surfaces.31 The sensitivity of ionic piezocapacitive sensors was enhanced in a composite model based on a PVDF-HFP matrix incorporating fibers and ionogels.32 Collectively, the PVDF-HFP-based ionogels inherently integrates ionic thermoelectric, piezoelectric, and capacitive functionalities, offering a platform for bimodal sensing.

In such a single-material system, achieve decoupled temperature and pressure sensing remains challenging. In previous work, we revealed the synergistic enhancement of ion thermoelectric performance by the piezoelectric internal electric field in the PVDF-HFP system, demonstrating its feasibility for simultaneous response to thermal and pressure stimulus.33 However, such differential algorithmic decoupling based on the single signal of voltage increases system complexity and requires additional computational power for data processing. Therefore, optimization of the sensing performance of PVDF-HFP system still remains to be explored.

Herein, we report a bimodal sensor based on PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] ionogel. This design exploits two sensing mechanisms originating from the same material: the Soret effect for temperature and the EDL capacitance for pressure. The inherent spatial separation enables intrinsic decoupling and enhances sensing performance, which achieved temperature and pressure sensitivities of −4.2 mV K−1 and 1.3 kPa−1, LOD of 0.05 K (SNR = 10.4 dB) and 11 Pa (SNR = 14.7 dB), and response times <10 s and < 60 m s, respectively. Furthermore, the sensor retained 93% of its original performance after three months of storage without encapsulation. The prototype of bimodal sensor array on the glove demonstrates accurate feedback for temperature and pressure stimuli in real-world scenarios, showing significant potential for applications in intelligent robotic perception and smart wearable devices.

Results

Material preparation and characterization

The ionogel, composed of PVDF-HFP polymer matrix and [EMIM][TFSI] ionic liquid, maintains a quasi-solid state macroscopically while allowing ion mobility microscopically. The ionic liquid acts as a weak crosslinker, facilitating the formation of a physically crosslinked network that aids charge carrier migration.34 The polar β-phase of PVDF-HFP can engage in ion-dipole interactions with the ions, modulating their thermal diffusion.35 To quantify the thermoelectric performance, the thermopower (or temperature sensitivity) is defined as α = –ΔV/ΔT, where ΔV is the potential difference and ΔT is the temperature difference between the hot and cold sides of sensor; the sign of α is determined by the charge carrier with higher thermomobility.36 Given that the microstructure and phase content are predominantly determined during the curing process, and directly dictate the thermopower, we first investigate the influence of curing temperature on the microstructure and sensing properties.

In Figure 1A, the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of pure PVDF-HFP show dominant α-phase peaks at 18.1° and 19.9°, assignable to the (100) and (110) planes, respectively.37 Upon incorporation of the ionic liquid of [EMIM][TFSI], these peaks merge into a single characteristic β-phase peak at 20.2° ((110) plane). Normalized FTIR spectra (see in Figure S1 in Supporting Information) show peaks at 480 and 615 cm−1 (α-phase), 510 cm−1 (β-phase),38 and peaks at 741, 1330, and 1347 cm−1 confirming the presence of the [TFSI]- anion. With curing temperature rising, the enlarged view (440–1000 cm−1, Figure 1B) reveals that the relative intensity of the β-phase peak (510 cm−1) increases compared to the α-phase peak (480 cm−1) indicating enhanced β-phase formation. This conclusion is corroborated by Raman spectroscopy (Figure 1C), where the intensity of the β-phase peak (840 cm−1) increases relative to the α-phase (796 cm−1) and γ-phase (811 cm−1) peaks.40,41,42,43 The increasing of curing temperature also caused a blue shift of the FT-IR peak from 1272 cm−1 to 1276 cm−1, attributable to crystallite size effects and enhanced ion-dipole interactions between the polymer and the imidazolium ring.44,45,46 Detailed deconvolution of the FT-IR spectra (3050–3250 cm−1) was performed using Peakit software to precisely determine the peak positions of the C-H stretching vibrations in the imidazolium ring. Figure S2 (Supporting Information) revealed four primary peaks near 3104, 3125, 3158, and 3176 cm−1. Peaks at 3158 and 3176 cm−1 are associated with polymer-complexed and excess un-complexed [EMIM]+, respectively.47 The intensity ratio I3158/I3176 increased from 1.65 to 3.78 with elevated curing temperature, indicating a greater proportion of cations engaged in ion-dipole interactions with the polymer chains.48 This enhanced cation complexation reinforces the dominance of anionic charge carriers, leading to the observed increase in the negative thermopower (Figure 1D). Corresponding Raman analysis (Figure S3, Supporting Information) shows a decrease in the I2950/I2980 ratio (Peak at 2950 cm−1 assigned to symmetric stretching of the methyl group in [EMIM]+),49 indicating a reduced population of free cations, further evidencing the role of curing temperature in tuning structure-property relationships.

We further investigated the shrinkage rate and the density of PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] ionogels cured at different temperatures. As illustrated in Figure 1E, shrinkage became more pronounced with increasing curing temperatures. Since rapid solvent evaporation may cause internal bubbles, density measurements were employed to assess bubble formation. The initial gradual density increase with increasing curing temperature suggests the formation of a more compact polymer network, corroborated by SEM images (Figure S4, Supporting Information). When the curing temperatures beyond 90 °C, a significant density drop and data scatter indicated excessive bubbles, undermining structural homogeneity. Furthermore, the long-term stability of PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] ionogel was evaluated by monitoring the thermopower retention (α/α0, where α0 is the initial thermopower) under unencapsulated conditions (Figure 1F). PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] ionogel cured above 70 °C maintained retention rates exceeding 80% after 90 days, highlighting superior stability. Balancing sensor performance and structural stability, 80 °C was selected as the optimal curing temperature for all subsequent studies.

Temperature sensing performance

Temperature sensing is based on Soret effect. As established previously, the ionogel exhibits a stable negative thermopower dominated by anions. The effective sensing range was evaluated by measuring α at different ambient temperatures (Figure 2A). At 30 °C, α = −4.17 mV K−1 with a linearity of 0.998. With increasing ambient temperature, the magnitude of α (|α|) monotone decreased to 0.68 mV K−1 at 80 °C with high linearities over 0.980. Figure 2B shows the stable electrical response of the sensor under different temperature gradients, from which the LOD was determined. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), defined as the ratio of the output signal to the noise, was calculated using the formula SNR = 20Log10(N/δN) (unit: dB), where N is the peak response signal and δN is the environmental noise level.59 A higher SNR indicates a stronger signal relative to the noise and thus better signal quality. Following the industry standard for sensors, where an SNR ≥ 10 dB is considered the minimum threshold for reliable detection.60 The sensor shows an SNR of 10.4 dB when ΔT = 0.05 K which meets the detection criteria, confirming a minimum LOD of 0.05 K for temperature difference. The response time (t90), defined as the duration required for the sensor output to reach 90% of its final value following a step change in the physical stimulus, was characterized in accordance with IEC 60050–300. As shown in Figure 2C, under varying temperature gradients, the response time exhibited minor fluctuations but remained consistently below 10 s. The recovery time shows a slight rising into about 20 s (ΔT = 0.5 K) and 30 s (ΔT = 5 K). Pressure sensing performance

The pressure transduction mechanism of PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] sensor is based on the modulation of the electrical double-layer (EDL) capacitance at the ionogel-electrode interface. Under pressure, the geometric change of the ionogel dielectric layer prompts the redistribution of ions, forming the EDL layer between ionic-electronic contacts.61,62 The effective capacitance C is defined by the formula C=11Ctop+1Cbottom+Cp. The primary variation in the C is predominantly induced by the modulation of the top (Ctop) and bottom (Cbottom) EDL capacitances, with the bulk capacitance (Cp) contributing minimally to the overall change. Pressure sensitivity (S) is defined as S=δ(ΔC/C0)δP, where ΔC is the capacitance change, C0 is the initial capacitance, and P is the applied pressure.

Figure 3A presents the relative capacitance change curve (sensitivity curve) of the PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] pressure sensor. The sensitivity demonstrating a wide operational range, which reached 1.35 kPa−1 in the low-pressure range (<3.8 kPa), 0.39 kPa−1 in middle-pressure range (3.8–90 kPa), and 0.06 kPa−1 at high-pressure range (90–185 kPa). Gauge factor (GF) values of PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] ionogel under different compressive strain (ε) intervals are presented in Figure S8 (Supporting Information). As shown in Figure 3D, the sensor exhibits stable responses under various pressures, an SNR of 14.7 dB was achieved at the pressure of 11 Pa, confirming its LOD. To investigate the crosstalk between different stimulus, we characterized α under varying static pressures and S under different temperature gradients. As shown in Figure 4B, the applied pressure significantly modulates α. Below a threshold of 28 kPa, α increases linearly with pressure (α = –4 – 0.15P, R2 = 0.984), reaching a maximum enhancement of approximately 2-fold (−7.9 mV K−1). The applied stress compresses the physical spacing between PVDF-HFP polymer segments, which significantly strengthens the ion-dipole interactions between the polar β-phase and the ionic liquid. This densification promotes ion dissociation and reinforces the separation of cations and anions under a temperature gradient. However, beyond 28 kPa, α decreases because intense carrier scattering within the polymer matrix drastically reduces the ion diffusion coefficient. This hindered charge diffusion leads to the observed attenuation of α.33

In stark contrast, S is virtually unaffected by temperature variations (Figure 4C). This insensitivity arises because the EDL cannot sustain a significant thermal gradient, and the capacitive pressure response is instantaneous compared to the slower thermal diffusion process. Consequently, the voltage signal (ΔV) encodes combined temperature and pressure information, while the capacitance signal (ΔC/C0) responds exclusively to pressure. The external pressure (P) is first determined via the temperature-independent capacitance signal, then utilized to select the corresponding α for back-calculating the accurate temperature change (ΔT) from the voltage output. This intrinsic decoupling allows for the straightforward extraction of both stimuli from two independent electrical readouts, eliminating the need for complex algorithms.

To further investigate the response of the dual-mode sensor to dynamic pressure and thermal stimuli, a 4 × 4 sensing array was fabricated by integrating the PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] ionogel with a flexible printed circuit board (Figure 4D). Copper block shaped as the letters “S”, “I”, and “C” with different masses were placed on the array. Color-mapped distributions of ΔV and ΔC/C0 clearly visualized the shapes and weight of the blocks. The decoupled temperature difference and pressure values are annotated numerically in the figure. The results confirm that the array accurately reconstructs the spatial distributions of both temperature difference and pressure, validating the spatial accuracy and reliability of the array for tactile and thermal mapping.

Furthermore, a five-channel array was employed and integrated onto a glove, simulating the multipoint tactile perception of human skin (Figure 4E). When grasping a weight using 3 fingers, sensors 1, 2, and 3 exhibited rapid and distinct responses in both voltage and capacitance signals, while sensors 4 and 5 remained at baseline (Figure S9, Supporting Information). When gripping a water cup with nonuniform temperature distribution, clear differences in the voltage response amplitudes were observed. The decoupled temperature difference and pressure stimuli are numerically labeled in the corresponding figure, reflecting both the pressure differences across the fingertips and the temperature profile of the cup. Comparison with infrared thermal images showed that the sensor achieved a temperature response accuracy of 93%. Notably, subtle real-time fluctuations in the capacitance signal captured transient pressure variations during the pickup and release process, demonstrating the potential for wearable sensing applications.

Discussion

A high-performance and highly stable bimodal sensor from the PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] ionogel realized simultaneous and decoupled temperature and pressure sensing, which based on two spatially distinct transduction mechanisms within a homogeneous material: the Soret effect for temperature sensing and the interfacial EDL capacitance for pressure sensing. This architecture bypasses the need for complex structural integration or post-processing algorithms, offering a streamlined approach to multi-parameter detection. The practical viability of this technology was convincingly demonstrated in a typical wearable scene of glove. Future exploration could broaden single-material platforms to include strain and humidity sensing by tailoring the ionogel’s viscoelasticity and hygroscopicity. Such multifunctional designs offer a scalable paradigm for next-generation E-skin and wearable electronics.

Limitations of the study

While the PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] ionogel demonstrates exceptional performance in decoupled temperature and pressure sensing, certain constraints warrant further consideration. First, the potential for cross-interference from environmental humidity remains an unresolved challenge. Second, current fabrication limitations hinder miniaturization of the sensor, which remains at the millimeter scale. Future research will focus on micro-scale architectures to improve integration density and wearable comfort.

Method details

Materials and reagents

PVDF-HFP, acetone, [EMIM][TFSI], PDMS and silver paste were obtained from commercial sources as listed in the key resources table.

Fabrication of PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] ionogel

PVDF-HFP was dissolved in acetone (0.15 g mL−1) at 65 °C for 2 h [EMIM][TFSI] was added (mass ratio [EMIM][TFSI]:PVDF-HFP = 4:1) and stirred for another 2 h. The mixture was cast into a polypropylene mold and dried in an oven at temperatures ranging from 25 °C to 120 °C for 12 h. The resulting PVDF-HFP/[EMIM][TFSI] ionogel film, with a thickness of 0.5 mm, was then precisely cut into uniform pieces with dimensions of 5∗5 mm2 using a square puncher.

Fabrication of a wearable glove and 4 × 4 sensor array

A wearable sensing glove was fabricated by sandwiching an ionogel cured at 80 °C between two copper foils, which were then attached to a commercially available glove.

The sensor array consists of top and bottom flexible printed circuit boards (FPCBs) with electrodes, a PDMS support layer, and the ionogel sensing material. The PDMS support layer was prepared by thoroughly mixing the PDMS base and curing agent at a weight ratio of 10:1, pouring the mixture into a stainless-steel mold, degassing it in a vacuum chamber, and curing it on a hot plate at 100 °C for 2 h. The uniform PDMS layer was then laser-cut (UV Laser Micro-machining System, FM-UVM7) to form a 4 × 4 array of of circular cavities with a diameter of 4 mm (Φ 4 mm), precisely matching the electrode pattern (Φ 4.2 mm) on the FPCBs.

The laser-cut PDMS support layer was aligned and placed onto the bottom FPCB, ensuring the cavities corresponded precisely to the electrode pattern. A uniformly mixed ionogel precursor was then dispensed into the cavities and dried in an oven at 80 °C for 12 h. Silver paste was applied onto the cured ionogel to fill any voids and ensure electrical contact. After drying, the top FPCB was carefully aligned and placed over the structure, and the assembly was secured and encapsulated using UV-curable adhesive, resulting in a flexible sensor array.

Characterization

Crystallinity was assessed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 DISCOVER DAVINCI, Bruker, Germany). Chemical structure was characterized by FT-IR spectrometer (Nicolet iS5, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Raman spectroscopy (inVia, Renishaw, UK). Morphology was analyzed by high resolution field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, SU8220, Hitachi, Japan). The densities were measured by the drainage method. Since no in-plane shrinkage was observed, the volumetric shrinkage was determined solely by the linear shrinkage in the thickness direction, which was measured using a digital caliper.

The thermoelectric performance was characterized using a custom-built measurement system. A temperature difference (ΔT) across the ionogel was established by thermoelectric cooler (TEC) elements controlled by a DC power supply (SPPS-D305-232, Nanjing Kuaiqu Electronic Co., Ltd., China). Square copper electrodes with dimensions of 8∗8 mm2 were employed. The temperatures at both ends were precisely monitored using calibrated thermocouples (Multichannel Temperature Tester, ZC3000, Changzhou Zhongce Instrument Co., Ltd., China). The resulting voltage output was measured by a Keithley multimeter system (DAQ6510, Keithley Instruments, USA). The entire measurement setup was housed within a custom environmental chamber (Constant Humidity Chamber, CMA930S(H), Shanghai Xianke Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., China) to maintain a constant relative humidity of 70% throughout the testing process, ensuring environmental stability. In the temperature response time characterization, both ends of the ionogel were first equilibrated at an identical ambient temperature. Subsequently, one end was rapidly brought into contact with a pre-temperature-regulated thermal stage to instantaneously establish a temperature gradient. For the pressure sensitivity (S) measurements, Flectromechanical Universal Testing Machine (CMT6103, MTS Systems (China) Co., Ltd., China) was employed to apply precisely controlled and continuously varying pressure, while Keithley multimeters recorded the corresponding capacitance changes throughout the process. The pressure response time was evaluated by applying transient pressure stimuli using calibrated weights of different masses.

The thermopower (α) under different pressures was measured by applying static loads with standard weights to establish a stable pressure platform. The remaining testing procedures were consistent with the aforementioned thermopower characterization method. The pressure sensitivity (S) at varying temperatures was determined by measuring the capacitance under a constant applied pressure while introducing different temperature gradients. The ΔC/C0 obtained at identical temperature differences were then subjected to linear regression analysis to extract the S. For the application demonstration, voltage and capacitance were simultaneously recorded by Keithley multimeters, and surface temperature distribution was monitored with an infrared thermal imager (UTi260 E, UNI-T, China).

Quantification and statistical analysis

All graphs presented in the main text and supplemental information were generated from raw data using Origin 2025. Detailed deconvolution of the FT-IR spectra (3050–3250 cm−1) was performed using Peakfit v4.21 software to precisely determine the peak positions of the C-H stretching vibrations. The volumetric shrinkage was calculated based on the linear shrinkage in the thickness direction measured by a digital caliper. The pressure sensitivity (S) was extracted through linear regression analysis of the relative capacitance changes (ΔC/C0) recorded under varying pressures. The surface temperature distributions were visualized by an infrared thermal imager (UTi260 E) and the captured thermal images were processed using UTi Thermal software.
