Examining the profile of couples attending a violence prevention intervention: A dyadic latent profile analysis
Authors: Anvita Bhardwaj, Rashelle J. Musci, Alexandra Blackwell, Danielle Roth, Yvonne Agengo, Joe J. Gallo, Sarah M. Murray, Kathryn Falb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:Existing research points to how increasing male engagement in public health interventions without addressing gender inequities can perpetuate power imbalances in a relationship which may lead to increased stress and anxiety for women. The aim of this study was to identify profiles of couples who attended a violence prevention intervention based on their attitudes toward gender norms and power sharing within a couple, determine if these profiles have different women’s mental health outcome and examine whether male engagement moderates this relationship.Methods:Using data from a cluster randomized controlled trial conducted in North Kivu, Democratic Republic of Congo, we conducted a latent profile analysis to categorize different profiles amongst couples in the intervention (n=202) and conduct logistic regression to examine the study aims.Results:The latent profile analysis identified three profiles of couples: (1) discordant dyads, (2) concordant high gender equity dyads, and (3) concordant average dyads. There were no differences in women’s depression symptoms at the end of the intervention amongst the three profiles. Male engagement, measured through attendance data, did not moderate the relationship between the couple’s relationship profile and women’s mental health.Conclusion:Small sample size, measurement sensitivity, and potential response bias to the scales assessing gender norms, power dynamics and mental well-being might have led to the null results we see. Yet, future studies should further explore the potential for differences in mental health outcomes and the impact of interventions on these outcomes based on intercouple dynamics in understanding and expression of power and gender norms.

INTRODUCTION

The International Conference on Population and Development’s 1994 Cairo Action Document emphasized the role and responsibilities that men hold in advancing women’s health (Lazarus, 1999; Nelson et al., 1996). In the 30 years since the adoption of this document, tremendous efforts have been made to engage men in family planning and reproductive health interventions with women, and subsequently, in prevention of men’s use of violence against women (Casey et al., 2013). Interventions that have engaged men in efforts to reduce or end gender-based violence range from broad universal prevention programs, such as Program H in South and Central America, to more targeted intervention programs like the Bandebereho couple’s intervention in Rwanda (Casey et al., 2013; Doyle et al., 2018; Ricardo et al., 2010). Overall, these interventions have stressed the critical role that addressing toxic masculinity, gender inequity and power dynamics play in gender-based violence (Casey et al., 2013; Doyle et al., 2018; Onyi et al., 2025; Ricardo et al., 2010).

The involvement of men in violence prevention programs is a positive step forward in the field of women’s health, as it can foster the dismantling of dominant harmful patriarchal cultural norms that impact women’s health and wellbeing (Derné, 1994). However, engaging men in violence prevention interventions has the potential for multifaceted impacts and unintended consequences that could include negative effects on women’s mental health (Fleming et al., 2014; Pease, 2008). Gender norms, particularity those associated with hegemonic masculinity, can perpetuate control and dominance within a relationship and lead to intimate partner violence (IPV) (Aloyce et al., 2023; Pichon et al., 2020). Extensive research has documented depression, anxiety, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and alcohol use disorder as common mental health outcomes associated with women’s experiences of intimate partner violence (Blasco-Ros et al., 2010; Hameed et al., 2020; Hossain et al., 2021). Thus, there is a clear potential pathway between gender inequity and unequal power relations between men and women in a relationship, the perpetuation of violence, and in turn, poor women’s mental health outcomes (McAllister et al., 2018).

Research has shown that increased involvement from male partners in women’s health services and programs can be deleterious to women if men’s harmful gender attitudes are not changed (Dudgeon & Inhorn, 2004; Mohammed et al., 2019). For example, men’s involvement can decrease women’s autonomy and thus negatively impacts women’s mental well-being has been identified as a key pathway (Comrie-Thomson et al., 2015; Mohammed et al., 2019). As such, interventions conducted in Zambia and Guatemala have shown that engaging men could inadvertently compromise women’s autonomy and decision-making authority in the household (Mweemba et al., 2021; Schuler et al., 2015). Additionally, a qualitative study in Ghana asked men about their perceptions on being involved in newborn care and some male participants voiced concern about how increasing male involvement would reinforce gender hierarchies, potentially causing them to resort to using violence in their relationship as they would feel disobeyed (Dumbaugh et al., 2014). In a recent systematic review of sexual and reproductive health interventions that engage men and boys, the results highlighted that engaging men in the intervention could negatively impact women’s autonomy the interventions negatively especially when health professionals and male partners were more educated than mothers (Ruane-McAteer et al., 2019). Furthermore, there is robust evidence showing that lack of autonomy and ability to make health care related decisions can negatively impact women’s mental well-being (Organization, 2000). Ultimately, engaging men in interventions without explicit attention to gender inequities can undermine women’s rights, autonomy and can perpetuate issues related to male privilege and power in relation to women (Ruane-McAteer et al., 2019).

Existing research also strongly points to how increasing male engagement in interventions without addressing gender inequities can perpetuate power imbalances in a relationship (Comrie-Thomson et al., 2015; Dudgeon & Inhorn, 2004; Dumbaugh et al., 2014). Power imbalances in a couple have contributed to increased stress, anxiety and depression for both men and women (Vyas & Jansen, 2018; Wang et al., 2008). Women report feeling helplessness, low self-esteem and emotional distress when they experience power imbalances in their relationship which could lead to poor mental health outcomes (Gracia-Leiva et al., 2023). Power imbalances within a couple have been linked to increased rates of IPV, which can lead to severe psychological consequences (Conroy, 2014; Gracia-Leiva et al., 2023). However, there remains an evidence gap in understanding whether participating in an intervention designed to strengthen families and address that power imbalances cause unintended negative consequences on women’s mental health among couples for which there was baseline levels of gender inequity and power imbalances.? In the violence prevention intervention space, engaging men in the intervention is extremely important. (Namy et al., 2017; Vaillant et al., 2020) One issue is that exposure to conflict violence can normalize violence in the household and thus if the intervention does not address the normalization of violence it can be unsuccessful.(Bourey et al., 2024) Interventions that include women without directly engaging men can lead to low involvement of men and at times even lead to retaliatory violence and perpetuation of negative norms around the women’s role in the household. (Campbell et al., 2010; Tarzia et al., 2020) It is safe to assume that within a violence prevention program couples have varying levels of gender equity attitudes and power sharing norms, yet no intervention tailors the intervention based on this heterogeneity despite the fact that these couples may experience the intervention differently and have different needs (Ogbe et al., 2020). The purpose of this study was to address this gap and understand the relationship dynamics amongst couples in a violence prevention intervention, how these profiles differ, and if these profile impact women’s mental health differently.

METHODS

Study Setting and Population

The present study used data collected as part of a pilot cluster randomized controlled trial (cRCT) trial of a family well-being and violence prevention intervention, Safe at Home, conducted by the International Rescue Committee (IRC) between November 2019 and September 2021 in four conflict-affected villages in North Kivu, Democratic Republic of Congo. Study methods and the CONSORT diagram demonstrating how the trial was conducted have been published in detail previously (K. L. Falb et al., 2023b). In brief, heterosexual couples were enrolled in the program with half of the couples randomized to the intervention and the other half to a waitlist control arm. Individuals were recruited from the communities of Kingarame, Kalangala, Bhuhumba and Munigi. Specifically, IRC facilitators conducted community outreach in collaboration with community leaders to share information about the program. Interested participants who came forward were then screened for eligibility based on the following criteria: 18 years of age or older, living with a partner in a monogamous relationship who was also willing to participate in the intervention; spoke Swahili, Kinyarwanda or French; and, had a child between ages 6 and 12.

The Safe at Home program is a community-based discussion group intervention consisting of a combination of single-sex sessions (i.e., separate sessions for men and women) held weekly along with family discussion groups (which included the couples’ eligible child) held monthly. In total, there were 24 sessions for men, 18 for women, and 5 family sessions held over 6 to 8 months (K. Falb et al., 2023). The overall goal of the Safe at Home program was to improve family functioning and reduce violence towards women and children in the household. More information about the program can be found in Falb et al., (2023) (K. L. Falb et al., 2023b). Findings from the pilot cluster-randomized controlled trial showed that the intervention was highly effective in preventing violence in the household and improving equitable attitudes amongst the couple (K. L. Falb et al., 2023b).

Ethics

The study was approved by the IRC Institutional Review Board (WPE #1.00.014) and the Comité National d’Ethique de la Santé-Direction Provincial du Sud-Kivu and registered on Clinical Trials (#NCT04163549). All participants provided oral consent in Kinyarwanda, French or Swahili.

Research Questions

The specific research questions we sought to answer were:

Research Question 1.

What are the presenting relationship dynamics amongst the couples who participated in a violence prevention intervention.

Research Question 2.

How do experiences of interpersonal violence in the relationship differ for couples who have different patterns of gender norms and power sharing dynamics?

Research Question 3.

How do the couples’ relationship dynamics profile impact women’s mental health measured at the end of the Safe at Home Intervention, and does male engagement in the intervention moderate this relationship?

Measures

All measures were conducted in either Kinyarwanda, French or Swahili and were read out loud to the participant by the research team who then used a tablet to record their answers.

Women’s Mental Health

The primary outcome of interest of the present analysis was women’s mental health, which was measured among women at endline using the Patient Health Questionnaire 4 (PHQ-4), a validated brief scale for measuring psychological distress (Kerper et al., 2014). The PHQ-4 asks over the last two weeks, how often have you been bothered by the following feelings: feeling nervous, anxious or on edge; not being able to stop or control worrying; feeling down, depressed or hopeless; or feeling little interest or pleasure in doing things. We calculated a continuous score by summing the responses to the four questions. The range of the possible PHQ-4 scores were 1 to 12 (Kroenke et al., 2009). Modeling the PHQ-4 score as a continuous variable is consistent with its prior used in DRC with refugee populations (Kasujja et al., 2022) (Visscher et al., 2018). The Cronbach’s alpha for internal consistency was 0.8 for men and 0.7 for women.

Gender Norms

Attitudes towards gender norms, specifically beliefs in the promotion of equity for women, in a couple were measured at baseline and endline among both women and men using the Gender Equitable Men (GEM) Scale(Pulerwitz & Barker, 2008). The GEM scale’s 24 items have been found to comprise 7 domains in prior study (Pulerwitz & Barker, 2008). For this study, only the daily tasks and chores and domestic violence domains were used because they were determined to be the most relevant to the intervention’s targeted outcome of family functioning The scale was given to both men and women. Possible response categories were 0=Do not Agree, 1=Partially Agree, and, 2=Agree. Continuous total scores were calculated, with higher scores representing more gender-inequitable attitudes (K. Falb et al., 2023; K. L. Falb et al., 2023b). The Cronbach’s alpha for internal consistency was 0.80 for women and 0.89 for men.

Power Sharing

Perception of power sharing within a couple were measured at baseline and endline among both women and men. The Power Sharing in a Couple Relationships Scale (PSCRS) is a 30 item Likert scale that assesses power sharing within a couple, meaning the distribution of power experiences by individuals in a couple including items such as if both people have a say about decisions, if they share responsibilities such as household chores, and whether they listen to each other’s opinions (Arici Sahin & Keklik, 2019). There were five domains in this scale: friendship between partners, power over, reactivity to stress, openness to influence form a partner, and making a relational claim. Answer choices were on a 5-point Likert scale and ranged from completely applies=5 to does not apply at all=1 Answer choices were summed where high scores on this continuous scale reflected more balanced power sharing within the couple (total possible score range 29–145). The Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency for the 5 domains for women was: 0.66 for friendship between partners, 0.79 for power over, 0.63 for reactivity to stress, 0.35 for openness to influence form a partner, 0.31 and for making a relational claim. The Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency for the 5 domains for men was: 0.58 for friendship between partners, 0.78 for power over, 0.68 for reactivity to stress, 0.64 for openness to influence form a partner, and 0.33 for making a relational claim.

Male Engagement

Male engagement was measured using attendance data for men, specifically programmatic data documenting how many individual and family sessions a man attended. Facilitators of the intervention captured attendance data through taking weekly program records by hand and then inputted the data into excel documents at the end of the intervention. The facilitator would ask for a man’s name and then mark him as present. Anyone not marked as present was absent. There was no measure of active participation from the men in the study such as documenting how often he asked a question or if he was paying attention to the session. Men could have attended a total of 29 sessions, including up to 24 individual and 5 family sessions. Male engagement data was linked to outcomes data through identification codes.

Interpersonal Violence

Women’s experience of IPV was measured at baseline and endline using a series binary (yes/no) questions that asked if the woman had experienced 14 different acts of physical, sexual or emotional violence following by if they has experienced that act within the past 3 months (Falb et al., 2024). A composite variable was then calculated for each type of violence to indicate if the woman had experienced any act of physical, sexual, or emotional violence within the last three months and was used in this analysis. The IPV module from the World Health Organization multi-country study on women’s health and domestic violence against women was used to design these questions (García-Moreno et al., 2005). These questions were only asked to women in the dyad given ethical considerations.

Data Analysis

Exploratory analysis looking at demographic characteristics of the sample and the internal consistency of scales was conducted in Stata 15.1 IC (StataCorp, 2018). A latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted using Mplus 8 to determine patterns in the couples’ relationship dynamics pertaining to power sharing and gender norms using the 3-step approaches outlined by Asparouhov and Muthen and maximum likelihood estimation (2014) (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014). Gender norms and power sharing scores were standardized through grand mean centering in MPlus. Standardized scores for the five domains in the PSCRS and the total score from the GEMs scale were measured at baseline and used to build the profiles. In the first step, the Akaike Information Criteria (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), sample size adjusted BIC (aBIC), Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT), and entropy were used to determine the number of profiles to model. Lowest BIC value was examined and then assessed diminishing returns on that, subsequently the AIC was examined to determine which number of profiles had the lowest score. For entropy the highest value was examined and subsequently examined the LMR-LRT. The LMR-LRT compares the model with n profiles compared to the model with n-1 profiles and determines if the model with n profiles is a significantly better fit.

In the second step, couples were assigned to membership in a predicted latent profile using the conditional probabilities such that they were assigned to the class with the largest probability. Missing data was imputed using multiple imputation in Mplus (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2010). Male engagement was missing for 49.46% of male participants of which missing data was imputed. There were no differences in demographic characteristics including age, education, and number of people in the household between the male participants who were missing the engagement variable in comparison to those who were not. No other variables had missing data. For the multiple imputation, education, age and number of family members in the household were used to predict men’s attendance. 20 new data sets were made with these values and used for subsequent steps. In the third step, the manual BCH Method was used to get a Wald test to compare differences in means on the outcome of interest (endline PHQ-4 scores for women) by the latent profiles, and if male engagement moderates the relationship between latent profile and mental health (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014). The BCH method allowed us to accurately estimate the relationship between the latent profiles and our outcome of depression while accounting for the uncertainty of profile membership. Lastly, in step four, women’s experience of IPV was modeled on the predicted latent profile membership from step one using logistic regression.

RESULTS

A total of 202 couples were enrolled in the program, 186 (92%) of these couples (n=372) completed the intervention and along with the questionnaires administered at endline and were included in this study. As reported in Table 1, women’s mean age equal to 32.0 (SD = 8.56) and men’s mean age equal to 35.6 (SD = 8.63). Most of both men (45.16%) and women (43.01%) had at least a primary school level of education, though a greater proportion of women had completed less than primary school or had no formal education as compared to men (42.5% vs 12.4%).

Research Question 1 - Relationship dynamics amongst couples participating in Safe at Home

A three-profile model was selected as the best fitting model based on the fit statistics presented in Table 2, and primarily, the non-significant p-value of the LMR-LRT test indicating that the three-profile model better fit the data than a four-profile model. The three-profile model was named using the trends and patterns seen in the standardized conditional probabilities of the gender norms and power sharing scale responses from both individuals in the dyad: discordant dyads, concordant high gender equity dyads, and concordant average dyads (Figure 1)

Profile 1 (18.8%) was termed ‘discordant couples’, because they had a higher probability of holding disparate beliefs and opinions. The men’s scores reflect less balanced power sharing, while in this group women generally did not express any extreme (positive or negative) attitudes towards gender norms or power sharing. In contrast, men had a higher probability of holding extremely low power sharing scores across all domains, meaning that men in the couple feel as if there is a power imbalance in their relationship. Specifically, men expressed extremely unbalanced power sharing for the friendship and openness to influence domains. Men in this profile had a higher probability of expressing high support for gender inequitable norms.

In Profile 2 (26.9%), both men and women in the dyad had a higher probability of expressing a high degree of support for gender equitable views, meaning in the daily tasks and domestic violence domains both men and women in the dyad believed that responsibilities should be shared equally for daily tasks and domestic violence should not occur. Yet, both men and women had a higher probability of expressing unbalanced power sharing views in the power over and reactivity to stress domains. This means that both men and women in the couple had a high likelihood of expressing a feeling that there is a power imbalance in their relationship on those domains. This profile was thus termed as the ‘concordant high-gender equity’ couples’ group.

Profile 3 was the most common profile with a prevalence of 54.3%. Both men and women had generally the same trends in the power sharing domains in the dyad and were about average in their probability of relational claim, friendship and openness to influence for most of the questions. This means that couples have a higher probability of holding medium support for gender equity and that there might be some domains where there are power imbalances but not amongst most. The trends for power sharing were the same where both men and women had reported more balanced power sharing for friendship and openness to influence and unbalanced power sharing views for power over and reactivity to stress. This profile was thus termed the ‘concordant average gender equity attitudes’ couples’ group.

Research Question 2 – Investigating how experiences of IPV differences amongst profiles

Logistic regression analysis was conducted to look at the relationship between latent profile membership and women’s report of experiences of IPV (Table 3). Women who reported no experience of sexual IPV within the last 3 months (n=296 80%) at baseline were 89% (p =0.031) less likely to be a part of the concordant dyads high gender equity group (profile 2) compared to the concordant dyad’s average attitudes group (profile 3). No significant associations were identified between profile membership and experiences of physical and emotional IPV.

Research Question 3 – Do profiles have differing depression outcomes

The Wald’s test of parameter constraints with continuous PHQ-4 scores as a distal outcome was not significant (χ2 = 0.195, df=2, p-value=0.907), meaning the relationship between latent profile of dyads and depression was not statistically significant. When male engagement was added in as a moderator of the Wald’s test of parameter constraints, we found no significant moderating effects of male engagement on the relationship between dyad profile and women’s mental health (χ2 = 0.017, df=2, p-value=0.992).

DISCUSSION

Overall, the intent-to-treat analyses of the Safe at Home intervention was associated with significant reductions in family violence, including women’s experience of IPV, alongside improvements in power sharing, and gender equitable attitudes (K. L. Falb et al., 2023a). Additionally, participation in Safe at Home was associated with significant improvements in mental health for women, but not so for men (Balachander et al., 2024).

The present analysis builds upon this evidence by characterizing distinct profiles of couples based on their gender attitudes and power-sharing dynamics and examining whether these profiles were associated with differential mental health outcomes for women. Analyses revealed three different patterns of couples’ relationships regarding gender attitudes and power imbalances:1) men held inequitable gender attitudes and unequal power sharing views and women held neutral gender attitudes and power sharing views, 2) both men and women held equitable gender attitudes 3) both men and women held similar average viewpoints about gender norms and power sharing. Approximately half of the couples in the sample held average, concordant views on gender attitudes and power sharing while one-quarter held above average views towards gender equitable attitudes and power sharing within the couple. Unsurprisingly, women who reported sexual IPV in the past three months prior to baseline were less likely to be within a couple that held higher levels of equitable attitudes. Other forms of IPV were not associated with couples’ attitude and power profiles. There were no significant differences across the latent profiles regarding women’s depressive symptoms at the end of the intervention, and male engagement did not moderate this relationship either. The practical relevance of these findings is discussed in more detail below, but they are important to report so that future studies can delve deeper into these pathways and look more into if gender-transformative programing itself has a mechanism of action that might protect women from mental distress as that might have been seen in this study. The lack of significant results could have also been due to measurement sensitivity and the fact that the PHQ-4 scale is not validated for refugee populations in the DRC, sociocultural norms, conflict in the DRC, and small sample size. Previous studies have highlighted that the PHQ-4 scale performs poorly as a diagnostic tool in the DRC given cultural differences in how depressive symptoms are expressed specifically the fact that people living in the DRC often express somatic complaints when experiencing depression and also believe that mental illness is caused by a curse or witchcraft(Mutombo et al., 2024).

Few studies have examined how differences in couples’ gender attitudes and power sharing at the dyadic level may influence outcomes for family-based or couples-based interventions to prevent violence. Addressing this gap in knowledge is important as adverse health outcomes have been documented in women’s health intervention research for women in couples who held power imbalances and inequitable gender norms (Giusto & Puffer, 2018) when these power imbalances are not part of programming efforts. Alternatively, studies have shown that mental health outcomes are better for both partners in the Counseling Husbands to Achieve Reproductive Health and Marital Equity (CHARM), Banderbereho, and Mawe Tatu interventions when that intervention includes a component that explicitly addresses inequitable gender norms and attitudes (Pederson et al., 2014; Raghavan et al., 2022). These findings are consistent with Safe at Home’s overall impact on women’s mental health outcomes, particularly for the near quarter of the study population that held discordant gender attitude and power sharing views. It is also important to think about the cultural context of the DRC, specifically North Kivu. Given the conflict in North Kivu, there are high rates of gender-based violence (Altare et al., 2020). Recent studies have shown that women lack decision making power in the household for small decisions such as what food to buy, and that there are high rights of inequitable gender attitudes, which in other settings has been shown to be associated with depression (Hammarström & Phillips, 2012) (Stark et al., 2017) (Bapolisi et al., 2021).

Contrary to our hypothesis, we did not observe a significant association between women’s depressive symptoms and the identified couple profiles or level of male engagement in the intervention. This suggests that the gender-transformative content of Safe at Home, which explicitly addresses inequitable gender attitudes and power dynamics within the household, could potentially mitigate the impact of baseline imbalances of gender inequitable attitudes on women’s mental health. Our non-significant finding is therefore relevant to the gender-transformative intervention field as it points to the need of exploring unintended benefits of gender-transformative programming which might include reducing mental health symptom severity among women.

Nonetheless, the lack of association observed in our findings may also be due, in part, to how male engagement was measured in our study. Like many others, we relied on men’s attendance data from the intervention’s administrative data. However, this data had a high degree of missingness, which may have compromised its validity. Although we attempted to address this through imputation, attendance alone does not reflect the extent to which men understood, actively participated in, or meaningfully engaged with the intervention. This is a common limitation in studies that aim to engage men to improve women’s health outcomes. Future research should explore more comprehensive yet practical ways to assess male engagement. An example of such a measure is the Male Involvement Index (MII), which has been used to measure men’s attendance, financial support and communication with their partner about the intervention which was focused on the Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission of HIV (PMTCT) in South Africa that could be adapted to measure male involvement in any type of intervention not just PMTCT (Peltzer & Shikwane, 2011; Rodriguez et al., 2020). A benefit of this scale is that it contains questions that pertain to a more passive form of male involvement such as communication and is administered to women as well so that they can give their input on how their male partner was involved and allows the researchers to compare both men and women’s responses (Rodriguez et al., 2020). Practitioners and researchers may benefit from including this or similar scales to better assess male engagement to monitor for unintended consequences or to better target their programming efforts.

Interpretation of results should consider limitations of this study. Primarily, male engagement was measured through attendance data which had a high level of missingness. Second, the small sample size may have been inadequate for latent profile analysis, which could lead to not accurately identifying the correct number of latent profiles in the Safe at Home Intervention (Spurk et al., 2020). However, our models were able to converge but we were limited in our ability to ensure this as we could not bootstrap or use the maximum likelihood estimator. The main drawback of using the BCH method that we used is that it assigned class membership at one point in time. Improving the participants’ mental wellbeing was not an intended outcome of the Safe at Home intervention; thus, depression was not measured at baseline. We were unable to a change of depression scores as the outcome which is important because we do not know if women were already exhibited depressive symptoms prior to entering the Safe at Home intervention of if depressive symptoms developed during the intervention. We also used the PHQ-4 to measure depression which has been used in similar contexts but is not validated for the DRC and has an array of issues. The PHQ-4 only asks about depression and anxiety symptoms thus does not get at the broader symptoms of mental health that need to be considered. Given that the PHQ-4 is not validated for this context, the 4 questions it asks might not be culturally appropriate or relevant to properly ask about depression and anxiety.

CONCLUSION

This study makes an important contribution to the women’s mental health intervention literature by identifying subgroups of couples who vary in their endorsement gender attitudes and power sharing. Future studies should further explore the potential for differences in mental health outcomes and the impact of interventions on these outcomes based on intercouple dynamics in understanding and expression of power and gender norms. Future research could also better assess male engagement beyond attendance to improve the robustness of these insights, along with looking at potential pathways by which gender-transformative programming might be benefiting women’s mental well-being. Ultimately, this type of research can advance science and practice by building better tailored interventions to the needs of different types of couples that are enrolled in the intervention.
