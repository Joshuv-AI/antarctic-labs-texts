Optimizing nitrogen fertilizer for improved root growth, nitrogen utilization, and yield of cotton under mulched drip irrigation in southern Xinjiang, China
Authors: Yu Luo, Hao Yin, Yue Ma, Juanhong Wang, Qingxuan Che, Man Zhang, Bolang Chen, Gu Feng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The root system plays a crucial role in water and nutrient absorption, making it a significant factor affected by nitrogen (N) availability in the soil. However, the intricate dynamics and distribution patterns of cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) root density and N nutrient under varying N supplies in Southern Xinjiang, China, have not been thoroughly understood. A two-year experiment (2021 and 2022) was conducted to determine the effects of five N rates (0, 150, 225, 300, and 450 kg N ha−1) on the root system, shoot growth, N uptake and distribution, and cotton yield. Compared to the N0 treatment (0 kg N ha−1), the application of N fertilizer at a rate of 300 kg N ha−1 resulted in consistent and higher seed cotton yields of 5875 kg ha−1 and 6815 kg ha−1 in 2021 and 2022, respectively. This N fertilization also led to a significant improvement in dry matter weight and N uptake by 32.4% and 53.7%, respectively. Furthermore, applying N fertilizer at a rate of 225 kg N ha−1 significantly increased root length density (RLD), root surface density (RSD), and root volume density (RVD) by 49.6–113.3%, 29.1–95.1%, and 42.2–64.4%, respectively, compared to the treatment without N fertilization (0 kg N ha−1). Notably, the roots in the 0–20 cm soil layers exhibited a stronger response to N fertilization compared to the roots distributed in the 20–40 cm soil layers. The root morphology parameters (RLD, RSD, and RVD) at specific soil depths (0–10 cm in the seedling stage, 10–25 cm in the bud stage, and 20–40 cm in the peak boll stage) were significantly associated with N uptake and seed cotton yield. Optimizing nitrogen fertilizer supply within the range of 225–300 kg N ha−1 can enhance root foraging, thereby promoting the interaction between roots and shoots and ultimately improving cotton production in arid areas.

Introduction

The demand for agricultural production has experienced a significant surge due to the global population increase and improved living standards1–3. Fertilizers play a vital role in ensuring agricultural productivity, particularly through appropriate fertilization practices, which enhance crop productivity, optimize resource utilization, protect soil and environmental health, and contribute to the goal of peak carbon emissions and carbon neutrality4–9. Therefore, improving fertilizer management practices becomes crucial in achieving higher yields, enhancing fertilizer use efficiency, and improving environmental conditions.

Nitrogen (N) fertilizer is the most widely used chemical fertilizer globally and plays a pivotal role in crop production10,11. Insufficient N fertilizer supply inhibits root development, reduces shoot growth, induces premature senescence, and ultimately diminishes crop yields12–14. Conversely, excessive N fertilizer application contributes to problems such as soil acidification, degradation, and compaction. It promotes excessive vegetative growth, reduces crop yield and N use efficiency, and increases N losses and environmental pollution15–20. Therefore, implementing scientifically and reasonably sound N fertilizer management methods is crucial for improving crop productivity and N use efficiency21–23. Additionally, a comprehensive understanding of crop nutrient uptake mechanisms is essential for optimizing nutrient management and further advancing sustainable agriculture24.

The root system plays a vital role in water uptake, nutrient absorption and recycling, plant–microbe interactions, and hormone synthesis and transport in plants25–28. As the “receptor” of environmental changes perceived by crops, root growth significantly regulates shoot growth and influences yield formation29–32. Crops have developed various strategies to respond to N supply through morphological and physiological processes33,34, including altering the length, number, and density of total roots, fine roots, and root hairs, root–shoot ratio, nitrogen distribution, and enzyme activity35–37. For example, low N levels enhance root elongation, stimulate nitrate reductase activity, and inhibit shoot growth3,38,39, while excessive N supply reduces root biomass and lateral root density, as well as inhibits the growth of deep roots and enzyme activity. Optimal N levels may increase the distribution of roots in nutrient-rich patches40, promote nutrient absorption41, and enhance crop photosynthetic capacity42. Therefore, optimizing N management to regulate root morphological and physiological processes could improve nitrogen use efficiency and crop yield43,44.

Cotton is a globally significant oil and fiber crop45, with China being one of the top three cotton-producing countries worldwide46. Xinjiang, located in the arid region of Northwest China, is the largest cotton production region in the country, accounting for 83% of the national cotton sown area and over 90% of the national annual production47. With high radiation and advanced mulch drip irrigation technology, cotton lint yields in the region (1.5–2.5 t ha−1) are 2–3 times higher than the global average48. Nitrogen fertilizer is the primary limiting nutrient in cotton production and is suitable for drip irrigation under mulch due to its water-soluble nature. Nitrogen fertilizer application significantly affects cotton growth and development, nutrient accumulation, photosynthetic physiology, dry matter distribution, and yield components under mulched drip irrigation49–51. Zhu et al.11 reported that low N supply restricts cotton root and shoot growth but prolongs lateral root lifespan. Many researchers have investigated the effects of N application rates on cotton growth, yield, and nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) under mulched drip irrigation. It has been found that cotton shoot growth and yield increase with increasing nitrogen rates, but NUE and root system development decrease significantly under high-N supply conditions52. Moreover, the optimal N application rate for cotton varies greatly depending on planting patterns, planting densities, geographical regions, and management practices, such as 200–320 kg N ha−1 in Northern Xinjiang8,53, 300–380 kg N ha−1 in Southern Xinjiang54,55, 240 kg N ha−1 in the Yellow River Basin of China56, and 200 kg N ha−1 in Australia57. Therefore, further studies are needed to determine the optimum N rate based on cotton’s biological characteristics (root system and shoot biomass) and yield.

Despite the widespread use of nitrogen (N) fertilization for cotton cultivation in Southern Xinjiang, the specific effects of N rates on the cotton root system, dry matter accumulation and distribution, N uptake and distribution, and seed cotton yield in this region are still poorly understood. Particularly, the relationship between root dynamics and N uptake remains unclear. Therefore, the objectives of the present study were to investigate the impact of N fertilizer on cotton growth, root system development, N utilization (including N accumulation and distribution, as well as residual soil NO3−-N), and yield. Additionally, we aimed to evaluate the relationship between root dynamics and nitrogen uptake, and to determine the soil depths at which roots were most responsive to N content and effective in promoting N uptake and seed cotton yield.

Materials and methods

Field experiments were conducted during the cotton growing seasons in 2021 and 2022 at the Xinjiang Shaya Cotton Science and Technology Institute of the Ministry of Education, in the cotton field of Hailou Village, Hailou Town, Shaya County, southern Xinjiang (41°28′N, 82°71′E),The soil at this location is classified as brown desert soil according to the Chinese soil classification system. Prior to planting in 2021, soil chemical properties were measured at nine randomly selected points within the test area on two layers of the top 40 cm soil profile (0–20 and 20–40 cm) (Table 1). The research area is located in a temperate continental arid and semi-arid region. Total precipitation during the cotton growing season was 64 mm in 2021 and 36 mm in 2022, primarily occurring in the summer and early fall. The average monthly temperature was 11.0 °C in 2021 and 11.9 °C in 2022. Cotton is typically planted in mid-April and harvested at the end of October. Experimental design

The field experiment followed a randomized complete block design, with the same soil conditions, and nitrogen (N) fertilization applied at five different levels. The control treatment (N0) received no nitrogen fertilizer (0 kg N ha−1), while the other treatments included N150 (150 kg N ha−1, representing a small amount of N fertilization), N225 (225 kg N ha−1, representing reduced N fertilization), N300 (300 kg N ha−1, representing normal N fertilization), and N450 (450 kg N ha−1, representing excessive N fertilization). Each treatment was replicated four times per year. Urea (46% N) was used as the nitrogen fertilizer. The specific amounts of N applied for each treatment and growing season are presented in Table 2. Phosphorus (P) fertilization involved the application of 150 kg P2O5 ha–1 as triple superphosphate with 46% P2O5, while potassium (K) fertilization involved the application of 75 kg K2O ha–1 as potassium sulfate with 50% K2O, both applied prior to sowing through broadcast application. The total irrigation amount ranged from 3500 to 4000 m3 ha–1. Irrigation commenced on June 15 (bud stage) and was subsequently applied as drip irrigation once per week until the end of harvest, resulting in a total of 10 irrigation events. Each test plot measured 9.7 m in length and 6.9 m in width, with an area of approximately 66.9 m2. Three 2.05-m-wide sheets of plastic film mulch covered each plot. Each sheet contained six rows of cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L. cv. Yuan Cotton No. 11) and three rows of drip irrigation belts. There was a spacing of 10 cm between rows 1 and 2, between rows 3 and 4, and between rows 5 and 6. Additionally, there was a spacing of 66 cm between rows 2 and 3, and rows 4 and 5. Drip irrigation lines were installed between rows 1 and 2, between rows 3 and 4, and between rows 5 and 6 in each sheet47. In 2021, cotton was sown on April 15 and harvested on October 3, while in 2022, it was sown on April 16 and harvested on October 1. The planting density was 190,000 plants ha–1 after emergence of the seedlings. Sampling methods and measurements

Plant sampling

At three key growth stages, namely the seedling stage (40 days after planting (DAP)), budding and flowering stage (75 DAP), and peak boll stage (120 DAP), plant samples were collected from the center of the designated sampling area within each experimental plot. Fifteen plants were randomly sampled from each plot. In the laboratory, the samples were separated into roots, leaves, stems, and reproductive organs (buds, flowers, and bolls). The fresh plant organs were initially dried at 105 °C for 30 min and subsequently dried at 70 °C for 72 h in a ventilated oven until a constant weight was achieved. The dried samples were then ground to pass through a 1-mm sieve to obtain dry ash. The particulates were digested using a mixture of H2SO4-H2O2, and the resulting digest was used to determine the nitrogen (N) concentration using the Kjeldahl method58. The reproductive dry weight (DW) comprised the DW of buds, flowers, and bolls. The nitrogen accumulation in the plant organs was determined by multiplying the organ biomass by the corresponding N concentration.

Soil sampling

At the same three growth stages mentioned above, soil samples were taken from the center of the experimental plot beneath the drip irrigation belts. At each sampling point, soil samples were collected at depths of 0–20 cm, 20–40 cm, and 40–60 cm using a soil drill with a diameter of 5 cm. Five samples from the same depth within each plot were thoroughly mixed and transported to the laboratory for analysis of soil nitrate nitrogen and ammonium nitrogen concentrations. For analysis, 5.0 g of fresh sieved soil (< 2 mm) was extracted with 50 ml of a 2-mol L–1 CaCl2 solution. The soil extract solution was then analyzed using an AA3 continuous flow analyzer (SEAL Analytical Ltd., Germany) following the method described by Norman and Stucki59 to determine the concentrations of soil total nitrate nitrogen and ammonium nitrogen. Soil bulk density was determined using the ring knife method. The accumulation of soil NO3-N was calculated using the formula: A = c × h × BD × 0.1, where A represents the NO3-N accumulation (kg ha−1), c is the NO3-N content (mg kg−1), h is the depth of the soil layer (60 cm), BD is the soil bulk density (g cm−3), and 0.1 is a conversion factor. The residual nitrogen was calculated using the formula: Fertilizer nitrogen residue = An − A, where An and A represent the accumulation of NO3-N in the fertilized area after planting cotton (kg·ha−1) and the accumulation of NO3-N in the non-fertilized area (kg·ha−1), respectively. The fertilizer nitrogen apparent residue rate (%) was calculated as follows: (An − A)/c, where c is the total N application (kg·ha−1).

Root sampling

After the aboveground shoot harvest at the seedling stage, budding and flowering stage, and peak boll stage, the roots of the sampled plants were harvested using a monolith method with a root collector (60 cm long × 10 cm wide × 40 cm high). Soil cubes with volumes of 500 cm3, 500 cm3, 500 cm3, 500 cm3, 500 cm3, 500 cm3, and 1000 cm3 were separately obtained from soil layers of 0–5 cm, 5–10 cm, 10–15 cm, 15–20 cm, 20–25 cm, 25–30 cm, and 30–40 cm, respectively (Fig. S1). A total of 42 monoliths were collected for the two plants. Each soil cube was placed in a separate plastic bag labeled with spatial coordinates. All visible roots within each soil block were carefully extracted using forceps, stored in an icebox, and thoroughly cleaned before scanning. Root morphological parameters, such as total root length, total root surface area, and average root diameter, were quantified from digital images using Win-Rhizo Pro software (Regent Instruments Inc., Canada). The root systems were classified into fine roots (0–0.4 mm), middle roots (0.4–1.2 mm), and coarse roots (> 1.2 mm) based on their diameters60. The root samples were placed in a glass rectangular dish (200 mm × 150 mm) filled with water (depth: 4–5 mm) to untangle the roots and minimize overlap for scanning. If the roots within a single soil cube were too large to fit in the dish, they were separated into subsamples to accommodate the dish size.

The densities of root length, root surface area, and root volume were calculated as follows: root length density (RLD, mm/cm3) = RL/v; root surface area density (RSD, mm2/cm3) = RS/v; and root volume density (RVD, mm3/cm3) = RV/v, where v represents the volume of the soil cubes (cm3).

Cotton yield

For each sampled plot, the seed cotton was harvested from a 6.67 m2 area in the middle of the plastic film on October 3, 2021, and October 1, 2022, respectively. Randomly select five sampling points from five nitrogen fertilizer treatment areas, take the average weight of seed cotton at each point, record it, and calculate the cotton yield within a range of 10m2. Regional seed cotton yield per hectare = Cotton yield within a range of 10m2 *1000.

Statistical analysis

Data processing was performed using Microsoft Excel 2003 (Microsoft, Bothell, WA, USA). All descriptive statistical and comparative analyses were conducted using SPSS 16.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Tukey’s test was employed to determine the significance of differences among means, and a difference with p < 0.05 was considered significant. Origin 2022 software (OriginLab, Inc., USA) was used for plotting dry weight and N accumulation, cotton yield, correlation heat maps, and nitrate nitrogen and ammonium nitrogen concentrations. Surfer 15.0 software (Golden Software, Inc., USA) was utilized for mapping the spatial distribution of root parameters.

Results

Impact of N application rate on dry matter weight

The dry matter weight (DMW) of different cotton organs was significantly influenced by the N fertilizer treatments during the 2021 and 2022 growth seasons (Figs. 2, 3, and 4). At the seedling stage, the stems’ DMW showed significant increases of 25.0%, 42.1%, 44.8%, and 43.4% in 2021 and 33.0%, 35.2%, 63.6%, and 75.0% in 2022 for the N150, N225, N300, and N450 treatments, respectively, compared to the N0 treatment (CK). Likewise, compared to CK, the leaves’ DMW for the N150, N225, N300, and N450 treatments exhibited significant increases of 10.3%, 16.6%, 30.4%, and 15.2% in 2021 and 17.4%, 43.8%, 66.1%, and 78.5% in 2022, respectively. The root DMW of the N fertilizer treatments in 2022 was significantly higher than CK (with an average value of 61.6%). At the bud and peak boll stages, the maximum DMW in roots, stems, leaves, and reproductive organs was observed for the N300 treatment, followed by N225 and N450, during both experimental years (Figs. 3 and 4). The DMW of cotton organs and the total plant increased as the growth period progressed from seeding to bolling stages. The majority of the dry matter (DM) was allocated to the leaves (52.7% and 42.4%), followed by stems (34.9% and 39.6%), with the remaining DM distributed in roots (12.4% and 11.8%) and reproductive organs (6.2%) at the seedling and bud stages, respectively (Figs. 2 and 3). Impact of N application rate on N uptake and distribution

Nitrogen uptake corresponded to DM accumulation patterns at the seedling, bud, and peak boll stages. The application of nitrogen fertilizers significantly increased nitrogen accumulation in various cotton organs throughout the 2021 and 2022 growing seasons (Figs. 5, 6, and 7). At the seedling stage, nitrogen content in stems exhibited substantial increments across N150, N225, N300, and N450 treatments, with increases of 34.4%, 64.1%, 71.0%, and 56.4% in 2021 and 44.4%, 58.2%, 112.2%, and 146.3% in 2022, respectively, when compared to the control (CK) (Fig. 5). Similarly, compared to CK, nitrogen content in leaves showed significant increases across N150, N225, N300, and N450 treatments, with increments of 11.7%, 26.6%, 59.3%, and 25.1% in 2021 and 28.5%, 85.9%, 119.1%, and 104.7% in 2022 (Fig. 5). At the bud and peak boll stages, the maximum N content in roots, stems, leaves, and reproductive organs was observed for the N300 treatment, followed by N225 and N450, during both experimental years (Figs. 6 and 7). The N content of cotton organs and the total plant increased progressively from the seedling to the bolling stages. At seedling, bud, and peak boll stages, the total N accumulation for each N treatment ranged from 45.7 kg/ha−1 to 102.2 kg/ha−1, 104.7 kg/ha−1 to 173.1 kg/ha−1, and 173.7 kg/ha−1 to 427.8 kg/ha−1, respectively. Impact of nitrogen application rate on residual nitrogen

The soil NO3−-N, NH4+-N, residual N, and N apparent residual rates for 2021 and 2022 are depicted in Fig. 14. The average soil NO3−-N and NH4+-N content within the 0–60 cm soil depth was higher in 2021 compared to 2022. Soil nitrogen balance

Two years of consecutive experiments indicate that, aside from the N225, N300, and N450 treatments, the nitrogen expenditure for both the control (CK) and N150 treatments exceeded their income, resulting in a deficit (Table S2). The nitrogen profit and loss for the N300 treatment in 2019 and 2020 were 25.59 kg·ha−1 and 2.23 kg·ha−1, respectively. Thus, it is evident that the N300 treatment caused a minor depletion in soil nitrogen reserves. Compared to traditional fertilization, it enhanced the efficiency of fertilizer use and reduced the rate of soil nitrogen supply, thereby unlikely to significantly affect long-term yield sustainability or the soil’s capacity for nitrogen supply.

Impact of N application rate on seed cotton yield

The N application rate significantly influenced seed cotton yield in both 2021 and 2022 (Fig. 15). Initially, seed cotton yield exhibited an increasing trend with the N application rate, but subsequently declined upon surpassing a certain threshold. Among the N treatments, the N300 treatment demonstrated the maximum seed cotton yield, augmenting the yield by 76.3%, 44.8%, 21.2%, and 42.3% in 2021 and by 69.4%, 41.4%, 25.7%, and 30.6% in 2022, respectively, compared to the CK, N150, N225, and N450 treatments. Discussion

Dry matter accumulation and distribution

Dry matter accumulation serves as the foundation for achieving stable and high cotton yields and is highly influenced by nitrogen status61. This process involves the accumulation and distribution of photosynthetic products, regulated by various factors. For cotton under drip irrigation, key regulatory factors include plant density21, sowing date21, variety62, and fertilization management63. Nitrogen emerges as the primary limiting nutrient for cotton dry matter accumulation64.

In our study, we observed a modal response in the root biomass, stem biomass, leaf biomass, reproductive organs, and total plant biomass of cotton, peaking at an N rate of 300 kg N ha−1 (utilizing N levels of 0, 150, 225, 300, and 450 kg N ha−1). However, both limited (0 and 150 kg N ha−1) and excessive (450 kg N ha−1) N treatments restricted seedling photosynthesis physiological function. These findings suggest that an appropriate amount of N fertilizer promotes photo-assimilation and positively affects the accumulation of photo-assimilation in reproductive organs.

Previous studies have also highlighted the promotion of cotton growth and dry matter accumulation by optimal N levels, albeit with varying thresholds. Chen et al.56 observed a significant increase in total shoot biomass and boll biomass with moderate N treatment (240 kg ha−1) in the Yellow River Valley, China. Similarly, Wang et al.52 identified an N rate of 250 kg ha−1 as the optimal fertilization strategy for cotton biomass and production in arid regions of northwest China. Moreover, Wang et al.8 recommended N treatments of 250 kg ha−1 and 400 kg ha−1 as optimal strategies for fresh (1.5 dS m−1) and brackish (7.8 dS m−1) water irrigation, respectively, in arid regions of northwest China. In the Yangtze River basin, Liu et al.14 suggested applying 180 kg N ha−1 at early flowering for optimizing cotton biomass and yield in wheat-cotton double cropping systems. Additionally reported the benefits of an appropriate N application rate (300 kg ha−1) for dry-matter accumulation in cotton in the North China Plain. Zhang et al.65 highlighted the role of rational N fertilizer application (168.75 kg N ha−1) in maintaining higher lint cotton yield and biomass accumulation of intercropping cotton in the Yellow River Basin by enhancing soil C and N fractions. Furthermore, Pokhrel et al.64 recommended N fertilizer application rates of 134–179 kg N ha−1 to achieve a lint yield goal of 1681 kg ha−1 and a higher biomass of 19,241 kg dry matter ha−1 in southern Georgia, USA. These differences in optimal N levels across cotton-growing regions worldwide are primarily attributed to variations in cotton planting density, yield, environmental conditions, and field management practices.

Dry matter distribution among different tissues is a critical determinant of yield production, source-sink relationship adjustment, and comprehension of the relationships across various developmental stages in cotton14. Nitrogen rates profoundly influence economic yield and growth processes by impacting biological yield and allocation indices14,66. Optimal nitrogen fertilizer rates can enhance the distribution of dry matter to reproductive organs67 and improve the harvest index in later stages.

In our study, we observed that the proportion of sink organs (roots) initially increased and then decreased with the rise in N rate, while that of source organs (leaves) increased during the seedling stage. Furthermore, at the bud and peak boll stages, the maximum proportion of reproductive organs was recorded for N225 and N300 treatments. These findings suggest that optimal N application rates facilitate high cotton yields by manipulating dry matter accumulation and distribution.

Plant N uptake and distribution

Nitrogen uptake in crops is a fundamental physiological process involving reduction, transport, and metabolism to coordinate protein and carbon metabolism56. N uptake is significantly influenced by the N application rate and generally increases with higher N application rates68. In our study, the total N accumulation in cotton exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease with varying N rates. The maximum N content in roots, stems, leaves, and reproductive organs was observed for the N300 treatment during the cotton growth seasons over two years, aligning with findings from previous studies. For instance, Liu et al.14 reported optimal N rates for early maturing cotton in Northeast China, drip irrigation cotton in Northwest China, and late-planted cotton under a wheat-cotton cropping system in Central China, respectively, ranging from 240 kg N ha−1 to 180–360 kg N ha−1, based on the nitrogen nutrition index (NNI). Additionally, Bronson69 determined the optimal N rate for irrigated cotton in the southwestern United States to be within the range of 91–133 kg N ha−1, as per internal N use efficiency (iNUE) recommendations. Furthermore, Bai et al.54 observed an increase in N uptake in cotton under mulching and drip irrigation in Northwest China within a certain fertilizer application threshold of 0–300 kg N ha−1. These findings collectively indicate that both excessive and deficient N applications inhibit cotton growth, thereby influencing N accumulation in vegetative and reproductive organs.

The N content of the total cotton plant peaked at the boll stages, initially distributed in the leaves at early stages and then primarily allocated to reproductive organs at later stages. Similarly, Singh et al.70 observed N distribution primarily in vegetative (leaves and stems) organs of cotton at the seedling stage and predominantly in reproductive organs at the boll stage.

Root morphological parameters distribution in various soil layers

Root morphological parameters serve as crucial indices for crops in exploring water and nutrients66. Root length, surface area, and volume are appropriate metrics for assessing root system development71. The distribution density of root system parameters, such as root length density (RLD), fine root length density (FRLD), middle root length density (MRLD), root surface area density (RSD), and root volume density (RVD), within soil layers directly reflects soil moisture and nutrient status26. Moreover, N application significantly influences root development or distribution72.

In our study, we observed that the highest values of RLD, FRLD, MRLD, RSD, and RVD at the 0–40 cm soil depth were obtained in the N225 treatment, indicating that root development was hindered by both N deficiency and excess. N deficiency can diminish root activity and the capacity for water and nutrient uptake, ultimately impeding the transport of carbohydrates from shoots to roots23,56. Conversely, excess N can suppress the activity of endogenous hormones and nitrate reductase in roots, leading to decreased root development23,73.

Furthermore, we found that RLD, FRLD, MRLD, RSD, and RVD generally decreased with increasing soil depth but increased with the growth period, with the highest values observed in the 0–15 cm layer. This trend aligns with findings reported by Chen et al.56. The root length, surface area, and biomass in the 0–20 cm soil depth accounted for approximately 65% of the corresponding totals, consistent with studies by Min et al.67 and Chen et al.56. While Min et al.68 reported that 85–90% of total root length was distributed in the 0–20 cm layer, Chen et al.56 found that 50% of total root length was distributed in the same layer. This disparity may stem from variations in irrigation methods, soil texture types, and cropping patterns among the studies, as drip irrigation tends to distribute more water in shallow soil layers than flood irrigation, and high bulk density and plant density can limit root extension.

Residual nitrogen and cotton yield

Soil nitrate nitrogen and ammonium nitrogen serve as primary indicators for soil nitrogen diagnosis, typically increasing with nitrogen application rates and decreasing with soil depth under drip irrigation74,75. Increased nitrogen application leads to a significant rise in residual nitrogen in the soil, enhancing nitrogen supply capacity and the risk of loss47.

In our study, we observed a positive correlation between soil NO3−-N content at the 0–20 cm soil depth and N application rates, indicating an increase with higher N rates. This finding contrasts with the results of Wu et al.53, who reported a significant increase in soil nitrate nitrogen content in the topsoil with increasing nitrogen rates. This discrepancy may be attributed to the greater use of inorganic nitrogen fertilizer and the shedding of leaves and withered cotton76. However, soil NH4+-N content did not vary significantly among all treatments in the 0–60 cm soil layer, consistent with findings by Wang et al.8.

In our experiment, residual nitrogen amount increased with increasing nitrogen application, peaking in the N450 treatment (138.8 kg ha−1 in 2021 and 85.5 kg ha−1 in 2022), aligning with the findings of Wang et al.47, who demonstrated a gradual increase in residual N with higher N rates. The formation of residual N occurs due to more nitrogen dissolution in water and transport towards deeper soil layers or incomplete absorption by crops77. Additionally, we observed that the residual nitrogen apparent residual rate was nearly 30% of the total applied N, consistent with previous studies by Shareef et al.77, which recorded 32–64% of N-leaching from fields fertilized.

Proper N fertilizer application is crucial for improving cotton yield, as excessive or deficient N fertilization does not increase cotton yield3,52. In our study, we found that cotton yield initially increased and then decreased with increasing N application rates, with the highest seed cotton yield obtained in the N300 treatment (300 kg N ha−1). Similar conclusions were also reported by Wang et al.52 (250–300 kg N ha−1), Lu et al.78 (300–375 kg N ha−1), and Tian et al.3 (240 kg N ha−1).@@@

Conclusion

The study demonstrates the substantial impact of nitrogen application rates on soil nitrogen status, plant growth, nitrogen nutrient accumulation, and cotton productivity under drip irrigation with mulch. Moderate nitrogen levels (225 and 300 kg N ha−1) facilitate the distribution of NO3−-N in the topsoil, leading to increased root elongation, optimization of the root system, enhanced nitrogen uptake, aboveground biomass, and ultimately, improved seed cotton yield. However, both higher and lower nitrogen application rates hinder root development and plant growth, consequently diminishing seed cotton yield.

Furthermore, our findings highlight the significant association between nitrogen uptake, seed cotton yield, and root morphology parameters (RLD, RSD, and RVD) at specific soil depths: 0–10 cm in the seedling stage, 10–25 cm in the bud stage, and 20–40 cm in the peak boll stage. These insights provide valuable contributions to the development of fertilizer management strategies aimed at fostering more sustainable and efficient cotton production in arid agroecological regions.
