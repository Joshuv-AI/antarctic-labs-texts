Multiscale investigation of CNC-stabilized foams: From interfacial design to energy-relevant fluid displacement
Authors: Tianhan Xu, Wenhua Zhao, Gaowen Wang, Haonan Wang, Lihui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study investigates the mechanisms of enhanced oil recovery using a cellulose nanocrystal (CNC)-stabilized foam system. A systematic approach—encompassing foam characterization, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), interfacial tension and wettability measurements, microfluidic displacement visualization, and low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) analysis—was employed to evaluate the CNC-enhanced foam system. The results demonstrate that the optimized formulation improved foamability and stability, with foam volume and half-life increased by 15.7% and 35.2%, respectively. The incorporation of CNCs reduced the contact angle from 64.9° (conventional system) to 49.6°, indicating enhanced wettability alteration capability. Real-time microfluidic displacement experiments and LF-NMR analysis revealed that the CNC-enhanced nanofluid-assisted water alternating gas (NWAG) process increased the local sweep efficiency to 47.21% and improved micro-pore recovery by 28.5 percentage points. The NWAG process achieved an average ultimate oil recovery of 50.5%, demonstrating a 15.1 percentage-point improvement compared with conventional WAG injection.Subject areas: Engineering, Energy systems, Dynamical system

Introduction

Enhanced oil recovery (EOR) techniques, particularly WAG injection, have been widely employed to enhance hydrocarbon recovery from heterogeneous reservoirs.1,2 However, conventional surfactant-assisted WAG processes often suffer from low sweep efficiency and severe gas channeling in strongly heterogeneous conglomerate reservoirs, limiting their effectiveness.3 Surfactant-stabilized foams have been proposed to mitigate these issues by improving mobility control and reducing gas mobility.4,5 Despite these advantages, the instability of foam under reservoir conditions (e.g., high temperature, pressure, and crude oil presence) remains a critical challenge.6 Recent studies have explored nanoparticle-stabilized foams to enhance foam stability and interfacial properties, yet the synergistic mechanisms between nanoparticles and surfactants in WAG applications remain poorly understood.7,8

Cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) have emerged as promising stabilizers due to their biocompatibility, high surface area, and ability to modify interfacial properties. Previous research has demonstrated that CNCs can enhance foam stability by forming rigid interfacial layers and reducing coalescence.9 However, most studies have focused on aqueous systems, with limited investigation into oil-nanofluid interactions and their implications for EOR.10,11,12

Recent findings suggest that CNCs can significantly alter interfacial properties by reducing IFT and shifting reservoir rock surfaces toward more water-wet conditions. These effects are attributed to their amphiphilic surface chemistry and large specific surface area, which enable strong adsorption at both fluid-fluid and fluid-solid interfaces.13 Similar interfacial behavior has been reported for other nanomaterials, such as silica and metal oxide nanoparticles, which have also demonstrated the ability to modulate IFT and wettability in complex oil-brine-rock systems.14,15 These interfacial modifications are believed to contribute to improved oil mobilization and more uniform displacement fronts during injection processes.

While some studies have examined nanoparticle-surfactant synergies, the optimization of CNCs concentration for foam performance under reservoir conditions has not yet been systematically addressed.16 Advanced characterization techniques, such as microfluidic models and low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), have provided critical insights into fluid displacement mechanisms in porous media.17,18 Microfluidic studies have revealed foam behavior at the pore scale, while NMR allows for real-time monitoring of fluid distribution in different pore sizes.19,20 Despite these advancements, the combined application of these techniques to elucidate CNCs-enhanced foam dynamics in heterogeneous reservoirs remains underexplored.21

This study systematically investigates the synergistic effects of CNCs and surfactant mixtures (SDS + AES) on foam stability, interfacial properties, and EOR efficiency in conglomerate reservoirs. First, foam performance and interfacial characteristics were evaluated under varying oil-nanofluid conditions. A visualized microfluidic model using natural cores was employed to reveal the microscopic mechanisms of CNCs-surfactant synergy. Second, online low-field NMR (LF-NMR) core flooding experiments were conducted to compare the performance of waterflooding, WAG, and nanofluid-assisted water alternating gas (NWAG) in high- and low-permeability zones. The results demonstrate that CNCs significantly enhance foam stability and oil displacement efficiency, providing insights into the mechanisms of NWAG processes in heterogeneous reservoirs.

Results

Optimization of CNCs concentration in foam systems

Previous optimization studies identified a blended surfactant system containing 0.1 wt % AES and 0.1 wt % SDS as exhibiting optimal foam performance,22 demonstrating a stable foam volume of 700 mL, foam half-life of 105 min, and foam index (S) of 55,125 mL min. This study systematically evaluated the enhancement effects of CNCs at concentrations ranging from 0.05 to 0.15 wt % on this baseline foam system.

As shown in Figure 1, CNCs incorporation significantly improved both foamability and stability. The maximum foam volume of 848 mL was achieved at 0.1 wt % CNCs concentration, representing a 21.1% improvement over the baseline system. However, further increases in CNCs concentration led to reduced foamability, demonstrating clear concentration-dependent behavior. Foam stability tests revealed that even the lowest CNCs concentration of 0.05 wt % extended the half-life to 146 min, corresponding to a 39.0% enhancement compared to the control. Notably, higher CNCs concentrations resulted in progressively shorter half-lives, likely due to nanoparticle aggregation effects.

Through comprehensive analysis of both foamability and stability parameters, 0.075 wt % CNCs was determined to be the optimal concentration. This formulation demonstrated excellent integrated performance with a foam volume of 810 mL, representing a 15.7% increase over the baseline; a half-life of 142 min, showing 35.2% improvement; and a foam index (S) of 86,265 mL min, marking a 56.5% enhancement. Based on these results, the optimized formulation for subsequent microscopic characterization was established as 0.1 wt % SDS + 0.1 wt % AES + 0.075 wt % CNCs.

For comparison of nanoparticle-enhanced foam performance, we refer to the type III foam system23 reported in the literature (0.3 wt % MES + 0.1 wt % colloid SiO2 nanoparticles), which exhibited a foam volume of 410 mL, a half-life of 427 min, and a foam index (S) of 131,302 mL min. In contrast, the foam system investigated in this study (0.1 wt % SDS + 0.1 wt % AES + 0.075 wt % CNCs) showed a foam volume of 810 mL, a half-life of 142 min, and a foam index (S) of 86,265 mL min. Although the foam index (S) of our CNC-enhanced system is lower than that of the silica-NP system, it is noteworthy that both the surfactant and CNCs concentrations in our system are significantly lower. Despite the lower concentration, our system still maintains a high foam volume and good stability, demonstrating that CNCs can effectively enhance foam performance under low-concentration conditions, providing a basis for improved microscopic displacement in EOR applications.

Influence of oil content on foam performance of CNC-enhanced surfactant system

This section employs the optimal system selected in the previous section (0.1 wt % SDS + 0.1 wt % AES + 0.075 wt % CNCs) for experiments. The tests were conducted under simulated reservoir temperature (67°C) to evaluate the influence of oil content on the foaming performance and foam stability of the CNC-enhanced surfactant system.

With increasing oil content, both the foam volume and half-life of the foam system showed a declining trend. When the oil content was below 10%, the foam volume and half-life remained essentially unchanged. However, as the oil content further increased to 25%, the foam volume decreased from 810 to 569 mL, and the foam half-life was significantly shortened from 142 to 23 min (Figure 2).

Flow behavior evaluation of foam in porous

The experimental results indicate that both excessively low and excessively high gas-liquid ratios adversely affect foam quality. At low gas-liquid ratios, foam generation is slow and limited in quantity, resulting in low pressure buildup within the core and a correspondingly small resistance factor. When the gas-liquid ratio is too high, the generated foam exhibits poor quality, characterized by large and sparse bubbles with weak stability, leading to a decrease in the resistance factor within the core. Within the tested range, a gas-liquid ratio between 1:1 and 2:1 was found to be optimal. Foam generated in this range is fine-textured, stable, and exhibits high apparent viscosity. At a gas-liquid ratio of 1:1, the resistance factor reached a maximum value of 38.6, confirming that the foam system (0.1 wt % SDS + 0.1 wt % AES + 0.075 wt % CNCs) possesses excellent mobility-control capability, as shown in Figure 3.

Microstructural morphology of CNC-enhanced surfactant system

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization of the foam microstructure (Figure 4) revealed that CNCs incorporation significantly altered the foam morphology. Quantitative analysis through manual counting at four representative locations demonstrated a 22% increase in bubble density per unit area in the CNC-enhanced system compared to the baseline, accompanied by more uniform bubble size distribution. This highly homogeneous cellular structure effectively reduced internal mass transfer gradients, stabilizing the foam thermodynamically.

At the lamellar scale, selective CNCs adsorption at gas-liquid interfaces increased film thickness through three synergistic mechanisms: (1) physical barrier formation by CNCs’ rigid rod-like structure, (2) enhanced interfacial film elasticity via hydrogen bonding between CNCs surface hydroxyl groups and surfactant headgroups, and (3) markedly reduced drainage rate due to nanocellulose-induced capillary effects.

Figure 4 distinctly illustrates CNCs’ interfacial distribution pattern, showing nanofibers bridging adjacent surfactant molecules to form interconnected interfacial structure. This architecture provides three key stabilization advantages: (1) improved mechanical strength of liquid films, (2) suppressed bubble coalescence through steric hindrance, and (3) prolonged film stability via hydroxyl-mediated hydration layers. These microstructural features fundamentally explain the foam stabilization mechanism by CNCs.

Wettability alteration behavior of CNC-enhanced foam system

Contact angle measurements were employed to investigate the wettability alteration capability of CNC-enhanced foam systems on core surfaces (Figure 5). Experimental results demonstrated that crude oil-aged cores exhibited typical oil-wet characteristics, with the conventional AES/SDS foam system reducing the contact angle from initial oil-wet conditions to 64.9° after 300 min of treatment, achieving 42% wettability reversal. In contrast, the CNC-enhanced system showed superior wettability control, further decreasing the contact angle to 49.6° under identical conditions, corresponding to 52% reversal efficiency—a 10 percentage-point improvement over the conventional system.

This enhanced performance originates from CNCs’ amphiphilic nature and unique surface activity: (1) hydrophilic functional groups on CNCs preferentially interact with water molecules to form hydration layers on rock surfaces, and (2) CNCs incorporation increases surfactant adsorption density at the rock interface. These synergistic effects collectively promote the transition to water-wet conditions. Microscopic mechanistic studies reveal that CNCs enhance the wettability modification capacity of foam systems through molecular-level interface engineering and structural regulation.

Temperature effect on interfacial tension of CNC-enhanced foam systems

Interfacial tension measurements under varying temperatures (25°C–85°C; Figure 6) revealed that both the baseline AES/SDS system and CNC-enhanced system exhibited decreasing oil-water interfacial tension with increasing temperature, with the CNC-enhanced system demonstrating a more pronounced reduction rate. Notably, while CNCs incorporation only marginally reduced absolute interfacial tension values (0.29 mN/m decrease at reservoir temperature 65°C), the overall variation remained limited.

Combined with contact angle results, the interfacial modification mechanism of CNCs can be attributed to: (1) formation of stable hydration layers through hydrogen bonding between water molecules and hydrophilic functional groups on CNCs surfaces and (2) enhanced surface activity at oil-water interfaces due to CNCs’ nanoscale effects (diameter: 5–20 nm). As illustrated in Figure 7, these characteristics promote unique molecular alignment at interfaces. Although the absolute interfacial tension reduction is modest, CNCs improve the mechanical strength and thermal stability of interfacial films through structural modification rather than pure tension reduction—a critical factor for foam stabilization.23,24

Visualization of displacement mechanisms in natural core microfluidic models

Real-time pore-scale analysis (Figure 8) revealed distinct displacement patterns among different flooding methods. The magnification of the images in Figure 8 is ×10. The 2D visualization experiment was conducted using the setup shown in Figure 9, and the local pore-scale swept areas were calculated using the irregular polygon method (Figure 10). The initial saturated oil distribution is shown in Figure 8A. During waterflooding, injected fluid preferentially fingers through high-permeability channels from the lower-right inlet (Figure 8B), and subsequent water injection continues to isolate corner-trapped oil (Figure 8C), resulting in heterogeneous displacement with a local sweep efficiency of 24.84% and leaving substantial crude oil unproduced in dead-end pores. The WAG process demonstrated more complex fluid distribution: CO2 injection generates multidirectional flow paths (Figure 8D), and continued alternating gas-water injection further distributes fluid (Figure 8E), increasing sweep efficiency to 37.89%, though subsequent gas breakthrough reduces displacement efficiency at the microscale. Notably, the NWAG approach shows superior performance: the injected fluid forms an intricate four-directional flow network (Figure 8F), effectively mobilizes oil in challenging regions (Figure 8G), and further extends displacement to upper-left and lower-left zones (Figure 8H). Quantitative analysis confirms a local sweep efficiency of 47.21% for NWAG, representing a 9.32 percentage-point improvement over WAG (Table 1).

Based on the microscopic displacement mechanism, Figure 11 illustrates the dynamic mobilization of residual oil trapped in pore corners during the NWAG process. The magnification of the images in Figures 11 and 12 is ×30. In the initial stage, a single large bubble blocks the main pore channel (Figure 11A), and continued water-gas flow further isolates the corner-trapped oil (Figure 11B), consistent with the Jamin effect typically observed in conventional foam systems. As gas-water interactions proceed without CNCs, transient interfaces partially disturb larger pores (Figure 11C), and bubble splitting occurs along the main channels while the corner oil remains largely immobile (Figure 11D). In the final stage, the introduction of CNCs fundamentally changes the displacement behavior: the nanofluid penetrates narrow throats to mobilize adjacent oil (Figure 11E), and CNC-stabilized interfaces induce bubble deformation, splitting, and cross-throat displacement, disrupt residual oil films, and enable the complete mobilization of corner-trapped oil (Figure 11F). This sequence demonstrates that the synergistic mechanisms of CNCs, including selective adsorption at the oil-water interface, micro-scale roughness-induced film disruption, and enhanced interfacial elasticity, are crucial for efficiently mobilizing residual oil in confined pore corners.

Figure 12 illustrates the displacement evolution across all six subfigures. In Figure 12A, the injected water moves along the main flow paths, but its limited ability to enter confined regions leaves the corner-trapped oil unaffected. Figure 12B shows that as water flooding continues, flow diversion becomes more pronounced, isolating the corner regions further and causing the trapped oil to persist. When gas is introduced during the WAG process, Figure 12C indicates that transient gas-water interfaces reduce viscous resistance in larger pores, yet the gas slippage prevents effective contact with the corner oil. Figure 12D further demonstrates that although alternating phases induce additional interfacial disturbance in wider pore spaces, the absence of surface-active components means the capillary-bound corner oil remains largely immobile. A distinct displacement behavior appears in Figure 12E, where the CNC-containing nanofluid during NWAG penetrates the ultra-narrow throats connecting corner regions to adjacent pores, improving wetting and enabling fluid access to previously uninvaded areas. Finally, Figure 12F shows that CNC-stabilized interfaces promote bubble deformation, splitting, and localized interfacial activation in the neighboring pore, followed by bubble collapse and capillary-pressure redistribution, ultimately leading to complete mobilization of the corner-trapped residual oil.

Dynamic monitoring during the water flooding stage (0.9 PV) showed that the oil saturation of core 1 decreased from an initial 100%–68.1%. Changes in the T2 spectrum suggested that, due to the hydrophilic nature of the rock, water preferentially invaded the micropores, leading to a 46.2% reduction in their signal intensity, whereas the macropores were less affected, with only an 18.7% reduction in signal. During the subsequent WAG process, the displacement exhibited distinct stage characteristics. In the early stage (0–0.3 PV), oil content in the micropores decreased significantly, with signal intensity reduced by 32.4%, while changes in the macropores were minimal (<5%). In the mid-stage (0.3–0.6 PV), oil redistribution occurred, as evidenced by a 12.3% increase in macropore signal intensity.

The CNC-enhanced NWAG system exhibited unique displacement behavior. Compared with conventional WAG, the NWAG stage improved micropore recovery by 28.5%, reaching 61.3%. Mechanistic analysis indicates that CNCs enhance displacement efficiency through multipl@e mechanisms: (1) the adsorption of CNCs on pore surfaces alters wettability; (2) the network structure formed by CNCs effectively suppresses gas channeling; and (3) reductions in oil-water interfacial tension and optimized mobility ratio further contribute to enhanced displacement.

Discussion

The CNC-enhanced foam system, optimized at 0.075 wt % CNCs, demonstrated markedly improved performance, achieving a foam volume of 810 mL and a half-life of 142 min, corresponding to a 56.5% increase in the comprehensive foam index (S) compared to the conventional AES/SDS system. SEM observations revealed that CNCs adsorbed at the gas-liquid interface to form a robust three-dimensional network, enhancing film elasticity, inhibiting bubble coalescence, and improving both thermal and mechanical stability through increased bubble density and uniformity. Real-time microfluidic displacement experiments and LF-NMR analysis indicated that the NWAG process increased local sweep efficiency to 47.21% and significantly boosted micropore recovery by 28.5 percentage points. Experimental results showed that the CNC-enhanced NWAG process achieved an average ultimate oil recovery of 50.5%, demonstrating a 15.1 percentage-point improvement compared with conventional water alternating gas injection. These findings demonstrate that the synergistic action of CNCs and surfactants not only stabilizes foam but also substantially enhances oil mobilization in both macro- and microporous domains, providing critical mechanistic insights for optimizing foam-based EOR in heterogeneous reservoirs.

Limitations of the study

The T2 cutoff used to distinguish micro- and macro-pore regions was adopted from published literature17 and determined from the valley between the two peaks of the bimodal T2 relaxation spectrum. To evaluate the sensitivity of the results to cutoff selection, the cutoff value was varied within ±5% of the valley-based T2 position. The relative evolution trends of fluid distribution and displacement behavior remained unchanged within this range. Therefore, the T2 segmentation was applied for relative comparison of CO2-induced fluid redistribution rather than for absolute pore-size determination.

Method details

Concentration optimization of CNCs-enhanced surfactant system

Sample preparation

Under ambient conditions (25°C, 1 atm), 100 mL of a 0.1 wt % SDS/AES mixed surfactant solution was prepared using deionized water. The prepared solution was aged in a constant-temperature chamber at simulated reservoir temperature (67 °C) for 24 h to ensure system stability.

Foam performance testing

Foam performance was evaluated using a modified Waring blending method with the following experimental procedure. For oil-containing foam tests, a predetermined volume fraction of simulated crude oil was added to the foaming solution and stirred at 500 rpm for 10 min to ensure uniform dispersion prior to foam generation. (1) Transfer 100 mL of the aged foaming solution into a Waring blender. (2) Continuously inject high-purity CO2 (≥99.9%) at a constant flow rate (50 mL/min) for 1 min to establish a CO2-saturated environment. (3) While maintaining CO2 injection, shear the solution at 3000 ± 50 rpm for 2 min. (4) Immediately transfer the generated foam into a 100 mL graduated cylinder with a stopper and seal with Parafilm. (5) Record the foam volume (Vmax) and foam half-life (t1/2, defined as the time required for the foam volume to decay to 50% of its initial volume).

Microscopic morphology of CNCs-enhanced CO2 foam

The macroscopic foam evolution was first monitored in real-time using a high-resolution imaging system (Nikon D850, 45.7 MP) under controlled conditions (25°C). For microscopic analysis, SEM sample preparation followed ASTM E2142 standards: samples were collected in a clean bench using acetone-cleaned tools, uniformly coated on conductive adhesive-treated substrates, and rapidly frozen with liquid nitrogen. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (Hitachi SU8010, 5 kV) at 500-5000× magnification was then employed to examine lamella microstructure. Comparative analysis between conventional AES/SDS systems and CNCs-enhanced systems revealed the foam-stabilization mechanisms of CNCs.

Oil-CNCs nanofluid contact angle measurements

This study conducted contact angle measurements following ASTM D7334 standard procedures. Rock core slices were first cleaned for 8 min using a Harrick plasma cleaner (PDC-32G, 13.56 MHz), then dried to constant weight in a temperature-controlled oven (67±0.5°C). The pretreated slices were aged in crude oil (from the lower karamay formation, viscosity 18.6 mPa·s@67°C) for 72 h to establish oil-wet conditions. Initial contact angles were measured using a Krüss DSA100 instrument (25°C ambient, 2 μL test droplets, Young-Laplace fitting algorithm).

To investigate the wettability alteration capability of foam systems, the aged rock slices were immersed in either: 100 mL baseline AES/SDS solution, or CNCs-enhanced foam system for four predetermined durations (50, 100, 200, and 300 min). At each time point, samples were: Rinsed with ultrapure water, Dried under nitrogen atmosphere, Immediately subjected to contact angle measurement (5 replicates per time point).

All experiments were conducted under reservoir temperature conditions (67°C). The temporal evolution of contact angles provided quantitative evaluation of each system’s wettability modification efficiency.

CNCs nanofluid IFT measurements

Interfacial tension measurements were carried out following the ASTM D971 standard using the pendant-drop method. The procedure involved the following steps: (1) The U-shaped quartz measurement cell was thoroughly cleaned by sequential treatment with HPLC-grade acetone in an ultrasonic bath, rinsing with ultrapure water, and flushing with the corresponding test solution to ensure complete removal of contaminants. (2) The prepared solutions (100 mL of 0.1 wt% AES/SDS baseline system and 0.1–0.15 wt% CNCs-enhanced systems) were aged at reservoir temperature (67 °C) for 24 h. (3) IFT measurements were performed using a Krüss DSA100 tensiometer under precisely controlled conditions, where 5 μL crude-oil droplets were generated with a Hamilton microsyringe in the solution-filled cell. (4) After a 10-min equilibration period, IFT was continuously recorded for each formulation. The CNCs-enhanced system was measured only after complete cleaning of the apparatus. (5) All tests were conducted under a CO2 atmosphere, and the results were analyzed using ADVANCE software (v2.5) based on Young–Laplace equation fitting. Quantitative comparisons were made using the average IFT values obtained from the stable measurement period (15–30 min) across triplicate runs.

Core WAG flooding and online LF-NMR test

The experimental system, primarily consists of a temperature-controlled chamber (±0.5°C), an ISCO constant-flow pump, high-precision pressure transducers, and a MacroMR12-150H-I low-field NMR analyzer (Niumag Technology). Throughout the experiments, a constant temperature of 67°C was rigorously maintained to simulate actual reservoir conditions, with the NMR system conducting online measurements within a magnetic field strength range of 0.3±0.05 T and pulse frequency range of 2-30 MHz, enabling simultaneous acquisition of T2 spectra and NMR imaging data.

The experimental procedure commenced with core sample pretreatment, including drying at 105°C to achieve constant weight, precise measurement of geometric dimensions, vacuum saturation with heavy water (D2O) for pore parameter determination, and crude oil saturation to establish initial oil saturation. During the waterflooding stage, a constant injection rate of 0.1 mL/min was maintained until the water cut reached 98% (0.9PV), followed by the initiation of WAG and NWAG displacement experiments. The WAG displacement was conducted under a backpressure of 20 MPa, employing an alternating injection protocol of 0.3 PV CO2 and 0.3 PV AES/SDS system, completing two cycles (cumulative 1.2 PV), with online NMR testing performed every 0.15 PV injection. To ensure the reliability of LF-NMR relaxation measurements, the non-reactivity of D2O with the CNCs/surfactant system was experimentally verified prior to testing. No observable changes were detected in the physical appearance, pH, or interfacial behavior of the solution after D2O substitution. These results demonstrate that under the experimental conditions, D2O does not chemically interact with CNCs or surfactants and does not interfere with the relaxation behavior of the fluid phases.
