Endovascular Repair of an Ilio‐Iliac Arteriovenous Fistula Presenting as Acute Lower Limb Edema
Authors: Yudai Hagihara, Hiroki Okada, Yoshihiro Honda, Kenji Sakakibara, Shigeaki Kaga, Takuji Araki, Shoji Suzuki, Hiroyuki Nakajima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA ruptured iliac aneurysm with an arteriovenous fistula is a life‐threatening disease that requires immediate treatment. However, an arteriovenous fistula due to iliac aneurysm rupture is rare and challenging to diagnose, especially in the absence of classic symptoms. Endovascular repair is a minimally invasive and effective treatment option, leading to symptom improvement.Keywords: arteriovenous fistula, endovascular repair, internal iliac aneurysm rupture, internal iliac artery—External iliac vein fistula

Introduction

Acute unilateral lower limb edema is a common symptom; however, lower limb edema caused by an arteriovenous fistula (AVF) due to iliac aneurysm rupture is rare and potentially fatal. We should diagnose an iliac aneurysm rupture with a fistula and select surgical management with due consideration of the patient's condition and risks.

We herein report a case of symptomatic AVF that was successfully treated with endovascular stent‐graft insertion.

Case Report

Case History

A 56‐year‐old man presented with severe left lower limb edema that suddenly developed several days prior to visiting a local hospital. The patient did not have any abdominal, back, or pelvic pain. Computed tomography (CT) revealed a left common iliac artery aneurysm and bilateral internal iliac artery (IIA) aneurysms. The patient was then transferred to our hospital for further investigation and surgical treatment.

Investigations and Treatment

His medical history included a stroke 10 years ago and complicated hypoesthesia of the right lower limb. On examination, he was found to have severe edema in the left lower limb, and a bruit was audible over his lower abdomen. His blood pressure was 108/59 mmHg, pulse rate was 73 beats/min, respiratory rate was 12 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation level was 98% (air). Laboratory investigations revealed no evidence of anemia: Hemoglobin was 14.1 g/dL, and hematocrit was 41.6%. Renal function was within normal limits: Serum creatinine was 0.81 mg/dL, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was 77 mL/min. Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) was 11.7 pg/mL, which lies within the reference range (< 18.5 pg/mL). Chest radiography revealed no pulmonary congestion or cardiomegaly. He underwent contrast‐enhanced CT, which identified a 38‐mm left common iliac artery aneurysm and bilateral internal iliac aneurysms on the right and left at 39 and 50 mm in maximum diameter, respectively. More importantly, the arterial phase of the contrast‐enhanced CT demonstrated early, rapid enhancement of the venous system. It also revealed a left internal iliac artery‐external iliac vein fistula at the proximal IIA and compression of the proximal external iliac vein by the internal iliac aneurysm (Figures 1 and 2). However, there were no obvious signs of rupture, such as inflammatory changes or clot formation in the pelvis. Echocardiography showed a good left ventricular ejection fraction (73%) and a normal‐sized right ventricle with trivial tricuspid regurgitation and no pulmonary regurgitation. Ultrasonography revealed a small perforation (5.6 mm) and shunt of blood flow from the left internal iliac aneurysm to the left external iliac vein. Because the patient was hemodynamically stable and did not have heart failure, a semi‐urgent surgery was performed on day 5 of hospitalization.

Under general anesthesia, endovascular repair combined with transarterial embolization was performed via bilateral femoral arteriotomy. Intraoperative digital subtraction angiography (DSA) delineated the iliac veins and inferior vena cava via an AVF adjacent to the left IIA aneurysm (Figure 3A). To prepare for endovascular stent grafting, we occluded the middle sacral and lumbar arteries with coils (C‐STOPPER COIL: PIOLAX Inc., Yokohama, Kanagawa, Japan; Target: Stryker Neurovascular, Fremont, CA, USA). The distal part of the right internal iliac aneurysm was embolized from the right superior and inferior gluteal arteries to the distal part of the right IIA with a vascular plug (Amplatzer Vascular Plug 4; AGA Medical Co., Golden Valley, MN, USA) and coils. Subsequently, the left iliolumbar and inferior gluteal arteries were treated with vascular plugs and coils. The left superior gluteal artery was obstructed spontaneously. The entrance of the AVF was embolized using three detachable coils (Target XXL; 24 mm × 50 cm). Although the shunt blood flow through the AVF did not completely disappear, we expected thrombogenesis of the fistula after stent graft insertion. Next, an Excluder (W.L. Gore and Assoc., Flagstaff, AZ, USA) was applied to repair the aneurysm‐fistula complex. The main body (RLT231218J; 23 mm × 12 mm × 18 cm) was deployed distal to the renal artery with the ipsilateral limb extending into the right external iliac artery, and the contralateral limb extending into the left external iliac artery using the contralateral legs (PLC141200J; 16 mm × 12 mm × 10 cm). The total procedural time was 5 h and 40 min, and the blood loss volume was 92 mL (Figure 3B,C).

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Postoperative CT showed no endoleak with complete exclusion of the left internal iliac artery‐external iliac vein fistula (Figure 3D). After endovascular treatment, the patient's left lower limb edema improved, and his left thigh circumference immediately decreased from 48 to 40 cm (Figure 4A,B). The patient did not complain of buttock pain and was discharged on postoperative day 7. At the one‐year follow‐up, CT showed a slight type II endoleak due to backflow from the inferior mesenteric artery, whereas the size of the aneurysms reduced without residual fistula and deep vein thrombosis. The patient was asymptomatic, and no procedure‐related complications, such as hip or buttock claudication, were observed.

Discussion

Spontaneous ilioiliac fistulae are rare. Among ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysms, aortocaval fistulae are noted in 3%–4% of cases [1, 2]. Iliac AVFs are even rarer, accounting for only 1.8% of major abdominal AVFs [3]. In previous reports, the “triad of symptoms” was associated with iliac AVF, including high‐output cardiac failure of precipitous onset, a pulsatile abdominal mass accompanied by a thrill and bruit, and unilateral lower‐extremity ischemia or venous engorgement [4]. However, all 3 of these signs have been found in only 32.7% of cases with iliac AVF, while 3.6% of cases had only unilateral venous engorgement or leg ischemia [5]. Furthermore, some cases of AVF are asymptomatic [6]. Especially in the presence of no abdominal pain or signs of high‐flow heart failure, an AVF may be overlooked. Thus, both contrast‐enhanced CT and ultrasound are critical for the prompt and accurate diagnosis of AVF [5, 7, 8].

In the present case, we initially suspected deep vein thrombosis due to vein compression caused by an abdominal aortic aneurysm because the patient presented with only the left lower limb edema. However, computed tomographic angiography revealed an AVF caused by a ruptured left internal iliac aneurysm. We assumed that compression of the proximal external iliac vein by the internal iliac aneurysm would lead to unilateral lower extremity edema as the main symptom and prevent progression to heart failure in this case. If a patient presents with abrupt lower limb edema, an abdominal examination should be performed, considering the possibility of the presence of an iliac AVF.

According to some systematic reviews, the common iliac AVF is the most frequent type of spontaneous iliac AVF due to rupture of an iliac aneurysm [5, 9]. However, as demonstrated in the present case, perforation of the left IIA aneurysm into the left external iliac vein is extremely rare [10]. Furthermore, there are no reports on endovascular management of such fistulae.

Both open surgery and endovascular repair have been proposed as treatment options for iliac aneurysms with AVF. Open techniques have previously been the recommended treatment, but they involve high risks of bleeding due to high venous pressure, wound infection, and mortality, particularly if the patient has significant comorbidities [11]. If open surgery had been applied in this particular case, the bilateral internal iliac arteries would have been ligated because of their very small diameter and treated with a bifurcated (Y‐shaped) prosthetic graft. The fistula should be closed either directly or via patch closure. Vessel wall adhesion between the IIA and internal iliac vein carries a risk of vessel wall rupture or wall dissection during surgical repair, which may cause fatal bleeding. In open repair of iliac AVFs, a previous case series reported that the rates of complications and deaths were 15% and 6.5%, respectively [5]. Endovascular treatment can decrease these risks; therefore, it is an effective and minimally invasive alternative to open surgery for the treatment of AVFs secondary to iliac artery aneurysms.

An endovascular technique to close the iliac AVF has a lower bleeding risk and is a less invasive approach than the open technique, but it is not always successful. Endovascular repair may not be possible in cases of severe aortic tortuosity or calcification of the access vessels. In addition, the risk of potential endoleaks is not negligible. A recent literature review showed that 55% of patients developed endoleaks in the postoperative course of iliac AVFs due to iliac aneurysm rupture, and almost all of them needed additional intervention [5]. Long‐term results of endovascular repair are lacking; thus, follow‐up is essential to diagnose and treat graft complications.

In the surgical treatment of iliac artery aneurysms, preserving the blood flow to at least one IIA or its major gluteal branches is recommended [12]. Failure to do so may lead to symptoms, including buttock claudication, colonic ischemia, pelvic necrosis, or erectile dysfunction. An iliac branch endoprosthesis (IBE) is a potential treatment option, considering the preservation of pelvic circulation in endovascular treatment. However, in the present case, IBE was not selected because of anatomical limitations: (i) bilateral IIA aneurysms with a maximum diameter > 30 mm, (ii) distal IIA non‐aneurysmal seal zone < 10 mm and small superior gluteal artery, and (iii) large mural thrombus in IIA aneurysms. Although IIA coil embolization in two stages to secure the collateral vessels was also considered, the patient's rapidly progressing leg edema caused concern about the development of acute heart failure. We therefore placed embolizing materials in the proximal portion of the main stem of the gluteal arteries to maintain communication between the major gluteal branches. There were no signs of pelvic ischemia after endovascular repair, and the patient's postoperative course was excellent.

Conclusion

We diagnosed a rare case of an AVF due to an iliac aneurysm rupture using contrast‐enhanced CT and successfully treated it with endovascular stent‐graft repair. Endovascular approach is a safe, low‐invasive, and reliable treatment option, leading to successful results.
