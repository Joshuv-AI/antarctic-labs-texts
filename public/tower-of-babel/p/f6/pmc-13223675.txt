Development and validation of a screening scale to predict postdischarge depression in patients with acute coronary syndrome after percutaneous coronary intervention: a prospective longitudinal study
Authors: Mana Doi, Yuko Sakamoto, Asumi Tanaka, Rieko Tsunashima, Akemi Miyabe, Maya Minamizaki-Kanno, Kiyoshi Hibi, Yuka Kanoya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop a simple screening scale to predict depression after discharge in patients with acute coronary syndrome after percutaneous coronary intervention (ACS-PCI) and to verify its reliability, validity and cutoff value.DesignProspective longitudinal study was conducted 1 week and 3 months after discharge.SettingTwo hospitals where PCI is performed in Japan.ParticipantsA total of 183 patients were potential candidates for the survey, of whom 42 provided valid responses (response rate: 23.0%).ResultsThe number of items was reduced from 14 to 12 with item-total correlations and principal component analysis. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.832 and the intraclass correlation coefficient (1, 2) was 0.811 (95% CI 0.650 to 0.898). Significant correlations were observed for concurrent validity (r=0.699, p<0.01) and predictive validity (ρ=0.538, p<0.01). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.799 (CI 0.629 to 0.970). A cut-off value of 28 points was obtained from the Youden index and demonstrated a sensitivity of 1.000, specificity of 0.553, positive predictive value (PPV) of 0.190 and negative predictive value of 0.000.ConclusionsThis study developed a simple screening scale for predicting postdischarge depression in patients with ACS-PCI (SDACS-12) and demonstrated its reliability, validity and predictive ability with 12 items. Nevertheless, its results should be interpreted cautiously given the moderate variance explained by PCA and the low specificity and PPV.

Introduction

The incidence of depression in patients with heart disease, such as heart failure and myocardial infarction, is high, ranging from 16% to 33%.1 However, the factors contributing to depression vary by disease, necessitating disease-specific considerations.2 3 Among patients with ischaemic heart disease, which is the most common type of all heart disease,4 approximately 80% experience acute coronary syndrome (ACS), require hospitalisation following an acute onset, and typically undergo emergency percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).5 These patients, treated shortly after symptom onset and typically discharged within 1 to 2 weeks, face significant life changes in a short time.6 They may develop a fear of imminent death. Over 60% of patients with ACS after PCI (ACS-PCI) report fear of recurrence or chest symptoms postdischarge, and 5.6% develop depression 3 months after discharge.6 Changes in Universal Uncertainty in Illness Scale (UUIS) scores have also been identified as factors associated with depression.6 Despite these challenges, the short hospitalisation period (1 to 2 weeks)7 including the hyperacute treatment phase limits the ability of medical professionals to provide comprehensive psychological care, such as listening to their feelings and working with clinical psychologists, especially in patients with severe mental health issues, resulting in inadequate mental health support.

Several scales are available to evaluate depression in myocardial infarction patients, including the Beck Depression Inventory,8 Zung’s Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS)9 and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).10 However, these tools assess the current state of depression and do not predict future mental health outcomes. While it may be possible to predict depression after discharge using HADS scores combined with changes in UUIS scores measured during hospitalisation and at 1 week postdischarge,6 this approach involves answering over 40 questions at two different time points.6 11 12 On the other hand, a screening scale with fewer items is likely to reduce patient burden, especially those in the recovery phase of ACS-PCI. Additionally, the introduction of a simple screening scale could facilitate early intervention by identifying patients who require specialised mental healthcare, thereby reducing the risk of recurrent coronary events and depression-related mortality,13 and ultimately enhancing prognosis. Thus, this study aimed to develop a simple screening scale to predict postdischarge depression in patients with ACS-PCI through a single-point assessment, and to verify its reliability, validity and cut-off value. The proposed scale, consisting of fewer items, is designed to be user-friendly and suitable for clinical settings.

Methods

Study design and participants

In this prospective longitudinal study, participants diagnosed with ACS and treated with PCI were recruited from two hospitals in the Tokyo metropolitan area. Recruitment occurred between 3 April 2022 and 31 December 2023, with follow-up extending until 31 March 2024. Patients were included unless they met one or more of the following exclusion criteria: (1) aged under 20 years; (2) undergoing coronary artery bypass surgery; (3) undergoing elective PCI; (4) prior highly invasive treatment or surgery, such as for cancer or subarachnoid haemorrhage; (5) coronary artery stenosis due to Takayasu’s disease; (6) deemed inappropriate for participation by their physician; (7) diagnosed with depression at baseline; (8) unable to communicate in Japanese or (9) diagnosed with dementia at baseline. Patients under 20 years of age were excluded because they are less likely to develop ACS. Additionally, they are believed to exhibit distinct psychological characteristics, such as those associated with adolescence and the development of self-identity.14 Another reason for excluding teenagers is that unlike individuals in their 20s and older, depression in teenagers requires support and items for its screening that take into account developmental and educational considerations.14 Elective PCI cases were excluded due to differences in clinical course compared with urgent PCI.15 Depression and dementia at baseline were determined by whether or not a doctor’s diagnosis was recorded in the electronic medical record. In addition, the details of the study were explained in advance to the patients’ primary physicians, and they were asked to make a medical judgement on whether it was appropriate for potential participants to participate.

The sample size was calculated as 140 cases (10 times the initial 14 items) based on the calculation using the COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments (COSMIN) and factor analysis.1618 There were 183 candidates; however, possibly due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, 97 patients were discharged earlier than planned or underwent an unplanned transfer to another hospital. The remaining 86 patients were recruited.

Scale development process and data collection

The draft scale was developed based on the HADS and UUIS,10 11 which have been shown to be associated with postdischarge depression, as well as the factor ‘feeling annoyed by troublesome tasks after discharge’.6 A preliminary draft consisting of eight items was created. Additional items were identified through a literature review on depression-related factors in heart disease, leading to a 10-item draft. The draft was reviewed by nine experts: one psychiatric nursing researcher, four cardiology nurses, one industrial health nurse supporting patients with ACS-PCI in the workplace, and three researchers experienced in scale development. Following their input, a 14-item draft focusing on ‘predicting’ depression after discharge was finalised.

A preliminary survey was conducted face-to-face with 10 hospitalised patients with ACS-PCI to identify items that were difficult to answer or understand.19 Based on the results, a revised 14-item scale was developed (online supplemental table S1).

For the main survey, patients with ACS who were hospitalised after PCI and provided informed consent were asked their preferred survey method—mail or online—during their hospital stay. The survey was administered at 1 week and 3 months postdischarge using the selected method. Patients who did not respond 1 week after discharge were not included in the 3-month survey.

Instruments

Sociodemographic and medical data, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), type of ACS, length of hospital stay and cohabitation status, were collected from medical records and patient reports. Psychological assessments included the scale draft, SDS, Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and HADS-D (the depression subscale of HADS). These scales were selected due to their widespread use in depression assessment and their applicability to patients with coronary heart disease.2022

In order to reduce the burden on study participants, we carefully considered the analytical purpose when selecting scales and the timing of each measurement, and minimised the number of measurements necessary to develop a screening scale. Our scale was intended to be administered 1 week after discharge and predict depression 3 months after discharge. Therefore, we first considered a scale for validating the cut-off value. HADS is also administered to general medical outpatients,11 which most closely resembles the situation in which the scale developed in this study will be used. Therefore, we chose it to determine the cut-off value for our scale. Next, the PHQ-9 is recommended by the American Heart Association as a measure of depression in coronary heart disease.23 Therefore, we considered it suitable as an outcome measure for verification of predictive validity and used it 3 months after discharge. Finally, we decided to use the SDS—which has been reported to correlate (>0.6) with the HADS24—as a measure to verify concurrent validity with our scale at baseline. In light of the above, the scale draft and SDS were administered 1 week after discharge, while the scale draft, PHQ-9 and HADS-D were administered 3 months after discharge. The setting is based on the assumption that use of our scale 1 week after discharge will predict depression 3 months after discharge. This is because ineffective responses occurred on ACS patients were showed that owing to higher levels of uncertainty due to changing environment caused by ACS occurrence, PCI and discharge may lead to depression.6

Self-rating depression scale

The SDS is a 20-item scale designed to measure the severity of depression.25 Each item is scored on a four-point scale, ranging from 1 (a little of the time) to 4 (most of the time). The total score ranges from 20 to 80. The Japanese version of the SDS, developed by Fukuda et al, has been validated and shown to be reliable (the correlation based on the retest methods was r=0.85).26 The study sample yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83.

Patient health questionnaire-9

The PHQ-9 is a nine-item self-report scale used to assess depression severity, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 for reliability.27 The total score ranges from 0 to 27, with a commonly used cut-off score of 10 to identify clinically significant depression. The validity of the Japanese version of the PHQ-9 was confirmed by Muramatsu et al.28 The study sample yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.78.

The hospital anxiety and depression scale

The HADS is a 14-item scale developed to assess anxiety and depression in patients with physical illnesses.11 It comprises two subscales: the seven-item anxiety subscale (HADS-A) and the seven-item depression subscale (HADS-D). Each item is rated on a 4-point scale, from 0 to 3, with a higher score indicating greater severity of anxiety or depression. The total score for each subscale can range from 0 to 21. A score of ≥8 on the HADS-D is considered doubtful cases of depression,11 and many earlier studies have set 8 as the cut-off point. The reliability and validity of the Japanese version of the HADS29 were verified by Azuma et al,30 who evaluated patients at a gastroenterology outpatient clinic. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for HADS-D was 0.75 for men and 0.76 for women, while the criterion validity coefficients were 0.46 for men and 0.57 for women, demonstrating significant correlations with the SDS.24 The study sample yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.80.

Statistical analysis

Inter-item correlations and item-total (IT) correlations were evaluated before performing principal component analysis (PCA). Inter-item correlations were assessed with a criterion of a high average ρ of 0.25 or more.31 For IT correlations, items with Spearman’s correlation coefficients that were too low (<0.2) or greater than 0.9 were identified.32 PCA was conducted under the assumption of unidimensionality, with the number of factors set to one. To evaluate the validity of applying PCA, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of sphericity were performed. Eigenvalues, cumulative contribution ratios (set at >20.0%),31 and principal component loadings (set at >0.4) were reviewed to refine the items.

The reliability of the proposed scale was assessed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, with a threshold of 0.7 or higher.32 Test–retest reliability was evaluated by re-administering the scale 3 months later, calculating the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) (1, 2), which was confirmed to be 0.7 or higher.32

To confirm structural validity, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed in addition to PCA (COSMIN).16 In addition, concurrent validity was assessed by calculating the correlation coefficient between the scale draft and the SDS. Predictive validity was evaluated by calculating the correlation coefficient between the scale draft (measured 1 week after discharge) and the PHQ-9 (measured 3 months after discharge).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed using a cut-off value of 8 for the HADS-D score. The area under the curve (AUC) was interpreted as moderate (0.7–0.9) or high (>0.9).32 The Youden index was calculated to determine the cut-off value that maximised sensitivity and specificity.32 Positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) were also calculated.

All analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics V.29.0 (IBM), with a significance level set at α=0.05.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

A total of 86 patients were recruited, and 76 provided informed consent. Of these, 11 patients were excluded because their condition became serious or they did not respond. Additionally, 16 patients dropped out at the 3-month follow-up period, and 7 patients were excluded due to missing data in the psychological scales. As a result, 42 patients completed the follow-up and were included in the final analysis (of 183 eligible candidates, yielding a response rate of 23.0%) (figure 1). The mean age was 65.2 years (SD, 11.2 years), and the mean BMI was 24.2 kg/m² (SD, 4.1 kg/m²). The majority of patients were men (85.7%). The distribution of ACS types was as follows: ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) in 81.0%, non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) in 16.7% and unstable angina (UA) in 2.4%. Additionally, 4.8% of patients had a history of PCI. The mean length of hospital stay was 12.7 days (SD, 5.6 days).

Regarding psychological assessments scores, the mean SDS score, evaluated 1 week after discharge, was 39.7 (SD, 9.5). The mean PHQ-9 and HADS-D scores, assessed 3 months after discharge, were 3.6 (SD, 3.3) and 3.8 (SD, 3.5), respectively. Item analysis

Inter-item correlation, IT correlation and PCA were conducted for all 14 items. Items 7 and 11 were excluded due to low IT correlations (<0.2) and principal component loadings (<0.4).

For the remaining 12 items, inter-item correlations ranged from −0.008 to 0.685, with a mean absolute ρ value of 0.301 (>0.25) (online supplemental table S2). IT correlations ranged from 0.299 to 0.822. PCA for the 12 items indicated a KMO test measure of 0.679, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was statistically significant (p<0.001), confirming the appropriateness of applying PCA. The eigenvalue was 4.499, and the cumulative contribution ratio was 37.494%. Principal component loadings for all 12 items exceeded 0.4, confirming that they were associated with the first principal component (table 2). EFA with a principal factor method and promax rotation yielded an eigenvalue of 4.499 for the first factor (37.494% of the variance), 1.561 for the second factor (13.007% of the variance) and 1.298 for the third factor (10.815% of the variance). The scree plot indicated a single factor, and the Kaiser-Guttman criterion (eigenvalues >1) indicated a three-factor structure. The amount of variance accounted for by the first factor (eigenvalue=4.499) was higher than that of the others, exceeding 20%,31 suggesting unidimensionality. Furthermore, the inter-factor correlations between three factors revealed by the EFA were 0.451–0.581, supporting this conclusion.31 33

The mean score of the 12-item scale (score range: 12–60), named the SDACS-12, was 31.6 (SD, 8.4). Results of the Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that the SDACS-12 scores followed a normal distribution (p=0.184).

Reliability

The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.832, and the ICC (1, 2) was 0.811 (95% CI 0.650 to 0.898).

Validity

For concurrent validity, significant correlations were observed between the SDACS-12 and SDS scores (r=0.699, p<0.01). For predictive validity, significant correlations were also identified between the SDACS-12 score at 1 week after discharge and the PHQ-9 score (ρ=0.538, p<0.01).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to develop a simple screening scale for predicting depression after discharge. Our findings demonstrate the reliability, validity and predictive ability of this scale in identifying depression in patients with ACS-PCI using a reduced number of items.

This screening scale comprises items derived from previous research,6 11 12 which are likely to predict depression 3 months after discharge based on an evaluation conducted only 1 week after discharge. It was hypothesised that a score above a certain threshold on the SDACS-12 at 1 week postdischarge could predict suspected depression 3 months later (HADS-D score ≥8). The procedures of this study were then established to evaluate its validity and determine the cut-off value.

The reliability of the SDACS-12 was supported by acceptable values for Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (0.832) and ICC (1, 2) (0.811). Although careful interpretation is required for the ICC due to the 3-month retest interval, the scale showed acceptable reliability in terms of internal consistency, as indicated by a Cronbach’s alpha greater than 0.7. Construct validity, indicated by its correlation with SDS (r=0.699) and predictive validity, indicated by its correlation with PHQ-9 (ρ=0.538), were also acceptable. The AUC value (0.799, p=0.051, 95% CI 0.629 to 0.970) suggests that the SDACS-12 is a moderate discriminating scale (AUC>0.722). The small sample size may have influenced the discrepancy between the p value and the 95% CI in determining statistical significance.34 When comparing the approximate value AUC (0.75) shown in this study, with AUC (0.5), the number of samples required for ROC analysis was 18 (when the correlation is 0.6) and 28 (when the correlation is 0.3). These were supported, supporting the idea that the SDACS-12 was not nullified.31

The cut-off value was determined based on the sensitivity and specificity combination yielding the highest Youden Index.31 The sensitivity was 1.000, and specificity was 0.553. Although no suspected depression cases were missed (HADS score ≥8), there is a potential for overdiagnosis, which could lead to psychological stress in patients who may not require concern. Furthermore, since the PPV is 0.190 and the NPV is 0.000, there are likely to be a few cases where patients who exceed the cut-off point develop depression. This is supported by the fact that the depression rate was 5.6% in ACS-PCI patients.6 Although the prevalence of depression among ACS-PCI patients is relatively low, it remains clinically significant due to its considerable adverse impact on patients’ health outcomes. Therefore, while the Youden Index provides a basis for setting the cut-off value, it is essential to reassess its appropriateness in actual clinical practice.

Considering the reliability, validity and AUC, the SDACS-12 appears to be a practical and effective scale for predicting subsequent depression based on evaluations conducted only a week postdischarge. However, it should not be used to definitively diagnose depression 3 months later. Instead, high scores should prompt follow-up evaluations by psychiatric specialists or inquiries into daily life challenges, ensuring that necessary support is provided. The SDACS-12 should be viewed as an opportunity for medical professionals to connect with patients and facilitate necessary care, rather than as a tool to instil pessimism about future health outcomes.

In clinical practice, the SDACS-12 should be used after discharge rather than during hospitalisation, because ineffective responses owing to higher levels of uncertainty due to changing environment caused by ACS occurrence, PCI and discharge may lead to depression.6 The scale could potentially be administered online, allowing patients to receive messages encouraging them to seek medical help if they score high. For patients with ACS-PCI who tend to focus only on their physical health, this approach increases awareness about mental health risks and fosters attention to both mental and physical health. Most patients experience the feeling of imminent death,35 but with the use of minimally invasive treatment, like PCI, they can recover and even return to work.36 Although patients with ACS-PCI face unique challenges such as limited social support compared with patients with cancer,6 37 tools like the SDACS-12 can help identify high-risk patients and provide necessary care to maintain their quality of life.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is that we focused specifically on patients with ACS after PCI and developed a screening scale with a limited number of items that could predict postdischarge depression at a single time point. With only 12 items, the scale can be quickly assessed postdischarge, minimising the burden on patients. Furthermore, the scale can be administered with minimal effort and could be a fairly user-friendly tool if its applicability to online administration is assessed in future studies. Several limitations are present in this study. First, the sample size was small. The survey was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, during which the ward organisation at the surveyed facilities changed, leading to a reduction in the number of hospitalised patients with ACS-PCI. Additionally, some patients experienced worsened physical conditions after PCI and required treatments other than PCI, or were diagnosed with other conditions. This contributed to a certain number of dropouts after consent was obtained. Furthermore, some participants responded 1 week after discharge but dropped out within 3 months, and some had missing data in the psychological assessments. As a result, the final analysis is based on a small sample size. This may have resulted in a wider range in CI for the AUC. In addition, it cannot be ruled out that conducting the survey during the COVID-19 pandemic may have affected the psychological state of the subjects. Also, as participants were recruited at a time when hospital visits were restricted due to COVID-19, their responses may have been influenced by the Hawthorne effect.37 Furthermore, the purpose of this study was to develop a screening scale with a small number of items. The results of the PCA confirmed that the SDACS-12 is unidimensional. However, the variance of 37.494% is not particularly high,31 which is the third limitation of this study. Further investigation is needed into the structure between the items. On the other hand, considering the reliability, validity and AUC, the SDACS-12 appears to be a practical and effective measure for predicting subsequent depression. Although its cut-off value was determined based on the Youden Index, the specificity, PPV and NPV were low. Therefore, when using the SDACS-12 in clinical settings, we hope that it will not cause excessive anxiety to patients, but that it will provide an opportunity for medical professionals to connect with patients and promote necessary care, while being careful in interpreting its scores. A fourth limitation is that the measurement scales and frequency of measurements were kept to a minimum to reduce the burden on study participants, which affected the interpretation of the ICC and made it impossible to observe changes in scores on depression scales. Future studies, once the COVID-19 situation has subsided, should conduct larger-scale surveys across multiple facilities, considering the timing of measurements, to validate these results and explore the possibility of applying SDACS-12 beyond Japan, including its online use.

Conclusions

This study developed a simple screening scale for predicting post-discharge depression in patients with ACS-PCI (SDACS-12), demonstrating its reliability, validity and predictive ability with 12 items. However, the potential impact of the sample size cannot be ruled out. Given the low specificity, PPV and NPV, the SDACS-12 should be applied cautiously in clinical settings, ensuring that it does not cause undue anxiety in patients while providing an opportunity for healthcare professionals to engage with patients and promote appropriate care.
