Structural insights into the photochemistry of the LH1–RC complex from the marine purple phototrophic bacterium Rhodovulum sulfidophilum
Authors: Xing-Yu Yue, Guang-Lei Wang, Shinya Kosaki, Kenji V. P. Nagashima, Yu-Lu Wu, Yuki Kobayashi, Tomoya Sugiyama, Ryo Kanno, Endang R. Purba, Shinichi Takaichi, Toshiaki Mochizuki, Akira Mizoguchi, Bruno M. Humbel, Michael T. Madigan, Hiroyuki Mino, Kazutoshi Tani, Yukihiro Kimura, Zheng-Yu Wang-Otomo, Long-Jiang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The marine purple nonsulfur phototrophic bacterium Rhodovulum (Rdv.) sulfidophilum (Alphaproteobacteria) has been a model organism for bacterial photosynthesis research because of its unusual ability to grow phototrophically (anoxic/light) using high concentrations of inorganic or organic sulfur compounds as electron donors or by respiration under fully oxic conditions. Here we present a 1.81 Å-resolution cryo-EM structure of the light-harvesting 1–reaction center (LH1–RC) photocomplex from the Rdv. sulfidophilum type strain W4 with a focus on RC structure and function. The Rdv. sulfidophilum RC is characterized by its cytochrome (Cyt) subunit that contains three heme groups and is anchored by its intact N-terminal domain in the membrane. In contrast to a methionine as the 6th axial ligand to the heme-2 in other bacterial RC-bound triheme and tetraheme Cyt subunits, the outmost heme-2 in the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit is ligated by a cysteine residue, resulting in a significant downshift of reduction potential of 470 mV compared to that of a methionine-ligated heme-2. A nonheme Fe ligated by a histidine of the Cyt subunit and five water molecules was identified in close proximity to heme-2, implying a potential role in electron transport from soluble electron donors to heme-2. The Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1 complex forms an open ring structure consisting of 16 αβ-subunits with a gap formed where the N-terminal transmembrane domain of the RC Cyt subunit and a newly identified protein with three helical domains (designated as protein-3h) are located. Protein-3h corresponds to the truncated N-terminal fragment of a gene product encoded by the pseudo-gene urf1 in the NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase (complex I) nuo operon in the genome of Rdv. sulfidophilum W4. Genes urf1 are also found in other purple nonsulfur bacteria and in aerobic anoxygenic phototrophic bacteria, and their putative products all share a common structural motif of N-terminal transmembrane U-shaped tandem helices. Based on structural and spectroscopic data, possible electron transfer pathways between the Rdv. sulfidophilum RC Cyt subunit and soluble electron donors and potential roles of protein-3h in the structural integrity of LH1–RC are discussed.

Introduction

The marine purple phototrophic bacterium Rhodovulum (Rdv.) sulfidophilum (originally Rhodopseudomonas sulfidophila) type strain W4 (DSM 1374T) was isolated over 50 years ago from intertidal mud flats of the Dutch Waddenzee1. Since then, this organism has been used as a model for both fundamental and applied studies in purple bacterial metabolism, regulation, genetics, and photochemistry because of its ability to grow under diverse phototrophic (light/anoxic) and respiratory (dark/oxic) conditions. Although phylogenetically falling within the purple nonsulfur bacteria (class Alphaproteobacteria), Rdv. sulfidophilum, as its species name implies, is characterized by its high sulfide tolerance and remarkable ability to oxidize sulfide or thiosulfate to sulfate without accumulating elemental sulfur as an intermediate1. The expression of photosynthetic complexes in Rdv. sulfidophilum has been extensively investigated because, unlike most purple nonsulfur bacteria, the synthesis of its photocomplexes is not significantly repressed under aerobic conditions2–4.

A characteristic feature of Rdv. sulfidophilum photocomplexes is the lack of a heme-1 (the most distal heme from the special pair) binding site in the reaction center (RC)-bound cytochrome (Cyt) subunit5, a site present in tetraheme-Cyt-subunit-containing purple bacteria and also in filamentous anoxygenic phototrophs. The absence of this heme in Rdv. sulfidophilum raises the question of how electron transfer occurs between its triheme-Cyt subunit and soluble electron donors and indicates that an alternative mechanism to that seen in tetraheme Cyt subunits likely exists. This conclusion is supported by the fact that heme-2 in the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit has a significantly lower reduction potential (Em) of –160 mV6 compared with heme-1 in the tetraheme Cyt subunits of Blastochloris (Blc.) viridis (–70 mV)7, Rubrivivax (Rvi.) gelatinosus (+70 mV)8, and Allochromatium vinosum (+10 mV)9. Several Cyt proteins have been identified as electron donors to the Rdv. sulfidophilum RC Cyt subunit10, including soluble Cyt c2 (Em = +358 mV)10 and membrane-bound Cyt c2m (Em = +369 mV)11.

Recently, cryo-EM structures12,13 were reported for a triheme Cyt subunit-containing core light-harvesting (LH1)–RC complex from the marine aerobic anoxygenic phototrophic bacterium Dinoroseobacter (D.) shibae. In one of these studies, heme-3 (the closest heme to the special pair) in the RC-bound Cyt subunit was suggested to be the electron acceptor from soluble Cyt c212. This contrasts with studies employing site-directed mutagenesis coupled with redox titrations and EPR analysis of the Rdv. sulfidophilum RC, which proposed that heme-4 or heme-2 (or both) serve as the electron acceptor from soluble electron donors6.

To explore photosynthetic electron flow in Rdv. sulfidophilum in more detail, here we employ high-resolution cryo-EM structural and biochemical analyses of this phototroph’s LH1–RC complex. In brief, our results provide fresh evidence that points to heme-2 in the Cyt subunit as the most likely heme accepting electrons from soluble Cyt proteins. Previously unknown structural features of the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC complex was also revealed in our study, including the arrangement of LH1 αβ-subunits, the N-terminal domain of the Cyt subunit, and the presence of nonheme Fe and an auxiliary protein.

Results

Overall structure and characterization of the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC

The cryo-EM structure of the LH1–RC complex from Rdv. sulfidophilum type strain W4 (DSM 1374 T) was determined at 1.81 Å resolution (Fig. 1, Table 1, Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). The LH1 complex forms an open ring structure surrounding the RC and consists of 16 pairs of helical αβ-polypeptides, 32 BChls a, 15 spheroidenes, and 14 demethylspheroidenes (Fig. 1a–1c). The Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1 complex displays an absorption maximum (Qy transition) at 883 nm (Fig. 1d) and is characterized by a weak CD signal at 872 nm with a negative sign (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC spectrum remained unchanged upon addition of EDTA (Supplementary Fig. 3b), indicating that divalent metal ions such as Mg2+ or Ca2+ were not affecting absorption properties. Near the LH1 opening (gap), a large fragment of electron potential densities was observed in our refined map. Based on this high-resolution density map, we could trace most residues, and the derived protein consisted of three helical domains corresponding to the N-terminus-truncated portion of a pseudo-gene (urf1) product encoded in the Rdv. sulfidophilum strain W4 NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase (complex I) nuo operon (Supplementary Fig. 4). The Rdv. sulfidophilum RC contains a triheme Cyt subunit (Figs. 1a, 1b) with its intact N-terminal helix embedded in the membrane region forming a transmembrane domain. Light-induced P+/P absorption difference spectra (Supplementary Fig. 3c) showed signals primarily from the RC pigments: 1269 nm for the oxidized form (P+) of the special pair, 884 nm for the reduced form (P) of the special pair, and 789/811 nm for the accessory BChls a. Unexpectedly, a cluster of strong electron potential densities was detected near the heme-2 group signaling the presence of a metal ion coordinated by an RC His residue and five water molecules. Geometry-valence calculations14 revealed that the metal was best fit by an Fe ion with a geometry of octahedral coordination. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy further confirmed the existence of this nonheme Fe ion (see next section). The Rdv. sulfidophilum RC contains four BChls a, two bacteriopheophytins a, one 15-cis-spheroidene, and two ubiquinones (UQ)-10 at the QA and QB sites (Fig. 1c). Biochemical analysis revealed UQ-10 as the major quinone in both intracytoplasmic membranes (ICM) and in purified LH1–RC complexes (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Phosphatidylglycerols (PG) and phosphatidylethanolamines (PE) were identified in the Rdv. sulfidophilum ICM (Supplementary Fig. 5b) and a total of 12 PGs were fitted in the LH1–RC structure (Supplementary Fig. 5c). The high-resolution structure of the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC also identified 505 water molecules mainly distributed on the membrane and solvent-exposed surfaces of the complex (Supplementary Fig. 6a).

The soluble domain of the RC-bound Cyt subunit

The Rdv. sulfidophilum RC is characterized by its triheme Cyt subunit, lacking the outermost heme-1 prosthetic group present in tetraheme Cyt subunits (Fig. 2a). Comparison of amino acid sequences revealed that the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit has a relatively higher sequence similarity to the triheme Cyt subunit of the aerobic purple bacterium D. shibae12,13 (Supplementary Fig. 7) than to the tetraheme Cyt subunits from other anaerobic purple bacteria. The surface of the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit is negatively charged (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 8a, 8b) as a result of a high proportion of acidic residues, a common feature of Cyt subunits from halophilic purple phototrophs15–17. Indeed, the difference in compositions of acidic (Asp + Glu: 13.5 mol%) and basic (Lys + Arg: 6.5 mol%) residues in the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit is even greater than that of the Cyt subunit of the moderately halophilic purple bacterium Rhodothalassium (Rts.) salexigens18. A large opening was observed on the surface near the edge of heme-2 in the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit (Fig. 2b), making this portion of heme-2 readily accessible to solvents. Somewhat smaller gates were also formed near the edges of heme-3 and heme-4 (Supplementary Fig. 8a, 8b). For all hemes, their propionate groups were located near the surface and pointed to the openings. Heme-2 in the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit was axially ligated by a Cys residue (Fig. 2c) instead of a Met that is present at this position in all other RC Cyt subunits reported thus far. The nonheme Fe revealed in our structure was positioned near the heme-2 propionate groups, with the closest distances being 4.3 Å to one of the propionate oxygen atoms and 7.9 Å to the porphyrin edge (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 8c). This proximity suggests a potential role for the nonheme Fe in relaying electrons from soluble electron donors to heme-2. The nonheme Fe ion was hexacoordinated by five water molecules and His321 (Supplementary Fig. 8c), a residue unique to the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit hemes 3 and 4 had similar coordination patterns to those of corresponding hemes in other Cyt subunits, with axial ligands of Met264 for heme-3 (Supplementary Fig. 9a) and His162 for heme-4 (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Structural comparison of the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit with the tetraheme Cyt subunit of Blc. viridis19 revealed a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 1.62 Å for the mainchain Cα carbons with three regions showing relatively large deviations (Fig. 2d). One of the largely deviated regions (Ser316–Ser323) was around the nonheme Fe–His321–H2O cluster near the heme-2. Similarly, a large deviation (maximum 10.3 Å) was also observed for this region when compared with the triheme Cyt subunit of D. shibae (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Despite differences in protein structures, the positions and orientations of Cyt hemes-2, 3, and 4 remain nearly the same in Rdv. sulfidophilum, Blc. viridis, and D. shibae (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 8d).

EPR spectroscopy of the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC complex

To confirm the existence and identity of the nonheme Fe in the Rdv. sulfidophilum RC Cyt subunit, EPR measurements were conducted on purified LH1–RC (Fig. 3a, 3b). Five types of EPR signals were observed from iron centers at peak positions of g = 3.40, 3.05 (overlapped with the signal at 2.95), 2.64, 2.26, and 1.79. The g = 3.05 signal can be assigned to the nonheme Fe ion because this signal disappeared upon EDTA treatment. The signals at g = 2.64, 2.26, and 1.79 were assigned to the g-anisotropy (gz, gy, and gx, respectively) of heme-26. Signals at g = 3.40 and 2.95 could be assigned to either heme-3 or heme-4 (for more detailed descriptions, see Supplementary Text). The g = 3.05 signal decreased in the presence of sodium ascorbate, whereas other Fe signals were insensitive, indicating that the nonheme Fe has a higher reduction potential than heme-bound irons. The octahedral coordination of the nonheme Fe (Figs. 2c, S8c) structure suggests that this Fe has an S = 5/2 spin with a weak ligand field20,21. Fig 3b (trace v) shows a simulated spectrum of the S = 5/2 spin with zero-field splitting parameters of D = 0.105 cm−1 and E/D = 0.05. The small D and E/D values indicate that the ligand field is weak and that the spin is very close to an axial symmetry, respectively. Microwave power saturations were measured for the CW-EPR signals at g = 3.40 (Fig. 3c, g = 2.64 (Fig. 3d). Half-saturation microwave powers P1/2 for the EDTA-treated sample were lower than those for the untreated sample at both g values. This results from the loss of the nonheme iron that acts as a spin relaxer. The relaxation effect on the g = 2.64 signal was significantly larger than that for the g = 3.40 signal. The relaxation mechanism is related to magnetic dipole-dipole interactions that strongly depend on the distances between electron spins. In summary, our EPR results are consistent with the structure of the Rdv. sulfidophilum RC and confirm that heme-2 (g = 2.64 species) is in close proximity to the nonheme Fe, while hemes 3 and 4 (g = 3.40 species) are located farther from the nonheme iron.

The N-terminal transmembrane domain of the RC-bound Cyt subunit

Rdv. sulfidophilum is characterized by a full-length RC-bound Cyt subunit with its intact N-terminal helical domain as the membrane anchor (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 10). This arrangement differs from that in many purple bacterial RC-bound Cyt subunits in which the N-termini are truncated and modified by acyl lipids that function as membrane anchors. The N-terminal transmembrane domain of Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt is located close to the LH1 gap (Fig. 1b) and interacts with nearby protein-3h and LH1-α1β1 polypeptides (Fig. 4a) on both the periplasmic (Fig. 4b) and cytoplasmic (Fig. 4c) sides. Superposition of the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC structure with that of Rhodobacter (Rba.) sphaeroides (now renamed Cereibacter sphaeroides) revealed that the N-terminal transmembrane domain (Trp8–Gly60) of the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit lies in a similar position in the RC as the PufX polypeptide does in the Rba. sphaeroides RC (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 10a). The phylogenetic relationship between the intact N-terminal portion of bacterial RC-bound Cyt and PufX polypeptide in the LH1–RCs from Rhodobacter species was pointed out previously22, and their structural similarities were initially confirmed in the LH1–RC complex of Rhodopila (Rpi.) globiformis23 followed by those from Roseospirillum (Rss.) parvum24 and D. shibae12,13. Structural comparisons showed that the N-terminal helix domain of Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt is positionally and conformationally closer to PufX than those from other purple bacteria (Fig. 4e), and the loop regions immediately following the helical domains overlap well with the PufX C-terminus for the Cyt subunits from all purple bacteria with known structures (Fig. 4e, 4g). By contrast, the N-terminal portions of the intact Cyt subunits from the filamentous anoxygenic phototrophs Roseiflexus (Rof.) castenholzii (transmembrane helix plus the following loop region)25 and Chloroflexus (Cfx.) aurantiacus (helical domain)26 were spatially more deviated from PufX (Fig. 4f), in agreement with the more distant phylogenetic relationship between purple bacteria (Proteobacteria) and the Chloroflexota.

It is notable that PufX has a longer transmembrane domain extended to the edge of the LH1 ring due to its largely tilted conformation than does the N-terminal helix domains of Cyt subunits (Fig. 4d–4f, Supplementary Fig. 10a). This can be explained by comparison with the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC structure that showed that all C-shaped monomeric LH1 rings of Rhodobacter species start from the third αβ-subunit (LH1-α3β3, Supplementary Fig. 10a) in the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1; thus, an extended PufX N-terminus would collide spatially with the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1-α2β2 subunit. Combined with α1β1, the presence of the first two subunits (α1β1 and α2β2) of Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1 prevents the nearby N-terminal transmembrane domain of the Cyt subunit from adopting a more tilted conformation (Supplementary Fig. 10a).

Auxiliary protein-3h

The newly identified protein-3h in the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC complex locates near the LH1 gap (Fig. 1a, 1b) and contains 105 amino acids (Arg5–Pro109) and several unique structural features (Fig. 5a). Protein-3h forms three helices with the first two of them (helix-1 and helix-2) being transmembrane domains while the third one (helix-3) lies on the cytoplasmic surface followed by a C-terminal loop that extends to the LH1 outer ring (Fig. 5a, Supplementary Fig. 11a). Protein-3h interacts extensively with the N-terminal transmembrane domain of the Cyt subunit and LH1-α1 and α16 polypeptides through its transmembrane helix-1 and helix-2. Protein-3h also interacts with the RC H-subunit through helix-3, and with the RC M-subunit and LH1-β10 and β11 polypeptides through its C-terminal loop (Fig. 5a). Collectively, these interactions should significantly stabilize both the LH1 gap region and the entire LH1–RC complex. Despite similar origins and structural folds, Rdv. sulfidophilum protein-3h is located at a different position in the LH1–RC from that of protein-O (or protein-LRC) in D. shibae12 (Supplementary Fig. 11b). This may be attributed to structural restraints in the D. shibae LH1–RC due to its closed LH1 ring of 17 αβ-subunits. The gene encoding protein-3h was not annotated as such in the genome database of Rdv. sulfidophilum strain W4. We have reanalyzed this genome and found that the gene encoding protein-3h exists between nuoE and nuoF in the nuo operon, which encodes NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase (complex I) (Supplementary Fig. 4) and corresponds to the pseudo-gene urf1 (unidentified reading frame) as designated in the nuo operon of Rba. capsulatus27,28. The gene urf1 of Rdv. sulfidophilum W4 contains 690 bp encoding a protein product (Urf1) of 229 amino acids. The putative Rdv. sulfidophilum Urf1 protein was predicted by AlphaFold to have a structure of three helical domains in the N-terminal region and a compact domain in the C-terminal region connected by a long linker loop (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Fig. 11c). Protein-3h is the N-terminal portion of Urf1 cleaved in the loop region (Fig. 5b) as confirmed by both our structure and SDS-PAGE analysis (Fig. 1d inset, Supplementary Fig. 12a). Searching the nuo operon database for purple phototrophic bacteria revealed that in addition to Rba. capsulatus, urf1 genes also exist in the purple nonsulfur bacterium Rba. sphaeroides and the aerobic anoxygenic phototrophs Roseobacter (Rsb.) denitrificans and D. shibae. Protein Urf1 (Type: PRK12373) in D. shibae was designated as full-length protein-O12 or protein-LRC13 whose partial N-terminal domain was also identified in their LH1–RC complexes but at a different position from that of protein-3h in Rdv. sulfidophilum (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Despite the lack of sequence similarities and the significantly different chain lengths among Urf1s (Supplementary Fig. 11d), the topological feature of a transmembrane U-shaped tandem-helix motif is conserved in all N-terminal structures determined or predicted for Urf1 proteins (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 13).

The Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1 complex

The Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1 complex is characterized by an open ring structure composed of 16 αβ-subunits and contains two carotenoids per αβ-subunit (Figs. 1 and 6). The complex is unique in this regard because LH1 complexes with known structures that contain more than 15 αβ-subunits all form closed ring arrangements12,29–32. The high carotenoid content in the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1 complex results in a tightly sealed LH1 “fence” (Supplementary Fig. 6b), leaving the LH1 opening as the only possible path for quinone transport between the RC and quinone pools (Fig. 6b). Pigment analyses revealed two major carotenoids in the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC complex: spheroidene and demethylspheroidene (Supplementary Fig. 5d), and both were identified in our high-resolution density map based on their distinct configurations. Spheroidenes were deeply embedded in the transmembrane region between the LH1 α- and β-polypeptides at similar positions and with a similar conformation as those in the LH1 complexes of other purple bacteria29,30,33. By contrast, demethylspheroidenes were located in between adjacent αβ-pairs, similar to their location in the LH1 complexes of many Rhodobacter species34–40 and adopted a largely bent conformation near the periplasmic surface (Fig. 6c, 6d). The demethylspheroidenes fill the space between adjacent αβ-subunits (Fig. 6b, Supplementary Fig. 6c) leading to blockage of the pores (channels) observed in LH1 complexes from purple bacteria that contain only one carotenoid per αβ-subunit. The Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1 consists of a single form of α- and β-polypeptides (Figs. 1b, 6c) containing 54 and 47 amino acid residues, respectively, as measured by TOF/MS (Supplementary Fig. 12b, 12c). The N-terminal Met residue is formylated in the α-polypeptides but not in the β-polypeptides where it is posttranslationally removed. Molecules of BChl a in Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1 forms a partially overlapping ring with average Mg–Mg distances of 9.2 Å within a dimer and 8.4 Å between dimers (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 6c). These intra- and inter-Mg–Mg distances are consistent with the finding that larger differences (≥0.8 Å) in the Mg–Mg distance tend to yield an LH1 Qy of ~880 nm31,41. The BChl a molecules in Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1 are ligated by histidine residues (αHis32 and βHis38), and the C3-acetyl oxygen atoms in BChl a form hydrogen bonds with αTrp43 and βTrp47 of their associated polypeptides as confirmed by the 1637 cm−1 signal from resonance Raman spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 3d).

Discussion

Rdv. sulfidophilum is a remarkable purple nonsulfur bacterium for several reasons. In addition to the fact that it is highly tolerant of sulfide—a property typically associated with purple sulfur bacteria—Rdv. sulfidophilum can respire a wide variety of organic compounds and shows essentially no repression of pigment synthesis under fully oxic conditions1,4. Moreover, biochemical studies revealed an unusual cytochrome subunit in the Rdv. sulfidophilum RC3,6. Collectively, this assortment of atypical properties motivated our high-resolution structural study of the topology and functioning of the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC.

The Rdv. sulfidophilum RC contains a Cyt subunit that contains three instead of four hemes and is anchored by its intact N-terminal domain in the membranes. Although triheme Cyt subunits are also present in the RCs of marine aerobic anoxygenic phototrophic bacteria12,13, the 6th axial ligand of Cys for the outmost heme-2 is unique to Rhodovulum species42 (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 7); this contrasts with a Met ligand for all other RC-bound triheme and tetraheme Cyt subunits. Substitution of the axial ligand from Met to Cys in heme-2 results in a drastic downshift in its reduction potential of heme-2 from +310 mV to –160 mV6, a value closer to the redox potentials of heme-1 in tetraheme Cyt subunits (–60 mV to +70 mV). In Rdv. sulfidophilum, two phylogenetically closely related Cyt proteins have been identified as electron donors to the RC Cyt subunit10,11. These are soluble Cyt c2 (Em = +358 mV) and membrane-bound Cyt c2m (Em = +369 mV); these proteins have a sequence identity of 54% for their functional domains, and both are physiologically capable of compensating for each other11. As a consequence of (i) the lack of heme-1, (ii) the downshift of the reduction potential of heme-2 and (iii) the discovery of a nonheme Fe–H2O cluster adjacent to heme-2 in the Rdv. sulfidophilum RC Cyt subunit (this work), four electron transfer pathways between the RC Cyt subunit and its electron donors can be envisioned, and each possibility is considered here, beginning with the most plausible one that incorporates all three of the above features.

In the absence of a heme-1, the difference in reduction potentials between heme-2 in the Rdv. sulfidophilum RC Cyt subunit and soluble Cyt c2 (or Cyt c2m) is about 525 mV. Overcoming this large uphill energy gap for electron transfer may be assisted by the nearby nonheme Fe–H2O cluster identified in our cryo-EM structure and confirmed by EPR measurement (Figs. 2c, 3, Supplementary Fig. 8c). That is, the nonheme Fe might function as a relay by accepting electrons from Cyt c2 and/or Cyt c2m and delivering them to heme-2. The His residue (His321) in the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit as a ligand to the Fe ion is also present in the RC-bound Cyt subunit of Rhodovulum strain KC1M42 but is not conserved in the Cyt subunits of other phototrophic purple bacteria including other Rhodovulum species, indicating that this electron transfer pathway may be limited to only certain Rhodovulum species.

Second, like the low-potential heme-1 in the RC-bound tetraheme Cyt subunits, the outmost low-potential heme-2 in the Rdv. sulfidophilum triheme Cyt subunit may accept electrons directly from Cyt c2 and/or Cyt c2m. This is consistent with a proposal that heme-2 or heme-4 (or both) in the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit may participate in electron transfer from its soluble partners6, and also with the observation that the heme-3 proximal to the special pair in the purple nonsulfur bacterium Blc. viridis RC cannot be directly reduced by exogenous Cyt c243. Numerous studies have convincingly shown that the outermost heme-1 in the RC tetraheme Cyt subunits is the direct acceptor from soluble electron donors including various c-type cytochromes8,43,44, and high-potential iron-sulfur proteins (HiPIP)45–47, and that the energetically uphill electron transfer from high-potential donors to the low-potential heme-1 is a common feature in phototrophic purple bacteria. Indeed, two structures of LH1–RC in complex with HiPIP have been determined for the purple sulfur bacteria Thermochromatium (Tch.) tepidum48 and Halorhodospira (Hlr.) halophila17, showing that HiPIP docks on the Cyt subunit surface near heme-1.

Third, alternative docking sites may exist in RC-bound triheme Cyt subunits. This is the case for a docking model of the D. shibae Cyt subunit–Cyt c2 complex12 predicted by AlphaFold3, in which Cyt c2 is attached to the surface of the Cyt subunit near heme-3. A similar result was obtained for the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit–Cyt c2 complex by AlphaFold3 (Supplementary Fig. 14). However, these docking models should be taken with caution because the AlphaFold confidence scores (ipTM, a measure of the accuracy of the predicted relative positions of the components forming the protein–protein complex) for D. shibae (0.71) and Rdv. sulfidophilum (0.61) fall into a “gray zone” (0.6–0.8) where predictions could be correct or incorrect49,50. In addition, even when computational predictions appear highly reliable, they can sometimes produce incorrect interaction modes between different domains in a single polypeptide; such was the case observed for the botulinum neurotoxin BoNT/A1 complex51. (Note: the subsequently updated AlphaFold can predict correct interaction modes.) Further tests of the AlphaFold docking performance revealed that it tends to give higher ipTM scores for the experimentally determined structures with correct relative positions and orientations, for example, Tch. tepidum RC Cyt subunit–HiPIP (ipTM = 0.80) and Hlr. halophila Cyt subunit–HiPIP (ipTM = 0.89) but gives lower scores for those lacking known docking structures, such as for Blc. viridis RC Cyt subunit–Cyt c2 (ipTM = 0.74) and Rvi. gelatinosus Cyt subunit–Cyt c8 (ipTM = 0.73) and Cyt subunit–HiPIP (ipTM = 0.75). Despite these uncertainties, all experimentally determined and AlphaFold predicted Cyt subunit docking sites are located on the surface of the protein near heme-1.

Fourth, multiple docking sites or electron transfer pathways may exist in the Rdv. sulfidophilum RC and operate individually and/or simultaneously depending on environmental conditions (temperature, pH, oxygen, salinity, and types and concentrations of electron donors). It has been shown that the reduction potentials of HiPIPs can be modulated by local environments (hydrophobicity, solvation) near the 4Fe–4S cluster52 and by variations in experimental conditions53. For example, the iso-2 HiPIP from the extreme halophile Hlr. halophila exhibited a significantly higher potential (+140 mV) upon binding to the RC compared with that (+50 mV) of the isolated protein measured at low salt concentrations53. The isolated HiPIP potential also shifted to a more positive value at high salt concentrations53. These findings indicate that reduction potentials of soluble electron donors can be substantially modified by the properties of the interface formed with their electron-transferring partners, and this may have ecological relevance for Rdv. sulfidophilum. For example, in addition to inorganic sulfur compounds such as sulfide (Em HS–/SO42–, –220 mV), Rdv. sulfidophilum can use dimethylsulfide (DMS), the most abundant organosulfur compound in marine environments, as an electron donor for phototrophic growth54. The Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt c2 was demonstrated to be the mediator accepting electrons derived from the oxidation of DMS to dimethylsulfoxide (Em = +230 mV) catalyzed by dimethylsulfide dehydrogenase55. Thus, Rdv. sulfidophilum must be equipped to accept electrons from donors of widely different reduction potentials and may accomplish this by varying its expression of electron acceptors and/or varying the interface between donor and any given acceptor. In contrast to the negatively charged surface of the RC Cyt subunit, the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt c2 has a positively charged surface near its heme opening (Supplementary Fig. 14c), indicating a potential electrostatic interaction between the two proteins. However, since surface charge distribution can be affected by pH48 and the local charge distributions around the heme groups in the Rdv. sulfidophilum Cyt subunit varies from the most negative heme-4 to the less negative heme-2 to the relatively neutral heme-3 (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 8a, 8b), we hypothesize that the activity of Cyt c2 is flexible and that the protein interacts with the most suitable electron-accepting partners under a given physiological condition.

The Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1 complex formed an open ring structure consisting of 16 αβ-subunits with a gap where the N-terminal transmembrane domain of the RC Cyt subunit and protein-3h are located (Figs. 1a, 1b, 6a). The Rdv. sulfidophilum protein-3h is the N-terminal fragment of the gene product Urf1 whose complete form is composed of N-terminal helices and a C-terminal compact domain connected by a long loop region. Notably, the loop region in Urf1 contains high contents of Ala (25.3%) and Pro (17.7%) (Supplementary Fig. 11d), a characteristic of unstructured long loops as reported for the Rdv. sulfidophilum membrane-bound Cyt c2m11 and the Rts. salexigens RC H-subunit18. Although urf1 gene homologs exist in genomes of other purple phototrophic bacteria, the homologs lack sequence similarity in the N-terminal transmembrane domains and differ significantly in length as reflected by their potential gene products that range from 70 (Rba. sphaeroides) to 239 (D. shibae) amino acids. The shortest Rba. sphaeroides Urf1 was predicted to have only a U-shaped transmembrane helical structure (Supplementary Fig. 13), followed by the Rba. capsulatus Urf1 that has an additional short helix with an extended loop but lacks the C-terminal compact domain. Similar to the Rdv. sulfidophilum Urf1, the Urf1s in Rsb. denitrificans and D. shibae contain a C-terminal compact domain that shows relatively high sequence similarities (Supplementary Fig. 11d). Although the functions of Urf1 proteins are unknown, they may play a role in regulating and assembling subunits of complex I28, the redox complex that forms NADH from ubiquinols during photoautotrophic and photoheterotrophic growth in Rba. capsulatus56. If so, differences in the length and topology of Urf1s from anaerobic to aerobic phototrophs likely reflect an evolutionary drift.

Despite these diversities, for all Urf1s examined in our work, the minimal structural motif is the N-terminal transmembrane U-shaped helices (Supplementary Fig. 13) whose conformation resembles that of protein-U (or protein-Y) in the Rba. sphaeroides LH1–RC34,37,57 as pointed out previously12. Despite different genetic origins and lack of sequence similarity between Urf1 and protein-U, protein-3h (Rdv. sulfidophilum), protein-O (D. shibae), and protein-U (Rba. sphaeroides) are all assembled into their LH1–RC complexes in the same orientation, and protein-O and protein-U are located at the same positions (Supplementary Fig. 11b)12. Therefore, these findings suggest that these U-shaped transmembrane proteins (or protein fragments) may function as “plugs” for filling into the cavities (sockets) of their LH1–RCs as a structural means of strengthening the entire LH1–RC complex.

Structural studies have revealed an incredible diversity in the architecture of purple bacterial LH1–RC photocomplexes. Although there are many common themes, there are also a host of variations that likely reflect the fine-tuning (or in some cases major alterations) necessary for a particular species to be competitive in its ecological niche. Indeed, the structural diversity of purple bacterial photocomplexes is a clear sign that even subtle environmental differences likely require modifications to the photosynthetic apparatus. In the case of Rdv. sulfidophilum, our results suggest that an open LH1–RC ring containing a previously unrecognized auxiliary protein and a triheme RC Cyt subunit is best suited to photosynthetic success in its marine environment.

Methods

Preparation and characterization of the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC complex

Cells of Rdv. sulfidophilum type strain W4 (DSM 1374T) were cultivated phototrophically (anoxic/light) as described previously5 at pH 7.0 and 25 ˚C for 7 days under incandescent light (60 W). Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5353 ×g for 10 min, and chromatophores were obtained by sonication (Ultrasonic Homogenizer JY92-IIN, SCENTZ, China) in 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) followed by ultracentrifugation (OptimaTM L-100 XP Ultracentrifuge, BECKMAN COULTER, USA) at 208,000 ×g for 90 min. Chromatophores were treated with 1.0% (w/v) n-dodecyl β-D-maltopyranoside (DDM) at 25 ˚C for 70 min followed by centrifugation at 4 ˚C and 208,000 ×g for 90 min. The extracts were loaded onto a DEAE column equilibrated with 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) containing 0.05% w/v DDM. The LH1–RC fractions were eluted by a linear gradient of NaCl from 50 mM to 250 mM. Fractions with a ratio of R883/280 (maximal absorbance of Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1 is at 883 nm) greater than 1.8 were collected and concentrated by Amicon 30,000 (MWCO) filters (Millipore). The purified Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC was analyzed by absorption (UV-1900i, Shimadzu), CD (J-720w, Jasco), light-induced P+/P absorption difference (V-780, Jasco), and Raman (NRS-7100, Jasco) spectroscopies (Supplementary Fig. 3). Masses of the LH1 polypeptides were measured by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectroscopy (MALDI-TOF/MS)58. Carotenoids were analyzed by HPLC59 using a μBondapak C18 column (Waters) and a photodiode array detector (Shimadzu). Quinones were extracted from the purified LH1–RC and chromatophores and analyzed using reverse-phase HPLC60. Phospholipids extracted from chromatophores were analyzed by 31P-NMR61. SDS-PAGE was conducted with 10–20% gradient gels, which were stained with CBB G250 or 3, 3’, 5, 5’ tetramethylbenzidine–H2O2 for heme detection.

EPR spectroscopy measurements

For X-band EPR measurements, a Bruker EMX spectrometer with a resonator (ER 4122SHQ, Bruker, DE) and a gas flow temperature control system (ESR900, Oxford Instruments, Oxford, GB) was used. EPR simulation was performed using EasySpin 6.062. For EPR measurements, the following samples were used: sucrose-density-gradient purified LH1–RC (untreated); LH1–RC containing 100 µM ascorbate; the LH1–RC treated with 10 mM EDTA followed by extensive washing with 20 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.5) containing 0.05% w/v DDM (EDTA-treated); EDTA-treated LH1–RC containing 100 µM ascorbate.

Cryo-EM data collection

The purified Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC complex (5 µL, OD883 = 60) was applied to glow-discharged holey Nitinol grids (M01Au300-R1.2/1.3, Nanodim) previously treated with Ar and O2 mixtures in a Solarus plasma cleaner (Gatan, Pleasanton, USA) for 80 s. The grids were then plunged into liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen using a FEI Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) under the following conditions: blotting time 3.5 s, waiting time 5 s, blotting force 3, humidity 100%, and temperature 4˚C. Initial cryo-EM data were collected on a Titan Krios G1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) electron microscope at 300 kV equipped with a Falcon3 camera (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology Graduate University (OIST). Final high-resolution datasets were acquired using a 300 kV Titan Krios G4 electron microscope equipped with a Falcon4 camera (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at Okayama University, Japan. A total of 8063 movies were collected at a magnification of 96,000 for the LH1–RC particles. The pixel size for each micrograph was 0.727 Å. Each frame was exposed for 0.213 s with a total exposure time of 6.80 s and a total dose rate of 50 e−/Å2.

Image processing

All stacked frames were subjected to motion correction, and defocus was estimated using cryoSPARC63. A total of 439,166 particles were selected from 8,063 micrographs using crYOLO64. All picked particles were further analyzed with cryoSPARC, and 234,971 particles were selected by 2-D classification and divided into four classes by 3-D classification, resulting in three good classes containing 108,964 particles. The initial 3-D model was generated by cryoSPARC. The non-uniform refinement without any imposed symmetry (C1) produced a map at 1.81 Å resolution after contrast transfer function refinement, masking, and post-processing, according to the gold-standard Fourier shell correlation using a criterion of 0.14365,66. The local resolution maps were calculated on cryoSPARC (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Model building and refinement of the LH1–RC complex

The atomic model of the Tch. tepidum LH1–RC (PDB: 5Y5S) was fitted to the cryo-EM map obtained for the Rdv. sulfidophilum LH1–RC using UCSF Chimera67. Amino acid substitutions and real-space refinement for the peptides and cofactors were performed using COOT68. Whole regions of protein-3h and LH1 αβ-subunit were modeled ab-initio based on their density. The manually modified model was refined in real space on PHENIX69, and the COOT/PHENIX refinement was iterated until the refinements converged. Finally, the statistics calculated using MolProbity70 were checked. Figures were drawn with the Pymol Molecular Graphic System (Ver2.5, Schrödinger)71, UCSF Chimera67, and Chimera-X72.

Statistics and reproducibility

Experimental errors for all spectroscopic data were within 1 nm. Experimental errors for the HPLC chromatogram were within 0.1 min. The cryo-EM data were collected from multiple grids. Micrographs contaminated with hexagonal ice were excluded from the reconstruction dataset through visual inspection. Data collection, processing, and refinement statistics are summarized in Table 1. All RMSD values for Cα positions between superimposed structures were calculated using PyMOL.
