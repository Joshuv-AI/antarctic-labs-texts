Asymmetric tripolar nerve cuff electrode designs for optimizing anodal block of myelinated fibers
Authors: Daniel Tovbis, Ryan G. L. Koh, Rania Jeong, Paul B. Yoo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAnodal block is a common mechanism used to generate unidirectional activation of myelinated nerve fibers. However, nerve block is often inconsistent and difficult to achieve. In this study, we used a computational model to investigate electrode cuff designs that can be exploited to enhance anodal block.MethodsAsymmetric bipolar and tripolar nerve cuff electrodes were initially tested and validated in a rodent sciatic nerve model. A finite element model was implemented, with physical dimensions approximating a rat sciatic nerve implanted with a flat-interface nerve electrode. The McIntyre–Richardson–Grill model was used to predict the activation, block, and reactivation thresholds of myelinated fibers (diameter = 5.7 to 16 μm) as the stimulation amplitude was increased. In vivo experiments were performed using identical nerve cuff electrodes. The asymmetric electrode designs were subsequently investigated using a human-sized model of a nerve implanted with a cylindrical cuff electrode. Key design parameters included the electrode edge length (EEL), anodic contact width, inter-electrode distance, and current division between primary and secondary anodes.ResultsThe rodent model reproduced blocking properties in both computational and in vivo studies, where maximum anodic block reached 88% and 75% of activated fibers, respectively. Simulations involving the human nerve model identified two critical design parameters that can markedly enhance anodal block. An optimal distal EEL (≤ 1 mm) enhanced the performance of bipolar stimulation (anodic block = 91%), whereas, optimizing the current ratio between the primary:secondary anodes (e.g., 6:4) enhanced nerve block in asymmetric tripolar electrodes (anodic block = 90%).ConclusionThis study presents a set of instructions for optimizing the anodal blocking properties of nerve cuff electrodes, where an asymmetric tripolar design with variable anodic current offers a novel method that can adjust for variability at the nerve electrode interface.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12938-026-01579-6.

Introduction

Depending on the intended functional or clinical outcome, electrical stimulation of peripheral nerves may require that only a specific subset of nerve fibers is activated. This notion of selective nerve stimulation is context dependent and will vary depending on the location of activated fibers or fascicles within the nerve (spatial selectivity), the diameter of the activated fibers (fiber selectivity), or specific end-organ function (functional selectivity). These properties are particularly relevant for mixed nerves containing multiple fiber types that neurologically control numerous functional targets, such as the vagus nerve [1]. In this study, we investigated the feasibility of improving directional nerve stimulation which is commonly referred to as selective generation of nerve action potentials travelling in a primarily afferent or efferent direction. The ability to achieve directional stimulation could have significant impact on clinically treating tremor or lower urinary tract dysfunction [2, 3] and there is ongoing interest in translating directional nerve stimulation for vagus nerve stimulation therapies [4, 5].

It has been shown that directional stimulation can be achieved via manipulation of stimulation parameters; for example, by using kilohertz block [6], or by modifying the shape of the stimulating pulse by the addition of quazi-trapezoidal falling edges or depolarizing pre-pulses [7]. These methods are well-demonstrated in silico as well as in vivo. A simpler method of achieving directional stimulation employs anodal block, in which monophasic pulses are used to hyperpolarize the area of the nerve under the anode [8]. Anodal block has been shown to be a viable method of achieving directional stimulation but may require high current amplitudes [5, 9]. When considering the anodal block approach, specific electrode designs meant to optimize the arrest of nerve action potentials have been of interest. For example, an asymmetric bipolar design was able to minimize current injected to achieve anodal block [10]. A shorter inter-electrode distance was shown to reduce onset response during high-frequency block [11], and multi-electrode arrays have been used to demonstrate changes in the activating function (AF) that augment anodal block [12–14].

Given the myriad factors that can affect nerve activation or blocking by nerve cuff electrodes, the objective of this study was to identify aspects of electrode design that could improve unidirectional stimulation of peripheral nerves by means of suppressing reactivation at the virtual cathode. In addition to common bipolar designs, we tested novel asymmetric tripolar configurations that consisted of a distal secondary anode designed to suppress the virtual cathode arising from the primary anode. To this end, a computational model with individual spatially distributed myelinated fibers (diameter = 5.7 to 16 µm) was first implemented and validated in a rat sciatic nerve. This methodology was then tested computationally in a larger (human-sized) model to further investigate the influence of different electrode configurations and design parameters on the activation, blocking, and reactivation thresholds of the modeled fibers.

Results

Unidirectional nerve stimulation was investigated by comparing bipolar and asymmetric tripolar configurations, where the nerve cuff electrode consisted of a proximal cathode and distally located anodic contacts (refer to Fig. 8). The distal anodes were used to optimize the nerve blocking properties of the tripolar configuration, and to also define different pairs of bipolar stimulating configurations: long bipole (cathode + primary anode) and short bipole (cathode + secondary anode). The various patterns of activation, block, and re-activation of myelinated nerve fibers (Fig. 1A) were studied as the electrode and stimulation parameters were varied, such as the anodal contact size, inter-contact distance, and electrode edge length. The asymmetric tripole was further optimized by splitting the return current passing through the primary and secondary anodes. The effectiveness of these design parameters were initially tested in rodents and then extrapolated to a larger model of a monofascicular human nerve.

Rat computational model

A finite element model of the rat sciatic nerve was created in COMSOL to generate voltage profiles for use in a McIntyre–Richardson–Grill (MRG) model to simulate monopolar anodic stimulation and block in a nerve cuff electrode. Anodic stimulation was used as the reference for neural activation to confirm that block did not occur in unexpected regions, ensuring that any observed block was strictly a function of our design parameters. These computer simulations yielded sigmoidal recruitment curves that were indicative of inverse-recruitment of myelinated nerve fibers (Fig. 1B, C refer to Fig. 8 for the cuff electrode configuration). As the amplitude was further increased beyond maximum fiber activation, the percentage of activated fibers began to decrease as anodal block progressively reduced the number of action potentials propagating past the distal anodes. A subsequent increase in the recruitment curve indicated an increase in the number of fibers being reactivated. Anodal block was achieved among the six electrode configurations (refer to Table 3) at both pulse widths tested, but with varying degrees of effectiveness. Stimulating with a 1.0-ms pulse width was able to produce greater maximum block in all cases, though the bipolar electrodes and tripole (1:9) were sensitive to pulse width. As shown in Fig. 1E, the long bipole and several of the tripolar variants (1:9, 5:5, 6:4) were able to block the highest proportion of fibers (range = 76% to 89%) at stimulation amplitudes close to 1 mA with pulse widths of 1.0 ms. One of the tripolar electrodes (9:1) reached peak nerve block (72%) before exhibiting fiber reactivation beyond 0.7 mA. The short bipole performed the worst reaching only 18% nerve block at 0.5 mA. When comparing the two bipolar configurations, the short variant failed to block small- to medium-sized fibers (5.7 µm, 7.3 and 8.7 µm) that were located further away from the anodic contact. These results suggest that either a longer inter-electrode distance (IED) or shorter electrode edge length (EEL) at the distal edge of the electrode correlate with blocking myelinated fibers. There were some differences between the electrode variants observed at the two differing pulse widths. The long bipolar and tripole (1:9) design both performed well at 1.0 ms, but were among the worst performers at 0.5 ms. This suggests that the choice of electrode design may be dependent on pulse width, though this investigation is outside the scope of this work.

As part of these simulations, we occasionally observed two unexpected phenomena: “bidirectional block”, where the cathodal virtual anode began to block fibers prior to reactivation by the anodal virtual cathode, and “distal block”, where this occurred after reactivation by the anodal virtual cathode. The conditions under which these alternate block thresholds were observed are provided in supplementary table T1. We note that it may be possible for these conditions to arise further outside the parameters explored by this study.

Experimental validation of the rat model

The rat sciatic nerve was used to validate the findings seen in the rat computational model above. Compared to monopolar anodic stimulation, a stimulation configuration that does not generate nerve block, (Fig. 2A), evidence of nerve block was observed in each configuration tested (Fig. 2B–D). Among the parameters tested (Fig. 2E), the longer PW (1 ms) appeared to have the largest effect on maximizing anodal block where both bipolar and tripolar electrodes reached a maximum decrease in RMS (range = 65% to 75%) at amplitudes beyond 0.8 mA. Similar to the computational work, the long bipole and tripole (1:9) showed large differences in performance at the two different pulse widths. Interestingly, there were differences observed between the metrics of maximum average block and average maximum block, indicating that max block was achieved at different amplitudes in different animals (Table 1). Notably, unlike the computational model, the average maximum block of the short bipole were not significantly worse than other electrode designs, with a maximum block of 66% at 1.0 ms, within 2% of all configurations except the long bipole, though its maximum average block of 42.5% at 1 ms was lower than tripole (1:9) and (9:1), (60% and 50%, respectively). Splitting the anodal current did not have a significant effect in the rodent model (Fig. 2E, Table 1). The two metrics showed consistently high performance for the long bipole and tripole (1:9), indicating that similar degrees of block occurred at similar amplitudes. Analysis of the EMG signals showed that our post-stimulus time window was consistent with the estimated latency range for large myelinated fibers (e.g., 10–18 µm, conduction velocity = 49–89 m/s, signal onset = 1.5–2.8 ms, n = 13).

Kolmogorov–Smirnov analysis of the results showed that the maximum block values and current amplitudes did not follow a normal distribution, so data were analyzed for significant differences using the Friedman test. No significant differences were observed between the different electrode designs for maximum decreases in RMS at 0.5 ms (p = 0.9208), nor for the currents at which they occurred (p = 0.7480). Similar results were determined for the 1.0 ms pulse width (max decrease in RMS, p = 0.6380, current at max decrease, p = 0.2227). However, comparing results between the 0.5 and 1.0 ms pulse widths showed statistical significance (p < 0.001), indicating that pulse duration has a key effect on block performance. Interestingly, no significant difference was found for the current required for maximum block between 1.0 ms and 0.5 ms (p = 0.1730), further supporting the importance of the pulse width in blocking nerve fibers.

Overall, while the experimental results fell short of statistical significance, they reproduced several key trends present in the computational model: the strong effect of pulse width on stimulation outcomes, superior performance of the long bipole at 1.0 ms, and the similar distribution of average maximum block (comparing Figs. 1C and 2E).

Histological analysis of nerve sections showed variability in the neuroanatomy of the rat sciatic nerve (Fig. 3). There was an average of 1.8 ± 0.4 and 4.0 ± 0.8 fascicles identified at the proximal and distal ends of the implanted nerve cuff electrode, respectively. The largest fascicle (i.e., tibial nerve) had a mean cross-sectional area of 1.27 ± 0.11 mm2, which was consistent with the single fascicle used in the rat computational model (1.24 mm2). The mean cross-sectional area of the tibial fascicle in vivo decreased between the proximal (1.59 ± 0.09 mm2) and distal (0.98 ± 0.07 mm2) ends of the cuff electrode. The tibial fascicle was on average three times larger in surface area than the next-largest fascicle under the cathode, and twice as large as the next-largest fascicle under the anode. Human computational model

The feasibility of translating the anodic blocking properties demonstrated in the rat sciatic nerve model was tested in a generic human peripheral nerve model, starting with a bipolar electrode design (Fig. 8B). As shown in Fig. 4A, reducing the length of the anode from 1 mm to 0.1 mm (10:1 ratio) resulted in a 12% increase in the maximum block percentage. Using this modified (10:1) ratio (Fig. 4B), we found that setting the IED at 1 mm achieved a good balance of improving anodal block (14% improvement over IED = 2 mm) while achieving peak nerve recruitment at 1 mA (vs. 2 mA at IED = 0.5 mm).Fig. 4Computational model of bipolar stimulation of a human-sized nerve, where optimized parameters were carried over to the next set of simulations (panels A to E). With the IED set at 1 mm, subsequent simulations were performed to examine the effects of EEL. As illustrated in Fig. 4C, symmetrically reducing the EEL from 2.5 mm to 1 mm enhanced anodal block by 33% at the cost of requiring higher stimulation amplitudes (maximum block current increased from 3.7 mA to 7.8 mA). These results suggested (1) an increase in current leakage at the proximal end of the cuff (proximal EEL) and (2) beneficial changes in the blocking properties at the distal end (distal EEL). Symmetrically reducing the EEL to 0.2 mm resulted in very poor nerve recruitment properties.

With previous design parameters maintained (10:1 ratio, IED = 1 mm, distal EEL = 2.5 mm), independently increasing the proximal EEL from 1 to 5 mm resulted in (1) a decrease in the current needed to reach peak fiber recruitment (1.5 mA vs. 1 mA) and (2) improvement in maximum block from 18% (2.6 mA) to 52.4% (4.9 mA). As shown in Fig. 4D, setting the proximal EEL at 2.5 mm provided a balance of nerve fiber recruitment (peak at 1 mA), anodal block (38% at 3.5 mA), and overall cuff length. The 5 mm proximal EEL setting, for example, would allow for a 31% increase in maximum block, but at the cost of a 43% increase in current. With the proximal EEL set at 2.5 mm (10:1 ratio and IED = 1 mm) in Fig. 4E, we found that the distal EEL could alter the maximum nerve block from as low as 25% (distal EEL = 3.75 mm) to as high as 90% (distal EEL = 1 mm). Consistent across this analysis of bipolar electrode configurations, maximizing anodal block required notably large stimulation amplitudes (10 mA for distal EEL = 1 mm). This location was selected based on preliminary simulations (data not shown) that showed the peak AF of the virtual cathode occurred near the distal edge of the cuff. A similar trend was observed in the tripolar form of Design 3, but maximum nerve block was not reached at the limit of stimulation (10 mA). Discussion

In this study, we performed computational simulations of extraneural stimulation of peripheral nerves with spatially distributed myelinated fibers of varying diameters. This model was used to initially test and validate cuff electrode designs aimed at optimizing anodal block using a rat sciatic nerve model. We examined asymmetric bipolar and tripolar configurations in the rodent model, which were able to achieve significant nerve block in both computational (76% to 88% block) and experimental (65% to 75% block) studies. The feasibility of potentially translating these results in humans was tested in a computational model, where we identified several key factors that contribute to anodal block. These included the IED, proximal EEL, distal EEL, and the addition of a secondary anode. When optimized both bipolar and tripolar configurations in the computational model were able to block up to 90% of nerve fibers of a human-sized peripheral nerve. This novel asymmetric tripolar design was introduced with the initial hypothesis that a secondary anode could suppress the virtual cathode generated by bipolar electrodes. Unexpectedly, we found that anodal block of the tripolar electrode can be controlled by adjusting the current between the primary and secondary anodes; our results here suggest that an even split of currents is not a good candidate for anodal block, as the 5:5 current split performed poorest in the human-sized nerve and was among the weakest performers in the rat models, requiring higher currents to initiate block. It was also identified that a bipolar electrode with a minimized distal edge length of 1 mm could block fibers was equally as effective as the tripolar design. Taken together, anodal block can be optimized by either strategically placing a primary anode near the distal edge of the insulating cuff or by employing an optional secondary anode that can suppress neural re-excitation by fine-tuning the anodal currents. If implemented as a multi-contact cuff electrode, this latter approach may serve as a backup in case the bipolar configuration fails to achieve effective nerve block post-implant.

It is interesting that our optimized bipolar electrode design (Fig. 4) shared similarities in the relative placement of electrode contacts which were originally proposed by Sweeney and Mortimer [10]. However, in lieu of (1) increasing the inner diameter of the cuff and (2) using quasi-trapezoidal waveforms, we demonstrated that optimal selection of the distal EEL can play a critical role in achieving anodal block of larger myelinated fibers. Using the activating function to locate the emergence of virtual cathodes (Rattay [17] and McIntyre and Grill [18]), we were able to mitigate re-activation of nerve fibers by adjusting the distal EEL and/or placing the secondary anode. This also extended the work by Van den Honert and Mortimer [19] by further enhancing anodal block through manipulation of the electric field outside the nerve cuff.

The topic of anodal block has been the subject of research for many decades [20], and seminal work that described electrode designs (e.g., asymmetric electrode configurations) were instrumental in improving our understanding of how electric fields and stimulation parameters can be manipulated to induce unidirectional action potential propagation [5, 10, 14, 21]. Recently, sophisticated computational models have been employed to study alternative stimulation methods. For example, improvements in high-frequency nerve stimulation block were demonstrated in bipolar electrodes by using narrower IEDs, varying by fiber diameter and location [11, 22]. Another computational study attempting to generate an optimized electrode array for spatially selective VNS in sheep found that an electrode separation of 3 mm was optimal, while values either above or below that value resulted in either higher activation thresholds or impracticalities given the size of the nerve [23]. Studies such as these motivate the use of computational modeling to investigate improvements in anodal block for achieving in vivo unidirectional activation of peripheral nerve fibers.

In our study, we generated a computational model of a FINE instrumented on a rat sciatic nerve as well as a cylindrical nerve cuff electrode implanted on a human-sized nerve. We chose to employ the MRG model in combination with COMSOL-derived voltage profiles, as these methods are well-studied and validated in the context of predicting neural thresholds both in rats and humans [24]. When comparing the outcomes of human and rat computational models, analogous design parameters were able to achieve similar levels of anodal block; for instance, a shorter distal EEL was found to be beneficial in both the rat and the human model. Current splitting, however, did not have a significant effect in the rat when compared to the human model. Rat sciatic nerves differ from human nerves not only in size but also in the distribution of fiber diameters, showing a more uniform distribution of fiber diameters while the human model, which took proportions from the vagus nerve, contains a relatively greater proportion of smaller fibers [25, 26]. Additionally, due to the smaller size of the rat sciatic nerve and its compression by the FINE, there is less variance in the thresholds between deeper and shallower fibers. It is likely due to these conditions that current splitting in the rat sciatic nerve had little-to-no effect. While we did not validate the results of the human model in vivo in this study, our computational model and its similarities and differences to the rat can provide the basis for hypotheses in future in vivo experiments in larger animal models.

Previous studies have shown that rat sciatic motor neurons consist mainly of diameters between 6.5 and 13 µm, which reflect both the values used in our model as well as the sizes calculated via the latency of the observed EMG signals [26]. Two main observations were made when comparing in vivo results with the computational rat model: (1) the computational model achieved greater level of anodal block and (2) fiber reactivation (≤ 1 mA) in the computational model occurred with PW = 0.5 ms, but not in vivo. These could be attributed to idealized conditions employed in the model; specifically the direct and complete contact between the stimulating electrode and the epineurium surface. Imperfect contact due to saline or connective tissue would result in higher activation and blocking thresholds, and therefore higher reactivation thresholds.

Our in vivo experiments did not reveal statistically significant differences between electrode configurations at either pulse width (Friedman test: 0.5 ms, p = 0.9208; 1.0 ms, p = 0.6380). The absence of significant differences between the current-split tripolar designs was anticipated from computational results, which predicted similar block performance across configurations at both pulse widths. We did expect the tripolar designs and the long bipole to outperform the short bipole at 1.0 ms, but this was not observed in practice. While performance trends consistent with our model were observed, the means and standard errors in Table 1 indicate large inter-animal variability. Interestingly, there was also no significant difference in stimulation amplitudes required for maximum block at 0.5 and 1.0 ms, though there was a significant difference in the maximum block achieved, indicating that longer pulse widths may improve maximum block without significantly increasing required currents. Future experiments will employ larger cohorts formally powered to detect clinically relevant effect sizes, with ENG recording at both ends of the stimulating cuff to provide a direct and fiber-specific measure of block performance.

Our model was implemented to generate recruitment curves for a population of myelinated fibers of varying diameters distributed within the nerve trunk. This enabled determination of optimal designs for blocking and activating fibers of specified diameters. It is important to note that fibers of different diameters are activated and blocked at different amplitudes, and peak block for fibers of different sizes occurred at different amplitudes in our study. Since our nerve-model is non-specific, we have reported block of overall fibers rather than splitting block proportions by fiber diameter. Next, our model was limited in its exploration of alternative activation paradigms; for instance, the phenomenon of “bidirectional block”, where the cathodic virtual anodes cause block prior to reactivation at the anodic virtual cathode; or “distal block”, where this occurs after reactivation and results in distal stimulation with proximal block. While we listed these phenomena when they were observed to occur within the study parameters, they were not always present, which could indicate their susceptibility to electrode design differences, or an inexhaustive set of stimulation parameters explored. These alternative behaviors should be explored and quantified more thoroughly in future work.

Although outside the scope of our study, our modeling methodology could be expanded to design electrodes for unidirectional activation of B fibers. B-fiber thresholds are typically 2.5–5 times higher than those of the A-fibers [27, 28], and unidirectional activation of these smaller diameter fibers would be considered desirable for reduction of off-target effects associated with the activation of large motor fibers [29–32]. However, calculating B-fiber thresholds directly in the current implementation of the model poses a significant problem, as computation time scales exponentially with decreasing fiber diameters due to the larger number of nodes of Ranvier. Therefore, B-fiber thresholds could be estimated at 2.5–5 times peak A-fiber activation amplitudes. This would enable development of electrode configurations with maximum A-fiber block at amplitudes corresponding to maximum B-fiber activation.

Our human computational model, which consisted of large, myelinated fibers consistent with the human vagus nerve, showed that maximum block is achieved at relatively higher amplitudes with > 4 mA being required for block of > 50% of fibers. However, clinical VNS is typically limited to amplitudes of 3–4 mA [33–35], which suggests that complete block of myelinated fibers (and thereby unidirectional stimulation) may be challenging with existing nerve electrodes, such as helical VNS electrodes which are susceptible to current leakage at high stimulation amplitudes [36, 37]. Another potential limitation of clinical translation is the need for multipolar current sources to provide current splitting for tripolar configurations, as only some implantable pulse generators [38], are capable of providing this in a clinical setting. While not a goal of this study, our safety analysis found that the charge/charge density ratios at current levels required for peak block would be higher than the Shannon safety limit. Future studies may further explore methods of reducing blocking thresholds by combining design factors that are conducive to reduced activation thresholds (longer IED and proximal EEL) with those conducive to anodal block (tripolar designs, narrower anodes, lowered distal EEL), and may additionally focus on identifying designs which provide maximized block at safe therapeutic ranges.

Our computational study contains certain limitations and simplifications. First, the model assumed a monofascicular geometry, which is considered atypical for most of the course of the human vagus [39] and rodent sciatic nerves [40]. It has been argued that the choice of specific fascicular geometry is irrelevant for comparative purposes, as geometry in any one subject cannot be known a priori and thus any fascicular geometry is valid [23]. Given that the tibial fascicle dominated the nerve cross-sections obtained as part of our histological analysis, the monofascicular approximation may be considered appropriate as long as its limitations are acknowledged. Indeed, the blocking properties of unidirectional stimulation that were observed in our rat experiments matched the predictions made by the computational model. However, the post-mortem samples of the rat sciatic nerve obtained in this study confirmed multiple fascicles within the region where the stimulating FINE was implanted. Activation thresholds are known to be lower in multifascicular nerves with smaller fascicles [41], although the effects of size and number of fascicles on anodal blocking properties have not been investigated.

Furthermore, the undulating nature of myelinated fibers could also potentially influence the activation and blocking thresholds [42]. Future computational studies involving anatomically realistic nerve cross-sections—in human, rodent and larger animal models—may be able to assess the accuracy of our findings [43]. In future work, sophisticated nerve modeling pipelines such as ASCENT [44] may be used to model blocking of multifascicular nerves. Finally, while we tested extensive sets of various electrode parameters, only three distinct tripolar designs were tested. It may be that step-based optimization of tripolar design parameters may provide further improvements to anodal block efficiency in tripolar configurations. Similar optimizations have been conducted by assigning weighted cost functions combining parameters such as selectivity and efficiency to determine an overall “best” configuration. Future work may investigate these tripolar designs in a more granular fashion.

A limitation of our in vivo experiments was the use of distal EMG activity as a marker for nerve fiber activation. While the computational model we used is limited to large, myelinated motor axons, which would be the cause of EMG activity, it is not known what direct contribution an individual motor axon has to the recorded amplitude of an EMG signal. Previous studies have attempted to define this relationship in drosophila [45], but data were not found for the rat sciatic nerve. To create a more accurate model, EMG recordings can be paired with single-axon stimulation to quantify twitch amplitudes and fiber diameters in response to stimulation. Other alternatives to quantify the performance of the computational model could be to record neural activity at both sides of the stimulating cuff, or to use physiological markers of nerve activation where appropriate [5]. Additionally, the potential effects of anodal break were not tested in this study, but future studies could employ methods such as quasi-trapezoidal pulses to confirm whether action potentials are elicited after a strong hyperpolarizing current pulse [46]. We used monophasic pulses for stimulation and blocking as their characteristics are much simpler and thus make it easier to tease out the effects of the asymmetric electrode with the primary and secondary anode as well as the current split. In practice, an asymmetric biphasic waveform could be used instead to maintain charge balance [47], while providing the effects seen in this study. Furthermore, different electrode materials such as conductive polymers (i.e., poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) (PEDOT)) or carbon nanotubes could be used to enhance the charge injection capacity and reduce impedance [48–50]. Finally, we used monopolar anodic stimulation as a comparator as a configuration where block is not expected to occur. It has been demonstrated in previous work [10] that monopolar cathodic stimulation can also result in anodic block and thus could be used as another comparator; however, most clinically used implanted peripheral nerve stimulators are bipolar due to the ease of current steering and lowered risk of off-target effects [51]. Our “standard” in the performance sense was therefore our basic bipolar cuff, with the monopolar anode serving as the “0 block” comparator.

Conclusion

Our work introduces and validates a computational model of peripheral nerve stimulation that can predict ranges of activation and anodal block using various electrode configurations and stimulation parameters. Based on our results, the asymmetric tripole, or a bipole with a minimized distal EEL, are potential design motifs that could enable selective activation of smaller diameter fibers which may have relevance for treating chronic heart failure, urinary voiding dysfunction, or epilepsy [52–54]. This model can be employed in iterative measures to design future electrodes that can achieve unidirectional stimulation under a variety of anatomic conditions. Selective stimulation is expected to enhance the efficacy of neuromodulation therapies by way of limiting adverse side-effects, while optimizing electrode design parameters should facilitate nerve block within desired stimulation amplitudes. Further translational research in larger animal models is needed to identify specific designs suitable for clinical use.

Generation of fiber recruitment curves

COMSOL was used to solve for the extracellular potential along the length of each of the three axons at inter-nodal distances corresponding to 16, 10, 8.7, 7.3, and 5.7 µm myelinated fibers (A-fibers). The voltage profiles were exported to MATLAB (r2023b, Mathworks Inc., Natick, MA) where the nerve activation threshold current was predicted using a MATLAB implementation of the MRG model [55, 56]. MRG simulations were repeated using pulse widths (PW) of 0.5 and 1.0 ms and a simulation time of 2.5 ms to determine the minimum current (within 0.01 mA) required to activate each fiber at each location.

The simulated current within the MRG model was further increased such that an anodal block and subsequent reactivation (via virtual cathode) was observed (Fig. 1A). The current amplitude at which these events occurred were defined as the blocking and reactivation thresholds, respectively. If reactivation occurred before blocking, both the blocking and reactivation thresholds were assigned the lower value.

A custom script was written in MATLAB that randomly distributed 1,000 fibers within the cross-section of the nerve endoneurium (Fig. 8E). The proportions of the varying fiber sizes (16, 10, 8.7, 7.3. 5.7 µm) were semi-randomly determined for each simulation, with proportions bound to within 5% of estimates based on distributions of rat sciatic nerve fibers found in literature [26]. Activation, blocking, and reactivation thresholds for each randomly distributed fiber were initially estimated via 1D modified Akima interpolation [57], based on its orthogonal distance from the electrode contacts relative to the three axons initially simulated in the FEM. The Akima algorithm is a piecewise-cubic interpolation method that reduces oscillations by using slope estimates based on local data gradients rather than global polynomials. It preserves local heterogeneity without oversmoothing or excessive undulation, yielding more physiologically realistic threshold maps.

In the initial MRG simulations, the position of nodes of Ranvier were kept consistent. In vivo, however, the positions of nodes of Ranvier of individual fibers may be displaced relative to other nearby fibers, which would affect relative activation and blocking thresholds. Using tripolar simulation (9:1 current split) as a representative model, the effect of the longitudinal position of nodes of Ranvier relative to the stimulating and blocking electrodes was determined as follows. For each myelinated fiber, simulations were conducted with nodes of Ranvier shifted by up to half of the inter-nodal distance in four equally spaced intervals. Thus, four activation threshold values were determined for each modeled fiber. The minimum (Tmin) and maximum (Tmax) activation thresholds were compared to the mean of the four values (Rmin = Tmin/Tmean, Rmax = Tmax/Tmean), and a range of variability (Rrange = Rmax – Rmin) was determined. For example, one fiber’s activation threshold may vary between 0.8 and 1.5 of the mean value, depending on the relative longitudinal position of its nodes of Ranvier. For fibers located toward the lateral edge of the nerve in the rat sciatic model, activation, blocking, and reactivation thresholds were adjusted according to the variable RLateral, which was determined for fibers of that size and distance from the electrode contact. A fiber located along the center-line of the nerve had RLateral = 1, and therefore its thresholds were not adjusted, in accordance with (1).

The state of each fiber (activated or blocked) was determined at stimulation amplitudes between the range of 0 to 1 mA, where the percentage of fibers activated at a given amplitude was calculated. The process of calculating the percentage of activated fibers was repeated 15 times and the average value was used to generate nerve recruitment curves. We note that while fibers of differing diameters were activated, blocked, and then reactivated at different amplitudes, our figures report activation percentages in terms of all fibers in the nerve overall.

In vivo validation of rodent model

A total of 7 Sprague–Dawley rats (470–526 g, mean: 493 g) were placed under isoflurane anesthesia (5% induction chamber, maintained with gas mask at 2–3%), then transferred to a surgical table in prone position, where vital signs were monitored via pulse-oximeter. After shaving of the lower back and both legs, two wire electrodes were implanted at the heel and large digital pad to record EMG signals from the foot while the ground needle electrode was inserted subcutaneously in the upper back. The signal was conditioned (gain = 20x, band-pass filter = 100 Hz to 3 kHz, SR560 Stanford Research Systems, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) and stored digitally (sampling rate = 10 kHz, Powerlab 16/35, ADInstruments, Inc., Colorado Springs, CO, USA).

The sciatic nerve was exposed via dorsal incision, separating the biceps femoris and vastus lateralis muscles. A tripolar FINE fabricated in-house with dimensions matching the computational model (Fig. 8A) was surgically implanted as close as reasonably possible to the spinal cord, and tested with different stimulating configurations as described in the computational model (Table 3). The tripolar configuration used a bread board and resistance box (RS-500 Resistance Substitution Box, Elenco Electronics, Wheeling, IL) to split the current between anodes. Stimulus pulses were applied at a frequency of 1 Hz, PW of 0.5 or 1 ms, and amplitude ranging from 0–1 mA (10 µA intervals) using a Model 2100 Isolated Pulse Stimulator (A-M Systems, Sequim, WA). Electrical stimulation was conducted in either increasing or decreasing order (randomly selected), while the location of stimulation (left vs. right hindlimb) and the order of configurations tested (monopolar, bipolar, tripolar) were also randomized. These images were used to analyze the size and number of nerve fascicles.

Human-sized computational model

A computational model of a larger peripheral nerve was implemented using a similar methodology to the rat model (Fig. 8B, C). As depicted in Fig. 8F, the nerve (diameter = 3.8 mm) consisted of an endoneurium (diameter = 3.2 mm), perineurium (thickness = 0.05 mm), and epineurium (thickness = 0.3 mm), where an insulating cylindrical cuff (thickness = 0.2 mm) was placed around the outer surface of the epineurium. The cuff electrode was initially centered at the midpoint along the nerve and modeled in a symmetric bipolar configuration (contact length = 1 mm). The electric field was solved in COMSOL using a model consisting of three axons located at the center, edge, and half-radius of the fascicle (Fig. 8F). Similar to the rat model, the electric field along the initial 3 axons were used to generate recruitment curves with spatially distributed and interpolated fiber thresholds (Fig. 8G), with the stimulation amplitude ranging from 0 to 10 mA. The proportions of the different myelinated fibers were obtained from estimates of the cervical vagus nerve [25]. Given the radial symmetry of this cylindrical model, fiber thresholds were adjusted using Eq. (1) only. Electrode design parameters were selected based on their ability to manipulate the electric field within the cuff. For example, higher current densities were tested by shortening the anodic contact lengths (i.e., stronger hyperpolarization) and current steering was induced by altering the IED and EEL of the cuff electrode. The effects of these parameters were examined in bipolar electrodes (Fig. 8B) and subsequently tested in asymmetric tripolar electrodes (Fig. 8C). The addition of a secondary anode was hypothesized to mitigate nerve fiber reactivation by minimizing the virtual cathode created by the primary anode. This mechanism was further examined by varying the ratio of anodic currents (primary:secondary). The asymmetric tripolar designs focused on examining select variables—proximal EEL, IED, and IAD (see Table 3). Design 1 had a relatively shorter IED and a longer proximal EEL, as these two settings individually resulted in superior performance in bipolar embodiments. Whereas design 2 tested the opposite configuration with a longer IED and a shorter proximal EEL, which by our hypothesis should show relatively poorer performance. Design 3 was the shortest tripolar design with a much shorter IAD, effectively providing a shorter distal EEL. The relative performance of this design as compared to designs 1 and 2 would determine the relative importance of distal EEL in the efficiency of anodal block. Other parameters were set as constant, based on results obtained from the bipolar electrode: narrower anodic contact (0.1 mm) and distal EEL = 0.2 mm. This resulted in cuff electrodes with total lengths ranging from 5.6 mm (Design 3) to 8.35 mm (Design 1). It is noted that the anodic current split (primary:secondary) was initially set at a ratio of 9:1 but was subsequently varied in Design 1. The bipolar version of the electrode designs in Table 3 was identical in length, differing only by the removal of the secondary anode. This removal resulted in different distal EELs—2.5 mm in bipolar Designs 1 and 2, and 1.0 mm in bipolar Design 3.

Data analysis

Computationally generated recruitment curves were plotted as a function of the stimulation amplitude where the nerve activation threshold, maximum blocking percentage, and amplitude at maximum block were compared among different electrode design parameters. Each recruitment curve was the average value obtained from 15 simulations. For comparison with the rat model, experimentally derived recruitment curves were plotted using the mean RMS of the foot EMG. The nerve blocking properties were assessed and compared among different electrode designs and experimental conditions.
