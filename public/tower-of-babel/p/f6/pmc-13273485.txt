Short-Acting Opioid Dose and Patient-Directed Discharge in Hospitalized Patients With Opioid Use Disorder
Authors: Alexander Logan, Taylor Baisey, Charles E. McCulloch, Alexander R. Bazazi, Marlene Martin, Leslie W. Suen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionAmong hospitalized patients with opioid use disorder, is early treatment of opioid withdrawal syndrome with short-acting opioids associated with reduced risk of early patient-directed discharge?FindingsIn this single-site cohort study comprising 669 hospitalizations, there was a dose-dependent association between short-acting opioid dose and reduced hazard of patient-directed discharge within 72 hours.MeaningThese findings support offering short-acting opioids alongside medications for opioid use disorder to patients experiencing opioid withdrawal to reduce risk of early patient-directed discharge.

Introduction

Management of opioid withdrawal in the hospital has become more challenging as fentanyl and other high-potency synthetic opioids have permeated the illicit drug supply, resulting in significantly higher levels of physiological tolerance among patients with opioid use disorder (OUD).1,2 Undertreatment of withdrawal and cravings contributes to patient-directed discharge,3,4,5,6,7 which is associated with higher rates of mortality, readmission, and overdose.8,9,10,11,12 Adequately treating opioid withdrawal in hospitalized patients with OUD may enable treatment of their acute illness, reduce patient-directed discharge, and improve outcomes.

Practice guidelines recommend treating opioid withdrawal with methadone or buprenorphine, also known as medications for OUD (MOUD), as well as nonopioid adjuncts.13,14,15,16 However, these medications are often insufficient to treat opioid withdrawal, particularly among patients who use fentanyl and have higher opioid tolerance.1,17 Furthermore, some patients may not be interested in initiating MOUD during their hospitalization. Increasingly, hospital-based addiction consultation teams treat opioid withdrawal with short-acting opioids, in addition to MOUD and nonopioid adjuncts.18,19,20 Initial case series and a recent cohort study demonstrate that this practice is feasible and well tolerated among hospitalized patients with OUD18,20; however, no published studies have examined the association between treating opioid withdrawal with short-acting opioids and patient-directed discharge.

At our hospital, clinicians commonly treat opioid cravings, withdrawal, and concurrent acute pain using short-acting opioids and adjunct medications in addition to offering MOUD. Our retrospective cohort study assesses 2 research questions. The primary question is whether the total daily dosage of short-acting opioids for the treatment of opioid withdrawal is associated with early patient-directed discharge (≤72 hours) among hospitalized patients with OUD. The secondary question is whether earlier initiation of short-acting opioids for the treatment of opioid withdrawal is associated with early patient-directed discharge.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of hospitalizations at an urban safety-net hospital in San Francisco, California, during which patients with OUD received short-acting opioids to treat opioid withdrawal between February 6 and December 31, 2024. The University of California, San Francisco institutional review board approved this study and granted a waiver of informed consent due to the use of deidentified data as part of a larger evaluation of our addiction care team. This report adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Clinical Context

Our hospital has had a consultative interprofessional addiction care team since 2019.21 Since February 2024, our hospital has had an opioid withdrawal order set available through the electronic health record (EHR), which offers standardized orders for the treatment of opioid withdrawal with short-acting opioids, including oxycodone and hydromorphone, and adjunct medications (eFigure in Supplement 1). The order set is available in all emergency department and hospital care settings, with or without an addiction care team consultation. Clinicians are advised to use the order set for patients experiencing or at risk for opioid withdrawal who have high opioid tolerance (eg, self-reported daily use of fentanyl or heroin) and to avoid using it for patients without OUD or for patients with OUD who have lower opioid tolerance (eg, patients using prescription opioid pills). The order set includes as-needed oral oxycodone or oral hydromorphone as the first-line short-acting opioids to treat opioid cravings and/or withdrawal (eg, clinical opioid withdrawal score ≥5) and as-needed intravenous hydromorphone as the second-line opioid medication for the same indications. The starting doses for these medications are higher than those used for patients without significant opioid tolerance. Ordering clinicians may titrate short-acting opioid doses to relieve opioid withdrawal symptoms as needed. In accordance with federal regulations, clinicians do not prescribe short-acting opioids for treatment of OUD or opioid withdrawal on hospital discharge.22 The order set also includes as-needed orders for nonopioid adjuncts (clonidine, hydroxyzine, ondansetron, and loperamide), which can be selected at the discretion of the ordering clinician, taking into account the patient’s symptoms, concurrent medical issues, and any potential medication interactions. When a patient has both opioid withdrawal and acute pain, the order set instructs clinicians to consolidate as-needed opioid orders and to add an indication for pain, for safety and clarity. Alongside short-acting opioids and nonopioid adjuncts for opioid withdrawal, we offer MOUD to patients with OUD. All clinicians can perform a methadone initiation using traditional dosing or can consult our addiction care team for assistance.23 Our addiction care team offers buprenorphine via multiple initiation strategies, rapid methadone titration using higher initial doses for patients who use fentanyl,24 and extended-release naltrexone for OUD.

Study Sample

We extracted all hospitalizations of patients with OUD during which a clinician ordered the opioid withdrawal order set during the study period. OUD was defined as having an International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code for OUD, dependence, or poisoning at discharge of the given encounter. We used the presence of the order set, rather than ICD-10 coding alone, to more specifically define a population in which there was clinical concern for opioid withdrawal. We excluded hospitalizations of patients who were pregnant, incarcerated, or in custody, as these were considered vulnerable populations afforded additional confidentiality protections. We excluded any hospitalization in which the patient remained hospitalized at the time of data extraction.

Data Collection and Measures

We extracted sociodemographic data (age, gender, race and ethnicity, housing status, and preferred language) and comorbidities (concurrent substance use disorders [SUDs]) from the EHR. We also extracted hospitalization characteristics (primary clinical team, addiction care team consultation, length of stay, and discharge type) from the EHR and medication dosing data (date and time, medication, formulation, and dosages) from the hospital Omnicell database. Urine toxicology results and clinical opioid withdrawal scores cannot be readily extracted from our EHR.

As abstracted, race and ethnicity categories were categorized as Black or African American, Latine, White, and other (American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, declined to answer, and other). We included the measure for race and ethnicity to assess for disparate associations between racial and ethnic categories. Gender was self-reported and categorized as man, woman, or gender minority (nonbinary or genderqueer, transgender female, transgender male, and other); if any gender values were missing, we used the recorded sex as an alternative. We defined concurrent SUD as the presence of a stimulant, alcohol, and/or a sedative use disorder according to ICD-10 coding at discharge; we did not include other SUDs due to a lack of data reliability. We also measured if the hospitalization had an addiction care team visit by checking for the presence of a note by an addiction care team clinician (physician or nurse practitioner). We reported receipt of MOUD as either no MOUD received, traditional methadone start, rapid methadone start, buprenorphine start, or already receiving MOUD prior to hospitalization. We categorized new methadone starts based on the presence of order sets for traditional and rapid initiations. We categorized new buprenorphine starts based on receipt of an initial dose of sublingual buprenorphine less than 16 mg or any dose of intravenous or transdermal buprenorphine, which, at our hospital, are used only for buprenorphine initiations. We categorized hospitalizations as already receiving MOUD if patients received methadone outside of an initiation order set or an initial sublingual buprenorphine dose of 16 mg or more. We did not include extended-release naltrexone due to the infrequency of its use for OUD at our hospital and its contraindication with full-agonist opioids. In our final analysis, we included receipt of MOUD as a binary measure to simplify analysis.

The primary exposure was the total daily dose of short-acting opioids, represented in oral morphine milligram equivalents (MMEs).25 The secondary exposure was the time to the first dose of short-acting opioids since emergency department intake. We included total doses of oral oxycodone, oral hydromorphone, and intravenous hydromorphone in our exposures, as these are the primary medications used to treat opioid withdrawal via the order set at our hospital. We did not include doses of other opioids, as these are used for other indications (eg, procedural sedation) at our hospital.

The primary outcome was early patient-directed discharge (first 72 hours of hospitalization), defined as a binary outcome when the EHR’s structured field for discharge type was either against medical advice (AMA) or absent without leave (AWOL) and the discharge occurred within 72 hours of emergency department intake. We limited the outcome to the first 72 hours of hospitalization, reasoning that during this period, patient-directed discharge was more likely to be driven by undertreated or untreated opioid withdrawal, relative to other factors.26,27

Statistical Analysis

We conducted statistical analysis between October 2024 and October 2025. We summarized categorical data as counts and percentages, and numerical data as mean (SD) or median (IQR) values, as appropriate. We compared cohort characteristics between those who experienced the event (patient-directed discharge ≤72 hours) and those who did not, using the χ2 test for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney test for all numerical variables. We set a statistical significance level at P < .05 (2-sided).

For our primary exposure of short-acting opioid dosage, our analysis leveraged a time-varying exposure of MMEs administered during hospitalization to more accurately evaluate the association between inpatient treatment with short-acting opioids and the likelihood of patient-directed discharge. By modeling MMEs as a time-varying exposure rather than as a fixed exposure, we accounted for the fact that patients who remain hospitalized longer have a greater opportunity to receive higher cumulative doses of short-acting opioids. This approach reduces potential confounding by length of stay, which is both a determinant of cumulative MMEs and an outcome-related factor, given that patients who are discharged early may have less time to receive opioids.

We performed a survival analysis via unadjusted and adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression models, with the time-varying exposure of the total daily short-acting opioid dose and the outcome of patient-directed discharge within the first 72 hours of hospitalization. We used the natural log of the exposure to account for outliers. We decided a priori to adjust for age, gender, race and ethnicity, any concurrent SUD, addiction care team consultation, and receipt of any MOUD as confounders. We analyzed all covariates as fixed measures except for receipt of MOUD, which was time varying. We tested for collinearity between covariates and found none. The maximum follow-up period was the first 72 hours of hospitalization, beginning from presentation to the emergency department. We defined censored events as regular discharge, transfer to a skilled nursing facility or other facility, or continued hospitalization past the 72-hour follow-up time.

For our secondary exposure of time to first dose of short-acting opioids, we also evaluated the treatment time-lag association with patient-directed discharge within 72 hours with unadjusted and adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression models. The time-varying exposure was defined as having received the first opioid dose by the start of each 24-hour period (yes or no), and the outcome was defined as having a patient-directed discharge by the end of the same 24-hour period (yes or no). The covariates, follow-up period, and censored events were the same as in the primary exposure model. By definition, all observations were unexposed at 0 hours, so there are no results to report for day 1.

For all models, we specified hospitalization encounter as the unit of analysis using counting-process notation and accounted for within-patient correlation using robust standard errors clustered at the patient level. We tested the linearity and proportional hazards assumptions for each model via Martingale and Schoenfeld residuals, respectively. We conducted all analyses in R, version 4.5.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing).28,29

Results

A total of 669 hospitalizations, representing 520 unique patients, met the study criteria and were included in the analysis (Figure). The cohort had a mean (SD) age of 44.1 (12.2) years and included 531 of 669 men (79.4%), 122 of 669 women (18.2%), and 16 of 669 gender minority patients (2.4%); and 159 of 669 Black or African American patients (23.8%), 66 of 669 Latine patients (9.9%), 390 of 669 White patients (58.3%), and 54 patients of other race or ethnicity (8.1%) (Table). The cohort predominantly identified as English speaking (655 of 669 [97.9%]) and unhoused (555 of 669 [83.0%]). Addiction care team clinicians provided consultation in 394 hospitalizations (58.9%). Most patients received MOUD during their hospitalization (510 of 669 [76.2%]), with 336 of 669 hospitalizations (50.2%) involving MOUD initiation within the first 72 hours and 39 of 669 (5.8%) involving MOUD initiation after the first 72 hours. Most hospitalizations (586 of 669 [87.6%]) had short-acting opioids administered within the first 72 hours of hospitalization, with a median time to first dose of 9.9 hours (IQR, 4.9-18.0 hours). Data describing the frequency and dosing of individual opioids can be found in eTables 1 to 4 in Supplement 1.

A total of 92 of 669 hospitalizations (13.8%) ended in a patient-directed discharge within the first 72 hours of hospitalization. In the bivariate analysis, hospitalizations with a patient-directed discharge within 72 hours were more likely than hospitalizations with no patient-directed discharge within 72 hours to involve younger patients (mean [SD] age, 39.5 [9.7] vs 44.9 [12.4] years; P < .001) and were less likely to include an addiction care team consultation (32 of 669 [34.8%] vs 362 of 669 [62.7%]; P < .001) or receipt of MOUD within 72 hours (54 of 669 [58.7%] vs 417 of 669 [72.3%]; P = .01) (Table). Hospitalizations with a patient-directed discharge within 72 hours also included significantly lower total doses of short-acting opioids within the first 72 hours than hospitalizations with no patient-directed discharge within 72 hours (median, 142.8 MMEs [IQR, 30.0-318.8 MMEs] vs 216.0 MMEs [IQR, 65.4-506.1 MMEs]; P = .009). Our secondary exposure, time from emergency department intake to first dose of short-acting opioids for opioid withdrawal, was not significantly associated with patient-directed discharge within 72 hours in the bivariate analysis (median time: patient-directed discharge ≤72 hours, 9.5 hours [IQR, 5.2-13.4 hours]; no patient-directed discharge ≤72 hours, 10.0 hours [IQR, 4.8-18.4 hours]; P = .26) or in the Cox proportional hazards regression models (eTable 5 in Supplement 1).

In Cox proportional hazards regression models, for every doubling of the daily dose of short-acting opioids for opioid withdrawal, the hazard ratio for patient-directed discharge within 72 hours was 0.91 (95% CI, 0.85-0.97; P = .004) in unadjusted analysis and 0.92 (95% CI, 0.86-0.99; P = .03) in the covariate-adjusted analysis.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of hospitalized adults with OUD experiencing opioid withdrawal, we found a significant, dose-dependent association between receipt of short-acting opioids and reduced hazard of patient-directed discharge at 72 hours. To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify an association between opioid withdrawal treatment with short-acting opioids and patient-directed discharge. A preliminary 2023 cohort study of 23 patients examining a pharmacist-guided intervention using short-acting opioids to treat opioid withdrawal suggested a possible reduction in patient-directed discharges relative to prior admissions among the same cohort.18 One large retrospective cohort study of hospitalizations between 2009 and 2015 found no association between receipt of short-acting opioids and patient-directed discharge among hospitalized patients with OUD.26 However, that study was conducted before clinicians regularly used short-acting opioids to treat opioid withdrawal, did not account for a potential dose-dependent association, and was conducted prior to the dominance of fentanyl in the illicit drug supply. In our study, we used a dedicated opioid withdrawal order set to both identify a population experiencing opioid withdrawal and examine the associations of short-acting opioids administered at therapeutic doses to treat opioid withdrawal. This added specificity in both population and clinical practice likely accounts for the difference in findings.

Given the increasing prevalence of hospitalizations involving patients with SUDs and the increased morbidity associated with patient-directed discharge among this population, it is crucial that clinicians identify interventions that may reduce patient-directed discharges.10,12,30 The association found in our study suggests that, for example, receipt of 160 MMEs of short-acting opioids (or roughly 5 doses of oral oxycodone, 20 mg) per day would be associated with a hazard ratio of 0.78 (95% CI, 0.64-0.91) for early patient-directed discharge relative to receipt of 20 MMEs per day. Our study supports the evolving view that clinicians should incorporate short-acting opioids in addition to MOUD when treating opioid withdrawal among hospitalized patients with OUD, particularly among those with higher opioid tolerance.17,19,31 Within our hospital, future quality improvement work will be directed at optimizing titration of short-acting opioids alongside MOUD for appropriately selected patients. The use of clonidine—a guideline-recommended adjunctive treatment for opioid withdrawal13,14—was low in our population (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). Clinicians and patients may have been less likely to use clonidine due to concerns about its effectiveness in treating fentanyl withdrawal or to minimize blood pressure effects during acute illness. The role of clonidine and other adjunctive treatments alongside MOUD and short-acting opioids for the treatment of opioid withdrawal in the fentanyl era merits further study.

We did not find an association between the time to the first dose of short-acting opioids to treat opioid withdrawal and the risk of patient-directed discharge. It is possible that there was insufficient variation in the time to first dose to detect an association in this population. In addition, patients with more severe OUD and thus more severe withdrawal symptoms may be more likely to both receive earlier attention from clinicians and experience patient-directed discharge, and our analyses were unable to adjust for the severity of OUD or opioid withdrawal.

In our bivariate analysis, age, consultation with our addiction care team, and receipt of MOUD were associated with reduced early patient-directed discharges. Older patients may be less likely to have patient-directed discharges due to having more complex medical illnesses and/or lower baseline opioid tolerance.32 There are multiple mechanisms by which consultation with the addiction care team could reduce patient-directed discharges, such as more aggressive opioid withdrawal management, including higher doses of short-acting opioids and faster methadone titrations, as well as through the psychosocial support that the team provides. Receipt of MOUD within 72 hours was also associated with a reduced risk of early patient-directed discharge, as has been found in other studies.26,33 That said, bivariate analysis likely overestimates the associations of addiction care team consultation and receipt of MOUD with our outcome because it does not account for the timing of these exposures as time-varying factors. These potential associations merit further study using dedicated models.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has some strengths; an important strength is the use of a Cox proportional hazards regression model to account for the associations of a time-dependent exposure, as well as to adjust for confounders such as consultation with our addiction care team. In addition, we used a dedicated order set to define a population of patients receiving opioids for the indication of treating opioid withdrawal.

Our study also has several limitations. First, although we found a dose-dependent association between short-acting opioid dose and early patient-directed discharge, it is likely that the absolute hazard reduction associated with any marginal dose increase is small. Second, we were unable to adjust for several potential confounders, such as severity of OUD, degree of physiological dependence, severity of opioid withdrawal symptoms, and severity of hospital illness, which may be associated with the outcomes in our population. In addition, clinicians who increase short-acting opioid doses more aggressively to treat opioid withdrawal may reduce patient-directed discharges through other means, and differences in care provision may confound our results. We expect that the inability to adjust for severity of dependence and withdrawal would attenuate our measured effect size because patients with more severe withdrawal would be more likely to both receive a higher opioid dose and experience a patient-directed discharge. Third, due to limitations in statistical power, we included receipt of MOUD as a binary variable in our final model, which prevented us from accounting for potential differential effects of methadone and buprenorphine or differences in MOUD dosing. Fourth, this study does not address the safety, tolerability, or potential unintended consequences of treating opioid withdrawal with short-acting opioids, which warrant further study. In 2 recent cohort studies representing 147 total hospitalizations, this practice was well tolerated when guided by an addiction consultation team or pharmacist.18,20 It is possible that aggressive treatment of opioid withdrawal with short-acting opioids may be associated with unintended consequences such as reduced uptake of MOUD or unnecessary increases in length of stay for patients who do not direct their own discharge. Although the rate of MOUD receipt was relatively high in our study population (76% of hospitalizations), our study was not designed to assess these potential downstream effects, which merit further study. Fifth, our results may not be fully generalizable to other hospitals. Although the population in this study included patients not seen by addiction specialists and short-acting opioids were often initiated by general clinicians, our addiction consultation team has provided education to many clinicians at our hospital on effective treatment of opioid withdrawal using short-acting opioids and was consulted during many of the hospitalizations in this study. Sixth, patient-directed discharge is a complex phenomenon associated not only with opioid cravings and withdrawal but also stigma, boredom, and social demands outside of the hospital.3,4,5,7 We examined early patient-directed discharges with the reasoning that these may be more likely to be associated with physical symptoms than other factors, but we were ultimately unable to account for these. Although our model included the presence of another SUD, this was assessed by ICD-10 coding, which may be unreliable, and we were similarly unable to account for contributions from craving for or withdrawal from other substances (eg, alcohol, benzodiazepines, or stimulants). Seventh, this population did not include pregnant or postpartum patients who, in our experience, require more aggressive titration of both MOUD and short-acting opioids due to an augmented drug metabolism.20

Conclusions

In this single-site retrospective cohort study, there was a dose-dependent association between receipt of short-acting opioids and reduction in patient-directed discharges within 72 hours among patients with OUD experiencing opioid withdrawal. These findings support treating opioid withdrawal using short-acting opioids alongside MOUD for hospitalized patients with OUD to alleviate symptoms and reduce the risk of patient-directed discharge. Future work should aim to better characterize the risks of this practice, optimize agent choice (eg, higher- vs lower-potency opioids) and dosing, and refine this practice in different populations, including pregnant and postpartum individuals. Hospitals and addiction consultation teams should invest in educating clinicians to titrate short-acting opioids doses in a safe and effective manner in addition to ensuring access to MOUD.
