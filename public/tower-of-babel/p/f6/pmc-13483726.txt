Host genetic architecture and gut microbiota cooperatively regulate early growth in goats
Authors: Hui-Long Mou, Zi-Xuan Wang, Ming-Ding Zhang, Yao-Long Liu, Tao Ren, Peng-Cheng Ruan, Yan-Guo Han, Yan Zeng, Hao-Yuan Zhang, Jia-Guo Lu, Yang Zhang, Cheng-Li Liu, Yong-Fu Huang, Yong-Ju Zhao, Zhong-Quan Zhao, Xiao Pan, Li-Ping Zhou, Simone Ceccobelli, Guang-Xin E.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early postnatal growth is a critical determinant of meat production efficiency and long-term genetic improvement in goats; however, the molecular mechanisms underlying individual variation in growth performance remain poorly understood. In this study, a total of 123 Hechuan white goats were included. First, a genome-wide association study (GWAS) for average daily gain (ADG) was performed using all 123 individuals. Subsequently, based on the coefficient of variation of ADG (CV = 65.6%), an extreme phenotype sampling (EPS) strategy was applied to select 39 individuals with extreme growth phenotypes for subsequent metabolomic, microbiome, and integrated mGWAS analyses.The results showed that ADG approximately followed a normal distribution across the 123 goats. GWAS identified 22 loci significantly associated with ADG, mapping to genes including DLK1, NCAPG2, LCORL, CNTNAP2, and SLC8A1, which are involved in pathways related to skeletal muscle development, cell cycle regulation, ion transport, and immune function. Metabolomic profiling detected 1,589 putative metabolites, revealing differential enrichment of lipid, amino acid, and bile acid metabolic pathways between fast- and slow-growing goats. Gut microbiome analysis demonstrated that Christensenellaceae_R-7_group and Monoglobus were significantly enriched in fast-growing individuals, whereas Desulfovibrio was more abundant in slow-growing goats.Integrated mGWAS analysis further revealed extensive effects of host genetic variation on gut microbiota and fecal metabolites. Specifically, 11 bacterial genera were significantly associated with host genomic variants, among which Desulfovibrio exhibited the highest number of associated loci. Integration of multiple variant types consistently linked Desulfovibrio, Eubacterium_hallii_group, and Candidatus_Saccharimonas with genes such as ARHGAP24 and IGF2BP2. In addition, 14 metabolites were significantly associated with host genetic variants, with Lysopc(14:1(9Z)/0:0) and glycocholic acid showing the strongest associations. Notably, the peak signal for Lysopc was located within HMGA2.Collectively, these findings define a coordinated host genome–gut microbiota–metabolite network underlying early growth variation in goats and provide a mechanistic foundation for precision breeding and targeted nutritional strategies in goat production systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-026-00597-y.

Introduction

The goat industry plays an important role in global animal production systems, supplying meat, milk, and fiber, while supporting income generation in many rural regions [1]. The Hechuan white goat is a representative indigenous breed in southwest China and has become closely linked to local agricultural development and rural revitalization initiatives. However, because breeding programs have historically relied on extensive management with limited structured genetic selection, the population shows considerable variation in productive performance and slow overall genetic progress. Improving the genetic basis of key economic traits is therefore necessary to enhance productivity and sustain competitiveness. Among these traits, average daily gain (ADG) is one of the most informative indicators of growth performance, as it directly influences feeding duration, feed efficiency, and production costs, making it a practical target for genetic improvement [2]. Growth rate during early life is particularly important, as it affects weaning weight, feed conversion efficiency, subsequent growth potential, carcass traits, and ultimately economic returns [3, 4]. For these reasons, understanding the biological mechanisms that regulate early growth has become a priority in ruminant breeding research.

With advances in high-throughput genotyping, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have emerged as a pivotal approach for exploring the genetic architecture of complex traits. By associating dense single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers with phenotypic variation, GWAS facilitates the identification of genomic regions and candidate genes influencing economically important traits [5, 6]. This approach has been successfully applied to traits such as milk yield in cattle [7], muscle development in pigs [8] and egg weight in chickens [9], illustrating its value for livestock improvement programs [10, 11]. In goats, GWAS has uncovered loci linked to horn type, meat quality and growth-related traits [12, 13]. In addition to GWAS, runs of homozygosity (ROH) analysis provides essential metrics regarding population structure, inbreeding levels, and historical selection. ROH-based approaches have been applied to identify genomic regions linked to growth, reproduction, and coat color in local goat populations, offering practical insights for both conservation and breeding strategies [14–16]. Nevertheless, growth is a multifaceted trait that cannot be fully explained by host genomic variation alone. Increasing evidence suggests that growth outcomes reflect interactions between host genetics and environmental factors, among which the gut microbiota plays a central role. The gut microbiota—comprising bacteria, fungi, viruses, and archaea—is often characterized as the host’s “second genome” due to its profound impact on systemic metabolism and physiological functions [17, 18]. Microbial community composition is influenced by host genotype, diet, and management conditions, and in turn affects nutrient utilization, immune function, and growth performance [19–21]. In ruminants, several studies have reported associations between gut microbial composition and growth-related traits [22–24], including differences in weaning weight observed in Hainan black goats [25].

Metabolomics provides an additional layer of information by capturing small-molecule metabolites that represent the downstream output of host and microbial activity. These metabolic profiles integrate the effects of genetic background, diet, and microbial metabolism, and have been increasingly used in livestock studies focusing on nutrition, health, and meat quality [26–29]. As a result, combining genomic, microbiome, and metabolomic data offers a systems-level perspective for investigating the mechanisms linking genotype to phenotype.

In the present study, an integrative multi-omics approach was applied to the Hechuan white goat population elucidating the biological mechanisms underlying early growth performance. Specifically, GWAS was conducted to identify loci associated with ADG during the early growth period and screened candidate SNPs and genes. In parallel, we characterized the fecal microbiota and metabolomic profiles, employing correlation and integrative association analyses to identify microbial taxa and metabolic pathways associated with average daily gain (ADG). Subsequently, mGWAS was utilized to link these ADG-associated gut microbial and metabolic signatures to host genetic variants. Ultimately, this study aims to provide mechanistic insights into early growth regulation, thereby supporting precision breeding and nutritional management strategies for goats.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and sample collection

A total of 123 Hechuan white goats were selected as experimental subjects. Blood samples were collected for genomic DNA extraction from a conservation population at Chongqing Yinghe Agricultural Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China (106.171° E, 29.954° N). Before weaning, all kids were reared under natural suckling conditions with their dams. After weaning at 60 days of age, they were transferred to an indoor housing system and provided ad libitum access to a standardized basal diet and high-quality forage.

Body weight (BW) was recorded at 60 and 90 days of age. Average daily gain (ADG, g/day) for the early growth period (60–90 days) was calculated as: ADG = (BW₉₀ - BW₆₀) / 30. The normality of ADG distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visualized via histogram. Pearson correlation analysis was performed between ADG (60–90 days) and subsequent BW at 2–3, 6, and 9 months of age. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses and visualizations (scatter plots with linear regression) were conducted in R (v4.4.1) using the ggplot2 and ggpubr packages.

To capture biological extremes for integrative multi-omics analysis, a subset of 39 kids was selected from the initial cohort (n = 123). Selection was primarily based on sex-specific ADG rankings. Additional criteria included high sample quality, complete metadata, and availability for matched multi-omics profiling. The selected animals were classified into four groups: Fast-growing males (M_F, n = 10, ADG > 120 g/day), Fast-growing females (F_F, n = 10, ADG > 110 g/day), Slow-growing males (M_S, n = 10, ADG < 40 g/day), Slow-growing females (F_S, n = 9, ADG < 35 g/day).

After overnight fasting, blood samples (approximately 5 mL) were collected via jugular venipuncture from all 123 animals, immediately aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C for genomic DNA extraction. From the selected subset of 39 kids exhibiting extreme growth phenotypes, matched fresh fecal samples were aseptically obtained by rectal collection directly into sterile cryovials, snap‑frozen in liquid nitrogen within 5 min, and maintained at − 80 °C for subsequent microbiome profiling (16 S rRNA gene sequencing) and metabolomic analysis. Prior to sampling, all animals were confirmed to be clinically healthy, with no history of antibiotic administration or clinical signs of infectious disease. All animal procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Southwest University (Approval No. IACUC‑20240710‑06) and performed in strict accordance with the committee’s guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Whole-genome sequencing and data processing

Genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood using the TaKaRa Universal DNA Extraction Kit following the manufacture instructions, and DNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop™ One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). DNA integrity was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the Annoroad® Universal DNA Library Prep Kit v2.0 (Illumina®, USA) and sequenced on a DNBSEQ-T7 platform (BGI, China) to generate whole-genome data.

Raw reads were quality filtered using Fastp (v0.20.1) [30] to obtain clean reads with the following parameters: sliding‑window trimming (4 bp, mean quality ≥ Q15), 3 bp trimming from both ends, and minimum read length of 40 bp. Filtered reads were aligned to the goat reference genome (Capra_hircus ARS1.2) using BWA-MEM2 (v2.2.1) [31] to generate BAM files.Subsequently, the resulting BAM files were sorted, PCR duplicates were removed, and the final alignments were indexed using Samtools (version 1.17) [32].

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and small insertions/deletions (InDels) were identified using the GATK (v4.5.0.0) [33] ‘Best Practices’ pipeline with base quality recalibration (BQSR). Raw variants were subjected to hard filtering using the following thresholds: for SNPs, QD < 2.0, FS > 60.0, MQ < 40.0, and SOR > 3.0; for indels, QD < 2.0, FS > 200.0, and SOR > 10.0. Variants tagged as low-quality (LowQual) were excluded. For downstream analyses, the variant set was further filtered to retain only those with a genotype call rate > 90% and a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05.

Copy number variations (CNVs) were detected using CNVcaller [34] with a sliding window size of 1,000 bp and a step size of 500 bp. CNV regions (CNVRs) were determined based on copy number status, window distribution, and correlations between adjacent windows. Finally, CNVRs not anchored to specific chromosomes were excluded from downstream analyses.

Structural variants (SVs) were detected using a consensus approach across three independent callers: Manta (v1.6.0) [35] Lumpy (v0.2.13) [36], and Delly (v1.1.7) [37]. All methods were performed using default parameters. High-confidence SVs were defined as those identified by at least two callers and merged using SURVIVOR (v1.0.3) [38] with a maximum breakpoint distance of 1000 bp, consistent SV type and strand orientation, and a minimum size of 50 bp.

The final, high-confidence variant sets (SNPs, InDels, CNVs, and SVs) were used as input for subsequent genome-wide association studies (GWAS).

To integrate CNVs and SVs into the GWAS framework, CNVs were encoded as ordinal variables based on copy number status: 0 (homozygous deletion), 1 (heterozygous deletion or duplication), and 2 (homozygous duplication). For complex multi-allelic CNV states, a letter-based scheme (A–J) was applied. SVs were encoded as binary variables: 0 (absence) and 1 (presence), with specific letter assignments for each variant type (inversion, deletion, duplication, insertion, translocation). The complete replacement rules are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Genome-wide association analysis and candidate gene annotation

A genome‑wide association study (GWAS) for average daily gain was conducted using the multi‑locus mixed model (MLMM) implemented in GAPIT3 [39]. The model included sex as a fixed effect and incorporated a genomic relationship matrix (derived from autosomal genetic variants) as a random effect to account for population stratification. The MLMM procedure iteratively identified significant Genetic variants as covariates until no new Genetic variants met the genome‑wide significance threshold (Bonferroni‑corrected P < 0.05/N, where N is the number of tested Genetic variants). Protein‑coding genes within ± 0.5 Mb of significant Genetic variants were defined as positional candidates. Candidate genes were annotated using ANNOVAR [40]. Gene Ontology (GO) biological process terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were carried out using KOBAS (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/). Enrichment significance was tested using the hypergeometric test, and P‑values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini‑Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Terms and pathways with an FDR‑adjusted P‑value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Metabolomic analysis and data processing

Fecal metabolomic profiling was performed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Metabolites were extracted from 50 mg of feces using 80% methanol containing 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine as an internal standard, with quality control samples prepared from pooled aliquots of all samples. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) with a mobile phase consisting of (A) 5% acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile/isopropanol (1:1, v/v) containing 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 0.40 mL/min. Mass spectrometry data were acquired in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes (m/z 70–1050) with data-dependent MS/MS acquisition.

After peak detection and alignment, features with > 20% missing values within any experimental group were removed. Missing values were imputed using the half-minimum method. Data were normalized by total ion current, and features showing relative standard deviation > 30% in quality control samples were excluded before log10 transformation. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching accurate mass (tolerance ± 5 ppm) and MS/MS spectra against the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB,http://www.hmdb.ca) and Metlin database (https://metlin.scripps.edu). Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was conducted using the ropls R package, with model validity assessed via cross-validation. Differential metabolites were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1, fold change (FC) > 2, and false discovery rate-adjusted P < 0.05. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using over-representation analysis against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, with significance defined as false discovery rate < 0.05.

Microbiome analysis and data processing

Fecal microbial DNA was extracted with the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio‑tek, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA integrity was evaluated by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 338F_806R (F: 5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’, R: 5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) [41] in a 20 µL PCR system containing 10 ng of template DNA, 4 µL of 5× TransStart FastPfu buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL of each primer (5 µM), and 0.4 µL of TransStart FastPfu DNA polymerase. PCR cycling conditions were: 95 °C for 3 min; 27 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 30 s; final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Amplicons were purified with a gel extraction kit (YuHua, China) and quantified with a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Libraries were prepared with the NEXTFLEX® Rapid DNA‑Seq Kit (Bioo Scientific, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (2 × 150 bp).

Raw reads were quality‑filtered with Fastp (v0.19.6) to trim low‑quality bases (quality score < 20) in a sliding 50‑bp window and discard reads shorter than 50 bp or containing ambiguous nucleotides. Paired‑end reads were merged using FLASH (v1.2.11; minimum overlap 10 bp, maximum mismatch ratio 0.2). Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at 97% similarity using the UPARSE pipeline, and chimeric sequences were removed with UCHIME. Alpha‑diversity indices (Chao1, Shannon) were calculated in Mothur [42] and compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank‑sum test. Beta‑diversity was assessed with Bray–Curtis distances, visualized by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), and tested by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA; 999 permutations) at P < 0.05. Differentially abundant taxa were identified with linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) using a logarithmic LDA score threshold > 2.0.

Integrative host–microbiome–metabolome analysis

Genus-level microbial taxa exhibiting significant differential relative abundance and metabolites with VIP > 1, P < 0.05, and FC > 1.5 were treated as quantitative traits for microbial/metabolite GWAS (mGWAS). mGWAS was performed using the MLMM implemented in GAPIT3 with Bonferroni correction (P < 0.05/N, where N represents the number of effective Genetic variants). Genes located within ± 0.5 Mb of the significant loci were annotated to identify host candidate genes associated with specific microbial taxa or metabolites.

Results

Early growth traits and body weight correlations in hechuan white goats

Analysis of average daily gain (ADG) in 123 Hechuan white goat kids revealed an approximately normal distribution across the population (Fig. 1A), indicating that this trait was well suited for subsequent GWAS. Correlation analyses demonstrated that the average daily gain for samples aged 2–3 months showed a significant positive correlation with their body weights at 6 and 9 months of age. (Fig. 1B–D). These results indicate that growth performance during the early post-weaning period is likely linked to later body weight, suggesting that ADG may serve as a reliable indicator for early selection in breeding programs.

Association of SNPs, InDels, CNVs, and SVs with early post-weaning ADG in Hechuan white goats

GWAS identified genetic variants significantly associated with ADG during the early growth phase in Hechuan white goats. To establish the genetic basis for this analysis, we performed whole-genome resequencing. Following stringent quality control and variant calling, a total of 3,637,201 high-confidence variants were detected across the genome. This comprehensive variant set comprised 2,358,515 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), 1,206,297 insertions and deletions (InDels), 59,164 copy number variation (CNV) regions, and 13,225 structural variants (SVs), with an average sequencing data yield of approximately 18.29 Gb per individual (Supplementary Table S2). Subsequent GWAS results based on these respective variant types are detailed below.

Furthermore, functional enrichment analysis was performed to explore the biological roles of the candidate genes. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that a total of 8 differentially candidate genes were mapped to 55 KEGG pathways, among which 15 pathways were significantly enriched. These mainly included the NF-κB signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway-multiple species, as well as pathways related to physiological regulation, such as insulin secretion and cardiac muscle contraction. In addition, the Hippo signaling pathway (involving genes such as WTIP) and the amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism pathway (involving genes such as PMM2) were also significantly enriched (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Table S4).

GO enrichment analysis indicated that 15 candidate genes were associated with 289 GO terms, among which 214 terms were significantly enriched. The significantly enriched GO terms primarily involved biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components related to ion channel regulation, nervous system function, and immune response.Notable specific terms included ion transport, neuronal cell body, and regulation of cardiac muscle contraction by calcium ion signaling (Fig. 2F, Supplementary Table S5).

Differential fecal metabolites reflect early growth rate in Hechuan white goats

Metabolite annotation was performed based on two confidence levels: B(i) (highest accuracy, exact MS/MS match using an in-house library) and B(ii) (exact MS/MS match using in silico spectra from public/theoretical databases). Results were filtered to retain only those with a B(i) score > = 35 or a B(ii) score > = 40.Based on the aforementioned criteria, untargeted metabolomic profiling identified a total of 1,589 tentative metabolite. OPLS-DA analysis revealed clear separation between fast- and slow-growing males (M_F vs. M_S) and females (F_F vs. F_S) (Fig. 3A, B), indicating that early growth rate substantially influences the metabolic profile. In males, 252 metabolites exhibited significant differential abundance (111 upregulated, 141 downregulated), whereas 208 differential metabolites were detected in females (119 upregulated, 88 downregulated) (Fig. 3C, D, Supplementary Table S6). HMDB classification indicated that these metabolites mainly belonged to alkaloids, lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic acids and derivatives, amino acids, and phenylpropanoids. A total of 82 metabolites were shared between sexes, whereas the remaining metabolites exhibited sex-specific patterns (Fig. 3E).

KEGG pathway enrichment revealed that differential metabolites in males (49 metabolites) mapped to 100 pathways, predominantly metabolic pathways (62 pathways). Secondary pathway analysis indicated enrichment in lipid metabolism, amino acid metabolism, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and digestive system pathways (Fig. 3F). After FDR correction, 12 pathways were remained significant (P adjust < 0.05), including choline metabolism, cholesterol metabolism, and fat digestion and absorption (Fig. 3H). In females, 48 differential metabolites were enriched in 109 pathways, mainly involving secondary metabolite biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, and membrane transport (Fig. 3G). FDR correction identified 12 significantly enriched pathways (P adjust < 0.05), including flavonoid degradation, ABC transporters, and pyrimidine metabolism (Fig. 3I, Supplementary Table S7).

Gut microbiome profiling in relation to early growth of goat

Alpha- and beta-diversity analyses were conducted to evaluate gut microbial differences between growth groups (Table 1). At the genus level, fast-growing males (M_F) exhibited higher species richness (Sobs index) and a trend toward greater community diversity (Shannon index) compared to slow-growing males (M_S), although differences were not statistically significant. Discussion

In this study, we systematically elucidated the genetic basis and host–microbiome interactions underlying early growth in Hechuan white goats by integrating multi-omics data, including genomics, metabolomics, and microbiome profiling. Early average daily gain (ADG) exhibited an approximately normal distribution across the population and was significantly positively correlated with later body weight traits, indicating that ADG serves as a reliable predictor of individual post-weaning growth potential and provides a theoretical foundation for early selection and genetic improvement strategies.

GWAS identified multiple candidate genes across various chromosomes significantly associated with ADG, encompassing key biological processes such as muscle development, skeletal growth, energy metabolism, and immune regulation. Among them, DLK1, a critical imprinted gene, plays a central role in muscle and adipose tissue development. Previous studies have shown that DLK1 promotes myogenic differentiation while inhibiting adipogenesis via the Notch signaling pathway [43, 44] and enhances muscle growth through the ACVR2B/Myostatin pathway [45]. High DLK1 expression has been consistently linked to muscle fiber hypertrophy and accelerated growth in cattle, pigs, and sheep [46]. The significant association observed in this study further supports its pivotal role in early muscle development and growth in goats.

The NCAPG2–LCORL locus has been repeatedly reported as a major determinant of body size and growth traits in livestock. NCAPG2, a regulator of the chromosome condensation complex, promotes cell proliferation and tissue growth through the STAT3/c-MYC signaling pathway [47, 48], while LCORL participates in lipid metabolism and skeletal muscle differentiation [49]. Variants in this locus are strongly associated with body length, height, and carcass weight [50, 51]. Collectively, our results suggest that DLK1 and NCAPG2–LCORL may form a coordinated “muscle–skeletal developmental axis” that cooperatively regulates early growth in goats.

Notably, several genes involved in neural and ion channel functions, including CNTNAP2, CNTNAP5, SLC8A1, and KCNN2, were also significantly associated with ADG. CNTNAP family members contribute to synapse formation and neural signaling, and their variants may influence energy metabolism and growth rate via the hypothalamic–pituitary–growth axis [52]. SLC8A1 encodes the sodium–calcium exchanger, which is essential for calcium homeostasis and muscle contraction [53], while KCNN2 regulates calcium-activated potassium channels, integrating neuromuscular signaling. Furthermore, enrichment of immune-related genes such as TLR4 and CARD11 indicates that inflammation and immune homeostasis contribute to early growth [54]. These findings collectively suggest that early growth is governed by interconnected regulatory networks spanning musculoskeletal, neural, and immune systems.

Metabolomic profiling revealed pronounced differences in metabolic signatures between fast- and slow-growing individuals. A total of 1,589 metabolites were detected by LC-MS, and multivariate analyses showed a clear separation between the fast-growth (F_F, M_F) and slow-growth (F_S, M_S) groups. Differential metabolites predominantly included amino acids, lipids, organic acids, and bile acids, reflecting systemic differences in energy metabolism, nutrient absorption, and oxidative stress responses. KEGG pathway enrichment demonstrated that metabolites related to energy metabolism and nutrient absorption were strongly enriched in fast-growing individuals, including choline metabolism, fatty acid β-oxidation, and bile acid biosynthesis and transport. Taurine-conjugated bile acids, such as taurocholic acid and glycocholic acid, not only facilitate lipid emulsification and absorption but also act as signaling molecules regulating glucose, lipid, and energy metabolism [55, 56]. Lipid metabolites, including diglycerides (e.g., DG(8:0/10:0/0:0)) and cholesteryl esters (e.g., CE(18:3(9Z,12Z,15Z))), modulate lipid metabolism and maintain intestinal and hepatic metabolic homeostasis [57, 58]. Lysophosphatidylcholine (LysoPC(14:1(9Z)/0:0)) contributes to improved growth performance and gut health [59], while choline enhances fat digestibility [60], and lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), the hydrolysis product of phosphatidic acid (PA(A-13:0/I-15:0)), alleviates intestinal inflammation [61]. Flavonoids, including taxifolin, luteolin, and eriodictyol, were enriched in fast-growing individuals, contributing to anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and gut barrier-protective effects [62–65]. These results indicate that fast-growing individuals exhibit enhanced metabolic capacity, lipid utilization, and anti-inflammatory regulation.

Microbiome profiling further revealed correlations between gut microbial composition and host metabolism. Alpha diversity was significantly higher in fast-growing individuals, suggesting a more stable and functionally diverse microbiome. Dominant genera included Christensenellaceae R-7 group, unclassified Ruminococcaceae UCG-014 and UCG-005, and uncultured Clostridia. Growth performance was closely associated with the abundance of specific microbial taxa, which could be categorized as growth-promoting or growth-inhibiting. In fast-growing individuals, beneficial genera such as Monoglobus, Acetitomaculum, and Marvinbryantia were significantly enriched. Monoglobus, a core functional genus, efficiently ferments dietary fiber and has been associated with enhanced nutrient metabolism in ruminants [66, 67]. Its growth-promoting effects are likely mediated by improvements in intestinal barrier function, including positive correlations with mucin gene Muc2 expression and mucosal repair in both ulcerative colitis models and engineered bacterial interventions [68, 69]. Acetitomaculum contributes to cellulose degradation and intramuscular fat deposition [70], whereas Marvinbryantia produces short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines, alleviating gut inflammation [71]. In contrast, growth-retarded individuals showed enrichment of potentially harmful taxa, including Desulfovibrio, Candidatus_Saccharimonas, and Subdoligranulum, which may compromise intestinal barrier integrity or alter lipid metabolism, thereby restricting growth [72, 73]. These findings provide potential targets for microbiome-based interventions to enhance growth performance.

The metabolome-wide GWAS (mGWAS) GWAS identified key gut metabolites associated with early growth and their host genetic regulators. Notably, Lysopc, taurocholic acid, and glycocholic acid were linked to multiple genes. A variant within the intronic region of HMGA2 (chr5:47188877) was strongly associated with Lysopc. HMGA2, a chromatin regulator, plays conserved roles in body weight, skeletal development, and fat deposition, with gene-editing studies confirming that its loss results in reduced body size and organ development defects [74]. Other genes within this locus, including E2F6 and KHDRBS3, contribute to cell proliferation and tissue development. SCARA5 and MGAT4D were associated with taurocholic acid, implicating roles in iron metabolism, PI3K/Akt signaling, and protein glycosylation [75, 76]. Associations between SLC2A13 and FHIT with sebacoyl-L-carnitine suggest their involvement in energy metabolism and oxidative stress adaptation. Collectively, these results indicate that HMGA2, SCARA5, SLC2A13, and other genes modulate tissue development, lipid metabolism, and energy transport, forming genetic couplings with gut metabolites that influence early growth.

Microbiome–host gene interactions revealed by mGWAS demonstrated that Desulfovibrio-associated SNPs were distributed across chromosomes 5, 6, 10, 11, 12, 21, and 29, involving ARHGAP24, DPH6, FANCL, and ATP12A. ARHGAP24 regulates inflammation via Rac1/NF-κB signaling [77], DPH6 participates in translational modification and metabolic adaptation [78], FANCL regulates DNA repair and oxidative stress response, and ATP12A modulates gut pH and epithelial barrier function [79]. Eubacterium_hallii_group was associated with LEKR1 and NRXN1, implicating insulin secretion regulation and neural energy signaling, respectively [80]. Candidatus_Saccharimonas was linked to IGF2BP2, which promotes glycolysis and muscle growth via m6A modification [81]. These results suggest that host genetic variants not only shape microbial colonization patterns but also indirectly influence growth via systemic metabolic modulation.

Integrated analysis of SNPs, INDELs, CNVs, and SVs revealed that Desulfovibrio, Eubacterium_hallii_group, Subdoligranulum, and Candidatus_Saccharimonas were consistently associated with host genetic variants across multiple genomic layers, involving genes such as ARHGAP24, DPH6 and IGF2BP2. These genes participate in inflammation regulation, mitochondrial homeostasis, metabolic signaling, and cell growth, highlighting the decisive role of host genetics in shaping gut microbiota and energy metabolism.Restricted by the population size of the Hechuan white goat, this study employed an extreme phenotype sampling strategy, selecting samples from individuals with significant differences in growth performance to conduct a metabolome-wide association study (mGWAS). Although the sample size utilized for the mGWAS analysis in this study was relatively limited (n = 39), the extreme phenotype design enhanced the detectability of genetic effects to a certain extent. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that this strategy can increase the detection power of association signals by maximizing phenotypic variance, making it particularly suitable for the preliminary screening of key candidate loci and metabolic markers associated with complex quantitative traits [82, 83]. However, compared to large-scale population studies, a limited sample size may still reduce the statistical power to detect loci with small-to-medium effects or low-frequency variants, and could compromise the accuracy of effect size estimation [84]. Given the complex polygenic architecture underlying growth traits, the candidate metabolites and associated genetic loci identified in this study provide important clues for elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of early growth in Hechuan white goats. Nevertheless, their biological significance and genetic effects require further validation in larger, independent cohorts.

Conclusion

This study provides the first comprehensive, integrative multi-omics framework elucidating the biological regulation of early growth in Hechuan white goats. By jointly analyzing host genomic variation, gut microbiome composition, and metabolic profiles, we demonstrate that average daily gain (ADG) during the early growth phase is governed by coordinated interactions among musculoskeletal development, energy metabolism, immune homeostasis, and microbial activity in the gastrointestinal tract.

At the host genetic level, the DLK1–NCAPG2–LCORL gene axis emerged as a central regulator of musculoskeletal growth, supporting its pivotal role in body size and growth performance. In parallel, genes such as CNTNAP2 and NRXN1 suggest an integration between neural development and metabolic signaling, highlighting the contribution of neuro–endocrine pathways to growth regulation. From a microbial perspective, the enrichment of Monoglobus and Acetitomaculum indicates enhanced energy harvest through short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)–mediated pathways, whereas the increased abundance of Desulfovibrio may reflect inflammatory or metabolic constraints that negatively affect growth efficiency.

The integration of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) with microbiome and metabolomic profiling allowed the construction of a host genome–gut microbiome–metabolite interaction network, providing mechanistic insights into how genetic predisposition and microbial metabolic functions jointly shape early growth trajectories. This integrative approach offers a robust theoretical foundation for early-life selection strategies, precision nutritional interventions, and microbiome-informed management aimed at optimizing growth performance in ruminants. Notably, this study represents the first systematic integration of GWAS, metabolomics, and microbiome data to dissect early growth regulation in goats, underscoring its novelty and translational relevance to livestock production.

Despite these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively limited sample size may reduce the power to detect rare genetic variants, and the absence of longitudinal sampling across multiple growth stages constrains causal inference. Moreover, functional characterization of the gut microbiome relied primarily on 16 S rRNA sequencing, without metagenomic or transcriptomic validation. Environmental and dietary variability, together with the restricted genetic diversity of the conservation population studied, may also influence the observed associations.Future studies with expanded cohorts, longitudinal metagenomic tracking, and functional in vitro validation are warranted to fully untangle the underlying molecular mechanisms.

Future research should therefore expand population size, incorporate longitudinal and multi-stage sampling designs, and integrate metagenomic, metatranscriptomic, and metabolomic datasets to capture the dynamic nature of host–microbiome–metabolite interactions. Such efforts will further refine the mechanistic understanding of growth regulation and accelerate the development of data-driven strategies for genetic improvement and precision nutrition in goats and other ruminant species.
