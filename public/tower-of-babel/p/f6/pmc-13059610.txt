Racial, ethnic, gender and age representation of clinical trials supporting FDA approval of cancer therapies
Authors: Mingye Zhao, Yunlin Jiang, Taihang Shao, Wenxi Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPivotal clinical trials provide the primary evidence base for US FDA oncology drug approvals; however, persistent demographic imbalances in trial enrollment may compromise generalizability and exacerbate inequities in cancer care. We quantified disparities in the representation of women, racial and ethnic minority groups, and older adults in pivotal trials supporting FDA oncology approvals and evaluated trial-level characteristics associated with underrepresentation.MethodsWe identified pivotal trials underpinning US FDA oncology drug approvals between January 2018 and February 2026 using the FDA Hematology/Oncology Approvals database. Demographic information on sex, race, ethnicity, and age was abstracted from published trial reports and registry entries. For each subgroup, we quantified representativeness using enrollment incidence ratios (EIRs), defined as the ratio of the subgroup’s share in the trial to its share of SEER incidence (EIR < 1 indicating underrepresentation). We pooled log-transformed EIRs with random-effects models and used meta-regression to examine time trends and trial-level factors associated with disparities. We additionally conducted stratified analyses by pediatric status, accelerated approval, and other trial characteristics.ResultsAmong 372 pivotal trials supporting US FDA oncology drug approvals (2018–2026), women, older adults, Black, and Hispanic individuals remained underrepresented relative to US cancer incidence. Pooled EIRs showed underenrollment of women (EIR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.88–0.95), Black participants (EIR, 0.25; 95% CI, 0.21–0.28), Hispanic participants (EIR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.45–0.58), and older adults (≥ 65 years) (EIR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.61–0.73). Meta-regression suggested a modest increase in female representation over time (β = 0.02, p = 0.03), with no significant temporal improvement for Black or Hispanic participants. Older adult representation declined slightly. Trial-level correlates of underrepresentation included industry sponsorship, use of overall survival as the primary endpoint, and accelerated approval designation.ConclusionsDespite recent regulatory initiatives, substantial demographic imbalances persist in pivotal oncology trials supporting FDA approvals. Women, Black and Hispanic individuals, and older adults remain underrepresented, potentially limiting the generalizability of efficacy and safety evidence to real-world cancer populations. Strengthened, accountable approaches—spanning more inclusive eligibility criteria and site selection, targeted recruitment, and enforceable diversity requirements—are needed to ensure equitable evidence generation in oncology drug development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12939-026-02800-7.Keywords: Representation, Oncology clinical trials, FDA approvals, Racial and ethnic, Gender and age, Health equity

Introduction

Pivotal clinical trials provide the principal evidentiary basis for US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approvals of oncology drugs, underpinning regulatory determinations and labeling indications [1]. Findings from these trials also shape clinical guidelines, reimbursement decisions, and the adoption of new therapies in routine care [2, 3]. Given their outsized influence on cancer practice, trial cohorts should mirror the demographics of patients affected by the disease. Underrepresentation of specific groups raises concerns about the external validity of trial results and the risk of unequal outcomes when therapies are applied in real-world populations [4].

Despite long-standing recognition of these concerns, inequities in clinical trial enrollment remain pervasive. Among trials supporting FDA oncology drug approvals from 2008 to 2018, Kalathoor et al. [5] reported that women comprised only 40.7% of participants, and Loree et al. [6] found that Black and Hispanic patients represented just 3.1% and 6.1%, respectively—well below their share of the US cancer burden. Older adults, despite accounting for most cancer diagnoses, also continue to be substantially underrepresented in oncology trials [7].

Limited enrollment of racial/ethnic minorities, older adults, and socioeconomically marginalized patients in oncology trials has clear equity consequences. When these populations are sparsely represented, trial estimates of benefit and harm may fail to reflect underlying biologic differences and real-world social contexts, reducing the transportability of results [8]. Because regulatory approvals and downstream guidelines often rely on relatively homogeneous trial cohorts, underserved groups may be less likely to receive—or to benefit from—newly approved targeted and immuno-oncology therapies, potentially widening existing gaps in treatment and survival [9]. These patterns underscore how structural barriers embedded in trial design and recruitment can translate into inequities in real-world cancer outcomes [10]. Improving inclusion across the research continuum, from protocol development to trial conduct, is therefore critical to ensure that therapeutic advances are equitably realized.

In the past several years, federal initiatives have aimed to narrow these enrollment gaps. In 2018, the National Institutes of Health (NIH) implemented a requirement for NIH-funded studies to justify planned enrollment by sex, race, and ethnicity (NOT-OD-18-014) [11]. In 2020, the FDA issued guidance encouraging sponsors to broaden eligibility criteria, reduce participation burden through decentralized approaches, and enhance outreach to underrepresented populations [12]. More recently, the 2022 Food and Drug Omnibus Reform Act (FDORA) authorized the FDA to require Diversity Action Plans for pivotal trials, including prespecified targets for enrollment by sex, race, ethnicity, and age [13].

Despite these efforts, it remains unclear whether participant representation in pivotal oncology trials supporting FDA approvals has improved since 2018, particularly as oncology drug development increasingly relies on biomarker-defined and molecularly selected populations in the era of precision medicine [14]. Precision oncology increasingly relies on biomarker-defined and molecularly selected populations, in which eligibility and prescribing are driven by genomic alterations and associated diagnostic testing [15]. In this context, participant representation is particularly critical because biomarker prevalence and tumor genomic features can vary across populations, and under-enrollment of specific racial/ethnic, sex, or age groups can limit the transportability of efficacy and safety evidence to real-world patients [16]. In addition, precision-medicine pathways often require timely molecular testing and access to specialized centers, which may introduce structural barriers to trial participation and exacerbate inequities [17]. Moreover, because biomarker selection partitions trials into smaller subgroups, lack of demographic diversity further reduces statistical precision for subgroup benefit-risk assessment. Therefore, evaluating representation in pivotal trials is essential for both scientific validity and health equity in the precision-medicine era. As targeted therapies and immunotherapies increasingly rely on molecular subtypes, demographic diversity is essential for both equity and scientific rigor [18]. To extend prior evidence on demographic representation in trials supporting FDA oncology approvals through 2018 [5, 6], we analyzed pivotal clinical trials supporting FDA approvals of oncology drugs from 2018 to 2026. We quantified the representation of women, racial and ethnic minority groups, and older adults and evaluated whether representativeness differed across trial characteristics. These results may help guide regulatory and sponsor strategies to strengthen inclusion in oncology research.

Method

Search strategy, study selection and data extraction

We reviewed all oncology drug approvals by the US FDA from January 2018 to February 2026, based on data from the FDA Hematology/Oncology Approvals database (available at: https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-approvals-and-databases/drugsfda-data-files). A formal study protocol was not preregistered because the analysis used secondary, publicly available data. Nevertheless, study objectives, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the analytical plan were defined before data extraction. Two reviewers (Z.M. and J.Y.) independently screened all retrieved records for eligibility according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov were used to retrieve primary trial reports and registry records and to verify trial identifiers (NCT numbers, drug names, and approved indications) for trials listed in FDA approval documents.

We included trials of solid tumors or hematologic malignancies that served as pivotal evidence for new FDA oncology drug approvals, thereby focusing on studies with direct regulatory relevance. We excluded device-related approvals because they follow different regulatory pathways and often do not provide demographic reporting that is comparable to drug trials. To prevent double counting, we also excluded label expansions without new clinical data and approvals that relied solely on previously published trials without additional enrollment. Trials lacking an accessible primary report or with missing demographic data were excluded to preserve completeness and ensure consistent comparisons across studies. Collectively, these criteria ensured that each pivotal trial contributed once to the analytic dataset and that the cohort reflected distinct, registration-grade evidence supporting FDA oncology approvals.

Two reviewers (Z.M. and J.Y.) extracted trial characteristics, including the proportions of male and female participants, age distribution, and racial and ethnic representation, in accordance with the FDA’s position statement (see eMethods in the Supplement for details). Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. This cross-sectional meta-research study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guideline (Table S1) [19].

Statistical analysis and outcome measures

The primary descriptive analyses were pre-specified before data extraction based on the study objectives. No formal hypothesis testing was planned. Additional subgroup tabulations were conducted exploratorily to provide contextual information.

For each trial, we quantified representation across sex, age, race, and ethnicity. We computed trial proportions (TPs) as the number of participants in a given subgroup divided by the total trial enrollment. Sex-stratified analyses excluded sex-specific malignancies (e.g., prostate and breast cancer). Age-disparity analyses were limited to non-pediatric trials that reported enrollment by age category (older patients [≥ 65 years] vs. younger patients [< 65 years]). To reduce bias from global recruitment patterns, the primary race/ethnicity analysis excluded indications with substantially higher incidence in Asia than in North America (liver, gastroesophageal, lung, cervical, and head and neck cancers) [6]. All indications were included in sensitivity analyses. Owing to sparse reporting and very small numbers, American Indian/Alaska Native participants were not analyzed separately [20].

We derived cancer incidence estimates stratified by age (≥ 65 and < 65 years), gender, race, and ethnicity from the National Cancer Institute’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program database (SEER 21) [21]. For each trial, incidence benchmarks were tailored to the approved indication and the trial’s median enrollment year to account for disease- and time-specific variation. We selected SEER as the reference because its demographic groupings are broadly consistent with FDA-recommended reporting conventions used in US clinical trials [20, 22]. Because FDA approvals are intended to inform treatment in the United States, our analyses benchmarked trial enrollment against US population-based cancer incidence (SEER 21) to evaluate representativeness of the registration evidence base for US patients, rather than to assess the geographic equity of trial recruitment across countries or regions.

We used enrollment incidence ratios (EIRs) to quantify differential enrollment across age, sex, race, and ethnicity. For each subgroup, the EIR was calculated as the subgroup’s trial proportion divided by its corresponding SEER incidence proportion:

EIR values > 1 indicated overrepresentation relative to population incidence, whereas values < 1 indicated underrepresentation. To ensure consistency with SEER classifications, participants reported as Asian and Pacific Islander were pooled into a single category [20]. Detailed method descriptions are in Method S1.

We pooled log-transformed EIRs and corresponding standard errors across trials using inverse-variance weighting. Overall enrollment disparities were summarized with random-effects meta-analysis, and between-trial heterogeneity was quantified using the I [2] statistic. We then fit random-effects multivariable meta-regression models to examine associations between trial-level characteristics and enrollment disparities, using trial-specific EIRs as the dependent measure (see Method S1 for detailed description). We further assessed temporal changes in enrollment disparities by regressing log-transformed EIRs on the FDA approval year of the investigational therapy evaluated in each trial. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors, with values < 5 indicating no material collinearity [23]. We performed two policy-informed stratified analyses using the trial enrollment midpoint, defined as the calendar midpoint of each RCT’s enrollment period. To reflect the NIH 2018 requirement to justify enrollment targets by sex, race, and ethnicity (NOT-OD-18-014), we compared trials with enrollment midpoint before versus on/after 2019. To account for delayed implementation of the FDA’s 2020 diversity guidance, we compared trials with enrollment midpoint before versus on/after 2021. For each stratification, we compared representation metrics between periods and repeated the primary disparity analyses within strata.

Sensitivity analyses

To examine robustness and potential sources of heterogeneity, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by (1) pediatric status; (2) FDA approval year; (3) accelerated-approval designation; (4) trial phase; (5) study design; (6) cancer type; (7) sample size; (8) industry sponsorship; (9) primary endpoint; and (10) sex composition of the enrolled cohort. Subgroup definitions are summarized in Table S2.

We conducted post hoc sensitivity analyses of race and ethnicity that included all trials irrespective of indication. To mitigate potential distortion from globally conducted studies with substantial recruitment in Asia, we also evaluated Black representation using an EIR defined by the trial Black-to-White enrollment ratio relative to the corresponding Black-to-White incidence ratio in SEER.

Results

We identified 399 pivotal oncology drug trials. Twenty-seven records were excluded: 10 because the approved indications were not cancer-related, three because they pertained to diagnostic assay approvals, six because they reflected label updates without additional trials, and eight due to unavailable data. The final analysis included 372 unique trials. Details of approval year, approved therapies, and the supporting trials are summarized in Table S3. Among the 372 included trials, pediatric studies comprised 21 (5.6%). Accelerated approval was granted for 71 trials (21.6%), which enrolled 14,521 participants (8.8%). 240 (64.5%) trials were phase Ⅲ. Trial sizes were small (< 100 participants) in 43 trials (11.6%), intermediate (100–500) in 192 (51.6%), and large (> 500) in 137 (36.8%), with large trials accounting for 130,385 participants (70.4%). The most commonly studied disease settings were hematologic malignancies (102 [27.4%]), lung and bronchus (69 [18.6%]), genitourinary cancers (48 [12.9%]), and digestive system cancers (50 [13.4%]). Breast cancer trials (33 [8.9%]) contributed the largest proportion of participants (39,030 [21.1%]). Targeted therapy (133 [35.8%]) and immunotherapy (97 [26.1%]) were the most frequently evaluated treatment regimens. Most trials employed non-randomized designs (259 [69.6%]), and 348 (93.6%) were industry-sponsored. Primary endpoints were predominantly progression-free survival; overall survival served as the primary endpoint in 78 trials (21.0%). Additional details are provided in Table 1.

Demographic differences between trial cohorts and the US population

Across the included trials, sex-specific enrollment was broadly consistent with population benchmarks. Women were slightly underrepresented (EIR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.88–0.95), whereas men were approximately proportionately represented, with only a marginal excess (EIR, 1.01; 95% CI, 1.00-1.04). Racial subgroup analyses indicated proportional representation of White participants (EIR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.97–1.01). By contrast, Black participants were markedly underrepresented (EIR, 0.25; 95% CI, 0.21–0.28), while Asian or Pacific Islander participants were overrepresented (EIR, 1.54; 95% CI, 1.33–1.79). For ethnicity, data from 144 trials showed substantial under-enrollment of Hispanic individuals (EIR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.45–0.58), accompanied by over-enrollment of non-Hispanic participants (EIR, 1.07; 95% CI, 1.05–1.10). Age-based analyses across 166 trials demonstrated pronounced underrepresentation of older adults (EIR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.61–0.73) and corresponding overrepresentation of younger participants (EIR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.15–1.38). Sensitivity analyses

Figure 3 shows that, in pediatric trials, representation was broadly proportional for female participants (EIR, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.91–1.20) and Black participants (EIR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.66–1.56). By contrast, Hispanic participants remained underrepresented (EIR, 0.62; 95% CI, 0.38–1.03). Estimates for Asian participants (EIR, 1.14; 95% CI, 0.63–2.07), non-Hispanic individuals (EIR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.02–1.22), and White participants (EIR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.80–1.05) are reported as previously presented, pending updated subgroup tables. In accelerated approval trials, female participants were approximately proportionally represented (EIR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.92–1.07), whereas Black (EIR, 0.30; 95% CI, 0.24–0.37), Hispanic (EIR, 0.32; 95% CI, 0.25–0.40), and older adult participants (EIR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.51–0.80) were substantially underrepresented. Across the remaining subgroup analyses, female representation was lower than expected in most strata; however, proportional representation was observed in phase 1 trials (EIR, 1.02; 95% CI, 0.92–1.12), skin cancer trials (EIR, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.91–1.18), and childhood cancer trials (EIR, 1.09; 95% CI, 0.94–1.28). Black participants were consistently underrepresented across subgroups, with the lowest EIR observed in respiratory system cancer trials (EIR, 0.04; 95% CI, 0.02–0.11). Patterns for Hispanic participants and older adults were generally consistent with the primary analysis. Additional subgroup-specific data for male participants, younger adults, non-Hispanic participants, White individuals, and Asian or Pacific Islander individuals can be found in Tables S4-S8.

Robustness checks based on sensitivity analyses produced patterns consistent with the primary results. Using Black-to-White enrollment ratios, Black representation remained low relative to White participants (EIR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.32–0.43) (Table S9). When all trials across indications were included, race-stratified analyses showed marked underenrollment of Black participants (EIR, 0.21; 95% CI, 0.19–0.24) and near-proportional enrollment of White participants (EIR, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.88–0.93), alongside substantial overenrollment of Asian or Pacific Islander participants (EIR, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.73–2.25). Ethnicity-stratified results were similar: Hispanic participants were underrepresented (EIR, 0.52; 95% CI, 0.46–0.59), whereas non-Hispanic participants were modestly overrepresented (EIR, 1.07; 95% CI, 1.05–0.09).

Meta-regression analyses

We examined four patient groups that have been historically underrepresented in clinical trials: female, older, Hispanic, and Black participants. Univariable meta-regression (Fig. 4) indicated a significant increase in the enrollment of female patients over time (β = 0.02, p = 0.048). In contrast, no significant temporal trends were observed for Black participants (β=-0.004, p = 0.88) or Hispanic participants (β = 0.04, p = 0.15). Enrollment among older adults showed a slight, non-significant decrease over time (β=-0.03, p = 0.21).

Trends over time in representation gaps in cancer clinical trials by race, ethnicity, sex, and age

Multivariable meta-regression (Table S10) showed that older-adult representation increased in male-dominated trials (β = 0.44, p < 0.001). Hispanic representation was lower in trials receiving accelerated approval (β=-0.54, p = 0.006). For women, higher representation was observed in studies using non-OS primary endpoints (β = 0.10, p = 0.002). Black representation was reduced in phase 3 versus phase 1–2 trials (β=-0.66, p = 0.002), in accelerated-approval trials (β=-0.40, p = 0.024), and in industry-sponsored trials (β=-1.00, p < 0.001), but was higher in trials with non-OS endpoints (β = 0.29, p = 0.022) and in pediatric trials (β = 0.90, p = 0.002). No multicollinearity was observed (Table S11).

In policy-informed stratified analyses based on the trial enrollment midpoint, EIRs showed no consistent improvement across groups. Female representation remained stable across both cut years. Black representation remained markedly low, with only modest variation across periods. Hispanic representation was numerically higher in the post-policy strata (post-2019 and post-2021), whereas older-adult representation was lower after 2019 and showed little difference across the 2021 cut. Detailed results are provided in Table S12.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to comprehensively assess demographic representation in pivotal oncology trials supporting FDA approvals in the post-2018 policy era. Earlier work has primarily examined prior time periods or selected tumor types, and has not fully accounted for the changing regulatory and operational context of contemporary oncology drug development. Despite increased regulatory attention to trial diversity [11, 12], women, older adults, and Black and Hispanic patients remain persistently underrepresented. This sustained mismatch between trial cohorts and the US cancer burden raises concerns about external validity and the equitable translation of newly approved therapies into clinical practice. As precision medicine advances, representative enrollment is essential for both scientific validity and health equity, because biomarker-driven trials generate subgroup-specific evidence and may magnify inequities related to access to molecular testing and specialized care [24].

Although some included trials enrolled patients internationally, benchmarking enrollment against SEER incidence remains appropriate because FDA approvals are intended to inform treatment for the US population. We also performed sensitivity analyses to address potential bias from indications with regionally skewed incidence patterns [6].

Women remained underenrolled in pivotal oncology trials. This pattern, also noted in prior work, likely reflects multiple contributing factors, including eligibility restrictions, recruitment and referral practices, and unequal access to specialty care [25]. In our analysis, trials using OS as the primary endpoint exhibited particularly low female participation, potentially related to longer follow-up requirements and stricter performance-status criteria. Given established sex-related differences in tumor biology, pharmacokinetics, and treatment toxicity, insufficient enrollment of women can compromise the generalizability of efficacy findings and the adequacy of safety characterization for oncology therapies [26, 27].

Our findings demonstrate substantial underenrollment of Black and Hispanic patients in pivotal oncology trials, with participation far below what would be expected based on their share of the US cancer burden. These gaps likely arise from persistent structural obstacles, including limited access to academic trial sites, socioeconomic constraints, and insufficient community-engaged recruitment. Because tumor biology and treatment response can vary across populations, inadequate inclusion compromises the ability to reliably characterize efficacy and safety for historically underserved groups [28].

Older adults, who account for most cancer diagnoses, were persistently underrepresented in pivotal oncology trials. Across the included studies, trial participants were generally younger than the population at diagnosis, indicating a sustained age-related enrollment bias [7]. This pattern may reflect restrictive eligibility criteria, concerns regarding comorbidity burden and polypharmacy, and clinician or patient hesitancy about trial participation among older individuals [29]. Underenrollment of older adults limits the external validity of trial evidence, particularly because aging is associated with differences in pharmacology [30], susceptibility to treatment-related toxicities, and the balance of benefits and harms. As oncology care becomes increasingly individualized, enrolling heterogeneous older populations is essential to support evidence-based, safe, and effective treatment decisions.

These persistent gaps are partly attributable to structural constraints that impede equitable access to clinical research. Financial strain, limited paid leave, and inadequate insurance can render participation infeasible for patients unable to cover travel, lodging, or time away from work [31]. The clustering of oncology trial sites within large academic centers further limits access for individuals in rural or under-resourced communities, where oncology capacity and referral pathways are less robust [9]. Transportation barriers, childcare obligations, and other indirect costs disproportionately burden racial/ethnic minority and low-income patients, widening enrollment differences [25]. In addition, historical and ongoing mistrust of medical research—shaped by inequitable experiences within the health system—may further reduce willingness to enroll [32]. Together, these overlapping socioeconomic and geographic obstacles constrain access for populations with substantial cancer burden, perpetuating inequities in both trial representation and downstream therapeutic benefit. Closing these gaps will require expanding decentralized and community-based trial infrastructure and providing financial and practical supports that lower the cost of participation [28].

Our meta-regression indicates modest progress over time, with trial cohorts becoming slightly more aligned with the demographics of the underlying cancer population. Nonetheless, meaningful gaps remain, especially for racial and ethnic minority groups. Black and Hispanic participants continued to be substantially underrepresented, and we did not observe statistically significant improvements in their enrollment. We also found that trial characteristics contribute to variability in diversity: industry-sponsored studies were associated with lower representation of Asian participants, and two-arm designs were linked to reduced Hispanic enrollment. These findings align with prior evidence that sponsorship, design complexity, and endpoint choices can affect trial accessibility and inclusiveness [33]. Reducing these inequities will likely require deliberate attention to trial design features alongside strengthened regulatory expectations and accountability.

The continued underenrollment of diverse populations in registration trials underpinning FDA approvals has important consequences for health equity. In the absence of representative participation, trial evidence may not adequately characterize subgroup differences in efficacy and toxicity, which can translate into unequal access to—and benefit from—newly approved therapies [34]. Closing these gaps will require coordinated, multilevel action, including more inclusive eligibility and trial designs, sustained community partnership to strengthen trust and participation, and rigorous implementation of federal diversity requirements [35]. As regulators place greater weight on demographic reporting and equity, achieving representative enrollment is both a scientific necessity and an ethical obligation.

Future directions

Advancing equity in pivotal oncology trials will require aligned action across trial design, regulatory oversight, and community partnership. Future work should focus on implementing and testing approaches that increase demographic representativeness, including routine expansion of eligibility criteria, decentralization of trial operations, and tailored outreach to groups that have been historically underenrolled [33]. Locating trial sites in community oncology practices and safety-net systems may improve access for racial/ethnic minority, low-income, and older patients [36]. At the same time, standardized, granular reporting of participant demographics—race, ethnicity, sex, and age—is necessary to track progress and pinpoint remaining gaps. Regulators and sponsors should move from nonbinding recommendations to enforceable accountability mechanisms, including Diversity Action Plans and incentive structures tied to enrollment performance [37]. These measures are critical to ensuring that clinical trial evidence reflects the populations most affected by cancer and supports equitable translation into clinical practice. For instance, Diversity Action Plans could require sponsors to prespecify measurable enrollment targets and actionable tactics as part of trial registration or funding review. Routine public disclosure of enrollment demographics would further support transparency and enable monitoring [9]. Adherence could be strengthened through performance-linked incentives (e.g., eligibility for supplemental funding or priority review) and, when warranted, corrective steps for persistent noncompliance [38]. FDA guidance on trial diversity offers an initial foundation for this approach, and recent oncology-focused proposals describe practical methods to operationalize and evaluate these strategies [37]. Embedding these tools into trial oversight would help ensure that equity goals translate into measurable and durable gains in representative participation.

Limitations

First, because we relied on publicly available data sources, our estimates may be subject to reporting bias and data incompleteness, particularly for unpublished studies or cancer cases not captured in registry reporting. Second, due to small numbers and inconsistent reporting, racial groups with low population counts—such as American Indian and Alaska Native individuals—were not analyzed as separate subgroups, which limits inference for these populations. Third, our focus on trials that directly supported FDA approvals emphasizes US regulatory evidence and may not reflect representativeness patterns in the broader global oncology trial ecosystem. Fourth, the inclusion of multiple malignancies, therapeutic modalities, study designs, and approval years introduces substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, which may dilute disparities that are specific to particular tumor types or drug classes despite stratified and sensitivity analyses. Finally, protocol amendments (e.g., eligibility changes, cohort expansions, or site additions) may influence subgroup recruitment, but amendment histories and their substantive implications are not uniformly reported in a structured, extractable format across trials. As a result, we could not reliably code amendment-related variables for inclusion in meta-regression analyses.

Conclusion

In this analysis of pivotal trials underpinning FDA approvals of oncology drugs between 2018 and 2026, women, older adults, Black patients, and Hispanic patients remained consistently underrepresented. Despite recent federal efforts to enhance diversity in clinical research, participation gaps persisted, showing only modest improvement over time. Closing these gaps will likely require broader eligibility criteria, more effective and targeted recruitment strategies, and intentional site selection so that enrolled populations more closely mirror the US cancer population.
