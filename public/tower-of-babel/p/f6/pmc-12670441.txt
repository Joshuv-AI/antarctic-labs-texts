Combined trabeculectomy with non-valved drainage implant under a single shared scleral flap: A novel approach to refractory glaucoma
Authors: Arnav Panigrahi, Nishtha Singh, Saloni Une, Viney Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This case concerns a young monoocular man with advanced glaucoma and twice explanted Ahmed Glaucoma Valve. A combined trabeculectomy with non-valved glaucoma drainage device implantation was performed, using a novel approach underneath a single shared scleral flap, following which the IOP was controlled at 6-month post-op, without any evidence of conjunctival retraction or tube exposure.

Transcript

Combined trabeculectomy with non-valved drainage implant under a single shared scleral flap-- A novel approach to refractory glaucoma. We present the case of a thirty-year-old monocular man with advanced glaucoma, and history of multiple failed glaucoma surgeries in his right eye. He developed bilateral secondary glaucoma, following vitreoretinal surgery for retinal detachment post congenital cataract surgery, 30-years back. He underwent trabeculectomy twice, both of which failed because of conjunctival scarring. He underwent AGV implantation twice after that, which had to be explanted in both instances, because of conjunctival retraction and tube exposure. He had undergone diode laser cyclophotocoagulation thrice after that as a temporary IOP control measure, which eventually resulted in early corneal decompensation. The patient now needs a repeat glaucoma drainage device implantation for IOP control. Considering the recurrent conjunctival retraction and tube exposure seen with AGV in this young patient, a posteriorly fixed non valved implant, placed using a long tunnel technique, seems to be the safest bet. However, the immediate postoperative IOP control remains a question with this procedure. Hence, we decided to combine it with a trabeculectomy.

A traction suture is passed with 6-0 Vicryl, and BSS is injected subconjunctivally to lift the conjunctiva up, thereby creating a plane for surgical dissection. We begin with a superior fornix-based conjunctival peritomy. The conjunctiva and Tenon's capsule are carefully dissected to expose the sclera in the superotemporal quadrant. Note the extensive subconjunctival fibrosis. The scleral bed is cauterized, and the conjunctiva reflected forwards to ensure adequate apposition. A 5 by 4 mm scleral flap is created at the limbus using a surgical blade. This flap is intended to cover the area of the trabeculectomy, as well as the site of tube entry. A partial thickness scleral incision is made 5–6 mm posterior to the flap, and a scleral tunnel is fashioned connecting the scleral well with the intended site of plate fixation. A three zero silk suture is passed through the lumen of the tube, thereby functioning as a ripcord and impeding free flow. The plate is positioned approximately 8–10 mm posterior to the limbus, and secured to the sclera using non-absorbable sutures, hence ensuring stable placement. The lumen is occluded with ligature knots using 8-0 vicryl sutures. The tube is passed underneath the scleral tunnel using a microsurgical forceps. Mitomycin soaked sponges are kept underneath the conjunctiva and the scleral flap, to reduce the risk of early postoperative fibrosis. Releasable sutures are passed in the meanwhile. The sponges are removed, and a thorough saline wash is given. The tube is cut bevel down, intended for sulcus placement. Entry is made in the posterior part of the scleral well with a 23 gauge needle, and the tube placed in the sulcus. An MVR entry is made anterior to the site of tube entry, and a scleral ostium fashioned using a Kelly's punch. The releasable sutures are firmly tied, and the free end of ripcord placed underneath the conjunctiva. Two wing sutures are tied. Either 10-0 nylon or 8-0 vicryl can be used. We've used the latter in this case, owing to its superior thickness and strength which ensures conjunctival apposition and prevents button-holing. A mattress suture is placed, and the conjunctival buttonholes closed with 8-0 vicryl suture. Given that the procedure was performed in a myopic, vitrectomised eye, intra- and postoperative hypotony and related complications are anticipated; therefore, a copious viscoelastic fill is recommended at the conclusion of surgery.

The patient was ambulatory, his best corrected visual acuity 6/60, and IOP 13 at the end of 1 week. At the end of 6 months, he had a visual acuity of 6/60 and an IOP of 12, without any medication. No conjunctival retraction or tube exposure was seen at this point. A combination of trabeculectomy with a non-valved implant has been previously documented in literature, albeit in a very few cases. However, our approach of using a single scleral flap as a site for both is novel. It helps in limiting the lateral extent of conjunctival dissection, which is advantageous in eyes with multiple previous intraocular surgeries, where the conjunctival reserve is poor. The tube is covered by the patient's own sclera throughout its extent, thereby eliminating the risk of exposure. Thank you for watching this video. Although surgically challenging, this technique can be attempted in such eyes where every other procedure is doomed to fail.

Claim of priority

After conducting a literature review on 12.06.25 utilizing PubMed, Google Scholar, and Semantic Scholar using the key words 'trabeculectomy', 'glaucoma drainage device', 'refractory glaucoma' OR 'failed glaucoma surgery', we did not find any prior reports of trabeculectomy combined with non-valved GDD under a single scleral flap. However, separate site simultaneous surgery have been previously described, and has been appropriately referred to in the video.
