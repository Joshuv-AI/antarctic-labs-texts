Relation of Cognitive Reserve Indicator to Heart Disease and Cardiac Structure and Function: A Large Community‐Based Longitudinal Study
Authors: Xuerui Li, Wenzhe Yang, Yuyang Miao, Abigail Dove, Jiao Wang, Tingting Du, Zhongze Fang, Weili Xu, Qiang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh cognitive reserve (CR) has been related to lower dementia risk, but its association with heart disease (HD) is unknown. We aimed to explore the relation of CR to HD and cardiac structure and function.Methods and ResultsWithin the UK Biobank, 349 907 HD‐free participants were followed up. A composite CR indicator involving education/occupation attainment/television viewing time/confiding frequency/social connection frequency/variety of leisure activities was generated, and further categorized into low/moderate/high levels. Incident HD, including coronary HD, cardiac arrhythmia, and heart failure, was ascertained on the basis of medical records. During the follow‐up, a subsample (n=31 182) underwent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging to assess ventricular structure and function. Data were analyzed using Cox regression, Laplace regression, and linear regression. Compared with low CR, the hazard ratio and 95% CI of any HD for high CR was 0.78 (0.75–0.80) (including 0.68 [0.66–0.71] for coronary HD, 0.91 [0.87–0.95] for cardiac arrhythmia, and 0.63 [0.58–0.68] for heart failure). Furthermore, high CR was associated with delayed HD onset by 1.59 (95% CI, 1.37–1.82) years compared with low CR. In cardiac magnetic resonance imaging data analysis, compared with low CR, high CR was associated with larger left ventricular end‐diastolic volume (β, 0.13 [95% CI, 0.09–0.17]), left ventricular end‐systolic volume (β, 0.05 [95% CI, 0.01–0.10]), left ventricular stroke volume (β, 0.16 [95% CI, 0.12–0.21]), and left ventricular ejection fraction (β, 0.08 [95% CI, 0.03–0.13]).ConclusionsHigh CR is associated with favorable HD health. Our findings suggest that the beneficial effect of CR is not limited to dementia but also HD.

Heart disease (HD) is 1 of the most common noncommunicable diseases and a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. 1 , 2 According to the World Health Organization report, the number of deaths from HD increased to nearly 9 million in 2019, accounting for 16% of total deaths from all causes. 3 Identifying modifiable risk and protective factors for HD is therefore of great significance for public health.

Engagement in mentally and socially stimulating activities has been associated with reduced risk of dementia, even in the face of age‐related pathologic changes, a concept known as cognitive reserve (CR). 4 Emerging evidence suggests that CR may reduce the risk of other medical conditions in addition to dementia. 5 , 6 Thus, further exploring the benefits of CR for common chronic diseases, such as HD, may provide new perspectives on CR. However, there is no standard definition for the assessment of CR, and the measurement of CR in previous studies has relied on proxies based on self‐reported measures of mental and social behavior, such as educational and occupational attainment, socioeconomic status, social engagement, leisure activities, and so on. 7 , 8 , 9

Favorable associations between individual CR‐related factors and HD have been documented. Higher education level, the most commonly used proxy of CR, has been linked to the delayed onset of HD. 10 , 11 A previous study reported an association between HD and occupational attainment, as another key proxy of CR, but the type of occupation most associated with HD was unclear. 12 Social activity, such as volunteering, has been identified as a potential protective factor for HD. 13 Finally, lower levels of sedentary behavior, such as less television viewing, have also been reported to be beneficial for cardiovascular health. 14 , 15 However, CR is a dynamic construct influenced by different experiences across the life course, and 1 reserve‐enhancing factor alone cannot fully explain the accumulation of CR. 9 , 16 To our knowledge, no study has explored the influence of a composite indicator of CR on HD.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMRI) provides accurate and reproducible measurements of HD pathophysiology and is considered the gold standard for the assessment of cardiac structure and function. 17 To date, literature on the association between CR‐related factors and cardiac structural measures is scarce. 18 Examining the relationship between CR indicators and CMRI measures reflecting the structure and function of the cardiac chambers has the potential to provide novel insights into the association of CR with HD processes.

In the current study, using data from the UK Biobank, we aimed to (1) assess the association between CR indicators and incident HD and HD subtypes and (2) explore the relationship between CR and measures of cardiac structure and function from CMRI.

METHODS

Data access to the UK Biobank will need to be requested through a standard data access procedure. Requests to access these data sets should be directed to http://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/register‐apply.

Study Population

The UK Biobank is a population‐based prospective cohort study of over half a million participants, aged 40 to 70 years, recruited from 22 sites across the United Kingdom. 19 Between 2006 and 2010, 502 394 individuals participated in the baseline survey and underwent sociodemographic, physical, cognitive, and medical assessments. After excluding 43 120 with prevalent HD at baseline and 109 367 with missing data on CR‐related factors, we could include 349 907 participants in the analysis of the association between CR and incident HD. Between 2014 and 2020, a subsample (n=32 586) of the population underwent a CMRI scan. After further excluding people with incident HD before the CMRI scan, 31 182 participants were available for the analysis of the association between CR and CMRI measures (Figure 1).

The UK Biobank project received ethical approval from the National Health Services National Research Ethics Service (reference 11/NW/0382). Data Collection

Data on demographic characteristics (age, sex, and race) and lifestyle factors (including alcohol consumption, smoking, and physical activity) were obtained at baseline through computerized touch‐screen questionnaires. Blood samples were collected at baseline for the measurement of biomarkers, including total cholesterol, fasting plasma glucose, and hemoglobin A1c.

Race was dichotomized as White versus mixed, Asian or Asian British, Black or Black British, Chinese, or other ethnic groups. Height and weight were measured, and body mass index was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2) and was further classified into 4 groups (ie, <18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9, and ≥30 kg/m2). Smoking and alcohol consumption were categorized as never, previous, and current smoking/drinking, and the information on the amount of smoking and alcohol consumption was detailed in Data S1. Regular physical activity was defined as at least 150 minutes of moderate activity per week, 75 minutes of vigorous activity per week, or an equivalent combination. 20

Total cholesterol was assayed using the Beckman Coulter AU5800 Platform. Blood pressure was measured using the Omron 705 IT electronic blood pressure monitor at baseline. Two readings were recorded for each participant, and the average of the 2 measurements was calculated. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, or the use of antihypertensive drugs. Diabetes was identified on the basis of medical records, self‐reported medical history, use of diabetes medication, hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%, or fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL. Stroke was ascertained from medical records or self‐reported medical history. Dementia was ascertained on the basis of information from self‐reports, medical records, and death records.

Furthermore, chronic diseases were determined using primary and secondary diagnoses from the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9), and International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10), hospital admission data from England, Scotland, and Wales, and cancer registry data. 21 ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 codes were organized into 12 chronic disease subgroups by ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 chapter (Table S1). Chronic disease status was dichotomized as 0 versus ≥1 of these disorders.

Assessment of CR

A CR indicator was generated on the basis of 6 reserve‐related factors: education, occupational attainment, television viewing time, frequency of confiding, frequency of social connection, and the number of leisure activities. 22 These factors were self‐reported through computerized touchscreen questionnaires at baseline.

Education

Education was categorized as follows: (1) no educational qualifications; (2) certificate of secondary education or equivalent, O levels/general certificate of secondary education or equivalent, and A/AS levels or equivalent; (3) other professional qualifications; (4) national vocational qualification, higher national diploma, higher national certificate, or equivalent; and (5) college/university degree.

Occupation Attainment

Participants self‐reported their occupations according to a prespecified list of job codes. These were matched to socioeconomic classification (SEC) levels based on the Standard Occupational Classification 2000 system developed by the UK Office of National Statistics. 23 SEC levels are ordinal variables, with lower values representing more advanced occupation attainment (ie, jobs that require a higher skill level and more training). 24 Occupational attainment was categorized as follows: (1) unemployed or SEC 7 (routine occupations); (2) SEC 4 to 6 (small employers and own account workers, lower supervisory and technical occupations, and semiroutine occupations); (3) SEC 3 (intermediate occupations); (4) SEC 2 (lower managerial and professional occupations); (5) SEC 1.2 or SEC 1.1 (large employers and higher managerial occupations, and higher professional occupations).

Television Viewing Time

Participants' self‐reported total hours of daily television watching (hours/day) were categorized as follows: (1) ≥4, (2) 3 to 3.9, (3) 2 to 2.9, and (4) <2 hours/day.

Confiding Frequency

Participants answered a question about how often they were able to confide in people close to them. Responses were categorized into 4 groups: (1) never; (2) about once a month or less; (3) 1–4 times a week; and (4) almost daily.

Social Connection

Participants answered a question about how often they made or received friend/family visits. Responses were categorized as follows: (1) about once a month or less; (2) about once a week; (3) 2 to 4 times a week; and (4) almost daily.

Engagement in Leisure/Social Activities

Participants were asked about the kinds of activities they attend once a week or more (including sports club or gym, pub or social club, religious group, adult education class, or other group activity). Number of activities was categorized as: (1) 0, (2) 1, and (3) 2 to 5.

Generation of CR Indicator

Latent class analysis is a statistical technique that can identify unobservable groups or clusters by grouping multiple observed variables into mutually exclusive latent classes to find the best‐fitting model for the data. 25 In the present study, we used latent class analysis to generate a CR indicator based on the 6 aforementioned reserve‐related factors. After comprehensively considering the statistics of the model selection and the uncertainty of the posterior classification, a 3‐latent class model was identified (Table S2). Latent class 1 was characterized by generally high levels of CR‐related variables, with participants having more favorable levels of education, occupational attainment, television viewing time, confiding frequency, and social connection. Latent class 2 was characterized by a moderate level of CR‐related factors. Latent class 3 was represented a relatively lower levels of all CR‐related factors (Table S3). On the basis of these classes, CR level was categorized as high, moderate, and low, respectively.

Assessment of HD

In the current study, HD was defined as the presence of coronary HD (CHD; including myocardial infarction, heart attack, and angina pectoris), cardiac arrhythmias, or heart failure and assessed from medical records (field identifier: 131297, ICD‐10: I20–I25) or self‐reported medical history (field identifier: 3627, 3894, 6150, and 20002 [1074 and 1075]). Additionally, HD was also defined in terms of the total number of com orbid HDs (CHD, cardiac arrhythmias, or heart failure): 0, 1, 2, or 3.

Assessment of CMRI Measurements

Details of the UK Biobank CMRI imaging protocol have been described previously. 26 Briefly, all examinations were performed on a wide‐bore 1.5‐T scanner (MAGNETOM Aera, Syngo Platform VD13A, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany), with typical cine parameters as follows: repetition time/echo time=2.6/1.1 milliseconds, flip angle 80°, Grappa factor 2, voxel size 1.8×1.8×8 mm, and a slice gap of 2.0 mm. Long‐axis cines and a complete short‐axis stack of balanced steady‐state free precession cines were acquired for the left and right ventricles to assess cardiac function. Examination of CMRI measures was performed manually by observers under supervision across 2 core laboratories in London and Oxford, according to preapproved standard operating procedures using dedicated postprocessing software (cvi42, version 5.1.1, Circle Cardiovascular Imaging Inc, Calgary, Canada). In the current study, we analyzed the following CMRI parameters: left ventricular end‐diastolic volume (LVEDV) and right ventricular end‐diastolic volume, left ventricular end‐systolic volume (LVESV) and right ventricular end‐systolic volume, left ventricular stroke volume (LVSV) and right ventricular stroke volume, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and right ventricular ejection fraction, and left ventricular mass. All CMRI parameters were converted to z‐scores in statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics of study participants by CR level were compared using 1‐way ANOVA for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables.

Cox proportional hazard regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs of HD and its subtype incidence according to CR level. Follow‐up time was calculated as the time from the study entry to the first occurrence of HD, death, or the end of follow‐up (February 28, 2022). The 10th percentile difference in HD and subtype onset time in relation to CR indicators was estimated using Laplace regression, which allowed us to quantify the absolute differences in the HD onset among people with different CR levels. We also performed stratified analyses by baseline age (middle‐aged [<60 years] versus older age [≥60 years]), sex (men versus women), and chronic condition status (no versus yes). Multiplicative interaction was examined by incorporating the CR status, the factor of interest (age, sex, or chronic condition status), and their cross‐product term in the same model to detect the difference between stratifications. Linear regression models were used to test the association between CR indicators and standardized cardiac structural and functional parameters in the subsample with CMRI data. All analyses were first adjusted for age, sex, and race, and then further adjusted for smoking, alcohol consumption, body mass index, physical activity, total cholesterol, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and dementia. We have performed the collinearity examination of covariates using variance inflation factor, and the results showed no collinearity between covariables (mean variance inflation factor=1.22; range, 1.02–1.91).

In the sensitivity analysis, we repeated the analysis using a Fine and Gray competing risk regression model with death as a competing event, using individual CR indicators, and imputing CR‐missing data using the Rubin rule for pooling estimates. We also used a Cox regression model to verify the association between CR and subsequent dementia in the current study.

In all tests, 2‐tailed P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata SE 15.0 for Windows (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

RESULTS

Characteristics of the Study Population

Among all HD‐free participants (mean±SD age, 54.42±7.75 years; 55.74% women), 52 954 (15.13%), 147 999 (42.3%), and 148 954 (42.57%) were included in the low, moderate, and high CR groups, respectively. Compared with participants with low CR, those with moderate or high CR were younger, more likely to drink alcohol, not smoke, and engage in physical activity, more likely to have a lower body mass index, and less likely to have diabetes, hypertension, stroke, and dementia. Additionally, compared with participants with low CR, those with moderate CR were more likely to be women and those with high CR were more likely to be non‐White race and have lower cholesterol (Table 1). The characteristics of participants with and without CR‐related factors are shown in Table S4. Furthermore, the distribution of participant characteristics by CR status was similar in the subsample with CMRI data (Table S5), although this subsample was younger and had fewer chronic health conditions than the overall study population (Table S6).

Relationship Between CR Indicator and HD

During the follow‐up (median, 12.81 years; interquartile range, 11.97–13.61 years), 33 091 (9.46%) participants developed HD. In multiadjusted Cox models, compared with participants with low CR, the HR (95% CI) of HD was 0.84 (0.82–0.87) for those with moderate CR and 0.78 (0.75–0.80) for those with high CR (Table 2). Kaplan‐Meier estimate curves for HD by different CR groups are presented in Figure S1. Laplace regression analysis showed that the multiadjusted 10th percentile onset time of HD was 12.9 years for participants with low CR, 14.0 years for those with moderate CR, and 14.5 years for those with high CR. Having a high CR indicator therefore delayed the onset of HD by 1.59 (range, 1.37–1.82) years compared with having low CR (Figure 2).

In terms of specific forms of HD, compared with participants with low CR, those with high CR also had lower risk of CHD (HR, 0.68 [95% CI, 0.66–0.71]), cardiac arrhythmia (HR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.87–0.95]), and heart failure (HR, 0.63 [95% CI, 0.58–0.68]). Additionally, compared with low CR, high CR was associated with 2.61 (10th percentile difference [95% CI, 2.31–2.91]) years delayed onset of CHD, 0.57 (10th percentile difference [95% CI, 0.27–0.87]) years delayed onset of cardiac arrhythmia, and 2.12 (10th percentile difference [95% CI, 1.76–2.47]) years delayed onset of heart failure (Table 2 and Table S7).

Furthermore, considering the possibility of comorbid HDs, high CR was associated with lower risk of 1 HD (HR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.77–0.83]), 2 HDs (HR, 0.69 [95% CI, 0.64–0.74]), and 3 HDs (HR, 0.61 [95% CI, 0.52–0.71]), indicating that the beneficial effects of CR were strengthened when multiple HDs were co‐occurring (Table S8).

In stratified analysis by chronic conditions, the significant association between CR and HD was similar in participants with and without chronic conditions (P interaction=0.670; Table S9). In terms of age and sex, the CR‐HD association was also significant in each stratification of age and sex, especially among middle‐aged participants (<60 years) and women (all P interaction<0.001; Tables S10 and S11).

Relationship Between CR and Cardiac Structural and Functional Parameters

In linear regression models, compared with the low CR, moderate CR was associated with higher LVEDV (standardized β, 0.09 [95% CI, 0.05–0.13]), LVESV (standardized β, 0.05 [95% CI, 0.00–0.09]), and LVSV (standardized β, 0.11 [95% CI, 0.06–0.15]), and high CR was associated with increased LVEDV (standardized β, 0.13 [95% CI, 0.09–0.17]), LVESV (standardized β, 0.05 [95% CI, 0.01–0.10]), LVSV (standardized β, 0.16 [95% CI, 0.12–0.21]), and LVEF (standardized β, 0.08 [95% CI, 0.03–0.13]) (Table 3). Because of the small sample size available for the right ventricular parameters, there was no significant relationship between CR and indicators of right ventricular structure and function (Table S12).

Supplementary Analysis

Similar results to the main analyses were obtained when we (1) accounted for the competing risk of death (Table S13); (2) considered smoking and alcohol consumption as continuous variables (Table S14); and (3) imputed missing data of CR (Table S15). Furthermore, as verification of the association between CR and dementia in the current study, the HRs (95% CI) of dementia were 0.64 (0.58–0.71) for moderate CR and 0.54 (0.48–0.61) for high CR compared with low CR (after excluding participants with dementia at baseline, n=349 813). Moreover, when using individual CR indicators to repeat the association with HD, the higher level of education, occupational attainment, and confiding frequency and lower television viewing time were associated with lower risk of HD, whereas no significant protective role of social connection frequency and number of leisure/social activity types against HD was found (Table S16).

DISCUSSION

In this large population‐based longitudinal study from the UK Biobank, we found that (1) both moderate and high levels of CR, reflecting education level, occupational attainment, screen‐based sedentary behavior, confiding frequency, and social activities, were associated with lower risk of HD (including CHD, cardiac arrhythmia, and heart failure) and could delay the onset of HD by >1 year; and (2) high CR was related to more favorable indicators of left ventricular structure and function, including LVEDV, LVESV, LVSV, and LVEF.

CR's association with reduced dementia risk is well established, but it seems that CR may have benefits beyond dementia. In a previous study from our group, higher levels of a composite CR indicator were associated with prolonged disability‐free survival. 5 Additionally, CR‐related factors have been individually related to reduced HD risk. Several studies indicate that people with higher education levels are more likely to have better cardiovascular health, 27 , 28 and a Mendelian randomization study found that low education is a causal risk factor in the development of HD, suggesting that improving education may result in substantial health benefits. 29 In addition to education, other CR‐related factors have also been associated with heart health. A meta‐analysis of 15 prospective cohort studies reported an increased risk of HD with longer television viewing time. 30 A cohort study from Iran indicated that clerical support workers have a higher risk of ischemic heart disease in comparison to craft and related trades workers, 31 whereas another cohort study from Japan did not detect a significant difference in HD risk between manual and nonmanual workers. 32 Research from the US HRS (Health and Retirement Study) demonstrated that social engagement (ie, volunteering and informal helping) is related to lower HD incidence, although social participation (ie, including attendance at religious services and social group meetings) is not. 13

However, these single components may not fully reflect CR, which is a dynamic construct that can be influenced by a range of experiences throughout the life course. Although the use of composite indexes of CR is increasingly used in dementia research, no studies, to our knowledge, have examined the association between a composite CR indicator and HD. In the present study, we used latent class analysis to generate a composite CR indicator based on education level, occupational complexity, and engagement in mentally or socially stimulating activities, and found that compared with low CR, moderate and high CR were associated with a lower risk of HD and could delay the onset of HD (including CHD, cardiac arrhythmia, and heart failure) by >1 year. Our findings may also suggest that the well‐established reduced risk of dementia related to high CR might be partially attributable to the lower risk of HD.

HD, a chronic disease driven by the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis, and subclinical alterations in cardiac structure and function might begin several years before the onset of overt cardiovascular symptoms. 33 , 34 Therefore, exploring alterations in cardiac structure and function through CMRI may provide new insights into the possible mechanisms underlying the CR‐HD association. A prospective cohort from Denmark has indicated that low education (<8 years) is associated with left ventricular hypertrophy and dilatation, reduced LVEF, and severe diastolic dysfunction, but not mild diastolic dysfunction. 27 Data from a German population‐based sample have shown that women with low education levels tend to have a higher ventricular mass and lower ejection fraction. 18 Previous research from the UK Biobank has suggested that higher levels of exercise may result in higher left ventricular, right ventricular, and left atrial volumes. 33 In the current study, we found that high CR was related to relatively larger left ventricular structure and function, including LVEDV, LVESV, LVSV, and LVEF. These findings may indicate that in addition to cognitive health, high CR was associated with favorable cardiac magnetic resonance metrics heaping to resist cardiac aging. However, clinical significance of CMRI markers should be interpreted with caution. Despite a larger left ventricular volume observed in individuals with higher CR, participants who underwent magnetic resonance imaging assessment had a better health status and the ventricular indexes were all within the normal range regardless of different CR groups. Therefore, a larger left ventricular volume might be less likely to reflect cardiac hypertrophy and enlargement. Nevertheless, considering the finding that high CR is associated with reduced risk of heart disease, it is plausible that the cardiac structure and function changes may underlie the association of CR with HD.

The mechanism underlying the CR‐HD association is unclear. Previous studies have suggested that lack of education is related to lower health literacy, limited access to higher‐quality health care, and lower adherence to HD therapies. 27 , 35 Social participation may promote health by influencing health‐related behaviors relevant to HD (such as smoking cessation, adherence to medical treatment, and access to health information) on one hand, and psychological processes (such as emotional support and stress management) on the other. 36 , 37 Furthermore, sedentary behavior has been adversely associated with fasting glucose, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and waist circumference, 38 and these risk factors may further decrease insulin sensitivity and impair vascular endothelial‐dependent dilation, key antecedents of HD. 39 In our study, high CR was associated with larger LVEDV, LVESV, LVSV, and LVEF, suggesting that CR might reduce HD risk by enhancing left ventricular contractile function and improving the pattern of ventricular remodeling. However, further clinicopathologic research will be needed to identify the structural, biochemical, and molecular mechanisms underlying the ability of CR to improve cardiac pathology.

Strengths and Limitations

A strength of the current study is the use of latent class analysis to generate a composite CR indicator incorporating multiple CR‐related factors. Additionally, information on structural cardiac function from CMRI scans has been taken into consideration, allowing us to assess possible mechanistic explanations for the association between CR and HD. Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the CR‐related factors were ascertained through retrospective self‐report, which could be subject to measurement error. Second, HD diagnosis during the follow‐up was identified through medical records, which only included patients who sought treatment in inpatient or outpatient clinics. Therefore, people with milder symptoms or those who did not seek treatment might have been misclassified as HD free, although it is not clear whether misclassification is differential or nondifferential across the exposure groups. Third, because CMRI was performed at only 1 time point, we could not assess the association between CR and changes in cardiac function over time. Finally, the UK Biobank sample is highly selected, which predominantly had highly educated, White participants of European ancestry. Furthermore, compared with individuals who were excluded because of unavailable CR‐related data or who did not undergo CMRI scans, those included in the current study or the cardiac imaging study population tended to have healthier conditions and therefore were less likely to develop cardiovascular events. This could limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations and may have contributed to an underestimation of the magnitude of the associations of CR with HD and measures of cardiac structure and function.

In conclusion, in addition to the mitigating effect of CR on dementia risk, our study provides new evidence that a higher level of CR is associated with a lower risk of HD and significantly delayed onset of HD, including CHD, cardiac arrhythmia, and heart failure. Furthermore, high CR is related to larger left ventricular structure and function, including LVEDV, LVESV, LVSV, and LVEF. Our findings highlight the potential role of mental and social stimulation for the prevention of HD. Additional longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify the mechanisms underlying the association between CR and HD.
