Reproductive health knowledge and preferences in adolescent rheumatology patients: a convergent mixed methods study
Authors: Acela C Rosado, Josheili Llavona-Ortiz, Lisabeth V Scalzi, Cynthia H Chuang, Timothy Hahn, Catherine A Bingham, Cuoghi Edens, Darilyn Rivera-Collazo, Kimberly Hays
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSexual and reproductive health (SRH) guidelines exist for adults with rheumatic diseases (RDs), but not for their adolescent counterparts. SRH discussions are regarded as important areas of discussion by pediatric rheumatology providers. However, adolescents with RDs have unique needs that are not well elucidated. The purpose of this study was to explore SRH knowledge and information sources among female adolescents with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), undifferentiated connective tissue disease (UCTD), mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD), or an overlap disease given their increased risk for SRH-related adverse outcomes.MethodsThis convergent mixed methods study sought to survey and interview participants from a healthcare system in central Pennsylvania. Eligibility included: (1) being 15–21 years old, (2) having SLE, UCTD, MCTD, or an overlap disease, (3) receiving care from a pediatric rheumatologist, (4) being assigned female at birth, and (5) having access to a computer or phone. Quantitatively, descriptive statistics and the Mann-Whitney U test were used for data presentation and assessment. Qualitatively, a semi-structured interview guide was developed, and thematic analysis was conducted. Quantitative and qualitative findings were assessed for convergence and presented using a joint display.ResultsSixteen participants completed the survey, and ten also completed an individual interview. Limited understanding of SRH concepts, the impact of pregnancy, and medication safety were noted in both the survey and interview results. Participants with teratogenic medication use had lower general (2.4 vs. 2.7) and lupus-specific (3.3 vs. 4.4) SRH knowledge scores compared to their counterparts with no teratogenic medication use. Qualitatively, participants voiced concern about the interplay between their disease and pregnancy, reported differences in their information sources, and discussed trustworthy sources of SRH information.ConclusionsThis study identified gaps in SRH knowledge among female adolescents with susceptible RDs. Many participants are receiving SRH information from non-health care sources, but desire to have discussions on SRH with their provider. These findings signal a need for additional research in this area with expansion to other RDs and males. Clinicians and researchers can work alongside this population to develop tools and inform SRH guidelines to address patient concerns and improve overall outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12969-025-01165-5.Keywords: Reproductive health, Pediatric rheumatology, Adolescent medicine, Reproductive health knowledge, Teratogenic

Background

Understanding sexual and reproductive health (SRH) in adolescents with rheumatic diseases (RDs), particularly, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and similar connective tissue diseases, is a crucial yet under-explored area of study. Across the literature, elevated risks related to pregnancy among women diagnosed with RDs, such as SLE, have been established. Some of these risks include elevated rates of miscarriage, pre-eclampsia, preterm birth, and infants with low birth weight [1–4]. Although these risks are known among healthcare professionals, research teams have discovered that awareness of the overall risks of pregnancy among patients with RDs is lacking. Recently, García and colleagues described, through a self-reported questionnaire (rheum reproductive behavior questionnaire), that more than one-third of women with RDs did not receive counseling related to pregnancy risks (34%), contraceptive use (35%), nor safe use of medication during pregnancy (37%) [5]. Previously, Marinello et al. found, via a narrative-based medicine approach, that almost 70% of women with a RD had not received counselling prior to their pregnancy [6]. In the US, Harding and colleagues found that women with SLE from the Maternal Autoimmune Disease Research Alliance (MADRA) Registry were defined as “not medically ready” for pregnancy and experienced significantly worse disease and pregnancy-related outcomes [7]. The data highlights the need to improve the reproductive health knowledge of female patients diagnosed with RDs.

Within the US, adolescent girls between the ages of 15 to 19 experienced the highest rates of unintended pregnancies (≥ 70%) from 2010 to 2019, compared to all other age groups 20 years or older [8]. Moreover, Lindberg & Kantor recently reported that SRH education, by any means, has decreased among younger populations in the US when compared to data from 25 years ago in the national survey of family growth [9]. A crucial intervention that addresses these SRH-related knowledge gaps by providing comprehensive sexual education can increase safe sex behaviors and reduce teenage pregnancies [10]. Early patient-provider discussions to improve informed-decision making and pregnancy planning is also key to helping patients understand how reproductive health can be addressed while acknowledging the potential risks involved within the context of their chronic diseases [11, 12]. On average, SLE presents among female patients between ages 15 and 45, with earlier diagnosis related to more severe disease [13]. In fact, 20% of SLE occurs before the age of 18; therefore, it is important to assess the current state of understanding related to reproductive health in this younger high-risk population [14]. A recent study team conducted focus groups with US-based rheumatology providers and highlighted the importance of identifying communication strategies to discuss SRH with patients with RDs regardless of age [15]. However, available research on this topic from the patient perspective remains limited and involves participation mainly of adult women (≥ 21 years old) [5, 6, 16]. SRH knowledge among adolescent patients with RDs is likely to be lower than adult counterparts, but this has not been widely assessed.

This study sought to characterize and describe the reproductive health beliefs and knowledge of female adolescent patients diagnosed with primarily SLE followed by mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD) or undifferentiated connective tissue disease (UCTD), and their willingness to talk about this topic through a convergent mixed-methods approach. We chose this subpopulation of pediatric rheumatology patients as the female to male prevalence ratio of SLE is 6:1 in adolescents and 9:1 in adults. Female patients with SLE have a high likelihood of receiving teratogenic medications, have higher rates of organ-threatening disease compared to the general population, and are reported to have equal or higher rates of sexual activity and risky sexual behavior compared to healthy counterparts [17, 18]. Studying this unique population to improve health care delivery has the potential to improve health related quality of life for patients with RDs. The main research question that guided this study was: What is the understanding of female adolescent patients with SLE, MCTD, and UCTD, regarding sexual and reproductive health, how it relates to their disease, and how would they like to learn about this topic?

Methods

This convergent mixed methods study was conducted to elucidate the current reproductive health knowledge, beliefs, and practices of adolescent females diagnosed with SLE or related connective tissue diseases in a single center.

Participants and study instruments

Study participants were eligible if they were (1) between the ages of 15 to 21 years old, (2) diagnosed with SLE, MCTD, UCTD, or an overlap disease that included SLE, (3) receiving care by a pediatric rheumatologist at the site, (4) assigned female at birth, and (5) had access to a computer or phone to complete the study activities. Patients were recruited from a clinic within a large healthcare system in south central Pennsylvania. The survey assessed participants’ sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, race, ethnicity, education level, prescribed medication for RD) and SRH knowledge, comfort discussing the topic, and preferred sources of information. The survey items assessing general reproductive health knowledge were drawn from the Mathtech questionnaire, which is a sexuality questionnaire for adolescents known as the Knowledge Test developed by Dr. Douglas Kirby [19]. The connective tissue disease specific questions were curated by study team members. All authors involved in this study revised the questions. The study survey was administered using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) platform.

A semi-structured interview guide was developed by the study team informed by the Health Belief Model, a framework for health behavior research that focuses on understanding what may influence health behaviors such as perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, self-efficacy, and cues to action [20]. The guide (see Additional File 2) included questions developed to assess the same SRH-related topics of the survey (i.e., knowledge, history of discussion, current and preferred information sources). Through interviews, our team sought to acquire further insight into patient personal experiences to amplify the voices of this understudied population. The protocol for this study was approved by the Pennsylvania State University Institutional Review Board on December 20, 2023 (STUDY00014038).

Recruitment methods

Eligible participants for the study were identified by an information management staff member using electronic health record (EHR) data by verifying their age and diagnosis. The list of potential participants was used to confirm participant eligibility using EHR and was updated every 1–2 months to assess if there were additional eligible participants that became age-eligible within the study period (March-September 2024). After confirmation of eligibility, participants were approached in-person during scheduled pediatric rheumatology clinic visits and were provided a QR code to complete a survey and scheduled for the interview via Zoom©. The interviews were recorded for transcription purposes. Participants received a $15 gift card for their time completing each component of the study (up to two $15 gift cards if participants completed both the survey and interview). Recruitment for this study occurred from March 2024 to September 2024.

Quantitative data collection and analysis

All sociodemographic variables, except for insurance type, were self-reported by participants. For clinical characteristics, all variables were extracted from the EHR, except for history of birth control use which was self-reported. Teratogenic medications assessed for this study were cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, and mycophenolate mofetil. Non-teratogenic medications examined were hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine, and azathioprine. Medications evaluated with limited safety data included belimumab, cyclosporine, and rituximab. Medications noted with special considerations were nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and prednisone. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of participants were assessed through frequencies (for categorical variables) and means (for continuous variables) using STATA/BE, a statistical software [21]. Items related to general knowledge and lupus-specific knowledge about reproductive health were re-coded as ‘1’ for correct answers and ‘0’ for incorrect answers to quantify knowledge scores for each section. The maximum possible scores for each section were 5 for items related to general knowledge and 6 for items related to lupus-specific reproductive health knowledge. To assess if differences in general and lupus-specific knowledge scores by use of teratogenic medication were significant, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test for unmatched data was used. Statistical significance was evaluated using an alpha level of 0.05. Frequencies were used to assess items related to the participants’ comfort talking about the topic and their preferred sources of information.

Qualitative data collection and analysis

The study interviews were conducted via Zoom©, which also provided auto-generated transcriptions that were reviewed for accuracy using the audio recordings. A study codebook was developed using a priori (deductive) coding, meaning that the main codes (11) were based on the semi-structured interview guide questions. Sub-codes (47) within the main codes were informed through inductive coding, which refers to the identification of the most common responses within the collected data. Thematic analysis was deployed to identify common themes across participants’ responses regarding the overarching study topics related to SRH: knowledge, comfort talking about the topic, and preferred sources of information. All transcripts were analyzed independently by two study team members using MAXQDA24, a qualitative software [22]. After the first round of analysis, the coders reached a kappa of 0.52 and all coding discrepancies were addressed through detailed review of the meaning and context of the responses to the main question under which the response was shared to achieve a kappa of 1.

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics for the 16 study participants are shown in Table 1. The mean age of participants was 18.7 (range: 16–20); more than half were Caucasian (56.3%) and non-Hispanic (56.3%). Almost 70% of participants had completed high school by the time of the survey, and 56.3% of the population had public health insurance. Regarding their diagnosis, almost two-thirds had SLE (62.5%) as their sole RD and more than half (56.3%) were currently on or had previously used a teratogenic medication. Antiphospholipid positivity was seen in approximately a third of participants (31.2%).

Knowledge of sexual and reproductive health

Knowledge scores by general or lupus-specific topic and use of teratogenic medication are shown in Table 2. Regarding general knowledge about reproductive health, the mean score was 2.6 (standard deviation [s.d.]: ±1.0), with patients that reported never using teratogenic medication having a higher mean score (2.7) than patients that currently use or have used teratogenic medication (2.4). When it came to lupus-specific knowledge about reproductive health, the mean score across all participants was 3.8 (s.d.: ±1.4). Similar differences were observed for this topic when scores were compared by use of teratogenic medication, with those who have not used teratogenic medication having a higher mean score (4.4) than those who currently use or have used (3.3). However, these findings were not statistically significant. A full breakdown of responses by survey item is found in Additional File 1. In regard to lupus specific knowledge, 50% of patients answered that they did not know if pregnancy could cause lupus to flare, 31% responded ‘no’ or ‘I do not know’ to whether some medications used in lupus can affect a baby, and 37.5% were unaware if certain birth control pills were not recommended in lupus due to increased risk of thrombosis in some instances.

Health communication, sources of information, and preferences

Most participants (87.5%) reported that they had discussed the topic of reproductive health with someone. When asked about their main sources of information related to SRH (participants could select multiple options), family and friends were the top selected source (87.5%), followed by their pediatric rheumatologist (31.2%) and their primary care provider (25.0%). Participants were specifically asked about their comfort discussing SRH with their pediatric rheumatologist, with 93.8% indicating that they would be comfortable. Moreover, when asked about their interest in discussing pregnancy, prevention of pregnancy, and the impact of their RD on future pregnancy, 73.3% said that they would like to speak about it. Interview results

Of the 16 participants that completed the survey, 10 agreed to provide more in-depth information via online interviews. Three main themes were identified related to SRH beliefs and knowledge, information sources, and willingness to talk about the topic among study participants. Examples of participant quotes have been included for each theme to share their authentic voices. Descriptors of participants included random assigned ID alphabetical letters and EHR-confirmed history of teratogenic medication use.

Theme 1: Participants express worry, concern, and uncertainty related to their overall health within the simultaneous occurrence of a pregnancy, the use of rheumatic disease-related medications, and their rheumatic disease

One of the main inquiries addressed through this study was to understand the way participants believe that their RD may impact or be impacted by pregnancy. Across responses, participants expressed feeling worried or concerned about how pregnancy might impact their health or their baby’s health.

And I kind of think (…) if that time were to come I’d be concerned (…), if I’m going to be okay, or if my child’s gonna be okay and how that works, and that just causes a lot of worry for me, because I wonder if it’s going to be a healthy, viable pregnancy overall. -Participant G, No Teratogenic Medication History

Yo siento que mi mayor preocupación sería perderlo [el bebé] porque, como tomo tantos medicamentos. Y yo siento que si tuviera que cortar el tratamiento en algún momento, eso le afectaría más [al bebé], porque también me está afectando mi salud. [I feel that my main concern would be to lose [the baby] because I take so many medications. And I feel that if I had to stop my treatment at any given point, that would affect [the baby] more because that would also be affecting my health.] -Participant J, Has Teratogenic Medication History

Another concern shared by a participant was the inability to differentiate between pregnancy-related symptoms and disease-related symptoms.

I guess I’d sort of be worried about how to differentiate between (…) pregnancy symptoms and lupus symptoms, and how it might be intensified. -Participant E, Has Teratogenic Medication History

Participants also expressed uncertainty about their current readiness regarding a potential pregnancy due to their lack of information about the topic and what could happen with their health if they were to get pregnant.

Man, even after completing the other little test thing (study survey) I had to do, even then I wasn’t really fully sure what it would do in regard to my reproductive health. I was kinda like, “Huh…” I mean, I guess it affects it somehow. But I’m not quite sure how it affects it. -Participant F, Has Teratogenic Medication History

I don’t think I’d be mentally or physically ready for that, and I’d be afraid of what would happen as far as my lupus, because it’s only been a few years. I don’t really know what exactly is the best thing for me yet. and how much it would affect me. and how much it would affect the baby. -Participant H, No Teratogenic Medication History

When talking about medications, half of participants mentioned being aware of at least one medication that could have an impact on a pregnancy or the baby’s in-utero development. Out of these five participants aware of adverse effects, four of them were currently using or had used teratogenic medications.

I’m pretty sure prednisone could be pretty harmful to a baby. I’m not sure about my other medications, but I know that it’s a steroid, and (…) honestly (…) any type of (…) immunosuppressant isn’t great when you’re pregnant because you want to keep your immune system lively. -Participant B, No Teratogenic Medication History

Overall, only four out of the nine participants, with current or previous use of teratogenic medication, were aware of the possible risks of their medication if they were to become pregnant. All other participants currently on or who previously used teratogenic medication indicated that they did not know or were not sure about the impact the medications they were using might have on their overall health or the baby during pregnancy.

I’m not quite sure if the new medication would affect anything in regard to (…) reproductive health and stuff. -Participant F, Has Teratogenic Medication History

Theme 2: The main sources of information for sexual and reproductive health vary across participants, with some of them discussing their selection based on the source’s accessibility and understanding of the topic

Participants were asked about their main sources of information related to SRH. The most common source mentioned was the internet, where seven out of ten participants use this to find information. A participant described this below:

Probably mainly the internet, because my parents, like they’re not super well versed, and neither are (…) my friends, like I’m probably the only person with lupus that any of my immediate friends or family know. -Participant B, No Teratogenic Medication History

The second most mentioned source of information for participants were their providers, including their pediatric rheumatologists. For example, one participant shared that her providers shared information since the moment of diagnosis of their disease.

I know when I first was diagnosed with them, obviously, like the doctors would come in and tell me all that, and (…) every single appointment it’s always like “Don’t do this because …”, yeah. -Participant D, Has Teratogenic Medication History

Another participant reported that she felt she could trust her rheumatologist more than other specialists when discussing SRH.

If I have any questions, I wait to see Dr. (last name), and he usually answers it. (…) I like talking about my reproductive health with my rheumatologist. They know more. -Participant A, Has Teratogenic Medication History

Family members and educational courses were equally noted by five participants each. One participant highlighted that, although she asks her family member for information, the insight could be limited due to the family member not having the participant’s disease.

Every now and then I’ll ask my sister. But she doesn’t have mixed connective tissue disease, so she has no idea what (…) the medicines would do to a baby. -Participant F, Has Teratogenic Medication History

For participants that mentioned educational courses as their information source, they mentioned course projects and conversations during school lessons related to SRH as learning venues. In general, nine out of ten participants defined their information sources as ‘somewhat trustworthy’ or ‘trustworthy’.

Theme 3: Providers and peers with the same diagnosis are seen as trustworthy sources of information related to pregnancy among patients with rheumatic diseases

As part of the interviews, participants were also asked to share their insights about sources they would like to get information from. The top two sources were providers and other patients with RDs. One of the participants mentioned her interest in receiving information from providers across different specialties.

I just mostly think of like (…) doctors who specialize in the different (…) areas that would be in any way related to, you know, reproductive health, or (…) the health of your own body getting in the way of reproductive health. -Participant F, Has Teratogenic Medication History

Moreover, two participants shared the importance of trusting their provider to facilitate the conversation about SRH. One of them expressed the importance of education as well as the desire to ask and converse more about SRH.

A similar train of thought was shared by the second participant which provided additional details of specific areas of interest that she finds important to discuss.

Obviously (…) having a provider that you trust with your reproductive health and (…) your reproductive worries, and like having, (…) a working knowledge of (…) your reproductive health, and knowing what’s normal within your body, and what’s (…) a little concerning, or what’s worth (…) a doctor’s visit, or what’s worth (…) a lifestyle change. -Participant B, No Teratogenic Medication History

Two participants shared their desire to learn more about the topic through the voices and experiences of other patients with RDs and considered these sources reliable.

…maybe talking to someone who’s dealt with this and knows more about it than I might, [be]cause I just got diagnosed a few years ago. So, someone who’s had it longer, probably has more knowledge about it. -Participant C, No Teratogenic Medication History

I think maybe knowing the stories of women who have autoimmune disorders, and you know, their pregnancies, what they were worried about compared to what actually happened. That way it might quiet some worriness or stress that I might be thinking about, because it’s not like every person has this. It’s easier to relate to someone who’s been through something similar. -Participant G, No Teratogenic Medication History

Other helpful sources brought up by participants included having a more accepting community and the use of pamphlets. These sources may help them to navigate information about topics they may not be ready to talk about.

Discussion

This convergent mixed methods study explored reproductive health knowledge, beliefs, and behaviors among female adolescent patients with SLE and similar connective tissue diseases in one pediatric rheumatology center. The findings of this study suggest considerable gaps in both general and lupus-specific SRH knowledge among adolescent females living with RDs such as SLE, MCTD, UCTD, and overlap disease including SLE. Overall, sparse understanding of general SRH concepts, the impact of pregnancy, and medication safety within the context of their RD diagnosis were noted. In terms of general SRH knowledge, the study participants had a mean score of 2.6 out of a maximum of 5. No participant achieved a score of 5 regarding questions about general SRH knowledge given that none of them selected spermicidal jelly as the least effective birth control method from the list provided in the survey (see Additional File 1). In addition, participants had a mean score of 3.8 out of a maximum of 6 for RD-specific knowledge related to SRH. In this part of the survey, only half of participants accurately indicated that pregnancy may lead to disease flares while the other half indicated that they did not know, suggesting uncertainty around their understanding of the topic (see Additional File 1). Low scores were also observed after stratifying participants by use of teratogenic medications (never used vs. past or current use). Further inquiry of these topics through qualitative data collection among a subset of participants confirmed the observation of limited understanding, revealing existing uncertainty and worry about how their SRH may impact or be impacted by their RD.

Our findings suggest that participants on teratogenic medications had lower knowledge scores for both general and lupus-specific SRH within a small sample of female patients with RD in south central Pennsylvania. Qualitatively, less than half of participants (four out of nine) on teratogenic medication demonstrated awareness of the potential risk of a RD flare during pregnancy. In comparison, these findings are approximately 20% lower than the results obtained by Huynh, Ott, & Tarvin, where only 62% of patients between 14 and 23 years old using teratogenic medications knew the risks of these medications in pregnancy [23]. The medications that we deemed teratogenic for our study were methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclophosphamide, but there are medications that may possibly be harmful depending on dose and timing during pregnancy, such as prednisone, and this can be confusing for participants. Some participants inaccurately identified pregnancy-compatible medications as harmful, such as hydroxychloroquine, while failing to recognize the risk associated with methotrexate. Such misconceptions reflect a lack of counseling, lack understanding of counseling provided, and/or the complexity of medication safety in the context of RDs. Further, these findings suggest an apparent disconnect between exposure to high-risk medications and accurate understanding of the risk, which underscores a critical education gap in clinical care. The reasons for this disconnect were not fully explored but may include time constraints during visits, lack of formal training, or assumptions about patient readiness or interest.

Other studies quantitatively assessing perceived knowledge related to reproductive health among female patients with RDs have results suggestive of limited understanding for multiple reasons. In a recent study by García and colleagues, the study team found that only 38% of women with RDs (such as rheumatoid arthritis and SLE) felt that they received adequate information about pregnancy-related risks within the context of their diagnosis [5]. A study team that surveyed patients with RDs, from clinical sites across Italy, found that about 35% reported not receiving counseling before pregnancy and knowledge scores were between 0.5 and 0.6 out of a maximum score of 1 [24]. When asked about the impact of pregnancy and the potential worsening of their RD, 58.1% of patients with connective tissue disease and 76.2% of patients with chronic arthritis responded ‘No’ or ‘Do not know’ [24]. In another study including participants diagnosed with connective tissue diseases mostly from European countries but also including a small representation from the US, the authors found that almost 69% of participants reported not receiving any type of counseling prior to their planned pregnancy [6].

Qualitatively, similar concerns brought up by participants in this study have been brought up in other studies interviewing female patients with RDs regarding pregnancy. For example, concerns about medication safety were brought up among female patients with RDs receiving healthcare in Mexico [25]. In another study conducted in the US by Carandang and colleagues, adolescent and young adult female patients reported receiving limited information from their rheumatologists about the interplay between their disease and pregnancy [16]. For example, providers told patients to not get pregnant if they were using a teratogenic medication, but no additional information was given to explain the recommendation or any other concerns related to this indication. Another key finding of this study were the multiple ways described on how to incorporate parents within SRH discussion with providers [16]. This latter finding resonates with findings of our study where we found that almost 90% of participants report that their family and friends are among their sources of SRH information. The combination of these findings suggests the need to incorporate rheumatology providers, patients, and their loved ones among SRH conversations to have a better knowledgeable and supportive system for the patient.

Moreover, participants from our study demonstrated the desire to learn more about the interplay between their RD and SRH to improve their decision-making process related to their health. The next question would be who should be providing this information. The respondents of the questionnaire support that pediatric rheumatologists can be key players, as almost 94% of participants indicated that they would be willing to talk about SRH with their pediatric rheumatologist. This presents a clear opportunity for providers since they are seen as trustworthy and approachable yet are underutilized in delivering critical SRH education. Although the American College of Rheumatology published reproductive health guidelines in 2020, these guidelines are tailored to the adult population that has different considerations to contemplate, such as different barriers to access, confidentiality, education, lack of transportation, parental insurance, and cost concerns, to name a few [26]. Additional work establishing protocols for adolescent patients to standardize SRH discussions between providers and patients diagnosed with RDs is imperative to bridge this existing gap.

Another important finding was that peers diagnosed with similar RDs were mentioned as preferred sources of information by study participants. More specifically, study participants wanted to hear about their peers’ experiences with pregnancy or SRH-related decisions. Peer-based narrative communication interventions have been previously tested to assess changes in SRH communication practices and knowledge among adolescents and young adults, with findings suggesting improvements in both components across SRH-related topics [27–29]. The use of videos related to SRH to increase SRH-related knowledge and awareness among adolescent and young adult females in the US has also been previously tested [30]. Thus, different educational approaches that have been previously assessed and found to improve SRH-related knowledge and behavioral outcomes may be feasible interventions to test among adolescent and young adult females with RDs. Future directions to improve educational interventions for this population may include the evaluation of peer-based communication strategies (e.g., educational videos, in-person peer-led discussions, SRH brochures) in preliminary pilot studies. The findings of these pilot studies may help inform future randomized clinical trials to compare differences between intervention and control groups based on different evidence-based approaches that can be tailored to educate young female patients diagnosed with RDs on key SRH topics.

Strengths and limitations

One of the main strengths of this study is the use of a convergent mixed methods design which provided an opportunity to quantitatively and qualitatively assess knowledge gaps and information sources of SRH among pediatric female patients diagnosed with a RD. By having both types of data, it is possible to gain informative insight related to the topic of interest from quantitative data (e.g., mean scores of general and lupus-specific SRH knowledge) that can be further explained in better detail through qualitative data (e.g., identifying specific areas of uncertainty about their knowledge). Second, through the use of EHR data, it was possible for our team to accurately assess key clinical characteristics of participants that shape SRH conversations led by providers. This was particularly important to identify patients that had used or were currently using teratogenic medications, which are known to have a negative impact on fetal development. Third, the study population has similar proportions of participants who were on teratogenic medication and those who were not in both components of the study, providing an opportunity to compare quantitative and qualitative responses between these subgroups. Finally, from a qualitative data perspective, the sample size was adequate for the scope of this study which inquired about SRH knowledge and sources of information.

This study also has some limitations. Although adequate from a qualitative perspective, the sample size was small and only represented participants from a single health system site in south central Pennsylvania. Even though the racial and ethnic proportions of participants are somewhat reflective of the general population within the site’s county, the findings may not be generalizable to other populations. Moreover, certain minority subgroups were not well represented within our sample (e.g., Asian, American Indian or Pacific Islander), thus limiting the opportunity to assess differences across diverse racial and ethnic groups. Additionally, study participants were recruited via convenience sampling, which means that potential participants unable or unwilling to discuss SRH might not have been represented within our study. Within responses, it is possible that some were impacted by social desirability bias if participants believed that there were specific responses that would align closer to their perceived expectations of the study team. Moreover, there are existing limitations to openly discuss SRH (e.g., personal beliefs, religious teachings), which may have impacted the openness of participants’ responses, either in the survey or the interview. Differences in responses to similar questions asked in both study instruments were observed and could be explained by question order bias. Lastly, given a modification to the study population, which initially only included patients with SLE and then included patients with UCTD, MCTD, or overlap disease that include SLE, the study SRH items were lupus-specific and were not updated after the inclusion of the latter patients.

Conclusions

Findings from this study provide evidence of gaps in knowledge and sources of information of SRH as reported by a younger population of female rheumatology patients (16–20 years old). This adds to the current literature which mainly focuses on women ≥ 21 years of age. Moreover, this study’s findings may help ongoing efforts to inform existing clinical guidelines on how to discuss SRH specifically with female adolescent patients who have been diagnosed with RDs, such as SLE, MCTD, UCTD, or overlap disease. Providing information about current SRH knowledge and conversations in this younger population directly responds to the call by the American College of Rheumatology, which acknowledged the need for data on adolescents given that their current version was specifically tailored for adults [26]. Additionally, this study provides insight about SRH knowledge both quantitatively and qualitatively by use of teratogenic medication. By understanding the unique needs of this population, improvements can be made on existing approaches to provide essential SRH information for the patients’ health decision-making. Females with RDs are concerned about the interplay between their disease and a potential pregnancy, want to openly voice their questions, and are willing to talk with their pediatric rheumatologists about these topics. This provides an opportunity for clinicians and researchers to work alongside this patient population to develop necessary tools to address unique patient concerns and improve their overall SRH outcomes.
