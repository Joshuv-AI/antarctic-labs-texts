Crosstalk in the cold: host–microbe interactions in insect diapause
Authors: Heidi McKee, Aldo A Arellano, Erica B Young, Kerri L Coon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the overwhelming diversity and ecological impact of insects across most ecosystems, their responses to environmental stress remain underexplored. To survive temperate seasonal stressors, particularly winter cold conditions and nutrient and water shortage, insects undergo physiological changes and overwinter in a diapause state. During diapause, cessation of development is coupled with reduced metabolic activity, analogous to hibernation in mammals, in which gut microbiota help manage waste and recycle nitrogen. Similarly, many insects have obligate symbiotic relationships with microbes that support nutrient acquisition. However, unlike mammals, many insects have open or transient gut communities, making it more likely that microbial dynamics are strongly shaped by environmental microbes acquired through food or habitat. This review synthesizes recent literature on the roles of host-associated microbial communities in insect diapause, spanning nutrient provisioning, nitrogen recycling, and host immunity, including how associated microbes contribute to pathogen defense during dormancy. We propose future research avenues for more mechanistic understanding of host–microbe interactions involved in diapause, drawing on conceptual parallels established from mammalian hibernation research.

Introduction

Insects underpin ecosystem functions as pollinators, decomposers, prey, and pests, and their evolutionary success reflects extraordinary plasticity in the face of environmental stress, especially seasonal changes. A central adaptation to such stress is diapause—a genetically programmed, environmentally triggered, and hormonally regulated state of developmental arrest, or dormancy, coupled with marked metabolic reorganization (Hahn and Denlinger 2011, Harsimran Kaur Gill et al. 2017). Many insect species undergo obligate diapause, requiring a period of metabolic arrest to coordinate further development, irrespective of winter survival or desiccation tolerance (Storey and Storey 2007). Diapause occurs across multiple life stages, shaping phenology, survival over winter, and fitness trajectories (Schiesari and O’Connor 2013, Easwaran and Montell 2023). Diapause is well documented in holometabolous insects, which undergo complete metamorphosis through distinct life stages differing in structure and often life history (eg moths and butterflies in the order Lepidoptera). However, diapause is also observed in some hemimetabolous insects, which undergo incomplete or partial metamorphosis without a distinct larval or pupal stage—developing instead through a series of nymphal instars that resemble the adult form (eg grasshoppers and crickets in the order Orthoptera) (Fielding 2006, Tanigawa et al. 2009). During diapause, insects typically shift from active feeding to reliance on stored reserves, accumulate cryoprotectants (eg glycerol, trehalose, proline), reconfigure endocrine signaling (notably via insulin-like peptides [ILPs]), and exhibit altered immune investment, features that collectively support tolerance to cold, desiccation, and nutrient scarcity (Denlinger 2023).

Within this physiological context, insect host-associated and environmental microbiota have the potential to influence diapause outcomes through diverse metabolic activities and signaling interactions. Microbial communities in insects span a continuum from obligate, vertically transmitted endosymbionts to facultative or transient gut microbes acquired from food and habitats (Mullins and Cochran 1974, Tada et al. 2011, Hu et al. 2018, Shan et al. 2024). Insect-associated microbial communities are taxonomically diverse and can include bacteria and eukaryotic microbes such as fungi; however, most mechanistic insights to date derive from studies of bacterial symbionts. Insect-associated bacteria are known to supplement limiting nutrients, recycle host wastes, detoxify environmental compounds, and modulate immune and developmental pathways (Coolen et al. 2022, Liu et al. 2022, Short and Hahn 2023). Despite established roles during active seasons, the microbiota’s contributions during diapause—before, during, and after the dormant period—remain underexplored compared to vertebrate systems such as mammalian hibernation, where gut microbes are thought to facilitate urea-nitrogen salvage and seasonal reprogramming of host metabolism (Sonoyama et al. 2009, Carey et al. 2013, Wu et al. 2013, Mushegian and Tougeron 2019, Greene et al. 2022, 2025, Sadowska et al. 2024, Rice et al. 2026). Throughout this review, we use the terms “microbiota” and “microbiome” broadly to refer to host-associated and environmentally acquired microbial communities. Although these terms are sometimes distinguished, ie microbiota for the microbial community itself and microbiome for the broader system including their genes and activities (Marchesi and Ravel 2015), we do not draw a strict distinction here.

An emerging body of literature spanning multiple insect orders reports consistent taxonomic changes in host microbiota associated with diapause physiology relative to the active season. This dynamic includes reduced microbial diversity or abundance, enrichment of anaerobes, and shifts in dominant taxa during the host dormancy period. The variation in these patterns likely reflects differences in diapause depth (Koštál 2006), the extent to which metabolic activity, development, and other physiological processes are suppressed, as well as variation in diapause duration, feeding status, and habitat (eg aquatic vs. terrestrial). Functional studies have begun to link microbes to prediapause fattening (Li et al. 2015), diapause cryoprotection (Christensen et al. 2024), developmental control (Romoli et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2021), and nitrogen recycling (Sabree et al. 2009, Hu et al. 2018). In addition, recent work highlights roles for microbes in pathogen protection during dormant stages, including the persistence of microbial associates through diapause that can contribute to immune priming or pathogen exclusion (Sheehan et al. 2020). Together, these findings outline a path toward mechanistic understanding of host–microbe interactions during diapause, identifying outstanding key questions, including: (i) how host–microbial associations are initiated, maintained, and regulated under the physiological conditions associated with diapause; (ii) whether convergent microbial strategies underlie energy conservation, waste reuse, and stress tolerance across insect taxa and life stages during diapause; and (iii) how these interactions shape host insect phenology, fitness, and resilience in changing climates.

In this review, we synthesize post-2019 literature on insect-associated microbiota during diapause, with a primary emphasis on bacterial symbionts and gut-associated taxa for which functional evidence is most developed, while also considering the potential roles of environmentally derived microbes that may interact with diapausing insects. Where relevant, we incorporate studies of nonbacterial microbes (including yeasts and other fungi) that persist through diapause or contribute to host nutrition, immunity, or pathogen defense. This review is organized around three functional themes: nutrient provisioning, host development and immunity, and waste recycling during dormancy. Under the second theme, we consider microbial contributions to host defense against—and susceptibility to—pathogens. Where mammalian hibernation research offers established mechanisms, particularly in community restructuring and nitrogen recycling, we draw on these comparisons to help contextualize parallel patterns observed in insects. Our goal is to stimulate cross-disciplinary research among microbial ecologists, entomologists, and evolutionary biologists toward a mechanistic understanding of insect–microbe symbioses in dormancy.

Diapause Challenges and Metabolic Shifts

Energetic Reprogramming and Fuel Management in Dormancy

Dormancy strategies such as insect diapause are fundamentally shaped by the challenge of long-term energy limitation. Diapausing insects preserve energy and survive periods of temperature and nutrient stress by suppressing metabolic rate while using lipid and other energy reserves accumulated prior to dormancy to fuel metabolism throughout this period (Hahn and Denlinger 2007, Koštál et al. 2017). Diapausing insects undergo metabolic transitions that commence prior to diapause, during an environmentally induced preparatory phase (a common trigger is photoperiod, although temperature and other cues may also play important roles in some species), and persist through diapause maintenance, termination, and reactivation (Koštál et al. 2017). Across insect taxa, a conserved metabolic feature during diapause is the upregulation of gluconeogenesis, which enables the conversion of noncarbohydrate substrates into glucose to maintain energetic homeostasis during prolonged fasting, as well as glycolysis to support energy production under low-oxygen conditions (Baker and Russell 2009, Emerson et al. 2010, Hahn and Denlinger 2011). Diapausing insects access stored lipids and amino acids as alternative energy sources via gluconeogenic pathways (Melkonian et al. 2025), a strategy observed across multiple life stages, including embryonic and pharate first-instar diapause in mosquito species such as the yellow fever mosquito, Aedes aegypti (Order: Diptera) (da Silva et al. 2019). At the molecular level, this metabolic switch is tightly regulated; in the pitcher plant mosquito, Wyeomyia smithii, for example, diapause-associated expression of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK), a rate-limiting enzyme in gluconeogenesis, controls carbon flux through the tricarboxylic acid cycle to support the metabolic suppression that characterizes diapause (Emerson et al. 2010, Hahn and Denlinger 2011). Convergent reliance on PEPCK regulation, often alongside expression of cell-cycle-associated genes such as proliferating cell nuclear antigen, appears to underlie diapause control in many insect lineages (Poelchau et al. 2013, Huang et al. 2015). Recent literature offers a comprehensive synthesis of metabolic changes underlying insect diapause (Izadi 2025).

Energy storage prior to diapause is essential not only for survival but also for post-diapause reproduction (Hahn and Denlinger 2007, Yan et al. 2025). For example, in the willow leaf beetle, Plagiodera versicolora (Order: Coleoptera), prediapause lipid accumulation supports successful spring emergence as fat body reserves are redirected toward egg production even under conditions of nutrient scarcity (Yan et al. 2025). In adults of the Northern house mosquito, Culex pipiens, the behavioral transition in preparation for diapause includes cessation of host-seeking for a blood meal and a shift toward sugar feeding, accompanied by elevated expression of fatty acid synthase genes and downregulation of digestive proteases (Robich and Denlinger 2005). These metabolic changes are paired with cellular and structural adjustments, including altered membrane lipid composition associated with cold tolerance and the accumulation of heat shock proteins and storage hexamerins that stabilize tissues during prolonged metabolic suppression (Denlinger 2002, Hahn and Denlinger 2011, King and MacRae 2015). In shield bugs (Order: Hemiptera), the fat body plays a dual role, provisioning both energy reserves during diapause and vitellogenin for post-diapause egg development (Brent 2021).

Cellular and Physiological Adaptations for Abiotic Stress Tolerance

In addition to energy conservation, successful insect diapause requires protection against abiotic stress, particularly cold and desiccation. Insects experience extended exposure to low temperatures during winter diapause and often face substantial water loss from the drying effects of subfreezing temperatures. Maintaining somatic integrity under these conditions requires coordinated osmoregulatory and cryoprotective strategies, including altered cuticular hydrocarbon composition, reduced metabolic rate, and shifts in excretory physiology that limit dehydration. Although cold is a prominent feature of diapause in temperate insects, the thermal environments experienced by dormant insects vary widely, from subfreezing winters to dry-season high temperatures, which insects survive through estivation. Local adaptation along latitudinal gradients reflects this variability, with photoperiod serving as a reliable token cue for diapause induction in many above-ground species, correlating closely with latitude and winter severity (Tyukmaeva et al. 2020). For ground-dwelling and soil-inhabiting insects with limited light exposure, nonphotic cues such as soil temperature, moisture, or CO2 levels may serve as important inductive signals. Once induced, diapause maintenance and depth are often reinforced by persistent cold exposure; thus, the energetic costs of diapause maintenance vary geographically, being lowest where hosts diapause at colder temperatures (Bell and Adkisson 1964, Hahn and Denlinger 2007).

As ectotherms, insects cannot regulate body temperature endogenously and therefore rely on biochemical cryoprotection to tolerate freezing or chilling injury. Insects preparing for diapause synthesize and accumulate cryoprotectants such as glycerol and trehalose, which stabilize proteins and cellular membranes at low temperatures (Kučera et al. 2022). Amino acids including proline and alanine further enhance cold tolerance, as demonstrated in taxa ranging from the parasitic jewel wasp, Nasonia vitripennis (Order: Hymenoptera), to the vinegar fly, Chymomyza costata (Order: Diptera) (Koštál et al. 2011, Li et al. 2014). These compounds serve dual roles, functioning both as cryoprotectants and as metabolically accessible substrates during extended dormancy.

Endocrine and Metabolic Regulation of Diapause Depth and Timing

Overlaying metabolic and physiological adjustments are endocrine and stress-responsive pathways that fine‑tune diapause depth and timing. Before and during diapause, nutritional sensing pathways—particularly insulin/insulin‑like signaling—coordinate energy storage and utilization, and these metabolic states in turn regulate the release of hormones that determine developmental trajectories (Sim and Denlinger 2013). Lipid and glycogen reserves feed back into endocrine pathways governing juvenile hormone and ecdysteroid production, thereby influencing whether insects initiate, maintain, or terminate diapause (Sim and Denlinger 2013). Superimposed on these endocrine cues are molecular responses to physiological stress, many of which converge on the cellular redox environment.

Dormancy metabolism is tightly linked to changes in redox balance. Metabolic suppression alters mitochondrial activity and oxygen use, promoting the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and activating hypoxia‑inducible signaling pathways, with redox cues increasingly recognized as key regulators of diapause across insect taxa (Zhang et al. 2017). Within this framework, the antioxidant glutathione (GSH), the major intracellular antioxidant, plays a central buffering and signaling role. The ratio of reduced (GSH) to oxidized (GSSG) glutathione provides a dynamic indicator of cellular oxidative status (Asensi et al. 1994), and experimental manipulation of glutathione levels can influence diapause entry and termination in the domestic silk moth, Bombyx mori (Zhao et al. 2014). ROS themselves also function as signaling molecules, contributing to thermosensation and the induction of heat shock proteins and antioxidant defenses (Suzuki and Mittler 2006, González-Tokman et al. 2025), although prolonged elevated ROS can impose physiological costs under stress (González-Tokman et al. 2025).

Mechanistic evidence from the flesh fly, Sarcophaga crassipalpis, illustrates how redox signaling interacts with metabolic control during diapause. Experimental disruption of ROS levels or stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), which are oxygen- and redox-sensitive transcription factors that reprogram cellular metabolism under low oxygen and oxidative stress, interferes with sustained metabolic suppression, instead producing episodic spikes in metabolic rate (Chen et al. 2021). Although ROS accumulation and hypoxia are often viewed as opposing states, mitochondrial ROS can themselves stabilize HIFs, linking redox status to the hypoxia signaling pathway that helps sustain diapause-associated metabolic depression. These responses are mediated through HIF‑dependent regulation of key metabolic enzymes, including mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase and carnitine palmitoyltransferase I, as well as phosphorylation of adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase, linking cellular redox state directly to systemic energy balance (Chen et al. 2021). Such findings highlight redox regulation as an integrative node connecting endocrine signaling, metabolic suppression, and stress physiology during diapause.

Because microbes can both consume and supply thiols and antioxidants to hosts (Hand and Honek 2005, Gout 2019), the microbiome has the potential to influence host redox balance and therefore also the endocrine and metabolic pathways underlying diapause. For example, host-associated bacterial pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus (Phylum: Bacillota) and Francisella tularensis (Phylum: Pseudomonadota) can exploit host glutathione pools (Kies and Hammer 2022). However, the extent to which beneficial or commensal microbes modulate sulfur metabolism and antioxidant capacity in diapausing insects remains largely unexplored. Together, these findings emphasize that diapause is maintained through tightly coordinated interactions between endocrine regulation, metabolic suppression, and redox homeostasis, with emerging evidence that microbial processes may intersect with these pathways to influence diapause outcomes.

Immune Modulation and Host–Microbe Interactions During Dormancy

Diapause-specific physiological and behavioral shifts, particularly altered immune function, insect aggregation, and reduced feeding, occur against a backdrop of changing biotic pressures and dynamic interactions with endosymbiotic and environmentally acquired microbes, both beneficial and pathogenic. Insects that aggregate during diapause may face elevated risks of parasite or pathogen transmission, as observed in the multicolored Asian lady beetle, Harmonia axyridis, which hosts fungal and mite ectoparasites in winter hibernacula (Knapp et al. 2022). Conversely, other aggregating species, such as the caddisfly, Neophylax fuscus (Order: Trichoptera), exhibit little apparent parasite burden (Martin and Barton 1987), highlighting strong species-specific variation. Diapause-associated behavioral and immunological changes also have the potential to influence pathogen dynamics; for example, seasonal comparisons in bumblebees (Order: Hymenoptera) show lower viral loads in spring foragers than in fall populations (Wham et al. 2024), although this pattern does not directly demonstrate pathogen clearance during diapause. General principles of pathogen risk during insect diapause remain poorly characterized; however, these observations parallel seasonal patterns in other systems (Brown et al. 2022), suggesting that shifts in pathogen exposure and susceptibility may be common features of overwintering biology.

As discussed above, diapause is accompanied by immune modulation that may influence both pathogen susceptibility and microbiome persistence—a topic explored in greater depth in the section on immune-mediated microbial filtering below. Within this context, insect-associated microbes emerge as potentially important, yet underexplored, mediators of diapause biology. The coordinated shifts in host metabolism, endocrine signaling, and immune regulation during diapause are likely to restructure host–microbe interactions, influencing both symbiont maintenance and susceptibility to opportunistic pathogens. Given the established roles of microbes in nutrient provisioning, metabolic integration, and stress tolerance, these diapause-associated changes create opportunities for microbial pathways to intersect with host energy balance, cryoprotection, and post-diapause recovery. As a result, the microbiome represents a plausible, but still poorly understood, contributor to variation in diapause outcomes across insect systems.

Microbial Dynamics During Insect Diapause

Physiological transitions associated with diapause substantially modify the selective environment experienced by insect-associated microbial communities. Suppressed metabolism, cessation of feeding and altered nutrient flux, and changes in immune regulation are all likely to reshape microbiome composition and function during host insect diapause. However, common microbial indicators of insect diapause are yet to be clearly defined, and the functional significance of microbiome shifts during diapause remains underappreciated (Christensen et al. 2024). Evidence from systems in which microbial associations have been explicitly linked to diapause-related functions suggests that dormancy-associated microbial dynamics track changes in host physiological state (Christensen et al. 2024). For example, in a species of ground-nesting solitary bee, microbial communities change across successive stages of dormancy in patterns consistent with host needs for nutritional provisioning, cryoprotection, and antifungal defense (Christensen et al. 2024). This research provides a framework for generating hypotheses about microbial roles in diapause preparation, maintenance, and survival. For example, microbial associates may: (i) provision lipids and other metabolic precursors that support prediapause energy storage (“preparation”); (ii) contribute metabolites such as proline or polyols to host cryoprotectant pools (“maintenance”); and (iii) confer antifungal or antibacterial protection that improves survival through prolonged dormancy (“survival”).

Spectrum of Symbiosis: Obligate Endosymbionts to Transient Communities

Patterns of microbial persistence during diapause will vary along a spectrum of insect–microbe symbiosis. Among obligate endosymbionts, outcomes appear strongly host- and symbiont-specific, as illustrated by studies of the cosmopolitan intracellular bacterium Wolbachia (Phylum: Pseudomonadota). In both field-collected and laboratory-reared Gryllus veletis crickets, overwintering individuals showed shifts in gut microbial community composition, including higher relative abundances of gut-associated anaerobes compared to active hosts; patterns for intracellular Wolbachia were more variable, with lower abundance during winter but inconsistent detection across active-season samples (Ferguson et al. 2018). These results highlight the importance of distinguishing between total and relative abundance when interpreting any microbial dynamics. In contrast, during lab incubations of N. vitripennis wasps, prolonged diapause resulted in the stochastic loss of one or both Wolbachia strains in co-infected lines (Perrot-Minnot et al. 1996), suggesting that the costs of harboring the endosymbiont were too great and that the metabolic coupling underlying its persistence was not present during dormancy. Consistent with this pattern, Wolbachia-cured N. vitripennis (ie lacking the endosymbiont) experience depletion of active-season dominant bacteria such as Providencia (Phylum: Pseudomonadota) and enrichment of anaerobic taxa during diapause (Dittmer and Brucker 2021). Similarly, in C. pipiens mosquitoes, Wolbachia abundance declined under both field and laboratory-simulated diapause, whereas Asaia (Phylum: Pseudomonadota) increased in relative abundance within dormant host mosquitoes (Denlinger 2023).

Endosymbiont dynamics during diapause likely reflect shifts in host metabolic demands and differences in the degree of nutritional complementation provided by symbionts. In the pear psyllid, Cacopsylla pyricola (Order: Hemiptera), co-infecting obligate endosymbiotic bacteria exhibit contrasting responses; Carsonella (Phylum: Pseudomonadota) declines substantially during host overwintering, while Psyllophila (Phylum: Pseudomonadota) remains at relatively stable titers (Štarhová Serbina et al. 2023). Given that Carsonella provisions 8 of 10 essential amino acids in this symbiotic system, while Psyllophila complements tryptophan, B-vitamin, and carotenoid biosynthesis, these dynamics may suggest shifts in host nutritional demands during dormancy (Štarhová Serbina et al. 2023). These findings align with broader models in which dynamic host physiological state modifies the cost–benefit balance of symbiosis, such that metabolic stress, host-mediated symbiont suppression, or relaxed selection for nutritional provisioning can favor reductions in specific endosymbionts (Douglas 2000, Parker et al. 2021, Whittle et al. 2021, Renoz 2024). Cold temperatures and extended dormancy may further intensify these tradeoffs by reducing post-diapause host fecundity and symbiont transmission to the next generation, selecting against maintenance of energetically costly intracellular symbionts at the population level (Kriesner et al. 2016, Charlesworth et al. 2019).

Common Taxonomic Trends in Non-Endosymbiotic Communities

Beyond obligate endosymbionts, dormancy-associated restructuring of gut and environmentally acquired microbial communities exhibits several recurrent taxonomic patterns across insect orders. Across Coleoptera, Diptera, Hemiptera, Hymenoptera, Lepidoptera, and Orthoptera, diapause is frequently associated with shifts toward microbial taxa capable of persisting under low-resource, nonfeeding conditions (Wang et al. 2017, Ferguson et al. 2018, Wang et al. 2019, Dittmer and Brucker 2021, Denlinger 2023, Fluch et al. 2024, Moussa et al. 2024, Wang et al. 2024, Hotchkiss et al. 2025). These shifts often co-occur with reductions in overall diversity or bacterial titer, but more importantly reflect changes in the relative representation of functional groups, including declines in copiotrophic taxa and enrichment of microbes adapted to host-derived substrates such as mucin, chitin, and metabolic waste products. Comparable taxonomic restructuring during host dormancy has been observed across diverse animal systems, including cnidarians (Brown et al. 2022), mammals (Sommer et al. 2016, Carey and Assadi-Porter 2017, Greene et al. 2022, Popov et al. 2023), and copepods (Datta et al. 2018), suggesting a broadly conserved ecological response of host-associated microbiomes to reduced host feeding and metabolic suppression (Fujita et al. 2006, Carey et al. 2013, Dill-McFarland et al. 2014, Datta et al. 2018, Brown et al. 2022). Despite these emerging patterns, relatively few studies explicitly link changes in microbial taxa to shifts in substrate availability during diapause. As a result, mechanistic connections between host metabolic state, resource limitation, and microbiome taxonomic composition remain underdeveloped in insect systems.

Even among insects, studied dormancy-associated taxonomic dynamics vary with the depth and duration of diapause, host metabolic state, and sampling design. Some studies report relatively stable or even increasing diversity during diapause induction or early diapause, when hosts remain metabolically active, but these phases may still involve taxonomic turnover not captured by diversity metrics alone (Liu et al. 2016, Didion et al. 2021, Qian et al. 2024). Other studies rely on pre- and post-diapause comparisons of active insects collected under different seasonal conditions, which can obscure diapause-specific taxonomic patterns by conflating them with broader environmental variation (Bosmans et al. 2018, Piccinno et al. 2025). Longitudinal and high-frequency sampling across diapause phases is therefore essential to resolve how specific microbial taxa change through induction, maintenance, and termination phases. Cross-system patterns may provide a general framework for interpreting diapause-associated microbial restructuring, but host-specific studies reveal how particular taxa respond within distinct ecological and physiological contexts.

Host- and Habitat-Specific Variation in Diapause-Associated Microbiota

The importance of host system-specific context is illustrated by research on the brown marmorated stink bug, Halyomorpha halys (Order: Hemiptera). While microbial diversity appears relatively stable when comparing pre- and post-diapause individuals (Piccinno et al. 2025), sampling during the overwintering diapause state revealed pronounced taxonomic shifts, including higher relative abundance of the nutritional symbiont Pantoea (Phylum: Pseudomonadota) and lower abundance of Commensalibacter (Phylum: Pseudomonadota), a bacterium dominant in the gut during the active season (Fluch et al. 2024). These findings demonstrate that diapause can reshape the representation of specific microbial taxa linked to host nutrition, even in cases where overall diversity remains stable.

Feedback between host physiology and environmental exposure is likely a key driver of these dynamics. During diapause, prolonged residence in spatially constrained hibernacula, combined with reduced feeding and altered immune regulation, may increase host exposure to localized microbial species pools while intensifying host-mediated selection of microbial associates (Robinson et al. 2019). Intrinsic microbial responses to the diapause environment, including differential survival, dormancy, or sporulation at low temperatures and shifts in competitive dynamics as available substrates decline, may also independently contribute to community restructuring, independent of or in concert with host-mediated selection.

Host–microbe–environment interactions are especially evident in systems where dormancy occurs within specialized microhabitats. In the bumble bee-mimic digger bee, Anthophora bomboides stanfordiana, the highest abundances of Actinomycetota (eg Streptomyces, Nocardioides) and fungal associates within the Basidiomycota (eg Moniliella) occur during prepupal dormancy within brood chambers (Christensen et al. 2024). These taxa contribute to antifungal defense and cryoprotection and appear to be maintained independently of local environmental species pools, highlighting the importance of host-mediated selection and pathogen pressure in structuring taxonomic composition during dormancy. Wyeomyia smithii develops within the water-filled leaves of the carnivorous purple pitcher plant, Sarracenia purpurea (Bradshaw and Lounibos 1972). When eggs are exposed to the critical photoperiod, hatched larvae will initiate diapause at the third larval instar (Bradshaw and Lounibos 1972). This system provides opportunities to examine how taxonomic composition of microbial communities in both host and environment shifts during diapause preparation, maintenance, and termination. Previous work suggests that host–microbe feedbacks in this system are mediated through environmentally structured microbial communities, further emphasizing the ecological context of diapause-associated microbial community composition dynamics (Arellano and Coon 2022, Arellano et al. 2024, Arellano et al. 2025).

Immune-Mediated Microbial Filtering Across Insect Diapause

Both host-mediated selection and stochastic environmental acquisition shape insect-associated microbial communities across life stages, but these processes may operate differently during diapause. The sustained suppression of feeding and metabolism, prolonged residence in constrained environments, and coordinated shifts in immune function characteristic of diapause create distinct and extended selective conditions for microbial community assembly. However, the relative contributions of host filtering and environmental acquisition under these conditions remain poorly understood and may vary seasonally or among host taxa. In particular, dormancy-associated shifts in immune function are likely to influence which microbes are maintained within the host.

Studies in the common fermentation fly, Drosophila melanogaster, provide a mechanistic foundation for understanding host-driven microbial selection. Insects acquire microbes from the environment, yet they maintain a relatively stable gut community through finely tuned local immune regulation (Ryu et al. 2008). In the gut lumen, the transcription factor Caudal (Cad) suppresses excessive antimicrobial peptide (AMP) production, thereby preventing inhibition of commensals, while still restricting opportunistic taxa (Ryu et al. 2008). More broadly, endocrine–immune interactions in other insects also shape how hosts regulate microbial associates. Across multiple orders, ILPs modulate AMP expression while simultaneously suppressing systemic metabolism (Becker et al. 2010, McCormack et al. 2016, Dolezal et al. 2019, Chowański et al. 2025), linking nutrient sensing, metabolic rate, and immune investment. These pathways suggest that the metabolic depression characteristic of diapause may shift immune set points in ways that alter which environmentally acquired microbes are selectively maintained versus excluded.

Shifts in immune investment during diapause may relax constraints on some microbial associates while intensifying selection for taxa capable of persisting under low-energy, nutrient-poor, low-oxygen, and metabolically suppressed conditions (Sinclair et al. 2013). For example, innate immunity in the pupal stage of the Ailanthus silkmoth, Samia cynthia pryeri, is influenced by both diapause status and temperature, with key immune functions remaining active even as metabolic activity declines (Nakamura et al. 2011). In parallel, changes in gut physiochemical conditions (ie in the luminal environment) may further filter microbial taxa. Several studies report enrichment of Pseudomonadota in diapausing insects relative to active individuals (Liu et al. 2016, Dittmer and Brucker 2021, Didion et al. 2022). However, this pattern likely reflects shifts among specific taxa within this diverse phylum rather than a uniform response and may be driven by changes in host physiology and gut conditions (eg nutrient limitation or altered redox environment) rather than reduced oxygen availability per se (Poelchau et al. 2013, Huang et al. 2015).

The adaptive acquisition of locally beneficial microbial partners from the environment has been proposed as a general eco‑evolutionary strategy in insects (Ravenscraft and Coon 2026) but remains untested in diapause specifically, though parallel hypotheses have emerged in other dormancy systems (Mushegian and Tougeron 2019, Yao et al. 2025). This raises a key question: do insects selectively acquire or amplify particular taxa during preparation for diapause because those partners confer advantages under prolonged energetic and hypoxic stress? These considerations highlight diapause as a distinct physiological context that can reshape the balance between host filtering and environmental acquisition, reinforcing the need for integrative, longitudinal studies linking microbial community dynamics to host physiology and ecological conditions.

Microbial Contributions to Insect Nutrient Provisioning

Representative examples of the host–microbe interactions that contribute to insect diapause physiology, spanning a range of taxa and functional roles, are summarized in Table 1. The provisioning of lipids prior to or during diapause is the process where microbes have most frequently been implicated in supporting host diapause (Li et al. 2015, Liu et al. 2016, Tan et al. 2016, Wong et al. 2016, Valzania et al. 2018a, 2018b). A more complete case study is with C. pipiens, which undergoes diapause as an adult and also accumulates fat before diapause, and commensal microbes contribute to this process (Didion et al. 2021, Denlinger 2023). Culex pipiens mosquitoes grown with an experimentally low bacterial load (LBL) had lower lipid accumulation and lower body mass during diapause preparation, resulting in higher mortality than mosquitoes reared with a conventional (CNV) microbiome, and mosquitoes maintained without microbes (axenic) frequently died during diapause (Denlinger 2023). The LBL mosquitoes also had higher carbohydrate stores than controls, implicating microbes in the process of converting carbohydrates into lipids, a key step in diapause preparation (Denlinger 2023).

Additional microbial compounds used by insect hosts include sterols, which are essential components of eukaryotic cell membranes (Jing and Behmer 2020). Microbes such as fungi and yeasts are well-established sources of sterols (eg ergosterol) (Weete et al. 2010), and an increasing number of bacteria have also been identified with sterol biosynthesis potential (Wei et al. 2016, Khmelinskaia et al. 2020). Because insects cannot synthesize sterols de novo, they must obtain them from dietary or symbiotic sources (Jing and Behmer 2020). This dependence may be especially consequential during diapause, when feeding is reduced or absent and external sterol intake is limited. Under these conditions, endosymbiotic microbial provisioning or recycling of sterols could contribute to maintaining membrane integrity and cellular function during prolonged metabolic suppression and, in many cases, low-temperature stress.

In addition to sterols, related lipid classes such as sphingolipids may also play roles in diapause-associated physiology. Insects can increase reliance on sphingolipids when dietary sterol availability is constrained (Carvalho et al. 2010). These compounds are integral to membrane structure and cell signaling, including pathways regulating stress responses and metabolic homeostasis (Bieberich 2018). Evidence from other host systems suggests that sphingolipids and ceramides are involved in responses to nutrient limitation and metabolic stress, for example, through the modulation of insulin signaling pathways (Cui et al. 2017). Microbial contributions to these lipid pools, whether through direct synthesis or interaction with host pathways, may therefore influence insect responses to the energetic and physiological constraints of diapause (Johnson et al. 2020).

Although the role of microbial sterol and lipid provisioning in insects remains incompletely understood, these compounds represent a potentially important interface between microbial metabolism and host survival during diapause. Future work linking microbial lipid production to host physiological state will be essential for determining whether these associations are specifically adaptive in the context of dormancy or reflect more general features of insect–microbe interactions.

Microbial Contributions to Cryoprotection

Insects lack endogenous, internally regulated thermogenesis and therefore rely on the accumulation of cryoprotectants prior to winter to prevent freezing (Koštál et al. 2011, Ramirez et al. 2017). Some microbes can act as ice nucleators (Lee et al. 1993), and it is suggested that before or during the winter diapause, some insects may clear their guts, including any gut microbes, to prevent ice crystal formation from damaging their cells (Olsen and Duman 1997, Olsen et al. 1998). Microbes can influence the production of cryoprotective compounds, including exopolysaccharides, that primarily enhance microbial stress tolerance but may also indirectly benefit insect hosts during desiccating winter conditions. In free-living environments, diverse cold-adapted microbes produce exopolysaccharides that stabilize cells and extracellular matrices under freezing conditions, as documented in glacier-associated bacteria (including multiple Pseudomonas species within the Pseudomonadota) and in halophilic lactic acid bacteria (Phylum: Bacillota) exposed to cold stress (Deming and Young 2017, Ali et al. 2020, Zhang et al. 2023). Similarly, non-host-associated microbes in non-host-associated settings can accumulate compatible solutes such as proline in response to environmental stress, further illustrating conserved microbial strategies for coping with osmotic and thermal extremes (Liang et al. 2013). Host-associated microbes may influence insect cold tolerance more directly through 2 routes: (i) provisioning cryoprotective compounds or their precursors, or (ii) signaling to the host to induce cryoprotectant synthesis. While insects typically produce trehalose and other endogenous cryoprotectants, microbial associates may contribute compounds such as glycerol and certain amino acids, potentially as an alternative to dietary inputs (Elbein et al. 2003, Moos et al. 2022). For example, when larvae of N. vitripennis wasps were induced into diapause, axenic individuals contained significantly lower levels of glycerol than microbe-associated controls, despite no differences in total body mass (Cross et al. 2021, Dittmer and Brucker 2021). Larvae colonized with CNV bacterial communities or experimentally associated with Providencia rettgeri (Phylum: Pseudomonadota) showed higher glycerol accumulation in response to cold, highlighting a role for microbes in modulating cryoprotective responses during diapause. However, it remains unclear whether this reflects direct microbial production of glycerol or microbe-stimulated host synthesis. These observations suggest that microbial stress responses may shape the pool of cryoprotective compounds available to host insects during cold exposure.

Extending this host-associated perspective, experimental manipulations suggest functional links between microbial inputs and host cryoprotectant pools. Proline supplementation of the host increased cold resistance in N. vitripennis (Li et al. 2015). In the Japanese red bug, Parastrachia japonensis (Order: Hemiptera), antimicrobial treatment with rifampicin during diapause reduced total amino acid stores, especially proline, implicating microbes in maintaining amino acid availability under cold stress (Kashima et al. 2006). Although direct demonstrations that microbes synthesize proline in diapausing insects and that this proline is subsequently routed into host cryoprotectant pathways remain limited, the convergent evidence is consistent with both routes (Elbein et al. 2003). Alongside environmental cases where microbes produce cryoprotective compounds and compatible solutes, these findings suggest that microbial processes—both free-living and host-associated—can shape the pool of cryoprotectants available to insects during cold exposure and highlight a clear priority for future work to clarify routes.

Microbial Regulation of Diapause Initiation and Termination

In addition to environmental cues, microbes may also influence the initiation and termination of diapause by regulating developmental competence, ie post-diapause capacity to reactivate metabolism and resume development. A well-characterized example of microbe-dependent insect development occurs in mosquitoes. Under active-season conditions, mosquito larvae require microbial inputs to initiate and sustain development, progressing successfully through larval, pupal, and adult stages (Coon et al. 2014, 2017). This microbial requirement for development appears to be conserved across mosquito taxa (Coon et al. 2014, 2016a, 2016b, 2020, Wang et al. 2018, Arellano and Coon 2022) and is mediated in part by the provisioning of key microbially derived metabolites, including B-vitamins such as riboflavin and folate that are essential for cellular metabolism and growth (Romoli et al. 2021, Wang et al. 2021). Microbial metabolites thus function as permissive or proximate regulators of developmental progression in mosquitoes.

Other animal models also support a general role of microbes in developmental competence that could extend developmental arrest during diapause initiation and resumption during termination. For example, the water flea, Daphnia magna, requires a living gut microbiota to maintain normal rates of growth, survival, and fecundity (Sison-Mangus et al. 2015), while metamorphosis in several groups of cnidarians is regulated by microbial chemical cues (Cavalcanti et al. 2020). This illustrates conserved roles for microbes in coordinating host growth supportive of reversible regulation in the context of insect diapause.

Waste Management and Nitrogen Recycling

Microbes can process nitrogenous compounds that many animals cannot, and this capacity becomes especially important when intake and excretion are constrained. In mammalian hibernators, nitrogen-salvage pathways in commensal microbes (eg urease) reduce nitrogen loss and prevent waste buildup during periods when hosts are not actively eating or excreting (Rice et al. 2020, 2026, Regan et al. 2022, 2025, Sadowska et al. 2024). Nitrogenous waste compounds are transformed into essential macronutrients, including short-chain fatty acids and amino acids to support host metabolism (Regan et al. 2025, Rice et al. 2026). In hibernating wood frogs, gut microbes hydrolyze host-derived urea, indicating conserved contributions of host-associated microbes to nitrogen cycling during dormancy (Wiebler et al. 2018). In insects, similar microbial functions are likely, and often necessary, given that many insect lineages have incomplete urea processing pathways (eg widespread loss of ornithine carbamoyl transferases and limited arginine biosynthesis) (Sabree et al. 2009, Martins et al. 2025). Well-studied insect–bacteria symbioses across Hemiptera, Blattodea, and Hymenoptera provide mechanistic routes for host-derived nitrogen recycling that can operate during dormancy and active periods (Kashima et al. 2006, Sabree et al. 2009, Hu et al. 2018).

Obligate associations illustrate 1 end of this continuum. In Japanese red bugs, Erwinia-like symbionts (Phylum: Pseudomonadota) enable hosts to recycle nitrogen during adult reproductive diapause (Kashima et al. 2006). These bacteria express urease, uricase, allantoinase, and allantoicase enzymes, converting nitrogenous waste products such as urea and uric acid into bioavailable nitrogen that can be assimilated by the host or other gut microbes to synthesize nitrogenous compounds (Kashima et al. 2006). The microbial symbionts colonize specialized midgut crypts, a spatial organization that stabilizes the association, and are transmitted to offspring via maternal feces, underscoring the evolutionary importance of this partnership (Kashima et al. 2006, Tada et al. 2011).

However, microbial contributions to host nitrogen cycling are not limited to obligate endosymbionts and also emerge from diverse, flexible gut communities. In general, gut bacteria are essential for digestion and waste management in insects (Kashima et al. 2006, Engel and Moran 2013). In herbivorous Cephalotes turtle ants (Order: Hymenoptera), which harbor highly structured and conserved gut microbial communities, metagenomic analyses suggest tight metabolic coordination between host and symbionts that supports nitrogen processing (Hu et al. 2018). Although this interaction is not specific to diapause, it highlights the importance of symbiont-mediated nitrogen recycling in insects, a function that may become particularly critical during diapause when feeding ceases so nitrogenous wastes need to be recycled. In the tephritid fruit fly, Bactrocera dorsalis, gut microbes provide critical nitrogen substrates for host amino acid acquisition (Ren et al. 2022). In larvae, urea hydrolysis depends on a taxonomically diverse consortium that includes Morganella morganii, Klebsiella oxytoca, and other Enterobacterales (Phylum: Pseudomonadota), as well as Lactobacillales (Phylum: Bacillota), Orbales (Phylum: Pseudomonadota), Pseudomonadales (Phylum: Pseudomonadota), Flavobacteriales, and Bacteroidales (Phylum: Bacteroidota) (Ren et al. 2022).

Insect-associated bacteria can sometimes reside in the Malpighian tubules, which are analogous to mammalian nephrons (Nocelli et al. 2016, Özyurt Koçakoğlu et al. 2025), and microbes can help maintain Malpighian tubule function (Liu et al. 2024). Specific waste management strategies in Malpighian tubules may depend on terrestrial versus aquatic habitat and may also involve different microbes. Aedes aegypti mosquitoes exhibit distinct transcriptomic profiles in Malpighian tubules between larval and adult stages, indicating stage-specific regulation; in aquatic larvae, ammonia excretion is favored, whereas in adults, excretion of uric acid is more likely to minimize dehydration (Isoe and Scaraffia 2013, Mazzalupo et al. 2016, Li et al. 2017). Malpighian tubules empty into the lower digestive tract, delivering nitrogen compounds to gut bacteria for recycling (Hu et al. 2018). Malpighian tubules also may play important roles in transstadial transmission of commensal bacteria from adults to eggs in the Asian malaria mosquito, Anopheles stephensi (Chavshin et al. 2015). In C. pipiens mosquitoes, excretion management in Malpighian tubules shifts during diapause to emphasize water retention (Yang et al. 2017), but the direct and specific impact on host-associated bacteria is yet to be measured.

Because of the generally critical role of the fat body in nutrient and energy metabolism (Hahn and Denlinger 2007), nitrogen transformations in fat bodies are likely also important during insect diapause. In termites (Order: Blattodea), the fat body is a site of uric acid production and accumulation, which can be excreted as a nitrogenous waste through feces, and in larval-stage silk moths, nitrogen is secreted in silk polymers (Thong-On et al. 2012, Fujii and Banno 2019). Cephalotes turtle ants maintain a diverse gut microbial community, including representatives from Burkholderiales and Rhizobiales (Phylum: Pseudomonadota), as well as Opitutales (Phylum: Verrucomicrobiota), which carry out collaborative nitrogen recycling, and 15N stable isotope tracer experiments demonstrated significant provisioning of amino acid sources to the insect host (Hu et al. 2018). Many of these microbial genomes encode nitrogen-cycling enzymes that form complete or nearly complete pathways for breaking down environmentally acquired and host-derived uric acid and urea. Many Burkholderiales symbionts of the Cephalotes turtle ants are also able to produce urea, contributing to regulation of nitrogen cycling (Hu et al. 2018). Turtle ants have a southern US distribution and have no described winter diapause period, but the host–microbe collaborative nitrogen cycling provides a useful model for designing research in diapausing insect species.

These case studies reveal a spectrum of microbial strategies—from obligate, vertically transmitted symbionts in specialized tissues to dynamically assembled gut communities—that converge on waste transformation and nitrogen retention, particularly under low-nitrogen diets or during dormancy. This underscores the critical, conserved roles of host-associated microbes in nitrogen and waste management during insect diapause.

Future Directions: Toward Mechanistic and Tractable Approaches

A central question for the next phase of diapause research is whether insect‑associated microbes expand or modulate host metabolic capacity in ways that influence overwinter survival, emergence timing, or post‑diapause fecundity, and whether these effects are repeatable in a manner consistent with adaptive significance. Toward addressing these questions, it will be essential to identify microbial functions that modulate host physiology under realistic ecological conditions. Host-associated microbial taxa have been suggested to confer benefits to extent host phenotypes, especially under diapause-associated stress (Kolodny and Schulenburg 2020, Mueller et al. 2020, Henry et al. 2021, Decaestecker et al. 2024). However, mechanistic studies remain essential to move beyond theory and descriptive taxonomic inventories. It should also be recognized that microbes can have consistent, measurable effects on host diapause without having evolved specifically “for” host benefit: such traits are shaped by selection acting on microbial fitness and may benefit hosts only incidentally, where microbial and host interests overlap rather than coincide (Moran and Sloan 2015, Behar and Lauzon 2023). Progress in understanding mechanisms of host–microbe interactions will depend on comparative, hypothesis‑driven physiological research, while recognizing that experimental manipulation in diapausing insects is often constrained by long developmental times, environmental sensitivity, and limited opportunities for repeated sampling.

Experimental designs should ideally follow hosts and their microbes across the full diapause trajectory, from induction through maintenance to recovery, so that microbial composition and activity can be related to changing host states. In practice, such longitudinal sampling is often limited by the difficulty of maintaining insects under controlled diapause conditions and by mortality during extended dormancy. As a result, carefully designed cross-sectional studies at key diapause stages, with parallel field collections, may provide a feasible alternative for many systems. When possible, studies should document host behaviors, including whether and how often individuals feed during diapause, because feeding strongly influences microbial acquisition and persistence. Ecological realism remains important, but experiments may need to balance realism with tractability when implementing temperature fluctuations, photoperiod regimes, and substrate conditions.

Establishing causality requires manipulating community membership and function, but such approaches remain challenging in most diapausing systems. Gnotobiotic and defined‑community methods provide strong inference (Wu et al. 2023, Arellano et al. 2025), yet their application is often restricted to tractable model systems or early life stages. Where direct manipulation is not feasible, indirect approaches, such as antibiotic perturbations (Kashima et al. 2006), microbiome transplants (eg in mosquitoes, Coon et al. 2022, Hegde et al. 2024), or comparisons across natural populations (Ferguson et al. 2018), can provide valuable insight. Interactions “within” microbial communities, especially groups of coexisting bacterial taxa, may also influence host–microbe signaling and exchange of compounds, so the complexity of microbial community activities needs to be considered. Multi‑omics offer additional leverage for resolving coordinated host–microbe metabolism (Li et al. 2024), but microbial cells and insect systems pose practical constraints due to limited biomass, particularly at tissue or organ scales. Continued development of low‑input DNA/RNA protocols will therefore be essential (Williamson et al. 2025), and in many cases, targeted approaches, including lipidomics to characterize diapause-associated lipid remodeling (Lehmann et al. 2020, Cedden et al. 2025) and mass spectrometry-based metabolomics to detect cryoprotectants and metabolic fluxes with microliter-scale extracts (Liu et al. 2019, Horvath et al. 2021), may be more tractable than full multiomics pipelines. Multiomics approaches applied at stage-resolved timepoints also offer the potential to distinguish stable from transient host–microbe associations and identify which microbial pathways are engaged at entry, maintenance, and recovery phases (Liu et al. 2024). Across these approaches, careful normalization and experimental design are critical when comparing host and microbial signals across matrices.

Linking microbial identity to function also requires spatial and metabolic resolution. Techniques such as fluorescence in situ hybridization and laser-capture microdissection provide powerful tools for localizing microbes within host tissues and connecting location to activity, and protocols have been adapted for insect gut tissues (Kariu et al. 2011), although their application in diapausing insects may be limited by tissue preservation challenges and low microbial biomass. Complementary approaches such as stable isotope tracing offer a promising avenue for quantifying in vivo metabolic activity; for example, 13C tracing in Drosophila has demonstrated microbial provisioning of amino acids and cross-feeding between gut bacteria, with live microbial metabolism required for host development (Henriques et al. 2020), though their use may be constrained by small body size and long experimental timescales. Adapting these methods to tractable systems will be important for determining whether microbial processes measurably contribute to host nutrient balance and energy budgets during diapause (Hu et al. 2018).

Climate change adds urgency to understanding host–microbe interactions during diapause because temperature regimes are shifting, and the variability and frequency of freeze–thaw events are increasing (Leduc and Logan 2025). These changes have the potential to alter not only diapause timing and duration but also the stability and functional capacity of associated microbial communities. It will therefore be important to determine how microbial community composition and metabolic activity respond to these environmental changes, and whether any shifts are driven directly by the environment, mediated by altered host physiology, or both. Are beneficial functions disrupted under novel thermal regimes? Can microbial associates buffer hosts against abiotic stress or instead introduce additional constraints (Lange et al. 2023)? However, experiments that fully replicate future climate scenarios may be difficult to implement in laboratory settings, particularly for species with long diapause durations or specific environmental requirements. Integrating laboratory and field-based approaches, including longitudinal monitoring of natural populations across climate gradients, will therefore be especially valuable for capturing ecologically relevant host–microbe dynamics. By linking microbial function to host survival, reproduction, and ecological performance, future work can improve predictions of insect population dynamics under environmental change and inform strategies for conservation of beneficial species as well as management of pests and vectors.
