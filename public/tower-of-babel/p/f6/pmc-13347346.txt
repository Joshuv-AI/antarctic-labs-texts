Study on the Thermodynamic Characteristics of Methane Adsorption–Desorption in Coal under Infrared Radiation Heating
Authors: Yapeng Wang, Yongli Zhang, Xinle Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coalbed methane (CBM) is unconventional natural gas. In this study, the thermodynamic characteristics of the coal adsorption–desorption methane process under infrared radiation heating (IRH) conditions were investigated. These findings indicate that as the infrared radiation power increases, the lag effect of methane desorption significantly decreases. The methane desorption process exhibits nonspontaneous endothermic entropy-increasing characteristics, and the required energy is significantly higher than that of the spontaneous exothermic adsorption process. Infrared radiation increases the thermodynamic energy of the system, which disrupts the adsorption potential equilibrium and effectively induces methane desorption. The difference in adsorption–desorption volume work decreases with increasing infrared power, whereas the increase in critical desorption pressure reduces the proportion of desorption lag volume energy in the total energy, increasing CBM extraction efficiency. This study reveals the thermodynamic mechanisms of methane desorption lag under infrared heating conditions, providing a theoretical basis for the development of CBM.

Introduction

Coalbed methane (CBM) is an unconventional natural gas resource. The commonly used technologies for CBM mining include drainage depressurization, hydraulic fracturing, horizontal wells, and gas displacement. − Recently, many scholars have reported that injecting heat to increase CBM production is a highly promising way to increase CBM production. How can clean and efficient heat be obtained by injecting heat to increase CBM production? Infrared radiation heating (IRH) is a good method. IRH has the advantages of fast heating, high thermal efficiency, and radiation heat transfer without the need for a medium. −

To investigate the adsorption–desorption mechanism of methane in coal seams, numerous scholars have investigated the variations in methane adsorption and desorption quantities. Zhang revealed the role of the physicochemical structure of coal in gas adsorption at the molecular scale, providing a microscopic theoretical basis for understanding coal–gas interactions. Deng et al. employed the grand canonical ensemble Monte Carlo simulation method to examine the adsorption behavior of equimolar CH4 and CO2 gases in a coal slit pore model at various temperatures and pore sizes and reported that their isotherms closely matched those of the Langmuir adsorption theory model, confirming the significant effect of coal pore structure on gas adsorption. Ma and Ba reported that increasing the electromagnetic wave radiation power significantly increases the efficiency of methane desorption from coal. By constructing a three-dimensional model on the basis of the Euler function, they effectively revealed the nonlinear coupling relationships among electromagnetic wave power, coal moisture content, and equilibrium gas pressure. This research not only validates the inducing effect of electromagnetic wave excitation on methane desorption but also provides a theoretical basis for optimizing gas extraction techniques in low-pressure, low-permeability coal seams, particularly emphasizing the critical role of moisture uniformity control and electromagnetic wave parameter matching in practical applications. Zhu et al. reported that gas saturation is a key parameter for the selection and development of CBM and discussed the effects of temperature and pressure on coal adsorption and desorption capacity. Zheng et al. conducted experiments on free-state methane downstream at different temperatures on the basis of low-field nuclear magnetic resonance technology. The results indicated that an increase in coal reservoir temperature reduces the amount of methane desorbed from coal samples by CO2 displacement. Scholars such as Han have systematically modeled and compared the adsorption behavior of water-bearing coal rocks using various adsorption models. The results indicate that the saturated adsorption capacity of coal rocks with different moisture contents decreases to different degrees with increasing temperature. Considering the problem of low methane desorption due to desorption hysteresis, many scholars have researched the mechanism of desorption hysteresis. Chen used COMSOL software for numerical simulation and reported a critical burial depth (600–800 m). When the burial depth is less than the critical burial depth, the desorption rate increases with increasing burial depth. After the burial depth is greater than the critical burial depth, desorption hysteresis occurs and gradually becomes significant. Research by Xiao et al. has shown that the source of CBM production comes from the adsorption of CBM, which releases expansion energy through desorption and expansion processes while continuously occupying the flow space of coalbed water. Experimental data from Zou revealed that within the temperature range of 298–318 K, the methane saturation adsorption capacity of the five coal samples decreased with increasing temperature, indicating a negative correlation. Li et al. reported that when coal and rock adsorb gas, the absolute value of equal adsorption heat decreases with increasing temperature and adsorption capacity. However, within the applicable pressure range of Henry’s law, this parameter is not affected by temperature or pressure and can be used as an effective indicator for evaluating the gas adsorption affinity in low-pressure areas of coal and rock. Wang reported that an increase in temperature significantly reduces the adsorption capacity of CH4, which can be accurately fitted by the L–F model. Heating causes CH4 molecules to migrate from high-energy preferential adsorption sites to low-energy preferential adsorption sites while increasing the system entropy value, further weakening the adsorption driving force and ultimately resulting in a gradual decrease in gas adsorption capacity. Lin et al. reported that an increase in the number of freeze–thaw cycles directly affects material behavior, with increases in the standard adsorption enthalpy, standard adsorption Gibbs free energy, standard adsorption entropy, equal adsorption heat, and average equal adsorption heat.

This study conducted relevant experiments based on a self-developed IRH device for measuring the amount of methane gas adsorbed by coal. Combining adsorption science and thermodynamics, the changes in the thermodynamic parameters of the system and the lag mechanism of methane desorption during coal adsorption–desorption under IRH conditions were investigated.

Coal Sample Pore Proportion

As can be seen from Figure , the microstructure of the coal samples changed after IRH, with the size of the surface pores and cracks increasing. After IRH, some of the original pores and cracks became interconnected, surface particles detached, and micropores and microcracks became more obvious. The expansion of micropores and microcracks contributes to the desorption and diffusion of methane gas, which is consistent with the previous finding that the adsorption capacity and saturated adsorption capacity of coal samples decrease with an increasing IRH power. The IRH environment can cause deformation of the internal surface of coal samples, enlarging primary pores and cracks, causing surface particles to detach, and thereby generating new pores and cracks, thus providing more favorable channels for gas desorption and diffusion.

The number of micropores in coal is positively correlated to its specific surface area, which reflects the effective contact area during methane adsorption and can characterize the adsorption capacity of the coal. Therefore, changes in the microscopic pore distribution of the coal samples can explain variations in the macroscopic adsorption capacity. In this study, IRH altered the surface structure of the coal samples, and the proportion of micropores decreased. This reduced the probability of methane molecules making contact with the coal surface, which further verified, from a microscopic perspective, that the increase in the IRH power led to decreases in the adsorption capacity and saturated adsorption capacity of the coal samples.

Adsorption–Desorption Experimental Methods

On the basis of the core principle of GB/T 19560–2008 “Experimental method of high-pressure isothermal adsorption to coal” and combined with the characteristics of IRH technology, this experimental method is developed to provide a scientific basis for the determination of the adsorption–desorption capacity of coal samples. A “layered temperature control + real-time feedback” strategy was employed in this study to accurately distinguish the sources of temperature rise in coal samples. The outer-layer thermostat, integrated with a PID control module, ensured that the ambient temperature of the main equipment (sample cylinder and reference cylinder) fluctuated within ±0.5 °C. In the inner-layer IRH zone, a PT100 thermocouple is employed for real-time temperature feedback of the heat source, and a PID control module is integrated to maintain the sample cylinder’s temperature fluctuation within ±0.5 °C. Based on the experimental data correlating IRH power with temperature, the fitted functional relationship is expressed as eq . 1 TIRH=−1.582×10−4×P3−0.016×P3+3.202×P+71.734

The experimental procedure is as follows:(1)The experimental apparatus containing the coal sample was evacuated to create a vacuum.(2)The temperature of the constant-temperature chamber and the power of the IRH tube were set, and then the power was turned on to apply IRH to the coal sample.(3)The inlet valve connected to the high-pressure gas cylinder was opened, the balance valve between the two cylinders was closed, and methane gas was charged into the reference cylinder until the pressure reached the preset value. Then, the inlet valve was closed, and the pressure in the reference cylinder was recorded.(4)The balance valve was slowly opened, allowing gas to flow from the reference cylinder into the sample cylinder. The pressures in both cylinders were allowed to stabilize and reach equilibrium, and then the pressure and temperature values in both the sample and reference cylinders were recorded.(5)The experimental setup was kept running for more than 12 h. After this period, the coal sample had fully adsorbed the methane gas, and the pressure reading had stabilized, indicating adsorption equilibrium. The equilibrium pressure and temperature were recorded.(6)After completing the above adsorption experiment, steps (2) to (5) were repeated: the reference cylinder was recharged to the preset pressure, the inlet valve was closed, the balance valve was opened, and the equilibrium pressure as well as the pressure and temperature in both cylinders was recorded. The system was maintained for another 12 h or longer, and then the final equilibrium pressure and temperature values were recorded. The entire process was repeated multiple times, and all corresponding experimental data were recorded.

IRH can significantly reduce the methane adsorption capacity of coal and promote methane desorption. Compared with traditional constant temperature control technologies (water bath/incubator heating), under the same temperature condition, the gas content of coal samples treated with IRH is significantly lower than that of untreated coal samples. Figure a–d presents the experimental data of methane adsorption–desorption of coal under IRH powers of 10, 25, 40, and 50 W, respectively. The results indicate that without IRH, the methane adsorption capacity of coal samples increases with the rise of pressure under isothermal conditions, conforming to the typical Langmuir isothermal adsorption characteristic and eventually reaching saturation. The desorption process exhibits an obvious hysteresis effect. After applying IRH, the microscopic structure of coal undergoes significant changesthe number of micropores decreases, while the development degree of mesopores, macropores, and fractures increases. The change in pore structure directly leads to the decrease of methane adsorption capacity. Under the same temperature and pressure conditions, the methane adsorption capacity of coal samples treated with IRH is significantly lower than that of untreated coal samples. The underlying mechanism can be attributed to the fact that the energy input by IRH compensates for the energy difference in the coal–methane adsorption system, reduces the adsorption potential, and weakens the van der Waals force between the coal matrix and methane molecules, thereby inhibiting the adsorption process and promoting desorption.

As shown in Figure , the isothermal adsorption and desorption experiment of methane directly characterizes the characteristics of methane desorption hysteresis. According to the classification of the isothermal adsorption curves, the desorption characteristics of methane before and after IRH conform to those of the Langmuir type I adsorption isotherms, as shown in Figure , and the desorption curve lags significantly behind the adsorption curve. This finding indicates that for the same amount of methane to be desorbed, more pressure needs to be reduced to provide energy for methane. , In the low-pressure zone (0–0.5 MPa), the adsorption of the coal monolayers is strong, the activity of the adsorbed methane molecules is low, and the adsorption curve increases rapidly. Because of the relatively low-pressure potential energy at this time, less energy is obtained through depressurization, resulting in slow desorption and a significant lag in the desorption curve. In the higher-pressure range (2–3 MPa), the adsorption process tends to slow down, and the pressure potential energy is higher. The pressure potential energy that decreases during the pressure relief process is greater. In terms of energy conservation, methane desorption causes further increases in energy, the desorption kinetics are stronger, and the desorption hysteresis sensitivity is higher. In addition, the injection of IRH energy increases the thermodynamic energy of the coal methane adsorption system. With increasing IRH power, a large amount of adsorbed methane absorbs the energy of IRH and converts it to its own thermodynamic energy. Ultimately, the thermodynamic energy of methane is greater than the adsorption potential energy of coal for methane, and the desorption ability of methane increases, whereas desorption hysteresis decreases.

The Langmuir parameters of adsorption–desorption can directly characterize the methane desorption ability and thus the hysteresis characteristics of methane desorption. As shown in Table , at lower IRH power, the difference between the parameters V L and a, which characterize the saturation adsorption capacity, is relatively large. This is because the compensation energy given by external IRH is relatively small. At this time, the coal adsorption capacity is strong, the desorption capacity is weak, and the desorption lag is significant. As the IRH power increases, the Langmuir volume V L decreases, and the difference from the desorption equilibrium constant a decreases, indicating that the injection of IRH energy decreases the coal adsorption capacity and increases the desorption capacity of adsorbed gases and that desorption hysteresis is not significant. The desorption equilibrium constant b is defined as the ratio of the adsorption rate to the desorption rate, which characterizes the magnitude of the desorption rate. The data in Table indicate that at lower IRH, the desorption equilibrium constant b is greater than 1, indicating that the adsorption rate is greater than the desorption rate and that desorption lags behind adsorption. However, as the IRH power increases, the b value becomes less than 1, and the deviation from 1 increases. The IRH energy accelerates the desorption rate of CBM and reduces the desorption lag. Analysis of the Characteristics of ΔG 0 in the Adsorption–Desorption Process

The ΔG 0 values of the coal adsorption and methane desorption processes were calculated on the basis of the fitted b values in Table using eqs and . The curves of ΔG 0 of the coal adsorption and methane desorption processes as a function of IRH power are shown in Figure . The functional relationship between ΔG 0 and Q m is fit, and the undetermined coefficient method is used to obtain the results of the characteristic constants k 1 and k 2. The variation in ΔG 0 during the coal methane adsorption and desorption processes was analyzed, and the reasons for the difference in Gibbs free energy during the adsorption and desorption processes were explored.

Variation in ΔG 0 with increasing IRH power during the absorption–desorption process.

As shown in Figure , ΔG 0 is linearly correlated with the IRH power Q m, and the correlation is greater than 0.99. In the methane adsorption and desorption process, substituting the fitting coefficient into eq yields a characteristic constant, k 1, of 42%, whereas the results of k 2 are significantly different. The difference in the variation in free energy with IRH power between the coal adsorption and methane desorption processes is reflected mainly in the characteristic constant k 2. In terms of coal methane adsorption and desorption, the physical meaning of k 2 is the same, reflecting the absorption of external IRH energy by the coal methane adsorption–desorption process. The larger the k 2 is, the more external energy it absorbs. The methane desorption process absorbs 14.8% of all IRH energy, whereas the k 2 in the adsorption process is only 0.016%, indicating that almost no IRH energy is absorbed. According to the second law of thermodynamics, the process of coal methane adsorption and desorption is irreversible. Compared with the process of coal methane adsorption, absorbing external compensation energy is a necessary condition for the methane desorption process, but the process of coal methane adsorption does not require external compensation energy and occurs spontaneously. The difference in compensation energy absorption indicates that the methane desorption process lags behind the coal adsorption process. Owing precisely to the difference in compensation energy demand and the irreversibility of the coal–methane adsorption–desorption process, a delay in methane desorption is caused.

As shown in Figure , the ΔG 0 of the adsorption process is less than 0 and increases linearly with increasing IRH power. The ΔG 0 of the desorption process is greater than 0 and decreases linearly with increasing IRH power. The increase in free energy during methane desorption is significantly greater than the decrease in free energy during coal adsorption of methane, and this energy difference decreases with increasing IRH power. The positive or negative sign of ΔG 0 indicates that the process of coal adsorbing methane is spontaneous, whereas the process of methane desorption is nonspontaneous. On the basis of the principle of minimum energy, the surface of coal will spontaneously adsorb methane to reduce surface free energy, but methane desorption will absorb external IRH energy, causing a nonspontaneous increase in surface free energy. The greater the change in Gibbs free energy is, the greater the motivation for the adsorption or desorption process to reduce or increase energy. Compared with the adsorption process of coal on methane, the desorption process of methane requires more Gibbs free energy to ensure smooth desorption of methane, which directly results in the nonconservation of Gibbs free energy in the coal methane adsorption system and the irreversibility of the desorption process. As the IRH exposure time increases, the internal kinetic energy of methane molecules during the desorption process increases, the number of methane molecules that desorb from the adsorption sites increases, the number of methane molecules on the adsorption sites decreases, the adsorption capacity of coal for methane decreases, and the absolute value of ΔG 0 during the adsorption process decreases. After the coal sample reaches its maximum temperature, the temperature of the coal sample remains unchanged, and the coal methane adsorption system is in a thermal equilibrium state. Methane desorption no longer absorbs energy, and the dynamic force of Gibbs free energy increase during the desorption process decreases. The absolute value of ΔG 0 during the desorption process gradually decreases, and simultaneously, both the adsorption process ΔG 0 and the desorption process ΔG 0 approach zero until their absolute values reach equilibrium. At this point, the difference in Gibbs free energy during the coal–methane adsorption and desorption process is 0, and the coal methane adsorption and desorption process transitions from a low-temperature equilibrium state to a high-temperature equilibrium state. The hysteresis characteristics of methane desorption have decreased, and the problem of desorption hysteresis has improved.

According to Figure , the enthalpy of the adsorption process decreases by 17.63 kJ/mol, whereas the enthalpy of the desorption process increases by 25.166 kJ/mol. The adsorption process is exothermic, and the adsorption method is physical adsorption. The desorption process is endothermic. As the temperature increases, the adsorption–desorption equilibrium of coal methane shifts toward an increase in enthalpy. Therefore, increasing the temperature induces desorption. The delay in desorption causes an increase in the overall heat of the coal–methane system. The adsorption and desorption process of coal methane is not adiabatic and requires an additional 18% of heat absorption from the external environment to successfully desorb methane.

The magnitude of the change in entropy during the adsorption–desorption process can reflect the strength of specific interactions between the adsorbent and the adsorbate, as well as the limited fluidity of the adsorbed molecules. As shown in Figure , the adsorption entropy decreases by 0.037 kJ/(mol·K), whereas the desorption entropy increases by 0.093 kJ/(mol·K). When coal adsorbs methane gas, the free gas molecules that move in three dimensions become adsorbed gas molecules that move in two dimensions on the surface of the coal, and the molecular translation is restricted, resulting in a decrease in the degrees of freedom. After IRH, the kinetic energy of the methane molecules increases, and the disorder changes from the adsorbed state to the free state. The net entropy of the system increases by 44% throughout the entire adsorption–desorption process of methane.

The entire coal methane adsorption–desorption process is irreversible and involves increasing entropy and absorbing heat. Combined with the analysis of adsorption–desorption spontaneity, coal methane adsorption is a process of decreasing enthalpy and entropy, and the lower the temperature is, the more spontaneous the adsorption process. Methane desorption is a process of increasing enthalpy and entropy, and the higher the temperature is, the more spontaneous the desorption process becomes.

Therefore, by reducing the volume energy loss during the adsorption and desorption process of coal methane, the hysteresis area of desorption can be reduced, and the desorption efficiency can be improved.

Results and Discussion

1During CBM recovery via IRH, the desorption hysteresis of methane is closely correlated to IRH power. Notably, this hysteresis effect is highly sensitive to high radiation power levels: increasing the IRH power can effectively mitigate desorption hysteresis and enhance methane desorption efficiency.2The difference in Gibbs free energy changes during the adsorption–desorption process of coal methane causes a lag in the methane desorption process compared with the adsorption process. Specifically, the Gibbs free energy change, ΔG 0, during the pressure-boosting (adsorption) process is smaller than that during the pressure-lowering (desorption) process. As the IRH power increases, this hysteresis effect changes. The ΔG 0 of the coal methane adsorption and desorption process decreases linearly toward 0, and the difference between the ΔG 0 of the desorption process and the adsorption process decreases, resulting in a slower desorption lag effect. In terms of thermodynamics, methane desorption is nonspontaneous, whereas coal adsorption of methane is spontaneous. During the methane desorption process, both enthalpy and entropy increase, with the increase being greater than the decrease in enthalpy and entropy during the coal adsorption of methane. The methane desorption process results in an irreversible increase in endothermic entropy, and the greater the IRH power is, the easier the desorption process is.3The gas volume energy during the coal methane adsorption–desorption process is not conserved, and the greater the difference in volume energy between the adsorption and desorption processes is, the greater the desorption lag. With increasing IRH, the gas expansion work during the desorption process increases, the gas compression work during the adsorption process decreases, the volume work of desorption hysteresis decreases, and desorption hysteresis improves. The higher the critical desorption pressure is, the smaller the lag of methane desorption during depressurization and extraction, and the higher the efficiency of methane desorption. During increasing CBM production through IRH, releasing pressure from a higher critical desorption pressure and increasing the IRH power are recommended to increase production.4The adsorption of methane gas by the coal matrix primarily occurs within the microporous structure. The lower the proportion of micropores in the coal pores, the fewer gas adsorption sites are available, making it more difficult for methane gas molecules to be adsorbed within the pores. Analysis of SEM images of coal samples reveals that infrared radiation heating induces changes in the microstructure of the coal matrix, specifically manifested as a reduction in the micropore proportion and an increase in the proportions of mesopores and macropores. Macroscopically, this results in a decrease in the methane adsorption capacity of coal. From a thermodynamic perspective, methane adsorption occurs in the form of physical adsorption, with adsorbed methane molecules residing in a stable state of lower energy. When infrared radiation heating is applied, the temperature of the coal seam system rises rapidly, increasing the kinetic energy of methane molecules. Consequently, more methane molecules overcome the binding forces on the coal surface and transition from a low-energy state to a high-energy free gaseous state, manifesting macroscopically as continuous nonspontaneous methane desorption. The Gibbs free energy changes (ΔG 0) for the adsorption and desorption processes are not equal; there exists an energy difference between them, which constitutes the thermodynamic origin of desorption hysteresis. The desorption process requires overcoming a higher energy barrier, and in the absence of external energy input, desorption cannot occur spontaneously or completely. Infrared radiation heating weakens the adsorption potential of adsorption sites on the coal matrix, significantly lowering the energy barrier for the desorption process and markedly reducing the desorption hysteresis effect. When methane gas enters the pore space during desorption, it must overcome capillary resistance and the molecular sieving effect within pore throats, performing worki.e., desorption hysteresis is accompanied by a loss of volume work. Infrared radiation heating induces thermal expansion of the coal matrix, increases the proportions of mesopores and macropores, enlarges pore throat diameters, and reduces the resistance to methane migration through pore throats. Concurrently, the temperature rise leads to a decrease in methane gas viscosity and an increase in the mean free path of molecules, enhancing the gas permeability within the pore network. These two factors collectively reduce the loss of volume work.
