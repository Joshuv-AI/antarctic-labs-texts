Effects of different pneumoperitoneum pressures on the upper airway assessed by ultrasonography: a prospective, randomized, observer-blinded clinical study
Authors: Engin Cetin, Bedirhan Gunel, Mehmet Yilmaz, Ayse Zeynep Turan Civraz, Mehmet Tahir Hosgoz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the effects of low-pressure (10 mmHg) and standard-pressure (14 mmHg) pneumoperitoneum on upper airway dimensions during laparoscopic cholecystectomy performed in the reverse Trendelenburg position, using bedside ultrasonography.MethodsIn this prospective, randomized, observer-blinded study, 76 ASA I–II patients undergoing elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy were allocated to either 10 mmHg or 14 mmHg pneumoperitoneum. Upper airway parameters—including lateral pharyngeal wall (LPW) thickness, tongue thickness and width, parapharyngeal tissue thickness, and midsagittal tongue area—were measured at five time points (T0–T4). Perioperative data and postoperative respiratory complications (stridor, desaturation, re-intubation) were also assessed.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between groups. All upper airway measurements increased over time, with significantly greater changes in the 14 mmHg group (mean LPW: 12.3 ± 1.1 mm vs. 11.2 ± 0.7 mm; p < 0.001). Group, time, and group*time interactions were significant for all sonographic parameters (all p < 0.001). Early postoperative respiratory complications were infrequent and similar between groups.ConclusionsIn summary, standard-pressure pneumoperitoneum (14 mmHg) was associated with more pronounced ultrasonographic changes in upper airway anatomy compared with low pressure (10 mmHg). This was not accompanied by statistically significant differences in early postoperative respiratory complications between groups.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07021482), registered on June 05, 2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03801-3.

Introduction

During laparoscopic procedures performed under general anesthesia, changes in patient positioning and intraabdominal pressure can produce mechanical and hemodynamic effects that may lead to edema and narrowing of the upper airway tissues [1]. These effects may be further exacerbated by factors such as the duration of surgery, airway trauma related to endotracheal intubation, and position-induced tissue stasis. Consequently, difficulties in ventilation and extubation may arise, and in some cases, re-intubation or the use of supraglottic airway devices may be required to provide adequate respiratory support [2].

At present, laparoscopic cholecystectomy is regarded as the gold-standard minimally invasive technique for the management of symptomatic cholelithiasis and cholecystitis [3]. Although this technique offers advantages such as a shorter hospital stay, reduced postoperative pain, and faster recovery, the creation of pneumoperitoneum during the procedure induces notable physiological alterations [4]. Insufflation of carbon dioxide into the abdominal cavity elevates the diaphragm, thereby reducing lung compliance, limiting venous return, and increasing intrathoracic pressure [5]. These alterations may lead to venous congestion in the head and neck region, thereby facilitating interstitial fluid accumulation within the upper airway tissues [6, 7].

The level of pneumoperitoneum pressure is a key determinant of the severity of these physiological effects. Low-pressure pneumoperitoneum (approximately 10 mmHg) has been shown to mitigate adverse impacts on pulmonary mechanics, preserve hemodynamic stability, and support postoperative recovery [8]. On the other hand, standard-pressure pneumoperitoneum (12–14 mmHg), while providing improved surgical visualization, may theoretically increase the risk of upper airway edema by elevating thoracic pressure and promoting venous congestion [9].

In addition to pneumoperitoneum, the surgical position itself is a critical factor influencing the development of upper airway edema [2]. The steep Trendelenburg position markedly compromises venous return, thereby predisposing patients to an increased risk of upper airway edema [10]. Sonographic assessments performed in robotic pelvic surgeries have demonstrated significant increases in tongue volume and lateral pharyngeal wall thickness following extubation [11]. Similarly, measurable alterations in upper airway structures have also been reported via ultrasonography following routine anesthetic procedures [12].

However, evidence regarding the presence of such changes in laparoscopic cholecystectomies performed in the reverse Trendelenburg position remains markedly limited [13].

In recent years, ultrasonography has emerged as a valuable tool for the noninvasive, rapid, and reproducible assessment of upper airway anatomy [14]. It has been demonstrated that measurements such as lateral pharyngeal wall thickness, tongue volume, and subglottic diameter can enable the early detection of post-extubation edema [15]. Considering that pneumoperitoneum created during laparoscopic cholecystectomy in the Trendelenburg position has the potential to increase intrathoracic pressure and impair venous drainage from the head and neck, it remains unclear whether these physiological changes lead to measurable edema within upper airway structures. In this context, evaluating the impact of different pneumoperitoneum pressures on upper airway anatomy using ultrasonographic techniques may help address a noteworthy gap in the existing literature.

The hypothesis of this study is that patients undergoing standard-pressure pneumoperitoneum (14 mmHg) will exhibit a more pronounced, ultrasonographically measurable increase in upper airway edema compared with those receiving low-pressure pneumoperitoneum (10 mmHg).

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed as a prospective, randomized, observer-blinded, controlled clinical trial and was conducted in the Department of Anesthesiology and Reanimation at Kocaeli City Hospital. The study was planned in accordance with the CONSORT [16] guidelines and carried out in compliance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Kocaeli University Clinical Research Ethics Committee on May 15, 2025 (approval number: KAEK/09.bI.01). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after providing detailed information regarding the purpose, methodology, and potential risks of the study. Additionally, the trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov under the registration number NCT07021482. No changes were made to the trial methods, eligibility criteria, or prespecified outcomes after trial commencement.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the change in lateral pharyngeal wall (LPW) thickness measured ultrasonographically across all predefined time points (T0–T4).

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes were designed to evaluate the broader effects of different pneumoperitoneum pressures on the upper airway. Accordingly, time-dependent changes in additional ultrasonographic parameters—including tongue thickness, tongue width, parapharyngeal space thickness, and midsagittal tongue area—were assessed. The relationship between these measurements and postoperative respiratory complications such as extubation difficulty, stridor, oxygen desaturation, and re-intubation was also examined. Furthermore, the potential impact of airway edema on postoperative recovery and the duration of stay in the post-anesthesia care unit was analyzed.

Participants

Patients aged 18–65 years, classified as ASA physical status I–II, with a body mass index (BMI) < 30 kg/m², and scheduled for elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy were included in the study. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: known anatomical abnormalities of the upper airway, acute upper respiratory tract infection, history of difficult intubation, obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (STOP-Bang > 3), severe cardiopulmonary disease, pregnancy, or the need for emergency surgery [17]. Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, or reporting of this study.

Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to the 10 mmHg (low-pressure) and 14 mmHg (standard-pressure) groups using a computer-generated sequence (random.org). The randomization sequence was generated by an independent anesthesiologist who was not involved in patient care. A second investigator enrolled eligible participants, while a third investigator opened the sealed, opaque allocation envelopes and informed only the anesthesia team responsible for setting the pneumoperitoneum pressure. Both patients and investigators performing the ultrasonographic assessments were blinded to group allocation; all outcome assessors remained blinded, and patients were unaware of group assignment throughout the study.

Anesthesia protocol

Premedication consisted of intravenous midazolam at a dose of 0.03 mg/kg. Anesthesia was induced with propofol 2 mg/kg, fentanyl 1 µg/kg, and rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg. Neuromuscular function was monitored intraoperatively using train-of-four (TOF) neuromuscular monitoring, and additional doses of rocuronium were administered when the TOF count exceeded 2 to maintain adequate neuromuscular blockade during pneumoperitoneum. All patients were intubated with a 7.5-mm cuffed endotracheal tube. Tracheal intubation was performed by experienced anesthesiologists. All patients included in the analysis were successfully intubated on the first attempt, and no intubation-related airway trauma was documented.

Anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane (0.7–1 MAC) in a mixture of 60% air and 40% oxygen, supplemented with a remifentanil infusion.

Mechanical ventilation was provided in volume-controlled mode with a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg of ideal body weight, a PEEP of 5 cmH₂O, and an I: E ratio of 1:2. End-tidal CO₂ was maintained between 35 and 40 mmHg. Intraoperative airway pressures (including peak and plateau airway pressures) were continuously monitored as part of standard anesthesia monitoring. Ventilatory settings were adjusted as needed to maintain adequate ventilation and the target end-tidal CO₂ range throughout the procedure.

Surgical technique

All patients underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy using the standard 3–4 port technique. Pneumoperitoneum was established by insufflating CO₂ through a Veress needle, and the procedure commenced once the target intra-abdominal pressure was achieved. All surgeries were performed in the reverse Trendelenburg position. To ensure procedural consistency and minimize variability, all operations were conducted by the same surgical team.

Ultrasonographic assessment

Bedside ultrasonographic measurements were performed by a certified anesthesiologist experienced in airway ultrasonography who had previously performed more than 150 airway ultrasound examinations, using a 7–15 MHz linear transducer and a 5–8 MHz curvilinear transducer (LOGIQ P9, GE Healthcare, Chicago, USA). The parameters evaluated included lateral pharyngeal wall (LPW) thickness, parapharyngeal space thickness, tongue thickness (anterior–posterior axis), tongue width (transverse axis), and midsagittal tongue cross-sectional area (Fig. 1). Measurements were obtained at five predefined time points: T₀ (pre-induction), T₁ (30 min after initiation of pneumoperitoneum), T₂ (5 min post-extubation), T₃ (1 h post-extubation), and T₄ (2 h post-extubation). Lateral parapharyngeal wall thickness

LPW thickness was measured using a 7–15 MHz linear transducer. The probe was positioned in the cervical region and oriented toward the external auditory canal to visualize the internal carotid artery (ICA). The lateral pharyngeal wall appeared as a distinct hyperechoic line. Swallowing-related vibration artifacts were used for dynamic confirmation of pharyngeal localization. LPW thickness was defined as the perpendicular distance between the ICA and the echogenic pharyngeal mucosal surface. In accordance with prior airway ultrasound literature, this measurement was operationally reported as LPW thickness in the present study. The identity of the ICA was confirmed using color Doppler imaging.

Parapharyngeal space thickness

Parapharyngeal space thickness was measured in the same imaging plane as the LPW assessment and defined as the perpendicular distance between the ICA and the cutaneous surface.

Tongue width

Tongue width was measured in the transverse submental view using a 5–8 MHz curvilinear transducer. The distance between the two most lateral borders of the tongue at the midline level was recorded on a frozen image obtained at end-expiration.

Tongue thickness

Tongue thickness was assessed with the patient in the supine position and the mouth closed. A 5–8 MHz curvilinear transducer was positioned sagittally under the chin while the patient maintained a relaxed tongue position. Tongue thickness was defined as the maximum vertical diameter from the dorsal tongue surface to the submental skin while visualizing the tongue root.

Midsagittal tongue cross-sectional area

To obtain the midsagittal tongue cross-sectional area, a 5–8 MHz curvilinear transducer was placed under the chin in the mid-sagittal plane. The tongue outline was manually traced on a frozen image using the system’s built-in area-calculation software, which automatically computed the cross-sectional area.

Intraoperative and postoperative monitoring

All patients were continuously monitored for noninvasive arterial blood pressure (NIBP), heart rate (HR), oxygen saturation (SpO₂), and end-tidal CO₂ (EtCO₂). Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was maintained within the range of 65–75 mmHg, with fluid replacement or vasopressor support administered as needed. Estimated blood loss was assessed by the surgical team based on the suction canister volume (after accounting for irrigation fluids) and surgical sponge evaluation, consistent with routine clinical practice in laparoscopic surgery.

In the postoperative period, patients were observed in the post-anesthesia care unit until achieving an Aldrete score ≥ 9. During this time, SpO₂ levels, the presence of stridor, signs of respiratory distress, the need for re-intubation, and any requirement for supplemental oxygen were documented. Extubation difficulty was defined as the need for airway support maneuvers (e.g., jaw thrust), use of an airway adjunct, or delayed extubation due to concern for inadequate airway patency. Stridor was defined as an audible high-pitched inspiratory sound associated with increased respiratory effort after extubation. Respiratory distress was defined as tachypnea and/or use of accessory respiratory muscles, with or without oxygen desaturation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum, number, and percentage (n, %). The distribution of variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For normally distributed independent numerical variables, Student’s t-test was used, and results were reported as mean ± standard deviation. For independent categorical variables, Pearson’s chi-square test with Yates’ correction was applied, and categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For repeated-measure outcomes—including LPW thickness, tongue thickness, parapharyngeal space thickness, tongue width, and midsagittal tongue area—a generalized linear model was employed. The main effects of group and time, as well as the group–time interaction, were examined. Bonferroni correction was used for pairwise comparisons. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. All analyses were performed on the per-protocol population, excluding participants who did not complete ultrasonographic measurements or who required postoperative ICU admission. An intention-to-treat analysis was not performed because patients with missing ultrasonographic measurements could not be meaningfully imputed for repeated-measures analysis.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software version 3.1.9.7. The calculation was based on an effect size derived from the study titled ‘Ultrasonographic evaluation of postoperative airway edema after robotic prostatectomy [11], in which a Cohen’s d of approximately 0.33 was reported. This value was converted to a Cohen’s f of 0.23 to conform to the repeated-measures ANOVA model.

The parameters used for the calculation were as follows: effect size (Cohen’s f), 0.23; number of time points, 5 (T0–T4); number of groups, 2; significance level (α), 0.05; power (1–β), 0.80; within-subject correlation (ρ), 0.5; and nonsphericity correction (ε), 1.0.

Based on these assumptions, a minimum of 34 patients per group (68 total) was required. Allowing for an estimated 10% data loss, the final target sample size was set at 76 participants.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

Participant recruitment began on June 18, 2025, and was completed on September 17, 2025. A total of 81 patients were initially enrolled in the study; however, two patients were excluded due to postoperative intensive care requirements, one patient was excluded because of conversion from laparoscopy to open surgery, and two patients were excluded because ultrasonographic measurements could not be completed in accordance with the study protocol. Consequently, the final analysis included 76 patients (Fig. 2) who met all criteria and completed all assessments (n = 38 in the 10 mmHg group; n = 38 in the 14 mmHg group).

In both groups, progressive increases were observed in LPW thickness, tongue thickness, parapharyngeal space thickness, tongue width, and midsagittal tongue area following pneumoperitoneum; however, these increases were markedly greater in the 14 mmHg group. LPW thickness increased from a mean of 10.3 ± 0.3 mm at T0 to 12.0 ± 0.6 mm at T2 in the 10 mmHg group, whereas it increased from 10.9 ± 0.6 mm at T0 to 13.9 ± 0.5 mm at T2 in the 14 mmHg group. Group, time, and group–time interaction effects were statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Tongue thickness increased in both groups in the postoperative period, with significantly greater elevation in the 14 mmHg group (p < 0.001). Parapharyngeal space thickness increased from 14.9 ± 0.9 mm to 15.6 ± 1 mm in the 10 mmHg group and from 14.9 ± 0.9 mm to 17.8 ± 0.8 mm in the 14 mmHg group (p < 0.001). Tongue width and midsagittal area also increased over time, and these differences were statistically significant particularly in the higher-pressure group (p < 0.05).

As shown in Table 4, group effects, time effects, and group–time interactions were significant for all ultrasonographic parameters (all p < 0.001). These findings indicate that higher pneumoperitoneum pressure (14 mmHg) results in more pronounced upper airway edema than lower pressure (10 mmHg), both across time points and relative to baseline.

Discussion

In this prospective, randomized, observer-blinded clinical study, the effects of different pneumoperitoneum pressures on upper airway anatomy during laparoscopic cholecystectomy were evaluated using ultrasonography. The findings demonstrated that all upper airway parameters—including LPW thickness, tongue thickness, parapharyngeal space thickness, midsagittal tongue area, and tongue width—increased both intraoperatively and in the early postoperative period. However, these changes were significantly more pronounced in the group that received a pneumoperitoneum pressure of 14 mmHg. This observation supports the hypothesis that higher intra-abdominal pressure may impair venous return, leading to venous congestion and interstitial fluid accumulation in the head and neck region [1, 18].

The cardiopulmonary effects of pneumoperitoneum have long been recognized. Upward displacement of the diaphragm increases intrathoracic pressure, resulting in reduced lung compliance, reduced venous return and cardiac output, and venous stasis in the head and neck region [1, 19]. These hemodynamic alterations are thought to contribute to the development of edema within upper airway tissues, particularly when higher pneumoperitoneum pressures are used. In our study, the significantly greater increase in LPW thickness observed in the 14 mmHg group compared with the 10 mmHg group further supports this mechanism.

Previous sonographic studies in robotic pelvic surgeries have demonstrated that the steep Trendelenburg position, combined with prolonged pneumoperitoneum, leads to the development of upper airway edema [2, 10]. In the study conducted by Aytaç and Soyal [11], significant increases in LPW thickness were observed following robotic prostatectomy, and these changes were associated with the duration of surgery. Similarly, Gaszyński [6] reported postoperative increases in tongue thickness and parapharyngeal space dimensions on ultrasonographic measurements after laparoscopic procedures.

The reverse Trendelenburg position used in this study is physiologically more favorable with respect to upper airway edema, as it enhances venous drainage from the head and neck compared with the steep Trendelenburg position commonly applied in robotic surgeries. Consequently, although increases in ultrasonographic parameters were observed, these changes were more limited than those reported in steep Trendelenburg studies and did not translate into clinically significant complications [20].

Several meta-analyses have reported that low-pressure pneumoperitoneum (≤ 10 mmHg) exerts fewer adverse hemodynamic and pulmonary effects compared with higher pressures [8, 18]. Reijnders-Boerboom et al. [9] reported that low-pressure laparoscopy reduces postoperative pain, the incidence of PONV, and the inflammatory response. Our findings are consistent with this literature, as upper airway edema parameters were significantly lower in the low-pressure group. This suggests that low-pressure pneumoperitoneum not only mitigates cardiopulmonary effects but also decreases edema formation within upper airway tissues.

Clinically, although low-pressure pneumoperitoneum is often presumed to compromise surgical visibility, performing all procedures by the same surgical team minimized operator-related variability and demonstrated that low-pressure pneumoperitoneum did not result in significant differences in operative duration or pneumoperitoneum time. This finding supports the clinical feasibility of the low-pressure approach [8, 9, 13, 18].

In the literature, measurements such as LPW thickness, tongue volume, and parapharyngeal space dimensions have been reported to be associated with postoperative respiratory complications, including airway distress and stridor following extubation [21–23]. In our study, although the incidence of stridor and difficult extubation was higher in the high-pressure group, the difference did not reach statistical significance. This may be attributed to the low-risk nature of the study population, which consisted of ASA I–II patients with a BMI < 30.

Notably, although the ultrasonographic differences between pneumoperitoneum pressures were statistically significant, they did not translate into significant differences in early clinical respiratory outcomes in this relatively low-risk cohort (ASA I–II and BMI < 30 kg/m², with patients at increased risk for obstructive sleep apnea excluded according to the study criteria). Therefore, our findings primarily demonstrate physiological differences in upper airway structures rather than proven differences in clinically meaningful outcomes. Nonetheless, these physiological changes may be more clinically relevant in higher-risk populations such as obese patients, individuals with obstructive sleep apnea (particularly those with elevated STOP-Bang scores), or patients with limited airway reserve, in whom relatively small increases in upper airway edema may potentially influence extubation safety and postoperative airway management. Future studies focusing on higher-risk patients are warranted to clarify the clinical implications.

Considering the growing importance of airway ultrasonography in anesthetic practice, the findings of our study indicate that ultrasonographic measurements may serve as a valuable noninvasive method for assessing airway edema. The ability to monitor upper airway structures in a real-time and repeatable manner provides meaningful advantages in enhancing extubation safety and predicting the risk of respiratory complications [24].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center and involved a relatively low-risk patient population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader and more heterogeneous groups. In addition, upper airway edema was assessed exclusively through ultrasonographic parameters; the absence of radiological (CT or MRI) or endoscopic confirmation represents an important limitation.

All measurements were performed by a single investigator, which may introduce observer-dependent variability, and inter-observer reliability was not assessed. Although variables potentially influencing edema—such as pneumoperitoneum duration, positioning time, and fluid management—were standardized as much as possible, individual differences could not be entirely controlled.

Finally, because the study focused solely on the early postoperative period, potential late-phase clinical consequences of upper airway edema could not be evaluated.

Conclusion

In summary, standard-pressure pneumoperitoneum (14 mmHg) was associated with more pronounced ultrasonographic changes in upper airway anatomy compared with low pressure (10 mmHg). This was not accompanied by statistically significant differences in early postoperative respiratory complications between groups.
