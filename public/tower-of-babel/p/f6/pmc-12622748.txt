Prognostic factors and prediction model for facial scar improvement in laser-treated patients: A machine learning-based retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ting Xie, Xuan Dang, Yan Jiao, Xintong Jiang, Hengxin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The face, being central and exposed, is highly susceptible to trauma and subsequent scar formation. Laser therapy is a common and effective treatment method for facial scars. However, treatment outcomes vary substantially. Consequently, we aimed to identify key prognostic factors and develop a predictive model for laser treatment outcomes using machine learning. We retrospectively enrolled patients seeking laser treatment for facial scars at our institution (2014–2024). Based on expert consensus, literature review, and clinical experience, we defined 21 potential predictors and 2 outcomes. Predictive models were developed using 7 machine learning algorithms (including Random Forest and XGBoost), supplemented by univariate and multivariate analyses. Performance was evaluated via receiver operating characteristic curves and decision curve analysis, with Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) providing model interpretability. The study included 1456 patients. Univariate and multivariate analyses identified 8 significant predictors, including sun protection and duration of care. The XGBoost model demonstrated superior performance in discrimination (area under the curve = 0.859), calibration (Brier score = 0.137), and precision-recall capability (precision-recall-area under the curve = 0.721). SHAP-based summary plots enabled global and local interpretation of the model. Machine learning proves a reliable tool for predicting facial scar laser treatment outcomes. The SHAP method effectively explains the XGBoost model’s mechanisms, enabling clinicians to optimize personalized treatment strategies.

1. Introduction

The face, a central feature of human identity, requires intact skin for expression and social interaction.[1] Epidemiological evidence indicates high susceptibility to head and neck scarring due to the prevalence of inflammatory conditions (e.g., acne) and the region’s exposure compared to clothing-protected areas, increasing vulnerability to burns and trauma.[2,3] Consequently, facial scar management remains a priority in dermatology and plastic surgery. Among conventional treatments (including surgery, radiation, silicone sheeting, corticosteroid injections, and laser therapy) laser has emerged as the foundational and preferred initial intervention,[4–8] though substantial prognostic variability persists clinically despite its efficacy. Given the high incidence and clinical significance of facial scarring, developing a laser treatment-based predictive model to identify prognostic determinants and optimize outcomes is imperative.

This innovative study employs machine learning (ML) (an artificial intelligence subfield focused on pattern recognition and algorithmic prediction with proven success in medical research) for factor selection and predictive modeling. This study represents the 1st application of ML to identify prognostic factors and optimize predictive models following laser therapy for facial scars. Our approach overcomes limitations of conventional analytics in handling sample constraints, variable selection, complex interactions, and nonlinear relationships. We enhance model credibility and clinical utility through multi-algorithm optimization and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) interpretation, which uniquely elucidates feature-prediction dynamics. This ML investigation in the field aims to: identify key prognostic factors via institutional patient data analysis; establish an optimal predictive model through comparative ML evaluation; and develop a clinically actionable risk-stratification system for adverse laser therapy outcomes.

2. Patients and methods

2.1. Patients

This retrospective study, conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by our institutional ethics committee with documented patient consent, enrolled patients receiving laser treatment for facial scars at our department between January 2014 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria comprised: age 18 to 70 years without comorbidities affecting scar treatment; treatment exclusively administered by 1 of 2 senior attending physicians; complete clinical documentation including serial pre-/post-treatment imaging and Observer Scar Assessment Scale scores. Exclusion criteria were: perioperative surgical interventions; concurrent facial therapies (e.g., microneedling or energy-based procedures); incomplete or inaccessible datasets.

2.2. Data preprocessing

Data preprocessing was performed using R software (version 4.3.2; Posit, PBC, Boston). Missing data were handled using multiple imputation with the “MICE” package in R. Five imputations were performed, excluding variables with more than 20% missing data.

2.3. Predictor selection and definition

Based on literature review, expert consensus, and clinical experience, we evaluated 21 potential predictors encompassing: demographic factors (age, sex, occupation), environmental exposures (sun protection, daily sun exposure), scar characteristics (pretreatment severity, facial location), comorbidities (facial comorbidity status, family history), behavioral factors (alcohol use, smoking, sleep patterns), psychological assessments (anxiety, depression), skin attributes (skin type, self-emotional care), therapeutic education (skincare education frequency/duration), and treatment parameters (laser type, combination therapy). Variables demonstrating statistical significance (P < .05) in univariate analysis were subsequently entered into multivariate logistic regression, with those maintaining P < .05 retained as final predictors.

Treatment outcomes were dichotomized as improved/not significantly improved using the Observer Scar Assessment Scale. This clinician-administered instrument evaluates 5 objective parameters: vascularity (erythema intensity), pigmentation (vs surrounding skin), thickness (palpation/ultrasound), relief (visual/instrumental assessment), and pliability (skin distensibility), each scored via 10-point Likert scales (1 = normal skin; 10 = worst pathology; total score = 50). Scars were stratified into 4 severity grades: mild (6–15; all parameters ≤ 3), moderate (16–25; ≥2 parameters ≥ 5), severe (26–35; ≥3 parameters ≥ 7), and very severe (≥36). Improvement required ≥ 1-grade reduction in severity classification post-treatment.[9,10]

2.4. Model assessment

We developed prediction models using Python 3.7, incorporating 7 ML algorithms (Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, Adaptive Boosting, Multilayer Perceptron, Gradient Boosting Machine, and Extreme Gradient Boosting [XGB]) along with the conventional Logistic Regression model. For each of these models, a hyperparameter tuning strategy was employed, utilizing grid search combined with 5-fold cross-validation to identify the optimal parameter set.

2.5. Model evaluation

The receiver operating characteristic was plotted to assess discriminatory ability, with an AUC ≥ 0.70 considered indicative of good discrimination. Calibration plots were used to visualize the agreement between the mean predicted risk and the actual event occurrence. A closer alignment of the solid line representing the prediction model’s performance with the dashed diagonal line representing perfect prediction by the ideal model indicates better calibration agreement. A Brier score of <0.25 was deemed to represent acceptable overall performance in terms of both discrimination and calibration. Decision curve analysis was employed to measure clinical utility by evaluating the net benefit across different threshold probabilities. In addition to the aforementioned metrics, the present study constructed a composite score based on sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value to evaluate the models, thereby facilitating the selection of the optimal model.

2.6. Model interpretation

We employed SHAP to interpret the model. This method, proposed by Mitchell et al in 2022,[11] explains the predictions generated by ML models and elucidates the contribution of each feature. The entire framework of the interpretable ML process is illustrated in Figure 1.

3.2. Variable selection

Building upon the results screened by univariate analysis, multivariate analysis was performed (Table 2). Factors demonstrating statistically significant differences included: scar etiology, where compared to surgical scars, those resulting from infection (P < .001, OR = 3.041) and burns (P = .004, OR = 2.064) were significantly more likely to be associated with poor prognosis after treatment; scar location, where compared to forehead scars, perioral (P < .001, OR = 5.285) and periorbital scars (P < .001, OR = 7.233) were significantly more likely to be associated with poor prognosis; sun protection measures, where compared to individuals who never or only occasionally used protection, those who had recently prioritized it (P < .001, OR = 0.466) and those who had consistently prioritized it (P < .001, OR = 0.170) were significantly more likely to achieve a better prognosis; duration of sun exposure, where compared to daily exposure exceeding 5 hours, durations of 0 to 2 hours (P < .001, OR = 0.164) and 2 to 5 hours (P < .001, OR = 0.278) were significantly more likely to be associated with a better prognosis; Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale score, where compared to no anxiety, mild (P < .001, OR = 2.222), moderate (P < .001, OR = 2.762), and severe anxiety (P < .001, OR = 4.363) were significantly more likely to be associated with poor prognosis after treatment; number of nursing education sessions received, where compared to zero sessions, 1 to 2 sessions (P < .001, OR = 0.138) and > 2 sessions (P < .001, OR = 0.119) were significantly more likely to be associated with better prognosis after laser treatment; total duration of nursing care received, where compared to 0 to 4 hours, durations of 4 to 8 hours (P < .001, OR = 0.368), 8 to 12 hours (P < .001, OR = 0.390), and > 12 hours (P < .001, OR = 0.252) were significantly more likely to be associated with a better prognosis; and combination therapy, where compared to laser therapy alone, laser therapy combined with traditional therapy (P = .005, OR = 0.578) was significantly more likely to improve prognosis.

3.3. Model evaluation

Figure 2 and Table 3 demonstrate that in the test set, the XGB model exhibited superior discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.874). Figure 3 presents the calibration plot and Brier score, revealing that the XGB model achieved the highest calibration performance (Brier score = 0.126). In the clinical decision curve analysis (Fig. 4), the net clinical benefit of all models exceeded both the “treat all” and “treat none” strategies across various threshold probabilities, with minimal differences observed between the models. Additionally, the precision-recall curve (Fig. 5) showed that the XGB model attained the largest area under the curve (precision-recall AUC = 0.818). Collectively, these results indicate that the XGB model is the optimal prediction model.

4. Discussion

This study developed and validated a ML model for predicting the prognosis of facial scars following laser therapy while enhancing model interpretability. A total of 21 relevant factors were analyzed, including occupation, ultraviolet (UV) protection measures, daily sun exposure duration, pretreatment scar severity, facial scar location, skin type, self-emotional care, skin care education, number of nursing education sessions received, total duration of nursing care received, and types of laser therapy administered. Subsequently, 9 key factors including age and nursing-related variables were selected for ML modeling. The excessively small or large OR values may be related to random errors caused by “small sample sizes in certain subgroups” (e.g., the subgroup with Nurse preach = 0 comprised 189 individuals, accounting for 12.98% of the total sample). Despite the presence of excessively small or large OR values, the model’s discriminative ability, as measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, remains unaffected. Among all constructed models, XGB demonstrated optimal performance, exhibiting superior discriminatory ability and calibration performance. Furthermore, it achieved the highest net benefit in clinical decision-making across intervention thresholds, establishing XGB as the benchmark model. Additionally, we employed the SHAP algorithm with the XGB model to visually rank influential factors by importance magnitude. This approach demonstrated SHAP’s advantages in providing a consistent interpretative framework and quantifying feature contributions.

Traditional ML, as understood today, traces its earliest origins back to the 1950s and 1960s.[12] Over the subsequent 5 decades, significant progress in ML theory has enabled its more direct application within the medical field. Furthermore, driven by the rapid advancement of big data technologies, the volume of clinical data available in healthcare continues to grow.[13] This has allowed artificial intelligence techniques to be employed for interpreting vast datasets in increasingly sophisticated ways. Notably, ML excels at effectively capturing nonlinear relationships between patient characteristics and clinical variables. For instance, 1 study demonstrated that a ML-based model could accurately predict the likelihood of response to fluid resuscitation in patients with oliguric acute kidney injury.[14] Another predictive ML model, named the “Artificial Intelligence Sepsis Expert,” can predict the onset of sepsis at least 4 to 12 hours earlier than intensive care unit clinicians by analyzing data trends in real-time. These trends are extracted from a large volume of continuous vital sign measurements and laboratory test results within the Electronic Medical Record.[12] In the field of plastic surgery, ML applications are also emerging. Examples include using ML to analyze facial features and patterns to aid in facial recognition and analysis,[15] as well as research combining photographs of free flaps with clinical assessment metrics to build prognostic prediction models using ML.[16] A 2025 systematic review documented the application of artificial intelligence and ML in the intelligent analysis of skin scar images for laser therapy. It reported that these technologies enhance scar classification accuracy to 98% and, through integration with 3D reconstruction, enable quantitative treatment assessment via precise measurement of scar volume changes.[17] Further studies have investigated integrated AI-laser systems, leveraging convolutional neural networks and deep learning to modulate laser intensity and depth in real-time for treating scars and other skin structural anomalies.[18] A separate review the same year further evaluated the potential of ML in scar research, highlighting its dermatological applications for predicting individual responses to laser therapy. It underscored the use of ML models in automating scar subtyping and optimizing treatment plans, thereby facilitating personalized laser interventions.[19] However, overall, the application of ML in plastic surgery remains at a nascent stage. Compared to traditional methods, ML offers several advantages. Firstly, ML data analysis does not require explicit statistical assumptions, such as linear relationships or independence among variables. Consequently, ML can automatically capture nonlinear relationships and interactions. This is particularly beneficial for handling complex real-world data. Secondly, regarding feature selection, traditional methods often necessitate manual variable screening, whereas ML incorporates automated feature selection capabilities. Techniques like LASSO and Random Forest’s feature importance assessment allow for more efficient identification of key risk factors. This can be highly advantageous for research, especially when dealing with a large number of potential predictor variables. Thirdly, concerning overfitting: traditional methods are prone to overfitting with small sample sizes, while ML employs methods like regularization and cross-validation to effectively prevent overfitting and enhance model generalizability. This is particularly crucial for medical research, where sample sizes are often limited. Fourthly, in terms of predictive performance, ML models such as Random Forests and Gradient Boosting Trees often achieve higher accuracy rates, especially performing better with large datasets. This potential improvement in predictive effectiveness could help us more accurately forecast treatment outcomes or risk factors. Of course, this does not imply that current ML is superior to traditional analytical methods in every aspect. For example, traditional methods retain an advantage in terms of interpretability.

Currently, ML is primarily divided into 3 categories: supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning, with our research falling under supervised learning, which constitutes the core component of ML algorithms and encompasses the core functions of classification and regression. Simply put, the steps of supervised ML involve[20]: obtaining a dataset and splitting it into separate training, validation, and test sets; using the training and validation sets to train the model to recognize the relationships between features and the target; evaluating the model using the test set to determine its predictive performance. The advantages of supervised ML (including its high accuracy, interpretability, and flexibility in directly solving classification/regression problems) make it particularly suitable for our research. Furthermore, we employed the SHAP method, a model interpretation framework based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory, whose core principle lies in fairly allocating the contribution of each feature variable to the prediction outcome.[21] Compared to traditional feature importance analysis, SHAP offers advantages such as: a unified scale of explanation (the sum of all feature contributions equals the difference between the prediction and the baseline value); support for individual sample prediction interpretation (e.g., visualizing the direction and magnitude of the effect of variables like a specific patient’s age or scar area); the ability to reveal feature interaction effects by decomposing synergistic influences through conditional expectation functions. In clinical prediction models, quantifying SHAP values can thus assist physicians in understanding the decision-making rationale of the ML “black box.”

In selecting variables for inclusion, besides numerous factors related to laser therapy and facial scars, we also incorporated several nursing-related factors, such as the frequency and total duration of care education received. This decision was made not only because daily care is crucial for laser treatment outcomes but also due to our consideration of psychological factors’ impact on prognosis. Previous studies have demonstrated that facial scars significantly affect patients’ psychological states, leading to depression or anxiety.[22] Furthermore, research has established a direct correlation between patient self-assessed scar severity and the level of psychological distress experienced. Therefore, we posit that effective nursing care for facial scar patients (including post-treatment wound care and psychological support) can also improve patients’ self-perception of their scars and their prognosis. This may be related to how anxiety can affect cortisol secretion, disrupt the autonomic nervous system, and reduce patient treatment adherence.[23] Additionally, the cause of the scar (whether from burns or infection) also influences prognosis. For burn-related scars, we attribute this primarily to burn depth; deeper burns may lead to overactive fibroblasts during repair, resulting in excessive collagen fiber deposition that adversely affects outcomes.[24] Persistent infection, particularly long-term infection, maintains the wound in a continuous inflammatory state, stimulating collagen deposition. It also disrupts the normal wound healing process, leading to abnormal scar structure formation that complicates repair.[25] Regarding the influence of scar location, we believe that perioral and periocular areas are high-tension regions[26]; routine physiological activities like blinking, eating, chewing, and speaking can affect fibroblast arrangement and collagen deposition during wound healing. For the detrimental effect of sun exposure on scar prognosis, we suggest it may be associated with direct UV damage to the scar, oxidative damage, and induced wound inflammation (particularly in scars within 6 months post-laser treatment) which are more susceptible to UV damage and poorer outcomes.[27] Concerning laser therapy combined with Chinese patent medicine, although previous Chinese studies suggest this combination can improve scar prognosis, we did not observe this effect in our current study. Conversely, laser therapy combined with other conventional treatments such as radiation therapy and hormone injections did demonstrate improved outcomes.

Our study also has certain limitations. Firstly, being a retrospective observational study, it may be subject to selection bias. Furthermore, the extended temporal scope of this study means that evolving clinical practices over this period could represent a potential confounding factor for the outcomes. Secondly, although we included variables as comprehensively as possible, potential omissions could still exist, potentially introducing bias.

5. Conclusion

In summary, ML not only enables the prediction of prognostic risk factors for laser treatment outcomes in facial scars but also facilitates the selection of optimal predictive models through comparison of multiple ML methods. Furthermore, it serves as an effective tool for visually demonstrating the relative impact magnitude of various influencing factors, thereby facilitating the development of more effective clinical protocols and the implementation of precise decision-making.
