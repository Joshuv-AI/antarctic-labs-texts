Evolution of transbronchial needle aspiration needles: Over the last half century
Authors: Yin Zhu, Hui Shen, Andrew D Lerner, Qin Li, Si Chen, Lingxiao Zhou, Jiaqi Zhou, Yang Xia, Kopen Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransbronchial needle aspiration (TBNA) is a commonly used sampling approach in the diagnosis of hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy as well as peripheral lesions. As a very important tool, the continued innovation of TBNA needles is a vital driving force for the development of the technique. Although TBNA plays an important role in interventional pulmonology, there are no clear standards guiding operators to choose an appropriate needle for their operation. In recent decades, with the advent of endobronchial ultrasound-guided TBNA (EBUS-TBNA), the real-time visualization of TBNA has been enabled. These modern TBNA needles, such as ViziShot2, FLEX 19G, Acquire FNB, and EchoTip ProCore, have made significant progress in specimen collection, convenience, and safety, though still remain grounded in the basic premise and initial upgrades to the original conventional TBNA (cTBNA) needles. This review introduced the developmental history of WANG cTBNA needles, and summarized the lessons of success and failure and the enlightenments for currently used EBUS- and other emerging TBNA needles, aiming to provide a significant reference for pulmonologists who lived through the cTBNA era and for junior physicians who start working in the EBUS-TBNA era. Despite its long history, TBNA is still playing significant roles in the diagnosis of pulmonary diseases. A deeper understanding from the historical perspectives would facilitate continued innovations in the field of TBNA and beyond.Keywords: Bronchoscopy, Endobronchial ultrasound, Needle, Transbronchial needle aspiration, Needle sizes, Bronchoscopy navigation systems, Robotic bronchoscopy

Introduction

Following the invention of the flexible bronchoscope in 1967, significant advancements have been made in the diagnosis and treatment of pulmonary disease.1 As flexible fiberoptic bronchoscopy ushered in the ability to visualize deeper areas of the lungs with less invasiveness, sampling technologies evolved and advanced in parallel. Where traditional small forceps were limited in their ability to break through the barrier of the bronchial mucosa, the invention of transbronchial needle aspiration (TBNA) opened up a new era of diagnostic bronchoscopy by enabling biopsies of lesions beyond the airway wall.

Before becoming one of the most commonly used diagnostic approaches for paratracheal, perihilar, and peripheral lesions, the prototype of TBNA was first recorded in Argentina in 1949 when Dr. Schieppati2 obtained cytological specimens from a subcarinal lymph node using a rigid bronchoscope. Little progress was made until nearly 30 years later, when in 1978, Dr. Wang3 reported the successful diagnosis of a paratracheal mass using cytological specimens obtained from TBNA using a 25-gauge rigid needle and a rigid bronchoscope. Another breakthrough occurred one year later when Oho et al4 performed the first TBNA using a flexible bronchoscope with a corresponding flexible needle, thus initiating the modern era of transbronchial needle biopsies that were performed without the use of a rigid scope. Today, TBNA remains widely used in the diagnosis of hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy and peripheral pulmonary lesions (PPLs).5

Over the past 50 years, the modern TBNA procedure has undergone significant development, including advanced localization technologies and improved needle design. Two commonly used techniques for localization and puncture are conventional TBNA (cTBNA) and endobronchial ultrasound-guided TBNA (EBUS-TBNA). In this review, we recount the history and upgrades of the TBNA procedure, the current landscape of TBNA needles, and comparisons of different sampling techniques.

WANG cTBNA needles

WANG cTBNA needles have undergone three major upgrades. Despite their distinctions, these three generations of needles follow the same overall approach: (1) the needles need to be passed smoothly through the working channel of the flexible bronchoscope with the goal of minimizing damage to the scope; (2) the needles are flexible enough to be advanced peripherally while also being stiff enough to achieve successful puncture; and (3) needle aspiration enables sufficient cytological or histological specimen sampling (Table 1).

First-generation WANG needles

The first generation of WANG needles contained three series: W-122, W-222, and W-322. The Letter “W” stands for “WANG” and the last two digits represent the outer diameters of the needles. Note that 22G and 21G needles are traditionally utilized for cytological specimens, while 19G and 18G needles are commonly used for histological specimens.6

W-122 was a single-lumen fixed needle with a round-tip guidewire designed to provide sufficient stiffness upon puncture.7 It was suitable for hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy, allowing aspiration without completely removing the guidewire7 (Fig. 1A). However, there was a high probability of scope damage caused by the fixed needle even under the protection of the guidewire. To address this issue, W-222 abandoned the fixed needle design and introduced a double-lumen retractable design, offering improved protection to the bronchoscope and airway.7 In addition, W-222 is soft enough to achieve a higher success rate of puncture than forceps biopsy and bronchial brush alone or in combination (47.8% vs. 30.4%) for peripheral pulmonary nodules.8 However, the need for complete removal of the guidewire before applying suction caused an inconvenience (Fig. 1B). As a solution, W-322 re-adopted the fixed needle design but altered it to a flat needle tip to protect the bronchoscope. Additionally, a bevel-tip guidewire was included inside to penetrate the airway wall and prevent needle blockage7 (Fig. 1C). To obtain larger histological samples, W-418 was proposed as the first generation of histological needles. W-418 consists of a 21G inner needle for puncturing and preventing blockage, and a fixed flat-tip 18G outer needle for specimen collection7 (Fig. 1D). Despite these improvements, limitations in scope and airway protection still persisted.9 W-418 was gradually replaced by the MW-319 needle, which will be discussed below.

As demonstrated, the evolution of these initial needle designs established the foundation for subsequent WANG needle enhancements, with a primary focus on three key principles: achieving successful puncture, ensuring bronchoscope and airway protection, and optimizing sampling efficiency. However, there are safety concerns with W needles that cannot be ignored.

Second-generation WANG needles

The second generation of WANG needles offers improvements in safety and provides adjustable needle flexibility and blockage avoidance. The second-generation needles are divided into four series: MW-122, MW-222, MW-322, and MW-522 needles (Fig. 1E-H).

The MW-122 needle is a single-lumen retractable needle with a screw nut at the proximal end to provide adequate stiffness for puncture (Fig. 1E).9 This unique design not only increases the hardness of the needles during penetration but also enhances protection for the scope. However, the rigidity of MW-122 is non-adjustable since it is directly connected to the guidewire (Fig. 1E). As a result, it is primarily utilized for hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy. MW-522 shares a similarity in structure with MW-122; however, its distal end of the guidewire is twisted by two soft and fine wires, providing even greater flexibility and thus, easier access to peripheral lesions, compared with MW-122 (Fig. 1H).

MW-222 is a double-lumen retractable needle whose guidewire can be partly removed, thus addressing the issue of MW-122’s inability to adjust hardness.10 Another modification is the inclusion of a side arm at the proximal end of the needle, allowing aspiration without completely removing the guidewire (Fig. 1F).9 MW-322 is a double-lumen retraceable mini-trochar needle with a flat tip that houses a bevel-tip guidewire (Fig. 1G). This innovative design prevents blockage of the 22G needle during puncture, which is not avoidable with the aforementioned MW needles.9 Despite the advantages of these MW needles, the pulmonologists call for a design that could obtain histological and cytological samples using a single needle.

Therefore, the second-generation histological needle MW-319 appeared, and it is the mainstream cTBNA needle still used today. MW-319 features a double-lumen trochar design with two retractable needles that have beveled tips.11 The outer needle, with a gauge size of 19G, is connected to a spring for sample collection. The inner needle, with a gauge size of 21G, is connected to the distal end of the guidewire for tissue puncture and to prevent blockage (Fig. 1I).11 The MW-319 needle can obtain both cytological and histological samples as needed and protect the bronchoscope and airway adequately.

The second-generation TBNA needles greatly enhance the protective capabilities for the scope and airway while still retaining the advantages of the first-generation needles. Furthermore, they allow for adjustable needle flexibility. Additionally, the invention of MW-319 has made it possible to obtain both histological and cytological samples simultaneously, resulting in a significant advancement in TBNA. However, these needles are primarily suitable for hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy, which poses a challenge for using TBNA in the diagnosis of peripheral lesions.

Third-generation WANG needles

There are three series of the third-generation WANG needles: SW-121, SW-221, and SW-521. All of these needles are single-lumen retraceable needles with “SW” indicating “Spring WANG.” The SW needles, characterized by unparalleled flexibility, solve the problem of cTBNA needles being unable to access peripheral lesions. Besides, they are distinguished by a spring located at the distal end of the catheter, with two ends connected to the needle and guidewire for increased elastic potential energy and stronger puncture force. In addition, the SW needles also simplify the procedures of sample preparation. Their guidewire protrudes into the needle when aspirating, which allows for the storage of samples at the end of the needle. From this, the samples can be directly blown out and smeared on to the slides for cytological examination without instillation of solution to release the sample.10

The SW-121 needle is a stiff needle primarily used for hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy (Fig. 1J). In contrast, the SW-221 needle can be both flexible and stiff since the proximal end of the spring is compressible allowing for adjustments in stiffness based on the length of the spring (Fig. 1K).10 During TBNA, the SW-221 needle becomes more flexible when retracted, as the proximal end of the spring relaxes and the guidewire is withdrawn from the needle cavity. To achieve maximum flexibility, SW-521 adopts a structure similar to MW-522, with the distal end of its guidewire twisted by two soft and fine wires (Fig. 1L). This makes the SW-521 needle the most flexible among the SW needles and is thus used for peripheral lesions.12

Apparently, the third-generation TBNA needles are safe and effective needles for peripheral lesions. These needles offer a stronger puncture force with simplified operational steps and make automatic-adjustment flexibility possible.

EBUS-TBNA needles

There is no doubt that the use of EBUS to guide TBNA biopsies has become a milestone. The EBUS bronchoscope, equipped with an ultrasound probe at its distal end, provides real-time, two-dimensional ultrasound images of paratracheal lesions that cannot be seen with a conventional bronchoscope.13 Moreover, the use of color Doppler guidance helps avoid vascular injury, enhancing safety and diagnostic accuracy.14 EBUS-TBNA is primarily used for hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy.15,16

Overall, EBUS needles inherited and developed from cTBNA needles. In addition to providing excellent protection and sampling capability, they also have their own unique considerations, including: (1) the quantity of the specimen; (2) the flexibility of the needles; and (3) ultrasound visualization (Table 2). The uniqueness of EBUS needles is attributed to the continuous upgrading of their materials and the progress in mechanical manufacture.

Mainstream EBUS-TBNA needles

Olympus needles

The ViziShot EBUS-TBNA needle (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) series consists of the ViziShot 21G/22G needles and their upgraded versions: ViziShot2 FLEX 19G needles and ViziShot2 21G/22G needles (Fig. 2A). Additionally, there are EZ Shot 3 Plus needles available.

The overview of EBUS-TBNA needles. (A) The classic Olympus needles include ViziShot needle, ViziShot2 needle, and ViziShot2 FLEX 19G needle. (B) The classic Boston Scientific needles include Expect needle. (C) Cook Medical needles mainly include EchoTip Ultra needles and EchoTip ProCore needles. (D) The classic robotic bronchoscopy platforms ION uses a soft Flexision needle for biopsy. EBUS-TBNA: Endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration.

Generally, the stiffness of the ViziShot 21G/22G needles limits their angulation. ViziShot 21G needles can obtain larger samples compared to ViziShot 22G needles. However, ViziShot 22G needles are comparatively more flexible to puncture mediastinal lesions at a larger angle.17 Thus, a retrospective study evaluated the diagnostic yield of the ViziShot 22G needles for peripheral pulmonary nodules and found that they achieved a diagnostic yield of 86.6%, which requires further validation.18 To overcome these limitations, the ViziShot2 series needles offer increased flexibility, stronger puncturing capability, and larger sample size.

The ViziShot2 FLEX 19G needles utilize innovative spiral laser cuts and have a large inner space, providing unmatched flexibility and enabling the collection of larger samples in difficult-to-access regions. These needles are also characterized by the spiral echogenic markings at their distal end, which improve the real-time visuality under ultrasound (Fig. 2A). Additionally, the ViziShot2 FLEX 19G needles enhance operational safety with a unique double-locking feature to prevent accidental needle protrusion.19

The EZ Shot 3 Plus needle (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) is composed of a multi-layer coil metal sheath, a Nitinol needle, and a Menghini tip. This design offers exceptional flexibility for accessing challenging areas and sharpness for puncturing. Smooth Shot Needles also have a unique stainless steel sheath to protect the biopsy channel during insertion. Moreover, Smooth Shot Needles can be moved smoothly with a shorter and ridged distal end.

The PeriView FLEX TBNA needle (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) has the same spiral pattern of laser cuts as the ViziShot2 Flex 19G for flexibility. In particular, this needle can be used with an Olympus 2.0 mm guide sheath, which extends the working channel of the bronchoscope and enables better access.

Boston Scientific Needles

EBUS-TBNA needles developed by Boston Scientific (Marlborough, MA, USA) include three series: Expect needles, Acquire fine needle biopsy (FNB) needles, and eXcelon needles (Fig. 2B).

Constructed with cobalt chromium, Expect needles are sharper and have greater ability to penetrate and resist deformation following multiple pass attempts (Fig. 2B). In addition to the advantages of material, the Acquire FNB needle has a tip consisting of three symmetrical cutting surfaces with formed heels. This provides greater stability and cutting ability during tissue puncture, and significantly increases the sample size with fewer passes.20 Acquire FNB needles obtained samples with higher quality and better histopathologic structure, although they performed similarly to conventional EBUS-TBNA needles in terms of diagnostic yield.21 Thus, the Acquire FNB needles have increased diagnostic yield for benign lymphadenopathy and are more beneficial for the process of next-generation sequencing (NGS)22 and the evaluation of the expression of programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1)23 for lung cancer compared to the conventional EBUS-TBNA needles.

The eXcelon needles, originally designed for cTBNA,24 are distinguished by a coil and an “X-Catheter.” It is the integrated structure of the distal coil and needle, along with a button lock system, that enhances the flexibility of the needles while maintaining sufficient stiffness for puncture. The “X-Catheter” acts as a bridge between the coil and needle, allowing the needle to pass the distal coil smoothly and improving the responsiveness and kink resistance during puncturing. Studies have shown that eXcelon needles have comparable safety and efficacy to MW-31925 or MW-12224 needles.

Cook medical needles

EBUS-TBNA needles developed by Cook Medical (Bloomington, IN, USA) include EchoTip Ultra needles and EchoTip ProCore needles (Fig. 2C).

A dimple pattern design of EchoTip Ultra needles greatly enhances needle visibility under ultrasound, similar to the spiral echogenic markings of the ViziShot2 FLEX 19G needles (Fig. 2C). These needles offer improved flexibility due to a coiled sheath wrapped at the end. The EchoTip ProCore needles, an upgrade over the EchoTip Ultra needles, feature a similar dimple pattern and coiled sheath design, but also include a unique Menghini bevel that improves sample size and diagnostic accuracy.26 In addition, ProCore needles have a core trap structure below the Menghini bevel that consists of a lateral opening and a reverse bevel (Fig. 2C). This allows for hooking and cutting additional samples.27, 28, 29 Compared to fine-needle aspiration (FNA), ProCore needles can obtain larger samples with fewer passes, although they perform similarly in terms of sampling adequacy, diagnostic accuracy, and tissue core acquisition.30,31 Besides, studies have found no significant difference in diagnosability and sampling adequacy when comparing ProCore 22G needles with standard 22G needles. However, the ProCore group had a significantly longer mean operating time compared to the standard group (P = 0.002).27,32

DT EBUS-TBNA needles

Based on the previous framework of WANG cTBNA needles, the Detian (DT) needles (Changzhou, Jiangsu, China) are designed to meet the demand for specimen collection and ease of use. DT needles are commonly used in China.

DT-EN-W122 is a single-lumen retractable needle with a transparent outer catheter, making it convenient to observe the needle and samples. DT-EN-W320 is a double-lumen retraceable needle that integrates puncture and aspiration by directly connecting the round-tip guidewire to the piston of syringe. This allows for the automatic adjustment of the guidewire position with the movement of the piston, improving TBNA efficiency. The DT-EN-W420 needle is also a double-lumen retractable needle. However, it is unique in that it connects the guidewire to the proximal screw nut instead of the piston of the syringe, allowing for adjustable needle rigidity. However, the guidewire must be withdrawn 3–5 cm for negative suction when aspirating. As a needle specialized for EBUS-TBNA, DT-EN-W522 provides both Nitinol and stainless-steel inner catheters. The plasticity and shape memory of Nitinol allow this needle to be used with a Fuji EBUS bronchoscope (Tokyo, Japan) and enable a large angulation at its distal end.

The development of cTBNA needles mainly focused on improving the structure of needles to enable biopsy of hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy and peripheral lesions in a safe manner. On this basis, the EBUS needles have been further improved in terms of needle flexibility (e.g., proposing needles made of Nitinol and Cobalt Chromium and adding spiral laser cuts on the needle tip) and visibility (e.g., adding echogenic dimpled pattern on needle tip and applying green sheath).

Impact of EBUS-TBNA needle size on diagnosis

The most widely used EBUS-TBNA needle sizes are 19G, 21G, 22G, and 25G. A series of studies have compared these sizes with regard to sampling efficiency, operation difficulty, and diagnostic suitability/yield for both benign and malignant diseases. We summarize these comparative studies here.

Needle size comparison: 21G–22G

In clinical practice, the sizes of EBUS needles that are most commonly utilized are 21G and 22G. Studies have found no significant difference in overall diagnosis rate and sampling adequacy between them.33, 34, 35, 36 However, 21G needles yield larger specimens and better preserve histological structures.33,37,38 A focused study by Saji et al39 demonstrated that the combination of cytologic and histological evaluation resulted in a significantly increased diagnostic accuracy with 21G needles compared to 22G needles. Another study showed that 21G needles were more effective in diagnosing benign lesions (such as sarcoidosis) compared to 22G needles (83% vs. 60%, respectively; P <0.01), but both needles had a similar diagnostic yield for malignant tumors in lungs. However, 21G needles provide better sample characteristics and had a higher diagnostic rate for non-small cell lung cancer.40

Cytological samples obtained by both 21G and 22G needles can be used for PD-L1 testing with a high success rate, but 21G performed better than 22G.41 This may be due to the fact that the 21G needle allows for larger samples for mutation and immunohistochemical analysis.40

19G–21G/22G

As mentioned earlier, ViziShot2 FLEX 19G needles have improved flexibility and good scope safety. This is due to the spiral laser cuts design and double-locking safety feature, allowing the 19G needles to access the lesions in difficult areas.42 Although there is no significant difference in the diagnostic rate between 19G and 21G/22G needles,43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48 combining 19G and 22G needles has been shown to increase diagnostic rate from 92% to 99% (P = 0.045).49 In the case of sarcoidosis and lymphoma, 19G EBUS needles have shown increased diagnostic yield and sensitivity when compared to 21G and 22G needles.6,50 However, another study showed the opposite result with respect to diagnostic yield.51 These contradictory conclusions may arise from the fact that specimens obtained by EBUS-TBNA needles are often a conglomeration of red blood and tissue cells. Although 19G needles have superiority of obtaining larger samples due to their larger needle lumen, there may be more blood contamination within the specimen that may negatively affect the diagnostic yield.

There was no significant difference in the performance of 22G and 19G EBUS-TBNA needles for molecular testing.47 In a study, samples obtained by the 19G needle were successfully used for PD-L1 testing in 90% of the patients with NSCLC and also had high success rates in detecting EGFR and ALK mutations.52 Of the samples collected by the 22G needles, 84% were successfully used for PD-L1 testing and 88% were successfully used for NGS or ALK gene-mutation detection.53,54

22G–25G

According to some studies, there is no significant difference in the diagnostic yield and sampling adequacy between 22G and 25G needles.55,56 Both types of needles can obtain sufficient samples for small- or large-panel NGS testing, with a success rate above 80%.57,58 However, there still remains some differences between them. Compared to 25G needles, 22G needles generally obtain larger samples with more tumor cells.59 When diagnosing benign diseases like sarcoidosis, the diagnosis rate of 25G needles is relatively low due to insufficient sample acquisition.60 However, another study demonstrated that ProCore 25G needles might help reduce operation time and needle passes compared to conventional 22G needles.61

Comparative studies have shown that the differences between the different sized TBNA needles are variable. But certain patterns arise: benign conditions require relatively larger sample sizes, while malignant disease (especially lung carcinoma) can be more easily diagnosed via smaller specimens. This general “rule of thumb” may be changing given the current need for advanced molecular testing for malignant disease, such as PD-L1 and NGS, where larger specimens are beneficial. Therefore, we cannot ignore the problem of unsatisfactory specimen acquisition with EBUS-TBNA needles, which results from inadequate sampling and incorrect puncture angles causing needle blockage. Rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE), similar to the frozen-section evaluation, can effectively perform on-site preparation, staining, and observation of puncture specimens obtained by TBNA. This allows for timely and accurate evaluation of the biopsy specimens and for the preliminary pathological diagnosis.62,63 During the EBUS-TBNA, operators can determine whether further sampling is necessary or if a change in the puncture site is required for obtaining higher quality specimens based on the ROSE results. This ensures sampling adequacy and quality, reduces the complications, and increases the diagnostic rate effectively.64 Of note, ROSE enables EBUS-TBNA to provide samples with sufficient tumor burden for molecular testing and contributes to genotyping of lung cancer.65

Besides, the methods used for specimen preparation can also affect the final diagnostic yield. It has been shown that the cell block preparation can provide additional information for the diagnosis with a nearly 7% increase in the diagnostic yield compared to the conventional smears.66, 67, 68 In addition, combining cytology slides with core-tissue or cell-block samples has resulted in improved diagnostic accuracy.69

Other TBNA needles

Bronchoscopy navigation systems and robotic bronchoscopy are emerging to facilitate the access to peripheral lesions. Previous studies have shown that peripheral TBNA plays a dominant role even with the use of electromagnetic navigation.70, 71

Currently, the SuperDimension system (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) is the mainstream electromagnetic navigation system.72 Although most traditional needles can be used with a steerable catheter that has a sensor tip for guidance,73 specific needles are designed for the SuperDimension system. These needles are featured by a short needle length for more flexibility to access distal airways compared to the traditional needles.70 Beyond that, the Arcpoint™ Pulmonary Needle (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) offers greater trackability and maneuverability due to a braided and tapered sheath design. It also provides various stylets with different levels of rigidity. Notably, for peripheral pulmonary nodules, electromagnetic navigation system-guided FNA shows good performance with a diagnosis rate of 50–77%,74, 75, 76, 77 while the diagnosis rate of conventional bronchoscopy ranges from 14% to 69% depending on the nodule size and location.78

On the other hand, there are two robotic bronchoscopy platforms: the Monarch (Auris Health, Sunnyville, CA, USA) and ION (Intuitive Surgical, San Carlos, CA, USA) robotic system. Their working channels are compatible with the biopsy tools used in conventional 2.0 mm bronchoscopy.79 The Monarch platform was originally equipped with a specialized needle that was later recalled. Hence, it now mainly uses third-party needles.80 The Monarch platform-guided needle aspiration can achieve a diagnostic yield of 74% in peripheral pulmonary nodules.81 Additionally, Intuitive Surgical designed the Flexision needle for the ION robotic bronchoscopy system, available as 19, 21, and 23-gauge needles.72 The Flexision needle is soft enough to pass through bends in a proper radian and advance in a straight path toward the target (Fig. 2D). Generally, the operation of the Flexision needle is quite similar to that of the classic Olympus EBUS needle. However, with the support of shaping technology, the direction of puncture can be confirmed and the resultant accuracy reached 93.3%.82

With the goal to obtain larger samples in a more efficient and safer way, some innovations such as mini-forceps83 and cryo-probe84 have been applied to EBUS-guided biopsy tools for hilar lymphadenopathy biopsies. However, since this review focuses on the history and innovation of TBNA needles, we would prefer to provide a detailed description of these new and creative techniques in our future work.

Conclusion

The continued evolution of emerging TBNA needles stems from the basic premise and initial upgrades of the original cTBNA needles. The improvements made to the cTBNA needles mainly focus on the structure of the needles, aiming to provide a safe and effective biopsy tool for hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathies and peripheral lesions. However, EBUS- and other emerging TBNA needles have been developed further in terms of sampling efficiency, materials, flexibility and visibility with the rapid progress of mechanical manufacturing and material science. With improved diagnostic accuracy and ease of use, operators can choose a suitable needle for lymphadenopathy or lesions according to its specific characteristics. Despite its long history, TBNA still plays a significant role in the diagnosis of modern pulmonary diseases.
