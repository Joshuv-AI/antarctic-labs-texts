The lacunar infarction with persistent trigeminal artery and delayed symptomatic hemorrhagic transformation: a case report
Authors: GuoDong Yang, Ying Tang, Wu Wei, YaoYao Shen, Xin Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHemorrhagic transformation (HT) is usually seen in patients with massive cerebral infarction, or patients undergoing thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy, which usually occurs in the acute phase of ischemic stroke.Case presentationWe present a case of a 61-year-old male patient with acute lacunar infarction in left posterior limb of the internal capsule with right persistent trigeminal artery (PTA). On the 20th day after the disease onset, the patient developed cerebral hemorrhage at the corresponding site. In addition. The susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI) showed multiple microbleeds in the brain.ConclusionsPTA is a risk factor for exacerbation of acute ischemic stroke, which may lead to secondary cerebral hemorrhage through some potential mechanisms.Keywords: Lacunar infarction, Persistent trigeminal artery, Delayed hemorrhagic transformation, Anterior choroidal artery, Case report

Background

HT is a serious complication of acute ischemic stroke, especially seen in patients with thrombolysis or thrombectomy, which affects 10–40% of people who have had an ischemic stroke [1]. Generally speaking, lacunar infarction is less prone to HT. PTA is a congenital vascular dysplasia, with incidence of 0.1 to 0.6% [2], which can cause acute cerebral infarction through steal phenomena or thrombosis in the anterior circulation and also lead to cerebral hemorrhage due to the combined aneurysm and arteriovenous malformation [3]. However, cases with both infarction and hemorrhage are rarely reported. Here, we report a case of PTA with delayed cerebral hemorrhage secondary to acute progressive lacunar infarction.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old male patient was admitted to the hospital at 10:00 on August 27,2024 due to slurred speech for 3 h. He had a history of diabetes for more than 20 years with poor glycemic control. Emergency head computed tomography (CT) showed an old softening lesion in the right basal ganglia (Fig. 1a). The emergency blood analysis showed high levels of glycosylated hemoglobin (8.8%; 4% < reference range < 6%), and the levels of blood routine, biochemistry, and coagulation function, free T4, T3 were all within the normal range. A slight high signal in the posterior part of the posterior limb of the left internal capsule seems to be visible in the diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) (Fig. 1b). He was diagnosed with acute ischemic stroke with a National Institute of Health stroke scale (NIHSS) score of 2, and refused the intravenous thrombolysis. At 12:00, the patient complained of decreased muscle strength of the right limb, which was evaluated as grade 4. No large vessel occlusion was presented and the right PTA was found in emergency digital subtraction angiography (DSA) (Fig. 1c d). At 17:00, the right limb muscle strength decreased to grade 0, despite continuous tirofiban was given, and the NIHSS score was 11. The second DWI showed significant high signal in the posterior limb of the left internal capsule (Fig. 1e). Be given systematic treatment, the patient ‘s right lower limb can be lifted off the bed surface, and the muscle strength was grade 3. Unexpectedly, the patient developed drowsiness and was unable to move the right lower limb again on September 16. The head CT revealed cerebral hemorrhage (Fig. 1f) and SWI showed multiple microbleeds (Fig. 2a b). To rule out other potential systemic problems, we reperformed the blood analysis again and found no abnormal levels of white blood cell count, red blood cell count, platelet count, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, creatinine, prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time. During the treatment period, his blood sugar and blood pressure were well controlled. The patient has provided informed consent for publication of the case.

Discussion and conclusions

According to a retrospective study, symptomatic acute cerebral small vessel strokes are the ischemic stroke subtype with the best functional prognosis and that the pathophysiology, clinical features and outcome of lacunar strokes are different from other acute ischemic cerebrovascular diseases [4]. However, our patient not only experienced the progressive stroke but also the delayed symptomatic HT. Although the observation periods of each study were different, the conclusion that non-thrombolytic HT generally occurred about 7 days after infarction, and a few may exceed 2 weeks is basically consistent, with an incidence of about 12% [5]. Generally, massive cerebral infarction is regarded as the most significant factor leading to the HT, it is worth noting that the populations of most previous studies were patients with non-lacunar infarction [1]. At present, there are few reports focus on the HT time of lacunar infarction, even so, the study led by Professor Muscari suggested that the incidence of HT is close to 0 at 6 days after lacunar infarction, even for patients with non-lacunar infarction, the probability of HT after more than 20 days is very low [6]. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of HT in acute ischemic stroke with PTA. Combined with the report by Professor Rinsho Shinkeigaku, two patients with delayed HT were cryptogenic stroke and patent foramen ovale respectively [7], suggesting that patients with delayed HT may be associated with some relatively special diseases.

For patients with acute ischemic stroke, the PTA is a double-edged sword. When stroke occurs, it may be a very important collateral circulation, which greatly reduces the infarct size [2]. However, in most cases, it may be a risk factor for the occurrence and aggravation of stroke [8, 9]. In general, infarction is relatively more common on the ipsilateral PTA due to steal blood phenomena. If it is cardiogenic embolism or arterial plaque shedding, it may cause multiple cerebral infarction lesions. This report presents that PTA combined with contralateral internal capsule infarction and secondary symptomatic HT during the subacute stage of infarction. We suggest the following points may be possible mechanisms. Firstly, under normal circumstances, the posterior limb of the internal capsule is supplied by the anterior choroidal artery (AchA), which is an important blood supply artery of the brain in the early stage of embryonic development. With embryonic development, its function gradually deteriorates and will be replaced by the middle cerebral artery and posterior cerebral artery. The PTA is an abnormal anastomotic branch formed between the internal carotid artery and the basilar artery during embryonic development, significantly altering the hemodynamics of the posterior circulation. Due to its ability to steal blood from the internal carotid artery, it often lead to severe dysplasia or absence of the ipilateral AchA, and a reduction in the range of the contralateral AchA blood supply area, and the corresponding parts are compensated by the posterolateral choroidal artery issued by the posterior cerebral artery [10]. The current DSA showed saltzman type III PTA [3], with a thin left AchA (Fig. 2c). Therefore, we suggset that the blockage of the left posterior cerebral artery branch is the responsible vessel for the posterior internal capsule infarction in this patient, Suo Yin, et al., reported a patient with left PTA accompanied by infarction of the right uncinate gyrus area, which belongs to the area supplied by the AchA [11]. We know that reperfusion injury is one of the important mechanisms of HT. Compared with AchA, the blood pressure in the PTA is relatively high. Since it directly originates from the internal carotid artery, the risk of rupture of blocked blood vessels is higher in the event of infarction. Secondly, PTA is an abnormal development of blood vessels, the terminal vessels from which may be hypoplastic, increasing the risk of bleeding [10]. Thirdly, multiple cerebral microbleeds (CMBs) have been proved to be a risk factor for HT, meta-analysis showed that antiplatelet therapy can increase the risk of HT in patients with multiple CMBs by 16 times compared with patients without CMBs [12], SWI showed multiple CMBs in our patient, moreover, who also received dual antiplatelet therapy.

There are several limitations in this report. Firstly, we did not re-examine the head CT before the patient showed symptomatic HT, and thus could not confirm whether the patient had already experienced asymptomatic HT in the early stage. Secondly, in clinical practice, we were unable to provide direct evidence of PTA supplying blood to the posterior branch of the left internal capsule. In the future, we may obtain the accurate “brain tissue-arterial correspondence map”, with the development of vessel-selective arterial spin labeling. Moreover, since PTA causes HT may be the relative high blood perfusion, does he blood pressure control of these peoples need to be more precise?

In conclusion, for patients with unconventional risk factors for cerebral infarction, whether lacunar infarction or non-lacunar infarction, we should be alert to the occurrence of HT. Regarding that the presence of white matter hyperintensities is another significant sign of cerebral small vessel disease on conventional MRI [13], once detected in the patient with lacunar infarction, suggesting that SWI should be completed as early as possible, in addition, the frequency of head CT monitoring should be appropriately increased during the treatment period if necessary to detect asymptomatic HT at the early stage, providing a reference for subsequent antithrombotic treatment decisions.
