AhBWR15 , A Novel RLK Gene, Confers Resistance to Ralstonia solanacearum in Peanut
Authors: Zenghui Cao, Kai Zhao, Hao Li, Xingli Ma, Di Cao, Yanzhe Li, Sasa Hu, Qian Ma, Yaoyao Li, Yi Fan, Mengtian Hou, Kunkun Zhao, Ding Qiu, Fangping Gong, Zhongfeng Li, Rui Ren, Dongmei Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBacterial wilt (BW), a severe soil‐borne disease caused by Ralstonia solanacearum , significantly impedes global peanut production. Despite its impact, the mechanisms underlying BW resistance in peanut remain unclear. Herein, we selected the highly resistant variety Nongdahua108 (H108) and the susceptible variety Nongdahua107 (H107) to develop a genetically segregating population. Bulked segregant analysis sequencing (BSA‐seq) of the F2 population (n = 432) revealed a novel major quantitative trait locus (QTL) qBWR15, located within a 5.9‐Mb region on Chromosome 15 (B05). Subsequently, 25 new molecular markers were developed within this region, allowing for the fine mapping of qBWR15 to a 668‐kb region containing four genes with non‐synonymous mutations. Notably, one of these genes, a leucine‐rich repeat receptor‐like kinase (LRR‐RLK) designated as AhBWR15, exhibits a non‐synonymous G > A mutation in the first exon. This mutation results in alterations to the encoded amino acid and the three‐dimensional structure of the protein. The 238 natural germplasms analysis also identified this novel mutation occurring exclusively in the peanut variety H108. AhBWR15 is localised on the plasma membrane and is responsive to R. solanacearum infection and phytohormone inductions. Overexpression of the LRR‐RLK gene AhBWR15 can significantly enhance resistance to BW by activating abscisic acid (ABA)‐signalling pathways. Our research provides valuable insights into a novel LRR‐RLK‐mediated disease resistance pathway, which has significant implications for enhancing peanut resistance to BW.

Introduction

Peanut ( Arachis hypogaea L.) is one of the most globally significant oil and food crops (Yin et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2021). As a competitive crop in international markets for China, enhancing yield and quality remains pivotal for advancing the peanut industry (Liu et al. 2024). Bacterial wilt (BW) is a devastating peanut disease caused by one of the most important phytopathogenic bacteria, Ralstonia solanacearum (Mansfield et al. 2012; Zhang et al. 2017; Luo et al. 2019). The soil‐borne R. solanacearum infects over 200 plant species (Peeters et al. 2013; An and Zhang 2024), which poses a serious threat to global food security. This vascular pathogen proliferates within the xylem, producing exopolysaccharides and toxins that block water transportation, causing severe wilting and sudden death of infected plants (Kashyap et al. 2021; Wang, Luo, et al. 2024; Wang, Qi, et al. 2024). Under certain conditions, BW leads to decreased seed quality and serious yield loss (50%–100%) of peanut (Yu et al. 2011; Zhang et al. 2019). Despite various chemical and biocontrol agents having been developed, breeding of crop varieties with durable BW resistance and desired agronomic traits by taking advantage of natural resistance is the most efficient and eco‐friendly managing strategy (Pavan et al. 2010; Huet 2014; Jiang et al. 2017). The peanut variety Nongdahua108 (H108) was characterised by its high productivity and high resistance to BW (Zhao et al. 2022), leading to its widespread adoption and large‐scale application in agricultural production. Understanding the resistance mechanisms against BW of H108 is of great interest.

Plant resistance to R. solanacearum , mainly involved in inhibiting bacterial reproduction proliferation, delaying wilting and reducing the mortality rate, is typically determined by quantitative traits (Zhao et al. 2016; Luo et al. 2019). The mapping of quantitative trait loci (QTLs) for resistance to peanut BW is crucial for molecular marker‐assisted selection (MAS) breeding of resistant varieties. To date, only several major QTLs conferring resistance to BW have been identified from distinct cultivars, such as Yueyou 92 (qBW‐1 and qBW‐2), Yuanza 9102 (qBWB02.1 and qBWA12), and Zhonghua 6 (qBWRB02‐1) (Zhao et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2018; Luo et al. 2019, 2020; Qi et al. 2022). The qBW‐1 and qBW‐2 were located on the Chromosomes (Chr.) A01 and A10, respectively, and qBW‐1 was fine‐mapped into a 14.4‐cM interval harbouring a nucleotide‐binding site‐leucine‐rich repeat (NBS‐LRR) type resistance (R) gene (Zhao et al. 2016). The qBWB02.1 and qBWA12 in Yuanza 9102 were mapped in a 2.3‐Mb region on Chr. 12, containing 9 NBS‐LRR genes (Wang et al. 2018; Luo et al. 2019; Qi et al. 2022). Another QTL, qBWRB02‐1, in Zhonghua 6 was also mapped on Chr. 12 nearby qBWB02.1 and qBWA12 (Luo et al. 2020). Although several loci associated with BW resistance have been genetically mapped, no candidate gene has been cloned and functionally characterised.

In addition to the typical NBS‐LRR resistance (R) genes, leucine‐rich repeat receptor‐like kinases (LRR‐RLKs) also confer the BW resistance in peanut. The firstly cloned R. solanacearum resistance genes, designated as RRS1‐R and ERECTA, were identified to encode NBS‐LRR and LRR‐RLK proteins in Arabidopsis thaliana , respectively (Godiard et al. 2003; Deslandes et al. 2002). Overexpression of the peanut homologous genes of RRS1‐R (AhRRS5) and ERECTA (AhRLK1) enhances the resistance to R. solanacearum in tobacco (Zhang et al. 2017, 2019). However, few LRR‐RLK type R‐genes have been identified and characterised in peanut, and their roles in peanut resistance to BW remain elusive. Moreover, genes enriched in plant hormones' signalling, mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway and plant‐pathogen interaction were also involved in peanut resistance to BW (Chen et al. 2014; Zhao et al. 2024; Wang, Luo, et al. 2024). However, the underlying resistance mechanisms of peanut to BW require in‐depth investigation.

This study aims to systematically explore the factors contributing to H108's resistance to BW. To achieve this, F2 and recombinant inbred line (RIL) populations were developed from a cross between H108 and a highly susceptible variety Nongdahua107 (H107) for the genetic mapping of major QTLs conferring resistance to BW in H108. A major QTL, designated qBWR15, was identified in a 668‐kb genomic region on Chr. 15 (B05), distinct from previously reported QTLs. Finally, we identified a LRR‐RLK gene as a probable contributor to the resistance observed in H108. Overexpression of the AhBWR15 gene enhances resistance to BW in peanut by activating an abscisic acid (ABA)‐mediated defence response. These findings not only characterise a novel resistance gene, but also provide a molecular framework for precision breeding strategies.

Results

BAS‐Seq Analysis of the BW Resistance in H108 Variety

An F2 population, derived from the cross between H107 and H108, was developed to facilitate the genetic mapping of major QTLs associated with BW resistance in H108 (Figure 1). Three‐week‐old seedlings from H107, H108, and the F2 population were inoculated with R. solanacearum . Observations at 21 days post‐inoculation (dpi) indicated that nearly all H107 seedlings exhibited wilting, whereas the H108 seedlings remained viable. From this population, 30 F2 individuals exhibiting extreme susceptibility (ES) and 30 individuals displaying extreme resistance (ER) were selected to form the susceptible bulk (SB) and resistant bulk (RB), respectively. Subsequently, a high‐throughput bulked segregant analysis sequencing (BSA‐seq) approach, along with genome re‐sequencing of H107, H108, SB, and RB, was employed to identify genomic variations associated with BW resistance. In total, 54.34 G base (Gb), 50.14 Gb, 77.62 Gb, and 76.28 Gb of sequencing data were generated for H107, H108, SB, and RB, respectively (Table S1). Comparative genomic analysis identified 1 502 829 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and 103 996 high‐quality insertion/deletion (InDel) mutations across these four samples. Through a sliding window analysis of SNP‐index and Δ(SNP‐index) plots comparing the SB and RB, a 5.9‐Mb genomic interval on chromosome 15 (Chr. B05:131975428–137765046) was identified as the candidate region significantly associated with the major QTL conferring BW resistance in H108 (Figure 2a,b). This major QTL has been designated as qBWR15 and is located in a novel genomic region that is distinct from previously reported BW resistance QTLs.

Fine‐Mapping of the Novel Locus qBWR15

Among the 1214 SNPs and 832 InDels across the entire Chr. B05, the majority of mutations are intergenic (1300, 63.5%), with the remaining variations distributed across upstream (145, 7.1%), downstream (95, 4.6%), intron (71, 3.5%), and exon (70, 3.5%) regions (Figure 2c). To refine the 5.9‐Mb genomic region, 20 InDel, 3 simple sequence repeat (SSR), and 2 SNP markers were developed within or adjacent to qBWR15 based on these mutations (Table S2). Eight of the 25 molecular markers exhibited polymorphisms between the parental lines H107 and H108. The InDel and SSR markers were employed to genotype the RIL population derived from the cross between H107 and H108, consisting of 151 F5 lines developed through the single seed descent method (Figure 1; Table S3). The RILs were inoculated with R. solanacearum in a field setting, and their phenotypic data were utilised for genetic linkage analysis.

Subsequently, qBWR15 was precisely mapped to a 668‐kb region between markers ZH_SSR2 and ZH_InDel9 (Figure 2d; Figure S1). The 668‐kb genomic region containing qBWR15 was examined, resulting in the identification of 32 candidate genes (Figure 2d; Table S4). Among the genes analysed, the majority have not undergone mutations. However, four genes, specifically those encoding the LRR‐RLK (AH15G25210) and pentatricopeptide repeat‐containing proteins (PPR) (AH15G25380 and AH15G25450), exhibited non‐synonymous mutations (Figure 2e; Table S4). Tissue‐specific gene expression analysis revealed that only the LRR‐RLK gene (AH15G25210) is highly expressed across various tissues and is significantly upregulated in the H108 line in response to R. solanacearum infection (Figure 2f; Table S5). Consequently, the LRR‐RLK gene AH15G25210, designated as AhBWR15, is hypothesized to play a crucial role in conferring resistance of qBWR15.

Haplotype and Phylogenetic Analysis of the AhBWR15

AhBWR15 has a non‐synonymous SNP through allelic comparison of the coding sequence (CDS) between H107 and H108 (Figure 3a). A guanine (G) to adenine (A) substitution was identified in the first exon at position 1216 bp of AhBWR15. This novel mutation was analysed among the 238 natural germplasms (Zhao et al. 2025), and it exclusively presents in the peanut variety H108 and its derivatives (Table S6). Subsequently, haplotype analysis was conducted based on the genotypes of the G > A SNP in 151 RILs (Table S6; Figure 3a). It was revealed that 76% of susceptible RILs possess a susceptible haplotype (Hap‐S), while 64% of resistant RILs exhibit a resistant haplotype (Hap‐R) (Figure 3b). Protein secondary structure prediction indicates that AhBWR15 encodes an LRR‐RLK protein comprising 1115 amino acids, including a signal peptide, three LRR domains, a transmembrane (TM) domain, and a serine/threonine kinase (STK) domain (Figure 3c). A SNP mutation leads to the substitution of glycine (G) with aspartic acid (D) at the 618 amino acid position, located between the TM and STK domains (Figure 3c). Analysis using SWISS‐MODEL revealed significant conformational differences between the Hap‐S and Hap‐R haplotypes of the AhBWR15 protein. These findings underscore the potential utility of the SNP, designated as ZH_SNP1, as a Kompetitive Allele Specific PCR (KASP) marker in MAS for BW resistance.

To further characterise the LRR‐RLK gene AhBWR15, a phylogenetic analysis was performed using the full‐length deduced amino acid sequences of cloned LRR‐RLK type resistance genes from various plant species (Figure 3d; Table S7), revealing that these LRR‐RLK homologues predominantly cluster into two distinct groups (Figure 3d). Notably, AhBWR15 and its paralogous protein AH05G29080 showed closer relationships with the well‐known LRR‐RLK type rice bacterial leaf blight R‐proteins Xa21 and Xa26 (Ronald et al. 1992; Song et al. 1995; Ercoli et al. 2022). This suggests that AhBWR15 shares structural similarities with these cloned R‐genes, which function as regulators of LRR‐RLK mediated plant immunity.

Expression Profiling and Subcellular Localization of AhBWR15

The promoter sequence of AhBWR15, spanning 2000 bp, was analysed for cis‐elements, revealing various elements associated with plant responses to phytohormones and biotic and abiotic stresses (Figure 3e). Furthermore, expression profiling of AhBWR15 under R. solanacearum infection and plant hormone inductions was conducted using quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) (Figure 3f). Under R. solanacearum infection, the expression of AhBWR15 in peanut roots exhibited up‐regulation, with significantly higher levels observed in the H108 compared to H107 (p < 0.05) (Figure 3f). The rapid and sustained up‐regulation of AhBWR15 in H108 roots following infection underscores its direct involvement in the pathogen response. Exogenous salicylic acid (SA) treatment resulted in a pronounced up‐regulation of AhBWR15 in both cultivars, with H108 showing a significantly stronger induction than H107 (p < 0.05). Jasmonic acid (JA) also up‐regulated AhBWR15 in both cultivars; however, expression levels in H107 exceeded those in H108 at 1.0‐ and 7.0‐day post inoculation (dpi). Similarly, ABA treatment elicited higher AhBWR15 expression in H107 at 1.0 dpi. These findings suggest that AhBWR15 integrates SA, JA, and ABA signalling pathways to mediate BW resistance.

To determine the subcellular localization of AhBWR15, a recombinant plasmid expressing the fusion protein AhBWR15‐yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) was constructed by inserting the full‐length CDS of AhBWR15 into the binary plant expression vector pCambia1300‐YFP. Subsequently, the constructs pCambia1300‐YFP (serving as the control) and pCambia1300‐AhBWR15‐YFP were transiently co‐expressed with a plasma membrane marker vector encoding the fusion PIP2a‐red fluorescent protein (RFP) in tobacco leaves via agroinfiltration. Confocal laser scanning microscopy revealed a pronounced fluorescence signal from the YFP and protein fusions (Figure 3g). The co‐expression of AhBWR15‐YFP and PIP2a‐RFP was specifically localised to the plasmalemma, whereas the free YFP exhibited a diffuse distribution throughout the cell. This subcellular localization was further corroborated in tobacco protoplasts, where AhBWR15‐YFP was predominantly co‐expressed with PIP2a‐RFP on the plasmalemma, in contrast to the cytoplasmic dispersion of free YFP. These findings are consistent with the hypothesized role of AhBWR15 as a plasmalemma‐associated LRR‐RLK.

Overexpression of AhBWR15 Enhances Peanut and Tobacco Resistance to R. solanacearum

To assess the resistance conferred by AhBWR15 in H108 against R. solanacearum , peanut leaves transiently expressing AhBWR15‐YFP and YFP (as a control) were inoculated with R. solanacearum , and the subsequent immune response was evaluated (Figure 4a). Constructs containing AhBWR15‐YFP and unfused YFP were overexpressed in peanut leaves through the infiltration of Agrobacterium tumefaciens cells carrying the pCambia1300‐AhBWR15‐YFP and pCambia1300‐YFP vectors, respectively. After a three‐day incubation period, R. solanacearum was introduced using a wounding method. Successful overexpression of AhBWR15 led to a reduction in cell death and lesion formation in peanut leaves at 3.0 dpi (Figure 4b–d). Additionally, defence‐related genes, including AhDef2.2, AhPYL6, and the peanut homologue of the hypersensitive response‐related 203 (AhHRS203), were significantly upregulated (Figure 4e).

Furthermore, the resistance mediated by AhBWR15 against R. solanacearum was assessed in tobacco leaves. Reverse transcription (RT)‐PCR analysis demonstrated that AhBWR15 was highly expressed in tobacco leaves, whereas it was undetectable in the control samples (Figure 4f). Tobacco leaves overexpressing AhBWR15 did not exhibit significant symptoms when compared to the mock control, in contrast to the YFP control leaves, which displayed extensive mesophyll cell damage and necrotic lesions (Figure 4g–h). Furthermore, trypan blue and diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining indicated a reduction in cell death and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation in tobacco leaves overexpressing AhBWR15‐YFP (Figure 4i). The expression levels of defence‐related genes, such as hypersensitive response‐related 203 (NbHRS203), non‐expressor of pathogenesis‐related 1 (NbNPR1), ethylene‐responsive 26 (NbEFE26), and nonrace‐specific disease resistance 1 (NbNDR1), were analysed in tobacco leaves using qRT‐PCR. These genes, including NbHRS203, NbNPR1, NbEFE26, and NbNDR1, were significantly upregulated in AhBWR15‐YFP‐overexpressing leaves under R. solanacearum infection compared to the control (Figure 4j). Overall, AhBWR15 enhances resistance to R. solanacearum in both tobacco and peanut by activating defence responses.

To further explore the role of AhBWR15 in BW resistance, A. rhizogenes ‐mediated hairy root transformation was performed. Approximately 60 healthy peanut plants with hairy roots overexpressing AhBWR15 were selected for BW resistance evaluation. The AhBWR15‐overexpressing plants (OE‐AhBWR15) and H107 wild‐type (WT) plants were inoculated with R. solanacearum (R. S), and the effects of BW infection were assessed at 28 dpi (Figure 5a). The expression levels of AhBWR15 in OE‐AhBWR15 plants were 7–8 times higher than those in WT plants, and BW infection significantly upregulated AhBWR15 expression (Figure 5b). A significant difference was observed in the disease index between OE‐AhBWR15 and WT plants over the course of the infection (Figure 5c). By 28 dpi, most OE‐AhBWR15 plants survived with a low disease index (approximately 0.2), whereas all WT plants exhibited wilting and mortality. Furthermore, the quantity of R. solanacearum in the roots of OE‐AhBWR15 plants was one‐quarter of that found in WT plants (Figure 5d–e). Collectively, these findings indicate that overexpression of AhBWR15 significantly enhances peanut resistance to R. solanacearum .

AhBWR15 Confers BW Resistance via Activating ABA‐Dependent Defence Response

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying AhBWR15‐mediated BW resistance in peanut plants, a comparative transcriptome analysis was conducted. Root tissues from OE‐AhBWR15 (abbreviated as OE) and WT plants, both infected with R. solanacearum and mock‐inoculated controls, were harvested at 3 dpi for total RNA extraction and RNA sequencing library preparation. Following the removal of low‐quality and unidentified invalid reads, a total of 596.1 million clean reads, equating to 89.4 GB, were obtained from an initial 651.1 million reads, or 97.7 GB (Table S9). The alignment rates of these reads varied between 94.39% and 96.99% (Table S10). In total, 3332, 23 743, 22 916, and 4099 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in the OE‐CK versus WT‐CK, WT‐R. S versus WT‐CK, OE‐R. S versus OE‐CK, and OE‐R. S versus WT‐R. S comparisons, respectively (Figure 5f).

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed that these DEGs were implicated in a variety of pathways, including transcription regulation, response to biotic and abiotic stresses, and protein ubiquitination (Figure S2a). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis further revealed that the interaction of multiple signalling pathways, notably plant‐pathogen interaction, plant hormone signal transduction, photosynthesis, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, plays a pivotal role in AhBWR15‐mediated BW resistance in peanut (Figure S2b). Notably, 574 DEGs that were both upregulated in the OE‐R. S versus OE‐CK and OE‐R. S versus WT‐R. S comparisons were identified as key genes involved in the resistance, predominantly enriched in defence response, transcription regulation, and photosynthesis, ABA‐signalling pathway, and cell wall organisation (Figure 5f). The expression levels of DEGs associated with the ABA‐signalling pathway, defence response, and photosynthesis were significantly elevated in OE‐AhBWR15 plants compared to other treatments (Figure 5g; Tables S11–S14). Endogenous ABA levels in the roots of WT and OE‐AhBWR15 plants after R. solanacearum infection were measured, and an increase in ABA in OE lines was detected (Figure S3a). Application of the ABA biosynthesis inhibitor sodium tungstate to H108 plants weakened their resistance to R. solanacearum (Figure S3b). These results indicated that AhBWR15 confers resistance via activating ABA‐dependent defence response.

Among the 574 DEGs, 38 genes related to photosynthesis were greatly upregulated (Figure 5g; Table S14), indicating an enhancement of photosynthetic activity mediated by AhBWR15 in peanut plants under R. solanacearum infection. Measurements of the maximum potential PSII efficiency (Fv/fm values) and chlorophyll content (SPAD values) further supported the maintaining role of AhBWR15 in photosynthesis during BW resistance (Figure 5h). Additionally, analysis of the phenylpropanoid metabolism pathway revealed significant induction of phenylalanine ammonia‐lyase (PAL), chalcone isomerase (CHI), cinnamoyl‐CoA reductase (CCR), and peroxidase (POD) in plants infected with bacterial wilt (Figure S4). Given the involvement of phenylpropanoid metabolism in lignin biosynthesis and cell wall organisation, phloroglucinol (PG) staining and suberin fluorol yellow (FY) staining were conducted to examine the peanut root cell wall (Figure 5i; Figure S5). PG staining of transverse sections of hairy roots demonstrated that R. solanacearum infection inhibited cell wall development in the roots of WT peanut plants, whereas OE‐AhBWR15 plants exhibited less severe root cell damage. Suberin FY staining demonstrated that the root cell walls of OE‐AhBWR15 plants accumulated more lignin. These findings collectively demonstrate that AhBWR15 confers peanut BW resistance by promoting ABA signalling‐dependent defence responses, as well as maintaining cell wall and high‐efficient photosynthesis.

Discussion

Peanut production is increasingly threatened by global warming and extreme climatic conditions, as well as various biotic and abiotic stresses. Consequently, it is imperative to develop new peanut varieties that exhibit stable high yield and durable disease resistance. Trade‐offs between yield and resistance are common in crops, but developing varieties with both high yield and robust resistance remains challenging. Several genes, such as OsDes1, OsWRKY36, and StTuA/B, have been identified to enhance resistance without compromising crop yield (Qiu et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2025; Qi et al. 2024). H108 is a newly developed peanut variety characterised by its high yield and high resistance to BW. It has been widely adopted in agricultural production and serves as an excellent genetic resource. Therefore, uncovering the secret of the perfect trade‐off in H108 is of great value. In this study, we successfully identified a novel BW resistance gene in H108. This gene, AhBWR15, encodes an LRR‐RLK, and it was proved to both enhance the BW resistance and maintain photosynthetic efficiency under R. solanacearum infection (Figure 5). Cloning and well characterisation of the AhBWR15 would greatly promote the breeding of peanut varieties with desired traits.

Genetic mapping of major BW‐resistant QTLs enables MAS and transferring of the durable resistance into elite peanut cultivars by developing functional molecular markers. Herein, a SNP presenting in the first exon of AhBWR15 was developed into a KASP marker ZH_SNP1. In addition, this mutation leads to alterations in the encoded amino acid sequence and tertiary structure of the AhBWR15. Notably, such structural modifications may potentially affect the protein's functional domains and its interaction with downstream signalling components, which could mechanistically explain the observed phenotypic variations between H108 and other peanut varieties. Our previously constructed pangenome of H108, along with other wild and cultivated peanuts, reveals substantial genomic variations facilitating identification of key genes related to yield and resistance (Zhao et al. 2025). Pangenome‐based analysis among the 258 natural germplasms revealed this novel mutation exclusively presents in the peanut variety H108 (Table S6). The establishment of the KASP molecular marker facilitates the screening of new peanut varieties for disease resistance, thereby advancing breeding applications.

LRR‐RLKs are known as typical R‐proteins, playing crucial role in plant immunity against virial, bacterial and fungal pathogens (Jones et al. 2024; Nabi et al. 2024; Ronald et al. 1992; Song et al. 1995; Ercoli et al. 2022). Transmembrane pattern recognition receptors, including LRR‐RLKs and receptor‐like proteins, recognising pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), thereby triggering a series of non‐specific pattern‐triggered immunity responses (Lu and Tsuda 2021; Zhang et al. 2023; Ercoli et al. 2022). In the present study, an LRR‐RLK gene AhBWR15 was demonstrated to confer the resistance of qBWR15 (Figures 2, 3). The AhBWR15 is plasmalemma‐localised, and is closely grouped with the rice leaf blight R‐protein Xa21 and Xa26, which mediates immune response against bacterial infection by directly binding pathogenic ligands via their large extracellular domains (Ronald et al. 1992; Song et al. 1995; Sun et al. 2004; Ercoli et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023). Interestingly, the BW resistance protein AhRLK1 in peanut, along with its paralog AH03G07130, exhibits closer genetic affiliations with the BW‐resistance protein CaLRR‐RLK1 in pepper (Mou et al. 2019), the leaf rust resistance protein LRK10 in wheat (Feuillet et al. 1997), the stem rust resistance protein RPG1 in barley (Mirlohi et al. 2008), and the resistance protein PTO against Pseudomonas syringae in tomato DC3000 (Martin et al. 1993). Furtherly, it indicated the novelty of AhBWR15 which is distinct with cloned BW resistance genes including AhRLK1.

Dozens of defence related genes, such as genes encoding tobacco mosaic virus resistance protein N (TMV‐N), innate immune receptor Resistance to P. syringae pv. maculicola 1 (RPM1), calcium‐binding protein CML24, and protein phosphatase 2C (PP2C) and the sucrose non‐fermenting‐1‐related kinase (SnRK), were greatly up‐regulated by AhBWR15 in transgenic peanut roots under R. solanacearum infection (Figure 5g; Table S12). Therefore, it was inferred that AhBWR15 contributes to BW resistance in H108 by activating downstream defence response. Signalling of phytohormones were identified to play key roles in peanut resistance to BW (Chen et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2017, 2019; Cao et al. 2024). AhBWR15 was significantly up‐regulated by exogenous SA, JA, and ABA (Figure 3f), and overexpression of AhBWR15 promotes the expression of those phytohormone‐signalling related genes (Figure 4e,j), suggesting the role of AhBWR15 in integrating SA, JA, and ABA signalling to mediate BW resistance. Additionally, ABA‐signalling pathway and cell wall organisation were involved in AhBWR15‐mediated BW resistance. ABA regulates plant defending against diverse pathogens by modulating the ABA receptor pyrabactin resistance 1‐like (PYL) and the core PP2C‐SnRK complex (Lim and Lee 2015; Huang et al. 2024). Secondary cell wall (SCW) acts as physical barriers against R. solanacearum colonisation, and deposition of cellulose and lignin was correlated with the resistance (Denancé et al. 2013; Peeters et al. 2013; Wang, Qi, et al. 2024), which is confirmed by the histological section analysis in this study (Figure 5i; Figure S4). It has been demonstrated the critical role of ABA in enhancing plant defence against various pathogens including R. solanacearum , by modulating biosynthesis of cellulose and lignin in SCW, and reinforcing cell wall integrity (Hernández‐Blanco et al. 2007; Ton et al. 2009; Feng et al. 2012; Liu et al. 2021; Molina et al. 2024; Lei et al. 2025). In addition, photosynthesis was also involved in the AhBWR15‐mediated BW resistance (Figure 5f–h), and OE‐AhBWR15 plants exhibited higher photosynthetic efficiency under R. solanacearum infection (Figures 5h). Recent studies have elucidated multiple molecular mechanisms by which ABA regulates photosynthetic pathways, particularly in protecting photosynthetic systems under stress conditions (e.g., low temperature, drought) (Gu et al. 2025). Our findings indicated that AhBWR15 might confer the BW resistance in H108 primarily by activating ABA‐mediated defence responses, maintaining photosynthesis, and promoting lignin biosynthesis and regulating other biological processes (Figure 5j).

In conclusion, we successfully identified a new important gene AhBWR15, conferring resistance to BW in peanuts through regulating the ABA signalling pathways. We also established a KASP marker to enhance the breeding of disease resistance in peanuts. This study highlights a new RLKs‐mediated disease resistance pathway for peanut improvement and provides a potential approach for future crops breeding.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Mapping Populations

The peanut cultivar H108 [registration number: GPD Peanut (2020) 410056] is highly resistant to BW, while H107 [registration number: GPD Peanut (2021) 410136] is susceptible (Zhao et al. 2022). Specifically, H108 was developed through hybridization using Youhua10 as the female parent and Baisha1016 as the male parent, whereas H107 was derived from Nongdahua103 through EMS‐induced mutagenesis and subsequent selection. To map the BW resistance gene in H108, F1, F2, and RIL populations derived from the cross of H108 (R) × H107 (S) were developed (Figure 1). Following self‐pollination, 432 F2 individuals were acquired from 50 F1 seeds. Through the single seed descent (SSD) method, the RILs (F5, 151 lines) were generated for fine mapping of qBWR15 (Figure 1). Peanut and tobacco seeds were obtained from the Henan Engineering Research Center for Peanut Functional Genomics and Molecular Breeding (at Henan Agricultural University).

R. solanacearum Inoculation and Resistance Assessment

Peanut seedlings of H107, H108 and F2 individuals were planted in plastic pots (10 × 10 cm) in a greenhouse at 28°C ± 2°C with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod and a relative humidity of approximately 60%. Three‐week‐old peanut seedlings were inoculated with the R. solanacearum strain 180731‐1 obtained from the Plant Protection Institute, Henan Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Briefly, the R. solanacearum isolate was propagated on the triphenyl tetrazole chloride (TTC) agar medium (5.0‐g/L peptone, 2.0‐g/L glucose, 0.5‐g/L 2,3,5‐triphenyl tetrazolium chloride, 0.1‐g/L casein hydrolysate and 15.0 g/L agar), and incubated at 28°C for 48 h. Virulent bacterial colonies were picked up with sterile toothpicks, and incubated in TTC broth at 28°C and 200 r/min for 48 h. The bacterial inoculum was prepared with an optical concentration of approximately 108 cfu/mL (OD600 = 0.5). Subsequently, peanut seedlings were inoculated with R. solanacearum using the leaf‐cutting method as Zhang et al. (2017) described. Control plants were inoculated with distilled H2O. The resistance levels of infected peanut plants were scored at 7 dpi as follows: 0, resistant (R, no wilting or wilting present in some infected leaves); 1, moderate resistant (MR, wilting present in the uncut leaves and stem of the inoculated branches); 2, moderate susceptible (MS, wilting present in the leaves and stems of uninoculated branches); 3, susceptible (S, wilting present at the whole plant or whole plant dead).

BSA‐Seq Analysis

Bulks of RB and SB were established by selecting 30 ER and 30 ES F2 individuals, respectively. Genomic DNA was abstracted from leaf tissues of H107, H108, RB, and SB plants using the RapidPure Plant DNA Isolation Mini Kit (DC104, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Equal quantities of DNA from RB and SB plants were mixed and broken for BSA‐seq using the Illumina HiSeq platform (Biomarker, Beijing China) as Zhao et al. (2023) described. Sliding window analysis of SNP‐index and Δ(SNP‐index) plots between the SB and RB was conducted, and the genomic region above the threshold (p < 0.01) was regarded as the candidate region of qBWR15.

Phenotyping and Genotyping of RILs

To narrow down the candidate region of qBWR15, genetic linkage analysis based on the phenotyping and genotyping of the RIL population (151 F5 lines) was performed (Figure 1). RILs were planted in a disease nursery in Zhengzhou city, Henan province, China, with three replications. Peanut plants were inoculated with R. solanacearum by watering with 100 mL prepared bacterial inoculum per plant, and the inoculation was repeated once again at 7 days post first inoculation. The resistance levels of RILs were assessed according to the survival rate (SR) at the harvesting stage: highly R (HR, SR > 90%), moderate R (MR, 65% < SR < 90%), S (35% < SR < 65%), and highly S (HS, SR < 35%).

The SR (%) was calculated with the following formula: SR%=No.of survival plantsNo.of total plants×100%

Totally, 20 InDels, 3 SSR and 2 SNP makers internal or nearby qBWR15 were developed for genotyping of RILs (Table S2). The targeted genomic reference sequence of qBWR15 was downloaded from the Peanutbase website, and SSRs were identified using the SSRHunter 1.3 software (Li and Wan 2005). SSR, InDel and SNP makers were developed by designing paired primers amplifying DNA fragments containing the SSRs and genomic variations between H107 and H108. The primer pairs were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 (Premier, Palo Alto, CA, USA), and were screened for polymorphism using PCR, polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and product sequencing. Finally, only seven polymorphic markers were used for genotyping of RILs (Tables S2–S3; Figure 2d).

Fine‐Mapping of qBWR15

The phenotypes serve as a morphological marker, of which RILs with a SR (%) > 65% were considered to be R, while those of SR (%) < 65% were S (Table S3). The phenotypic and genotypic data of RILs were used for genetic linkage analysis using the JoinMap 4.0 software (van Ooijen 2006). A minimum logarithm of likelihood ratio 3.0 was used to test the linkage. Candidate genes located in the flanking region of qBWR15 were predicted according to the reference peanut genome of Shitouqi. Subsequently, variations in the CDS of those candidate genes were analysed based on the genome re‐sequencing data. Genes harbouring nonsynonymous InDel and/or SNP mutations between the two parents were selected for further transcriptomic expression analysis. The public tissue‐specific expression data and our published transcriptome data were used for expression profiling of selected genes. Finally, only nonsynonymous‐mutated genes, which are highly expressed and up‐regulated by R. solanacearum , are considered as probable genes underlying the resistance of qBWR15. The nonsynonymous SNP in AH15G25210 was further verified by CDS comparison. Moreover, haplotype analysis of AH15G25210 was carried out by genotyping of 60 RILs using the ZH_SNP1 as a KASP marker (Table S6; Figure 3a).

Architecture, Phylogenetic, and Promoter Sequence Analysis

The domains and architectures of putative proteins were predicted using the online software SMART (http://smart.embl‐heidelberg.de) and InterProScan (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/InterProScan). Protein secondary and tertiary structures of AhBWR15 were predicted using the SMART and SWISS‐MODEL online software, respectively. Phylogenetic analysis of AhBWR15 and cloned LRR‐RLK type R‐proteins from various plant species was conducted with the full‐length deduced AA sequences (Table S7). A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the software MEGA 5.2 (https://www.megasoftware.net/) with a neighbour‐joining approach and the bootstrap values determined through 1000 random replications. The online PlantCARE software (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) was used to analyse the cis‐elements in the 2000‐bp promoter sequence of AhBWR15 (Lescot et al. 2002), and various cis‐elements associated with plant response to phytohormones, biotic and abiotic stresses were predicted.

qRT‐PCR

Expression patterns of AhBWR15 under R. solanacearum infection and phytohormone treatments were assessed using qRT‐PCR. Root tissues from H107 and H108 were collected at 0, 0.5, 1.0, and 7.0 dpi, while leaves from 3‐week‐old seedlings treated with ddH2O, 3‐mM SA, 100‐mM JA, and 50‐μM ABA were sampled at identical intervals. Total RNA was extracted using a FastPure Plant RNA Kit (RC411, Vazyme) and reverse‐transcribed with TransScript gDNA Removal & cDNA Synthesis Kit (AT311‐02, TransGen Biotech). Specific primers of AhBWR15 were designed for qRT‐PCR using the Primer Premier 5.0 software (Premier, Palo Alto, CA, USA; Table S8). Gene expression was quantified on a Bio‐Rad CFX96 system using PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix (AQ101‐01, TransGen Biotech), with AhActin7 as internal controls. Relative expression was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCT method using three replicates.

Construction of the Recombinant Plasmid

The full‐length fragment of AhBWR15 was amplified from cDNA of H108 with the primer pairs 1300‐AH15G25210‐F/R (Table S8), and the purified PCR products were cloned into the linearized pCambia1300‐YFP vector using the Seamless Assembly Cloning Kit (CloneSmarter, Houston, TX, USA). The recombinant construct was transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) and confirmed by sequencing. Finally, the pCambia1300‐AhBWR15‐YFP and empty control vectors were introduced into A. tumefaciens EHA105 for overexpression assays.

Transient Overexpression and Subcellular Localization Analysis of AhBWR15

Transient overexpression of AhBWR15 in tobacco and peanut leaves was achieved by agroinfiltration. Peanut seedlings were cultivated as described above. Wild‐type (WT) tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) plants were grown in a mixed substrate of vermiculite and nutrient soil (2:1, v/v) in controlled growth chambers maintained at 24°C, under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod with a light intensity of 15 000 lx and approximately 60% relative humidity. A. tumefaciens cells harbouring the pCambia1300‐AhBWR15‐YFP and pCambia1300‐ YFP (control) were propagated on Luria‐Bertani (LB) agar medium with appropriate antibiotics, respectively. Each colony was picked, and cultured in LB broth (containing 50‐μg/mL kanamycin) at 28°C for 18 h. Prepared cultures were pelleted by centrifugation at 2500 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in infiltration medium (100‐mM MgCl2, 200‐mM MES, 100‐μM AS) with an adjusted concentration (approximately OD600 = 0.5). Following incubation at 28°C for 2–3 h, the infiltration mediums were infiltrated into fully expanded tobacco and peanut leaves with needleless syringes, respectively. After being kept in darkness for 12–24 h, expressions status of AhBWR15‐YFP fusion and free YFP in infiltrated plants were determined through fluorescence observation, RT‐PCR and qRT‐PCR.

For subcellular localization analysis of AhBWR15, equal volume of infiltration mediums of the plasma membrane marker expressing the AtPIP2a‐RFP fusion were mixed with that of pCambia1300‐AhBWR15‐YFP and pCambia1300‐YFP (control). Therefore, the plasmembrane localised AtPIP2a‐RFP fusion was transiently co‐expressed with AhBWR15‐YFP fusion and free YFP in tobacco leaves by agroinfiltration, respectively. Infiltrated tobacco leaves were incubated at 28°C in darkness overnight. Fluorescence signals from the protein fusions were visualised and captured using an LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss Inc., Jena, Germany), with 3–5 representative images randomly acquired for each sample. Protoplasts isolated from tobacco leaves above mentioned were used for further subcellular localization analysis. Tobacco leaves were cut into 0.5–1.0 mm strips and incubated in freshly prepared enzyme solution as described by Yoo et al. (2007). After digestion in darkness at 28°C with gentle shaking (30 rpm) for 5–6 h, released protoplasts were purified and adjusted to an appropriate density. The fluorescence was observed using an LSM710 confocal laser scanning microscope.

Functional Identification of AhBWR15 Using the Transient Expression System

The transient expression system of tobacco and peanut leaves (Zhao et al. 2022), were employed for functional identification of AhBWR15 in resistance against R. solanacearum . The tobacco and peanut leaves transiently expressing AhBWR15‐YFP and YFP (control) was inoculated R. solanacearum with infiltration and leaf‐cutting methods, respectively. The leaf tissues were collected at 3 dpi, and the expression of AhBWR15 was detected by RT‐PCR. R. solanacearum infection leads to cell death and necrotic lesions on the leaves which would be reduced by the overexpression of R‐gene. Hence, the lesion areas on the peanut and tobacco leaves were measured with the digital image analysis program of ImageJ (153‐win‐java8). Meanwhile, trypan blue and DAB staining were conducted for cell death visualisation and ROS detection, respectively. Additionally, the expression levels of defence‐related genes in tobacco (including the NbHRS203, NbNPR1, NbEFE26 and NbNDR1) and peanut (including the AhEFE26, AhDef2.2, AhPYL6 and AhHRS203) were investigated by qRT‐PCR (Cao et al. 2024; Zhao et al. 2022). Primers of those defence‐related genes were designed using the Primer Premier 5.0 software (Table S8), and qRT‐PCR was performed as mentioned above. The resistance level of the R‐gene to BW is evaluated by considering the degree of cell death and the size of necrosis areas, as well as the regulation of R‐gene to the expression of the defence‐related genes.

Trypan Blue and DAB Staining

For cell death visualisation, leaves were boiled in Trypan Blue staining solution (Solarbio Life Sciences Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) for 2 min, cooled, and stained at 28°C overnight. Stained leaves were destained in chloral hydrate solution (1.25 g/mL) with shaking (50 rpm) at 25°C. The destaining solution was replaced every 3 h until leaves were fully cleared. For hydrogen peroxide detection, leaves were vacuum‐infiltrated with DAB staining solution [50 mg DAB, 90 mL sterile H2O, 10 mL 10 × phosphate‐buffered saline (Solarbio), 25 μL Tween 20 (Solarbio), pH 3.0] at 0.8 MPa for 2 min, incubated in darkness at 28°C overnight, and decolorized in 90% ethanol until chlorophyll was removed. Cell death and ROS accumulation were observed under a stereomicroscope.

A. rhizogenes ‐Mediated Hairy Root Transformation

For further functional characterisation, AhBWR15 was also overexpressed in peanut through A. rhizogenes ‐mediated hairy root transformation following the protocol established by Kereszt et al. (2007) with minor modification. The recombinant plasmid pCambia1300‐AhBWR15‐YFP was transformed into the A. rhizogenes strain K599 for transgenic hairy root induction. Subsequently, 5‐days‐old germinated peanut seedlings incubated in wet vermiculite were inoculated with prepared A. rhizogenes cells by piercing the central part of the hypocotyl using a sterile needle with the bacteria paste on the tip. Kep the infected plants in wet vermiculite and maintain a high humidity condition in 12 h light/12 h dark at 28°C/25°C for 2–3 weeks. Once the hairy roots are long enough to support growth (approximately 5–10 cm), the peanut seedlings were transferred into new pots after removing primary roots.

Healthy seedlings with coincident hairy roots were selected for BW resistance assessment, and H107 seedlings served as wild type (WT) control. The OE‐AhBWR15 and WT plants were inoculated with R. solanacearum using a root‐tip cutting method as Zhao et al. (2022) described. Root tissues of OE‐AhBWR15 and WT plants were collected at 1.0 dpi, and the expression levels of AhBWR15 were examined by qRT‐PCR. The resistance levels of OE‐AhBWR15 and WT plants were estimated by calculating the disease indexes at 0, 7, 14, and 28 dpi. Disease indexe=0×No.ofRplants+1×No.ofMRplants+2×No.ofMSplants+3×No.ofSplants4×TotalNo.of plants

Moreover, R. solanacearum amount in the roots of OE‐AhBWR15 and WT plants was determined as Zhao et al. (2024) described.

RNA‐Seq

Roots of OE‐AhBWR15 and WT plants under control and R. solanacearum infection were collected at 1.0 dpi for RNA‐seq. Total RNA was extracted, and cDNA libraries were constructed with the TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina). RNA‐seq was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Raw reads were filtered and aligned to the peanut reference genome ( Arachis hypogaea cv. Shitouqi) using the TopHat2 software. Transcript abundance was quantified using the FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) metric. Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2, applying stringent thresholds (|log2 fold change| > 1 and FDR‐adjusted p‐value < 0.05) for identifying significant DEGs. Functional annotation was conducted through GO analysis using topGO and KEGG pathway analysis using clusterProfiler. Data visualisation was implemented in R‐4.0.2 and TBtools.

Determinations of ABA Involvement, Photosynthesis Efficiency, and Chl Content

To determine involvements of ABA in AhBWR15‐mediated BW resistance in peanuts, endogenous ABA levels in the roots of WT and OE‐AhBWR15 plants after R. solanacearum infection were measured using a commercially available enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Shanghai Enzyme‐linked Biotechnology Co. Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The results were expressed as mg/g fresh weight (FW). Each assay included three technical replicates. Additionally, exogenous ABA (50 μM) and ABA biosynthesis inhibitor (sodium tungstate, 1 mM) were applied to three‐week‐old H108 plants. Control plants were treated with an equal volume of water. The H108 seedlings were inoculated with R. solanacearum by hydroponic root application as described above at 24 h post treatment. Phenotypes of each treatment group were observed and recorded at 7 dpi.

Photosynthetic performance and Chl content in newly emerged tobacco leaves were assessed following the protocol established by Ren et al. (2025). The maximal quantum yield of PSII photochemistry (Fv/fm) was determined using a FluorCam7 Chl fluorescence imaging system (Photon Systems Instruments, USA), while relative Chl content was quantified as SPAD values using a SPAD‐502 Chl meter (Minolta, Japan).

PG and Suberin Staining

The method of Ren et al. (2025) by reference, lignin accumulation in peanut root cell walls was assessed by phloroglucinol (PG) staining at 21 days post‐treatment (dpt). Root tips were fixed in FAA (50% ethanol, 10% formaldehyde, 5% acetic acid) overnight, then dehydrated through an ethanol series, paraffin‐embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm (Microm HM 355 S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After dewaxing and washing, sections were stained with 1% PG‐HCl (w/v) for 5 min. Lignin deposition was visualised and imaged using an optical microscope (DM4000B, Leica, Germany).

Suberin lamellae accumulation in peanut roots was visualised using FY088 staining. Root sections collected at 3 dpt were processed as previously described, then stained with 0.01% FY088 in lactic acid (70°C, 5 min, dark). After counterstaining with 0.5% aniline blue (30 min, RT) and ddH2O washing, sections were mounted for observation. Suberin lamellae (yellow fluorescence) were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss Inc., Jena, Germany), with random image acquisition from three biological replicates.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 18.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Treatment differences were assessed by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. The data are displayed as the average of three biological replicates, with standard deviation (SD) represented by error bars. Duncan's multiple comparison test was performed post‐ANOVA, with significant differences (p < 0.05) denoted by lowercase letters. Boxplots and bar graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0.
