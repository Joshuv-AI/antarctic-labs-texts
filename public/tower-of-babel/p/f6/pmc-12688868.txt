Unusual presentation of dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans of the thigh: a case report
Authors: Unza Shaikh, Saim Mahmood Khan, Zaara Zahid, Mahmood Ahmad Khan, Muhammad Muheet ul Haque, Muhammad Saad Allauddin, Muhammad Nouman Javed, Zemichael Getu Alemayehu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Dermal fibroblasts can develop into dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP), a rare low-grade sarcoma that grows slowly, is locally invasive, and has a high recurrence rate. Usually, it affects the proximal extremities or trunk. In contrast to its more typical occurrence on the trunk and proximal upper limbs, lateral thigh DFSP is highly unusual and may resemble benign entities such as lipomas, dermatofibromas, or pigmented nevi. Since DFSP on the lateral thigh is rare, diagnosis and treatment may be delayed because it could be confused with benign lesions. By highlighting this rare thigh presentation in a young adult, we aim to raise clinical awareness of DFSP’s varied manifestations and underscore the value of early histopathologic evaluation. This could ultimately reinforce the need for wide excision to minimize recurrence.Case presentation:Initially believed to be a mole, a 24-year-old Pakistani woman appeared with a pigmented lesion on her right lateral thigh that was gradually growing. It grew in size over the course of 3.5 years with only minor discomfort. An examination showed a pigmented lesion that was hard and fixed. In line with DFSP, an excisional biopsy revealed a spindle cell tumor with a storiform morphology and diffuse CD34 positivity. Due to the narrow margins, a broad local excision with 2 cm margins was made. Postoperative recovery was complicated by a superficial wound infection, which resolved with antibiotics. Metastasis was not detected by follow-up imaging. The patient was still functionally intact and free of recurrences at 3 months.Clinical discussion:This example demonstrates the difficulties in diagnosing DFSP in unusual settings. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry are necessary for an accurate diagnosis. The secret to lowering recurrence is surgical excision with distinct margins. Long-term monitoring is crucial.Conclusion:Even in uncommon locations like the thigh, DFSP should be considered in cases of chronic, atypical skin lesions. For the best results, interdisciplinary care, extensive excision, and early diagnosis are essential.

Introduction

Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a rare, low-to-intermediate grade cutaneous sarcoma that develops from fibroblasts of the dermis. It is characterized by slow but infiltrative growth and a high tendency for local recurrence[1]. DFSP generally presents as a hard nodule or plaque, which converts into ulcerative or protuberant features. Histologically, it has a storiform spindle-cell pattern with diffuse CD34 positivity[2]. It is caused by a t(17;22)(q22;q13) translocation that forms a COL1A1–platelet-derived growth factor subunit B (PDGFB) fusion gene, making PDGFB signaling constant and thus causing uncontrolled tumor growth[1]. DFSP comprises 1%–6% of all soft tissue sarcomas and has an annual incidence of 1 in 1 million, while reported cases of DFSP on the inguinal or lateral thigh are rare[1,3]. In such an atypical inguinal location, the lesion may be mistaken for a benign tumor, such as lipomas, hernias, or dermatofibromas, which may delay the diagnosis and increase surgical complexity[4]. Reports of giant DFSP variants underscore the challenges of achieving wide excision and reconstruction in anatomically constrained areas[3,5]. The present clinical case describes DFSP in a 24-year-old female patient on the lateral aspect of the thigh, detailing its protracted growth, comprehensive diagnostic work-up, including imaging and core biopsy, and the multidisciplinary surgical strategy employed to obtain clear margins while optimizing functional and aesthetic outcomes. Case presentation

Case history

A 24-year-old unmarried Pakistani female with no known comorbidities presented with a brown, smooth-surfaced mole on the lateral aspect of her right mid-thigh, first noticed approximately 3.5 years prior. The lesion remained asymptomatic and stable in size, about the size of a lentil, until 1 year before presentation, when the patient observed the development of two to three foci of dark (black) pigmentation and gradual enlargement to nearly 1 cm in diameter. She reported occasional dull aching pain on direct compression of the lesion. The discomfort was mild, non-radiating, self-resolving, and did not require any analgesics. During the same interval, a second small brown mole appeared nearby.

The patient denied any history of inguinal swelling or systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or night sweats. Her past medical history was positive only for recurrent tonsillitis, which was treated intermittently with antibiotics. She had no known drug allergies, did not smoke or consume alcohol, and maintained a healthy lifestyle. Family history was positive for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hyperthyroidism, acid peptic disease, and hypercholesterolemia. Her mother also underwent local excision of a similar pigmented lesion on the left eyebrow. However, no pathology records were available.

Examination

On physical examination, a solitary, irregularly shaped, brown–black pigmented lesion was found measuring approximately 1 × 1 cm on the lateral aspect of the right thigh. It was located 17 cm proximal to the lateral femoral condyle (Fig. 1). The lesion showed irregular borders and an uneven surface but retained a smooth texture. It was firm in consistency, non-tender, and fixed to the underlying subcutaneous tissue. Additionally, a second benign-appearing, circular, brown lesion was noted 30 cm proximal to the lateral femoral condyle (Fig. 2). The remainder of the skin was normal, with no evidence of distal neuromuscular or neurovascular impairment. Methods (differential diagnosis, investigation, and treatment)

Following baseline laboratory investigations and obtaining informed consent from the patient, the primary lesion was excised under local anesthesia (1% Xylocaine with Adrenaline) with a 1 cm clinical margin, and the defect was primarily closed (Fig. 3). Histopathological examination of the excisional biopsy revealed hyperplastic epidermis (Supplemental Digital Content, available at, http://links.lww.com/MS9/A970). It was, overlying an ill-circumscribed spindle-cell proliferation in the dermis and arranged in a storiform (cartwheel) pattern without significant cellular atypia or mitotic activity. Further, immunohistochemical staining demonstrated a strong CD34 positivity in the spindle cells, while HMB-45 appeared negative with no fungal elements identified. Moreover, the margin assessment showed that the tumor was 0.4 cm from all peripheral margins and 0.1 cm from the deep margins.Figure 3.Primary lesion excised under local anesthesia (1% Xylocaine with Adrenaline) with 1 cm margin; defect closed primarily.

Based on the spindle-cell morphology, storiform architecture, and CD34 positivity, a diagnosis of DFSP was confirmed. Due to the proximity of the deep margins, ongoing follow-up and consideration of wider re-excision or adjunctive therapy were recommended.

Outcome and follow-up

Following a multidisciplinary discussion with an oncologist, a plastic surgeon, and a radiation oncology team, the patient underwent a wide local removal of the DFSP with 2 cm peripheral margins down to the deep fascia, and a 1 cm margin excision of the adjacent proximal brown lesion. The defects were closed primarily under a strict aseptic technique. Further, the staging chest CT scan showed no evidence of pulmonary metastases.

Postoperatively, the patient developed a superficial wound infection, which was managed based on the culture and sensitivity results. The infection resolved completely within 5 weeks. The patient maintained full function of the affected limb without any complications or recurrence during the 3-month observation period.

Discussion

DFSP is a rare cancer of soft tissue that mainly arises on the chest and limbs, often presenting as a slow-growing, bluish discoloration plaque[1]. The abnormal tissue generally starts in the dermis but also has a proclivity for penetrating the deep tissue[7]. Typical DFSP sites include the trunk (≈50% of cases), followed by the proximal extremities and head/neck region[8]. These sites often present with indurated plaques or nodules that raise early clinical suspicion. In contrast, our patient’s lateral thigh lesion closely resembled a benign mole both in size and pigmentation, contributing to diagnostic delay. The thigh, being a less common site, makes DFSP more likely to be mistaken for benign skin tumors (e.g. dermatofibroma, nevus, lipoma), especially in young adults. This explains why DFSP at this location often mimics harmless skin lesions and underscores the need for biopsy of persistent or atypical lesions.

The clinical presentation changes from patch-like or small-sized nodules to unequal masses. DFSP is commonly a slow-growing, firm, and multiple-nodular lesion[8]. Often appearing as an indurated plaque with raised violet to red-brown nodules. It usually appears in the third to fifth decade of life. The colored form of DFSP (Bednar tumor) and the fibrosarcomatous form account for less than 5% up to 15%, respectively. The tumor can extend up to many centimeters in diameter and may become infected or painful. DFSP is most commonly found on the chest (50%), limbs (30%), and head and neck (15%). The shoulder and pelvic regions are also frequently involved[9].

Microscopically, DFSP is characterized by an increase of spindle-shaped cells organized in a storiform or whorled design. These cells are often associated with fibrous septa and adipose tissue, producing a characteristic “honeycomb” appearance, which can sometimes complicate diagnosis[10]. In our case, the decision to proceed with wide excision was not taken in isolation but was the outcome of a multidisciplinary tumor board discussion involving surgical oncology, plastic surgery, and radiation oncology. The team considered the narrow margins of the initial excision, the potential morbidity of a wider excision in the thigh region, and the need to balance oncological clearance with limb function and cosmetic outcomes. Ultimately, a 2 cm margin excision down to the deep fascia was selected, reflecting consensus-based management aimed at achieving local control while preserving mobility and aesthetics. Ninety percent of the individuals with DFSP also include those patients with fibrosarcomatous form, having the translocation of chromosomes of t (17; 22) (q22; q13). A substitute translocation that involves the PDGFB gene found on chromosome 22 may exist in patients without t (17;22) (q22;q13) translocation. In specific events, the advancement of DFSP to DFSP-FP is linked with genetic gain that affects the PDGFB/COL1A1 chimeric gene, although this transformation is variable[1].

Clinicians often face difficulties in differentiating DFSP from other soft tissue cancers, like dermatofibroma, fibrosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, undifferentiated or unclassified soft tissue sarcoma, or atypical fibroxanthoma[1]. An incisional or excisional skin biopsy should be made for diagnosis. Differential diagnoses involve cutaneous melanoma, dermatofibroma, dermatologic indications of metastatic carcinomas, keloid and hypertrophic scars, and morphea. This is a poorly confined cancer; a surgical technique named Mohs micrographic surgery ensures complete histopathologic margin control with lower recurrence rates. Mostly, 2–5 cm wide-ranging localized removal is the commonly used treatment option. Regional lymph node dissection is not recommended given the lower lymphatic metastatic rates[11].

The following risk factors show worse outcomes in patients: large tumors, local recurrence, myxoid type, expression of cyclin D1, and deep invasiveness. Meta-analysis performed with eight observational studies manifested excision with a margin less than 3 cm, which yielded a poor prognosis as compared to previous studies[12].

Due to the very high recurrence rate, patients with DFSP are required to follow a strict follow-up regimen. Recurrence is particularly common in tumors located in the head and neck region. Mohs technique or a 3D margin assessment should be used to assess the margins. Long-term monitoring is required to document the progress of the disease. The primary site should be assessed every 3–6 months to evaluate the progress of the disease. This surveillance is carried out every 6–12 months. If the duration of disease-free survival exceeds 3 years, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines recommend performing a biopsy every 6–12 months. According to European norms, a clinical examination should be performed every 6 months for the first 5 years and once a year for the next 10 years. Imaging should be reserved for DFSP with sarcomatous alterations or DFSP that occurs frequently. In patients with sarcomatous alteration, recurrence, or positive margins, screening for metastases is essential[13].

All patients undergoing reconstruction should go through a rigorous screening process in which the physician can play a vital role by informing parents about the importance of follow-up and promptly reporting any new lesions. Healthcare professionals who treat patients with operated tumors can educate patients about postoperative care following reconstruction using skin flaps or skin grafting, as well as provide cancer guidelines. Furthermore, a multidisciplinary approach comprising the radiation oncologist, plastic surgeon, and nurse is necessary for the efficient management of these cases[12].

Conclusion

DFSP most commonly arises on the trunk, but its occurrence on the lateral thigh represents an unusual presentation that can mimic benign pigmented or soft tissue lesions, often delaying diagnosis. Atypical presentations require careful correlation of clinical, radiologic, and histopathologic information to distinguish DFSP from benign soft tissue tumors. Conclusive treatment with broad local excision, with adequate surgical margins to ensure local disease control and tissue preservation. As demonstrated in this case, multidisciplinary decision-making is crucial to optimize both oncological safety and functional preservation. Reconstruction with local tissue promotes healing of the wounds and restores limb function. Ongoing monitoring is crucial due to the possibility of local recurrence.
