Decitabine-induced DNA methylation remodeling reveals targetable biological processes in gilthead seabream pituitary and liver explants
Authors: E Perera, C Navarro-Guillén, J Román-Padilla, R Huesa-Cerdán, J A Hidalgo-Perez, JA Martos-Sitcha, G Martínez-Rodríguez, Daniel Alpízar-Pedraza, Jorge M O Fernandes, Javier A Rodriguez-Casariego
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEpigenetic manipulations have the potential to improve traits in farmed fish. To explore this opportunity in the gilthead seabream (Sparus aurata), the catalytic domains of five DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) were predicted by homology modeling, and their interaction with the inhibitor 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine (DAC) was assessed by docking, revealing that the inhibitor can bind all DNMTs with similar energy. Then, pituitary and liver explants from gilthead seabream were exposed to DAC for 24 h, and changes in genome-wide DNA methylation (RRBS) and gene expression (RNA-seq) were assessed. In the liver, functional enrichment revealed upregulation of ribosome biogenesis and protein synthesis, while mitochondrial functioning, genome stability, and DNA and amino acid metabolism were downregulated. Exposed pituitaries displayed upregulation of ribosomal biogenesis and protein synthesis, alongside mitochondrial functioning and genome stability. Nucleotide-level methylomes were obtained for the first time in this species, with hypomethylated sites observed in 3’UTRs, promoter regions, and introns of highly expressed genes across both tissues. A higher level of DNA methylation at exons was found in highly expressed genes in the liver. The seabream pituitary was more permissive to DNA methylation remodeling than the liver. Functional Epigenetic Module analysis revealed seven interactome hotspots in liver and four in pituitary, mostly related to protein trafficking and signal transduction in the liver, and mitochondrial functioning in the pituitary, indicating that these functions can potentially be targeted by epigenetic interventions in seabream. The data resources generated in our study may be used to explore novel avenues to boost seabream performance and welfare.KEYWORDS: DNA methylation, fish, liver, pituitary, tissue explant, epigenetics, Sparus aurata

Introduction

Different successful strategies have been followed to advance fish farming, such as genetic selection [1], and improvements in feed formulation and feeding practices [2] considering growth performance, immunity, and welfare. Moreover, several studies in fish have been made on the programming of productive traits by different environmental interventions, including nutritional ones. There are now documented programming effects on fish growth, survival, brain development, and nutrient metabolism [3], and references therein]. While these programming effects are thought to rely, at least in part, on epigenetic mechanisms, such mechanisms have only been investigated in a limited number of fish species despite compelling evidence on their value to improve fish production [4] as occurs in livestock [5,6]. Indeed, fish programming studies have adopted a top-down approach, focusing on specific traits such as carbohydrate utilization [7,8], use of alternative proteins/oils [9–15], stress resistance [16–18], muscle growth [19,20], tolerance to mild hypoxia [21], swimming performance [22], as well as in behavior and microbiota composition [18]. However, little is known about the influence of the epigenome in these processes. Currently, different studies focused on the effect of environmental interventions on fish epigenomes have been performed in Atlantic salmon ([4,16,17,23–27], rainbow trout [28], European seabass [29], and zebrafish [30,31], while little effort has been placed in understanding how epigenetic regulation of gene expression can be manipulated to improve phenotypes in other important farmed fish. This knowledge gap is critical as identifying which biological processes are amenable to epigenetic modulation is essential for designing effective interventions. The gilthead seabream (Sparus aurata), one of the cornerstones of the European aquaculture, is among the most extensively studied fish species from a programming perspective [11–13,18,21,22,32–34. Curiously, it remains the only major farmed fish species in Europe for which no single-base resolution data of the epigenome is currently available.

Among epigenetic mechanisms, DNA methylation is the most studied in fish [35,36], and particularly in farmed species [4,37]. In seabream, DNA methylation has been linked to important biological processes [13,38], and recent methyl-binding domain sequencing (MBD-seq) studies revealed the relationship between DNA methylation and genetic background [39] and biological age [40] in this species. However, no detailed genomic screening with the potential to identify processes susceptible to epigenetic reprogramming by interventions is available.

In this regard, given the key roles of pituitary and liver tissue in productive and well-being traits such as behavior, appetite, growth, stress response, osmoregulation, and metabolism, among others, we previously validated the use of explants of these tissues for epigenetic studies [41]. Using a global quantitative approach, we demonstrated that the explants responded differently to the DNA methylation inhibitor 5-aza-2′ -deoxycytidine (DAC). Pituitary was found to be more responsive than liver, exhibiting changes in gene expression and enzyme activity of endogenous epigenetic regulators such as DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) and ten-eleven translocation proteins (TETs) [41]. In the present study we exposed explants of pituitary and liver of seabream to DAC and analyzed changes in nucleotide-specific DNA methylation using Reduced Representation Bisulfite Sequencing (RRBS) and concurrent changes in gene expression with RNA-seq. This study provides the first single-base resolution methylomes of the gilthead seabream, and information on the role of DNA methylation in the regulation of gene expression in these tissues. We also make available a list of biological processes that are putatively susceptible to environmental manipulation through DNA methylation remodeling, given their responsiveness to the inhibitor. The data resources generated here may be further used to explore novel avenues to boost performance and welfare in farmed fish, either through the continuous presence of epigenome remodeling factors (e.g., specific nutrients) or through the programming of long-term phenotypes by short-term exposures.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement: All procedures involving animals complied with the Guidelines of the Spanish (RD53/2013) and European Union Council (2010/63/EU) for the use and experimentation of laboratory animals, and were reviewed and approved by the Spanish National Research Council (CSIC) Bioethical Committee and that of the Autonomous Andalusian Government (reference number 24/10/2023/092). We have adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines for this study.

Comparative modelling of the catalytic domain of DNA methyltransferases from S. aurata: DNA methyltransferases from S. aurata protein sequences were collected from the Gene Database in the GenBank (https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/) [42]. In the S. aurata genome, five DNMT genes were found: dnmt1 (gene id: 115,575,267) in chromosome 23, dnmt3a (gene id: 115,597,760) in chr16, dnmt3ba (gene id: 115,585,308) in chr 7, dnmt3ba (gene id: 115,574,210) in chr 22, and dnmt3bb (gene id: 115,585,491) in chr 7. The catalytic domain of each sequence was identified using the Conserved Domain Database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd/). The 3D structure prediction was performed through comparative modelling using the protein structure prediction server Robetta (https://robetta.bakerlab.org/). The template selection follows two criteria, 1) a high score obtained in the blastp procedure [43] against the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/), and 2) the availability of the 3D structure in an active conformation. The human DNMT1 (PDBID: 7XI9), DNMT3a (PDBID: 5YX2), and DNMT3b (PDBID: 6KDA) were used as templates. As input, a file containing a sequence alignment between the sequence of interest and the selected template, and the spatial coordinates of the template structure were employed.

Docking: Docking was performed employing an iterated Monte-Carlo search, combined with a BFGS gradient-based optimizer, implemented in the program AutoDock Vina v1.2.0 [44] and [45]. As ligand, the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine (decitabine) was employed. Its structure was retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) with code 451,668. Both ligand and catalytic domains were prepared employing the software AutoDock Tools v1.5.6 [46]. All hydrogen atoms were added to the molecules, Gasteiger charges were calculated, nonpolar hydrogens were removed, and the atom type AD4 was assigned to each atom. The conformational search space was defined using a cubic box of dimensions 25 Å × 25 Å × 25 Å and centered at coordinates x: 113.117, y: 105.436, z: 93.499, encompassing the catalytic site. Molecular docking simulations were carried out by keeping the catalytic domain residues rigid, while all rotatable angles of the ligand were considered flexible. The employed docking parameters were exhaustiveness = 50, energy_range = 4 kcal, and num_modes = 20. The best result was selected by comparing with the structure of human DNMT1 in complex with ubiquitinated H3 and hemimethylated DNA analog obtained by Cryo-EM (IDPDB: 7XI9).

Fish and housing conditions: Gilthead seabream (body mass 37 ± 0.8 g; mean ± sem) was provided by Fabatmar Mareny, S.L. (Spain), and transferred to the ICMAN-CSIC experimental facility (REGA ES110280000311). Fish were kept in aerated 120 L tanks in an open system under natural conditions of photoperiod and temperature (19–22°C), salinity of 34 ppt, oxygen level above 90%, pH 8.0 ± 0.1, and NH4 < 0.25 mg L−1. They were fed by hand to apparent satiety twice a day with a commercial diet (Perla MP, Skretting, Burgos, Spain).

Explant culture: Fish were anesthetized first for 1 min and then euthanized with 2-phenoxyethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Germany) at 300 ppm and 600 ppm, respectively. Finally, their spinal cord was sectioned. Culture of pituitaries (entire gland) and liver explants (1–2 mm3) was performed as extensively detailed before [41] and exposed during 24 h to 15 µM decitabine (5-Aza-2’-deoxycytidine; DAC) dissolved in DMSO, or 0.2% DMSO as the control. Tissues were collected in DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research). Liver samples consisted of explants from seven individual fish (n = 7) while pituitary samples were five pools (n = 5), each containing five glands from different individuals.

RNA and DNA extraction: Whole tissues were sent to Zymo Research (Irvine, CA) for RNA-seq and RRBS analyses. The Quick-DNA/RNA Plus Kit Microprep Kit was used for the simultaneous extraction of RNA and DNA. RNA quality was checked in an Agilent TapeStation.

Library preparation and sequencing: RNA-seq libraries were prepared from total RNA using the Zymo-Seq RiboFree Total RNA Library Prep Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Successful library construction was confirmed with Agilent’s D1000 ScreenTape Assay on TapeStation. RNA-Seq libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq to a sequencing depth of at least 30 million read pairs (150 bp paired-end sequencing) per sample.

For RRBS 200 ng of genomic DNA was digested with 30 units of MspI (NEB). Fragments were ligated to pre-annealed adapters containing 5’-methyl-cytosine instead of cytosine according to Illumina’s specified guidelines. Adaptor-ligated fragments ≥50 bp in size were recovered using the DNA Clean & Concentrator™-5. The fragments were then bisulfite-treated using the EZ DNA Methylation-Lightning™ Kit. Preparative-scale PCR was performed, and the resulting products were purified with DNA Clean & Concentrator™-5 for sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150 bp PE reads).

Bioinformatic and statistical analyses: All statistical analyses were completed in R (v4.4.3 [47]) with RStudio (v2025.05.0 + 496; R Studio Team 2025) unless stated otherwise.

The Zymo Research RNA-Seq pipeline was used with some modifications to align and quantify S. aurata transcriptome. Briefly, quality control of raw reads was carried out using FastQC (v0.12.0 [48], available at http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc). Adapter and low-quality sequences were trimmed from raw reads using Trim Galore!(v0.6.10 [49]). Trimmed reads were aligned to the reference genome assembly fSpaAur1.1 (GCF_900880675.1) using STAR (v2.6.1d [50]) with nf-core/RNAseq pipeline parameters [51]. BAM file filtering and indexing were carried out using SAMtools (v1.22.1 [52]). RNAseq library quality control was implemented using RSeQC (v5.0.4 [53], and QualiMap (v2.3 [54]). Duplicate reads were marked using Picard tools (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). Library complexity was estimated using Preseq (v2.0 [55]). Duplication rate quality control was performed using dupRadar (v3.21 [56]). Reads overlapping with exons were assigned to genes using featureCounts [57]. Count data was filtered to exclude genes with a mean count < 1 and normalized using the variance stabilizing transformation implemented in DESEQ2 (v1.48.1 [58]). Transcriptional discrimination across treatments was evaluated and visualized through Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC) [59–61], based on Manhattan distances and a number of retained PCAs defined through cross-validation using the Xval.dapc function in R [62]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using a generalized linear model approach implemented in DESEQ2 (Wald approach). Genes with FDR ≤0.1 were considered differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analysis was achieved using rank-based gene ontology analysis with adaptive clustering based on the Mann-Whitney U test (GO-MWU [63]) and a p-value ≤0.05.

RRBS data bioinformatic analysis: Sequence reads from bisulfite-treated RRBS libraries were identified using standard Illumina base calling software and resulting FASTQ files were adapter-timmed, filled-in nucleotides, and quality-trimmed using TrimGalore! (v0.6.10 [49]). FastQC (v0.12.0 [48]) was used to assess the effect of trimming and overall quality distributions of the data. Alignment to reference genome assembly fSpaAur1.1 (GCF_900880675.1) was performed using Bismark (v0.22.3 [64]). Methylation level at each CpG site was called using MethylDackel (https://github.com/dpryan79/MethylDackel) and contrasted with beta-value calculation using the R package methylKit (v1.34.0 [65]). DMCs, DMGs, and DMPr were identified using a Chisq-test with basic overdispersion correction (MN [66]) implemented in methylKit. DMRs were evaluated with DSS (v2.56 [67]), considering a minimum region length of 50bp, a minimum of 3 CpG sites per region, percentage of CpG sites in the region with significant p-values greater than 50%, delta of 0.1, and a p.value < 0.05. DNA methylation was summarized and evaluated for differential methylation at nucleotide (DMCs), region (DMRs), gene body (DMGs), and promoter levels (2 kb; DMPr). Genome features were defined for detailed analyses as described in Saito et al. [24]. Briefly, exons and introns were merged as gene body (GB), proximal promoter was defined to 250 bp upstream TSS (P250), promoter was 250bp-1Kb upstream TSS, distal promoter was 1–6 kb upstream TSS, and the remaining regions were broadly categorized as intergenic. Additionally, in this work, we considered lncRNA, transposable elements and 3’UTR, characterized following available annotation for the species or constructing the track as in [68]. DNA methylation across gene-related features was characterized in the context of expression level, distance from TSS and interaction with predicted CpG islands (obtained from https://genome.ucsc.edu).

RRBS and RNA-seq data integration: Identification of gene modules with coordinated differential expression and methylation was performed using the Functional Epigenetic Modules (FEM) algorithm [69], modified for non-human models [70]. The gilthead seabream protein-protein interaction (PPI) database (StringDB version 12, obtained Dec 2024) was used as a scaffold to identify modules of interaction around hub genes. Gene Onthology (GO) functional enrichment of genes associated with differential methylation was performed using a hyper-geometric test (adjusted p value ≤0.05) implemented in the R package clusterProfiler [71].

Results

DAC binds to all DNA methyltransferases from S. aurata with similar energy

The catalytic domains of the five DNA methyltransferases found in the S. aurata genome were predicted by homology modeling with human enzymes as templates. The obtained 3D structure shows that the catalytic domain is well conserved among all enzymes (Figure 1(A)). Then, the interaction of DAC with the different seabream enzymes was assessed by docking (Figure 1(B)). We found that the binding pattern of DAC was the same for all enzymes and that the interaction strength was similar with Dnmt1 and all Dnmt3s, with binding energy values of ΔG = −6–6.4 kcal/mol (Figure 1(C)). Hence, our results point that DAC may exert its effect in both proliferating and non-proliferating cells within the explants, although through different mechanisms (i.e., maintenance versus de novo methylation).

Transcriptomic responses to DAC in pituitary and liver explants

We performed RNA-seq analysis on pituitaries and liver explants exposed for 24 h to DAC or to control vehicle (DMSO). A total of 79–91% of obtained reads aligned with the reference genome. On average, 20,574 genes were retrieved from liver samples, while 22,754 were covered from pituitaries (Supplementary Table S1). These tissues significantly differed in transcript levels of over 12,000 genes (6,784 overexpressed in pituitary and 5,307 in liver; FDR ≤0.05, Log2F > |1|; Supplementary Table S2). Clustering of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) showed that the expression patterns were consistent within tissues and different between them irrespective of the presence of DAC (Supplementary Fig S1). Regarding core DNA methylation remodeling factors, dnmt1 and dnmt3ba were more expressed in control pituitaries, while dnmt3aa, dnmt3bb.1, dnmt3bb.2, tet1, tet3, and mbd5 were more expressed in control livers. No differences between tissues were found in other epigenetic core factors such as tet2, dmap1, tdg, ogt, gadd45, and the methyl-CpG binding domain genes mbd1b, mbd2, mbd3a, mbd3b under control conditions (Supplementary Table S2). DEGs between untreated tissues and related to immunity, hemostasis, endocrine and metabolic processes were enriched in the liver, while those related to neurotransmitter transport and secretion, and synaptic processes were enriched in pituitary (Supplementary Table S3).

Differential expression analysis with DAC exposure identified 18 DEGs in liver explants (Figure 2(A); Supplementary Table S4), whose expression discriminates between control and DAC-treated explants as shown through DAPC density plots (Figure 2(C)). Six genes were up-regulated, elac2, ddx23, trmt1, esco2, znf664-like, and tma16. Moreover, 12 genes were down-regulated, including etfb, arrdc3-like, and lrrc8d-like as the most divergent under DAC treatment. Interestingly, betaine – homocysteine S-methyltransferase 1 (bhmt1), a well-known epigenetic regulator, was also downregulated (Supplementary Table S4). In contrast to most other GO enrichment analysis, Rank-Based Gene Ontology Analysis with Adaptive Clustering (GO-MWU) does not look for GO categories enriched among ‘significant’ genes. Instead, it measures whether each GO category is significantly enriched by either up or down-regulated genes using the whole dataset to gain information. GO-MWU revealed 30 GO Biological Processes, 8 GO Cellular Components, and 4 GO Molecular Functions (Figure 2(E), Supplementary Table S5) modulated by DAC in liver. Most down-regulated biological processes were Metallo-sulfur cluster assembly, and other terms related to mitochondrial respiratory chain functioning, genome stability, DNA metabolism, and different biological processes associated with amino acid metabolism such as those related with the synthesis of serine and aspartate families of amino acids (Figure 2(E)). Conversely, the top nine biological processes upregulated in liver after DAC exposure were related to ribosome biogenesis and protein synthesis, as well as processes involving rRNA, tRNA, and ncRNA (Figure 2(E)). Accordingly, cellular component and molecular functions in liver explants affected by DAC exposure were related to ribosome biogenesis and RNA processing (Figure 2(E)).

In the pituitary, 10 DEGs were found upon DAC exposure (Figure 2(B); Supplementary Table S6), whose expression did not completely discriminate between control and DAC-treated pituitaries (Figure 2(D)). Genes upregulated by DAC exposure were a ribonuclease inhibitor-like, ecm2-like, nrsn1, nms, casp7, and dnajc3, while downregulated genes were an uncharacterized protein (LOC115568381), and three genes involved in immune response (mr1-like, igl1-like and Ig kappa chain V region 3381-like gene) (Supplementary Table S6). GO-MWU analyses revealed 112 GO Biological Processes, 56 GO Cellular Components, and 33 GO Molecular Functions potentially affected by DAC exposure in pituitaries (adj.pvalue < 0.01, Supplementary Table S7). The biological processes most upregulated were related to rRNA maturation and ribosomal biogenesis, as was observed in liver explants, but differently from liver, also with mitochondria functioning and genome stability (Figure 2(F)). Accordingly, all top upregulated cellular components were related to ribosomal biogenesis, protein synthesis, and mitochondrial functioning (Figure 2(F)). In addition, the highest up-regulated molecular function was telomerase activity, related to genome stability (Figure 2(F)). The most downregulated biological process was endocardial cushion formation, but other processes related to immunity (entry into host cell) and signaling (regulation of toll-like receptor 2 signaling pathway) were also downregulated (Figure 2(F)). The most downregulated cellular component was related to endocytosis (endosome lumen) while the most downregulated molecular function was related to immunity (lipoteichoic acid binding) (Figure 2(F)).

DNA methylomes of control pituitary and liver explants

We obtained an average of 24.7 M reads from liver samples and 23 M from pituitaries after adapter and quality trimming. A total of 60.8% and 62.5% of reads from liver and pituitary, respectively, mapped to the genome, covering 9.5 M unique CpGs in liver and 6.3 M in pituitary (Supplementary Table S9). As expected, DNA methylation levels were the lowest around transcription start sites and CpG islands in both tissues (Figure 3(A)), rapidly increasing towards the gene body and to the periphery of the island. The distribution of methylated CpGs through genome features in both tissues was similar; intergenic regions (40-41% of CpGs) and introns (24-27% of CpGs) were enriched in highly and moderately methylated CpGs, while CpGs with low methylation were comparatively enriched at exons (26% of CpGs) and promoters (Figure 3(B)). However, control pituitaries and liver explants differed in the methylation of 309,411 CpGs (DMCs) and in 23,433 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) (Supplementary Table S9). Many biological processes were found to be linked with genes overlapping DMRs between tissues (Supplementary Table S10), especially those related to signaling, cell communication, development, and growth, and other matching tissue-specific functionalities.

We explored the relationship between DNA methylation levels at different gene regions (intron, exon, promoters, and 3’UTR) and gene expression levels under control conditions. In both liver and pituitary, reductions in DNA methylation at 3’UTRs and introns were associated with high levels of expression (Figure 3(C)), although a binomial distribution of methylation was maintained, with high and low methylation sites present in all genes. In the case of promoters, a clearer relationship was seen in proximal regions (250 bp and 1kb from TSS; P250 and P1K) with almost complete shifts of the binomial distribution towards hypomethylation in highly expressed genes (Figure 3(C)). In all these cases, the trends were observed covering the whole range of gene expression levels. Interestingly, shifts to higher levels of DNA methylation at exons were found to be associated with high expression only in liver, and rather than a continuous trend, a shift in exon methylation occurred from medium to low expression level (Figure 3(C)).

Effects of DAC on methylome

Exposure to DAC resulted in 2,490 DMCs and 26 DMRs in liver explants. DMRs in liver were sparse throughout the genome, with most chromosomes showing none or a few DMRs (Figure 4(A)). DMCs and DMRs in DAC-exposed livers were significantly less abundant at exons and more at introns with respect to all covered CpG (Chi-squared test: p.adj < 0.05, Figure 4(B)). While DAC produced both hypo- and hypermethylation in gene regions, the former outnumbered the latter in introns and 1K promoters (Figure 4C), but most DMCs occurred in introns. In pituitary DAC exposure resulted in larger DNA methylation reshaping, with 8,944 DMCs and 370 DMRs. Conversely to the liver, DMRs in the pituitary were more homogeneously distributed through the genome, with nearly all regions that exhibited hypermethylation also experienced hypomethylation in neighboring regions (Figure 4(A)). Also, in contrast to liver, DAC-induced DMCs and DMRs were not differentially distributed across genomic features (Figure 4(B)) and produced a similar number of hypo- and hyper-methylations across gene regions (Figure 4(C)).

Five genes were differentially methylated in liver explants (DMGs; q-value < 0.1, methylation change > 10%) (Figure 5(A), Supplementary Table S11): tfe3, lsm4, pgrmc1, angptl5-like (LOC115595171) and an uncharacterized gene (LOC115584763). Moreover, six genes had differentially methylated promoters (DMPr), xylt1, gadd45g, hars1, zinc-binding protein a33-like (LOC115582906), taar13c-like (LOC115576564), and flocculation protein FLO11-like (LOC115569953) (Figure 5(B), Supplementary Table S11). All these genes exhibited modest (10–20%) changes in DNA methylation after DAC exposure except gadd45g with >25% methylation increase. Additional genes of interest containing at least three DMCs in extended gene regions (gene body +2 kb promoter region) are shown in Figure 5(C). Clear methylation change signals gene-wise were observed for mmp17, tymp, ryr3, whrnb, gabbr2. Only one gene had over three DMCs in the promoter region (2 kb), an uncharacterized gene ortholog of a reverse transcriptase domain-containing protein in Japanese medaka (Figure 5C). Functional enrichment of genes with differential DNA methylation (3-DMCs-overlapping, DMGs and DMPr) showed only 2 GO terms significantly enriched (q.value ≤0.05): the molecular function ligand-gated monoatomic ion channel activity (GO:0015276) and the cellular component side of membrane (GO:0098552).

In pituitary explants, consistently with the larger DAC effect over the methylome, 29 genes were differentially methylated after DAC treatment (Figure 6(A), Supplementary Table S12). Moreover, 26 genes had differentially methylated promoters (Figure 6(B), Supplementary Table S12). Larger changes in DNA methylation were observed for promoters of the insulin like growth factor 2 receptor (igf2r) (~30% hypermethylation) and a peroxisomal biogenesis factor (pex7) ( > 30% hypomethylation) (Figure 6(B)), while <20% hypermethylation was noted in the gene body of plp1 (Figure 6(A)). Other genes exhibited smaller changes, but some of them approaching 20% such as fapb6 and rnf180b (Figure 6(A)). Five additional genes containing at least three DMCs in their promoter region and several other genes containing more than five DMCs in both gene body and promoter were identified (Figure 6(C)). Clear hypo- and hyper-methylated under DAC clusters were observed (Figure 6(C)). Within promoters, the most consistent switches in DNA methylation were observed for adcyap1a and nxph3 (Figure 6(C)). GO enrichment analyses considering DMC-enriched extended genes (gene body +2K promoter) revealed 27 significantly enriched GO terms (q.value ≤0.05, Supplementary Table S13) that were reduced by semantic similarity and represented in a semantic space PCA (Supplementary Fig S2). Most enriched biological processes were related with cell adhesion, transmembrane receptor signaling pathways and axon guidance, and accordingly, the enriched molecular functions and cellular components were related with these processes (Supplementary Fig S2).

Association between gene expression and DAC-induced DNA methylation changes

No DEGs/DMGs overlaps were observed in liver or pituitary explants for all gene-methylation estimation strategies (DMC-overlapping genes, DMG, and DMPr). However, some interesting associations were found. Correlation analysis of all DMCs overlapping extended genes (gene body +2K promoter) and genes with log2fold change gene expression > 1, revealed associations with 10 genes in liver and 12 in pituitary explants (Figure 7(A)). Hypomethylation and upregulation of gene expression was the most frequent relationship in the liver, while different DNAm:GE relationships were equally observed in pituitary (Figure 7(A)). DNAm:GE interaction was further evaluated with functional epigenetic modules (FEM) analysis using promoter methylation. In the liver, seven interactome hotspots were found for promoter methylation (seeds: epc2, gga3, clocka, ift22, ift88, rab35, and gga1) (Supplementary Table S14). Clear associations for DNA methylation and gene expression were found for a few genes within modules, such as decreased promoter methylation and increased expression of gga3, ift22, and ift88, or hypomethylation at the promoter and decreased expression of chdh (Figure 7(B)). In pituitary, examination of DNA methylation at promoters: GE interaction with FEM analysis revealed four interactome hotspots (e.g., tnn1/tnnt2e/tnnt2a and tomm20/tom5/tomm40) but with no clear associations at a single gene level within modules (Supplementary Table S14, Figure 7(B)).

Discussion

Methodological considerations

Given that the demethylating agent DAC targets more strongly DNMT1 than DNMT3s in some higher vertebrate models [72], that DNMT1 mostly affects DNA methylation in proliferating cells though interfering maintenance methylation, and that cell proliferation occurs in pituitary and liver explants of sea bream at a low rate [41], we first assessed if such difference in DNMTs-DAC interactions occurs in our fish species.

DAC is known to be passively incorporated into the DNA and to irreversibly bind DNMT1, thereby blocking maintenance methylation in proliferating cells, but also known to be incorporated into the DNA by active mechanisms [72]. This is suited to our model because a high rate of cell proliferation does not occur in the explants [41]. Instead, the cells in explants proliferate at a similar rate as in live fish [41] providing a realistic model for DNA methylation remodeling, away from a generalized erasure of DNA methylation marks. Moreover, here we predicted that in gilthead seabream DAC interacts with Dnmt3 enzymes as strongly as with Dnmt1, thus supporting its use to inhibit also de novo methylation. Finally, it is worth noting that we exposed explants to DAC for 24 h, but there is little consensus on the half-life of DAC, with reports from four to 21 h, although most studies reported values around 12 h [73]. Although these values correspond to decomposition at 37°C and we kept explants at 23°C as a physiologically relevant temperature for this fish species, we acknowledge that true exposition time may be less than 24 h. Thus, biological processes susceptible to epigenetic manipulations reported herein should be the most sensitive, and do not cover the true potential to manage seabream pituitary and liver physiology. Moreover, we targeted the juvenile stage (donor fish) because it is a developmental stage of high robustness for a variety of interventions, but other processes may also be susceptible to manipulation at earlier, more plastic, ontogeny stages.

Although pituitary and liver contain different cell types and we used whole tissues, it is known that methylation differences between tissues outnumber differences between cell types of the same tissue [74]. While DNA methylomes of cells sharing the same developmental origin are more similar than those of cells from the same tissue but with a distinct origin [75], most cells in our model tissues are also of different origins (i.e., pituitary from ectoderm; liver from endoderm/mesoderm). Yet, single-cell DNA methylome profiling would provide further insights into DNA methylation diversity within tissues and its role in their functional heterogeneity.

Regarding the methylation profiling used, RRBS remains a cost-effective alternative to WGBS. While RRBS does not discriminate 5mC from 5 hmC, it allows us to detect intermediate steps of active demethylation, which are also phenotypically relevant [76,77]. Indeed, our previous study revealed that one difference in the response of pituitary and liver explants to DAC is that only the former responds by increments in TETs expression and TET enzyme activity, leading to an overall increase in 5 hmC [41]. Other studies also indicated active mechanisms behind DNA methylation remodeling in fish [78].

DAC increased protein synthesis in both tissues but differentially impacted mitochondrial functioning and genome stability

Most of the DAC-induced upregulated genes in liver are related to ribosome biogenesis and transcription (elac2, ddx23, trmt1, znf664-like, tma16). Indeed, all nine upregulated biological processes in liver after DAC exposure were related to ribosome biogenesis, RNA processing, and protein synthesis. Strikingly, these observations agree with results obtained in salmon fed 1C-nutrient rich diets, where hepatic tissue enhanced protein synthesis [26]. It is unknown how treatments supposed to produce divergent effects yielded similar results regarding hepatic protein synthesis. Most downregulated genes were etfb, involved in mitochondrial fatty acid and amino acid catabolism, arrdc3-like, which regulate cell-surface expression of adrenergic receptors and probably other G protein-coupled receptors, and lrrc8d-like, required for adaptation to osmotic changes. Increased protein synthesis in seabream liver does not occur in line with enhanced mitochondrial functioning and genome stability. Indeed, the most downregulated biological process was metallo-sulfur cluster assembling, related to these processes [79]. These results suggest that the presence of a demethylating agent may lead to increased oxidative stress risk in the liver tissue. Actually, in a previous study with live fish, we observed clear signs of increased oxidative stress (i.e., higher mitochondrial ROS production and lipid peroxidation) in the liver of seabream ingesting the natural DNA methylation inhibitor genistein [18]. On the other hand, downregulation was also observed in the synthesis of aspartate (e.g., methionine) and serine (e.g., homocysteine, serine) family of amino acids, which are linked with 1-C metabolism and DNA methylation. Homocysteine is synthesized from methionine and it has been correlated with DNA methylation [80], and serine makes a major contribution of methyl groups for homocysteine remethylation back to methionine [81]. Less use of SAM in DAC-treated liver explants may lead to less homocysteine to be recycled by BHMT, a key enzyme in choline metabolism [82] [83], whose gene was downregulated in seabream liver explants exposed to the inhibitor.

Transcriptional changes in pituitaries due to DAC exposure were more evidenced by upregulation of genes involved in RNA stability (a ribonuclease inhibitor-like), cell adhesiveness (ecm2-like), signal transduction (nrsn1), generation of neuropeptides (nms), and in the response to endoplasmic reticulum, hypothermic, and starving stress (casp7, dnajc3). However, functional analyses suggested an increase in ribosomal biogenesis and protein synthesis as observed in liver explants while, conversely to liver, also with mitochondria functioning and genome stability. Indeed, the most upregulated biological process was protein UFMylation, involved in the protection against endoplasmic reticulum stress, disrupted translational homeostasis, and DNA and telomere damage [84]. Our observations suggest that metabolic changes induced by a demethylating agent in the pituitary may not increase the risk of oxidative stress or genome instability, which seems to match with the role of pituitary as a master gland of the endocrine system. However, our functional analysis of the effect of DAC on pituitary revealed few, but undesirable, effects on processes related to immunity. For instance, the most downregulated molecular function was related to lipoteichoic acid binding, suggesting that the response to infection may be locally decreased. The depletion of this molecular function agrees with the downregulation of the biological processes toll-like receptor 2 signaling pathway, as lipoteichoic acid is an agonist of TLR2 in the nervous system that mediate immune activation [85]. Culture media was supplemented with 100 IU/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL of streptomycin, and 2.5 ug/mL amphotericin B, and we found no evidence of bacterial growth by histological examination [41], which may lead to confounding results.

DAC had a more pronounced effect in the pituitary than in the liver methylome

It is well known that both methylation patterns [86] and variability [87] are tissue-specific and related to tissue-specific gene activity [74]. Accordingly, seabream liver and pituitary harbor very different methylomes, yet the general distribution of methylated CpGs in the genome is rather similar, with most of them in intergenic regions and introns. One interesting finding is that the seabream pituitary is by far more permissive to DNA methylation remodeling than the liver in terms of number of DMCs and DMRs (2490 DMCs in liver vs. 8944 in pituitary; 26 DMRs in liver vs. 370 in pituitary). This observation agrees with our previous findings; DAC produced increments in both DNMT3 and TETs gene expression in seabream pituitary, while no such responses occurred in liver explants [41]. The different susceptibility to DAC of methylomes from different tissues has been acknowledged before [88]. It has to be noted that RRBS does not discriminate between 5mC and 5hmC. Thus, many sites reported here as hypermethylated might be actually hydroxymethylated as the result of active demethylation by TETs, known to be enzymatically more active in DAC-treated pituitaries than in the liver [41].

Most DMCs and DMRs were found at intergenic regions and introns in both tissues, following the distribution of CpGs in control tissues. Enrichment of DMCs at these regions was also reported in the liver of zebrafish fed a rich arachidonic acid (ARA) diet [89] as well as in salmon fed with a 1C-supplemented diet [24]. Conversely, DMCs in liver were enriched at promoter regions in a study in zebrafish feeding a diet deficient in 1-C nutrients, while depleted in exons and intergenic regions [30]. Also, a clear difference between seabream tissues is that DMCs and DMRs were less enriched in exons in the liver than in the pituitary. In both plants and animals, methylation in exons is preferentially found in moderately, constitutively expressed genes [90]. Thus, it can be hypothesized that some effects of the liver methylome remodeling would be better revealed in live fish under different challenging conditions. Indeed, the gene with higher change in DNA methylation in liver was gadd45g, whose transcript levels mediate the activation of stress-responsive MTK1/MEKK4 MAPKKK [91], and one of the most consistent switches in DNA methylation was observed in the promoter region of adcyap1a, which is a key mediator of liver in response to stress and homeostasis [92]. However, functional analyses with genes with at least one DMC revealed that most enriched biological processes in liver explants due to DAC were related to energy metabolism, cell communication, and calcium regulation, providing clues on the processes that would be targeted in further studies. To avoid false positives, we applied a more restrictive criterion (genes harboring at least three DMCs in gene body and 2K promoter) and found that only ligand-gated monoatomic ion channel activity and side of membrane were enriched in DMCs. Yet, these processes are related to transforming extracellular signals into cellular responses thus may modify the liver response to a variety of stimuli. In salmon liver, it is known that genes related with ubiquitination, translation, transcription, signaling, immune system, cell cycle, cell adhesion, and adipose tissue regulation are susceptible to changes in their DNA methylation after nutriepigenomic interventions (i.e., 1-C supplementation) [26].

Several genes in pituitary changed their DNA methylation at the promoter regions or gene bodies due to DAC. Higher changes in DNA methylation were observed at igf2r, involved in the intracellular trafficking of lysosomal enzymes and growth signaling, pex7, which is a peroxisomal biogenesis factor, plp1b, the major myelin protein in the nervous system, fapb6, involved in fatty acids transport, fear memory and anxiety [93], and rnf180b, related with the regulation of metabolism of norepinephrine and serotonin, and emotional and social behavior [94]. Accordingly, functional analyses of genes with at least one DMC in the pituitary suggested that DAC would affect synapse, neuron survival, and behavior. There is evidence for the key role of DNA methylation/demethylation on synapse function and neural plasticity [95–97] and stress-induced gene expression and behavior [98] in higher vertebrates, and although less studied, also in fish [99,100]. Further enrichment analyses considering only genes (gene body +2K promoter) that contained at least 5 DMCs revealed that more enriched biological processes were related to cell adhesion, transmembrane receptor signaling pathway, and axon guidance, which confirms previous predictions. Indeed, results from a recent behavioral assessment at our laboratory with seabream ingesting diets enriched in 1-C nutrients or a natural DNA methylation inhibitor, genistein, are suggestive of a role of diet-induced DNA methylation remodeling in behavioral outcomes (i.e., shy/bold) that are maintained after feeding a control diet for more than four months [18], although brain regions other than pituitary may be also implicated.

Connections between DNA methylation and gene expression

Aside from the better-known effects of promoter methylation, relationships between DNA methylation and gene expression in fish have been described for exons [26,101] and introns [26,102]. Accordingly, in both liver and pituitary, low DNA methylation at 3’UTRs, promoter regions, and introns was associated with a high expression level. In muscle and testis of European sea bass (Dicentrarchus labrax), an inverse relationship between first introns methylation and gene expression was found [102]. In salmon liver, a negative correlation between DNA methylation and gene expression was also found for first introns, while methylation at other introns was not correlated [103]. We could observe this relationship in liver and pituitary of seabream without filtering for first introns. Conversely, also considering all introns, high methylation at introns was associated with high expression in salmon liver [26]. Regarding DNA methylation at exons, we found that a high level of DNA methylation at exons was associated with high expression levels, although only in liver. A similar positive relationship was reported in salmon liver [26] and in different human tissues [104]. Conversely, considering the position of exons, other studies have found a negative correlation for first exon while not for the rest in salmon liver [103] and in muscle and testis of seabass [102], as reported in higher vertebrates [105].

Despite the clear connection between DNA methylation and gene expression in liver and pituitary of the seabream, no overlapping between DMGs and DEGs after DAC exposure was found in these tissues. The elucidation of relationships between changes in DNA methylation and gene expression has been challenging in fish. In zebrafish, 5 and 37 concordant genes between DMGs and DEGs were found in F0 and F1, respectively, after high ARA intake [89], while 4 concordant genes in F1 of parents with 1-C micronutrient deficiency [30]. In salmon, clear relationships were found for 10 [24] and 9 [26] genes after 1-C micronutrient supplementation. Some studies have used stringent criteria for DEGs (e.g., ± > 25%, q-value < 0.01), others considered a DMG if harboring variable (i.e.,1–3) DMCs, and others attempted analyses only with DMRs, but in any case, reports on DNA methylation changes at or close to genes that do not vary their expression are common in fish and also in higher vertebrates’ models [106], with uncoupling observed even at promoter regions [106] as we found in seabream pituitary for insulin like growth factor 2 receptor (igf2r) and a peroxisomal biogenesis factor (pex7).

Yet, we observed clear, although slight, associations for change in DNA methylation and gene expression, in both liver and pituitary. Moreover, FEM analysis revealed seven interactome hotspots in liver with clear associations for DNA methylation and gene expression within modules, such as decreased promoter methylation and increased expression of gga3, ift22, and ift88. The gga3 gene product participates in the sorting of newly synthesized proteins to various cellular destinations [107]. On the other hand, ift88 positively regulates primary cilium biogenesis, which are sensory organelles that act as signal transducers [108], and ift22 is involved in intraciliary protein transport, ensuring cilia functionalities such as signaling [109]. While involved in many other signaling pathways, the expression of some intraflagellar transport proteins has been related to redox signaling and hepatic oxidative stress [110], and elevated expression of ift genes has been suggested to be a compensatory reaction against oxidative stress [111]. Thus, DNA methylation-related upregulation of these genes, and changes in functionally related ones as revealed by FEM, indicate that intracellular protein trafficking and signal transduction in the liver of seabream can be targeted by epigenetic interventions. These predictions are in accordance with the enhanced protein synthesis revealed by our transcriptomic assessment, and also by our results in life fish ingesting the DNA methylation inhibitor genistein, which exhibited a higher level of mitochondrial ROS production and lipid peroxidation in the liver than control fish [18]. Our predictions suggest that protection against hepatic oxidative stress conferred by surplus 1-C nutrients [18] partially rely on DNA methylation remodeling.

Strikingly, hypomethylation at the promoter region of chdh decreased its expression in liver. This gene encodes choline dehydrogenase, an enzyme involved in 1-C metabolism by participating in the oxidation of the methyl donor choline into betaine. Methyl groups of betaine are subsequently transferred to homocysteine, which is remethylated by hepatic betaine homocysteine N-methyltransferase (BHMT) to methionine to produce S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), a gene that was downregulated in seabream liver explants due to the inhibitor. Collectively, these findings suggest a decrease in the availability of methyl groups for DNA methylation in the liver under DAC exposure. Yet, although the overall effect in liver was hypomethylation, several genomic regions underwent hypermethylation under these conditions.

In pituitary, FEM analyses evidenced four interactome hotspots with functional relevance for mitochondria functioning. It is known that tomm20 and tomm5 in the mitochondrial outer membrane aid in the recognition and translocation of cytosolically synthesized mitochondrial proteins (e.g., cytochrome P450) into the tomm40 translocation pore, and these three genes decreased DNA methylation, concurrently with upregulation of timm50 and downregulation of mtx2 and pvrl2l. The mtx2 gene encoded for a protein that also participates in the import of proteins into the mitochondrion, as well as in mitochondrial transport into both dendrites and axons [112], while pvrl2l has been suggested to interact with tomm40 to substantially increase Alzheimer’s disease risk in humans through an unclear mechanism [113].

We acknowledge that epigenetic modifications not assessed here such as histone modifications [114–117] may lie behind many transcriptional responses observed in this study. The fact that histone modifications can occur at different sites in the genome, at different histones, and at different residues within histones, adds layers of complexity that cannot be addressed with our data, and deserve further examination.

Concluding remarks

Our study was conducted with a low, non-cytotoxic, dose of DAC, a short exposure period, and under ex vivo conditions, and these allowed us to disclose a subset of biological processes that may be remodeled through epigenetic interventions. That is the case of protein synthesis, certain amino acids metabolism, 1-C metabolism, signaling pathways, neuron functioning, and mitochondria physiology, as judged by functional changes revealed by transcriptomic analyses. Moreover, this study provides the first single-base resolution DNA methylomes of the liver and pituitary in gilthead seabream, which differ markedly as expected, but also responding differentially to epigenetic manipulations. The pituitary is more permissive to change the DNA methylation landscape but also more active in keeping genome stability. Relationships between DNA methylation and gene expression in pituitary and liver of seabream are, in general, consistent with those reported in other fish. However, despite DNA methylation remodeling by DAC in both tissues, only a limited number of clear correlations between differential methylation and gene expression changes were detected. Nevertheless, the affected genes are involved in key biological processes and some of them are connected with signaling pathways that could trigger different transcriptional cascades, functional changes, and phenotypic outputs. Additionally, we unveiled different biological processes that may be targeted in seabream by epigenetic interventions, such as intracellular protein trafficking and signal transduction in the liver, and mitochondria functioning in the pituitary. As these processes are likely to influence the fish response to a variety of challenging conditions, such as those imposed by farming operations, the inclusion of novel feed ingredients, or global climate change, whether they can be targeted by nutritional interventions in live fish is currently under examination in our lab. Finally, given that the relationship between DNA methylation and the genetic background has been documented in this [39] and other fish species [118], it remains to be determined whether the effects of environmentally induced methylome remodeling on the processes described here are consistent across fish with different genetic backgrounds or varying degrees of genetic selection. Addressing this question will enhance the practical reach of our findings in aquaculture.
