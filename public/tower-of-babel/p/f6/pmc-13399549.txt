The Role of Anatomy‐Based Fitting in Bimodal Rehabilitation: Choosing Electrode Arrays Based on Cochlear Length
Authors: Andrea Canale, Arianna Daquino, Maria Gragnano, Claudia Filippini, Marco Boldreghini, Giuseppe Riva, Andrea Albera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo evaluate the clinical impact of anatomy‐based fitting (ABF) in cochlear implants for patients with asymmetric hearing loss.Study DesignProspective cohort study.SettingTertiary referral center specializing in hearing disorders.MethodsFifteen adult patients with asymmetric severe‐to‐profound sensorineural hearing loss or anacusis in the implanted ear, and residual low‐frequency hearing (≤ 65 dB HL at 125–500 Hz) in the better ear, were enrolled. All underwent cochlear implantation with bimodal rehabilitation (cochlear implant + hearing aid) and completed a 12‐month follow‐up. Cochlear duct length was measured preoperatively using OTOPLAN software to select the appropriate electrode array. Postoperative CT scans were analyzed with OTOPLAN to verify electrode positioning and generate the ABF map. Speech intelligibility in noise was assessed using the Italian Matrix sentence test in three listening conditions, comparing outcomes between the ABF and the default MAESTRO fitting maps.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were found in speech perception scores between ABF and MAESTRO maps across test conditions. However, 86.7% of patients (13/15) preferred the ABF map for daily use, citing improved sound quality and listening comfort.ConclusionABF, which aligns electrical stimulation with the patient's cochlear tonotopy by individualizing electrode selection and programming, may enhance the subjective auditory experience. While objective speech outcomes were comparable, patient preference strongly favored ABF, supporting its clinical relevance for optimizing cochlear implant performance in bimodal users.Keywords: anatomy‐based fitting, asymmetric hearing loss, cochlear duct length, cochlear implants

Introduction

In recent years, advancements in cochlear implant (CI) technology have significantly improved the rehabilitation of individuals with asymmetric hearing loss. Since 2010, the use of bimodal stimulation has increased notably [1]. This approach—combining a hearing aid (HA) in one ear and a CI in the contralateral ear affected by profound hearing loss—is widely regarded as the optimal solution for such patients.

Numerous advantages have been reported with the use of either a single HA or a CI, as each device contributes uniquely to auditory rehabilitation. HAs are particularly effective in amplifying low‐frequency sounds, while CIs—due to the placement of the electrode array in the basal turn of the cochlea—typically offer more consistent stimulation of high frequencies. However, the frequency map of adjacent spiral ganglion regions depends on the depth and position of the implanted electrode array within the cochlea [2].

As a result, CIs provide most of the speech information necessary for intelligibility, whereas HAs contribute valuable low‐frequency cues, including the perception of emotional prosody, consonant voicing, and suprasegmental features of speech [3, 4], leading to improved speech naturalness.

Many studies have investigated prognostic factors influencing outcomes following unilateral cochlear implantation. Key variables identified included age at onset of deafness, age at implantation, duration of deafness, years of CI use, etiology of hearing loss, and preoperative speech perception scores [5]. In contrast, recent reserch on bimodal stimulation has highlighted additional factors affecting speech perception, such as signal integration and interference, acoustic characteristics of speech, and the extent of residual acoustic hearing [1].

Beyond its primary goal of restoring binaural hearing by optimizing auditory input in each ear, bimodal stimulation facilitates central auditory processing of interaural timing, phase, and level differences. This integration enhances speech perception in noise and improves sound source localization [6, 7]. Speech perception in both quiet and noisy environments is further improved when the acoustically stimulated ear retains low‐frequency hearing thresholds better than 60 dB [8]. This improvement is attributed to the auditory brainstem's ability to accurately compare interaural phase and timing differences at low frequencies, a key mechanism underlying binaural unmasking which supports speech understanding in background noise [6]. In response to these findings, all CI manufacturers strive to enhance frequency discrimination, particularly in the low‐frequency range, although they employ different strategies to achieve this goal.

An additional factor influencing speech perception outcomes is the precise knowledge of electrode position within the cochlea relative to the cochlear place of acoustic frequency stimulation [9]. It is generally assumed that the closer the frequency assigned to each electrode aligns with its corresponding cochlear place, the better the central integragation of frequency information. This alignment is associated with improved speech perception in noise due to more effective use of binaural cues [10]. Otoplan, a software developed by CAScination AG (Bern, Switzerland) in collaboration with MED‐EL Corporation (Innsbruck, Austria), enables measurement of the cochlear duct length using computed tomography imaging [11]. This tool assists surgeons in selecting the most appropriate electrode array for each patient and, postoperatively, in evaluating the position of each electrode contact via a second CT scan. Accurate knowledge of electrode positioning enables anatomy‐based fitting (ABF), which minimizes frequency mismatch between the electrically and the acoustically stimulated ears.

This study aims to compare CI fitting outcomes among patients with asymmetric hearing loss using bimodal stimulation, evaluating standard automatic frequency allocation provided by the MAESTRO software versus ABF, which accounts for individual electrode positioning within the cochlea.

Methods

Patients

This prospective study included 15 patients (10 males, 5 females), all of whom used a HA with a receiver‐in‐the‐ear (RITE) configuration on the non‐implanted side. The HAs, which varied across individuals, were the same devices used prior to cochlear implantation. None of the patients used bimodal streaming via AudioStream. A sample size of 15 subjects was calculated to yield a two‐sided 95% confidence interval with a margin of error of 0.388 from the mean signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) difference between ABF and MAESTRO fitting methods, assuming a standard deviation (SD) of 0.700 for the paired differences.

All patients underwent unilateral cochlear implantation for asymmetric sensorineural hearing loss, defined by a pure‐tone average (PTA) > 70 dB HL (500–4000 Hz) in the implanted ear and a word recognition score (WRS) < 50% in aided free‐field conditions at 65 dB SPL. All participants completed a 12‐month follow‐up.

Inclusion criteria were: age > 18 years, PTA > 65 dB HL (125–500 Hz) in the better‐hearing ear, use of bimodal stimulation for auditory rehabilitation, functional activation of at least 10 CI electrodes.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee (Protocol n. 12/2021) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Electrode length for each subject was selected based on individual cochlear dimensions. All patients underwent both preoperative and postoperative CT scans. Preoperative imaging was analyzed using Otoplan to determine cochlear size, while postoperative scans assessed insertion depth and electrode posizioning.

Two different CI frequency mapping methods were applied in all patients:

1.Clinically based fitting (CBF—MAESTRO): a generic mapping technique based on a predefined frequency distribution for the electrode array, initially adjusted according to the patient's subjective feedback during the first fitting session. This approach aims to align the CI's frequency map with the cochlea's natural place‐pitch organization, improving fitting accuracy and bringing hearing outcomes closer to natural perception.

Intervention

Speech intelligibility in noise was assessed using the Italian Matrix sentence test (ITA Matrix) [13]. In this test, background noise was fixed at 65 dB SPL, while the signal intensity was adaptively adjusted based on the participant's responses.

The test material consisted of sentences composed of five phonetically balanced words, which the subject was instructed to repeat aloud. Scoring was based on the number of correctly repeated words and the outcome measure was the SNR at which the participant correctly recognized 50% of the words. For Italian‐speaking individuals with normal hearing, the reference SNR value is −7.3 ± 0.2 dB [13].

The ITA Matrix test was administered through two loudspeakers positioned one meter from the participant. Three spatial configurations were used to evaluate binaural hearing:

Summation condition (S0N0): speech and noise both presented from the front speaker;

Summation condition (S0N0): speech and noise both presented from the front speaker;

Squelch condition (S0N90): speech presented from the front and noise directed toward the CI side;

Squelch condition (S0N90): speech presented from the front and noise directed toward the CI side;

Head shadow condition (S90N‐90): speech presented to the CI ear and noise directed to the contralateral ear (HA side).

Head shadow condition (S90N‐90): speech presented to the CI ear and noise directed to the contralateral ear (HA side).

Each configuration was tested under two listening conditions: with the HA alone and with bimodal stimulation using both CI maps (ABF and MAESTRO), following the protocol described below.

CI activation occurred 4–6 weeks after surgery. Each patient was then randomly assigned to receive either the ABF or MAESTRO fitting map, which were labeled generically as “Map 1” or “Map 2” to ensure blinding. During the first 4 months, the two maps were alternated on a monthly basis; from the 5th month onward, they were alternated weekly to minimize long‐term adaptation to any single map.

The first evaluation took place 2 months after CI activation, testing with ITA Matrix all three spatial conditions (summation, squelch, head shadow) for both fitting maps. Further assessments occurred at 4 months post‐activation and monthly thereafter until the 9th month. During the 9th‐month assessment, patients were asked to independently choose their preferred map for continued use, based solely on subjective preference. A final evaluation was conducted at 12 months; at this stage, no further comparisons between the two maps were made, as participants had already adopted their preferred fitting strategy.

At the 12‐month follow‐up, pitch perception was also assessed using the MED‐EL Pitch Discrimination Test. This test evaluates the subject's ability to compare the frequency perception between the HA and CI ears. It involves three phases: two preliminary loudness‐balancing steps followed by the pitch discrimination task. Initially, with the CI turned off, two pure tones of 1000 Hz at varying intensities were presented in free field to the HA ear to identify the subject's most comfortable listening level. Once identified, loudness balancing between the HA and CI ears was then performed using pure tones with variable frequencies and intensities, both in free field and via Audiolink for the CI.

In the final phase, pitch discrimination was evaluated using two target frequencies (500 and 1000 Hz). Pairs of tones—one presented in free field to the HA ear and one via Audiolink to the CI ear—were delivered sequentially, with one tone randomly chosen from the target frequencies. A pitch‐matching graph was generated to represent the frequency perceived through the CI. This test was performed twice per patient, once using the MAESTRO map and once using the ABF map.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive stastistics were used to summarize clinical data, with results reported as means and SD. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was employed to assess the normality of continuous variables distributions. Comparisons between outcomes obtained with the two fitting strategies (ABF vs. MAESTRO) were conducted using the Wilcoxon‐Mann‐Whitney test for continuous variables and either the chi‐square (χ 2) test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. To enhance interpretability, the sample was stratified into quartiles based on SNR values. General linear models (GLMs) were applied to investigate potential differences in SNR outcomes across the three Matrix test configurations (summation, squelch, head shadow) for each fitting strategy. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, with a significance threshold set at p ≤ 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

At the 9‐month visit, patients were asked to select their preferred CI map for continued use based solely on subjective preference: 13 patients (86.7%) selected the ABF map as their preferred fitting strategy, reporting improved sound quality and greater listening comfort, while two patients (13.3%) opted to continue with the MAESTRO map. A binomial test indicated that this preference for ABF was statistically significant (p = 0.004), suggesting that the observed difference was unlikely due to chance.

In the pitch discrimination analysis at 500 Hz, congruence between acoustic and electric pitch discrimination was observed in 10 cases. Of these, six cases (60.0%) were associated with the ABF map and four cases (40.0%) with the MAESTRO map. Although the ABF strategy showed a trend toward improved pitch matching, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.392).

At 1000 Hz, correct pitch discrimination occurred in 6 of 22 measurements (27.3%), with five cases (83.3%) linked to the ABF map and one case (16.7%) to the MAESTRO map. This difference, while suggestive, also failed to reach statistical significance (p = 0.149).

Discussion

This study aimed to compare the standard MAESTRO CI fitting method with the ABF approach in a cohort of patients undergoing bimodal auditory rehabilitation. Speech in noise performance was evaluated across three listening conditions—head shadow, summation, and squelch—but no statistically significant differences emerged between the two fitting strategies.

Our inclusion criteria, which excluded candidates with low‐frequency thresholds exceeding 65 dB HL in the better‐hearing ear, resulted in a heterogeneous patient cohort. This variability was reflected in the broad range of SNR values. To improve interpretability, the data were stratified into quartiles based on SNR; however, even with this approach, no significant differences were observed between ABF and MAESTRO fittings (Table 4).

These results contrast with existing literature emphasizing the importance of accurate electrode‐to‐place frequency alignment. Prior studies have shown that mismatches between cochlear tonotopy and electrode mapping—often arising from disparities in cochlear duct length and electrode insertion depth—can adversely affect speech perception, particularly in cases of asymmetric hearing loss [14, 15]. In such scenarios, interaural mismatches may further complicate binaural integration, especially in noisy environments where low‐frequency synchrony is critical for speech understanding [6].

Several recent investigations have reported that tailoring frequency allocation to individual cochlear anatomy using ABF leads to better speech recognition and faster adaptation in postlingually deafened adults [16, 17, 18, 19]. While these studies support the theoretical rationale for ABF, our findings differ—possibly due to a key distinction in the study design. In our prospective protocol, electrode type and length were selected preoperatively based on cochlear duct length measurements obtained via Otoplan software. Therefore, this anatomical matching likely led to a default frequency allocation under MAESTRO that closely resembled ABF, thus minimizing the tonotopic mismatch even without a postoperative map reallocation. In our dataset, the median absolute difference in SNR between ABF and MAESTRO maps was less than 1 dB across listening conditions, indicating minimal divergence between the two fitting methods. The similarity in performance likely reflects the common use of anatomically selected electrodes, rather than any ongoing patient‐driven tuning of the MAESTRO map, which was adjusted only once during the initial fitting session. In contrast, many earlier studies applied a one‐size‐fits‐all approach to electrode selection. For instance, Maro et al. [18] reported a mean cochlear duct length of 41.34 mm (range: 37.5–46.8 mm), yet 90% of patients received a Flex28 electrode. Similarly, Fan et al. [19] used Flex28 arrays in nearly half their patients (43.8%), despite average cochlear duct lengths exceeding 40 mm.

Our individualized electrode selection may explain the minimal performance gap observed between the two fitting strategies. This interpretation is supported by Dietreux et al. [20], who found that smaller cochleae were associated with deeper electrode insertion angles and reduced mismatch. Their findings suggest that preoperative imaging alone may be sufficient to guide optimal electrode selection and reduce frequency‐place discrepancies.

These results carry important clinical implications, particularly for pediatric patients. A recent review on otologic planning tools cited limited ABF application in children due to concerns about radiation exposure from postoperative CT scans [21]. Our findings suggest that with anatomically guided electrode placement, it may be possible to achieve effective frequency alignment—and thus favorable auditory outcomes—without the need for postoperative imaging, supporting safer and more streamlined care in pediatric settings.

Nevertheless, the finding that 86.7% of participants ultimately opted for the ABF map is noteworthy. This statistically significant preference reinforces the perceptual relevance of ABF, even in the absence of objective differences. While objective speech‐in‐noise results showed no significant advantage, patients may have perceived improvements in pitch naturalness or listening comfort under ABF. Although our pitch discrimination test did not show statistically significant differences between the two fitting strategies, a greater proportion of congruent pitch matches were observed under ABF at both 500 Hz and 1000 Hz.

A key strength of this study is its prospective design, making it the first to assess ABF in the context of asymmetric hearing loss and anatomically matched electrode selection. However, limitations include the relatively small sample size and the absence of subjective outcome measures such as quality‐of‐life assessments or patient satisfaction surveys. Including these tools might have provided further insight into the clear patient preference for ABF.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings indicate that when electrode selection is guided preoperatively by patient‐specific cochlear anatomy, no significant differences in speech‐in‐noise perception are observed between the ABF and standard MAESTRO fitting strategies. This highlights the critical role of individualized electrode selection in optimizing auditory outcomes and achieving alignment between electrical stimulation and the cochlea's natural tonotopic organization ‐ even in the absence of postoperative imaging.

Although ABF did not confer a statistically significant advantage in objective speech‐in‐noise measures, the strong patient preference (86.7%) for ABF suggests potential perceptual benefits beyond what is captured by standard testing—such as improved sound quality or listening comfort. ABF remains a valuable clinical tool, particularly in scenarios where preoperative electrode selection is less precise, where full array insertion is not achieved, or in anatomically atypical cochleae (e.g., with unusually large cochlear duct lengths and reduced insertion angles). In such cases, ABF can help mitigate frequency‐place mismatches and contribute to more natural and effective auditory perception.
