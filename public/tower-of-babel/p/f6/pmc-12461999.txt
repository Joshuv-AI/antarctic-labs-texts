The impact of sociodemographic, health status and health behavior factors on depression and quality of life in older adults
Authors: Edip Kaya, Mustafa Ozkan Firat, Ibrahim Halil Akçay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the determinants of depression and quality of life (QoL), emphasizing sociodemographic, health status, and health behavior factors. Additionally, it focused on depression as a mediator in the relationship between sleep disturbance and QoL.MethodThis cross-sectional study involved 365 elderly individuals aged 60–90 years in a district of Türkiye, conducted from October 1 to November 15, 2024. The data collection involved face-to-face surveys at Family Health Centers using convenience sampling. Researchers visited these centers periodically, distributed questionnaires to willing elderly participants, and observed the completion of forms. The WHO Quality of Life Assessment (WHOQOL-AGE), the Geriatric Depression Scale-15 (GDS-15), the Jenkins Sleep Scale (JSS), and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF) were used to assess QoL, depression, sleep disturbances, and physical activity. Hierarchical regression analyses identified determinants of depression and quality of life, while mediation analysis explored depression’s role in the relationship between sleep disturbances and QoL.ResultsThe study revealed a high frequency of depression (38.4%) among older adults, significantly associated with low QoL. Hierarchical linear regression analysis indicated that age negatively impacted QoL (β= -0.150) while higher family income positively influenced it (β = 0.189). Conversely, higher education level was negatively associated with depression (β= -0.143). Additionally, being overweight or obese adversely affected QoL (β= -0.113) and increased depression risk (β = 0.099). Chronic illness and dependency also contributed positively to depression (β = 0.136; β = 0.125 respectively). Furthermore, physical activity enhanced QoL (β = 0.241) but is negatively correlated with depression (β= -0.242), while sleep disturbance detrimentally affected both QoL (β= -0.123) and increased depression (β = 0.175) risk. Importantly, mediating effect analysis results showed that depression acted as a complete mediator in the relationship between sleep disturbance and QoL (β= − 0.202, Boot 95% CI [− 0.327, − 0.088]).ConclusionThis study showed that, in addition to sociodemographic factors, physical health status and health behaviors influenced both depression and QoL. Notably, depression fully mediated the relationship between sleep disturbance and QoL. These results emphasized the need to consider various factors in efforts to reduce depression and enhance QoL in older individuals. Additionally, interventions targeting sleep disturbance should prioritize strategies to alleviate depression, as this is crucial for improving overall QoL.Keywords: Depression, Quality of life, Physical activity, Sleep disturbance, Older adults, Public health, Health behavior

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) projects that by 2050, the proportion of the elderly population will increase from 12 to 22%, nearly doubling [1]. In Türkiye, this trend is also evident. The elderly population, which was approximately 7.5 million in 2019, experienced a 20.7% increase over five years, reaching around 9.1 million in 2024. As of 2024, the elderly consisted 10.6% of the total population. It is estimated that by 2060, this proportion will rise to 27.0% [2]. This global aging trend has elevated the importance of maintaining and improving the health and quality of life (QoL) for older individuals as a major global health priority [3]. In recent years, the significance of QoL has been recognized and prioritized by WHO and other international organizations. This growing focus is largely due to the link between QoL and critical health issues such as chronic diseases and mental health disorders [4].

Depression in older adults is a significant public health issue, linked to emotional distress, heightened healthcare costs, elevated suicide risks, and increased mortality from other health conditions [5]. Studies have indicated that depression is one of the most common mental health disorders and has increasingly affected older adults. For instance, a systematic review and meta-analysis have revealed that the prevalence of depression among older adults in developing countries is approximately 40.8% [6]. Additionally, a comprehensive community-based study conducted in Türkiye revealed that more than half of older adults exhibited symptoms of depression [7].

Sleep disturbances are a common issue among older adults and represent a significant public health concern [8]. Recent meta-analysis research estimates that approximately 45% of individuals aged 60 and above experience sleep-related problems [9]. Sleep disorders have been extensively studied and are shown to impact various aspects of health, especially in the elderly [10]. These conditions are linked not only to increased risks of all-cause mortality, mental illness, and cardiovascular disease but also contribute to symptoms such as fatigue, weakness, and cognitive decline [11–13]. These effects collectively increased risk of depression and diminish QoL among older individuals [10, 13].

Previous studies provide significant evidence regarding the relationship between depression and quality of life, as well as the factors that determine them. However, a substantial portion of this research has been conducted on younger populations, adults, or those living with chronic illnesses [14–16]. Studies involving older individuals, on the other hand, have predominantly focused on specific and distinct risk and/or protective factors [17–20]. However, research examining the combined effects of sociodemographic variables, health-related factors, and health behaviors on depression or quality of life in older adults, using a more rigorous methodological approach, remains quite limited [21]. Additionally, while evidence supports the link between sleep quality, depression, and QoL [17, 22], research examining the potential mediating role of depression in the relationship between sleep disturbance and QoL remains limited. Notably, we found only two studies have explored this specific relationship. One study found that depression fully mediated the relationship between sleep quality and QoL [23], while the other indicated that depression had a partial mediating role in this relationship [10]. In conclusion, this study is designed with two main objectives. Firstly, it aims to investigate the factors influencing both depression and quality of life (QoL), with a particular emphasis on sociodemographic characteristics, health status, and health-related behaviors. Secondly, the study aims to examine the mediating role of depression in the relationship between sleep disturbance and QoL, addressing a gap identified in existing research.

Method

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study involved 365 elderly individuals aged 60–90 years residing in Ağrı, Türkiye, and was conducted between October 1 and November 15, 2024. The study’s sample size was determined using G*Power software, focusing on predictors of QoL and depression within a multiple linear regression framework. A power analysis, assuming a medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), a Type I error rate of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.95, calculated a minimum requirement of 178 participants. To account for potential data losses and to allow for more comprehensive analyses, additional participants were recruited. During the study period, 383 elderly individuals who met the study criteria approached, 18 declined participations, resulting in a total of 365 participants (95.3%) who agreed to take part in the study.

The study faced obstacles in acquiring a random sample of the city due to financial limitations, transportation challenges, and difficulties in accessing participants. Despite these issues, efforts were made to ensure representation from various socio-economic groups by involving all Family Health Centers located in the city center. Researchers collected data using face-to-face surveys. They visited Family Health Centers periodically, distributed questionnaires to elderly participants who visited the centers and agreed to take part, and collected the completed forms under observation. For participants who were illiterate, researchers read the questions aloud and recorded their answers. The study included individuals aged over 60, while excluding those with cognitive impairments or those who could not understand or communicate in Turkish.

Instruments

Personal information form

This form; consists of 15 items, covering aspects such as age, gender, education level, marital status, and income level. Additionally, it includes questions aimed at assessing health status and behaviors. Physical health status regarding questions included participants’ weight and height for calculated Body Max Index (BMI), a question regarding had any chronic disease (Do you have a chronic disease diagnosed by a doctor?) and a question that evaluates whether elderly individuals depend on others for their daily activities or not (“Do you need any help from someone else for your daily tasks (such as eating, walking, bathing, dressing, etc.)?”).

WHOQOL-AGE

The WHO Quality of Life Assessment (WHOQOL-AGE) is a 13-item self-report instrument used to assess the QoL among older people in terms of their daily living. The total score is calculated by summing the individual items and converting the result into a standardized scale ranging from 0 to 100. All items were rated using a five point Likert-type scale, with a higher score indicating better QoL [24]. The WHOQOL-AGE has been validated in Turkish older people with good psychometric properties [25]. The internal consistency of the WHOQOL-AGE in the present study’s sample was excellent (α = 0.931).

Geriatric depression scale-15

The Geriatric Depression Scale-15 (GDS-15) is a shorter version of the original 30-item GDS, developed in 1986 [26]. It has been widely adapted into various languages and is commonly used to screen for depression in older adults. The GDS-15 is designed for simplicity, requiring only “yes” or “no” responses, making it particularly suitable for geriatric populations. For the Turkish version, a validity and reliability study was conducted by Durmaz et al., with a Cronbach alpha coefficient of 0.920, and it was calculated as 0.794 in the current study [27]. The threshold of 8 or higher is used to determine the presence of depression, as it is considered the optimal cut-off point for identifying potential cases, according to a recent meta-analysis [28].

Jenkins sleep scale

The Jenkins Sleep Scale (JSS) is a commonly used short tool used to evaluate sleep disturbances across multiple conditions. This scale consists of four questions that examine sleep problems experienced by individuals over the past four weeks. For each question, the individual indicates how many days they experienced issues during that time frame (0 = not at all, 1 = 1–3 days, 2 = 4–7 days, 3 = 8–14 days, 4 = 15–21 days, 5 = 22–28 days). The total score ranges from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating more severe sleep disturbances [29]. The Turkish validity and reliability study indicated strong psychometric properties, with a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.862 for the JSS [30]. In the current study, the Cronbach’s alpha value was determined to be 0.796.

International physical activity questionnaire-short form

The International Physical Activity Questionnaire-Short Form (IPAQ-SF) was used to assess the physical activity levels of the participants. The scale consists of 7 questions that cover various domains such as work, transportation, housework and leisure activities. Metabolic Equivalent of Task (METs)-minutes “in the past seven days” is calculated by multiplying the activity duration in minutes by the activity level coefficient. For example, walking is considered a mild activity and is given a coefficient value of 3.3. Moderate activities are rated at 4.0 coefficient, while vigorous intensity activities are assigned a higher value of 8.0 coefficient [31]. According to the IPAQ scoring procedure, the level of physical activity is divided into three categories: low (subjects not sufficiently active, total score < 600 MET. min), moderate; and [3] high (health-enhancing physical activity, i.e. total score ≥ 3000 MET.min.wk-1 or vigorous PA score ≥ 1500 MET.min.wk-1) [4]. The validity and reliability study of the Turkish version of the survey was conducted by Öztürk and it was found that the survey appropriate and applicable for Turkish society [32].

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 22.0 software along with the PROCESS macro version 5.0, as developed by Hayes [33]. Descriptive analysis was conducted on the socio-demographic and other characteristics of the participants. The normality of the data was tested using skewness and kurtosis values along with histogram charts. As a result, it was determined that GDS, WHOQOL-Age, and JSS scales conformed to normal distribution, whereas the IPAQ-SF scale did not exhibit normal distribution. Therefore, the scale scores were divided into three categories (low, average, high) based on the recommended cutoff points and included in the analyses accordingly. Pearson correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationships between sleep disturbance, depression, QoL and age.

Two hierarchical multiple linear regression analyses were run to determine the predictors of QoL and depression. To ensure a rigorous and structured research design, variables were categorized into three distinct groups as identified in the literature [21, 34]: sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, marital status, education level, and family monthly income), physical health status (BMI, chronic disease presence, and dependency on others), and health behavior-related factors (smoking, physical activity, and sleep disturbance). These variables were introduced into the regression model in three sequential steps: demographic characteristics in the first step, physical health status related variables added in the second step, and finally, health behavior-related variables included in the third step. Linear regression analysis assumptions were tested before conducting the analyses. The analysis ensured that there were no issues of multicollinearity among the independent variables, as verified by variance inflation factor (VIF) and tolerance values. Furthermore, the Mahalanobis distance test confirmed the absence of multivariate outliers at a significance level of p < 0.001.

A mediation analysis was performed to establish mediator role of depression between sleep disturbance (independent variable) and QoL (dependent variable) with SPSS PROCESS macro (model 4). Age, gender, education level, marital status, income, smoking, chronic disease, need help for basic needs, BMI, and physical activity were included analysis as covariates. The direct effect represents the relationship between sleep disturbance and QoL after accounting for the influence of depression. Meanwhile, the indirect effects capture how sleep disturbance impacts QoL through its relationship with depression. The study used 5000 bootstrap samples to ensure robust statistical analysis.

Results

Table 1 demonstrated the main sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the 365 elderly individuals evaluated in the current study. The mean age was 69.3 years, and 52.9% was male. Over half of the participants (53.4%) had any chronic disease, and nearly a quarter (24.9%) dependence on others for their basic needs. Additionally, most of the participants (69.0%) overweight or obese based on calculation and classified BMI. According to the GDS-15 scale cutoff point, it was found that about 38.4% of elderly individuals had probable depression. The mean score for the GDS-15 was calculated to be 6.6 (SD = 3.6). Most of the participants (68.9%) had low physical activity based on IPAQ-SF scale cut-off. The average sleep disturbance (JSS scale) score was 5.7 (SD:3.8), and the mean QoL (WHOQOL-AGE scale) score was 46.2 (SD: 15.2).

The hierarchical linear regression analysis presented in Table 3 identifies various predictors of QoL. Initially, age (β= −0.181, p < 0.01) and female gender (β= −0.127, p < 0.05) were found to negatively impact QoL, while higher education level (β = 0.128, p < 0.05) and higher family income (β = 0.221, p < 0.001) had positive effects. Discussion

Prevalence of depression

The findings of this study reveal that the prevalence of depression among older adults is significantly high (38.4%) and is associated with a low QoL (average: 46.2). Our findings on depression prevalence align with previous studies. For instance, a study conducted in China with 1,173 participants aged 65 and older reported a depression prevalence of 37.3% [18]. Similarly, a large-scale national study in Greece found a prevalence rate close to ours, at 32.4% [35]. Furthermore, a systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the overall prevalence of depression at 31.7%, with developing countries showing a higher prevalence of approximately 40.8% [6]. These results are consistent with our findings. However, some studies have reported notably lower depression rates. For example, a large-scale study in China found a depression prevalence of only 2.8% [36]. These discrepancies between studies can be attributed to differences in the characteristics of study populations, cultural variations, sample sizes, measurement tools, and other methodological factors [6, 18].

Predictors of depression and quality of life

Sociodemographic characteristics: In this study, among sociodemographic variables, older age and being female were found to be negatively associated with QoL, while higher income and higher education levels were positively associated with QoL. Findings related to the determinants of depression, as expected, showed the opposite. However, while gender was associated with QoL, it was not found to be associated with depression. The finding that age was negatively associated with QoL and positively associated with depression aligns with the literature [18, 35, 37]. This could be a result of increased physical and functional losses with advancing age [18]. Our findings regarding gender are partially consistent with the literature. Studies generally align with our findings, showing that women tend to have lower QoL [38, 39]. In this study, we did not find a relationship between depression and gender. However, many studies, including systematic reviews and meta-analyses, have shown that depression is more prevalent among elderly women [34, 40]. Consistent with this study, there are numerous studies indicating that higher education and better economic conditions are associated with better QoL [21, 41], while lower education and economic/income levels are linked to depression [42, 43]. Higher education and economic status are expected to positively influence individuals’ quality of life and better mental health by improving living standards and access to better healthcare. Additionally, higher education and economic conditions enhance individuals’ health-related knowledge and skills, facilitating access to services and promoting health improvement. This contributes to better mental health and QoL [44].

Physical health status: Physical health status measured with the presence of chronic illness, dependency on others for daily tasks, and BMI. Consistent with the literature, our findings regarding depression showed that all three variables tested were statistically significant. Numerous studies, including systematic reviews and meta-analyses, have similarly found that poor health status assessments, the presence and number of chronic illnesses, and high BMI are associated with an increased risk of depression [34, 36]. Our findings related to QoL were partially aligned with the literature. In the second model (which included socioeconomic and physical health variables), all three variables were significant. However, in the final model, after incorporating health behavior-related factors, only BMI remained significantly associated with QoL, while chronic illness and dependency on others lost their significance. A study conducted in Poland identified six chronic illnesses, including hypertension, stroke, and heart failure, as leading factors negatively affecting QoL [45]. Similarly, a systematic review found that studies conducted with individuals with chronic illnesses—particularly neurological conditions—tend to have lower average QoL scores compared to the general population studies [46]. In our study, after health behavior-related factors were added to the model, the relationship between chronic disease and dependency status with QoL disappeared. This may suggest that the impact of health behavior-related factors on QoL is greater and may have diminished the effect of variables related to physical health. This result also highlights the importance of the influence of health behavior variables on quality of life. The fact that BMI remains significant could suggest that health behavior factors may not affect BMI as strongly as variables like the presence of chronic illness or dependency on others.

Health behaviors: Sleep disturbances and physical activity identified as strong determinants of both depression and QoL, while smoking was not found to be associated with either QoL or depression in this study. Consistent with the literature, older adults experiencing sleep disorders reported more severe depressive symptoms. The relationship between sleep disorders and depressive conditions is bidirectional: sleep disturbances increase the risk of depression, while depression raises the likelihood of sleep problems [47]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have also demonstrated that sleep disturbances significantly elevate the onset of depressive episodes [47, 48]. Although this study did not focus on how sleep disturbances impact depression, previous research has shown that sleep deprivation negatively affects brain regions processing emotionally significant information, which are linked to an increased risk of depressive symptoms [49]. Furthermore, the negative impact of sleep disturbances on QoL aligns with existing literature [22, 50]. Sleep deprivation is known to adversely affect metabolism, endocrine function, and cognitive abilities [13]. These effects can exacerbate age-related chronic health issues, impair daily functioning, reduce attention, cause fatigue, and increase psychiatric symptoms—all of which contribute to a decline in QoL [47]. Our findings corroborate existing research, emphasized the critical role of sleep on depression and QoL. Sleep disturbances are particularly impactful for older adults, as they can lead to attention deficits, slower reaction times, memory and concentration issues, reduced ability to complete daily tasks, and increased risk of falls [13]. Therefore, improving sleep quality and addressing existing sleep problems in older individuals is vital for enhancing their QoL and reducing psychological symptoms.

This study revealed that physical activity is associated with both QoL and depression, in alignment with existing literature. A cross-sectional study conducted in Brazil observed that individuals with higher levels of physical activity scored better on QoL measures, while sedentary individuals had higher anxiety and depression scores [51]. Similarly, a recent study in Türkiye found that individuals who rarely or never engaged in physical activity had approximately double the risk of depression compared to those who exercised at least once a week [52]. The World Health Organization (WHO), in its Global Action Plan on Physical Activity 2018–2030, emphasized that regular physical activity is a protective factor against non-communicable diseases, such as cardiovascular conditions, obesity, and hypertension. Furthermore, it highlighted the benefits of physical activity for mental health, including delayed onset of dementia, improved QoL, and enhanced overall well-being [53].

Mediating role of depression between sleep disturbance and quality of life

Previous studies have highlighted that sleep disturbances in older adults are significant associated with depression, supporting the findings we emphasized earlier [21, 47, 48]. Additionally, numerous studies, consistent with our research, have demonstrated that poor mental health, particularly depression, has a negative relationship with the QoL in older adults [54, 55]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that depression might play a mediating or moderating role in the relationship between sleep disorders and quality of life. Mediating effect analysis results confirmed our hypothesis, showing that depression serves as a complete mediator in the relationship between sleep disturbance and QoL. Similarly, Ren et al.‘s study examining the mediating role of depression between sleep quality and QoL found that depression acts as a full mediator in this relationship [23]. However, Hu et al.‘s research suggested that depression has only a partial mediating role in this context [10]. These findings suggest that sleep disorders are more closely related to depression and mental health than directly to QoL. Depression, on the other hand, appears to be a more direct relationship with QoL. It has been suggested that sleep disturbance negatively associated with physical health by impairing emotional regulation and disrupting the body’s circadian rhythm [23, 56]. Therefore, the influence of sleep disturbance on QoL is primarily mediated through indirect pathways. Nonetheless, further research is needed to determine whether depression serves as a partial or full mediator in this relationship. These results imply that targeted interventions addressing depression could not only directly enhance QoL for older adults but also indirectly improve it by alleviating symptoms of sleep disorders.

The study acknowledges several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First and foremost is its cross-sectional design. While such studies are useful for identifying relationships between dependent and independent variables, they inherently lack the ability to establish causality. Another notable limitation is the restricted scope of generalizability. The findings are specific to the study group and cannot be extrapolated to represent broader populations, such as the entire province or country. Additionally, the study data only includes elderly individuals who visited the Family Health Center. The characteristics of those who visited may differ from those who did not, which introduces selection bias and limits the generalizability of the findings. To address these issues, future research should employ larger and more representative samples to enhance the applicability of results across diverse contexts. Furthermore, the study’s reliance on self-reported data introduces potential response and recall biases. Participants may inaccurately recall information or consciously alter their responses, which could compromise the reliability of the data collected. Lastly, it is important to acknowledge that there could be additional elements influencing depression and quality of life that were not addressed or explored in this research. The findings should be interpreted strictly within the context of the specific variables that were investigated. These factors underscore the need for more robust data collection methods in subsequent studies to ensure greater accuracy and validity.

Conclusion

Our study highlighted that depression was significantly prevalent among elderly individuals and substantially associated with their low QoL. Additionally, certain sociodemographic, health status, and health behavior related factors were found to associated with depression and/or QoL. Another key finding of this study is that depression played a complete mediating role in the relationship between sleep disturbance and QoL. Factors related to sociodemographic status and physical health are either non-modifiable (such as age and gender etc.) or difficult to alter (financial or educational status). This highlights the importance of health behaviors. Among these, higher levels of physical activity were found to be positively associated with better QoL while negatively associated with depression symptoms. Conversely, sleep disturbance has a negative relationship with QoL and positively correlated with depression. Thus, addressing improving sleep quality, alongside promoting physical activity tailored to the capacities of elderly individuals, are essential steps for support well-being and enhance QoL of older adults. Additionally, public health policy makers and practitioners should prioritize sleep hygiene education, routine depression screenings, and expanding access to mental health services for older individuals. Policymakers should focus on monitoring the mental health of elderly individuals, particularly those experiencing sleep disturbances, and implement strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of depression. Moreover, encouraging physical activity, fostering social engagement, and equipping healthcare providers with specialized training are also vital steps in addressing the unique challenges faced by older adults. These measures can significantly improve sleep quality, QoL, and overall well-being, supporting healthier aging for this vulnerable population.
