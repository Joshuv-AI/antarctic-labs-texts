A human-derived Bacteroides strain attenuates depressive-like behavior in a rat model of social defeat-induced stress
Authors: Marisol I. Dothard, Mariaelena Caboni, Daniel Norment, Nolan Sigmund, Sarah M. Allard, Jack A. Gilbert, Ekaterina Gavrish, Gabriel Al-Ghalith, Andre Der-Avakian, Philip Strandwitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe gut microbiome has been linked to mood disorders via communication along the gut-brain axis. We previously found levels of fecal Bacteroides were inversely associated with brain signatures of depression in the prefrontal cortex of human subjects. Bacteroides are important human commensals, playing a keystone role in regulating the immune system and producing bioactive metabolites, like the neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid and B vitamins.MethodsTo better understand the link between Bacteroides and depression – and test a candidate novel next-generation probiotic – we administered a human-derived Bacteroides salyersiae strain, HB32, orally in rats that were subjected to the repeated social defeat model of stress-induced depression. The fecal microbiome and the prefrontal cortex transcriptome were then profiled for intervention-induced alterations.ResultsOrally delivered B. salyersiae HB32 reduced depressive-like behavior in male rats comparable to the drug ketamine, independently of the strain being administered in its viable or inactivated (iHB32) form. Mechanistically, we observed that stress-induced anhedonia required the vagus nerve for its phenotype to develop, suggesting crosstalk between the gut and the brain. In support of this, we found HB32 and iHB32, but not ketamine, rescued stress-induced differential expression patterns in the prefrontal cortex, including those related to serotonin signaling and oxidative stress. In the gut, prolonged exposure to social defeat led to broad shifts in the composition of the gut microbiome, with a predominant reduction of the endogenous Bacteroides. Administration of HB32, iHB32, and ketamine attenuated the impact of stress on the microbiome, and intake of live HB32 resulted in a significant increase of the B. salyersiae species in the fecal microbiome.ConclusionsDepressive-like behavior in male rats induced by repeated social defeat requires vagal signaling, suggesting gut-brain-axis crosstalk. In animals with an intact vagus nerve, oral administration of viable or inactivated B. salyersiae HB32 reversed behavioral changes induced by chronic stress at levels comparable to ketamine. Additionally, supplementation of HB32 and iHB32 was associated with attenuation of stress-related microbiome and prefrontal cortex transcriptional changes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04787-y.

Background

Depression, a disorder affecting up to 9% of U.S. adults yearly and a leading cause of disability worldwide [1, 2], is characterized by persistent feelings of sadness, often co-occurring with altered mood states like irritability, fatigue, and anhedonia [3]. The etiology and pathology of depression are influenced by a complex interplay of external and intrinsic factors, such as diet [4], life events [5], socioeconomic status [6], stress [7], genetic predisposition [8], neurotransmitter dysregulation [9], altered immune functions [10–12], and disturbance of the gastrointestinal system [13, 14].

Links between depression and gastrointestinal disturbance suggest a role for the gut-brain axis in the development of mood disorders [15, 16]. Centrally, the corticolimbic system, and the prefrontal cortex (PFC) in particular, is implicated in depression [17, 18], as it is essential in emotional learning [19] and is involved in the modulation of the stress response via the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis [20]. In the intestine, the gut microbiome with its extensive functional potential [21] has been shown to mediate immune, endocrine, and neural responses, in part through sensing of microbial signals by components of the peripheral nervous system [22–24]. Among these, the vagus nerve constitutes a major communication pathway along the gut-brain axis, and microbiome shifts have been reported to alter its tone and impact on serotonin, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and dopamine neurotransmission, neuroinflammation, and digestion, ultimately affecting host behavior [25–28]. For example, studies in germ free animals have shown that the absence of a microbiome leads to sex dependent alterations in neurotransmitter production and inflammation which are associated with changes in animal behavior [29, 30]. Additionally, depressive-like behaviors can be transferred from humans to rodents via fecal microbiota transplant (FMT) [31, 32], and depressive-like phenotypes in rats can be rescued by using FMT derived from healthy animals [33], suggesting that the microbiome may be causal to these behaviors. We also previously found that brain signatures of depression – specifically hyperactivity of the PFC – negatively correlated with fecal levels of Bacteroides in a human cohort of Major Depressive Disorder [34]. Bacteroides are keystone human commensals, supporting host metabolism, regulating the immune system, and producing bioactive metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), K and B vitamins, and the neurotransmitter GABA [34–36].

Here, we tested whether oral administration of a human-derived GABA producing B. salyersiae strain, HB32, could ameliorate depression phenotypes in rats using the repeated social defeat (SD) paradigm. SD was chosen based on its ethological relevance and translational potential in mimicking physical and psychological triggers of human depression derived from disruptive social interactions, previously reported involvement of immune, HPA, and brain plasticity factors in the social stress response, and feasibility in our facility [37, 38]. In this model, rats are exposed to chronic social subordination and develop anhedonia-like behaviors, such as decreased sucrose preference [39–42], impaired reward learning [43], and deficits in brain reward system function [44, 45]. The effects of orally-delivered live or inactivated B. salyersiae HB32 microbiome interventions were compared to the drug ketamine, a well-known anesthetic recently approved as a novel treatment for depression [46, 47]. To support mechanistic assessment along the gut-brain axis, we leveraged vagotomy and multi-omics analysis of brain and fecal samples.

Methods

Subjects

Adult male Wistar rats (approximately 9 weeks old, n = 16/group) were used as experimental subjects (Inotiv Inc., formerly Envigo), and male and female Long Evans retired breeders (Charles River Laboratories) were used as residents for the SD procedure. Naïve unstressed control animals (n = 16) were pair-housed throughout the experiment in standard polycarbonate cages. Animals exposed to SD were housed individually in cages partitioned to also host a resident pair. All animals were kept under a 12 h reverse light–dark cycle (lights off between 7:00 AM and 7:00 PM) and received food and water ad libitum. Procedures were conducted in accordance with guidelines from the National Institutes of Health and the Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care and approved by the UC San Diego Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Humane endpoints were established per the IACUC-approved protocol, and animals were monitored daily for signs of pain, distress, or morbidity.

Interventions

The N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist ketamine hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich) was used as drug control. Ketamine was formulated in sterile saline and administered intraperitoneally (IP) at a subanesthetic dose of 10 mg/kg/day for 7 days.

B. salyersiae HB32 was previously isolated from a fecal sample of a healthy human adult male donor and maintained for long-term storage at −80 °C in 20% glycerol stocks. High density biomass of HB32 for the study was prepared as follows: glycerol stocks were streaked out on Brucella anaerobic agar plates (Anaerobe Systems) to isolate single colonies. Liquid cultures in Vegitone infusion broth (MilliporeSigma), supplemented with Yeast extract (Thermo Fisher Scientific), Vitamin K1-Hemin solution, L-Cysteine hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich), and MOPS buffer (Teknova) were inoculated from a single colony and grown anaerobically (5% H2, 10% CO2, and balanced with N2) at 37 °C in a Coy chamber (Coy Laboratory Products) to an OD600 of 3.5. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 30 min, washed with reduced phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), resuspended in 20% glycerol in PBS, aliquoted, and frozen at −80 °C until use. To prepare the inactivated HB32 intervention (iHB32), a subset of the HB32 biomass stocks underwent three cycles of heating and cooling (heated at 56 °C for 30 min and then cooled at room temperature for 30 min). These iHB32 aliquots were then frozen at −80 °C until use and lack of viability was confirmed via colony forming units (CFU) plating. Freshly, each day of treatment, HB32 and iHB32 stocks were thawed, diluted in reduced PBS, and delivered via oral gavage. Each animal received 1 × 109 bacterial cells/day (or the equivalent non-viable preparation for iHB32) for 7 days. Sterile PBS was used as oral vehicle control.

GABA production

B. salyersiae HB32 was anaerobically grown for 48 h in the supplemented Vegitone infusion broth liquid media with initial pH adjusted to 5.5, 6.0, 6.5, or 7.4. GABA concentration in the culture supernatants (n = 2 per pH condition) was quantified by LC–MS/MS analysis using GABA-d6 as internal standard (Drumetix Laboratories).

Repeated SD model

After two weeks of habituation, Wistar rats were randomly assigned to receive either SD or serve as unstressed naïve controls. Rats receiving SD (“intruders”) were transported to a separate vivarium room housing Long Evans rats (“residents”) selected for aggressive behavior. Each intruder was placed inside a cage with a resident pair (61 × 43 × 20 cm) behind a perforated Plexiglass partition physically separating the animals in two compartments, each with food and water available ad libitum. At 9:00 AM the following day, upon removal of the sex-mismatching member of the resident pair, the partitions were lifted to allow the sex-matching intruder/resident pair to come in contact. SD was defined as the intruder displaying a defensive, supine posture for 3 consecutive seconds. After SD or 3 min (whichever occurred first), intruders were transferred to a different cage hosting a new resident pair (separated by a partition) and remained there until the following day’s SD session. This was repeated for 21 days. Intruders were never paired with the same residents twice. On days 15–21 of SD administration, immediately after the daily SD session, stressed rats were administered either vehicle (SD-Vehicle), ketamine (SD-Ketamine), HB32 (SD-HB32), or iHB32 (SD-iHB32). Sucrose preference

On day 22, after the final SD session, stressed and unstressed rats were singly housed in standard polycarbonate cages in their respective vivarium rooms and provided with drinking bottles filled with 1% sucrose in water for approximately 20 h as a single source of drinking solution. The following morning, at 7:00 AM, the sucrose bottles were refilled and added back to each cage along with a bottle of standard drinking water. Baseline weight of both types of bottles was recorded. Twelve hours later, at 7:00 PM, the bottle positions in each cage were switched. The following morning, at 7:00 AM, the sucrose and water bottles were removed and weighed to assess consumption. Sucrose preference was calculated as the change in sucrose bottle weight over the 24 h test period divided by the total change in weight of the sucrose and water bottles. The behavioral assessment was completed on n = 16 animals in the Naïve, SD-Vehicle, and SD-HB32 groups, and on n = 14 animals in the SD-Ketamine and SD-iHB32 groups.

Pilot cohorts

Pilot studies were conducted prior to the interventional cohort to select relevant outcomes, group size, and target subject population displaying consistent stress-induced phenotypes in the SD model, which would then allow testing for the effects of the candidate interventions. In a pilot cohort, 8 male Wistar rats/group (Naïve, SD-Vehicle, SD-Ketamine) underwent SD, as previously described, and were subsequently assessed for anxiety-related behaviors via the elevated plus maze (EPM) test. Briefly, rats were placed in the center of a plus-shaped maze constituted of two open and two enclosed arms and were allowed to explore for 5 min. The time spent by each animal in either open or closed compartments was recorded. Additionally, 8 female Wistar rats/group (Naïve, SD-Vehicle, SD-Ketamine) underwent SD and were tested for sucrose preference. However, the SD procedure failed to induce increased anxiety in males (assessed via EPM test), and a behavioral phenotype in females (as per sucrose preference test, see Additional File 1: Fig. S1A and B, respectively). Therefore, the interventional study continued only with male animals and sucrose preference outcomes. Based on the results of the pilot studies and previous literature reports for the SD model [37, 38], group sizes for the interventional cohort described above were set at a minimum of n = 12 per condition. This cohort size was selected to ensure sufficient statistical power despite the unknown behavioral effect size of the microbial interventions. Additionally, the total number was adjusted to n = 16 to account for a predicted attrition rate (10–15%) resulting from potential procedural complications or health-related exclusions, ensuring the final analysis remained robust.

Fecal and tissue sample collection

Fecal samples were collected freshly from each rat on day 15 (pre-treatment) and on day 21 (post-treatment). To do so, rats were gently restrained in a towel, and fecal samples were collected in sterile cryogenic tubes and flash-frozen on dry ice. Upon completion of the behavioral assessment on day 23, euthanasia was performed by rapid decapitation without anesthesia. Whole brains were removed from the skull, rinsed in ice-cold PBS for approximately 30 s, and then sliced with a sterile straight-edge blade in a brain block. The prelimbic and infralimbic cortices were manually dissected and flash-frozen into sterile Eppendorf tubes on dry ice. All samples were stored at −80 °C until processing.

Vagotomy

In a separate cohort, male Wistar rats received either bilateral subdiaphragmatic vagotomy or sham surgery prior to SD and sucrose preference testing (Naïve, Sham n = 9; SD-Vehicle, Sham n = 10; Naïve, Vagotomy n = 6; SD-Vehicle, Vagotomy n = 10). During surgery, rats were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane (1–2%) and the abdominal area was shaved and cleaned using aseptic procedures. A small midline incision was made to expose the esophagus. In the vagotomy group, the ventral and dorsal truncal branches of the vagus nerve were severed, while in the sham surgery group, they were briefly manipulated with sterile forceps without damage. The abdominal muscles and skin were sutured, and animals were administered meloxicam (analgesic) and allowed to recover for one week before administration of SD and the sucrose preference test, as previously described [48].

Behavioral data analysis

Investigators were not blinded to group allocation during dosing and animal handling. However, all behavioral outcomes were based on objective weight-based measurements, and computational analyses were performed using automated pipelines. Sucrose preference results were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software). A Robust Outlier Identification (ROUT) test with a maximum desired false discovery rate (FDR) set at 1% was performed to identify potential outliers in the sucrose preference readout. For the main interventional cohort, two outliers were identified in the SD-Ketamine group, and three outliers in the SD-HB32 group. These were removed from the dataset prior to group comparison analysis via Ordinary One-way between-subjects ANOVA test followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. For the vagotomy cohort, no outliers were identified, and data were analyzed via Two-way between-subjects ANOVA test with Dunn–Šidák correction for multiple comparisons, using SD and surgery as independent variables. The threshold for significance was set at p < 0.05.

RNAseq sample processing and computational analysis

A subset of the rat PFC (combined left and right regions) was processed for RNA extraction, quantification, and quality control (QC) via Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and high-resolution automated electrophoresis TapeStation bioanalyzer (Agilent). Samples consisting of less than 2 mg of tissue, or that did not pass QC (acceptable metrics for RNA quality: RNA Integrity Number, RIN > 7.5, Degradation Value expressed as the percentage of RNA fragments greater than 200 nucleotides in length, DV200 > 70) were excluded from subsequent analyses, yielding n = 6 samples/group, except the SD-Ketamine group with n = 5 samples. Library preparation was performed after rRNA depletion and poly(A) RNA enrichment, and bulk sequencing done via Illumina HiSeq 2 × 150 bp (20–30 M reads/sample) (Azenta Life Sciences). The raw RNAseq datasets generated in the current study have been deposited in the NCBI BioProject repository (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA1299465) [49]. The integrated Differential Expression & Pathway analysis (iDEP, South Dakota State University) version 0.96 was used as RNAseq processing and analytical pipeline [50], with the Rattus norvegicus Rnor_6.0 assembly as reference genome (GenBank GCA_000001895.4). One sample in the SD-HB32 group was identified as a significant outlier via heatmap clustering and principal component analysis (PCA) and removed from subsequent analysis. Within the iDEP pipeline, the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were determined using DESeq2 [51], and analysis of pathways enriched in the DEGs was performed via mapping against the Gene Ontology (GO) Biological Process, and Molecular Function databases [52, 53]. Significance was set at adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05, and a Log2 fold change of ± 0.5.

Fecal metagenomic sequencing

Metagenomic sequencing of fecal samples from the interventional SD cohort was performed at the Microbiome Core of the University of California, San Diego, using previously published protocols [54]. Briefly, DNA extraction and purification were performed using the MagMAX Microbiome Ultra Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit and automated on KingFisher Flex robots (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Blank controls and mock communities (Zymo Research Corporation) were included as controls per each extraction plate and carried through all downstream processing steps. Input DNA was quantified using a PicoGreen fluorescence assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and metagenomic libraries were prepared with KAPA HyperPlus kits (Roche Diagnostics) following manufacturer's instructions and automated on EpMotion automated liquid handlers (Eppendorf). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform with paired-end 150 bp cycles at the Institute for Genomic Medicine, UC San Diego. The metagenomic datasets generated in the current study have been deposited in the NCBI BioProject repository (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA1299465) [49].

Metagenomic data analysis

Paired-end raw sequencing reads were subjected to QC using SHI7 (shi7_trimmer v0.92 g) [55]. Palindromic adapters (Illumina prep/TruSeq v3) were removed, read ends were trimmed (FLOOR 3 mode, Q12 quality filter), and low-quality reads were filtered out (< Q20 or < 75 bp). Samples with insufficient read counts post-QC (< 50,000) or lacking either one of the two timepoints (pre- or post-treatment) were excluded. The final number of animals in the analysis with acceptable metagenomic data for two timepoints is as follows: Naïve n = 12, SD-Vehicle n = 14, SD-Ketamine n = 13, SD-HB32 n = 16, and SD-iHB32 n = 13. Quality-controlled paired-end reads were then processed with XTree v2.00b [56] for taxonomic profiling using the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) R220 reference genome database and utilized the CAPITALIST [57] redistribution method as well as the coverage output modes and the –half-forage option to retain reads with preferable cross-mapping characteristics for redistribution (median post-QC and mapped reads = 5,287,096 pairs, with 87% of reads mapped). Statistical analyses were performed in R v4.5.0. Taxa with greater than 1 observation dataset-wide of at least 0.01% relative abundance or 1% proportion of genomic coverage were retained. For statistical tests, zero-count reads were imputed with a pseudo-count of 0.5 before sum normalization and Log10 transformation. The overall community structure (beta diversity) post-treatment (D21) was assessed via PCA performed on the Log10-transformed relative abundance data. To identify the components that maximized separation between experimental groups, Kruskal–Wallis tests were performed on the coordinates of each principal component; the two axes exhibiting the lowest p-values were selected for visualization in addition to the first two axes. Convex hulls were calculated using the chull function. Alpha diversity was quantified as species richness, defined as the total count of unique taxa present at a relative abundance greater than 0.0001 within a sample. Changes in alpha diversity and the abundance of specific taxa between timepoints were visualized using boxplots; the latter was additionally overlaid with jittered data points (stripchart function). ANOVA with Tukey's HSD for post-hoc group testing was used for post-treatment between-group comparisons, and Welch's t-tests in paired mode were run for within-group pre- vs post-treatment comparisons. P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR where appropriate, with a reporting threshold of < 0.25.

Results

Bacteroides salyersiae HB32 attenuates depressive-like behaviors induced by repeated social defeat, at levels comparable to ketamine

We previously observed that altered brain functions in subjects with depression were correlated with a microbiome signature of reduced relative abundance of GABA producing fecal Bacteroides species [34]. Here, we first confirmed that B. salyersiae HB32 could produce GABA in vitro at physiologically relevant pH for the human intestinal tract (Fig. 1B) [34], and then tested whether oral supplementation of this human isolate could ameliorate depression phenotypes in the SD preclinical model of chronic stress. The behavioral impact of repeated exposure to SD was assessed using the sucrose preference test as a measure of anhedonia, a core feature of depression [58] (Fig. 1C). Upon 3 weeks of SD, male animals showed a significantly decreased preference of sucrose over drinking water as compared to control animals that did not undergo SD (SD-Vehicle versus Naïve, adj. p = 0.0143, Mean Difference, MD = −11.45, 95% Confidence Interval, CI [−21.28, −1.626]) (Fig. 1C), indicating the model induced anhedonia. However, we found the SD model had no significant impact on anxiety behaviors in males, or in female rats (Additional File 1: Fig. S1A and B, respectively), therefore interventional experiments were only performed in males evaluated for anhedonia outcomes. Defeated animals displayed a reduction in weight gain as compared to naïve controls, an expected physiological response to social stress [59], which was already evident pre-treatment on day 15 (p = 0.0219) and was sustained until day 21 of SD (SD-Vehicle versus Naïve, adj. p < 0.0001, MD = −18.31, 95% CI [−26.74, −9.889]) (Fig. 1D). A 7-day IP treatment with the positive control ketamine improved the anhedonia phenotype compared to controls (SD-Ketamine versus SD-Vehicle, adj. p < 0.0001, MD = 19.69, 95% CI [9.079, 30.30]) (Fig. 1C). Similarly, administration of 1 × 109 viable HB32 cells, or an inactivated HB32 preparation (iHB32) exhibited reversal of the anhedonia phenotype to naïve levels (SD-HB32 versus SD-Vehicle, adj. p = 0.002, MD = 17.01, 95% CI [6.638, 27.39]; SD-iHB32 versus SD-Vehicle, adj. p = 0.0043, MD = 13.30, 95% CI [3.131, 23.47]; SD-HB32 or SD-iHB32 versus Naïve, adj. p = 0.5639, MD = 5.563, 95% CI [−4.813, 15.94], and adj. p = 0.9861, MD = 1.849, 95% CI [−8.320, 12.02], respectively, not significant), with a comparable effect to ketamine (SD-HB32 or SD-iHB32 versus SD-Ketamine, adj. p = 0.9611, MD = −2.677, 95% CI [−13.80, 8.447], and adj. p = 0.4780, MD = −6.391, 95% CI [−17.32, 4.541], respectively, not significant). No statistically significant differences were observed in the absolute total fluid consumption across experimental groups, confirming that the fluid intake, overall health status, and motivational state of the animals did not act as confounding variables influencing the sucrose preference outcome (average ± standard deviation of total fluid intakes in grams, Naïve = 51.96 ± 8.24; SD-Vehicle = 46.93 ± 4.57; SD-Ketamine = 46.90 ± 8.70; SD-HB32 = 47.65 ± 7.66; SD-iHB32 = 49.72 ± 14.12; not significant by One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, p = 0.8446, F-statistic = 0.3470; Dfnum = 4; Dfden = 43). Finally, no additional weight loss was detected upon administration of ketamine, HB32, or iHB32 on days 15 to 21 as compared to the pre-treatment day 15 baseline and the SD-Vehicle control (Fig. 1D), further suggesting interventions were well tolerated. Compared to the SD-Vehicle group, a trend towards attenuated weight loss was observed in the ketamine, HB32, and iHB32 groups on days 19 to 21; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Development of depressive-like behaviors in the social defeat paradigm requires an intact vagus nerve

Considering these results, we sought to determine whether the behavioral changes observed in the SD model were dependent on the vagus nerve – a major route of communication between the gut microbiome and the brain, previously shown to be important for the development of depressive-like behavior in other models [28, 60]. In a separate cohort of male rats, animals were subjected to vagotomy or sham surgery prior to three weeks of SD and sucrose preference testing (Fig. 2A). Exposure to social defeat alters the prefrontal cortex transcriptional profile, which can be restored by HB32 supplementation

Given the implications of the PFC in depression [19], and its responses to chronic stress and microbiome perturbations [34, 61, 62], we performed RNAseq and transcriptome analysis of the PFC in a subset of animals in our interventional cohort. Overall, we observed moderate but significant transcriptional changes, with PCA revealing separation of treatment groups in two main clusters: one enriched in SD-HB32, SD-iHB32, and Naïve samples, and the other enriched in SD-Vehicle and SD-Ketamine samples (Additional File 2: Fig. S2). This was also reflected in the differential expression (Fig. 3, and Additional File 3: Table S1A-G) and pathway analyses based on GO Biological Process and Molecular Function reference databases (Additional File 4: Table S2A-G, and Additional File 5: Table S3A-G). Using the SD-Vehicle stress group as baseline for comparison, we detected a total of 29 significant DEGs in the Naïve group (Fig. 3A), while 81 DEGs and 403 DEGs were found in the SD-HB32 and the SD-iHB32 groups, respectively (Fig. 3B, C). On the contrary, no significant DEGs or GO enriched pathways were detected in the SD-Ketamine to SD-Vehicle comparison (Fig. 3D, Additional File 3: Table S1D, Additional File 4: Table S2D, Additional File 5: Table S3D), suggesting that the antidepressant effect of ketamine in this model may not be mediated by transcriptional changes in the PFC. Notably, no genes were differentially expressed in either B. salyersiae groups when compared to Naïve (Fig. 3E, F), or when comparing SD-HB32 to SD-iHB32 (Additional File 3: Table S1G), indicating that supplementation of HB32 can broadly normalize stress-induced PFC transcriptomic changes in the model, independently of the strain being viable. When looking at specific functions, we observed that exposure to stress caused downregulation of the adrenergic receptor Adrb1 [63] (Fig. 3A), and of several factors involved in dopaminergic functions, glial proliferation control, and neuronal cell differentiation (e.g. Mdk, Prim1, Arpc1b, Metrn) (Additional File 3: Table S1A) [64–66]. Similar factors were also affected upon B. salyersiae administration, especially by the iHB32 inactivated formulation which overall had the broadest differential transcriptional impact compared to the SD-Vehicle model control, both at the gene and pathway level (Fig. 3C, Additional File 4: Table S2C, and Additional File 5: Table S3C). Amelioration of depressive-like behaviors correlates with intervention-specific attenuation of stress-induced microbiome changes

Perturbation in the microbiota-gut-brain axis has been implicated in a variety of mood disorders, including stress, anxiety, and depression [25, 78]. As such, we asked whether the observed behavioral effects and brain transcriptional signatures were associated with changes in the gut microbiota. We performed fecal shotgun metagenomic analysis of samples collected pre- and post-treatment (days 15 and 21, respectively) in stressed animals as well as the naïve controls (Fig. 1A). Microbiome beta diversity analysis by PCA on day 21 did not reveal major differences in the overall post-treatment community structure across groups (Fig. 4A). Once performed using variables selected for high discriminatory power (via the Kruskal–Wallis test), the PCA showed a separation between SD groups and the naïve subjects (Fig. 4B). This difference is likely attributable to the distinct housing, handling, and stress conditions experienced by the naïve controls. Similarly, no changes in alpha diversity by species richness were detected across all groups and timepoints (Fig. 4C). However, by comparing within-group changes at the taxa level between the pre- and post-treatment timepoints of the SD-Vehicle group (Additional File 6: Table S4A), we found that prolonged exposure to chronic SD (3 weeks versus 2 weeks) caused a microbiome perturbation characterized by more than 100 species showing significant changes in relative abundance (adj. p < 0.20), with the most significant being a decrease in endogenous Bacteroides stercoris (adj. p = 0.001) (Fig. 4D). All behavior-modulating interventions were able to attenuate SD-induced microbiome shifts while inducing intervention-specific microbial signatures (Additional File 6: Table S4A). Ketamine treatment primarily caused a relative increase in two Lepagella species (L. sp. 902494595, L. sp. 910584525; adj. p = 0.011). Interestingly, B. salyersiae administration induced distinct shifts reflecting the presence or absence of viable bacteria in the formulation: the post-treatment SD-iHB32 microbiome was characterized by increased relative abundance of Vermiculatibacterium sp. 910577415 (adj. p = 0.018) and Lactococcus muris (adj. p = 0.036) taxa, while the live SD-HB32 microbiome showed a significant increase in B. salyersiae (adj. p = 0.0032) (Fig. 4D), with only marginal evidence for other changes (e.g., decrease in Eubacterium_R sp. 910577195, adj. p = 0.095). Of note, B. salyersiae was the third most significantly different taxon across all groups post-treatment (ANOVA adj. p = 1.68 x 10-8; F-statistic = 21.128; Dfnum = 4; Dfden = 63), an effect driven exclusively by its relative abundance in the live SD-HB32 group, indicating supplementation specificity (Additional File 6: Table S4B). Discussion

A growing body of literature has identified the microbiota-gut-brain axis as a key mediator of responses to chronic stress and development of stress-related disorders, including depression and anxiety [26]. Preclinically, exposure to stress has been shown to alter the composition and function of the intestinal microbiota and in turn promote colitis and immune responses [79], development of food allergies [80], tryptophan-derived pathological metabolic pathways [81], and dysfunctional gut barrier and neurotransmission [28, 82], ultimately contributing to mood imbalance. Further, in animal models disruption of gut homeostasis by infection or antibiotic treatment, can heighten stress and visceral pain responses [83, 84], leading to neurological and behavioral disorders. Similar observations have been made in humans – stress and antibiotic use, but not antiviral or antifungal treatment, can increase risk of depression [15, 85, 86]. Combined, this suggests a disrupted microbiome could be causal to mood disorders in some cases, and signals comprised in the gut microbiota are sufficient to transfer and define behaviors in the recipient host even across taxonomy orders (i.e. humans to rodents) [31–33].

Importantly, there is accumulating clinical evidence that stress and depressive-like behavior can be improved by targeting the microbiome. This includes human trials leveraging FMT [87–90], FMT-like therapeutics [91], diet [92–94], and conventional probiotics [95–99]. However, FMT is difficult to scale, comes with uncertainty of its constituents, and has risks [100]. Diet appears to have variable effects within people, likely driven by variation in the microbiome, and can be difficult to adhere to [101]. Likewise, conventional probiotics contain a small minority of the taxonomic and functional diversity of the microbiome (mainly Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium), which are put in products largely due to their convenience and availability [102, 103]. It is likely the most promising microbiome interventions will be precision-based next-generation probiotics, containing symbionts evolved to co-exist and support humans.

The intervention tested in this study, B. salyersiae HB32, is a human commensal next-generation probiotic candidate. In a previous study we showed that naturally occurring Bacteroides were under-represented in the intestinal tract of subjects with depression [34]. Other groups supported these findings in cohorts of anxiety and depression [32, 104–106]. For example one study found prolonged physiological stress was associated with a reduction in gut Bacteroides and with deterioration of intestinal and immune health independently of the diet [85]. Members of the Bacteroides genus have been broadly reported to modulate the immune response [107–109], synthesize B and other vitamins [36], and produce active metabolites which may modulate behavior, such as GABA [9, 34, 110, 111]. Therefore, we tested the hypothesis that supplementation with B. salyersiae HB32, a strain capable of producing GABA at physiologically relevant pH of the human gastrointestinal tract and isolated from a healthy adult human donor, could safely support management of stress-induced depression. To do so, we utilized the rat SD model, in which repeated social conflict served as chronic stressor. Ketamine was chosen as drug control to assess predictive validity of the SD model [47, 112, 113]. Similarly to previous reports [42], the SD model effectively replicated components of the human stress response, leading to measurable depressive behaviors in the sucrose preference test. We found ketamine, as well as live and inactivated HB32, ameliorated these depressive-like symptoms. However, the anhedonia phenotype was only observed in male rats, limiting our ability to assess utility of HB32 in females, which may require a larger cohort size to capture more subtle changes, or a different behavioral testing method.

Several observations highlighted the relevance of the microbiome and the gut-brain axis for the SD-induced phenotype. First, we demonstrated the dependence of the anhedonia phenotypes on the vagus nerve. This aligns with existing literature according to which vagal afferents are essential for gut-brain crosstalk, HPA functionality, stress behaviors, and resolution [114–118]. Numerous reports have also shown that the vagus nerve is necessary to capture the bidirectional effects of the intestinal microbiota on the brain, including preclinical studies using similar stress models of depression and clinical trials in patients with depression where microbial-based interventions were administered in the form of FMT or probiotics supplements [25, 28, 60, 119, 120]. The same could be true for HB32. Importantly, our finding that vagotomy abolished the SD-induced anhedonia phenotype demonstrates that intact vagal signaling is required for this stress response to manifest, providing indirect support for vagal involvement in gut-brain communication in this model. However, because vagotomy eliminated the phenotype entirely, we could not directly test whether HB32 or ketamine exert their effects via vagal pathways. Future research may consider alternative methods to alter the vagus, such as mechanical or chemical partial ablation.

Second, using shotgun fecal metagenomic sequencing, we observed mild but relevant impacts of chronic stress and interventions on the resident microbiota. One of the most significant microbiome changes caused by three weeks of SD exposure was a reduction of Bacteroides stercoris, corroborating our original hypothesis and previous studies in mice showing reduction of Bacteroides abundance upon stress [121]. Intervention-specific changes in taxa relative abundance were similarly informative. For instance, ketamine treatment primarily caused a relative increase in two rodent Lepagella species, which belong to the Bacteroidales order. Importantly, HB32 supplementation did not significantly alter the overall microbiome structure and diversity as compared to the stress model control, with observed taxonomic shifts largely dominated by a few key low-abundance species. Among those, only live HB32 (but not iHB32) resulted in a significant increase of B. salyersiae as assessed by shotgun sequencing, suggesting successful engraftment, at least in the short term, without collateral disturbance to the resident intestinal flora.

Informed by our prior study connecting lower fecal Bacteroides abundance to PFC functional MRI signatures in humans with depression [34], we looked at the PFC transcriptome to investigate central components of stress and intervention responses in our model. Although milder than other rodent studies, the RNAseq analysis revealed PFC changes induced by SD in glial cell cycle activation (e.g. higher Prim1, Ncapg in SD-Vehicle compared to Naïve) and suppression of dendritic‑spine actin remodeling mechanisms (e.g. Arpc1b, Yap1) [61, 122]. This is consistent with previous reports pointing at glial proliferation and neuronal structural and plasticity weakening as the basis of chronic stress-induced cognitive disorders [123, 124]. Also, even though all interventions successfully reversed anhedonia, we found that the PFC transcriptomic signature of chronic stress was counteracted by HB32 and iHB32 supplementation, but not by ketamine administration, implying HB32 and ketamine may act via distinct mechanisms. Previous reports have seen PFC and hippocampus shifts in response to IP ketamine in stress and depression preclinical studies [125–129], suggesting that, at least in our model, the antidepressant action of ketamine may have been directed at synaptic or neurogenetic activities rather than changes in gene expression, or at different components of the corticolimbic system like the hippocampus, or at specific cellular subsets and therefore it was overlooked in the transcriptional analysis performed in bulk rather than at the single-cell level [122, 126, 130]. Interestingly, B. salyersiae in both viable and non-viable formulations, resulted in a PFC transcriptomic profile that was not significantly different from that observed in naïve animals. However, when comparing transcriptional effects to stress control animals (SD-Vehicle), iHB32 impacted a broader set of stress-induced DEGs and pathways as compared to HB32, indicating that the inactivation of HB32 may expose the host to a larger number of microbial stimuli. Still, both interventions contributed to a set of core adaptive quiescence and defense signals, consistent with halting stress‑associated glial proliferation (e.g. lower Ncapg) and stress hormone activation, and stabilization of redox and mitochondrial homeostasis in the PFC parenchyma. For instance, several of the overlapping pathways affected by the HB32 and iHB32 interventions as compared to the SD-Vehicle control included those involved in antioxidant defense and immune responses (e.g. Mt2A, Gpx3 or Gpx1, Cd46, Sh2d1a), the neuroprotective integrated stress response (e.g. higher Atf5) and melanocortin (e.g. lower Mc4r) systems, and neurotransmission, including serotonergic pathways (Npff, Gpr171 or Adora2a, Htr2b). Dysregulation of several of these functions has been previously associated with mood disorders, anxiety, repetitive and feeding behaviors, and fear conditioning [67–77, 131, 132]. Specifically, in clinically depressed patients, chronic stress has been linked to elevation of monoamine oxidase activity and neuroinflammation [133, 134] and a reduction of antioxidant defenses like glutathione [135], which can persist even after treatment [136]. We also found changes in serotonin pathways involved in the regulation of mood, sleep, and appetite [137, 138]. Serotonin signaling has been previously shown to be modulated by the microbiome, with germ free mice exhibiting anxiety-like behaviors and altered serotonin levels in the brain [29]. Vagal stimulation by the gut microbiota has also been reported to alter brain concentrations of neurotransmitters, including serotonin, GABA and glutamate [139, 140].

Given the observed iHB32 effect, B. salyersiae-derived amelioration of stress-induced anhedonia in this model is likely mediated by specific metabolites or structural components still present in the iHB32 biomass, rather than solely by the activity of live HB32 cells in the intestine. Although further studies will be needed to decipher the mechanistic contribution of HB32 strain-specific byproducts, several factors compatible with B. salyersiae genetic potential could be at play. Members of the genus Bacteroides are known to express anti-inflammatory variants of the gram-negative lipopolysaccharide that can dampen the overall microbiota-derived Toll-like receptor 4 signaling and support local and systemic immune balance [141]. Additionally, they support production of SCFA from the fermentation of complex polysaccharides, and vitamin biosynthesis, contributing to gut, metabolic, and immune health [35, 36, 142]. Finally, as mentioned before, Bacteroides synthetize a variety of neuromodulatory metabolites [108, 143], affecting peripheral and central GABAergic and serotonergic neuron excitability and activity [34, 144–146].

Limitations of the study

Despite the compelling evidence supporting B. salyersiae HB32 potential as a next-generation probiotic or postbiotic, it is important to acknowledge certain study limitations. A primary constraint is the absence of observable stress-induced behavioral phenotypes in female rats within our SD experimental setting. Future studies employing alternative stressors, such as exposure to male odorants or chronic social instability, may more reliably induce depressive-like behaviors in females, allowing for the validation of HB32 translatability across biological sexes. Further, our data established that an intact vagus nerve is a prerequisite for the manifestation of SD-induced anhedonia. However, the lack of sucrose preference changes in vagotomized animals limited our understanding of the relative contribution of vagal stimulation versus systemic circulation of bioactive metabolites in mediating HB32 beneficial effects during stress. The use of subdiaphragmatic deafferentation, which selectively blocks sensory vagal inputs, or pharmacological inhibitors after stress may better support isolation of cellular and molecular pathways affected by HB32 supplementation. Similarly, we have observed successful engraftment of live HB32 and behavioral rescue with both live and inactivated forms. The comparable behavioral outcomes of B. salyersiae HB32 formulations suggest that the observed effects are at least in part mediated by heat-stable cellular components or metabolites. However, future investigations are necessary to identify and compare the bioactive components and the respective molecular pathways underlying the probiotic and postbiotic effects of HB32 supplementation. A deeper mechanistic understanding would benefit from the integration of serum and fecal metabolomics longitudinal studies to better decipher the mechanisms behind HB32 local and peripheral physiological effects.

Conclusions

Overall, this study demonstrates that a human commensal B. salyersiae strain, HB32, can mitigate depressive-like behavior in a rodent model of chronic stress, and is associated with alterations of the PFC and changes in the gut microbiome. This ability remains intact regardless of bacterial viability, suggesting the potential for HB32 development as a next-generation probiotic or as a postbiotic. Our study also supports evidence that the vagus nerve is a key mediator of stress responses and mood balance along the gut-brain axis. More broadly, this builds upon growing literature that targeting the gut microbiome is a promising interventional strategy for mental health disorders.
