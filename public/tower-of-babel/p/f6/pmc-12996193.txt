Urethral diverticulum complicated by multiple calculi secondary to urethral mesh erosion: A case report
Authors: Maria Papastamatiou, Iakovos Theodoulidis, Pantelis Trompoukis, Sofia Papaioannou, Grigoris F. Grimbizis, Themistoklis Mikos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The case of a 65-year-old woman with a complication 15 years after mesh repair of vaginal prolapse is presented. The patient presented with chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs), foul-smelling vaginal discharge, and urinary frequency/urgency for the previous eight years. Magnetic resonance imaging led to the diagnosis of vaginal mesh erosion and a urethral diverticular calculus secondary to urethral mesh erosion. Transvaginal removal of the mesh erosion was performed with simultaneous exploration of the urethral diverticulum, removal of a urethral stone, and urethral reconstruction using a multi-layer approach. Three months later she was symptom-free and continent. This case adds to the limited literature on urethral diverticulum with calculi secondary to mesh erosion, and highlights the need for long-term follow-up and clinician awareness of this rare, delayed complication.

Introduction

Urethral diverticulum (UD) is a relatively uncommon pathological entity in women, characterized by a localized outpouching of the urethral mucosa into the surrounding periurethral tissues. The outpouching can form anywhere along the urethra and is most common at the mid or distal third of the urethra [1]. The estimated prevalence ranges from 1% to 6% among women presenting with lower urinary tract symptoms, although the condition is likely underdiagnosed owing to its often subtle and nonspecific presentation [2]. Common symptoms include dysuria, dyspareunia, post-void dribbling, and recurrent urinary tract infections, which may overlap with other lower urinary tract disorders, leading to diagnostic delay. Accurate diagnosis of UD requires a high index of suspicion and appropriate imaging. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is considered the gold standard method. Other modalities, including transvaginal or transperineal ultrasonography, voiding cystourethrography, or double-balloon retrograde urethrography, may also aid in diagnosis [3].

The occurrence of UD following placement of a synthetic mid-urethral sling (MUS) for stress urinary incontinence (SUI) is very low. Factors such as menopause, elevated body mass index (BMI), recurrent urinary tract infections (UTI), autoimmune conditions, or previous pelvic floor operations appear to mediate as conditions that predispose to a diminished mesh biocompatibility and UD [1]. While mesh erosion into the urethra has been reported as a complication of MUS procedures, such erosion following transvaginal mesh placement for pelvic organ prolapse is less common but can have similarly severe sequelae [4].

Stone formation within a urethral diverticulum is exceedingly rare, estimated to occur in less than 10% of patients with urethral diverticula, and is generally attributed to urinary stasis, infection, or the presence of a foreign body acting as a nidus for mineral deposition [5]. Chronic inflammation and mechanical irritation from eroded mesh may predispose to urethral injury, diverticular formation, and secondary calculus. An important diagnostic indication of a calculus in the diverticulum is the finding of a palpable hard mass on the floor of the urethra.

We describe a rare case of a urethral diverticulum containing multiple calculi secondary to erosion of a transvaginal prolapse repair mesh into the urethra. This report underscores the importance of recognizing mesh-related complications as a potential cause of lower urinary tract symptoms and highlights the diagnostic and surgical challenges encountered in such complex presentations.

Case Presentation

A 65-year-old woman (gravida 3, para 3) was examined in the outpatient clinic complaining of chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs), foul-smelling vaginal discharge, dysuria, and voiding frequency for the previous eight years. Her medical history included chronic medication for coronary heart disease, hypertension and hypothyroidism, along with multiple courses of antibiotics for UTIs during the last 4 years. She had undergone prolapse surgery 15 years previously; the operation included a transvaginal mesh-kit repair for stage III cystocele.

On clinical examination a mesh erosion was diagnosed at mid-line of the anterior vaginal wall and a tender, bulky urethra. Asymptomatic stage 2 anterior wall prolapse was diagnosed at POP-Q examination (Aa = +1, Ba = +1, C = -3, gh = 4, pb = 2, tvl = 10, Ap = 0, Bp = 0, D = -3). Pelvic floor ultrasound indicated an area of low echogenicity under the urethra. Further diagnostic evaluation, including MRI, revealed a large calculus underneath the urethra.

Following multidisciplinary evaluation, the patient was scheduled for transvaginal mesh excision and removal of the stone from the area under the urethra. After she had given informed consent, the patient was operated under regional anesthesia in the lithotomy position, and a 16G indwelling bladder catheter was inserted. Initially, the mesh was grasped and under tension an attempt was made to isolate it from the vaginal tissues. A piece of meshapproximately 3 by 4 cm, was removed from the anterior vaginal wall. The originally inserted mesh was a 4-arm kit and the distant parts of the mesh through to the arms were not excised but left within the lateral paravaginal pelvic side walls. The vaginal wound was closed with Vicryl 1.0 interrupted sutures.

Next, a mid-line incision was performed at the level of the urethra. An egg-shaped stone 3 cm diameter was discovered. It appeared to be encapsulated by an epithelial layer. The initial attempt to remove it was unsuccessful as it was found to be anchored at both sides. The vaginal incision was therefore opened further and a mesh string was recognized bilaterally arising from the lateral poles of the stone, indicating that the stone was formed around and secondary to the mesh (Fig. 1a – 1f). The stone was removed, but upon its removal a 10 by 10 mm communication of the capsule of the stone with the urethra was recognized, indicating that the stone was formed within a urethral diverticulum created secondary to urethral mesh erosion (Fig. 2). The surgical field was washed with copious saline, and multiple biopsies were taken from the urethral diverticular walls. The closure of the diverticulum was performed in layers: initially interrupted 3.0 Vicryl sutures were placed at the level of the neck of the diverticulum. Then, the fibromuscular walls of the diverticulum were used in an overlapping technique, in order to create a multilayer thick wall above the urethral gap. Finally, after adequate mobilization, the overlying vaginal epithelium was closed with interrupted sutures. A 20G indwelling bladder catheter was placed. Methylene blue was used to confirm that the closure was watertight. The blood loss was less than 100 ml. Peri-operative antibiotics and analgesia were administered.

There were no immediate post-operative complications, and the patient was discharged 48 h later with instruction to remove the Foley catheter 20 days later. At one-month post-operative follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of vaginal discharge, with no evidence of symptom recurrence or de novo lower urinary tract symptoms. The methylene blue test was repeated and there was no evidence of leakage. Histology of the biopsies returned showing no malignancy. Three months later she was symptom free and continent.

Discussion

In the present case, a transvaginal mesh for prolapse repair was complicated by erosion and formation of a urethral diverticulum, which was further complicated by a large intra-diverticular stone. Urethral diverticulum is an uncommon condition in women, and complications such as infection, obstruction, and calculus formation remain relatively rare. Mesh-related erosion into the urethra has been described; however, most published cases involve mid-urethral sling (MUS) procedures for stress urinary incontinence, where intraluminal exposure of synthetic fibres can serve as a nidus for stone formation [1], [6], [7], [8]. In contrast, urethral erosion following mesh repair of pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is exceedingly uncommon, and reports of subsequent diverticulum formation with calculi are limited to isolated cases. Because POP mesh is positioned along the anterior vaginal wall rather than at the mid-urethra, direct urethral involvement represents an unusual and unexpected late complication. The present case illustrates this rare phenomenon, with progressive mesh migration into the urethral lumen ultimately resulting in the development of a urethral diverticulum and a large intra-diverticular calculus.

The considerable size of the calculus suggests a longstanding pathological process. Chronic mechanical friction, incomplete tissue integration of the mesh, localized ischemia, and low-grade infection likely contributed to gradual urethral erosion. Once the mesh entered the urethra, it functioned as a persistent foreign body, promoting mineral deposition and progressive stone enlargement. Late-onset mesh complications, as in this case, may present with nonspecific urinary symptoms, including recurrent infections, pelvic discomfort, or persistent lower urinary tract symptoms. Because these symptoms are often subtle, diagnosis may be delayed unless there is a high index of suspicion. Women with a history of pelvic reconstructive surgery should therefore undergo thorough evaluation when presenting with new urinary complaints, regardless of the time elapsed since their initial operation [2], [9].

A few questions arise regarding the surgical technique in this case. First, the diverticular wall was not completely excised. There is literature indicating the risk of malignancy in urethral diverticula; therefore, several authors suggest complete excision of the diverticular wall [9]. However, in this case the urethral reconstruction was performed with the overlapping apposition of the urethral muscularis to thicken the posterior urethral wall and minimize incontinence.

Second, a concurrent anti-incontinence procedure was not undertaken, specifically the insertion of an autologous mid-urethral sling. Although there are reports of a fascial sling or cadaveric fascia being used to complete the surgical management of the excision of a UD secondary to MUS [6], [9], the present case was complicated by the presence of the stone and chronic inflammation, and so any SUI procedure was left for a second approach, if needed.

Third, a martius flap could benefit the urethral wall. The fact that a simultaneous mesh erosion was excised through another vaginal incision was a discouraging factor due to the fear of increased vaginal scarring in the setting, in the present case, of chronic infection [2].

Last, the choice to make a repair via the vaginal route is controversial in these cases, where the endoscopic/abdominal approach offers certain advantages, too [10]. Endoscopy (laparoscopic/robotic) appears to be as advantageous to the patient as the vaginal approach, offering minimal trauma and quick recovery with non-significant post-operative pain. However, although the option of total mesh removal is not feasible when operating vaginally, the reduction of intraperitoneal organ manipulation, the proximity to the surgical area of interest, and the use of regional-only anesthesia, without compromising the post-operative results, make the vaginal route a viable alternative [11]. Moreover, although synthetic MUS are under increased scrutiny in many countries due to concerns over patient safety, the use of transvaginal prolapse mesh-kits is restricted in the research setting because of the significant port-operative burden, including mesh-related complications, erosions, new onset of LUTs, and overall poor quality of life [12], [13].

In conclusion, a rare complication of mesh repair of vaginal prolapse involving the secondary appearance of a urinary calculus inside a urethral diverticulum after urethral mesh erosion is presented. The definitive management of such cases requires complete excision of the eroded mesh and diverticulum, followed by multilayer urethral reconstruction. Despite surgical challenges related to scarring and chronic inflammation, outcomes are generally favorable when performed by experienced surgeons. This case adds to the limited literature on urethral diverticulum with calculi secondary to POP mesh erosion, and highlights the need for long-term follow-up and clinician awareness of this rare, delayed complication.
