The Epidemiological Profile of Older Adults of Pacific Peoples Ethnicity in New Zealand Assessed for Home Support and Aged Residential Care
Authors: Xaviour Walker, Rebecca Abey‐Nesbit, Julia Kayes, Hans Ulrich Bergler, Gary Cheung, Debra Waters, Corina Grey, Marise Stuart, Hamish Jamieson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo provide a descriptive analysis of the overall health and well‐being of older adults of Pacific Peoples ethnicity using the interRAI‐Home Care assessments, compared with NZ Europeans.MethodsA cross‐sectional retrospective analysis was conducted of interRAI‐Home Care assessments assessed between 5 July 2016 and 31 December 2020. Basic descriptive information was gathered from Pacific Peoples and NZ Europeans separately. The frequency and percentage of the health conditions of interest were reported. Binary and multinomial logistic regression models adjusted for age and sex were used to investigate any differences between the prevalence of health conditions.ResultsA total of 123,023 interRAI‐HC assessments were examined, of which 5390 (4%) were Pacific Peoples and 117,633 (96%) NZ Europeans. The mean age of Pacific Peoples was 75.8 years (SD 8.4) and NZ Europeans 82.6 years (SD 7.5). After adjusting for age and sex, Pacific Peoples were less likely to smoke, consume alcohol, have coronary heart disease, fall, report loneliness or have difficulty hearing than NZ Europeans. In contrast, Pacific Peoples were more likely to have diabetes, cognitive impairment, congestive heart failure, require a mobility aid or be bed bound, experience bladder incontinence, have difficulty understanding others or have poorer vision than NZ Europeans.ConclusionsOlder Pacific Peoples who were assessed for home support and aged residential care have more complex needs and require services at an earlier age than NZ Europeans. There is a need for improvements in equity in the health system and underlying social determinants of health to raise the earlier age and lower the high burden of chronic diseases that Pacific Peoples face.Keywords: aged, big data, geriatric assessment, home care services, Pacific Island people

Introduction

Pacific Peoples are a growing and diverse population in New Zealand. With more than 17 different Pacific ethnic groups in New Zealand, Pacific peoples comprise 9% of the New Zealand population [1] compared to NZ Europeans, the largest ethnic group in New Zealand, Pacific Peoples have a disproportionately higher burden of health disparities and 4–5 years less life expectancy [2]. Pacific Peoples have a higher burden and incidence of chronic diseases such as diabetes, ischaemic heart disease and stroke than the rest of New Zealand's population [2].

As the growth of this older population continues to increase, research aimed at understanding their health is essential for best practice going forward. New Zealand's health system endeavours to support people with chronic disease to remain living in their own home for as long as is safely possible [3, 4]. Determining areas of health that can be modified within this group will assist in better targeting of support, specific to the needs of our older population.

New Zealand has a unique world‐leading international Resident Assessment Instrument (interRAI) database that could provide insight into the health status of older Pacific Peoples. Despite past interRAI research studies including Pacific Peoples, there is a gap in research to provide a comprehensive overview of the epidemiology of health and well‐being in older Pacific Peoples of New Zealand using the interRAI dataset. There have been previous interRAI studies where Pacific Peoples have been included, with topics such as falls [5], frailty [6], loneliness [7], continence [8], mental health [4] and dementia [9]. Although Pacific Peoples are included in these studies, there has not been a specific interRAI study published focusing on Pacific Peoples using the interRAI database to understand the true prevalence of these health conditions and indicators. There have been limited research studies that have focused on older Pacific Peoples, with an urgent need to have more comprehensive data available for policymakers in New Zealand [10].

This study aimed to provide a descriptive analysis of the overall health and well‐being of older Pacific Peoples using the interRAI‐Home Care (HC) assessments, compared with NZ Europeans. This study highlights areas of unmet need and inequality for Pacific Peoples. It will help focus future interRAI studies in Pacific Peoples, which could inform policy and resource decisions to improve inequalities with older Pacific Peoples.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

Our study used cross‐sectional data of home care clients who had an interRAI‐HC assessment between 5 July 2012 and 31 December 2020. We included people aged 55 years or older who identified as Pacific Peoples or Māori and Pacific Peoples, in accordance with their eligibility criteria for interRAI assessment, and people over 65 years who identified as NZ European and who were living at home and receiving home care services. Pacific Peoples included anybody who stated they were Samoan, Cook Island Māori, Tongan, Niuean, Tokelauan, Fijian, Other Pacific Island and Pacific Island not further defined. Only those who consented for their data to be used for research and planning purposes were included in the study cohort. Approximately 93% of people who receive an interRAI‐HC assessment consented for their data to be used for research purposes [4]. Where an individual had more than one interRAI‐HC assessment, only the first assessment during the study period was included.

Instruments/Variables

The interRAI‐HC is a comprehensive clinical assessment tool designed to identify areas of need in older adults. The assessment consists of 236 questions across multiple domains including communication and vision, psychosocial well‐being, functional status and disease diagnoses [11]. All assessments are conducted by a trained clinical assessor who completes the form after consultation with the care recipient, their family and any other relevant medical records. This assessment is mandatory for anyone accessing publicly funded home support services or entering aged residential care. All data are collected electronically and held by New Zealand's Technical Advisory Services (TAS) who release a de‐identified version of the data with the approval of the Ministry of Health (Technical Advisory Services 2021).

Variables of interest were identified based on previous literature and the research team's clinical insight. The outcome variables of interest were self‐reported health, smoking, alcohol, diabetes, cognitive impairment, coronary heart disease, congestive heart failure, falls, primary mode of locomotion, bladder continence, loneliness, ability to understand others and hearing ability. For disease diagnoses, the interRAI‐HC assessment allows four different response options: ‘Not present’, ‘Primary diagnosis/diagnoses for current stay’, ‘Diagnosis present, receiving active treatment’ and ‘Diagnosis present, monitored but no active treatment’. Cognitive impairment was defined as the presence of Alzheimer's disease, or of dementia other than Alzheimer's disease or a Cognitive Performance Scale (CPS) of 2 or higher. The CPS measures an individual's level of cognitive impairment based on items such as memory, decision‐making skills and level of consciousness, with scores ranging between 0 and 6.

For this study, we determined an individual's ethnicity as Pacific Peoples if one of the ethnicities an individual selected (maximum of three) was a Level 1 or Level 2 Pacific population stratum. Individuals who identified as NZ European were included in the NZ European stratum. Anyone who did not identify as either Pacific Peoples or NZ European was excluded from the study.

The interRAI dataset coding representing the District Health Boards (DHB) responsible for delivering health services across their respective geographic areas. (Note that while DHBs were restructured under Health NZ, geographic coding remains in place).

Statistical Analyses

Basic descriptive information was reported as a total for the cohort and then for Pacific Peoples and NZ Europeans separately. The frequency and percentage of the health conditions of interest were reported. Binary and multinomial logistic regression models adjusted for age and sex were used to investigate any differences between the prevalence of health conditions among Pacific Peoples and NZ Europeans, where NZ Europeans were used as the reference group. Missing data were pairwise omitted from relevant analyses. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 28.

The reporting of analyses followed RECORD guidelines. Ethics approval was granted from the University of Otago Ethics Committee (HD21/097).

The study cohort consisted of 123,013 people, and of those 5390 (4%) were Pacific Peoples and 117,633 (96%) were NZ Europeans. Nearly 65% of Pacific Peoples lived in the Counties Manukau (44%) and Auckland (21%) area, respectively. (Supplementary Table S1).

After adjusting for age and sex, Pacific Peoples were less likely to smoke, consume alcohol, have a diagnosis of coronary heart disease, fall, report feelings of loneliness or have difficulty hearing, compared with NZ Europeans (Table 2). In contrast, Pacific Peoples were more likely to have cognitive impairment, congestive heart failure, require a mobility aid, be bed bound, experience bladder incontinence, have difficulty understanding others and have vision issues than NZ Europeans in our study. The largest differences for Pacific Peoples compared with NZ Europeans were significantly higher rates of diabetes (OR 3.36), being bed bound (OR 3.11) and those who did not answer about self‐reported health, which is likely linked to communication difficulties. Pacific Peoples had much lower rates of falls (OR 0.63), smoking (OR 0.50) and alcohol consumption (OR 0.16), compared with NZ Europeans. (Table 3).

Discussion

With nearly 5400 individuals included in this analysis, to our knowledge, this is the largest epidemiology study focusing on older Pacific Peoples in New Zealand [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Pacific Peoples are eligible for interRAI‐HC assessments at a much earlier age than NZ Europeans. In our cohort, 24% of the Pacific cohort were < 70 years old and 3% aged < 60 years, validating the earlier eligibility for receiving support services. Pacific Peoples had over three times lower rates of aged residential care admission and there is a high degree of unpaid caregiving from families [11].

Similar to NZ Europeans, nearly 60% of interRAI assessments were for females and over 50% were for those either widowed or separated/divorced. The female predominance could be due to Pacific Peoples women living longer than men, yet there may also be an unmet need for men not accessing home care supports [1]. Over half of the individuals in our study were widowed, divorced or separated, and it is known that living alone increases the risk of social isolation, loneliness and risk of mortality [12].

Our research showed some significant areas of strength with older Pacific Peoples having healthier lifestyle risk factors—less likely to smoke or consume alcohol—and lower rates of coronary heart disease than NZ Europeans. However, in the general New Zealand population, Pacific Peoples are twice as likely to be smokers than NZ Europeans, due to higher use in younger adults [13]. Alcohol use has been shown to be lower in older Pacific Peoples in New Zealand, although there have been concerns about hazardous alcohol use for those who do drink alcohol, especially younger men [14, 15]. The rate of coronary artery disease is higher in Pacific Peoples than NZ Europeans [16, 17, 18], although it was noted to be lower in our older cohort. This may be due to the higher mortality risk associated with coronary disease, so people with coronary artery disease may be less likely to reach older ages [16, 18].

Older Pacific Peoples were also less likely to fall, report feelings of loneliness or have difficulty hearing compared to NZ Europeans. There are limited data on Pacific Peoples and falls, but Pacific Peoples have a lower rate of hip fractures from traumatic falls [19]. This may be due to lower levels of frailty and osteoporosis. Family is integral to Pacific Peoples, and they are more likely to be cared for at home, with lower rates of aged residential care admission than NZ Europeans [11]. Hearing loss prevalence data in older Pacific adults are limited, although Pacific Peoples have higher rates of hearing loss than NZ Europeans in those under 20 years of age due to high rates of congenital hearing loss and middle ear disease [20, 21]. There are significant cost and other barriers identified for Pacific Peoples to access audiology services, so there is likely to be a higher true prevalence rate of hearing loss [20, 21].

Pacific Peoples were more likely to have diabetes, cognitive impairment and congestive heart failure. We found the rate of diabetes to be more than double in Pacific Peoples (47%) compared to NZ Europeans/Other ethnicity (17%). This rate of diabetes is similar to other studies (48% in Pacific Peoples aged 65–74 years compared to 13% for NZ European/Others of the same age) [22]. The prevalence of cognitive impairment in Pacific Peoples (55%) was higher than among NZ Europeans (50%). This is consistent with other studies, which have found that Pacific Peoples have higher rates of dementia than NZ Europeans due to increased dementia risk factors such as hypertension, obesity and lower socioeconomic status [23]. We found that the prevalence of congestive heart failure was higher in Pacific Peoples. A recent study demonstrated that Pacific Peoples also showed higher prevalence rates of heart failure hospitalisation, were younger at presentation, had higher socioeconomic deprivation and less ischemic heart disease [24].

A key finding was that Pacific People who had an interRAI‐HC assessment were three times more likely to be bed bound and nearly 1.5 times more likely to use a wheelchair, which could be from patients and families not having assessment for support until care had become increasingly difficult. This high level of immobility is likely due to underlying medical conditions and suggests these patients would most likely qualify for a higher level of residential care [25]. There was also a higher rate of bladder incontinence, which has not been well studied in older Pacific patients [26]. The increased rates highlight the need for providers to discuss both bladder and bowel incontinence in a culturally sensitive way, which may require cultural training for health professional providers.

Another major finding was difficulty communicating, with 12% often and 4% rarely understanding English. We analysed this further and only 35% of Pacific Peoples in our cohort spoke English as their primary language, with the most common primary languages being Samoan (28%), Tongan (17%) and Cook Island Māori (8%) (Table S2). New Zealand has limited availability of translators, a key area that needs healthcare investment [27].

The major strength of our study is the large number of older Pacific Peoples, who I provided an epidemiology overview of older Pacific Peoples health using the in‐depth standardised interRAI‐HC assessment. As mentioned, there has been a limited number of research studies that have focused on the needs of older Pacific Peoples in New Zealand [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Our findings have identified areas that policymakers can focus on to improve equitable access for Pacific Peoples to support caregivers and translational services, as well as enhanced primary care that aims to reduce dementia and heart failure risk factors, such as diabetes, hypertension and obesity. With the longitudinal use of interRAI assessments in New Zealand, further monitoring of Pacific older adults' outcomes will be able to build on our research study.

Limitations of this study include that causation cannot be inferred as this was a prospective cross‐sectional study. The data captured a frail sub‐group of Pacific older adults and conclusions applicable to the wider Pacific older population can therefore not be made. This is particularly important for analysing Pacific People‐specific interRAI data as there may be lower utilisation for families accessing clinical needs assessment for home care supports. A direct comparison between Pacific Peoples and NZ Europeans was not possible for the age range 55–64 years as NZ Europeans are only eligible for assessments under specific circumstances. This makes interpretation of this age stratum difficult. Furthermore, we know that health system utilisation of Pacific Peoples is lower than that of NZ Europeans, meaning that we assume underreporting of health conditions and lower uptake of interRAI assessment services in the Pacific Peoples cohort. ‘Difficulty with communication’ and the majority of older Pacific not speaking English as their primary language could affect self‐reported questions. The interRAI‐HC uses priority coding, therefore people who have Māori and Pacific ethnicity would only be coded for Māori. The interRAI‐HC does not have ethnicity coding to differentiate Indo‐Fijian (who make up 33% of Fiji's population) and indigenous Fijians, the iTakei and Fijian‐Rotumans [28]. Although Pacific Peoples share some similar values, they are a heterogenous population with many different islands and cultures. Therefore, we must be mindful in generalising results. With most of Pacific Peoples in New Zealand now born in New Zealand and more likely to have a mixed heritage than older Pacific Peoples currently, we will need to constantly monitor the needs and information gained from interRAI assessments.

Conclusions

Older Pacific Peoples who receive home care in New Zealand have more complex needs and require support services at an earlier age than NZ Europeans. There is a need for improvement of equity in the health system and underlying social determinants of health to raise the earlier age and lower the high burden of chronic diseases that Pacific Peoples face. There needs to be an urgent review of supporting families who provide care for older Pacific Peoples.
