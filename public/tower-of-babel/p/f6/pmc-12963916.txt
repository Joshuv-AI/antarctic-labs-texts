Efficacy and safety of cardioneuroablation as a treatment for patients with functional bradyarrhythmia: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Aiyue Chen, Tao Zhang, Huanling Sun, Bin Tu, Pakezhati Maimaitijiang, Xinyi Wang, Lihui Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardioneuroablation (CNA) is a novel procedure that shows promising results in patients with functional bradyarrhythmia, including functional sinus node dysfunction (SND) and atrioventricular block (AVB). We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the efficacy and safety of CNA in this population. PubMed, Embase and Web of Science were searched from January 2005 to August 2025 following PRISMA guidelines. A single-arm proportion meta-analysis was performed using a random effects model. Nineteen observational studies comprising 482 patients with functional bradyarrhythmia who underwent CNA were included (mean age 38.1 ± 5.1 years; 55.7% male). Over a mean follow-up of 16.3 ± 3.6 months, the pooled recurrence rate was 13.1% (95% confidence interval [CI] 9.5% − 17.8%; I2 = 0%) and the pooled complication rate was 1.0% (95% CI 0.1% − 9.1%; I2 = 0%). Subgroup analyses suggested lower recurrence rates with functional SND (9.7% [6.3%-14.6%]; I2 = 0%), biatrial ablation (11.0% [6.7%-17.5%]; I2 = 10.7%), and the use of extracardiac noncontact vagal stimulation (ECVS) (8.3% [2.7%–22.9%]; I2 = 0%). No significant differences were observed among techniques used to identify ganglionated plexi (p = 0.938). Data synthesis indicated a non-significant trend towards increased heart rate, as well as shorter sinus node recovery time (SNRT) and corrected SNRT after CNA. These findings support that CNA may be effective and safe in functional SND and AVB; however, the overall certainty of evidence is low, and larger, well-designed clinical trials are required to inform future guideline recommendations.Keywords: Functional bradyarrhythmia, Autonomic nervous system, Cardioneuroablation, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Functional bradyarrhythmia refers to sinoatrial node (SAN) or atrioventricular node (AVN) dysfunction mediated by external factors, mainly the vagus nerve, and includes vasovagal syncope (VVS), functional sinus node dysfunction (SND) and functional atrioventricular block (AVB). [1], [2] This type of bradycardia typically occurs in young individuals and presents in a paroxysmal pattern. [3] Although generally benign, some patients experience fatigue, dizziness, or even syncope. According to current guidelines, permanent pacemaker implantation remains the only treatment for symptomatic bradycardia when reversible causes are excluded. [4] Nevertheless, younger patients may face repeated lead or pacemaker replacements over time, imposing a significant economic burden and negatively impacting quality of life. Furthermore, long-term non-physiological pacing may lead to atrial enlargement and the development of ventricular dysfunction. [5], [6].

Cardioneuroablation (CNA) is a novel therapeutic approach that targets the atrial ganglionated plexi (GP) to modulate cardiac autonomic tone, thereby increasing heart rate (HR) and relieving symptoms. [7] Several randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses have demonstrated its efficacy in patients with recurrent vasovagal syncope (VVS); [8], [9], [10] however, the effectiveness and safety of CNA in functional AVB and SND remain unclear. Unlike VVS, functional SND and AVB are primarily driven by a cardioinhibitory reflex, for which conventional therapies—including increased salt and fluid intake, physical counterpressure maneuvers, or pharmacological agents such as midodrine—are often ineffective. [11] Theoretically, CNA may represent a promising therapy in this patient population.

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the efficacy and safety of CNA in functional SND and AVB, with particular attention to clinical symptoms, electrocardiographic parameters, pacemaker implantation rates, and procedural complications.

Methods

This meta-analysis was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Table S1 and S2). [12] Institutional review board approval was not required, and the study was not prospectively registered in any public database.

Literature search

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase and Web of Science from January 2005 to August 2025 for relevant literature. Applied search terms were combinations of Medical Subject Headings (Mesh) terms related to “sinus node dysfunction”, “atrioventricular block”, “bradyarrhythmia”, “syncope”, “cardioneuroablation” and “catheter ablation”. There were no language restrictions. Detailed search terms and strategies are shown in Table S3. In addition, prior meta-analyses on CNA [9], [10] and the bibliographies of included studies were screened to ensure capture of all studies of interest.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Only articles that met the following criteria were included: (1) Clinical RCT, prospective cohort study or retrospective cohort study. Case reports, case series, reviews, editorials, letters, comments and conference papers were excluded; (2) Studies enrolling patients with functional SND or AVB, including pure SND or AVB cohorts and mixed cohorts (e.g., VVS, SND, and AVB). In mixed cohorts, only patients diagnosed with functional SND or AVB were included; (3) All patients treated with CNA, including patients with CNA combined with pulmonary vein isolation for comorbid atrial fibrillation; and (4) Primary efficacy outcome for the population of interest was reported separately; and (5) For similar studies published by the same group, only the largest or most recent study was included in this meta-analysis, based on the time of publication and details of the methods section of the article.

Data extraction

Two authors (A.C. and T.Z.) independently read the titles and/or abstracts to screen the articles using Endnote, and any uncertainties were resolved through discussion with a third author (H.S.). Two authors (A.C. and T.Z.) independently extracted the following data from included studies following the population, intervention, comparison, and outcome (PICO) principle: (1) General Information: name of the study, author, duration of the study, study design; (2) Patient: patient demographics and clinical characteristics, cardiac investigations, including HR, heart rate variability (HRV), and invasive electrophysiological measurements; (3) Intervention: ablation target, ablation method, GPs ablated, ablation endpoints; and (4) Outcome: outcome of primary and secondary endpoints, follow-up time, and complications.

End points

The primary efficacy endpoint was a composite outcome comprising: (1) recurrence of syncope in patients who had pre-procedural syncope; (2) ECG-documented recurrence during follow-up, characterized by sinus bradycardia, sinus arrest, or high-grade AVB associated with bradycardia-related symptoms and requiring clinical intervention; or (3) pacemaker implantation, either performed during the procedure due to unsuccessful CNA, or after the procedure because of recurrent bradycardia. The secondary efficacy outcome measures were changes in invasive electrophysiological and noninvasive HRV measurements. The safety outcome measure was the proportion of patients with all possible minor and major complications during the procedure.

Quality assessment

Two authors (A.C. and T.Z.) independently completed the quality assessment of the included studies, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third author (H.S.). As no RCTs were included in this meta-analysis; all studies were observational and were assessed using the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I), [13] which scores the studies in low, moderate, serious, and critical risk of bias evaluating in 7 domains: confounding, selection of participants, classification of interventions, deviations from intended intervention, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of the reported results.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as absolute number and/or percentage (%). Where necessary, data were converted to uniform units for consistency. Weighted means and SDs were calculated if applicable. Meta-analysis was performed for the primary efficacy and safety outcomes, and a random-effects model was used to account for the expected variability in the study population and ablation methods. Because studies reported high freedom from syncope rates close to the maximal limit, a logit transformation was performed. The results are presented as effect size with 95% confidence interval (CI) together with the adjusted weight for each study. Prespecified subgroup analyses were further performed based on disease type (SND and AVB), mapping method (electroanatomic mapping [EAM], spectral analysis [SA], fractionated electrogram [fEGM], high-frequency stimulation [HFS] and combined), ablation target (right atrium [RA], left atrium [LA] and biatrial ablation), and whether extracardiac noncontact vagal stimulation (ECVS) was used. Changes from baseline to postoperative assessment in invasive electrophysiological parameters were analyzed using paired Student’s t-tests. Sensitivity analysis was performed by removing one study at a time or by removing specifically selected studies each time. To further explore potential sources of heterogeneity, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted according to study design (prospective, retrospective, or ambispective), study population (studies enrolling mixed populations were classified as VVS-predominant if ≥ 50% of patients had VVS, and VVS-minor otherwise), and study origin (studies from the Aksu group, which contributed five studies, were analyzed separately from other studies). Heterogeneity was assessed using I2 and τ2 (Tau-squared). I2 represents the percentage of variability due to heterogeneity, with < 50% indicating low, 50–75% moderate, and > 75% high heterogeneity. τ2 indicates the between-study variance in a random-effects model. Meta-analyses were conducted using the inverse variance method with the DerSimonian-Laird estimator for τ2. Funnel plot, Begg’s and Egger’s test were used to evaluate publication bias. The significant level was set as 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using the R package meta (The R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). [14].

Meta‑analysis of the primary end point

The primary end point was reported in all 19 studies, including 66 events from 482 patients, detailed study outcomes are summarized in Table S7. In the random-effects analysis, the overall weighted prevalence of recurrence after CNA was 13.1% (9.5%-17.8%, I2 = 0%) (Fig. 2A). Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that sequentially removing each study did not materially change the pooled proportion of bradycardia recurrence (range 12.3%-15.2%) and I2 remained 0% throughout, indicating minimal between-study heterogeneity and robust results (Fig. S1). When considering the individual components of the composite primary endpoint, the pooled incidences of syncope recurrence and pacemaker implantation after CNA were 8.6% (4.0%-17.7%, I2 = 20.8%) and 9.2% (5.7%-14.5%, I2 = 44.8%), respectively (Fig. S2). Sensitivity analyses showed no significant differences in the primary endpoint across study designs, between VVS-predominant and VVS-minor studies, or between studies from the Aksu group and other studies (Fig. S3-S5), indicating that the results were robust.

Subgroup analysis for the primary end point

By disease type, functional SND was associated with a significantly lower recurrence rate after CNA (9.7% [6.3%-14.6%], I2 = 0%) compared with AVB (17.5% [13.4%–22.6%], I2 = 0%) (Fig. 2B, P = 0.017). Except for the multicenter study by Barrio-Lopez et al., [20] which could not be classified due to operator-dependent ablation strategies, all studies were included in the subgroup analysis of ablation techniques. GP mapping methods showed no significant differences (Fig. 2C, P = 0.938). The EAM-only subgroup reported a disproportionately low recurrence rate with high heterogeneity (10.5% [4.2%-24.0%], I2 = 54.7%); after excluding the study by Qin et al., [29] the pooled rate increased to 13.7% (5.1%-31.7%, I2 = 14.8%) (Fig. S6). Biatrial ablation (14 studies) showed a recurrence rate of 11.0% (6.7%-17.5%, I2 = 10.7%), compared with 18.8% (8.7%-35.9%, I2 = 0%) for RA-only ablation (2 studies) and 17.9% (10.5%-28.9%, I2 = 0%) for LA-only ablation (2 studies) (Fig. 2D and S7, P = 0.301). Studies using ECVS reported a recurrence rate of 8.3% (2.7%–22.9%, I2 = 0%), versus 12.7% (8.2%-19.1%, I2 = 1.5%) in those without ECVS (Fig. 2E, P = 0.475). It should be noted that although biatrial ablation and ECVS-guided strategies were associated with lower recurrence rates, the subgroup analyses did not reach statistical significance. Given the limited number of studies within each subgroup, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Changes in secondary efficacy outcomes

A detailed summary of secondary outcomes is provided in Table S6, including invasive electrophysiological data (HR, SNRT, and cSNRT changes) and non-invasive Holter results (preoperative and follow-up HR and HRV changes). For the invasive electrophysiological data, pooled analyses showed high heterogeneity and sensitivity to individual studies. After ablation, the change in resting HR was 24.03 bpm (−1.84 to 49.90, p = 0.057, I2 = 97.9%), the change in SNRT was −518.8 ms (−1112.79 to 75.19, p = 0.057, I2 = 49.6%), and the change in cSNRT after ablation was −188.02 ms (−292.81 to −83.23, p = 0.011, I2 = 73.5%), both with moderate-to-high variability across studies (Fig. 3 and S8). Sensitivity analyses indicated that exclusion of individual studies could alter the statistical significance of these outcomes (Fig. S9). Due to differences in follow-up times, Holter results are summarized in the Table S6 but not pooled. Trends suggested modest increases in mean and minimum HR, little change in maximum HR, and a decrease in SDNN postoperatively, consistent with the invasive data.

Periprocedural complications

Across 13 studies including 409 patients with functional bradyarrhythmia, the pooled rate of periprocedural complications after CNA was 1.0% (0.1%-9.1%; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4). The most frequently reported complication was transient tachyarrhythmia, occurring in 12/16 patients. In Addition, Aksu et al. reported two cases of groin hematoma, both of which improved with conservative treatment.[17] Rivarola et al. described two cases of acute sinus node artery occlusion during the procedure, and one of these patients required pacemaker implantation. [31] A full summary of periprocedural events is provided in Table S7.

Quality assessment and publication bias

Among the included studies, 18 observational studies were considered at high risk of bias, while only one study [29] was rated as having moderate risk (Fig. 5 and Fig. S10). There was no indication of publication bias: the funnel plot was symmetrical (Fig. 3D), and neither Egger’s test (P = 0.101) nor Begg’s test (P = 0.063) reached statistical significance.

Discussion

Major findings

This meta-analysis mainly aimed to describe the efficacy and safety of CNA as an emerging therapy for the treatment of functional SND and AVB patients. The overall recurrence rate was 13.1% (9.5%-17.8%). The overall adverse events incidence rate was 1.0% (0.1%-9.1%). Further subgroup analyses indicated higher efficacy in functional SND than in AVB. Recurrence rates were similar across GP-mapping methods, while bi-atrial ablation and ECVS-guidance strategies were associated with lower recurrence. However, given the low overall certainty of the evidence, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Clinical implications

Functional bradyarrhythmia may be malignant and can lead to substantial impairment in quality of life. [34] In patients with a predominant cardioinhibitory component—such as cardioinhibitory VVS, functional AVB, or functional SND—conventional strategies, including increased salt and fluid intake, physical counterpressure maneuvers, and vasopressor therapy (e.g., midodrine), are frequently ineffective. Although permanent pacing remains the only established treatment for severe cases, evidence from the ISSUE-3, VPS-II, and SUP-2 trials suggests that pacemaker therapy does not provide complete protection against recurrence. [35], [36], [37] Notably, these patients are often young, rendering long-term device-related complications unavoidable.

CNA is based on selective vagal denervation. Parasympathetic postganglionic neurons are located close to the heart (mainly in the GPs and atrial wall) whereas sympathetic postganglionic neurons are located far from the heart. [38] Thus, endocardial radiofrequency ablation of the GPs predominantly injures the bodies of parasympathetic postganglionic neurons and the axons of sympathetic postganglionic nerve fibers. The sympathetic system is less permanently affected because these axons could regenerate over time. [28] Overall, CNA is generally more acceptable to patients than permanent pacemaker therapy.

Patient selection

The success of CNA critically depends on appropriate patient selection. First, patients are typically young, with no significant history of cardiovascular disease or medications; younger age, particularly < 40 years, has been associated with better procedural outcomes. [24], [29] Second, diagnosis requires a clear temporal correlation between symptoms and electrocardiographic findings. Bradycardia-related symptoms are usually paroxysmal, occurring at rest or during sleep and resolving with exercise. In SND, episodes are characterized by progressively prolonged RR intervals leading to sinus pauses > 3 s, whereas AVB typically manifests as progressive PR prolongation (usually Mobitz Type 1 or 2:1 AVB) with concomitant sinus slowing. [7] Third, autonomic function testing is essential. Provocative autonomic tests, such as HUT or carotid sinus massage, can improve diagnostic accuracy. [39] An appropriate HR response to atropine (>25% increase or > 90 bpm) helps exclude intrinsic conduction system disease.

The main distinction of functional SND and AVB, as compared with typical VVS, is that episodes often lack typical triggers or overt syncope symptoms, and patients frequently show negative responses to HUT testing. In two studies including only patients with sinus bradycardia, a cSNRT > 525 ms was used as an inclusion criterion.[29], [40] However, even if patients exhibited some degree of conduction abnormality, those who demonstrated adequate sinus node responsiveness during stress or atropine testing were still considered likely to benefit from CNA.

In this study, we reported recurrence rates of 9.7% in SND and 17.5% in AVB after CNA. Several factors likely explain the higher recurrence in AVB compared with SND patients. First, patient heterogeneity: if inclusion criteria are not strict, some “functional” AVB cases—especially in older individuals—may have underlying intrinsic conduction disease. Second, anatomical differences: the SAN is typically innervated by a concentrated cluster of ganglia near the superior vena cava-right atrium junction (RSGP), [41], [42] whereas AV nodal innervation appears to arise from multiple small postganglionic fibers extending from several GP (PMLGP, RIGP, MTGP) toward the interatrial septum. [43], [44] These factors make AVB within the His bundle or bundle branches less sensitive to autonomic input than the SAN and render complete and durable denervation of the AVN more challenging.

Procedural setting

The effectiveness of CNA may vary according to the approaches used for GP ablation. In our analysis, single-atrial approaches (RA or LA-only)—particularly RA-only ablation—were associated with higher recurrence rate (18.2%) compared with bi-atrial ablation, a pattern that aligns with findings from previous meta-analyses in VVS. [9], [10] Studies have shown that LA ablation exerts a stronger influence on vagal modulation, whereas RA-only ablation primarily increases HR with limited vagal response. [24], [45] Complete autonomic denervation often requires a bi-atrial strategy.[46] However, given concerns that excessive denervation may compromise the heart’s physiological autonomic protection, leaving its long-term safety unclear. [47] Minguito-Carazo et al.’s study in VVS reported a simplified 3-GP ablation strategy (RAGP, LSGP, and PMLGP) have achieved autonomic denervation and clinical outcomes comparable to those of more extensive ablation strategies. [48] Whether such selective strategies are effective in SND or AVB remains unclear. Finally, this finding should be interpreted with caution, since the estimates for LA-only and RA-only approaches in our meta-analysis were derived from only two studies.

Unlike prior meta-analyses, our subgroup analysis did not detect meaningful differences among GP-targeting strategies. [9] This finding is likely influenced by the small number of studies within each subgroup and the considerable inter-study heterogeneity.

Study endpoints

First, procedural endpoints. The most commonly used endpoint is HR acceleration to the maximal rate achieved after preprocedural atropine administration. Elimination of vagal responses is another endpoint, although such responses are not consistently elicited. [49], [50] Absence of an atropine response suggests adequate denervation; however, repeated atropine use can confound intraprocedural HR evaluation when further ablation is required. Importantly, none of these endpoints permits real-time evaluation of autonomic denervation. To overcome these limitations, ECVS was introduced by Pachon et al. in 2015. [51] In the present analysis, ECVS was associated with a lower recurrence rate (8.3%), consistent with prior observational study. [52].

Second, clinical endpoints. Previous VVS studies used syncope recurrence as the clinical endpoint; however, patients with functional SND or AVB may lack a history of syncope, rendering syncope-based assessment insufficient. Accordingly, we defined recurrence using a composite endpoint that incorporated syncope, electrocardiographic recurrence, and the need for repeat interventions such as pacemaker implantation. Nevertheless, owing to the paroxysmal nature of functional bradyarrhythmia, electrocardiographic documentation during symptomatic episodes remains challenging, and some recurrences may be missed. Long-term monitoring with implantable loop recorders may improve arrhythmia detection and enhance both preprocedural diagnosis and postprocedural evaluation.[39].

An important finding of the meta-analysis is the discrepancy between syncope recurrence and pacemaker implantation after CNA. While syncope recurrence was relatively consistent across studies, pacemaker implantation rates demonstrated moderate heterogeneity. This observation suggests that pacemaker implantation after CNA may not solely reflect procedural failure, but may also be influenced by differences in patient selection, follow-up strategies, and procedural techniques. Importantly, thresholds for pacemaker implantation varied substantially across centres, as current ESC guidelines recommend pacing only in patients aged > 40 years with documented asystolic syncope.[4] This heterogeneity highlights the need for unified post-CNA management strategies and standardised criteria for pacemaker implantation when CNA is considered as an alternative therapy for functional bradyarrhythmias.

Future developments

A recent large European survey demonstrated that most physicians have high expectations for CNA and consider it a promising therapy for functional bradyarrhythmia. [53] However, despite this growing interest, several challenges remain to be addressed.

First, standardised diagnostic criteria for functional bradyarrhythmia are essential. Establishing a causal relationship between symptoms and bradycardia remains challenging due to the paroxysmal and unpredictable nature of the condition. In this context, implantable loop recorders may play a pivotal role in improving diagnostic accuracy and enabling reliable detection of recurrences during long-term follow-up. [39].

Second, procedural endpoints require further refinement and standardisation. While heart rate acceleration and atropine response are commonly used indicators of effective vagal denervation, these markers may be operator-dependent. Adjunctive tools such as ECVS offer objective, real-time assessment of sinus and atrioventricular node denervation and may help harmonise procedural endpoints across centres. [51], [52].

Third, as a single-arm meta-analysis, this study could not directly compare CNA with permanent pacing or conservative management in functional bradyarrhythmias. In patients with cardioinhibitory or mixed VVS, a randomized trial by Piotrowski et al. demonstrated that CNA was superior to conventional non-pharmacological therapy,[54] whereas a multicenter retrospective study by Gopinathannair et al. found similar syncope recurrence rates and safety profiles between CNA and pacemaker implantation.[55] Nevertheless, no controlled studies are available for patients with functional SND or AVB, and direct comparisons between CNA and pacemaker therapy are clearly needed.

Fourth, the placebo effect of CNA warrants careful consideration. Given that the available evidence is exclusively observational and device-based therapies are known to have substantial placebo effects, sham-controlled trials are essential to establish the true efficacy of CNA. This phenomenon is illustrated by VVS trials: the VPS trial reported an 85.4% relative reduction in syncope with pacemaker implantation versus no implantation,[56] whereas VPS II—where pacemakers were implanted in all patients but pacing was randomized ON or OFF—showed no benefit of active pacing, highlighting a significant procedural placebo effect. Notably, 40–50% of control patients remained syncope-free during follow-up, suggesting that a comparable proportion of patients undergoing CNA may not actually require intervention. [37], [57].

Finally, although a low periprocedural complication rate (1.0%) was observed, long-term safety data for CNA remain limited. By reducing cardiac parasympathetic tone, CNA may induce chronic sympathetic-vagal imbalance, which has been associated with increased cardiovascular risk, including atrial and ventricular arrhythmias. [58], [59] The potential consequences of excessive denervation may only emerge years later or under stress conditions such as myocardial ischaemia, highlighting the need for large-scale studies with prolonged follow-up.

Study limitations

There are several limitations in this study. (1) Study design: All included studies were observational cohorts, and 18 of the 19 studies were rated as having a high risk of bias, which may introduce confounding and publication bias; (2) Study population limitations: Most included studies were from mixed populations, which may influence the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the potential for patient duplication should be considered; however, inclusion periods were reviewed to ensure patient uniqueness. We further performed sensitivity analyses and found no significant impact from studies with predominantly VVS populations or from a single author group; (3) Analysis-related limitations: This single-arm meta-analysis, together with the high reported success rates of CNA (up to 100%), led to wider CIs despite data transformation. Subgroup analyses were exploratory and underpowered due to the small number of studies in each category, and no adjustment for multiple testing was performed.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis suggests that CNA may be a safe and promising therapeutic option for patients with functional SND and AVB. However, given the high risk of bias in the available evidence, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further large-scale, methodologically robust studies are required.
