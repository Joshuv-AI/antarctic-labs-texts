The multimodal neuroimaging signatures and gene expression profiles for adverse childhood experiences
Authors: Tong Yu, Guorui Zhao, Yaoyao Sun, Zhe Lu, Yundan Liao, Rui Yuan, Zhewei Kang, Xiaoyang Feng, Yunqing Zhu, Jing Guo, Yuyanan Zhang, Weihua Yue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdverse childhood experiences (ACEs) have been considered significant drivers of negative mental health and cognitive outcomes. However, identifying clear neurobiological signatures of ACEs has been challenging due to limited sample sizes, participant heterogeneity, and methodological variability.MethodsA whole-brain meta-analysis was conducted to identify functional, structural, and overlapping brain alterations in ACEs-exposed individuals compared to unexposed controls, using a large sample (functional analysis: 63 studies, 3549 participants; structural analysis: 38 studies, 2919 participants). Subgroup analyses were performed based on age, adversity type, diagnostic status, and functional magnetic resonance imaging task domains, providing a more nuanced aspect of ACEs’ effect on neurodevelopment. Furthermore, the BrainMap-derived task activation maps, atlas-based nuclear imaging-derived neurotransmitter maps, and postmortem gene expression profiles were integrated to examine spatial correlations between ACEs-related brain abnormalities and downstream behavioral processes, disease domains, and upstream receptor/transporter distributions and gene expression.ResultsACEs-exposed individuals exhibited significantly increased functional activity in the amygdala, increased gray matter volume (GMV) in the parahippocampal gyrus, and reduced GMV in the inferior frontal gyrus and supramarginal gyrus, with overlapping abnormalities observed in the parahippocampal gyrus. Subgroup analyses revealed distinct brain alterations. Furthermore, specific associations were identified between structural alterations and the distribution of dopaminergic, serotonergic, and GABAergic neurotransmitter systems. Enrichment analyses further emphasized the profound impact of ACEs on neurodevelopment, immunometabolism, and psychopathological trajectories.ConclusionsThese findings characterize the neural substrates of ACEs, providing valuable insights into their impact on neurodevelopment and suggesting potential targets for ACEs-related disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04387-2.Keywords: Childhood adversity, Coordinate-based meta-analysis, Functional magnetic resonance imaging, Gray matter volume, Allen human brain atlas

Background

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) have been associated with an increased risk of nearly 30% of psychiatric disorders, including anxiety disorders, mood disorders, schizophrenia, and substance use disorders [1–3], imposing significant societal and economic burdens [4]. Even in individuals without formal diagnoses, ACEs exert enduring effects on behavior, cognition, and social functioning [5]. Neuroimaging studies provide compelling evidence for the association of ACEs with neurodevelopmental outcomes, encompassing abnormalities in brain regions implicated in common psychiatric disorders [6–8]. However, there remains substantial heterogeneity across different studies and modalities, potentially due to variations in participant characteristics and methodological factors, such as age [9], types of adversity [10], diagnostic status [11–13], functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) task domains [7], and limited sample sizes [14]. This variability complicates the identification of consistent neuroimaging signatures of ACEs and hinders the development of targeted interventions for ACEs-related disorders. Therefore, a timely, comprehensive, meta-analysis is urgently needed to address these issues and provide robust evidence of ACEs-related abnormalities in brain development.

Specifically, abnormal brain development involves functional and structural impairments. Functional activity is typically measured using blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signals or cerebral blood flow (CBF) [15, 16]. BOLD signals are quantified through fMRI, while CBF is commonly assessed via positron emission tomography (PET) or single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT). fMRI can be further divided into task-based fMRI (t-fMRI), which examines neural responses to specific stimuli [17], and resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI), which captures spontaneous low-frequency fluctuations in the resting state [18]. Combining t-fMRI and rs-fMRI provides a more comprehensive framework for analyzing brain function by integrating spontaneous neural dynamics with stimulus-induced responses [19]. For structural alterations, voxel-based morphometry (VBM) is a widely used method to detect gray matter volume (GMV) changes. VBM enables voxel-wise comparisons of GMV without requiring a priori hypotheses, making it particularly sensitive to localized structural changes [20]. Differences in structural signatures may underlie functional neural activity, and a multimodal meta-analysis offers a more integrative approach to examining the association between structure and function [21].

Furthermore, ACEs-related functional and structural brain abnormalities may also be accompanied by alterations in neurotransmitter systems or gene expression. Neurotransmitters are critical for neural communication, information processing, and behavioral regulation [22]. Preclinical studies have suggested that dysfunctions in the serotonergic, dopaminergic, and GABAergic pathways, are associated with ACEs [23, 24]. Integrating neuroimaging with nuclear imaging to assess neurotransmitter systems provides a valuable cross-modal approach to understanding ACEs-related neuropathology [25]. Additionally, the Allen Human Brain Atlas (AHBA) microarray dataset allows for identifying spatial correlations between gene expression and neuroimaging endophenotypes [26], shedding light on the genetic basis of ACEs-related brain abnormalities. Previous studies have identified posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD)-risk genes that are significantly associated with structural brain alterations in PTSD patients [27, 28]. However, no studies have systematically explored the spatial correlations between neurotransmitter or transcriptomic systems and ACEs-related structural or functional brain alterations.

To address these gaps, we conducted a multimodal whole-brain meta-analysis using Seed-based d Mapping with Permutation of Subject Images (SDM-PSI) to identify functional and structural brain alterations in ACEs-exposed individuals compared to unexposed controls. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the influence of participant characteristics and methodological factors on neuroimaging signatures. We then overlapped functional and structural abnormalities to identify convergent brain regions. To further explore the potential mechanisms underlying these alterations, we integrated data from the BrainMap-derived meta-analytic task activation maps, atlas-based nuclear imaging-derived neurotransmitter maps, and postmortem gene expression profiles to assess spatial correlations with behavioral processes, disease domains, receptor/transporter distributions, and gene expression. Methods

Study selection and quality assessment

The meta-analysis was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Additional File 1: Table S1), and registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD42025633825). A systematic and comprehensive search was performed to identify studies published from inception to October 2024, using the databases PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, and PsycINFO. The search strategy incorporated the following keywords: (“adverse childhood experience*” OR “early life adversity” OR “early life stress” OR “childhood adversities” OR “child maltreatment” OR “childhood trauma” OR “child mistreatment” OR “child abuse” OR “child neglect”) AND (“structural neuroimaging” OR “structural magnetic resonance imaging” OR “sMRI” OR “morphometry” OR “voxel-wise” OR “voxel- based” OR “volumetric” OR “VBM” OR “grey matter” OR “gray matter” OR “functional magnetic resonance imaging” OR “fMRI” OR “task” OR “amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation” OR “ALFF” OR “low-frequency fluctuation” OR “LFF” OR “fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation” OR “fALFF” OR “regional homogeneity” OR “ReHo” OR “reho” OR “cerebral blood flow” OR “CBF” OR “positron emission tomography” OR “PET” OR “FDG-PET” OR “O-15 PET” OR “single photon emission computed tomography” OR “SPECT” OR “arterial spin labeling” OR “ASL”).

Studies were included if they met the following conditions: (1) conducted functional and/or structural neuroimaging analyses comparing individuals with and without ACEs at the whole-brain level; (2) reported three-dimensional standard stereotactic coordinates (Montreal Neurological Institute [MNI] or Talairach); and (3) were original research, written in English, published in peer-reviewed journals, and focused on human subjects. Studies were excluded if they (1) were not empirical studies (eq., case reports, reviews, meta-analyses, or theoretical papers); (2) lacked direct comparisons between individuals with and without ACEs; (3) restricted to region of interest (ROI) analyses; and (4) applied small volume correction methods. Actually, VBM data are variously referred to as GMV or density. For consistency, we adopted the term “GMV” throughout the manuscript, although in most cases, the values reflect modulated estimates rather than true structural volumes [29].

The quality of each study was assessed using a modified 10-point checklist specifically designed for imaging studies of ACEs (Additional file 1: Table S2) [30]. This checklist evaluated both clinical and demographic characteristics as well as the imaging methodologies employed in the studies. We included any study scoring > 6.0 points in the final analysis [31]. The literature search, study selection, and quality assessment were independently conducted by two authors (Y.T. and Z.G.R.), and the corresponding authors (Y.W.H. and Z.Y.Y.N.) double-checked the information.

Meta-analysis of functional and structural differences

Separate meta-analyses were conducted to investigate functional and structural brain differences between individuals exposed to ACEs and unexposed controls utilizing the SDM-PSI software (version 6.21) [32]. This voxel-based meta-analytic approach synthesizes peak coordinates and t-statistics from the included studies, converting them into Hedges’ g effect sizes and variances to generate whole-brain maps of group differences. SDM-PSI employs a random-effects model to calculate voxel-wise effect sizes across studies, accounting for effect sizes, null results, and both positive and negative peaks, which enhances accuracy and reliability, particularly in small-sample meta-analyses [33]. Statistical significance was determined using standard permutation tests with a voxel-wise threshold of p < 0.005 and a cluster-level threshold of 10 voxels [34]. Additionally, we reported results corrected for family-wise error (FWE) using p < 0.05 with the threshold-free cluster enhancement (TFCE) approach [21].

Subgroup analyses

To further explore the influence of various factors on brain abnormalities, we conducted separate meta-analyses for functional and structural imaging across specific subgroups: (1) age-based subgroups: adults (aged ≥ 18 years) and adolescents or children (aged < 18 years); (2) adversity type-based subgroups [10, 35]: threat traumas, involving direct threats to the child (e.g., emotional, psychological, or verbal abuse; physical abuse; sexual abuse; bullying; exposure to household or neighborhood violence); deprivation traumas, characterized by unmet needs or neglect (e.g., physical or emotional neglect; social isolation; parental divorce, separation, or abandonment; financial difficulties); and mixed traumas, involving both threat and deprivation; (3) clinical status-based subgroups: individuals diagnosed with PTSD, individuals diagnosed with depression, and healthy individuals. Additionally, to further ensure that the findings from the main analysis primarily reflect the effects of ACEs rather than clinical diagnoses, we conducted a validation analysis by excluding studies in which the ACE-exposed and unexposed control groups differed in diagnostic status (e.g., depression with ACEs vs. healthy controls). (4) Task domain-based subgroups [36]: emotion processing, inhibitory control, memory processing, and reward processing in t-fMRI studies. To assess the potential impact of methodological factors, we performed exploratory meta-analyses restricted to t-fMRI datasets within the pooled sample and across different subgroups. Notably, functional subgroup-analyses for the reward processing domain, as well as structural subgroup-analyses for the deprivation, depression, and PTSD subgroups, were not conducted due to the limited number of available studies (n < 5) [37].

Multimodal overlapping of the functional and structural differences

To identify brain regions with both functional and structural abnormalities in ACEs-exposed individuals, we performed a conjunction analysis based on the results of the functional and structural meta-analyses using SDM-PSI. This approach ensures consistency in identifying overlapping abnormalities across modalities by incorporating noise into p-value estimation [38]. The results are reported for both FWE-corrected (p < 0.05, TFCE) and uncorrected (p < 0.005) thresholds, consistent with the main analysis.

Meta-regression analyses

Meta-regression analyses were carried out to assess potential effects of demographic variables, including age, percentage of females, and intelligence quotient. Additionally, we evaluated the relationship between clinical variables, such as the onset age and duration of ACEs, and the identified functional and structural abnormalities. For these analyses, statistical significance was determined using p < 0.0005 with a cluster-level threshold of 10 voxels [39].

Analyses of heterogeneity, publication bias, and jackknife sensitivity

The inter-study heterogeneity for each cluster was assessed using the I 2 statistic, with I 2 values < 50% indicating low heterogeneity and < 75% representing moderate heterogeneity [40]. To evaluate potential publication bias, Egger’s test and funnel plots were subsequently employed, with significant asymmetry in the plots or a p-value < 0.05 suggesting publication bias [41]. Funnel plots allow visualization of how many studies report a relevant effect size for a given voxel and whether findings are predominantly driven by smaller studies [34]. Jackknife sensitivity analyses were conducted to further assess the robustness and replicability of the findings. The meta-analysis was iteratively repeated, with one dataset excluded in each iteration [42]. Brain regions that consistently remained significant across the majority or all iterations were considered robust.

Association with behavioral and disease

We analyzed the association between ACEs-related brain abnormalities and both behavioral and disease terms using the BrainMap database within the Multi-image Analysis GUI. The BrainMap database, which includes functional neuroimaging studies over 20 years, was utilized for behavioral analyses across its five domains (action, cognition, emotion, interoception, and perception) and sixty sub-domains, as well as for disease analyses based on neurological disorders classified under the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) [43]. Only z-scores > 3.0 were considered significant, corresponding to p > 0.05 after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons [43].

Association with receptors and transporters

To investigate the association between ACEs-related brain abnormalities and the expression profiles of specific receptors and transporters based on their topographical relationship, Spearman correlation coefficients were computed using the JuSpace toolbox [25]. This toolbox facilitates the integration of neuroimaging data with molecular imaging atlases. The analyzed neurotransmitter systems included the following: (1) serotonin transmission ((5-HT1A, 5-HT1B, 5-HT2A, 5-HT4 receptors, and serotonin transporter (SERT)); (2) dopamine transmission (D1 and D2 receptors, dopamine transporter (DAT), fluorodopa (FDOPA)); (3) gamma-aminobutyric acid type A (GABA(A)) receptors; (4) N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA); (5) noradrenaline transporter (NAT); and (6) metabotropic glutamate receptor 5 (mGluR5). Multiple comparisons were controlled using Bonferroni correction (p < 0.05/13).

Association with gene expression profiles

Whole-brain transcriptional profiles from six donated human brains were obtained from the AHBA dataset [44]. The microarray expression data from the AHBA and the Z-maps of meta-analysis-derived functional and structural brain alterations were preprocessed using the Python toolbox abagen [45]. A standardized preprocessing pipeline was implemented following the recommended protocol, which included six key steps [26]: gene information re-annotation, data filtering, probes selection, assigning samples to the HCP atlas [46], data normalization, and gene filtering. Since right hemisphere samples were available for only two of the six donors, the analysis was restricted to the left hemisphere to minimize variability across regions [26]. This preprocessing generated a gene expression matrix encompassing 180 brain regions and 15,631 genes.

Subsequently, partial least squares (PLS) regression was employed to investigate the transcriptional profiles associated with brain abnormalities. The first PLS component (PLS1) captured the spatial pattern that explained the largest proportion of gene expression variance across cortical regions. To evaluate the significance of PLS1, a spatial autocorrelation-adjusted analysis with 5000 permutations was performed, ensuring that the observed R 2 of PLS1 exceeded random expectations. Additionally, a bootstrapping method was applied to correct estimation errors in the weights assigned to each gene for the significant components, after which genes were ranked based on their adjusted weights. To further investigate the functional signatures of the identified genes, the Metascape website was employed for enrichment analysis [47]. The analysis encompassed Gene Ontology (GO) biological process, molecular function, cellular component, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway, with significant enrichment defined as Benjamini–Hochberg corrected q < 0.05.

Results

Study and participant characteristics

We identified a total of 101 eligible articles for the multimodal neuroimaging meta-analysis. For the functional meta-analysis, 63 independent studies were included, comprising 1683 ACEs-exposed individuals and 1866 unexposed controls. These studies included 6 using rs-fMRI, 55 using t-fMRI, and 2 using PET. For the structural meta-analysis, 38 independent articles were analyzed, involving 1494 ACEs-exposed and 1425 unexposed individuals. The demographic, clinical characteristics, and the quality scores of the included studies are detailed in Additional file 1: Table S3–5.

Functional and structural brain abnormalities for ACEs

In the functional analysis, individuals exposed to ACEs exhibited significantly increased functional activity in the right amygdala compared to unexposed controls. However, no brain regions exhibited decreased functional activity in ACEs-exposed individuals relative to unexposed controls. To explore the relationship between ACEs-related brain alterations and behavioral and disease profiles, we utilized the BrainMap database to identify functional associations between specific terms and brain regions. Significant associations were observed with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (Z = 5.84), negative emotion (Z = 5.80), and fear (Z = 4.94), among other domains (Table 1, Fig. 2a).

In the structural analysis, individuals exposed to ACEs exhibited increased GMV in the right parahippocampal gyrus and decreased GMV in the bilateral inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and left supramarginal gyrus. Among these, only the parahippocampal gyrus remained significant after FWE correction, indicating a more robust structural alteration. These structural abnormalities were strongly associated with AD (Z = 6.787), olfaction (Z = 5.658), and negative emotion (Z = 5.610), among others (Table 1, Fig. 2b).

Population- and modality-specific brain abnormalities for ACEs

To further investigate the influence of participant characteristics and methodological factors on neuroimaging signatures, we conducted multiple subgroup analyses based on age, adversity type, diagnostic status, and t-fMRI task domains (Table 1, Fig. 3).

Meta-analyses results comparing individuals exposed to ACEs and unexposed controls in a functional activity differences, and b structural GMV differences among different subgroups. Red regions indicate increased functional activity or GMV values, while blue regions indicate decreased values. SDM-Z values are represented by the color bar. Abbreviations: IFG, inferior frontal gyrus; SFG, superior frontal gyrus; STG, superior frontal gyrus

In the age-based subgroup analysis, the childhood/adolescent group (28 datasets, 1550 participants) exhibited increased functional activity in the right parahippocampal gyrus, left hippocampus, and right superior temporal gyrus. Notably, hyperactivity in the right parahippocampal gyrus remained significant after FWE correction. In contrast, the adult group (35 datasets, 1999 participants) demonstrated hyperactivity in the right IFG. Regarding structural analysis, the childhood/adolescent group (10 datasets, 697 participants) showed reduced GMV in the right fusiform gyrus, left angular gyrus, and left supramarginal gyrus. Conversely, the adult group (28 datasets, 2222 participants) displayed decreased GMV in the bilateral IFG, as well as increased GMV in the right parahippocampal gyrus. Consistent with the main analysis, the GMV increase in the right parahippocampal gyrus survived FWE correction.

In the adversity type-based subgroup analysis, individuals exposed to threat-related adversity (25 datasets, 1274 participants) exhibited elevated functional activity in the right insula, left postcentral gyrus, and right rolandic operculum, alongside reduced activity in the right superior frontal gyrus (SFG) and left IFG. In comparison, those exposed to deprivation-related adversity (8 datasets, 416 participants) showed increased functional activity in the bilateral amygdala, right postcentral gyrus, and left superior temporal gyrus. However, no significant functional abnormalities were observed in individuals exposed to mixed adversity types (30 datasets, 1859 participants). For structural analysis, individuals exposed to threat-related adversity (9 datasets, 398 participants) demonstrated increased GMV in the right precentral gyrus and decreased GMV in the right middle occipital gyrus and right primary visual cortex (V1), while individuals exposed to mixed adversity types (22 datasets, 1516 participants) displayed increased GMV in the right parahippocampal gyrus.

In the diagnostic status-based subgroup analysis, patients with PTSD (11 datasets, 418 participants) showed increased functional activity in the left SFG, right rolandic operculum, left postcentral gyrus, left postcentral gyrus, and left IFG. By contrast, the healthy subgroup (17 datasets, 1040 participants) exhibited elevated activity in the right striatum. No significant functional abnormalities were identified in the depression subgroup (8 datasets, 387 participants). Notably, when studies in which the ACE-exposed and unexposed control groups differed in diagnostic status were excluded, the results remained largely consistent with the main analysis (functional analysis 49 datasets, 2986 participants; structural analysis 29 datasets, 1994 participants). Specifically, individuals exposed to ACEs exhibited significantly increased functional activity in the right amygdala (uncorrected p < 0.001; x = 30, y = 2, z = − 18; voxels = 420), increased GMV in the parahippocampal gyrus (uncorrected p < 0.001, corrected p = 0.001; x = 19, y = − 8, z = − 18; voxels = 674), and reduced GMV in the IFG (uncorrected p = 0.002; x = − 50, y = 14, z = 14; voxels = 137).

When restricting the analysis to t-fMRI studies, the results were largely consistent with those obtained from combined modalities (Additional File 1: Table S6), except that hyperactivity in the postcentral gyrus among ACEs-exposed individuals in the PTSD subgroup was no longer significant. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were conducted across different t-fMRI task domains. Tasks related to emotion processing (27 datasets, 1869 participants) revealed hyperactivity in the right IFG. Additionally, meta-analysis of memory processing tasks (7 datasets, 402 participants) identified hypoactivity in the right SFG in individuals exposed to ACEs.

Overlapping structural and functional brain abnormalities

The multimodal conjunction analysis revealed that individuals exposed to ACEs exhibited concurrent increases in functional activity and GMV in the right parahippocampal gyrus compared to unexposed controls in the pooled sample. These overlapping abnormalities were significantly associated with negative emotion (Z = 5.502), AD (Z = 5.530), and fear (Z = 4.920), among others (Table 1, Fig. 2c). Further subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate overlapping functional and structural abnormalities across specific subgroups, including children/adolescents, adults, threat-related adversity, and various t-fMRI task domains. However, no significant overlap was identified within any of these subgroups.

Meta-regression and robustness analyses

At a threshold of uncorrected p < 0.0005, the meta-regression analyses did not identify any significant functional or structural changes associated with demographic (age, percentage of females, and intelligence quotient) or clinical variables (onset age and duration of ACEs). No brain regions exhibited significant heterogeneity (I 2 < 50%) or publication bias (p > 0.05 and symmetrical funnel plots) (Additional file 1: Table S7, Fig. S1–2), indicating robust findings. Jackknife sensitivity analyses are presented in Table 1, further confirming the high replicability and reliability of the findings.

Relevance of brain abnormalities to neurotransmitter distribution

While no significant associations were identified between receptor/transporter densities and ACEs-related functional abnormalities in the pooled sample, subgroup analyses revealed distinct patterns. In the childhood/adolescent subgroup, functional abnormalities were significantly associated with DAT (rho = 0.43, p < 0.001) and SERT (rho = 0.34, p < 0.001). In the deprivation adversity subgroup, significant correlations were observed with DAT (rho = 0.53, p < 0.001), SERT (rho = 0.49, p < 0.001), and D2 (rho = 0.38, p < 0.001). Among healthy individual subgroup, functional differences were significantly associated with DAT (rho = 0.35, p < 0.001) and negatively associated with 5-HT2A receptor (rho = − 0.33, p < 0.001). Further analyses based on t-fMRI domains demonstrated that functional differences were positively associated with 5-HT4 receptor (rho = 0.29, p = 0.001) during memory processing tasks.

Structural abnormalities in ACEs-exposed individuals demonstrated significant correlations with several neurotransmitter systems in the pooled sample, including 5-HT2A receptor (rho = − 0.41, p < 0.001), GABA(A) receptors (rho = − 0.37, p < 0.001), SERT (rho = 0.36, p < 0.001), and DAT (rho = 0.29, p = 0.002). Subgroup analyses further revealed specific patterns. In the childhood/adolescent subgroup, structural abnormalities were significantly associated with 5-HT2A receptor (rho = − 0.31, p < 0.001) and NAT (rho = − 0.29, p = 0.001). In the adult subgroup, significant correlations were observed with 5-HT2A receptor (rho = − 0.37, p < 0.001) and GABA(A) receptors (rho = − 0.32, p < 0.001). For the threat adversity subgroup, structural abnormalities were significantly associated with 5-HT2A receptor (rho = − 0.43, p < 0.001), GABA(A) receptors (rho = − 0.41, p < 0.001), and 5-HT1B receptor (rho = − 0.36, p = 0.001). Additionally, in the mixed adversity subgroup, significant associations were identified with mGluR5 (rho = − 0.34, p = 0.001) and 5-HT2A receptor (rho = − 0.27, p = 0.003).

Relevance of brain abnormalities to gene expression profiles

PLS1 accounted for 32.37% of the variance in functional alterations observed in individuals exposed to ACEs (p = 0.004; corrected for spatial autocorrelation using 5000 permutation tests). Additionally, PLS1 explained 23.35% of the variance in structural alterations in ACEs-exposed individuals (p < 0.001; corrected for spatial autocorrelation using 5000 permutation tests). Results from the GO and KEGG enrichment analysis are detailed in Fig. 4b and Additional file 1: Table S8. Genes associated with functional alterations (top genes FGFR1, AMBRA1, LYSMD4, PDS5B, DHRS11) were significantly enriched in neurodevelopmental biological processes (e.g., forebrain development, brain development, head development) and KEGG pathways related to psychiatric and autoimmune disorders (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, alcoholism, long-term depression) (Benjamini–Hochberg corrected q < 0.05). Similarly, genes linked to structural alterations (top genes ZHX2, SLC27A6, PTAR1, FGFR1, ZBTB20) were enriched in biological processes such as gliogenesis, brain development, and head development, as well as KEGG pathways including alcoholism, PI3K-Akt signaling, circadian entrainment, and long-term depression (Benjamini–Hochberg corrected q < 0.05).

Discussion

This study yielded several key findings regarding multimodal neuroimaging signatures for ACEs and their molecular mechanisms. First, meta-analytic evidence revealed that individuals exposed to ACEs exhibited increased functional activity in the amygdala, increased GMV in the parahippocampal gyrus, and reduced GMV in the IFG and supramarginal gyrus, with overlapping abnormalities identified in the parahippocampal gyrus. Second, population- and modality-specific brain alterations were identified, providing a more nuanced insights into how ACEs affect neurodevelopment. Third, structural abnormalities were spatially associated with dopaminergic, serotonergic, and GABAergic neurotransmitter systems. Fourth, enrichment analyses indicated that genes correlated with brain abnormalities were significantly enriched in several neurodevelopmental processes, pathways associated with immunometabolism, autoimmune, and psychiatric disorders. These findings highlight the profound impact of ACEs on neurodevelopment, potentially contributing to altered resilience or susceptibility to psychiatric disorders.

The functional meta-analysis revealed significant amygdala hyperactivity in individuals exposed to ACEs. The amygdala contains a high density of glucocorticoid receptors on stress-sensitive pyramidal cells, making it particularly susceptible to early-life stress [48]. Indeed, functional abnormalities of the amygdala have been widely reported in previous ROI-based studies [49]. ACEs are often conceptualized as threats to survival, physical integrity, or sense of self [10]. As a core region within the brain’s threat-detection and response circuit, amygdala hyperactivity may reflect heightened vigilance to potential environmental threats or increased emotional reactivity [6, 7]. While such heightened sensitivity may initially provide an adaptive advantage for survival in adverse environments, its persistent overactivation can increase vulnerability to future stressors, contributing to the development of psychopathology [50, 51]. Therefore, functional amygdala abnormalities has been implicated as a biomarker for PTSD [52], and is consistently observed in individuals with anxiety [53], mood disorder [54], and substance use disorder [55]. Recent studies have shifted focus toward the translational potential of amygdala research [56]. A recent open-label clinical trial investigating closed-loop neuromodulation therapy for treatment-resistant PTSD demonstrated that two individuals who received closed-loop amygdala-targeted stimulation exhibited clinically meaningful reductions in PTSD symptoms [57]. Moreover, advances in human non-invasive brain stimulation (NIBS), such as low-intensity focused ultrasound and temporally interfering stimulation, offer the ability to target small and deep human brain circuitry, paving the way for translational research [58, 59]. However, further longitudinal studies are needed to establish a causal relationship between ACEs and amygdala alterations before the amygdala can be confirmed as a promising NIBS target.

Across the structural meta-analysis, ACEs exposure was associated with decreased GMV in the IFG, a region within the PFC. Attenuated development of the PFC, including decreases in GMV, cortical thickness, and blood flow, have been consistently reported in ACE-exposed individuals [6, 60]. While prior research has primarily focused on the anterior cingulate cortex, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and orbitofrontal cortex, the IFG has received relatively little attention [60]. Structural abnormalities in this region have mainly been identified through exploratory whole-brain analyses, including changes in GMV [61], cortical thickness [61], and curvature [62]. As a key cortical node in emotion and cognitive control circuits, abnormalities in the IFG may contribute to ACE-related psychiatric disorders [63, 64], suggesting that it may serve as a signature of vulnerability. The structural meta-analysis also revealed increased GMV in the parahippocampal gyrus (BA28, entorhinal cortex), which showed both structural and functional alterations in the conjunction analysis. Notably, these alterations remained significant under more stringent correction thresholds in both the main and several subgroup analyses, suggesting that abnormalities in the parahippocampal gyrus may represent a more robust signature of ACE-exposed individuals. While hippocampal GMV abnormalities in ACEs-exposed individuals have been extensively studied, findings remain inconsistent [6, 60]. In contrast, research on the parahippocampal gyrus remains limited. The parahippocampal gyrus, particularly the entorhinal cortex, serves as a critical interface between the neocortex and hippocampus, playing a key role in declarative memory processing [65]. Meta-analyses have also identified abnormal parahippocampal activity in maltreated individuals [66]. Preclinical studies have further elucidated the role of the entorhinal cortex-ventral hippocampal circuit in modulating traumatic memories and facilitating adaptive responses, highlighting its key role in patients with PTSD [67].

A major source of heterogeneity across studies is age, as individuals at different stages of brain development exhibit distinct patterns of brain alterations. Previous study has shown that maltreated adults and adolescents differ in GMV within the middle cingulate/paracingulate gyri, and visual cortex [9]. Our finding revealed that ACEs were associated with abnormalities in regions of the theory of mind (ToM) network among children and adolescents, including the superior temporal gyrus and inferior parietal lobule (angular gyrus and supramarginal gyrus) [68–70]. ACEs are well known to impair social cognition [71], including ToM—the ability to infer the intentions, beliefs, and desires of oneself and others, and to recognize that others have minds distinct from one’s own [72]. Prior studies have also highlighted the impact of ACEs on the ToM network [69, 73]. The acquisition and refinement of ToM abilities typically occur throughout childhood, spanning from kindergarten age to the end of secondary school [74], which may account for the more significant abnormalities observed in children and adolescents. Furthermore, ACE-induced alterations in the ToM network closely resemble patterns observed in psychiatric disorders [75, 76]. These findings suggest that ACEs may disrupt the ToM network, potentially leading to impaired ToM abilities and contributing to psychopathology. In contrast, structural and functional abnormalities in the adult subgroup were predominantly observed in the IFG, consistent with the main analysis. Overall, our results suggest that ACEs may primarily affect ToM-related brain regions during early development, while impacting emotion and cognitive control-related regions in adulthood. However, more definitive interpretations would require normative brain development models derived from longitudinal samples as a reference.

Another key factor of heterogeneity is the type of ACEs. Emerging neurodevelopmental models classify ACEs into threat-related and deprivation-related adversities [10]. In the threat-related ACEs group, hypoactivity in the SFG (BA10, anterior PFC) and reduced GMV in the ILF were observed, while the deprivation-related ACE group primarily exhibited amygdala hyperactivity, consistent with the main analysis. Preclinical studies suggested that threat-related adversity, primarily through activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, significantly affects the amygdala, hippocampus, and PFC [10, 35, 77], while deprivation-related adversity, characterized by reduced sensory and cognitive input, primarily impairs the association cortex [10, 35]. The discrepancies between our findings and preclinical hypotheses may stem from the limited studies in the deprivation subgroup (n = 8) and the complexity of adversity exposure in humans. For instance, although institutionalization is often classified as deprivation-related adversity, it also involves disrupted expectations of caregiver presence during early development—a dimension that is not fully encapsulated by the deprivation-threat dichotomy [10]. Therefore, further research is required to disentangle the distinct impacts of different adversity types on brain development in human. Additionally, reduced GMV in the V1 was identified in individuals exposed to threat-related ACEs. Previous study has demonstrated that direct threats, such as sexual abuse, are associated with V1 GMV reductions, with the extent of reduction correlating with exposure duration and corresponding deficits in visual memory [78]. Preclinical research further indicates that the local corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF)-CRF receptor 1 system within V1 may mediate the long-term effects of early adversity on visual plasticity [79].

When t-fMRI tasks were stratified by domain, the findings exhibited variability, consistent with prior research [7]. Specifically, hyperactivity in the IFG was observed in emotion-processing tasks. Difficulties in emotion processing are well-documented features of psychiatric disorders, including anxiety, mood disorders, and schizophrenia [80–82]. Longitudinal studies of maltreated children further suggest that alterations in emotion regulation may act as risk factors of resilience for future psychopathology [83]. Consistent with our results, previous studies have reported abnormal functional connectivity and activity in the IFG in individuals with ACEs [49, 84]. Additionally, recent transdiagnostic meta-analyses have identified the IFG as a key region within the emotion regulation circuit [85]. These findings collectively suggest that altered IFG activity may represent a potential signature of emotion-processing alterations in individuals exposed to ACEs. Across the domain of memory processing, ACEs were associated with hypoactivity in the SFG (BA8), a key PFC region involved in working memory [86, 87]. Hypoactivity in the PFC has been extensively reported in ACE-related studies and summarized in multiple reviews and meta-analyses [6, 88, 89]. Despite the relatively small sample size in this subgroup (n = 6), these findings provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying ACE-related memory disruptions.

To investigate the potential mechanisms underlying ACEs-related brain abnormalities, we conducted spatial correlation analyses with nuclear imaging-derived neurotransmitter maps. Although preclinical studies have examined the effects of ACEs on neurotransmitter systems [23, 90], no prior cross-modal analyses have directly Linked ACEs-related brain alterations to nuclear imaging atlases in humans. Our results revealed significant spatial associations between ACEs-related structural abnormalities and the serotonergic, dopaminergic, and GABAergic systems. These findings are consistent with animal studies showing that ACEs can alter 5-HT2A receptor binding in the striatum and prefrontal cortex [23], and SERT mRNA expression in the dorsal raphe nucleus [91]. Furthermore, extensive evidence has highlighted the pivotal role of serotonergic dysfunction in the pathophysiology of psychiatric disorders [92–94]. The SERT, located on the axon terminals and somas of serotonergic neurons, facilitates the 5-HT from the synaptic cleft [93, 95]. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors increase extracellular 5-HT levels by inhibiting SERT, thereby exerting antidepressant and anxiolytic effects [95]. The 5-HT also contributes to synapse formation, connectivity, and neural circuit development. It modulates neuronal plasticity by regulating cell adhesion molecules and brain-derived neurotrophic factor [96, 97]. Animal experiments have further shown that SERT gene deletion in non-serotonin-producing neurons leads to long-lasting alterations in cortical neuron organization and dendritic arborization in sensory cortices [98]. In contrast, research on the effects of ACEs on the dopaminergic and GABAergic systems remains limited. One study suggested that GABA(A) receptors play a role in regulating stress-induced activation of the HPA axis, potentially contributing to psychiatric disorders [99]. The exploration of receptor transporters has emerged as a realm of interest for developing novel approaches for psychiatric disorders [100], and our findings provide a potential theoretical foundation.

Further neuroimaging–transcriptomic analyses revealed that genes associated with ACEs-related brain abnormalities were significantly enriched in pathways implicated in psychiatric disorders. Several top ACE-related genes, including AMBRA1, LYSMD4, ZHX2, and PTAR1, have previously been identified as risk genes for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression, and AD in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [101–104]. This gene set also aligns with earlier findings showing that epigenetic signatures and genes linked to maltreatment-associated brain connectivity alterations are involved in nervous system development and organismal growth [105, 106]. Notably, FGFR1 has been associated with brain developmental features such as vertex-wise sulcal depth and cortical surface area in previous studies [107, 108]. In addition, genes related to ACEs-associated brain alterations were enriched in pathways connected to autoimmune diseases and immunometabolic signaling, particularly the PI3K-Akt pathway. For instance, PDS5B has been identified in earlier research as a risk gene for autoimmune diseases [109]. Indeed, immune dysregulation has been implicated not only in ACEs but also in broader psychiatric pathophysiology [110, 111]. Previous studies have demonstrated bidirectional phenotypic and genetic associations between stress-related psychiatric disorders and autoimmune conditions [112, 113]. Several psychiatric risk genes have been linked to the PI3K-Akt signaling [114], and PI3K-Akt signaling is reported in various psychiatric disorders [115]. Overall, these findings suggest that disruptions in neurodevelopmental and immunometabolic pathways may contribute to the long-term mental health consequences of ACEs.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, in accordance with the standard guidelines for neuroimaging meta-analyses [34], only studies employing whole-brain analyses were included. While ROI studies may overemphasize predefined regions, it is noteworthy that prior ROI-based research has primarily focused on the amygdala, hippocampus, and PFC, consistently highlighting the impact of ACEs on their structure and function [6, 60]. Second, the limited number of studies examining the onset age and duration of ACEs makes it difficult to accurately identify brain regions most affected by the cumulative effects of prolonged adversity. Addressing this gap in future research will be critical for better understanding the temporal dynamics of ACEs on brain development. Third, the main analysis based on the pooled sample included individuals with diverse clinical diagnoses, which limited the ability to determine whether the observed brain abnormalities were attributable to ACEs or to diagnostic factors. Although we conducted additional analyses by excluding studies with unmatched diagnostic status and by performing subgroup analyses, the limited number of available studies restricted our ability to examine each psychiatric and neurological condition separately. Future research is needed to differentiate ACE-related from diagnosis-related brain alterations across distinct clinical populations.

Conclusions

This study identified distinctive neural substrates for ACEs and highlighted variations across subpopulations defined by participant characteristics and methodological factors, offering a more refined understanding of ACEs’ impact on neurodevelopment. Cross-modal integrative analyses further emphasized the profound influence of ACEs on neurodevelopment, immunometabolism, and psychopathology, while also revealing associations between structural brain abnormalities and dopaminergic, serotonergic, and GABAergic neurotransmitter systems. This multi-level neurobiological integration advances our understanding of ACEs-related brain abnormalities and potential underlying mechanisms, offering insights that may guide future targets for ACEs-related disorders.
