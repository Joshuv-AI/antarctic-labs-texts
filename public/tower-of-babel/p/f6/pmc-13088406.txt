Road safety threats and occupational health risk associated with psychoactive substance use and dependence risk among commercial drivers: WHO ASSIST evidence from Ghana
Authors: Joyce Bebangnidong, Forgive Awo Norvivor, Ali Umar, Daniel Addae Bautsi, Vincent Uwumboriyhie Gmayinaam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommercial drivers increasingly use psychoactive substances, raising the risk of road crashes and occupational injuries. Globally, 1.3 million people die in road crashes each year (90% in low- and middle-income countries), and Africa alone accounted for 250,000 of these deaths in 2021. Ghana as of 2026 had experienced a 18.2% increase in road traffic accidents with the National Road Safety Authority reporting 13,489 road crashes and 2,494 fatalities in 2024. In the first half of 2025 alone, the country had already recorded 7,289 crashes resulting in 1,504 deaths. In Ghana, studies report high prevalence and dependence on alcohol, cannabis, and tramadol among drivers. However, few studies have categorized drivers by ASSIST dependence risk levels (low/moderate/high). This study aims to address that gap and inform road safety and occupational health policy towards achieving SDG target 3.6 on traffic fatalities.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey of 204 commercial drivers (aged ≥ 18 years) at three bus stations in Duayaw Nkwanta, Ghana. Data were collected via face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire adapted from the WHO Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST). ASSIST composite scores were calculated for each substance and categorized as low, moderate or high risk. We summarized prevalence and mean scores descriptively and used multivariable logistic regression to identify factors associated with any substance use.ResultsAmong 204 commercial drivers (mean age 33 years), 68 (33.3%) reported past-3-month use of at least one non-alcohol psychoactive substance. Mean ASSIST involvement scores were highest for cannabis and for the tramadol/coffee category, indicating greater dependence risk. Based on WHO ASSIST risk classifications, 74% of tramadol/coffee users and all cannabis users were classified as high risk, whereas alcohol users showed lower risk levels (46% high risk, 50% moderate risk, and 4% low risk). In multivariable analysis, owning one’s vehicle (aOR 0.38, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.91, p = 0.030) and having rest breaks during work (aOR 0.44, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.90, p = 0.025) were independently associated with reduced odds of non-alcohol psychoactive substance use. High-risk dependence showed a different pattern. Having rest breaks during working hours was strongly protective, with markedly lower odds of high-risk dependence (aOR 0.14, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.41). Duration at the car park was associated in crude analysis but not after adjustment (cOR 1.33, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.73; aOR 1.28, p = 0.226).ConclusionsThese findings show critical road safety and occupational health concern. High ASSIST risk scores indicate potential substance-related problems among commercial drivers. Although our study did not measure impairment, withdrawal symptoms, or crash outcomes, the presence of moderate or high-risk scores suggests a need for routine screening, brief intervention, and referral services within occupational health and transport regulatory settings. These findings should be interpreted as indicators of possible risk rather than evidence of causation or driving impairment. Targeted interventions are needed in the transport sector. Ensuring compliance with rest-break regulations and promoting driver ownership models may help mitigate stress-driven substance use. Regular screening and counselling could be integrated into occupational health programs. Addressing driver substance dependence is essential for meeting global road safety goals (SDG 3.6) and protecting worker health.Keywords: Psychoactive substance use, WHO ASSIST, Substance dependence, Commercial drivers, Road safety, Occupational health

Background

Globally, more than 7,500 work-related deaths occur daily, including approximately 1,000 resulting from occupational accidents [1]. Recent evidence shows a slight rise in fatal occupational accidents, which also encompass road traffic incidents. Road crashes alone account for about 1.35 million deaths annually worldwide. With an average mortality rate of 27.5 per 100,000 population, low-income countries experience nearly three times the fatality rate observed in high-income countries, where the rate stands at 8.3 per 100,000 [2]. Recent evidence indicates that road traffic risk in Africa remains critically high, with the Africa Status Report on Road Safety 2025 estimating that approximately 650 people die each day in road crashes across the continent and that vulnerable road users pedestrians, cyclists, and motorcyclists account for nearly half of these fatalities [3]. This report highlights that Africa continues to experience road traffic death rates nearly three times higher than high-income regions and suffers economic losses of up to 3% of GDP due to road crashes [3].

An increasing number of commercial drivers are using psychoactive substances, contributing to the growing burden of these road traffic injuries and fatalities worldwide [4, 5]. Psychoactive substances are chemical compounds that influence the central nervous system, changing brain function and behaviour [6, 7]. These include alcohol, cannabis, tramadol, cocaine, amphetamines, and, in some cases, tobacco and coffee; these, substances are commonly used by drivers despite their negative effects [8, 9]. Globally, the use of psychoactive substances is on the rise [10]. The 2023 United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) World Drug Report estimates that 316 million people aged 15–64 used drugs in the past year, a rise that exceeds the pace of population growth, and points to a growing prevalence of substance use worldwide [11]. Cannabis remains the most widely used psychoactive substance, with 244 million users, followed by opioids (61 million), amphetamine-type stimulants (30.7 million), cocaine (25 million), and ecstasy (21 million) [11]. Since the working-age population dominates the overall population abusing these drugs, substance use among workers is of serious public health implications, especially from an occupational health perspective [12]. The use of psychoactive substances can impair judgment, reduce productivity, compromise overall job performance, and significantly increase the risk of workplace injuries [13].

In Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), commercial driving is a common livelihood for young and middle-aged men [14]. The nature of this occupation is characterized by extended working hours, irregular sleep patterns, poor road infrastructure, and economic pressures and often predisposes commercial drivers to resort to psychoactive substances as a coping mechanism for stress (11). The demanding nature of this job, characterized by long working hours, irregular sleep, poor road network, and economic pressure, often leads drivers to use psychoactive substances as a means of coping with stress (11). Unfortunately, the use of these substances increases the risk of road traffic accidents, injuries, and fatalities [15]. A large majority of road traffic deaths, over 90%, occur in LMICs, resulting in more than 1.3 million fatalities annually [16]. This means someone dies about every 23 s, with millions more injured or left disabled each year [17]. In other countries such as South Africa, the impact is severe whereby; 12,436 people lost their lives in 2022 alone, equivalent to a death rate of 20.7 per 100,000, while over 100,000 others sustained serious injuries [18].

In Sub-Saharan Africa, psychoactive substance use among commercial drivers is a significant public health concern in Sub-Saharan Africa [5, 19]; the region records high rates of road traffic injuries and fatalities, with a substantial proportion linked to substance use [16]. According to a WHO regional report, Africa accounted for approximately 250,000 road traffic deaths in 2021, representing nearly one-fifth of global road fatalities [20]. Empirical studies from countries such as Nigeria and Ethiopia have reported prevalence rates of psychoactive substance use among commercial drivers exceeding 70% [5, 15].

In 2025, Ghana experienced a significant 18.2% surge in road traffic fatalities, with 2,949 deaths recorded, up from 2,494 in 2024 [21]. In Ghana, the situation mirrors continental trends, with the National Road Safety Authority reporting 13,489 crashes and 2,494 deaths in 2024, followed by 7,289 crashes and 1,504 deaths in just the first half of 2025, reflecting a worsening national safety landscape [22]. Commercial vehicles remain a significant contributor to crash statistics; for example, in the Western Region alone, 423 commercial vehicles were involved in crashes in 2024, a pattern consistent with national data showing commercial vehicles accounting for roughly one-third of all traffic crashes [23].

The high prevalence poses a significant public health concern, as evidence from a study in Tanzania revealed that drivers who consumed alcohol were six times more likely to be involved in road traffic accidents compared to those who did not drink. The high prevalence is a major threat to public health, as a study in Tanzania found that alcohol use among drivers was associated with six times increase in the likelihood of being involved in a road traffic accident compared to non-drinking drivers [24]. Ghana is not exempt from this growing concern, with studies reporting a high prevalence and dependence on psychoactive substances such as alcohol, cannabis, and tramadol among commercial drivers [25, 26]. It is clearly stated as an aim in the Sustainable Development Goal 3.6 aims to halve the global number of deaths and injuries from road traffic accidents by 2030 [27]. However, if left unaddressed, psychoactive substance use among commercial drivers will continue to undermine road safety efforts and contribute significantly to preventable injuries and fatalities [5, 15].

While psychoactive substance use has been studied in Ghana, most of the existing studies have focused on its prevalence, associated factors, and reasons for use among adolescents and tertiary students [28–30]. However, only a few studies have examined this issue among commercial drivers [25, 26, 31]. Among the limited studies involving commercial drivers, there is little evidence assessing both the extent of the various psychoactive substances used and the level of dependence, classified as low, moderate, or high risk using the WHO ASSIST tool; consequently, this study examined these to generate evidence that can inform policy and intervention. The findings are expected to inform public health knowledge, guide road safety policies, and support targeted interventions aimed at reducing the use of psychoactive substances and related risks among commercial drivers.

Methods and materials

Study design, setting, and population

This study employed a descriptive cross-sectional survey design using quantitative data collection and analysis methods. The design was appropriate because it allowed data to be collected at a single point in time to assess the types, prevalence, levels of dependence, associated factors, and reasons for psychoactive substance use among commercial drivers [32]. The study was conducted in the Tano North Municipality, located in the Ahafo Region of Ghana, with Duayaw Nkwanta as its capital. The municipality lies between latitudes 7°00′N and 7°25′N, and longitudes 2°03′W and 2°15′W, covering a land area of about 837.4 square kilometres approximately 1.8% of the region’s total land area [33]. It shares borders with Offinso North District and Ahafo Ano North Municipal in the Ashanti Region, Tano South and Asutifi South Districts in the Ahafo Region, and Sunyani Municipality in the Bono Region [33]. Duayaw Nkwanta serves as a key transport hub with a large population of commercial drivers and is home to Saint John of God Hospital, a major referral facility with one of Ghana’s top orthopaedic centres. The study targeted all adult commercial drivers, aged 18 years and above, operating within the Duayaw Nkwanta Bus Station. Both male and female drivers who provided informed consent were included. Drivers who were not available at the time of the study, were not feeling well or declined to participate were excluded from the study.

Data were collected from all three main bus stations in the Duayaw Nkwanta community to ensure a broad representation of commercial drivers. A proportion-to-size allocation was first applied to determine the number of participants to be selected from each station based on the number of drivers registered at each site. Within each station, systematic sampling was employed using the driver register as the sampling frame. A sampling interval was calculated, and the first participant was selected randomly. Subsequent participants were then selected at regular intervals. This procedure was carried out independently at all three bus stations to achieve a total sample size of 204. Data collection tools and techniques

Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire consisted of four main sections: Section A captured the socio-demographic information of the respondents; Section B assessed the types and prevalence of psychoactive substance use; Section C measured the risk level of psychoactive substance dependence using WHO ASSIST scoring; and Section D measured the self-reported reasons for psychoactive substance use among drivers. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by trained research assistants at the three main bus stations in Duayaw Nkwanta (see more details of the tool in the supplementary file S1).

The WHO ASSIST tool [34] was adapted for these purposes. Minor wording adjustments were made after pilot testing to improve clarity in the local context; however, the constructs assessed and scoring procedures were unchanged. ASSIST Specific Substance Involvement scores were computed according to WHO guidelines, with standard risk thresholds applied (alcohol classified using its recommended alcohol-specific cut-offs). Tramadol and coffee use were assessed but combined within the ‘other substances’ analytic category due to very low prevalence. Interviewers were trained 4 days on standardised administration to the survey.

Data analysis

Data were extracted, cleaned, and analyzed using Stata version 17. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, the risk levels of psychoactive substance use, and self-reported reasons for use. Frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were presented as appropriate.

Psychoactive substance dependence was assessed using an adapted WHO ASSIST tool. Composite risk scores were calculated for each substance. For alcohol, a score of 0–10 (low risk), 11–26 (moderate risk), and a score of 27 or more (high risk). For tobacco, cannabis, and tramadol/coffee (other drugs), scores were interpreted as follows: 0–3 (low risk), 4–26 (moderate risk), and 27 or more (high risk) [34]. Tramadol and coffee (caffeine) were each assessed as separate items during administration of the WHO ASSIST. However, due to very low reporting frequencies, substance-specific scores could not be analysed reliably. Therefore, after calculating ASSIST involvement scores according to WHO procedures, these low-prevalence substances were combined into an ‘other substances’ analytic category. This aggregation was performed only for statistical analysis and does not imply pharmacological similarity or a unified dependence construct.

Two outcomes were analysed: (1) any current psychoactive substance use (binary), and (2) ASSIST dependence-risk level (high vs. moderate/low). Separate logistic regression models were fitted for each outcome. Bivariate analysis was performed using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent t-tests for continuous variables to explore associations between socio-demographic characteristics and psychoactive substance use. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Selection of covariates for multivariable logistic regression was guided by prior literature and conceptual relevance rather than statistical significance at the bivariate level. All variables considered potential confounders were included in the adjusted model irrespective of their unadjusted p-values.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of commercial drivers

A total of 204 commercial drivers participated in the study, with a mean age of 33.4 years (SD 5.9). Most drivers were married (69.6%), and nearly one-third were single (30.4%). Educational attainment was generally low: 44.6% had no formal education, while 27.9% had basic education and 27.5% had secondary or tertiary-level education. The majority of participants identified as Christian (63.5%), with 36.5% identifying as Muslim. Drivers had been working in the transport industry for an average of 5.5 years (SD 1.9) and had belonged to their current car park for an average of 5.1 years (SD 1.5). Vehicle ownership was limited: 69.1% did not own the vehicle or motor bus they operated, while 30.9% reported ownership. Drivers spend an average of 5.5 h (SD 1.4) driving per day. More than half of drivers (58.3%) reported taking rest during working hours, whereas 41.7% did not. Among those who did not rest (n = 85), the predominant reason was economic pressure, with 61 of 85 drivers citing the need to “make profit on account.” A smaller proportion indicated having no specific reason (18%) or not feeling tired (11%) (see details in Table 1).

Psychoactive substance used by commercial drivers (n = 68)

A higher proportion of drivers reported using substances while driving, particularly alcohol (73.5%) and other drugs (30.9%). In addition, 61.8% of respondents expressed a strong desire or urge to use alcohol, suggesting signs of dependency. Cannabis users were especially affected, 88.2% reported that their use had interfered with daily responsibilities. The ASSIST risk scores indicated the highest mean risk for cannabis use, 49.4(12.5), followed by tramadol/coffee, highlighting the elevated health and safety risks associated with these substances.

Level of risk of dependence on psychoactive substances among commercial drivers

The analysis revealed varying levels of dependence risk across the different psychoactive substances, as shown in Fig. 1. Among drivers who used tramadol and coffee, the majority (74%) were at high risk of dependence, while 26% were at moderate risk. Among drivers who reported cannabis use, all users were classified as high-risk on the ASSIST screening (10/10, 100%); however, this finding is based on a very small number of respondents and should be interpreted as descriptive rather than conclusive. Among tobacco users, 50% were at high risk, 43% at moderate risk, and 7% at low risk. Alcohol users had the lowest proportion at high risk (46%), with 50% at moderate risk and 4% at low risk of dependence.

Socio-demographic characteristics of commercial drivers stratified by psychoactive substance use

Out of the 204 commercial drivers, 33% reported using psychoactive substances. Drivers who used these substances were slightly younger on average (32.8 years) compared to non-users (33.7 years), though the difference was not statistically significant.

There were no significant differences in marital status, religion, education level, driving experience, or number of years spent at the car park between users and non-users. However, substance use was more common among drivers who did not own their vehicle (p = 0.001), and among those who did not take rest breaks during working hours (p = 0.001).

Among drivers who reported not resting during work hours, most (52%) said it was due to the need to make more profit, suggesting that economic pressure may influence both rest patterns and substance use.

Age, marital status, religion, education, driving experience, years at the car park, and daily driving hours were not significantly associated with substance use after adjustment. Ownership of a motorbus was protective. Drivers who owned their vehicle had lower odds of psychoactive substance use compared with those who did not (aOR 0.38, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.91, p = 0.030). This corresponds to about a 62% reduction in the odds of substance use. Having rest breaks during working hours was also protective. Drivers reporting rest periods had reduced odds of substance use (aOR 0.44, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.90, p = 0.025). The magnitude suggests approximately a 56% lower likelihood compared with drivers without rest.

High-risk dependence showed a different pattern. The strongest predictor was having rest during working hours. Drivers who had rest breaks had markedly lower odds of high-risk dependence (aOR 0.14, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.41, p < 0.001). This corresponds to an 86% reduction in the odds of high-risk dependence. Years at the car park showed an association at the crude level (cOR 1.33, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.73, p = 0.030) but lost significance after adjustment (aOR 1.28, p = 0.226).

Reasons for use of psychoactive drugs among commercial drivers enrolled in the study

Figure 2 shows the reasons why commercial drivers use different psychoactive substances. Among those who drink alcohol, the majority (82.7%) said they use it to relieve stress, and about 61% use it to help them relax. For cigarette smokers, over half (54%) said they smoke to feel high, while about 23% said they smoke because they feel they cannot do without it. Most of the drivers who use marijuana (89%) said they do so to feel high or get into the mood, and 78% said it helps them stay alert. As for tramadol, a large number (85%) said they take it to relieve pain, while about 36% said they use it to stay awake and focused.

Discussion

This study adds new evidence to the limited body of literature on psychoactive substance use among commercial drivers in Ghana. It provides a broader understanding by examining the prevalence, risk levels of dependence, associated factors, and underlying reasons for psychoactive substance use.

The findings revealed that more than one-thirds of the drivers had used at least one psychoactive substance, with alcohol, tramadol mixed with coffee, and cannabis being the most reported. A considerable proportion of these drivers exhibited moderate to high dependence levels, particularly on tramadol and cannabis. The study further identified vehicle ownership and the availability of rest breaks during work hours as protective factors against substance use. Many of these drivers showed moderate to high levels of dependence, particularly for tramadol and cannabis. The study also found that owning a vehicle and having rest breaks during work hours were protective factors against substance use. Additionally, most drivers reported using these substances to manage stress, stay alert, relieve pain, or feel high. These findings are consistent with earlier studies that have reported similar patterns of psychoactive substance use, motivations, and associated risk factors among commercial drivers [5, 15, 25].

The observed high prevalence of psychoactive substance use in this study is alarming. Alcohol consumption was reported by 83% of drivers, while 31% indicated using a combination of tramadol and coffee. Additionally, 88% of cannabis users acknowledged that their drug use had interfered with their daily responsibilities. The high prevalence of psychoactive substance use observed in this study is concerning. Alcohol use was reported by 83% of the drivers, 31% drivers reported using a combination of tramadol and coffee, and 88% of cannabis users indicated that their drug use had interfered with their daily responsibilities. Similar observations have been reported in Africa and Europe. A systematic review in Africa found tramadol use among commercial motorcyclists ranging from 7% to 35% [35]. Likewise, a cross-sectional study in Denmark reported alcohol as the most frequently used substance among drivers, with a prevalence of 89% [36]. These findings suggest that psychoactive substance use has become a common part of the daily routine for many commercial drivers. While this study found a high prevalence of psychoactive substance use, particularly alcohol, among commercial drivers, a cross-sectional study in Nigeria reported a lower alcohol use prevalence of 50% [5]. This variation may be due to differences in measurement tools, as the present study used the WHO ASSIST tool, whereas the Nigerian study did not specify its method.

Analysis using the WHO ASSIST tool revealed that 74% of drivers who used tramadol and coffee were at high risk of dependence. This is consistent with a mixed-methods study in Ghana, which found that half of tramadol abusers were classified as high risk for dependence [26]. Similarly, a qualitative study in Kumasi among commercial vehicle operators reported persistent non-medical tramadol use, with participants expressing no intention to quit due to reasons such as economic benefits and perceived enhancement of sexual performance [37]. These findings highlight a serious public health concern, as high-risk dependence increases the likelihood of withdrawal symptoms, impaired judgment, and reduced work performance, all of which can compromise road safety. All drivers who reported using cannabis were also classified as high risk for dependence. A similar finding was reported in Jamaica, where 87% of drivers who drove under the influence of cannabis were heavy users of the drug. The study noted that, out of 10 Jamaican drivers, 9 who were cannabis users were heavy users [38]. In contrast, a cross-sectional study among college students in the United States reported a lower risk level, with 72% of those who drove after marijuana use classified as high risk [39]. Such heavy cannabis use is concerning, as it has been linked to impaired thinking, slower reaction times, and poor coordination, all of which can undermine safe driving [32].

In this study, drivers who owned their vehicles were 59% less likely to use psychoactive substances. This finding aligns with evidence from a case study of commercial drivers in Ghana, which identified pressure from vehicle owners as one of the most common reasons for substance use. In the present study, drivers who owned their vehicles had a 59% lower likelihood of using psychoactive substances. This finding is supported by a case study among commercial drivers in Ghana, which identified pressure from vehicle owners as one of the most common reasons for substance use [25]. This suggests that vehicle ownership may reduce work-related stress, as drivers are not compelled to meet tight financial targets set by others. Lower stress levels may, in turn, decrease the perceived need for psychoactive substances.

Similarly, drivers who had rest breaks during their workday were less likely to use psychoactive substances. This contrasts with findings from a cross-sectional study in Ethiopia, which reported that drivers who took rest breaks during work hours were twice as likely to use such substances [15]. Interestingly, that same study found that drivers who had six or more hours of night’s rest were less likely to use psychoactive substances. This suggests that adequate night rest may be a more effective strategy for controlling psychoactive substance use among drivers than short breaks during the day. Adequate night rest may be more effective than short daytime breaks for controlling psychoactive substance use among drivers. Emerging evidence suggests that environmental and temporal factors may exacerbate psychoactive substance-related driving risks. Adverse weather has been shown to intensify alcohol-related crash risk by impairing perception and decision-making [40], while environmental stressors such as extreme heat or cold may increase substance use among commercial drivers as a coping response [41]. Substance use also occurs more frequently at night [42], and night-time heat has been associated with elevated road traffic mortality [43]. These overlapping risk pathways support our conclusion that ensuring adequate night rest may reduce drivers’ exposure to conditions that heighten psychoactive substance use and its related dangers.

Alcohol, used by the majority of drivers, was mainly consumed to relieve stress (82.7%) and to help them relax (61%). This is consistent with a descriptive cross-sectional study among commercial drivers in Abia State, Nigeria, where stress relief and relaxation were also cited as primary reasons for substance use [5]. In contrast, a three-month cross-sectional study in a teaching hospital among patients involved in road accidents found that alcohol was often consumed to improve sociability and boost confidence [44]. While stress management is a major reason for alcohol use among commercial drivers, social factors may be more influential in other groups. Tramadol, on the other hand, was primarily taken to relieve pain (85%) and maintain alertness (36%). A mixed-methods study in Ghana supports this finding, reporting that commercial drivers used tramadol mainly for pain relief and to stay awake during long hours of driving [26]. In Jirapa, Ghana, another study found that tramadol use was mostly for recreation and to improve sexual performance [45]. This means that, for commercial drivers, tramadol use is linked more to the demands of their work, while in other groups it is often for social or personal reasons [46]. Most drivers who used cannabis (89%) did so to feel high or get into the mood, while 78% said it helped them stay alert. A cross-sectional study in Canada reported a similar belief, where some drivers thought cannabis made them more cautious and alert [47]. Motivations for cannabis use vary; among university students, it is often used to get energized to study and manage stress [48], while in some groups, it is used to enhance social experiences and enjoyment [49]. For commercial drivers, the belief that cannabis improves alertness can be misleading because it can reduce coordination, slow reaction time, and increase the risk of accidents [50].

Limitations

This study relied on self-reported data, which may be affected by recall bias or underreporting, as some participants may view substance use particularly cannabis as a sensitive issue. The study was conducted in three registered bus stations within one municipality; therefore, findings may not generalise to informal or unregistered drivers, long-distance transport workers, or drivers operating in other regions. Future studies should consider longitudinal designs and, where resources permit, incorporate laboratory testing to objectively determine substance levels in blood.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study highlights a high prevalence and risk of psychoactive substance use among commercial drivers, with alcohol, tramadol, and cannabis being the most commonly used most used. The findings show that poor working conditions, including lack of rest and vehicle ownership, contribute to substance use. Addressing these issues through targeted interventions, education, and support systems is indispensable to improving driver well-being and ensuring road safety. It is recommended that transport unions and public health agencies collaborate to implement routine education on the dangers of psychoactive substance use, alongside regular health screenings that include mental health and substance use assessments. Future control efforts by transport unions should also focus on improving working conditions by ensuring adequate rest periods and providing access to counselling services, which can help reduce drivers’ reliance on substances as coping mechanisms.
