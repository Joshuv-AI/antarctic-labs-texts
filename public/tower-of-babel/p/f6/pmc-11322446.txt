Compact and transportable system for detecting lead-shielded highly enriched uranium using 252Cf rotation method with a water Cherenkov neutron detector
Authors: Kosuke Tanabe, Masao Komeda, Yosuke Toh, Yasunori Kitamura, Tsuyoshi Misawa, Ken’ichi Tsuchiya, Hiroshi Sagara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe global challenge of on-site detection of highly enriched uranium (HEU), a substance with considerable potential for unauthorized use in nuclear security, is a critical concern. Traditional passive nondestructive assay (NDA) techniques, such as gamma-ray spectroscopy with high-purity germanium detectors, face significant challenges in detecting HEU when it is shielded by heavy metals. Addressing this critical security need, we introduce an on-site detection method for lead-shielded HEU employing a transportable NDA system that utilizes the 252Cf rotation method with a water Cherenkov neutron detector. This cost-effective NDA system is capable of detecting 4.17 g of 235U within a 12 min measurement period using a 252Cf source of 3.7 MBq. Integrating this system into border control measures can enhance the prevention of HEU proliferation significantly and offer robust deterrence against nuclear terrorism.Keywords: Nuclear security, Cherenkov detector, Nondestructive assay (NDA), Highly enriched uranium (HEU), Neutron, NonproliferationSubject terms: Nuclear energy, Engineering, Applied physics

Introduction

The global priority for enhanced nuclear security has led to considerable efforts in developing nondestructive assay (NDA) techniques for nuclear materials1–3, a crucial development given the heightened risk that smuggled nuclear materials might be utilized for malicious purposes4. The challenge of detecting highly enriched uranium (HEU) concealed with heavy metals has been identified as a critical concern in nuclear security, particularly since the 9/11 terrorist attacks5–7. Despite this, the on-site detection of such concealed HEU poses significant challenges, necessitating further advancements in nuclear security measures8,9. On-site detection involves inspections at various locations—such as terrorism scenes, customs, airports, seaports, and border checkpoints—employing portable devices. According to the International Atomic Energy Agency’s (IAEA) Incident and Trafficking Database (ITDB)10, between 1993 and 2022, 4075 instances of illegal activities such as illegal possession, illicit trafficking, theft, and loss of nuclear and other radioactive materials were reported. Fourteen percent of these incidents involved nuclear materials, including 59 cases involving HEU and plutonium. Only a few cases, such as the 1994 smuggling of weapon-grade plutonium (560 g of plutonium–uranium mixed oxide powder) in Munich, Germany, involved the trade of large quantities (several hundred grams to kilograms) of nuclear material, and the majority were illicit cases involving smaller quantities of nuclear material.

NDA techniques for detecting nuclear materials are categorized into passive and active methods11–13. Passive methods directly detect spontaneous neutron or gamma-ray emissions from nuclear materials14–16, whereas active methods detect radiation from nuclear reactions triggered by external neutron or gamma-ray irradiation17. Active methods, including the differential die-away18, fast neutron direct interrogation19, and nuclear resonance fluorescence20 techniques, offer promising solutions for detecting concealed nuclear materials with superior sensitivity compared to passive methods. However, the application of these methods has been largely restricted to stationary settings, such as material accountancy, due to the prohibitive cost, size, and weight of the required equipment, coupled with the lack of a suitable external radiation source for on-site applications21–23. Radiation generators have lower flexibility of use than radioisotopes (RIs) in terms of cost, regulation, and portability. The fast neutron coincidence collar (FNCL) method is a portable active interrogation method using RI24. The low number of neutrons per decay of Am–Li sources requires the use of extremely high radioactivity sources to obtain practical neutron yields, involving strict radiation control. Additionally, a 252Cf source is not appropriate for neutron coincidence counting of FNCL method, and the main target for measurement is a fuel assembly, not a few grams of nuclear material.

In response to the need for improved methods to detect concealed nuclear materials, we proposed and validated an innovative, transportable active neutron method called the 252Cf rotation method25. This system comprises a neutron detector and a rotation device for the neutron source, generating pseudo-pulsed neutrons by rotating a 3.7 MBq 252Cf source. RIs such as a 252Cf source offer greater operational flexibility in terms of cost and regulations than radiation generators such as a D–T neutron tube. Time spectra of neutron counts, synchronized with the rotation timing, are obtained by the neutron detector, enabling the detection of nuclear material through spectral shape analysis26. Nb and Na are defined as the neutron counts measured within a certain time interval before and after the moment when the rotating source is closest to the target, respectively. Since fission reactions are induced by thermal neutrons generated through slowing-down, the reaction generally occurs in the latter time interval, and fission neutrons are observed in Na and the effect increases with rotation speed. In other words, the ratio of Na to Nb increases only when nuclear material is present, resulting in a more asymmetric shape of the neutron time spectrum at high rotation speed. Primarily designed for secondary inspections at border security checkpoints, this method can identify suspicious objects that have already been screened by passive gamma-ray detectors and X-ray inspection equipment, thus targeting the smuggling of gram quantities of concealed HEU. The sensitivity and measurement time of this technique primarily depend on the detection efficiency of the neutron detector because of an operational limit to the intensity of the 252Cf source to ensure safety during field operations. Traditional 3He detector bank, previously utilized for detecting fission neutrons, required approximately 60 min (when using a 3.7 MBq 252Cf source) to detect natural uranium containing 56.7 g of 235U, proving impractical for rapid inspection requirements due to their low fast neutron detection efficiency. The bank consisted of six 3He detectors (1 m sensitive length, 4 atm) enclosed in a 1.2 cm thick polyethylene box with a 0.1 cm thick cadmium plate attached to its surface. The option to increase the number of 3He detectors was deemed unfeasible due to cost and portability constraints, and alternatives such as 10B-doped detectors27,28, 6Li-containing detectors29,30, and organic scintillators31 were unable to meet these challenges effectively. 10B detector has low detection efficiency and toxicity issues in the form of BF3 gas, and 6Li detector also has issues of low detection efficiency and difficulty in increasing effective area (geometric efficiency) due to gamma-ray sensitivity. Organic scintillators have the challenge of low cross-sections for elastic scattering (proton recoil) reaction, and the combustibility of liquid scintillator is an obstacle for field operation.

To address these limitations, we developed a portable water Cherenkov neutron detector (WCND) specialized for the 252Cf rotation method, offering a viable solution for field applications in nuclear security32. The WCND consists of a container filled with Gd-dissolved water and photodetectors. Despite its high sensitivity to gamma-rays and large tank volume, which previously limited its use as a neutron detector in the field of nuclear security33,34, we realized a significant enhancement in fast neutron detection efficiency—over four times higher than conventional 3He detectors—at less than a tenth of the fabrication cost, effectively suppressing 2.223 MeV gamma-rays35. Through a demonstration experiment using the WCND in conjunction with the rotation device, we detected a natural uranium target containing 56.7 g of 235U in just 15 min using a 3.7 MBq 252Cf source. This demonstrates the 252Cf rotation method’s potential for on-site NDA operations.

Our current research presents the on-site detection of lead-shielded HEU (containing 4.17 g of 235U), representing a significant challenge in nuclear security due to its high unauthorized use value and detection difficulty. Initial gamma-ray spectroscopy using a germanium semiconductor detector (Ge detector) confirmed the difficulty of detecting such materials with even the most advanced passive detectors. However, employing the 252Cf rotation method with the WCND, we successfully detected an HEU sample containing less than 15 g of 235U—below the IAEA Category III threshold36—within a 20 min measurement period, highlighting the method’s effectiveness and potential in enhancing global nuclear security measures.

Results

On-site measurement of HEU using Ge detector

Prior to demonstrating on-site detection of lead-shielded HEU, we conducted a gamma-ray spectrometric measurement using a Ge detector to evaluate the effectiveness and innovation of the 252Cf rotation method combined with the WCND. We established a sufficient lead shielding thickness to block gamma rays emitted from HEU (containing 4.17 g of 235U, with enrichment of 92.7%, and dimensions of 63.5 × 63.5 × 2 mm). The experimental setup and analysis procedures are detailed in the Methods section. Figure 1 presents the gamma-ray spectra from the HEU across lead plate thicknesses of 0.0, 7.0, and 8.0 mm. Without lead shielding (0.0 mm thickness), gamma-rays at 143.76, 163.33, 185.72, and 205.31 keV, primarily originating from 235U, were detected. Additionally, a gamma-ray at 1001.03 keV from 234mPa, a daughter nuclide of 238U, was observed. Gamma-rays of 185.72 and 1001.03 keV, having high emission rates, are practically used to quantify uranium enrichment37. As the lead thickness increased, a notable decrease in gamma-ray counts occurred, leading to the complete suppression of gamma-ray peaks around 185.72 keV at an 8.0 mm thickness. Table 1 shows the analytical results of the net area counts for the 185.72 and 1001.03 keV peaks. The net area’s uncertainty is calculated as the square root of the sum of the squares of the gross area’s uncertainty and the weighted error of the background (BG). Net area counts are defined as gross area counts minus BG counts, and gross area counts are the sum of the counts in the region of interest (ROI). At an 8.0 mm thickness, the counts in the 1001.03 keV peak region were 20 ± 14, comparable to the surrounding BG events. Comparative analysis with the American National Standards Institute (ANSI) nuclear data library38 identified three nuclides—235U, 238U, and 40K—in the 0.0 and 7.0 mm thickness conditions, while only 40K was detectable at 8.0 mm. This study confirmed the challenge of detecting HEU concealed by 8.0 mm thick lead within a 20 min passive measurement using the Ge detector, highlighting that under real-world conditions with mixed BG radiation, detecting HEU would be more challenging, even with thinner lead shielding. Henceforth, the HEU shielded by an 8.0 mm thick lead plate is referred to as the shielded HEU.

On-site detection of the HEU sample using 252Cf rotation method with WCND

The 252Cf rotation method was employed to detect an HEU sample concealed with an 8.0 mm thick lead shield. The experimental setup and the appearance of the HEU sample are illustrated in Fig. 2. Comprehensive details regarding the setup, rotation device, WCND, and analysis procedures are elaborated in the Methods section. Measurements utilized a 2.2 MBq 252Cf source and were conducted under three distinct conditions: with nuclear material (the HEU sample with a moderator), without nuclear material (absence of the HEU sample but with a moderator), and a vacant condition (neither the HEU sample nor the moderator), at both high (4000 rpm) and low (300 rpm) rotation speeds.

Figure 3a and b show the neutron count time spectra for both with and without the nuclear material conditions. Panels (a) and (b) of Fig. 3 present the results at rotation speeds of 4000 and 300 rpm, respectively. The spectra exhibited a pattern of three peaks, with valleys on either side of the central peak. The spectrum featuring nuclear material exhibited a lower central peak compared to the moderator-only setup, indicative of the HEU and lead plate’s shielding effects.

The Rab value for each condition was calculated using the central positions (tc = 6312 μs at 4000 rpm and tc = 86,560 μs at 300 rpm), determined from the data in the vacant condition. The methodology for determining these central positions is detailed in the Analysis section. Table 2 summarizes the Rab values along with their 1σ statistical errors. The Rab values were consistent within the statistical error regardless of rotation speed for the conditions without nuclear material. In the presence of nuclear material, a significant increase in the Rab values was observed only at 4000 rpm. The ratio of Rab values at 4000 rpm to those at 300 rpm indicated a statistically significant increase of 1.6% in the presence of nuclear material, demonstrating the detection of the lead-shielded HEU. These experimental results aligned with simulated Rab values.

In summary, the on-site detection of lead-shielded HEU (containing 4.17 g of 235U) was successfully demonstrated using the 252Cf rotation method with the WCND within a 20 min measurement window. This quantity of 235U is below the 15 g threshold of IAEA Category III. To reach the IAEA’s significant quantity of 25 kg of 235U, over 5596 illicit transfers of quantities smaller than 4.17 g would be necessary39. In addition, the research results are also expected to contribute to the prevention of the use of small quantities of nuclear material in dirty bombs40 intended to cause social disruption and psychological anxiety. The described NDA system is cost-effective, transportable, and meets the 20 min measurement criterion for secondary inspection processes. A realistic operation is a "go/no-go" type system. The 252Cf rotation method surveys heavy metals (nuclear materials or other metals) found by X-ray or weight inspection and radioactive materials (RIs or nuclear materials) identified by passive radiation measurement. If Rab (4000 rpm)/Rab (300 rpm) is greater than 1, the suspicious object is nuclear material and the result is “no-go”. Even if Rab (4000 rpm)/Rab (300 rpm) is 1, the result is “no-go” if an extreme neutron count reduction (i.e., presence of neutron moderators to intentionally shield fission neutrons) is observed. Other than the above, the result is “go”. “No-go” tagged suspicious materials proceed to open inspection and nuclear forensics process41,42, followed by detailed analysis in the laboratory. Highly trained technicians are not required, and the inspector only places the suspicious object and checks the "go/no-go" result on site. Therefore, incorporating this system into border control and customs operations can significantly enhance the prevention of HEU smuggling, offering a potent deterrent against nuclear terrorism.

Discussion

In this study, a 2.2 MBq 252Cf source was utilized to detect a lead-shielded HEU sample (containing 4.17 g of 235U) through two separate measurements at high (4000 rpm) and low (300 rpm) rotation speeds, totaling 20 min. This result corresponds to detecting the same target in 12 min using a 252Cf source of 3.7 MBq, as envisioned in practical applications. Furthermore, potential improvements for both the WCND and rotation device have been identified.

Enhancements in the WCND, such as increasing the number of photomultiplier tubes (PMTs), could significantly improve neutron detection efficiency and gamma-ray discrimination capability. For instance, simulation results using the Geant4 code43 suggested that increasing the number of PMTs from four to eight improved the neutron detection efficiency by 1.4 times at the threshold value of pulse height discrimination for suppressing 2.223 MeV gamma-ray44. Furthermore, modifying the threshold value from 2.505 to 2.223 MeV improved the neutron detection efficiency by 1.1 times. In this experiment, the 60Co source was used as a reference for setting the threshold value for gamma-ray suppression owing to its availability. However, the primary background comprises 2.223 MeV gamma-ray from the capture reactions of hydrogen nuclei, and setting the threshold value at 2.505 MeV results in a loss of neutron detection efficiency.

Regarding the rotation device, increasing the rotation radius and speed is expected to improve detection performance. Simulation results using the PHITS code45 demonstrated that modifying the rotation radius to 20 cm and the speed to 6000 rpm could alter the Rab ratio (4000 rpm to 300 rpm) with nuclear material to 1.032 ± 0.00535, effectively halving the required measurement time. Therefore, implementing these proposed enhancements to the WCND and rotation device, the lead-shielded HEU sample (containing 4.17 g of 235U) could be detected in approximately 3.9 min using a 3.7 MBq 252Cf source.

Furthermore, the lower detection limit of HEU was estimated through Monte Carlo simulations for the current NDA system, comprising the rotation device and WCND. The modeling and simulation procedures are detailed in the Simulation section. Simulations were performed with a consistent measurement duration of 20 min and a 252Cf source intensity of 3.7 MBq. The Rab ratio at different rpm speeds (Rab (4000 rpm)/Rab (300 rpm)) was utilized to determine the minimum detectable amount of 235U that is significantly detectable exceeding 1σ statistical errors. Table 3 presents the calculated Rab ratios for varying quantities of 235U, along with their 1σ statistical errors. Using a conversion parameter derived from a comparison of previous experimental and computational results, the neutron flux in the PHITS simulations was converted into actual neutron count values to calculate the aforementioned statistical errors. The results indicated that at 2.48 g of 235U, the Rab ratio (4000 rpm/300 rpm) with nuclear material was 1.007 ± 0.003, showing no significant deviation below this quantity. This suggests that detecting HEU containing less than 2.48 g of 235U poses a challenge. Consequently, the established lower detection limit of 235U for the current NDA system is 2.48 g.

Future research will focus on enhancing the versatility and robustness of the 252Cf rotation method. The detectability of HEU is influenced by the target’s size, thickness, and installation conditions, even when the quantity of nuclear material remains constant. For instance, rotating the HEU sample by 90° to minimize the interrogation neutron cross-section could complicate detection. Moreover, selecting a versatile moderator shape is a critical challenge. The optimal moderator thickness strictly depends on the target sample’s size, quantity, shape, and arrangement. In practical applications, where the specifics of the measurement target are typically unknown, our aim is to validate the detection performance, including the lower detection limits under varying conditions, and to develop a moderator design that is adaptable to different target material conditions.

The 252Cf rotation method currently assumes the maximum measurement object size to be equivalent to that of a large suitcase (50W × 80H × 40T cm), matching the rotation diameter. If a suspicious object, such as a single metal plate suspected of concealing uranium, is identified in the primary screening, it can be surrounded with a moderator and placed between the rotation device and WCND for measurement. In such cases, the 252Cf rotation method has robustness against installation position errors and eccentric distribution of nuclear material by a few centimeters within the lead plate35. For instance, in the geometry of Fig. 5, the lead-shielded HEU and the moderator were considered as one suspicious object with gamma-ray and neutron shielding, and Rab (4000 rpm)/Rab (300 rpm) values were calculated at different X-axis positions. The integration range was ± π/4 and the amount of 235U was fixed at 4.17 g. Figure 5c shows the simulation model for X = − 5 cm. Assuming that this suspicious object is concealed in the baggage or suitcase, the change in X position simulates the variation of the suspicious object position in the container. Table 3 shows the calculated results for Rab (4000 rpm)/Rab (300 rpm) at X = ± 3 and 5 cm. As the X position shifted from the center (X = 0 cm), Rab (4000 rpm)/Rab (300 rpm) decreased, with greater variation on the negative side of the X axis. In the range of positional variation of less than ± 5 cm, the increase in Rab (4000 rpm)/Rab (300 rpm) with nuclear material was statistically significant, indicating the robustness of the 252Cf rotation method. On the other hand, the detection difficulty was confirmed for large variations of more than ± 5 cm. Thus, more significant deviations, on the order of several tens of centimeters, particularly when nuclear material is hidden within a large suitcase and an opening inspection is impractical, pose increased detection challenges. The 5 cm variation is large relative to the rotation radius (15 cm). Increasing the rotation radius is expected not only to improve detection performance, but also to improve robustness against variations in target position. Therefore, we plan to elucidate a versatile detector design and moderator shape and advance the 252Cf rotation method to accommodate more practical scenarios involving various sizes, arrangements, shielding conditions, mass, and ubiquity of measurement objects.

In conclusion, the 252Cf rotation method, in conjunction with the WCND, has proven effective for detection of lead-shielded HEU samples with a compact and transportable system, which remain undetectable by traditional Ge detectors. This NDA system is cost-effective, transportable, and offers significant flexibility in installation locations. Furthermore, it is capable of detecting a few grams of 235U within minutes, thereby having the potential as a substantial deterrent against nuclear terrorism. In practical cases, where nuclear materials are concealed in different positions in baggage or suitcases, their large deviations from the central position reduced detection performance. Therefore, future efforts will be directed towards improving the performance of the WCND and the rotation device, enhancing measurement methodology to increase system versatility and robustness, and designing a practical detection system that integrates this NDA system with other passive detectors for the effective identification of suspicious objects.

Methods

Experimental setup of gamma-ray spectroscopy

Gamma-ray spectroscopy on an HEU sample was performed at the Institute for Integrated Radiation and Nuclear Science, Kyoto University (KURNS). The primary objective was to assess the limitations of passive gamma-ray detection methods in identifying lead-shielded HEU and to highlight the superior detection capabilities of the 252Cf rotation method when combined with the WCND. The experimental setup for gamma-ray spectrometric measurements is depicted in Fig. 4. A portable Ge detector (Micro-trans-SPEC®, ORTEC®), recognized globally for its high energy resolution (2.15 keV FWHM at 1.33 MeV) and widely used in homeland security for on-site passive detection46, served as the gamma-ray detector. The failure to detect HEU with this high-resolution detector underscores the challenges faced when using detectors with lesser energy resolution, such as NaI (Tl) detectors47,48. To minimize environmental background gamma radiation, the Ge crystal was encased in lead blocks 5 cm in thickness. The HEU sample, containing 4.17 g of 235U at 92.7% enrichment and measuring 63.5 × 63.5 × 2 mm, was placed 8.5 cm in front of the detector. Variable lead plate thicknesses were tested to determine the minimal thickness necessary to shield gamma rays from the HEU. Adjustments were made in 0.50 mm increments using lead plates (PB-243461, 100 × 100 × 0.5 mm per plate, Nilaco Co.). Measurement duration was set at 20 min (live time) for each setup. Nuclide analysis software (MAESTRO®, ORTEC®) was employed for the data analysis, with ROIs identified at peak positions of 185.72 and 1001.03 keV to calculate net area counts and verify uranium detectability against the ANSI nuclear data library.

Experimental setup of 252Cf rotation method with WCND

The demonstration experiment for detecting concealed HEU was performed at KURNS, as depicted in Fig. 2(a). The experimental arrangement involved placing the HEU sample within a polyethylene moderator adjacent to the rotation device, with the WCND positioned nearby. The distances from the moderator to the rotation device and WCND were set at 1.0 and 10.5 cm, respectively. The moderator thickness was optimized to 5.5 cm on the front (source side), 3.0 cm on the back (detector side), and 5.0 cm on the sides to increase the number of fission neutrons, as shown in Fig. 2(b). The HEU sample, detailed in Fig. 2(c), consisted of a 63.5 × 63.5 × 2 mm block containing 4.17 g of 235U sandwiched between two lead plates (each measuring 100 × 100 × 8 mm). A subsequent 20 min passive gamma-ray measurement confirmed the Ge detector’s inability to detect the prepared sample. The alignment of the rotation device, HEU sample, moderator, and WCND was adjusted to ensure the collinearity of their central axes. A single 252Cf source, with an activity of 2.2 MBq, was mounted on the rotation disk within the device.

WCND and 252Cf rotation device

The WCND comprised a white acrylic vessel, 5 mm in thickness, filled with pure water doped with 0.50 wt% gadolinium chloride. The inner surface of the container was lined with a reflective material (0.75 mm thick, PMR10P1, THORLABS), and four PMTs (Hamamatsu H11284-100) were affixed atop the container using optical grease (TSK5353). A wavelength shifter (amino-G-salt49) was dissolved in the water at a concentration of 30 mg/L to enhance the detection sensitivity. Acrylic windows, 2 mm thick, were placed between the water and PMTs, with air bubbles at the PMT connection points removed to prevent undesired reflections at the boundary. The container was also shielded with 5 mm thick boron rubber to block thermal neutrons and wrapped in a double layer of black sheets to prevent external light penetration. The effective volume of the detector was 30W × 30H × 25T cm. Incident neutrons are decelerated in the water and captured by the Gd nuclei, emitting gamma-rays of up to approximately 8 MeV50. As a result of Compton scattering, electrons are emitted and Cherenkov light is generated when electrons with energies above 0.264 MeV pass through the water. Although WCND is also sensitive to gamma-rays above the energy of 0.423 MeV, neutrons can be selectively detected by using pulse height discrimination. The light yield of Gd-induced gamma-rays is greater than that of hydrogen-induced gamma-rays (2.223 MeV), the largest background noise.

The 252Cf rotation device, designed for field operation, features a compact size (50W × 62H × 40T cm) and can be easily transported to various locations. It incorporates an aluminum disk with a diameter of 32.4 cm, on which the 252Cf source is mounted, allowing for a rotation radius of 15.0 cm. The irradiation efficiency can be enhanced by mounting multiple sources. The maximum rotation speed was 4000 rpm, operating with a servo motor powered by 0.75 kW electrical consumption, and no cooling system was required. For safety, an interlock function is integrated into the ceiling cover to prevent access during rotation.

Data acquisition system

The data acquisition system for the WCND processed analog signals from each PMT. These signals were amplified using NIM modules (Models KN2107 and 612AM), summed up (NIM, Model 740), and then fed into a discriminator (NIM, Model 583). The PMTs’ gain was calibrated using cosmic rays, and the discriminator’s threshold was set to effectively suppress the combined gamma-ray emissions from a 60Co source (totaling 2.505 MeV). The discriminator’s output was converted to TTL signals (Model KN201) and recorded by a multi-channel scaler (MCS), which collected neutron count data. The MCS received the rotation timing signal from the servomotor as a trigger signal, and obtained time spectra of neutron counts synchronized with the rotation timing for the conditions with and without nuclear material.

Measurements were performed at two rotation speeds: 4000 rpm (high speed) and 300 rpm (low speed), with 300 rpm being the minimum rotation speed supported by the system. The MCS was configured with 1024 channels, with dwell times set at 160 μs for measurements at 300 rpm and 12 μs at 4000 rpm. Each measurement session lasted 10 min, encompassing conditions both with and without nuclear material. To establish a central channel for subsequent analysis, measurements under a vacant condition were also conducted at each rotation speed. The geometric conditions varied between measurements with and without the HEU sample due to the sample’s placement within or removal from the moderator. However, measurements conducted at both 4000 and 300 rpm maintained consistent geometric conditions, ensuring the reliability of comparative data analysis.

Analysis

Figure 3c and d present the time spectra of neutron counts under vacant conditions, illustrating a symmetric distribution with peaks at approximately 6350 μs at 4000 rpm and 86,000 μs at 300 rpm, corresponding to moments when the neutron source is nearest to the detector. These peak positions, determined under vacant conditions, serve as the central positions, reflecting the relative placement of the rotation device and WCND, the WCND’s detection efficiency asymmetry, and the synchronization accuracy of rotation timing. Simulation

To determine the lower detection limit of HEU, Monte Carlo simulations were employed, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Mimicking the experimental setup, the HEU, encased in an 8 mm lead shield, was positioned with a 2.0 cm gap from both the moderator and the WCND. The WCND’s proximity to the moderator was reduced compared to the experimental arrangement to enhance statistical reliability. Moderator thicknesses were set to 5.0 cm at the front and sides and 3.0 cm at the back. The Rab values were computed across various 235U quantities, maintaining a 92.7% enrichment. The thickness of the HEU (Y direction) was fixed at 2.0 mm, and the amount of 235U was controlled by varying the surface area (X × Z). The time distribution of the electron flux within the WCND, specifically for electrons with energies exceeding 1.994 MeV, was used for evaluation, reconstructing the experimental geometry using the PHITS code. This energy threshold corresponds to the maximum energy of Compton-scattered electrons from 2.223 MeV gamma rays, a key background component from the 1H(n,γ)2H reaction. The Rab value calculation spanned ± π/4 radians from the central position.
