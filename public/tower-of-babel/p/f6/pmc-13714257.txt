Rostral lesion shift and tract-specific degeneration drive functional heterogeneity in degenerative cervical myelopathy
Authors: Fauziyya Muhammad, Dario Pfyffer, Nicholas Dobson, Todd Parrish, Serena S Hu, John Ratliff, Nathaniel D Stetson, Yasin Dhaher, Andrew C Smith, Kenneth A Weber, Zachary A Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDegenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is the leading cause of spinal cord impairment worldwide, yet conventional magnetic resonance imaging metrics of canal stenosis fail to explain the remarkable heterogeneity in patient outcomes.MethodsWe applied automated deep-learning segmentation to map the topography of intramedullary lesions in 73 patients and determine their tract-specific functional impact. Functional measures included the modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association score, comprising upper- and lower-body motor dysfunction, sensory function, and sphincter function domains; the Nine-Hole Peg Test for dexterity; and the Standing Balance Test.ResultsHere we show presence of T2-hyperintense lesions in 45% of the cohort, defining a biologically distinct phenotype associated with greater neurological disability. Spatially, lesion density peaked at C4, rostral to the site of maximal compression (C5), implicating venous congestion and ascending degeneration as key pathophysiological drivers. Multivariate modeling revealed that balance impairment represents a systems-level failure, predicted independently by combined damage to the dorsal columns (P = 0.032) and lateral reticulospinal tract (P = 0.011). In contrast, dexterity deficits were not attributable to any single tract, suggesting reliance on global cord integrity. Importantly, we found that unlike in traumatic spinal cord injury, residual tissue bridges (spared tissue) did not universally predict function; they conferred protective benefit for balance (P = 0.044) only when reticulospinal tract integrity was accounted for.ConclusionsThese findings establish tract-specific lesion profiling as a mechanistic biomarker, shifting the clinical framework from measuring global compression to characterizing biological vulnerability.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Prognostic markers

Introduction

Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is the leading cause of nontraumatic spinal cord dysfunction in adults and a major contributor to neurological disability worldwide1–3. DCM arises from a complex interplay of static compression and dynamic biomechanical stress4,5. However, a critical barrier to effective clinical management is the profound discordance between conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings and patient presentation: individuals with comparable degrees of canal stenosis often exhibit remarkably heterogeneous functional impairments1–3,6,7. This clinical-radiological mismatch persists because conventional morphometric indices, such as cross-sectional area (CSA) or compression ratio, quantify the extrinsic deformation of the cord but fail to capture the intrinsic biological integrity of the parenchyma4,5,8,9. While intramedullary T2-hyperintense lesions are recognized markers of parenchymal damage, their clinical utility has been limited by a lack of spatial specificity10.

In acute traumatic spinal cord injury (SCI), the specific topography of lesion damage—and the preservation of residual “tissue bridges”—is a robust predictor of recovery. Yet it remains unknown whether these same structural rules apply to the chronic, progressive pathophysiology of DCM. Specifically, it is unclear whether functional deficits in DCM are driven by global cord compromise or by tract-specific hits to distinct sensorimotor pathways3,6,11,12.

In this multisite study, we used automated deep-learning segmentation (SCIseg)13 to map the topography of intramedullary lesions and quantify residual tissue bridges in 73 patients with DCM. We tested the hypothesis that functional heterogeneity is determined by the specific spatial distribution of irreversible injury. By dissociating the contributions of the dorsal columns (proprioception) from the lateral motor tracts (volitional movement), we sought to determine if balance and dexterity represent mechanistically distinct phenotypes and whether the preservation of anatomical tissue bridges confers functional protection in the setting of chronic degeneration3,11,13,14.

Materials and Methods

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

This retrospective, longitudinal, multisite study included patients diagnosed with DCM across four tertiary academic centers: the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center (OUHSC), Northwestern University, University of Texas Southwestern (UTSW), and Stanford University. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the OUHSC (IRB #15513). All patients provided written informed consent before enrollment. Participation in the study was voluntary, and all patients were informed of the study purpose, procedures, risks, and their rights to withdrew at any time. No Identifying patient information is included in the manuscript.

Patients and experimental design

A total of 73 patients with DCM were enrolled from the three academic institutions under the same principal investigators (ZAS and KAW): OUHSC (n = 51), UTSW (n = 10), and Stanford University (n = 12). Patient recruitment and MRI/clinical examinations were conducted between May 2021 and August 2025. All patients underwent baseline MRI and clinical assessments. Inclusion criteria required a diagnosis of DCM and visible cervical spinal cord pathology, as confirmed by T2-weighted (T2w) imaging15. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥18 years; (2) clinical diagnosis of DCM based on the presence of signs and symptoms of myelopathy (e.g., gait disturbance, hand clumsiness, sensory changes, hyperreflexia); (3) radiological confirmation of cervical spinal cord compression on MRI, defined as visible cord deformation or signal change at one or more cervical levels (C2–C7) on T2w imaging; and (4) ability to provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) nondegenerative etiologies of cervical myelopathy, including ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL)16, neoplastic compression, traumatic SCI, infection, or inflammatory/demyelinating disease; (2) prior cervical spine surgery; (3) thoracic-only spinal cord lesions without cervical involvement; (4) significant comorbid neurological conditions (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, stroke with residual motor deficit) that could confound clinical assessments; and (5) inability to complete the standardized clinical assessment battery.

Clinical assessments

We used the modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association (mJOA) score (range: 0–18), where lower scores indicate greater impairment. The mJOA evaluates four domains: motor dysfunction of the upper extremity (0–5), motor dysfunction of the lower extremity (0–7), sensory function of the upper extremity (0–3), and sphincter function (0–3). Patients were categorized as mild (mJOA 15–18), moderate (mJOA 12–14), or severe (mJOA <12) per established thresholds17. Upper-limb dexterity was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Toolbox (NIHTB) Nine-Hole Peg Test (9HPT). This validated test measures fine manual dexterity by timing how quickly participants place and remove nine pegs from a pegboard using one hand at a time. We used the quantitative postural sway analysis via the NIHTB Standing Balance Test to assess balance. Participants stood on a force platform with feet together and eyes open for 50 seconds. Center-of-pressure displacement was recorded as the raw postural-sway score. Rather than the raw scores, we analyzed the demographically corrected normative scores for dexterity and balance generated automatically by the NIHTB software. The NIHTB converts each raw score into normative scores by regressing performance on demographic variables within its nationally representative U.S. normative sample using fractional-polynomial models to capture nonlinear demographic effects and then re-centering and rescaling the resulting residuals. Fully corrected scores adjust for age, sex, race/ethnicity, and educational attainment. We extracted the fully corrected T-scores (mean = 50, SD = 10) directly from the NIHTB output; no manual conversion was performed. For both dexterity and balance, lower scores indicate greater impairment18–21.

MRI acquisition

T2w MRIs of the cervical spine were acquired at a magnetic field strength of 3.0 Tesla (3 T) on either a Siemens scanner equipped with a 16-channel spine coil (UTSW and Stanford) or a General Electric (GE) MR750w scanner equipped with a 16-channel head-neck-spine coil (OUHSC). Two acquisition protocols were used to capture T2w images with an isotropic resolution (0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm3) and a field of view (FOV) of 256 × 256 (see spine-generic protocol)17. Supplementary Table 1 provides detailed imaging parameters from each acquisition site. Both CUBE and SPACE are vendor-specific implementations of variable-flip-angle 3D fast spin-echo sequences that produce equivalent T2w contrast at matched voxel sizes. For additional protocol information, see the spine-generic protocol for MRI acquisition details22.

For image preprocessing, all Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) images were converted to Neuroimaging Informatics Technology Initiative (NifTi) format, reoriented to the right-posterior-inferior (RPI) spatial orientation, and rigidly registered for quality check and spinal cord segmentation. To mitigate residual inter-scanner variability: (i) All images were resampled to a common resolution (0.6 × 0.6 mm in-plane) before segmentation. (ii) Spinal cord and lesion segmentations were performed using deep-learning algorithms Spinal Cord Toolbox (SCT)’s deep-learning algorithms version 7.0 (SCT v7)14,23,24 and SCIseg v2.125, which have been validated across scanner vendors and field strengths. (iii) All segmentation masks underwent visual quality control with manual correction when necessary. (iv) Anatomical normalization to the PAM50 (1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm) template further harmonized spatial coordinates across subjects26.

Image analysis: Key morphometrics and lesion parameters

CSA: The total area of the spinal cord in the axial plane (mm2), averaged across C3–C7. CSA is a validated marker of global spinal cord atrophy in DCM3,11. Anteroposterior (AP) diameter: The maximum AP distance of the spinal cord (mm), and it reflects focal cord deformation and has been correlated with myelopathy severity3. Maximal compression level (MCL): The cervical vertebral level at which the spinal cord demonstrates the smallest AP diameter, indicating the site of maximal extrinsic compression3. The SCIseg algorithm (version 2.1.0)27, which employs deep-learning–based voxel classification to detect focal intramedullary hyperintensities, was used to identify and quantify intramedullary lesions. Lesion parameters were extracted using the sct_analyze_lesion and include: (i) lesion volume (mm3), which represents the total volume of the T2-hyperintense intramedullary lesion, reflecting the overall burden of parenchymal damage; (ii) lesion length (mm), the craniocaudal extent of the lesion along the spinal cord axis; (iii) the maximal axial damage ratio (MADR), the ratio of lesion area to total spinal cord CSA at the most affected axial level, expressed as a proportion, with a higher MADR indicative of greater intrinsic cord compromise at the lesion epicenter (analogous to the damage ratio used in traumatic SCI)28; and (iv) Tissue bridge width (mm), which is the minimum distance between the lesion boundary and the CSF–cord interface, measured in the axial plane. This represents the residual spared parenchyma surrounding the lesion core. Dorsal and ventral bridge widths were calculated separately and summed to obtain total bridge width29.

Lesions were confirmed in 33 patients; the remaining 40 patients had no visible cervical cord lesions. Lesion segmentation was performed on T2w images. We performed visual quality control and manual correction when necessary to ensure anatomical accuracy. For patients with intramedullary lesions (n = 33), tissue bridge width was defined as the minimum distance between the lesion boundary and the CSF interface. For patients without visible lesions (n = 40), the AP diameter at the MCL was used to characterize the extent of compression and preserved parenchymal tissue. All morphometric measurements were derived after nonlinear registration to the PAM50 spinal cord template, which normalizes for individual differences in cord anatomy and enables valid cross-subject comparisons26. When the intramedullary lesion traverses the full extent of the spinal cord at the maximum affected axial level, the tissue bridge is recorded as zero.

Coregistration of lesion masks to the PAM50 spinal cord template allowed for localization by vertebral level (C2–C7) and quantification of tract-specific lesion burden (fasciculus gracilis [GF], fasciculus cuneatus [CF], corticospinal, rubrospinal, and reticulospinal tracts)10,26,30. These metrics were subsequently used for structure–function correlation analyses. Visual depictions are presented in Fig. 1.

Statistics and Reproducibility

GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) and Python 3.9 libraries (Pandas, Matplotlib, Seaborn, Numpy, and SciPy Stats; Python Software Foundation, Python.org) were used for all statistical analyses. Using GraphPad Prism, we performed a D’Agostino–Pearson omnibus normality test to assess the distribution of continuous variables. For nonnormally distributed data, we used Spearman correlation coefficients to evaluate associations between lesion features (volume, length, width, MADR, and total bridge width) and clinical measures (mJOA, dexterity, and balance). Associations between tract-specific lesion burden and functional measures (Table 2) were evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients, as the tract-burden values were approximately normally distributed. Group-wise comparisons of continuous variables used independent t-tests for normally distributed measures and Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed measures. Chi-square tests were used for comparisons of categorical data, such as sex and mJOA severity groups.

To evaluate the association between lesion-related tract damage on functional outcomes, we constructed multivariable linear regression models in those with lesion (n = 33). The model specifications are as follows: For the dexterity model, we used the GF tract lesion burden (% overlap with GF) and lateral corticospinal tract (CST) lesion burden as the predictors of dexterity. For balance mode, due to severe multicollinearity between dorsal column and lateral tract variables (Variance Inflation Factors, VIF > 10; Supplementary Table 2), a single multivariable model including all tract predictors would produce unstable regression coefficients. We therefore constructed two complementary balance models: Model A (Dorsal focus) included the GF tract lesion burden as the primary predictor to evaluate the contribution of ascending proprioceptive pathways. The dependent variable is the standing balance score (quantitative postural sway, continuous). Model B (Motor focus) included the lateral reticulospinal tract lesion burden as the primary predictor to evaluate descending postural motor control pathways. This dual-model approach allowed us to separately estimate the independent contributions of sensory and motor pathways to balance impairment while avoiding statistical artifacts from collinear predictors. All models are adjusted for age (years) and tissue bridge width (mm) as covariates. Results are reported as beta coefficients (β) with corresponding model R² values. P-values reported for secondary and exploratory analyses are nominal and were not corrected for multiple comparisons.

Results

Patient characteristics and distribution of compression levels

A total of 73 patients with clinically and radiologically confirmed DCM were included. Among them, 33 patients (45%) exhibited intramedullary lesions on T2w MRI, whereas 40 (55%) showed no lesions (Table 1). Age and mJOA scores were not significantly different between groups (age: P = 0.106; mJOA score: P = 0.282). However, males comprised a significantly greater proportion of the lesion group than the non-lesion group (60.6% vs 32.5%, P = 0.030). When examining quantitative measures of motor and balance function, we found that patients with lesions demonstrated lower dexterity scores (36.1 ± 8.5 vs 40.6 ± 9.1, P = 0.031) and showed no significant difference in balance (34.7 ± 17.8 vs 39.9 ± 10.9, P = 0.471). Further, all patients demonstrated measurable cervical cord compression between C3 and C7. When considering all compressed levels, we found that C5 and C6 were the most affected. However, the primary MCL was most frequently located at C5 in the lesion group. Notably, compression at C6 was significantly more prevalent in the non-lesion group (odd’s ratio [OR] = 0.21, P = 0.002), whereas C3-level compression was more frequent in the lesion group (OR = 8.67, P = 0.041).

Scoring direction (lower = worse)

Lesion burden correlates with clinical and tract-specific functional impairment

Across patients with intramedullary lesions, increasing lesion burden was associated with worsening neurological and functional performance (Fig. 2). Lesion volume correlated negatively with overall disease severity (mJOA: ρ = −0.35, P = 0.045), dexterity (ρ = −0.39, P = 0.026), and balance (ρ = −0.38, P = 0.029) (Fig. 2). Larger lesions therefore predicted greater global and tract-specific impairment. Lesion length trended toward a correlation with functional measures (mJOA: ρ = −0.29, P = 0.100; dexterity: ρ = −0.31, P = 0.078) and was significantly correlated with balance (ρ = −0.47, P = 0.005). The MADR, reflecting the proportion of the cord’s CSA occupied by the lesion, showed the strongest overall correlations (mJOA: ρ = −0.44, P = 0.011; dexterity: ρ = −0.38, P = 0.030; balance: ρ = −0.38, P = 0.029).

Morphometric indices of compression and residual tissue preservation predict functional impairment

Quantitative morphometric analysis revealed that both global and focal spinal cord compression were strongly associated with clinical severity and sensorimotor performance (Fig. 3). Patients with intramedullary lesions demonstrated smaller AP cord diameter at the MCL and markedly lower bridge width than those in the non-lesion group (Table 1). Across all patients, smaller average CSA (C3–C7) correlated with lower mJOA scores (ρ = 0.25, P = 0.031) and poorer dexterity (ρ = 0.24, P = 0.045) and balance (ρ = 0.24, P = 0.049). Likewise, a reduction in AP diameter at the MCL correlated with dexterity (ρ = 0.24, P = 0.042) and trended toward a correlation with global neurological function (ρ = 0.23, P = 0.054). By contrast, bridge width did not correlate with any functional measure in our univariate analysis (all P > 0.1), suggesting that anatomical continuity alone does not necessarily confer preserved function once chronic compression is established.

Lesions localize to the dorsal and lateral funiculi and occur rostral to the site of maximal compression

Intramedullary lesions were most frequently observed between C4 and C6, with a peak at the C4 level (48.50%; Fig. 4A). Although the MCL was most often located at C5 (54.50%), the highest lesion density was detected one level rostral at C4 (74.80%), indicating that lesion formation tends to occur above the compression epicenter. Spatial mapping confirmed that lesions predominantly affect white matter (69.60%), with preferential involvement of the dorsal (34.60%) and lateral (31.20%) funiculi, and relative sparing of the ventral (3.80%) funiculi (Fig. 4B).

Relationships between lesion burden and spinal cord compression

Lesion burden (volume, length, and MADR) correlated inversely with average CSA and AP diameter at the MCL (Fig. 5). Smaller CSA was associated with larger lesion volume (ρ = −0.31, P = 0.076), greater lesion length (ρ = −0.41, P = 0.019), and a higher MADR (ρ = −0.53, P = 0.002). Similarly, smaller AP diameter was associated with larger lesion volume (ρ = −0.38, P = 0.031) and greater lesion length (ρ = −0.53, P = 0.002), reinforcing the link between mechanical stress and parenchymal injury.

Demographic and clinical factor effects on lesion prevalence

To evaluate whether demographic and clinical factors beyond sex influenced lesion prevalence, we performed a supplementary multivariable logistic regression analysis with lesion presence (yes/no) as the dependent variable and age, sex, and baseline mJOA as independent variables (n = 73 patients with complete data). In this model, male sex remained the only significant predictor of lesion presence (OR = 2.75, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.03–7.39, P = 0.044), whereas age (OR = 1.03, 95% CI: 0.98–1.08, P = 0.271) and baseline mJOA (OR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.78–1.16, P = 0.604) were not independently significant.

Tract-specific lesion burden predicts specific functional deficits

Correlational analyses between tract-specific lesion burden and clinical performance revealed distinct structure-function relationships (Table 2). For balance control, lesion involvement within the dorsal column pathways was significantly associated with poorer performance, including the GF (ρ = −0.37, P = 0.032) and CF (ρ = −0.39, P = 0.024). Notably, significant associations were also observed for descending lateral motor tracts, including the lateral reticulospinal tract (ρ = −0.46, P = 0.007) and CST (ρ = −0.35, P = 0.049). For dexterity, significant relationships were observed with dorsal sensory tracts (GF: ρ = −0.38, P = 0.029; CF: ρ = −0.42, P = 0.016) and with the lateral CST (ρ = −0.36, P = 0.040). Overall neurological severity (mJOA) was most strongly correlated with damage to descending motor systems, particularly the lateral reticulospinal tract (ρ = −0.52, P = 0.002) and rubrospinal tract (ρ = −0.45, P = 0.009).

Independent contributions of tract-specific injury and preserved tissue to functional impairment

To identify independent predictors of functional outcomes, we constructed multivariable linear regression models (Table 3). Multicollinearity diagnostics revealed severe collinearity between tract variables, with VIF exceeding 10 for the GF (59.5), CF (80.3), and lateral reticulospinal tract (20.2) (Supplementary Table 2). Co-occurrence analysis indicated that dorsal column tracts were simultaneously affected in 100% of cases, and lesions in the lateral reticulospinal tract co-occurred with lateral corticospinal tract lesions in 81.2% of patients (Supplementary Fig. 1). Owing to this shared variance, dorsal column and reticulospinal contributions could not be statistically separated in a single model.

For dexterity, the overall model remained significant (P < 0.05), identifying lesion burden within the GF and lateral CST as the primary contributors to reduced performance, although neither reached independent significance after adjustments for covariates (GF: P = 0.078; lateral CST: P = 0.155) (Table 3).

For balance, we constructed two separate models to account for multicollinearity (Table 3). Model A (Dorsal Focus) identified the GF tract as a significant independent predictor of balance impairment (β = −1.51 ± 0.67, P = 0.032) after adjustments for age and bridge width. Model B (Motor Focus) identified the lateral reticulospinal tract as a significant independent predictor (β = −1.35 ± 0.50, P = 0.011). Interestingly, in Model B, bridge width also emerged as a significant predictor (β = −4.66 ± 2.21, P = 0.044), indicating that when reticulospinal tract integrity is accounted for, the preservation of tissue bridges may offer additional protective value against balance loss. These results suggest that balance deficits in DCM are most likely driven by combined injury to dorsal proprioceptive and lateral motor pathways.

Discussion

In this multisite study, we demonstrated that intramedullary T2 hyperintense lesions in DCM represent a biologically distinct phenotype of irreversible spinal cord injury rather than a universal consequence of compression. By integrating automated deep-learning segmentation with tract-specific mapping, we show that these lesions are not random structural artifacts but mechanistic drivers of functional heterogeneity. Importantly, we identified a spatial decoupling where lesion burden peaks rostral to the site of maximal compression–implicating secondary vascular congestion and ascending degeneration as pathophysiological mechanisms distinct from focal mechanical deformation. These findings challenge the reliance on global morphometric indices, suggesting that clinical disability in DCM is determined not by the magnitude of canal stenosis but by the specific topography of parenchymal degeneration.

The rostral shift and vascular mechanisms

Our topographical analysis reveals an important mismatch: although maximal mechanical compression occurred most frequently at C5, the peak density of intramedullary lesions was located rostrally at C4. This “rostral shift” combined with a predilection for the dorsal and lateral watersheds suggests that venous congestion and retrograde ischemia—rather than focal compression alone—drive the expansion of irreversible cord injury. This vascular vulnerability may also explain the higher lesion prevalence that we observed in male patients; anatomical differences in canal diameter or microvascular resilience may predispose males to this ischemic cascade despite similar degrees of compression31,32. This rostral shift confirms that the lesion is not a simple compression at the site of impact but a vascular phenomenon spreading upstream.

The bridge width paradox: structural residual tissue vs functional viability

A key finding of this work is the conditional utility of tissue bridges. Unlike in traumatic SCI, where bridge width is a universal predictor of recovery6,29,33–35, we found that in DCM, bridge width did not predict function in the univariate analysis. However, in our multivariate models, bridge width emerged as a significant predictor of balance (Model B, P = 0.044) only after the integrity of the lateral reticulospinal tract was accounted for. This implies that “spared tissue” in chronic compression is not automatically functional; its protective value emerges only when specific descending motor pathways remain sufficiently intact to utilize that structural reserve. Furthermore, this finding suggests that approaches36 aimed solely at preserving anatomical width may be insufficient if tract-specific demyelination has already rendered the bridge physiologically silent. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated that despite adequate surgical decompression, a subset of patients with DCM fail to recover neurological function, particularly when preoperative intramedullary signal changes are present36,37. Suri et al.37 reported that patients with T2 signal changes had poorer surgical outcomes despite equivalent decompression, suggesting that intrinsic cord damage—rather than persistent compression—is the primary determinant of residual disability. Current clinical practice often involves a “watchful waiting” approach in patients with imaging evidence of cervical stenosis but mild or absent myelopathic symptoms, with surgical intervention typically reserved for progressive neurological deterioration or critical canal compromise38,39. However, our data suggest that this approach may allow subclinical tract-specific degeneration to progress unchecked, as lesion burden and tract-specific damage were already substantial in patients with only moderate mJOA impairment.

Tract-specific phenotyping

The significant correlation between lesion length and balance impairment (ρ = −0.47, P = 0.005) suggests that longitudinal lesion extent along the dorsal cord may preferentially disrupt ascending proprioceptive pathways. This is consistent with the known anatomy of the GF, which ascends through multiple cervical segments and is therefore vulnerable to longitudinally extensive lesions. Similar observations have been made in traumatic SCI, where lesion length independently predicts sensory outcomes29,40. Beyond regional topography, our tract-specific mapping revealed a mechanistic dissociation between postural stability and fine motor control. We found that balance impairment was strongly predicted by dual hit to the dorsal columns (proprioception, P = 0.032) and the lateral reticulospinal tract (postural control, P = 0.011). This confirms that stability requires the integration of both ascending proprioception and descending motor reflex loops. In contrast, dexterity deficits were not independently driven by any single tract in the multivariate model (P > 0.05 for all tracts). While univariate correlations linked dexterity to the corticospinal tract, this effect disappeared after adjustments for confounders. This suggests that unlike balance, which relies on specific pathways, dexterity is a highly complex, redundant function that degrades with global cord burden rather than focal tract failure. This distinction highlights that hand clumsiness may be a general marker of myelopathy, whereas balance deficits signal specific damage to the dorsal-lateral axis.

Clinical implications and future directions

The mechanistic insights derived from this study challenge the current “stenosis-centric” approach to DCM management. First, the identification of a “rostral lesion shift” suggests that surgical planning based solely on the site of maximal compression may fail to address the upstream vascular penumbra driving progression. If venous congestion propagates injury to C4 while the stenosis is at C5, extending decompression rostrally or caudally may be necessary to halt neurodegeneration. Second, the “Bridge Width Paradox” fundamentally alters patient counseling; surgeons must recognize that anatomical preservation of the cord does not guarantee functional recovery if tract-specific demyelination has already rendered the tissue physiologically inert. Finally, the dissociation between balance (tract-specific) and dexterity (global burden) supports the development of phenotype-specific rehabilitation protocols, moving beyond generic physical therapy to targeted sensorimotor retraining. Future prospective trials should stratify patients by these “dorsal” vs. “lateral” phenotypes to determine if they respond differentially to surgical intervention.

Limitations

Our study has limitations inherent to its retrospective, multisite design. First, while we utilized a standardized SCIseg deep-learning pipeline to minimize bias, potential inter-scanner variability and our modest sample size (n = 73) limit the granularity of subgroup analyses15,41. Second, the cross-sectional nature of our data precludes causal inference; only longitudinal tracking can confirm if tract degeneration precedes clinical decline. Third, we observed severe multicollinearity between tract damage variables (VIF > 10 for most tracts), which limited our ability to definitively isolate independent contributions of individual tracts to clinical deficits. We acknowledge that our modest sample size (n = 33 in the lesion group) may amplify this collinearity. However, we interpret the concurrent tract damage as also reflecting a genuine biological reality of DCM, in which chronic compression acts as a systems-level injury affecting anatomically adjacent and vascularly co-dependent pathways simultaneously. Future studies with larger, more heterogeneous cohorts are needed to determine whether selective tract involvement can be reliably identified and whether the observed collinearity diminishes with increased statistical power. Further, the duration of symptomatology was not systematically recorded across all recruitment sites and therefore could not be evaluated as a predictor of lesion prevalence17. Fourth, our balance assessment relied on quantitative postural sway during quiet standing. While this effectively isolates proprioceptive integrity, it may not fully capture dynamic fall risk during gait, which involves more complex supraspinal control. Fifth, formal cervical sagittal alignment parameters (e.g., C2–C7 sagittal vertical axis, Cobb angle) were not measured in this cohort. Although all MRIs were acquired in the supine position, standing alignment may influence dynamic cord compression and could represent an unmeasured confounder. Future studies incorporating upright or flexion-extension MRI may help clarify the contribution of alignment to lesion formation and functional outcomes38,42. Finally, our analysis focused on standard T2w hyperintensities, which represent a composite of edema, inflammation, and gliosis. We did not incorporate T1-weighted imaging to identify hypointense black holes indicative of cavitation. However, our findings demonstrate that even without these advanced sequences, rigorous quantification of standard clinical MRI yields robust, tract-specific prognostic data. Future multimodal studies integrating T1, T2, and diffusion metrics are necessary to fully stratify the reversible versus irreversible components of these lesions.

Conclusions

This multisite study establishes intramedullary T2-hyperintense lesions not merely as markers of severe disease but as the defining feature of a biologically distinct phenotype in DCM. By integrating automated deep-learning segmentation with tract-specific mapping, we provide in vivo evidence that lesion formation is driven by a “rostral vascular shift”—a decoupling of compression and injury that implicates upstream venous congestion as a primary driver of neurodegeneration. Importantly, we demonstrate a fundamental divergence from traumatic SCI: in chronic DCM, residual tissue bridges do not guarantee preserved function but instead represent a conditional structural reserve that relies on the integrity of specific motor pathways to remain physiologically viable.

Furthermore, we show that clinical disability is not uniform; rather, balance impairment represents a specific “systems-level” failure of the dorsal-reticulospinal axis, whereas dexterity relies on global spinal cord integrity. This dissociation challenges the utility of aggregate severity scores, suggesting that effective management requires stratifying patients by biological vulnerability rather than anatomical stenosis alone. Collectively, this work reframes DCM from a static mechanical disorder to a dynamic, tract-specific neurodegenerative condition, establishing quantitative lesion profiling as the cornerstone for the next generation of precision diagnosis and surgical timing.
