Multi-omics analysis reveals insights into the key genes involved in polysaccharide biosynthesis induced by red light intensity in Platostoma palustre
Authors: Fan Wei, Changqian Quan, Meihua Xu, Danfeng Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Platostoma palustre (Blume) A. J. Paton is a medicinal and edible plant in China, and polysaccharide is its main functional component, composed of various monosaccharides. This study aims to investigate the biosynthesis and regulatory mechanisms of polysaccharides in P. palustre.ResultsThe results showed that increased red light intensity promoted the growth of P. palustre, and an increase in light intensity to a certain degree increased the concentrations of monosaccharides. Transcriptome sequencing showed that a total of 55 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) encoding enzymes involved in polysaccharide biosynthesis were identified as key functional genes. The pathways for polysaccharide biosynthesis in P. palustre were also reconstructed. Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) revealed 4 modules significantly correlated with 9 monosaccharides. Based on the high connectivities and expression levels, a total of 17 hub TFs, belonging to the B3_superfamily, AP2/ERF, bHLH, WRKY, and MYB families, were considered key regulatory genes. Additionally, protein interaction network analysis revealed that there were interactions between transcript_43348 (B3_superfamily) and transcript_20138 (Sucrose synthase, SuSy), between transcript_1530 (B3_superfamily) and transcript_35035 (bHLH), between transcript_1530 (B3_superfamily) and transcript_41067 (Sucrose non-fermenting-1-related protein kinase 2, SnRK2), and between transcript_52483 (AP2/ERF) and transcript_23314 (ARR-B).ConclusionsThis study laid the foundation for elucidating the biosynthesis and regulatory mechanisms of polysaccharides in P. palustre.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07451-4.Keywords: Platostoma palustre (Blume) A. J. paton, Red light intensity, Polysaccharides, WGCNA, Transcription factor

Background

Light drives plant photosynthesis to produce carbohydrates, which play an important role in plant growth and development and affect their survival and fitness in natural ecosystems [1]. As one of the most critical environmental factors, light not only provides energy for photosynthesis but also influences plant growth and development as a signal factor [2]. Light acts on plants through light quality, photoperiod, and light intensity. Light intensity refers to the strength or amount of light produced by a specific lamp source. Plants adapt to different light-intensity environments through the activation of multiple physiological, metabolic, and molecular responses [3] to improve the efficiency of light utilization or avoid the damage of strong light [4]. Excessive light can induce photoinhibition, which not only impedes photosynthesis but also damages the photosynthetic system. Conversely, insufficient light similarly constrains photosynthetic activity and may ultimately result in plant death due to carbon starvation [5, 6]. In higher plants, photosynthesis primarily occurs in leaf tissues, which have evolved multiple regulatory mechanisms to dynamically adjust this vital process in response to fluctuating light conditions [7, 8]. In general, high light intensity enables plants to produce more photosynthetic products through photosynthesis and continuously increases their biomass [9]. As the intensity of light changes, physiological indicators such as chlorophyll, carotenoids, soluble proteins, and soluble sugars in leaves will also change [10, 11].

P. palustre, belonging to the genus Platostoma of the Lamiaceae family, is an annual or perennial herbal plant [12]. P. palustre is one of the most important medicinal and edible plants [13], which has been used for thousands of years [14]. Modern research shows that P. palustre has multiple biological activities, such as antioxidation [15, 16], antihypertensive effects [17], hypolipidemic effects [18], antibiosis [19], liver injury alleviation [20], etc. In addition, P. palustre has high edible value [21–23], which can be used as an herbal beverage in China and Southeast Asian countries, as well as a raw material source of coating agents, natural edible pigments, new refrigerants, edible films, etc [24–26]. With the development of the social economy and technology, as well as the changes in people’s lives and consumption habits, P. palustre is regarded as having great potential and market prospects.

P. palustre contains polysaccharides, flavonoids, triterpene acids, vitamins, and trace elements [27]. P. palustre polysaccharides (PPP), also named “Xiancao gum” in Chinese, is the main functional active substance, which is an important index for evaluating the quality of this species [28, 29]. Zhang et al. [30] found that PPP consisted of eight monosaccharides, containing galacturonic acid, glucose, galactose, xylose, mannose, rhamnose, ribose, and glucuronic acid, with molar percentages of 28.4%, 26.5%, 16.4%, 10.6%, 7.4%, 5.7%, 4.2%, and 0.9%, respectively. Among these, galacturonic acid accounts for the largest proportion and is the monomer of pectin molecules, which are most abundant in the plant’s primary cell walls and the middle lamellae. There is a diversity of polysaccharides that make up plant cell walls and that are categorized into one of three types: cellulose, hemicellulose, or pectin [31]. The current model of pectin biosynthesis predicts a Golgi luminal glycosyltransferase active site and a nucleotide-sugar substrate, and the pathways and enzymes involved in the production of the major nucleotide-sugar precursors for cell wall synthesis are well resolved [31, 32]. However, the biosynthesis pathways of polysaccharides in P. palustre are yet to be elucidated.

Our previous study showed that red light could promote the growth and quality of P. palustre [33]. Here, we further investigated the effects of different red light intensities on the polysaccharide biosynthesis of P. palustre using the transcriptome, full-length transcriptome, and targeted metabolomics techniques, to explore the molecular mechanism of red light-induced polysaccharide biosynthesis in a preliminary manner in this Chinese herbal medicine.

Results

Red light intensity influences the fresh weight of P. palustre

In this study, four red light intensity treatments (Fig. 1A-D) were carried out. Red light intensity significantly affected the growth of P. palustre (Fig. 1E-H). The fresh weight of P. palustre plants significantly increased with increasing red light intensity. The plant fresh weight increased by 36.7%, 79.1%, and 110.0% in T4, T6, and T8 treatments, respectively, compared to T2 (Fig. 1I). Similarly, the fresh weight of above-ground parts in T4, T6, and T8 treatments also increased by 81.5%, 181.1%, and 160.5%, respectively (Fig. 1J). Slightly different from the plant fresh weight and above-ground fresh weight, the root fresh weight in T6 and T8 treatments significantly increased compared to that in T2 and T4, and the root/shoot ratio in T6 treatment dramatically increased compared to that in T2 and T8 (Fig. 1K, L).

Quantitative analysis of monosaccharides in P. palustre under different red light intensities

To systematically investigate red light-mediated regulation of monosaccharide biosynthesis and metabolism in P. palustre, we performed quantitative analysis of leaf saccharides across four light-intensity treatments. A total of ten monosaccharides were detected across the treatments, including L-fucose (Fuc), D-arabinose (Ara), D-galactose (Gal), D-glucose (Glc), D-xylose (Xyl), D-mannose (Man), D-ribose (Rib), D-galacturonic acid (Gal-UA), D-glucuronic acid (Glc-UA), and D-mannuronic acid (Man-UA) (Fig. 2). As shown in Fig. 2A, the contents of Fuc, Ara, Gal, Xyl, Rib, Gal-UA, and Glc-UA increased initially and then decreased with increasing red light intensity, peaking under the T4 or T6 treatments. However, the content of Glc showed a positive correlation with red light intensity. In particular, Man-UA was undetectable in the T2 treatment, with no intergroup variation observed among the higher-intensity treatments. Regarding the proportional composition, the percentages of Fuc, Ara, Xyl, Man, Glc-UA, and Man-UA decreased with increasing red light intensity, whereas the percentage of Glc increased. Additionally, the percentages of Gal, Rib, and Gal-UA increased initially and then decreased (Fig. 2B).

SMRT sequencing and functional annotation

To identify the genes involved in polysaccharide biosynthesis in P. palustre, we employed SMRT sequencing on the PacBio Sequel platform for the assembly of full-length transcripts. A total of 25,249,357 subreads were generated with a maximum length of 218,347 bp, and about 25.3 Gb of clean reads were obtained (Table S1). The circular consensus sequences (CCS) were extracted from the original sequences according to the conditions of full passes ≥ 3 and sequence accuracy > 0.90. Results showed that a total of 198,125 CCS reads with a total length of 339,487,693 bp, 158,850 full-length nonchimeric (FLNC) reads with a total length of 181,372,688 bp, and 95,236 consensus reads with a total length of 116,187,503 bp were identified; the N50 of CCS reads, FLNC reads, and consensus reads were 20,212 bp, 16,829 bp, and 16,829 bp, while the GC contents of CCS, FLNC, and consensus were 30.446%, 43.484%, and 42.732%, respectively (Table S2). In addition, the proportion of CCS, FLNC, and consensus reads in the 0.5-6k length range accounted for 86.44%, 71.58%, and 71.5%, respectively (Fig. S1A-C). Subsequently, the integrity of the de-redundant transcripts was assessed using BUSCO (Fig. S1D).

To obtain a comprehensive functional annotation of the P. palustre transcriptome, we annotated 69,222 transcripts against six databases (GO, KEGG, COG, NR, Swiss-Prot, and Pfam databases). The annotation information in each database was obtained and analyzed statistically. In total, 46,885, 27,648, 48,066, 52,559, 42,849, and 39,308 transcripts were annotated against the GO, KEGG, COG, NR, Swiss-Prot, and Pfam databases, respectively, accounting for 67.73%, 39.94%, 69.44%, 75.93%, 61.9%, and 56.79% of all transcripts (Table S3).

Illumina sequencing and functional annotation

We collected twelve RNA samples from leaves and performed second-generation RNA sequencing on the Illumina platform. A total of 94.81 Gb clean data were obtained, and the clean data of each sample exceeded 6.28 Gb, and the percentage of Q30 bases was above 94.61%, with an average GC content of 47.90% (Table S4). After high-quality sequencing data were obtained, the clean data of all samples were de novo assembled using Trinity, and the assembly results were optimized and evaluated. The final assembly yielded 69,222 unigenes and 69,222 transcripts, with an N50 length of 1,954 bp (Table S5). Among the 69,222 unigenes, 46% (31,792 unigenes) were shorter than 1000 bp, 42% (28,822 unigenes) ranged from 1001 to 2500 bp, and 13% (8,608 unigenes) exceeded 2500 bp in length (Fig. S2A). The clean reads of each sample were mapped to the reference sequences obtained from the Trinity assembly, yielding mapping rates ranging from 64.03% to 67.60% (Table S6). To analyze the functions of the unigenes obtained from the full-length transcripts, we annotated them using the data from six nucleotide and protein databases (GO, KEGG, eggNOG, NR, Pfam, and Swiss-Prot) (Fig. S2B; Table S7).

Differentially expressed genes analysis

The expression levels of unigenes were calculated using the TPM method with RSEM software. Subsequently, sample correlation analysis and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed. The results showed that the biological replicates exhibited strong correlations, while the expression levels significantly differed between the two groups of samples (Fig. 3A-B). The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the six comparisons (T2-vs-T4, T2-vs-T6, T2-vs-T8, T4-vs-T6, T4-vs-T8, T6-vs-T8) were identified according to the following conditions: |log2FC| ≥ 1 and p-value < 0.05. Generally, the number of DEGs increased with increasing differences in light intensity. Compared to the T2 group, 443, 1363, and 2712 DEGs were up-regulated, and 1319, 1518, and 2818 DEGs were down-regulated in the T4, T6, and T8 groups, respectively. Compared to the T4 group, 779 and 1535 DEGs were up-regulated, and 268 and 768 DEGs were down-regulated in the T6 and T8 groups, respectively. In the T6-vs-T8 comparison, 633 DEGs were identified (343 up-regulated and 290 down-regulated) (Fig. 3C). The Venn diagram showed that a total of 19 common DEGs were identified among the six comparisons, with 14 being up-regulated and 5 being down-regulated (Fig. 3D).

The KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that all six comparisons shared the “Plant hormone signal transduction” pathway (Fig. 4), suggesting that plant hormones might be the dominant factors influencing the response of P. palustre to light intensity. In addition, five out of six comparisons (excluding T6-vs-T8) shared the “Starch and sucrose metabolism” pathway, which was closely associated with polysaccharide biosynthesis. Galactose is one of the monosaccharide components of P. palustre polysaccharides. The “Galactose metabolism” pathway was shared among the comparisons of T2-vs-T6, T2-vs-T8, T4-vs-T6, and T4-vs-T8 (Fig. 4).

Identification of genes involved in polysaccharide biosynthesis

To identify genes involved in the polysaccharide biosynthesis pathways in P. palustre, we identified the DEGs associated with “Starch and sucrose metabolism” (map00500), “Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism” (map00520), “Galactose metabolism” (map00052), and “Fructose and mannose metabolism” (map00051). Based on the previously reported polysaccharide biosynthesis pathways in plants [31], a total of 55 DEGs were identified in this study. These DEGs encoded enzymes involved in the biosynthesis of monosaccharide components, including sucrose synthase (SuSy), UTP-glucose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase (UGP2), fructokinase (scrK), beta-fructofuranosidase (sacA), UDP-glucose 6-dehydrogenase (UGDH), UDP-glucuronate 4-epimerase (GAE), UDP-apiose/xylose synthase (AXS), UDP- xylose synthase (UXS), UDP-glucose 4,6-dehydratase (RHM), UDP-glucose 4-epimerase (GALE), Hexokinase (HK), phosphomannomutase (PMM), phosphoglucomutase (PGM), mannose-1-phosphate guanylyltransferase (GMPP), and GDP-mannose 4,6-dehydratase (GMDS) (Table 1). Subsequently, the hypothetical polysaccharide biosynthesis pathways for P. palustre, combined with a gene expression heatmap, were depicted (Fig. 5).

Identification of WGCNA modules

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was used to identify key modules and hub genes associated with the contents of different monosaccharides. After sample clustering analysis and determination of soft threshold power (β = 3) (Fig. S3A-C), we conducted module detection, identifying six modules: blue, brown, green, turquoise, yellow, and grey (Fig. 6A, B). Each of these modules contained 3,992, 534, 184, 11,284, 400, and 3,268 genes, respectively (Fig. 6B). Moreover, the module-trait correlations were constructed to identify modules that were significantly related to the contents of different monosaccharides (Fig. 6C).

The weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). (A) Module partitioning diagram; (B) The statistics of genes in different modules; (C) The correlation between module and trait. The contents of monosaccharides were used as traits

The gene significance (GS) is defined as the correlation between gene expression levels and trait data. The greater the GS value, the stronger the correlation between a gene and a trait. The module significance (MS) is defined as the average GS of all genes in a module. The greater the MS value, the greater the correlation between a module and a trait. Hence, the target module for each trait was identified by calculating the MS value. As the results showed (Fig. 7), the highest MS values for five monosaccharides, i.e., fucose (MS = 0.60254), arabinose (MS = 0.57045), xylose (MS = 0.57854), ribose (MS = 0.50665), and galacturonic acid (MS = 0.52224) were obtained in the brown module (Fig. 7A, B, E, G, H). The turquoise module had the highest MS values for galactose (0.53392), glucose (0.59281), and mannose (0.5376), respectively (Fig. 7C, D, F). Likewise, the highest MS value for mannuronic acid was 0.48268 in the yellow module (Fig. 7I). In addition, the identification of WGCNA modules revealed that the blue module was significantly positively correlated with galactose and glucose (Fig. 6C), and it was the second largest module, containing 3,992 genes (Fig. 6B). To obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanism of polysaccharide biosynthesis, the blue module was also selected as a target module.

Selection of hub transcription factors

Module membership (MM) represents the correlation between a gene and a module. The greater the MM value, the greater the possibility that this gene belongs to the corresponding module. To narrow down the scope of candidate genes in each module, the threshold values (MM > 0.8 and |GS| >0.5) were set. In the brown module, a total of 173, 173, 173, 167, and 164 genes were selected and associated with fucose, arabinose, xylose, ribose, and galacturonic acid, respectively (Table S8-12). To further screen the hub transcription factors (TFs), Cytoscape software was used to visualize the gene regulatory networks. Based on the connectivity (within the top 10%), 17, 17, 17, 16, and 16 hub genes were selected for fucose, arabinose, xylose, ribose, and galacturonic acid, respectively (Fig. 8A, B). Among these, fucose, arabinose, and xylose shared the same 17 hub genes (Fig. 8A), while ribose and galacturonic acid shared the same 16 hub genes (Fig. 8B). It was noteworthy that these 17 and 16 hub genes shared three differentially expressed TFs, i.e., transcript_43348 (B3_superfamily), transcript_63921 (B3_superfamily), and transcript_73261 (B3_superfamily) (Fig. 8A, B), suggesting that these B3 transcription factors might play a critical role in the biosynthesis of fucose, arabinose, xylose, ribose, and galacturonic acid.

Similarly, in the yellow module, a total of 123 genes were selected (Table S13), potentially playing key roles in mannuronic acid biosynthesis. Further screening of the differentially expressed hub TFs was based on their high connectivity (top 10%). We identified transcript_17966 (CAMTA), transcript_63633 (AP2/ERF), and transcript_1530 (B3_superfamily) as key candidates (Fig. 8C).

In the turquoise module, a total of 3,406, 3,852, and 3,741 genes for galactose, glucose, and mannose, respectively, were screened (Table S14-16). Based on the connectivity (top 5%), a total of 170, 192, and 187 hub genes were selected for galactose, glucose, and mannose, respectively (Fig. 8D-F). Among them, 4, 6, and 6 differentially expressed hub TFs were identified, respectively. Notably, 4 hub TFs, including transcript_55196 (MYB), transcript_35035 (bHLH), transcript_57269 (MYB), and transcript_57705 (AP2/ERF) were shared among galactose, glucose, and mannose. The Other two hub TFs, transcript_1862 (MYB) and transcript_37770 (TCP), were shared between glucose and mannose.

In the blue module, 1,163 genes were screened for galactose, and 1,334 genes were screened for glucose (Table S17-18). According to the connectivity (top 10%), 116 and 133 hub genes were selected for galactose and glucose, respectively (Fig. 8G, H). Additionally, a total of 13 hub TFs were identified for galactose (Fig. 8G) and 14 hub TFs were identified for glucose (Fig. 8H). Notably, galactose and glucose shared 13 hub TFs.

In summary, we selected a total of 26 hub TFs associated with fucose, arabinose, xylose, ribose, galacturonic acid, mannuronic acid, galactose, glucose, and mannose. These TFs belonged to the B3, AP2/ERF, CAMTA, MYB, bHLH, TCP, WRKY, and NAC families. After excluding 9 TFs with extremely low expression levels, we identified a total of 17 hub TFs: six genes (transcript_51972, transcript_50107, transcript_27106, transcript_53344, transcript_63800, and transcript_36064) encoding WRKY, four genes (transcript_43348, transcript_63921, transcript_73261, and transcript_1530) encoding B3, three genes (transcript_63633, transcript_50474, and transcript_52483) encoding AP2/ERF, three genes (transcript_1862, transcript_63633, and transcript_55196) encoding MYB, and one gene (transcript_35035) encoding bHLH (Fig. 9A).

Protein interaction network analysis

Previous KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the “Plant hormone signal transduction” pathway might play an important role in polysaccharide biosynthesis in P. palustre. To uncover the underlying interaction network between hub TFs and DEGs involved in both polysaccharide biosynthesis and the “Plant hormone signal transduction” pathway, we performed protein interaction network analysis using the STRING database (http://string-db.org/). The analysis was based on Arabidopsis thaliana as a reference species, with a confidence score of 0.4. As the results showed, transcript_43348 (B3_superfamily) exhibited an interaction with transcript_20138 (Sucrose Synthase, SuSy), while transcript_1530 (B3_superfamily) interacted with transcript_35035 (bHLH). Moreover, transcript_1530 (B3_superfamily) also interacted with transcript_41067 (SnRK2) (Fig. 9B), which is an important component in ABA signal transduction [34]. Simultaneously, transcript_52483 (AP2/ERF) interacted with transcript_23314 (ARR-B) (Fig. 9B), which is involved in cytokinin signal transduction [35]. These gene interactions may be involved in the biosynthesis of polysaccharides induced by red light intensity in P. palustre.

Real-time quantitative PCR verification

To validate the accuracy and reliability of the RNA sequencing data, six DEGs were randomly selected for RT-qPCR analysis, including transcript_55196 (MYB), transcript_1862 (MYB), transcript_35035 (bHLH), transcript_50107 (WRKY), transcript_50474 (AP2/ERF), and transcript_63800 (WRKY). The results demonstrated a general consistency with the RNA sequencing data (Fig. S4), indicating the accuracy and reliability of the RNA-Seq results.

Discussion

Light serves as the primary power source for carbohydrate synthesis in plants. On this account, light factors including light quality, photoperiod, and light intensity can affect plant growth and development [1]. Our previous study revealed that compared to blue light, red light led to greater biomass, plant height, and root characteristics, as well as higher concentrations of soluble sugar and soluble pectin [33]. The current study demonstrated a significant increase in plant fresh weight and above-ground fresh weight (Fig. 1I,J), suggesting a positive correlation between red light intensity and P. palustre growth. However, root growth was optimal under the T6 treatment (Fig. 1K). These results suggested that different organs displayed distinct responses to red light intensity in P. palustre. Apart from plant growth, different light intensities also influence plant quality [36, 37]. Polysaccharides in P. palustre serve as an important index to evaluate its quality [29, 30]. Generally, the correlation between light intensity and polysaccharide content varies among different plant species [38, 39]. In the present study, the contents of ten kinds of monosaccharides were determined (Fig. 2). Notably, most monosaccharides exhibited the highest content in the T4, T6, or T8 treatments, suggesting that an increase in light intensity to a certain degree could increase monosaccharide contents, whereas excessively high light intensity may have a negative effect [39].

High-throughput sequencing technologies have been extensively utilized to explore the polysaccharide biosynthesis pathway in plants, including Codonopsis pilosula [40], Polygonatum cyrtonema Hua [41], and Panax ginseng C.A. Meyer [42]. However, the polysaccharide biosynthesis pathways in P. palustre have not been elucidated. Despite the striking differences in the structure of polysaccharides among different plant species, the biosynthesis process of polysaccharides remains conserved [40–42]. Enzymes such as sacA, HK, scrK, PMM, GMPP, isomerase, decarboxylase, etc., have been reported to play important roles in polysaccharide biosynthesis [31, 43]. In this study, a total of 55 DEGs involved in P. palustre polysaccharide biosynthesis were identified through transcriptomic analysis (Table 1; Fig. 5). These DEGs encoded enzymes in the biosynthesis pathways, including SuSy, PGM, UGP2, UGDH, RHM, and other enzymes. Similar to other higher plants, sucrose was the precursor of polysaccharide biosynthesis in P. palustre according to the proposed biosynthetic pathways (Fig. 5). However, not all 55 DEGs contribute to the polysaccharide biosynthesis, as some DEGs encoding the same enzyme showed different expression patterns (Fig. 5). For example, our study revealed that a total of 4 enzymes (including SuSy, PGM, sacA, and UGP2) which are encoded by 21 DEGs were upstream of glucose synthesis, and most of these DEGs showed a positive correlation with the content of glucose, except for transcript_30690 (UGP2). Numerous studies have concluded that genes encoding these enzymes play important roles in glucose synthesis [42, 44, 45]. Hence, these positively related DEGs may be the key genes contributing to glucose synthesis in P. palustre. Besides, 7 DEGs encoding GAE were identified, with only four (transcript_59017, transcript_40744, transcript_50770, and transcript_55815) positively correlated with the content of Gal-UA. In the synthetic pathways of Man and Fuc, most of the DEGs showed decreasing expression patterns with the increase of red light intensity, contrasting with the contents of Man and Fuc. It was inferred that these DEGs might play negative roles in the biosynthesis of polysaccharides in P. palustre. Similar results have been reported in Polygonatum sibiricum [46].

WGCNA can elucidate the interactions between genes and specific traits and identify the hub genes at the center of the regulatory network [47]. This study employed WGCNA to select key TFs involved in polysaccharide biosynthesis in P. palustre (Fig. 6). Based on their high connectivities and expression levels, a total of 17 hub TFs, belonging to the B3_superfamily, AP2/ERF, MYB, bHLH, and WRKY families, were selected. Among these, 8 hub TFs were negatively associated with polysaccharide biosynthesis, and 9 were positively associated with polysaccharide biosynthesis (Figs. 8 and 9). Many studies have demonstrated the crucial roles of TFs in regulating polysaccharide biosynthesis. In Arabidopsis, the mutation in MYB61 resulted in a significant reduction in the polysaccharide content of seed mucilage [48]. The GlbHLH mutants of Ganoderma lucidum exhibited a significant decrease in both intracellular and extracellular polysaccharide production [49]. Jiang et al. [50] reported that PcWRKY31 and PcWRKY34 bound to the promoter of PcgalE1, positively regulating its expression. PcgalE1 was a key enzyme in the polysaccharide biosynthesis pathway. Therefore, in this study, these 17 hub TFs might play important roles in regulating the biosynthesis of polysaccharides in P. palustre.

To uncover the potential molecular regulatory mechanisms between hub TFs and DEGs involved in polysaccharide biosynthesis, we performed a protein interaction network analysis. The results showed interactions between transcript_43348 (B3_superfamily) and transcript_20138 (SuSy), as well as between transcript_1530 (B3_superfamily) and transcript_35035 (bHLH) (Fig. 9B). These interactions might play important roles in the polysaccharide synthesis in P. palustre. Plant hormones, including ABA, gibberellin (GA), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), cytokinin (CYT), etc., have been reported to influence polysaccharide accumulation [51–53]. We also analyzed the interaction network between hub TFs and DEGs involved in the “Plant hormone signal transduction” pathway, given its significant enrichment in our study (Fig. 4). As the results showed, transcript_1530 (B3_superfamily) exhibited an interaction with transcript_41067 (SnRK2), while transcript_52483 (AP2/ERF) interacted with transcript_23314 (ARR-B) (Fig. 9B). SnRK2 is an important component in ABA signal transduction and it functions in the gene-regulation pathway of ABA signaling by phosphorylating ABA-responsive genes [54]. The type-B authentic response regulator (ARR-B) serves as a transcriptional activator in CYT signal transduction, which can lead to rapid induction of CYT-associated target genes [35, 55]. We speculated that these genes might be involved in polysaccharide biosynthesis in P. palustre.

Overall, we have preliminarily investigated the molecular mechanism of red light-induced polysaccharide biosynthesis in P. palustre, and identified some potential functional genes and transcription factors. In future studies, we will perform functional validation of key genes to gain a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying polysaccharide biosynthesis and regulation in P. palustre.

Conclusions

The plant fresh weight and above-ground fresh weight of P. palustre increased with increasing red light intensity, and the T6 treatment was most favorable for the root growth of P. palustre. Furthermore, most of the monosaccharides showed the highest contents in the T4, T6, or T8 treatment, suggesting that an increase in light intensity to a certain degree could increase the contents of monosaccharides. Transcriptome sequencing identified 55 DEGs encoding enzymes involved in polysaccharide biosynthesis. Based on the annotation results, the polysaccharide biosynthesis pathways were reconstructed. Moreover, WGCNA was used to identify 17 hub TFs related to monosaccharide biosynthesis, which belong to the B3_superfamily, AP2/ERF, MYB, bHLH, and WRKY families. Additionally, protein interaction network analysis showed interactions between transcript_43348 (B3_superfamily) and transcript_20138 (PpSuSy), between transcript_1530 (B3_superfamily) and transcript_35035 (bHLH), between transcript_1530 (B3_superfamily) and transcript_41067 (SnRK2), and between transcript_52483 (AP2/ERF) and transcript_23314 (ARR-B). These interactions might play dominant roles in the polysaccharide synthesis in P. palustre. This study provides valuable genes involved in the molecular mechanism of polysaccharide biosynthesis and laid a foundation for breeding a new variety with high polysaccharide content.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

We transplanted about 10-cm-tall P. palustre cuttings into nutrient cups filled with substrate, watered the substrate with Hoagland nutrient solution, and then placed the nutrient cups into a tray. Subsequently, the P. palustre plants were placed in a 28 ℃ room environment and subjected to different red light intensity treatments under a 14/10 h (light/dark) photoperiod: T2 (two LED tubes, 1010 lx), T4 (four LED tubes, 1203 lx), T6 (six LED tubes, 2058 lx), and T8 (eight LED tubes, 2389 lx). To ensure the growth of P. palustre, enough nutrient solution was added to the trays for each treatment. After one month, the 3rd−4th pairs of leaves from the top of the plant downwards were collected for transcriptome and targeted metabolomics analysis. At the same time, the roots, stems, and leaves of P. palustre plants were subjected to full-length transcriptome sequencing.

Determination of plant fresh weight

One month later, we took photos of the P. palustre plants treated with different red light intensities and then measured the spectra of different red light intensities using an Light-emitting diode meter equipment (UPRtek, MK350NPLUS). The plant fresh weight, root fresh weight, and above-ground fresh weight were determined from at least four biological replicates (individual plants), and then the average values were calculated.

Chemicals and reagents

Methanol and trifluoroacetic acid were purchased from ANPEL. Sodium hydroxide and sodium acetate trihydrate were purchased from Sigma, and ultra-pure water was prepared in-house using a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA).

Sample preparation and extraction

This study comprised four treatments, each replicated four times, yielding 16 samples for monosaccharide analysis. Approximately 5 mg of each sample was hydrolyzed with trifluoroacetic acid (2 M) at 105 ℃ for 6 h in a sealed tube. The sample was dried under a steam of nitrogen. Methanol was added for washing, followed by blow-drying, and this methanol washing step was repeated 2–3 times. The residue was re-dissolved in deionized water and filtered through a 0.22 μm microporous filter for measurement.

HPAEC conditions

The sample extracts were analyzed by high-performance anion-exchange chromatography (HPAEC) on a CarboPac PA-10 anion-exchange column (4.6 by 250 mm; Dionex) using a pulsed amperometric detector (PAD; Dionex ICS 5000 system). Flow rate: 0.5 mL/min; injection volume: 5µL; solvent system: solvent A (0.1M NaOH) and solvent B: (0.1 M NaOH, 0.2 M NaAc); gradient program: 95% A/5% B at 0 min, 80% A/20% B at 30 min, 60% A/40% B at 30.1 min, 60% A/40% B at 45 min, 95% A/5% B at 45.1 min, and 95% A/5% B at 60 min.

Data analysis and interpretation

Data were acquired on the ICS5000 (Thermo Scientific) and processed using Chromeleon 7.2 CDS (Thermo Scientific).

PacBio SMRT library construction, sequencing, and data analysis

The total RNA of the roots, stems, and leaves of P. palustre plants with three biological replicates were extracted with the Trizol RNA extraction kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). In particular, the 3rd−4th pairs of leaves from the top of the plant downwards were harvested. Subsequently, the high-quality total RNA of different tissues was mixed equally and reverse-transcribed to cDNA with Clontech SMARTer™ PCR cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Dalian, China), followed by PCR amplification, terminal repair, and purification. Then, the full-length cDNA library was constructed using SMRTbell express template prep 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After quality inspection, the library was subjected to SMRT sequencing on the PacBio Sequel platform. The raw data were analyzed by SMRT Link V6.0 software with a series of processes including identification of reads of insert (ROI), read classification, and read clustering and correction, to obtain high-quality full-length consistent sequences. After removing redundancy by use of CD-HIT software, the final unique isoforms were obtained and then functionally annotated as described by Zhu et al. [56]. Three experimental replicates were performed.

Illumina transcriptome library preparation, sequencing, and data analysis

This study employed four treatments, each with three replicates, resulting in a total of 12 samples for transcriptome analysis. Total RNA from leaves were extracted, and their integrity and purity were assessed as described above. The Illumina transcriptome library was prepared using a TruSeq TM RNA sample preparation Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (San Diego, CA). After validating the library, the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer was used for paired-end RNA sequencing. Using the PacBio-derived reference genome, clean reads were further analyzed [57]. The expression level of each transcript was calculated based on the transcripts per million reads (TPM) method, and gene abundances were quantified by RSEM (http://deweylab.biostat.wisc.edu/rsem/) [58]. The differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 [59], and genes with |log2FC|≥1 and P-adjust ≤ 0.05 were considered to be differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The gene functional annotation was performed as described by Tang et al. [33].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) validation

The cDNA synthesis, qPCR reaction system, and qPCR amplification procedure were conducted according to Tang et al. [33]. The qPCR primers were designed using Primer Premier 5 software and listed in Supplementary Table 19. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. The relative gene expression was calculated by the 2−△△Ct method [60].
